## 15. The Long Dark of Monsoon

The water came for them on the third night, and it did not knock.

Astrid woke to a sound like the world clearing its throat — a deep, grinding roar from up-canyon — and by the time she had her boots on, the river that had been a silver thread below the cave mouth was a brown ocean climbing toward them in surges. She stood at the lip of the glacier cave with Wren beside her and watched the flood swallow the gravel bar where they'd moored the *Kestrel*, take her lines off the boulders one by one like fingers uncurling, and leave the old boat straining at her last anchor, half-swamped, pitching against the current with a sound Astrid felt in her teeth.

"She holds," Wren said. It wasn't a question, but it wasn't quite a belief either.

"She has to." Astrid's voice came out flat and strange to her own ears. "She's all we have."

Behind them, in the blue ice-light of the cave, forty ledgers lay stacked in oilcloth like sleeping animals. Forty families. Forty thefts. And now, between them and the world outside, a monsoon with no end in sight.

* * *

**Dulce**

Dulce Okafor counted things when she was frightened. It was how she'd survived the cholera year in Port Meridian as a girl: count the roof tiles, count the steps to the well, count the days until your father's cough either broke or killed him. Numbers were a rope you could hold onto while everything else flooded.

So she counted.

Four days of rain without pause. Nine people in the cave — herself, Captain Vasquez, Wren, old man Thorn's body wrapped in sailcloth in the coldest crevice because the ground was too wet to dig, and five others who'd scattered when the syndicate boat went down-river to report: two deckhands named Sefu and Ilsa, the engineer Bram, a quiet boy called Tomas who'd signed on at Kettle Landing for the adventure of it and gotten the monsoon instead, and a trapper named Halloran who'd joined them at the Throat's mouth and hadn't said ten words since.

Forty ledgers. One map, soft as cloth now from handling. One boat, anchored in floodwater, taking water faster than they could pump.

And — she counted this three times to be sure — eleven days of food, if they ate thin. Seven if they didn't.

On the fifth day she told Astrid the numbers, because someone had to, and watched the captain's jaw tighten the way it did when she was deciding something.

"Eleven," Dulce repeated. "Maybe nine, if the cold makes us hungry."

"I know."

"And the syndicate knows we're here. The patrol boat turned back before the flood closed the river. They'll come when it opens."

"I know that too."

"So what I want to know," Dulce said, and her voice cracked on it, because she'd been holding this since the second day, "is what we're waiting *for*. We could take four of those ledgers — just ours, just enough — and be gone when the water drops. Sell them quietly. Live."

Astrid looked at her for a long moment. In the cave's light her face was all planes and shadow, and Dulce thought, not for the first time, that the captain looked less like a woman than like a coastline — something shaped entirely by what had been taken from it.

"You've read yours?" Astrid asked quietly. "Your family's page?"

Dulce had. Three nights ago, by candle stub, alone. Her grandfather's ferry license, sold for eleven pounds and a bottle of rum to a man whose signature she now knew by heart. The arithmetic of dispossession, laid out in a clerk's neat hand.

"Yes," she said.

"Then you know selling four doesn't buy anything back. It just moves the theft to whoever buys them." Astrid touched the oilcloth stack gently, the way you'd touch a wound. "I'm not asking you to be noble, Dulce. I'm asking you to wait. That's harder, and I know it."

It was. Dulce went back to counting. But that night, when Tomas asked her why she kept scratching marks into the cave wall, she found herself saying, "So we know how long we held," and the boy nodded like that meant something, and maybe it did.

* * *

**Halloran**

Halloran had killed a man once, over less than what was written in these books.

He sat apart from the others, near the cave mouth where he could watch the brown water rise and fall and rise again, and he turned his knife over in his hands and thought about the name on page thirty-one of the third ledger: Verrone. His mother's family. Their orchard terraces above Salt Creek, taken in a single season through a debt contract so crooked it had to have been drafted by the same pen that wrote the rest of this filth. He'd been nine. He remembered his mother kneeling in the mud of their own driveway, begging a man in a gray coat, and the man tipping his hat like she'd asked him the time.

The syndicate had a name for every trick in those pages. And the syndicate had men. Men could be found, when the water dropped.

"We should burn it all," the boy Tomas said behind him, startling him. The kid had a way of moving quiet as weather. "That's what the captain says. Burn it and everyone's free."

"Burning frees no one," Halloran said. "Burning just makes sure nobody ever gets justice. You think the syndicate forgets what they took? They'll keep taking. They'll just stop keeping records." He tapped the ledger spine beside him. "This is evidence, boy. This is leverage. Forty families, organized, with proof — that's an army. Ash is just ash."

Tomas frowned. "But the captain—"

"The captain's running from her own name. Ask yourself why a woman would rather torch her own inheritance than hold it in her hand." Halloran put the knife away. "I've waited twenty years to see names like these in daylight. I'm not watching them go up in smoke because burning feels clean."

He didn't say the rest of it. Didn't say that he'd already picked out which pages he'd take if it came to taking, or that he'd worked out, in the long gray hours, exactly how many of the others would stand with him and how many would stand with her. He didn't say that revenge, kept warm under the ribs for twenty years, does not care whose plan it rides in on.

But he thought it. All day. All night. The rain drummed on, patient as a debt collector.

* * *

**Wren**

Wren learned the cave the way they learned everything: by touch, by repetition, by pretending competence until it hardened into fact.

There was the entrance chamber, wide as a chapel, where the blue light came through the ice in the mornings and made everyone look drowned. There were the side galleries where the glacier groaned and ticked, adjusting itself around the warm bodies intruding on it. There was the vault niche where the ledgers slept, and the low crawl at the back that went nowhere but gave privacy, and after six days everyone had claimed their corner of the dark like cats.

And there was Astrid, who moved through all of it like the only fixed point.

Wren watched her on the seventh morning, when the food got thin enough that hunger had started making people strange. Sefu had accused Bram of hoarding biscuits; Bram had nearly swung on him. And Astrid had stepped between them — small, wiry, half a head shorter than either man — and said nothing at all, just stood there until the anger ran out of somewhere to go.

Afterward, Wren found her at the cave mouth, mending a net that didn't need mending.

"That's twice now," Wren said. "You do that thing where you don't say anything and it works. Where'd you learn it?"

"My father." She didn't look up. "He used to say a riverboat captain's real cargo is other people's tempers. Cargo you can lash down doesn't fight you at three in the morning."

"He sounds like he's worth knowing."

"He was." A pause, long enough that Wren understood the tense of it. Then, quieter: "You're good at this too. Holding people together. Salvage work teach you that?"

Wren's hands stilled on the net line. "Salvage work teaches you that everything's worth more broken up than whole. Crews included." They tied off a knot, undid it, tied it again. "I was good at breaking things up. For a while."

"And now?"

"Now I'm sitting in a cave full of proof that the thing I helped break was forty families wide." Wren finally looked at her. "I keep thinking about my mother's name in that book. Page nineteen. She never knew why we lost the house. She died thinking she'd failed us somehow. Twenty years she carried that, and it was never hers to carry." Their voice caught, and they let it, because Astrid was the first person in years they'd decided to let hear it. "I don't want anyone else's mother carrying it either. That's the whole of me now. That's everything left."

Astrid set down the net. For a moment her hand hovered near Wren's shoulder — Wren saw the hover, saw the decision inside it — and then she simply sat down close, shoulder to shoulder, and they watched the rain hammer the floodwater together, saying nothing, which was its own kind of saying.

* * *

**Astrid**

On the eighth night, the stories started, because Astrid made them start.

She'd done the arithmetic too — Dulce's numbers, plus fear, plus Halloran's silence, plus the way Tomas flinched now whenever the glacier boomed — and she knew that hunger and dark and helplessness were a tide, and tides drown people who stand still in them. So after the thin meal of rice and river-fish Bram caught off the cave mouth on a handline, she lit the last fat candle, set it in the middle of the floor, and said:

"Somebody tell a story. Rules are: it can't be true, and it has to be better than here."

They stared at her. Rain. Glacier-groan. Then Ilsa, surprising everyone including herself, said, "All right. There's a city where the streets fill with water every spring, and the rich people pay poor people to carry them on their backs through the floods—" and she was off, and it was ridiculous and wonderful, and by the time she finished, Sefu was adding to it, and then Bram, and the candle burned down another inch and nobody had checked the food stores or the rising water for a whole hour.

Astrid told one on the ninth night, about a compass that pointed not north but toward whatever you'd lost, and how its owner spent his whole life walking in circles because he couldn't stop looking backward. She didn't say who it was about. Everyone knew anyway. Nobody said so, and that was the intimacy of it — the way a story can hold a truth that speech would break.

On the tenth night, Halloran told one. It started as a story about a man who avenged his family and ended, somewhere in the middle, with the man standing in a burned courtroom realizing the verdict had freed no one, least of all himself. He finished it rough-voiced and wouldn't meet anyone's eyes, and afterward Astrid caught him looking at the ledgers with an expression she recognized from her own mirror: a man renegotiating with himself, and losing.

On the eleventh night, the food was nearly gone, and the stories were thinner, and Dulce read out the count in a voice like a bell tolling: "Two days of rice. Half a tin of oil. The water's dropped a handspan since dawn."

"A handspan," Astrid repeated. She stood, and everyone looked up, because eight days of stillness had taught them to read her movements like scripture. "Then listen. When the river opens, we move fast and we move together. Everything on the boat before the water's waist-deep on the bar. Ledgers, gear, all of it. No one stays, no one splits, no one takes a page and slips off into the hills." Her eyes went deliberately to Halloran, and held. "We're forty families tonight. Not one. Not nine. Forty. That's the only arithmetic that gets any of us home."

Silence. Rain. Then Halloran said, quietly, "And if the syndicate's waiting at the mouth?"

"Then we'll deal with them," Astrid said, "with all of it, or none of it. Together."

She didn't sleep that night. She sat at the cave mouth with the map across her knees and watched the floodwater fall, finger-width by finger-width, revealing the gravel bar like a wound closing. Somewhere downriver, she knew, the syndicate was doing the same arithmetic she was. Somewhere downriver, Mateo was waiting — she felt it the way you feel weather coming, in the scar tissue of the family she'd fled.

Near dawn, the glacier spoke.

Not its usual groan and tick, but a long, cracking roar from high up the canyon, followed by a white plume of spray visible even in the dark, and Wren was beside her instantly, both of them staring up at the pale mass of the icefield where, along its face, a seam of open water was beginning to show — the calving season starting early, the upper route unlocking itself.

"That's our window," Wren breathed. "Two days. Maybe less."

"And theirs," Astrid said.

Below them, far down the black throat of the gorge, a light appeared. Small. Moving. Steady as intention.

Someone else had been counting too.
