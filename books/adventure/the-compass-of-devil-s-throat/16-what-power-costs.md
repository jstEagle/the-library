## 16. What Power Costs

The rain had been falling on the confluence camp for six days when the syndicate's plane came in.

Mateo heard it before he saw it — a wrongness in the sky's sound, a drone too steady to be weather, cutting through the monsoon's ragged percussion like a blade through wet canvas. He stepped out of his quarters into the gray deluge and stood on the plank walkway above the mud, watching the floatplane descend through the murk with the arrogant calm of something that did not believe the storm could touch it. It came down the river valley low and flat-winged, skimming the whitecaps of the flooded confluence, and settled against the current at the company dock with a hiss of spray and displaced water that soaked him where he stood.

He didn't move. He let it soak him. That was a kind of discipline he'd learned years ago, standing in another downpour outside another office, waiting for men who made you wait so you'd remember your place. The rain was cold even in the warm season here, cold as glacier melt, and it ran off his jaw and dripped from the brim of his hat and he counted the drops because counting kept other things quiet.

Three figures disembarked. Two were security — big men in black oilskins with the syndicate's iron-key insignia stitched at the shoulder, moving with the careful balance of men who'd been trained on decks. The third was smaller, slighter, walking behind them under an umbrella one of them held, and Mateo felt his stomach tighten around its old knot before he could even make out the face.

Vess. Aurelia Vess, regional director of acquisitions, come upriver in the worst flying weather of the year, which meant she hadn't come to inspect. She'd come to collect.

He watched her cross the dock, watched the sentries snap straighter as she passed, watched the camp itself seem to contract around her the way a body contracts around a swallowed stone. And he thought, with a clarity that felt almost like peace: *I know how this ends. I've seen how this ends. I've done this to other men.*

* * *

They met in the operations room, the long timber building at the camp's heart where the river charts hung and the radio crackled and the woodstove fought a losing war against the damp. Vess dismissed the guards, which was worse than keeping them. It meant what followed would be intimate.

She was not what strangers expected. Too small, too neat, silver-streaked hair pinned back with plain combs, hands folded on the chart table like a schoolteacher's. Her voice was soft enough that you leaned in to hear it, and by the time you realized you'd been leaning you'd already given her something. Mateo had watched her do it to half the landowners along the upper basin. He had once believed himself immune.

"Captain Vasquez." She used the title they'd given him — not captain of anything but paper, a commission invented to flatter him into feeling owned rather than employed. "You look tired."

"The monsoon came early."

"The monsoon always comes early now. That's why we pay you the premium." She unfolded her hands and laid a single sheet of paper on the table between them, face down, the way a dealer lays a card. "Six days ago my office received a report from Kettle Landing. A riverboat, old hull, patched twice, running upriver against the season with a dying prospector aboard and a woman at the wheel who paid her moorage in cash and gave no name. Two days ago, a patrol boat out of Mile Forty reported contact with the same vessel. Its captain filed a verbal report before going silent. Do you know what he said, Captain?"

Mateo said nothing. The stove ticked. Rain hammered the roof in sheets.

"He said the boat's pilot argued with him about a surname," Vess continued, "and then slipped him, which our patrol captains are not supposed to be capable of being slipped. He said the woman's voice sounded familiar. He said—" and here she turned the paper over, and Mateo saw it was nothing but a supply requisition, blank on its face, a prop, theater, "—he said the name she used was Vasquez."

The word landed in the room and stayed there, ringing like a struck bell that wouldn't finish fading.

"And I thought," Vess went on, softly, leaning in just slightly, just enough, "how strange. Because there is a Vasquez who works for me. A Vasquez whose family held rights on this very stretch of river, before the basin consolidation. A Vasquez who signed those rights away — or rather, whose mother's shaky signature was witnessed and notarized by men we both know — and who has spent eleven years enforcing the terms of that consolidation with such diligence that the home office twice considered him for promotion." She smiled. It reached her eyes the way frost reaches a window: all surface, nothing behind it. "So either there are two Vasquezes on this river in one week, Captain, or your sister has finally come home."

"She's not my sister," Mateo heard himself say. "Not anymore. She cut that line herself."

"Mm." Vess studied him the way he'd seen her study a deed with a forged seal — patiently, looking for the flaw she already knew was there. "And the prospector? Elias Thorn. An old man with a map and a conscience that arrived thirty years too late. You knew him, I believe. Back when you were still signing things."

"I knew him."

"Then you'll understand my concern. Thorn helped build the ledger vault. Thorn knows what's written in those books. And Thorn is on a boat piloted by a Vasquez, heading upriver toward a gorge where, if certain documents were ever recovered, ever read aloud, ever entered into a court that wasn't ours—" she paused, delicately, "—the consolidation would begin to look less like commerce and more like theft. Forty-one families, Captain. Forty-one inheritances folded into company holdings. This region runs on those holdings. I run on those holdings." She straightened. "You run on them too. Your house. Your boat. Your daughter's schooling in the capital. All of it is built on the ledger staying exactly where it is, in the ice, unread."

*My daughter.* The words went through him lower than any threat to his own skin could have. Lucía, seven years old, who drew rivers in every picture she made because Papa told her stories about boats, who had never once been allowed to visit the river because Papa said the air up here was bad for children, which was a lie so large it had its own weather system. He thought of her small hand in his at the school gate, the way she looked back twice before going in, always twice, as if checking he was real.

"What do you want from me?" he asked, and hated how level his voice came out, how much it sounded like the man Vess thought she'd bought.

"Results. That's all I've ever wanted from you." She rose, smoothing her coat, and walked to the window where the rain blurred the world into watercolor. "Find the boat. Find the map. Bring me Thorn if he lives, and the woman regardless. And then, Captain—" she turned, and for one unguarded instant something moved behind her eyes that looked almost like regret, or perhaps only indigestion, "—you will accompany me downriver. There are matters in the capital requiring your signature."

"What matters?"

"Your mother's claim," Vess said. "The original Vasquez filing. There's a question of whether the witness signatures were properly executed. The home office wants it resolved cleanly. Permanently." She smiled again. "You'll want to be present, I think. Family should be present at these things."

* * *

After she left — after the floatplane roared back up through the rain and the river closed over its wake — Mateo sat alone in the operations room until the stove burned down to orange coals and the light failed.

He took out the bottle he kept for celebrations, which he had never once opened, because in eleven years there had been nothing worth celebrating, only things worth surviving. He poured two fingers and didn't drink it. He turned the glass slowly on the chart table, watching the lamplight bend through the amber, and he made himself do the arithmetic he'd been refusing to do for a decade.

He had signed on, in the beginning, because the alternative was losing everything anyway. That was the story he told himself, the story he'd polished smooth as river stone from handling: *they had us cornered; I took the only door open; I saved what I could.* His mother's grief. Astrid's fury and her flight downriver, cutting the family name off her own stern like a gangrenous limb. He had told himself he stayed close to the machine to keep it from grinding what remained of them finer. He had told himself that power shared was power softened, that a man inside the syndicate could blunt its edges, protect the small holders, be a kind of hidden mercy.

And some nights, in the early years, he had almost believed it. When he'd quietly rerouted an inspection away from a widow's dredge. When he'd misfiled a claim long enough for a family to harvest a season. Small mercies, pebbles dropped in front of a landslide.

But the landslide hadn't slowed. It had learned to use him. Every mercy he granted became currency — goodwill banked, favors owed, a reputation for reasonableness that made men bring their disputes to him instead of to lawyers, which made them easier to settle, which made the taking smoother. He wasn't blunting the machine. He was its most efficient part, the shock absorber that let it roll over families without waking the whole valley. The syndicate hadn't corrupted him, he understood now, sitting in the dark with his untouched drink. It had *hired* him. Corruption implied he'd once been something else. He had simply been raw material, like the riverbanks themselves, like the forty-one inheritances — useful, extractable, and ultimately disposable.

Because that was the other thing the arithmetic showed him, the sum at the bottom of the column he'd avoided longest. Vess hadn't come to threaten him. Threats were for partners. What she'd laid on the table was a schedule. His mother's claim, reopened, to be "resolved permanently" — with his own signature procured to bury the last document proving the Vasquez family had ever owned anything at all. They would take his name the way they'd taken everything else, and they would use his hand to do it, and afterward he would be exactly what the patrol captains were, what the dock foremen were, what the drowned informant at Mile Forty had probably been: a tool with knowledge, and tools with knowledge get retired.

He thought of the men he'd dispossessed. Old Ferro, who'd shaken his hand at the signing and called him a smart boy, and died within the year in a flophouse upcountry. The Okonkwo sisters, who'd fought the survey crews with shotguns and lost everything including the shotguns. The girl — he couldn't remember her name anymore, and that forgetting was its own indictment — the girl who'd spat at his feet on the courthouse steps and told him the river would remember. He had laughed, then. He had actually laughed, young and armored in paperwork, and told her rivers don't remember, they only flow downhill, and money flows faster.

Now the river was coming for him at flood stage, and it remembered everything.

* * *

Near midnight, unable to bear the walls, he walked the camp.

The rain had thinned to a steady drizzle, and the confluence lay under a sky the color of wet slate, the two rivers braiding together below the bluff in slow brown turbulence. Lanterns burned at the dock and the guard posts; somewhere a radio played tin-soft music to men playing cards. His camp. His operation. Eleven years of building it, and tonight it felt like what it had always been: a cage with the door painted on.

He stopped at the edge of the bluff where the chart stake stood, the brass marker the syndicate had planted the day the consolidation finalized, driving it into the earth like a flag into conquered country. Below it, the rivers met. Above it, far up the dark valley, the glacier sat in its cirque doing what glaciers do — keeping time, keeping everything, keeping the ledgers of forty-one families locked in blue ice while the world above forgot them.

His sister was up there. Somewhere in that black gorge, in the teeth of the monsoon, in a leaking boat with a dead man's map and a stranger he didn't know the shape of. Running the rapids he'd been too afraid to run at twenty-five, carrying the documents he'd been too afraid to read for eleven years.

And the terrible thing — the thing that made him grip the chart stake until the cold brass bit his palm — was that some part of him, some part he'd starved and chained and buried under commissions and premiums and his daughter's school fees, that part was *rooting for her*. Had been rooting for her since the moment Vess said the name *Vasquez* and his heart had done something shameful and glad in his chest.

He tried to imagine stopping her. Standing at the cave mouth with his guards, taking the ledgers from her hands, handing his sister to Vess the way he'd handed Ferro and the Okonkwos and the nameless spitting girl. He tried, honestly tried, to see himself doing it, and found that the image wouldn't form. Not because he was too good — he knew precisely how bad he was, he had receipts — but because the arithmetic had finally finished, and the answer was this: there was nothing left to buy him with. They were taking the last of it tomorrow. A man with nothing left to lose stops being an asset and becomes a liability, and liabilities on this river had a way of floating downstream facedown.

Unless he chose the losing first. Unless he got there ahead of the schedule.

Mateo looked up the valley, into the rain and the dark, toward the throat of the gorge where the ice kept time. Then he turned and walked back through his sleeping camp, past the lanterns and the card game and the radio's soft music, and began, quietly, methodically, to count his boats, his fuel, his men — and to wonder which of them, if any, would follow him when he pointed the bow upriver instead of down.

By dawn he had his answer. By dawn, the glacier had begun to groan.
