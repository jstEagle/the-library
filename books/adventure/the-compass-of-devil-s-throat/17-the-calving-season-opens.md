## 17. The Calving Season Opens

The sound woke them all.

It came up through the stone of the cave floor first — a deep, tectonic groan, like the mountain clearing its throat — and then rolled out across the water in a series of cracks so sharp and bright that Astrid's hand went to the knife at her belt before her mind caught up with the noise. Not gunfire. Not the syndicate. Ice.

She crawled out of the vault chamber into the grey pre-dawn light spilling through the cave mouth, Wren a half-step behind her, and together they stood at the lip of the flooded threshold and watched the glacier die by inches.

The face of it hung above the upper lake like the wall of some ruined cathedral, three hundred feet of blue-white ice seamed with veins of ash-grey moraine. And along that face, the morning was busy with ruin. A serac the size of a riverboat leaned, shuddered, and let go, falling in silence for one impossible heartbeat before it struck the water and threw up a wave that came rolling toward the cave mouth like a slow brown fist. Astrid counted without meaning to — she'd been counting waves since she was six years old, standing on her father's shoulders on the *Mariposa*'s deck — and this one would lift the boat where it was moored inside the second chamber, but not break it against the rock. Barely.

"Calving season," Wren said quietly. "I thought we had another week."

"The week was a lie we told ourselves." Astrid watched the ice face shed another fragment, smaller, its splash swallowed by the echo of the first. "The Throat doesn't keep calendars. It keeps accounts."

Behind them, in the lamplit dark of the vault chamber, the ledgers lay stacked in oilcloth bundles where they'd spent nine days hauling them out of the ice — forty-one volumes, each one a family's name written down and sold, each one heavier than its paper had any right to be. Nine days trapped by floodwater at the cave mouth while the monsoon hammered the gorge and the crew fractured and mended and fractured again. Nine days of rationed rice and rationed hope. And now the ice itself was telling her the window was open.

She could feel it the way she felt weather in the old scar on her knee: the pressure dropping, the floodwater in the throat of the gorge beginning to fall back, the river gathering itself for whatever shape it meant to take next. Two days. Maybe less. The calving would tear the ice dam at the head of the upper rapids loose, and for a brief, murderous interval the route downstream would run — high, fast, choked with brash ice, but *runnable*. After that, the channels would rearrange themselves into something no map had ever described, and they would all still be standing in a hole in a mountain next to a fortune someone was coming to kill them for.

Because someone was coming. She'd heard the engines the night before, far off, throttled low, the way men ran boats when they didn't want to be heard. Mateo's superiors hadn't come to negotiate. They'd come to seal the cave — with the ledgers inside it, and very likely everyone who'd touched them.

Astrid turned back into the chamber. The others were awake now, drawn out by the thunder of the ice: old Ferro with his bad shoulder, Junie with the shotgun broken over her arm, young Calloway pressed against the wall with his eyes too wide. Nine days of close quarters had worn them down to their truest shapes, and Astrid had learned which of them she could lean on and which she simply had to carry.

"Gather round," she said. "We're leaving."

* * *

They argued for an hour, because of course they did. Nine days of floodwater buys you nothing if not practice at arguing.

"We should burn them here," Junie said, for the fourth time. She said it the way other people prayed. "Right now. Before they come. Let the syndicate dig through ash and melted wax and find nothing but their own stink."

"And every family those books bind stays bound," Astrid said. "Every stolen inheritance stays stolen. You want your revenge; I understand that better than you know. But these pages are the only proof any of those families will ever hold. We don't burn proof. We *deliver* it."

"To whom?" Ferro asked, not unkindly. He was the oldest of them, a former syndicate clerk himself, gone soft in the middle years and hard again in the last ones. "The magistrates in Corvane are bought. The newspapers are bought. You've seen what happens to people who talk."

"I've seen what happens to people who stop," Astrid said. "That's different."

Wren said nothing through most of it. They sat apart on an overturned crate, arms around their knees, watching the lamplight move on the oilcloth. It was Wren who finally spoke, in the pause when the argument had burned down to embers.

"There's a landing," they said. "Eight miles below the confluence. Miller's Landing — it was a railhead once, before the syndicate bought the line and pulled the rails. There'll be a telegraph office. Even a dead line can be made to live if you know which wires to splice — and I know." They looked up, and in the lamplight their face was the face of someone who had decided something expensive. "Forty-one ledgers is too many to hide and too many to fight over. But copies aren't. If we get word out — to the families, to what's left of the honest press, to anyone — then burning becomes unnecessary and selling becomes suicide. Sunlight, Captain. It's the only cargo that appreciates."

Astrid looked at them for a long moment. Nine days ago she'd have called that naive. But she'd also watched Wren splice a split hull seam in freezing bilgewater with hands that never shook, and she'd stopped underestimating them somewhere around the third hour of the monsoon.

"Miller's Landing," she said. "Then we run the Throat."

Calloway made a small sound, like a man swallowing his own objection.

"Yes," Astrid said, turning to face him, facing all of them. "You heard me. Downstream. Through the narrows, past the Widow Stair, through the Squeeze, with the river running two fathoms above seasonal and full of calved ice. In a boat with a patched hull." She let the silence sit until it was unbearable, because they deserved to feel the whole weight of what she was asking. "Here's what I know about the Throat that the syndicate doesn't. Their pilots run it twice a season, in good water, with charts. My family ran it blind, in flood, for sixty years, because the river rights they stole from us included the right of passage, and my father used to say the Vasquezes read water the way priests read scripture. I have that reading in my blood whether I want it or not. There is no one alive who knows this gorge better than I do, and half of what I know isn't in any chart."

"And the other half?" Ferro asked.

"The other half is going to change in the next two days, and nobody knows it, including me. That's the gamble." She held up one finger. "But here's the arithmetic. The syndicate has to come upriver to seal this cave, which means running the narrows from below, against the current, in boats heavy with men and explosives. We go down with the current, light, and desperate. Desperate is faster." A second finger. "And the calving will fill the upper reaches with brash ice — which cuts both ways, but it cuts worse for them, because they'll be picking their way through it slow while we're riding it fast. Ice is just another kind of sandbar. I've spent my whole life reading sandbars."

Junie spat, which among river people was a form of assent. "And if the hull lets go in the Squeeze?"

"Then we drown quickly, and the ledgers sink, and the syndicate spends ten years dredging for paper." Astrid allowed herself the ghost of a smile. "There are worse legacies."

* * *

Loading took the rest of the morning, and it was holy work, though none of them would have called it that.

They ferried the ledger bundles down to the *Mariposa* where she lay moored in the second chamber, riding at the end of a hawser they'd rigged to a granite horn the day the flood sealed them in. Forty-one volumes, wrapped in oilcloth and tarps and, in the end, the last of their spare canvas, lashed down in the forward hold beneath the windlass where the hull was strongest. Astrid handled each bundle herself. She told herself it was about weight distribution, and it was, but it was also something else — the same thing that had moved through her the night she'd opened Thorn's letter and found not a daughter's name but a bearing, a set of coordinates, a confession pointing into the heart of the gorge like a compass needle finding north.

*Where the ice keeps time*, Thorn had raved, and he'd been literal, as dying men usually are. The syndicate had hidden its whole rotten architecture inside a glacier, believing cold and distance were the same as safety. They had never understood rivers. Rivers keep everything. Rivers remember everything. Every crime committed on the water eventually floats downstream.

Her own family's volume was in there — the Vasquez claim, the river rights, the tolls and passages and moorage fees that had been hers by birth and had been signed away in a room she'd never seen by men whose names she'd now read in Thorn's careful clerk's hand. She'd held it a long time the night they'd found it, feeling the weight of a decade of flight — every renamed boat, every false port, every night she'd slept with her boots on — and she'd understood at last why she'd never been able to stop moving. Stillness had always felt like surrender because, for her family, stillness *had* been surrender. The day they stopped fighting for the river was the day the river stopped being theirs.

Now the proof of it rode in her hold, and the question of what it was worth rode with it, and she had not yet answered that question, and she knew — with the decisive clarity that had been settling over her for days, cold and clean as meltwater — that she would have to answer it soon.

"You're doing the thing," Wren said beside her.

"What thing?"

"Where you look at the middle distance like it owes you money." Wren cinched a lashing and tested it with both hands. "I've been on boats with you long enough to know that look means you've already decided something and haven't told the rest of us yet."

Astrid checked the forward bilge pump, then the aft. The patch Wren had sewn into the hull at Mile Forty was holding — holding beautifully, actually, which frightened her more than a leak would have, because things that held beautifully were things you trusted, and trust was how people died in gorges. "When we clear the Squeeze," she said, "there's a bend below the Widow Stair where the syndicate will expect us to run for the lower landing. Everyone runs for the lower landing. It's the only deep-water egress."

"But?"

"But the lower landing is where they'll be waiting, and it's where they'll corner us, and anything we're carrying becomes a bargaining chip the moment they put guns on us. So we're not going to the lower landing." She traced the line of it in the air with one finger, the river's course written in muscle memory. "Below the Stair there's a side channel — the Drowned Orchard, my father called it. Flooded timber, braided shallows, no draft deeper than four feet. No patrol boat can follow us in. We ground out, go overland to Miller's Landing with what we can carry, and send the word out from there."

"That's insane," Wren said. "The Drowned Orchard eats boats."

"My father ran it eleven times."

"Your father also died broke on a sandbar."

"He died *free* on a sandbar," Astrid said, "which is more than the syndicate's pilots can promise." She saw Wren's expression and softened, just slightly. "I'm not romantic about it. I know what it costs. But I've spent ten years learning the difference between a risk and a trap, and the lower landing is a trap. The Orchard is just a risk. I'll take risks. Traps are for people who've already decided to lose."

Wren was quiet a moment. Then: "My name's in one of those books."

"I know."

"Not the syndicate's thefts. The other list — the one in the back of the Kestrel volume. Families who took syndicate coin to inform on their neighbors. My grandfather's name is there, and the amount, and the year." Wren's voice was level, but their hands, working the lashing, were not. "When this is over, when the sunlight gets in, that comes out too. I need you to know I'm not asking you to protect me from it."

"I know," Astrid said again. She put her hand briefly on their shoulder — the first time she'd touched them unprompted since the bilge, since the wet hours of shared labor when touch was just another tool. "On my boat, we deliver what we're given. All of it."

* * *

In the afternoon, the glacier gave them its answer to the plan.

It began as a tremor in the tea Junie had boiling on the spirit stove, rings spreading across the surface in delicate concentric circles, and then the sound arrived — not the sharp report of calving but a long, subterranean grinding, the sound of a mass the size of a cathedral deciding to move. Everyone froze. Then Ferro, who had spent thirty years as a syndicate clerk and knew the sound of large institutions shifting, said quietly: "The ice dam."

They crowded to the cave mouth. Below the glacier's face, where the upper lake had been backed up behind a plug of ancient ice for as long as any chart recorded, the water was moving. Not rising — *moving*, sliding, the whole brown surface tilting and accelerating as the dam began, grain by grain, to fail. Chunks of brash the size of tables spun free and swept downstream into the throat of the gorge, and beyond them, faintly, they could hear the upper rapids changing their voice — deepening, quickening, taking on the roar of water that has been given permission.

"How long?" Calloway whispered.

Astrid listened the way her father had taught her, eyes closed, reading the river's grammar through the stone. "The dam goes tonight or tomorrow morning. The flood surge will raise the narrows for a few hours after — suicidal water. Then it drops to the new channel, and we have maybe thirty-six hours before the calving chokes the upper gorge and the syndicate seals everything south of the confluence." She opened her eyes. "So we leave at first light. Not before — I won't run the narrows on a flood crest. Not after — I won't give them time to get ahead of us."

"And if the syndicate moves tonight?" Junie asked.

"Then we hear engines, and we leave in the dark, and God forgives us our haste." Astrid looked down at the *Mariposa*, riding her hawser in the swell, patched hull gleaming wet, hold full of other people's stolen lives. Ten years ago she'd have said the boat couldn't do it. Five years ago she'd have said *she* couldn't do it. But the clarity that had been building in her all week had finished its work now, and what remained was simple and cold and certain: the window was open, the river was calling in its debts, and she had spent a decade paying interest on a theft she hadn't committed.

Tonight, for the last time, she would sleep still. Tomorrow, she would move — and she would not stop until every page in her hold was sunlight.

That evening, as the light failed and the glacier thundered its slow applause, she stood alone at the cave mouth and felt the first true breath of the falling barometer on her face. Far downstream, faint beneath the roar of the failing dam, she thought she heard engines.

Or thought she wanted to.

Either way, she went below, woke Wren with a hand on their shoulder, and said the words she'd been carrying all day like ballast:

"They're coming. Wake the others. We run at first light — and Wren? Whatever happens in that gorge tomorrow, whatever the river asks of us—" she paused, and the lamplight caught the set of her jaw, "—the ledgers reach daylight. That's the whole voyage. Everything else is just water."
