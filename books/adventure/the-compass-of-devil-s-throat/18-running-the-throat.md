## 18. Running the Throat

The glacier spoke first.

It was not a sound so much as a pressure — a deep tectonic groan rolling down the gorge like a struck bell, and every soul on the *Kestrel* went still. Ice dust drifted off the cliff walls in pale veils. Somewhere above us, a serac the size of a chapel leaned out over the channel and decided, in its own slow time, whether to fall.

"Two days," Wren said beside me at the wheel, voice low. "Maybe less."

"Then we don't waste an hour of it." I checked the bilge pump gauge for the fourth time in ten minutes. The patch at Mile Forty was holding — barely — but the *Kestrel* was an old woman with a cracked rib, and I was about to ask her to run a race she had no business running. "Get the ledgers lashed down under canvas. If we ship water, they drown before we do."

Wren hesitated. "Astrid."

"I know what you're going to say."

"You're bleeding through that bandage."

I looked down at my left side, where Mateo's man's knife had opened me two nights ago at the confluence camp. The bandage was rust-streaked, and beneath it the pain had gone from sharp to deep, the kind of ache that whispers rather than shouts. I'd been ignoring it the way I ignored most things: by moving.

"It's holding," I said.

"That's what you said about the hull."

* * *

We cast off from the cave mouth as the sun cleared the eastern rim of the gorge, and the light came down into Devil's Throat like something poured — gold flooding the black water, catching the mist off the rapids ahead. The ledgers lay under oilcloth amidships, forty pounds of stolen inheritances, the names of families who had lost rivers and orchards and houses to men who never got their boots muddy. My family's name among them. I had held that page in the ice-cave lamplight and read my father's signature on a claim he'd never willingly signed, and I had understood then that there was no version of this journey where I turned back.

The first mile was fast water and clean lines. The *Kestrel* answered her helm the way she always did — grudgingly, then generously, like a mule deciding to be a horse. I read the channel the way my father taught me before he was anything but a riverman: watch the V's, trust the boils, never fight the current where you can borrow it. The gorge narrowed. The walls rose. The sound of the river became a single enormous syllable that swallowed everything else.

Behind us, half a mile back and gaining, came the patrol boat.

She was called the *Iron Meridian*, and she was everything the *Kestrel* wasn't — new steel, twin screws, a bow built to shove other boats out of her way. Her searchlight swept the canyon wall even in daylight, a habit of power, the gesture of people accustomed to being obeyed before they asked. Through my glass I could see figures at her rail. One of them raised a loudhailer, but the gorge ate the words whole.

"They'll have guns," Wren said, coming forward with wet hands and a face like flint.

"They've had guns since Kettle Landing." I handed over the glass. "What they haven't had is a pilot who knows this water."

"And you know it?"

"No." I grinned despite myself, despite the pain stitching my side. "But I know how to learn it faster than they do."

* * *

Mile six was called the Needle, and the Needle did not forgive.

The channel pinched to less than twice our beam between two fangs of granite, and the current through it ran like a spilled bucket — eight knots, maybe ten, the surface braided white and pocked with holes that would swallow a skiff. The guidebooks didn't cover the Needle because no one who wrote guidebooks had ever come through it. The syndicate's charts showed it as impassable. That was the whole reason I'd chosen it.

"Hard to starboard on my mark," I said. "Then hold. Don't correct. Whatever she does in there, don't correct."

Wren took position at the rail, ready to fend off. Old Tomas crouched by the engine hatch, one hand on the throttle linkage, praying in a language his grandmother had brought upriver fifty years ago. The ledgers were lashed. The pump was running. The ice groaned overhead like a door opening in another world.

"Now."

I threw the wheel over and the *Kestrel* leaned into the turn, and the Needle took us.

It lasted ninety seconds and it lasted nine years. The current grabbed our stern and swung it like a gate; the port fang slid past close enough that I could see the quartz veins in it, close enough to touch if I'd been fool enough to reach. Water came aboard in green tons, found the canvas over the ledgers, found the patched plank at Mile Forty and tested it like a dentist probing a bad tooth. Tomas gave the engine everything and the old girl screamed, and then — the way the river always decides things, all at once and without appeal — we were through, shot out the far side into calmer water, wallowing, shipping, alive.

Behind us, the *Iron Meridian* slowed at the mouth of the pinch.

Through the glass I watched them argue at her rail. A steel hull that wide couldn't take the Needle without risking her plating, and the men who ran the syndicate hadn't risen by risking their own property. They'd rise by risking everyone else's.

"She'll go around," Wren said. "The north chute. It adds four miles but it's wide."

"Four miles is twenty minutes." I wiped spray off the glass. "Twenty minutes is everything."

That was when the ice let go.

* * *

It began as a crack — a rifle-shot report echoing off both walls at once, impossible to place, everywhere. Then a second. Then a long tearing roar, and I looked up and saw the serac I'd been watching since dawn lean further out over the channel, shedding blocks the size of wagons, and the whole face of the glacier above the north chute begin to move.

"Not the north chute," I breathed. "Not anymore."

The calving came down in stages, each louder than the last, and the wave it sent into the river arrived thirty seconds later — not a wave really, a moving hill of water, and I put the *Kestrel*'s bow into it the only way there was: straight on, engine full, riding it up and over the way you ride any disaster, by refusing to look away from it. We surfed the calving surge for a quarter mile, Tomas shouting prayers and engine noise in equal measure, and when the water finally flattened behind us I looked back and saw the north chute gone — bridged with ice, choked with bergs, sealed shut like a door slammed by God.

The *Iron Meridian* was trapped above it.

For one long moment I allowed myself the luxury of thinking we'd won. Then the patrol boat backed water, turned, and started down the south fork — the long way, the hard way, but a way, and her engines were newer than ours and her hull wasn't bleeding.

"South fork," Wren said quietly. "They'll catch us at the Confluence Narrows."

"Then we'd better not be alone when we get there."

* * *

My side tore open at Mile Eleven.

It wasn't dramatic. That was the insult of it — no blow, no blade, just the wheel kicking hard in a cross-current and my body remembering the knife all at once. The bandage soaked through in seconds. The pain went white and total, and the gorge tilted, and I heard myself say, very calmly, from a great distance: "Wren. Take the wheel."

They were there before I finished saying it — hands replacing mine on the spokes, shoulder bracing my hip, taking the boat's weight and mine together. I slid down against the pilothouse coaming and pressed both palms over the wound and watched, through the red haze, Wren take the *Kestrel* through water I'd been afraid of since I first heard its name.

And Wren was good.

Not lucky-good, not desperate-good. Good the way salvage crews are good — reading water as structure, as load-bearing, as something you dismantle and rebuild rather than something you survive. Every correction was small and early. Every line taken was the line a wrecker would take, hugging the danger on one side to buy margin on the other. I realized, watching, that I had known since Mile Forty what Wren used to be, and that it had stopped mattering somewhere between the bilge and the ice cave, and that knowing it now, watching those hands work my wheel, mattered even less.

"Tomas!" Wren shouted. "Keep her at full! Trust the old girl!"

The old girl trusted back. She always had. Ten years I'd run this river under a name that wasn't mine, on a boat whose nameplate I'd painted over myself, telling myself that stillness was death and questions were passengers and the past was cargo best left sunk. And here was a stranger — no, not a stranger, not anymore, whatever they'd been sent here to be — steering my family's river with more care than I'd ever let myself feel for it.

"Astrid." Wren's voice, tight. "Stay awake. Talk to me."

"The V at the next bend lies left," I managed. "Real channel's right. There's a drowned snag — " pain folded me double — "there's a snag marks it. Half-submerged. Like a hand."

"I see the hand."

"Good. Shake its hand and keep going."

* * *

The Confluence Narrows opened before us at noon, and the *Iron Meridian* closed from behind, and between them, exactly where I'd prayed she wouldn't be and half believed she would be, sat a third boat.

She was small, battered, flying no colors — a converted fish-tender with a rusted crane arm and a hull I knew better than my own scars, because I'd grown up sleeping in her engine room. The *Mariposa*. Our mother's name for her. Mateo stood at her wheel, and even across half a mile of white water I could see that he was not wearing the syndicate's grey coat anymore. He wore work clothes. He looked, for the first time in fifteen years, like our father's son.

"He can't block that channel," Wren said. "Not with that boat. They'll cut him in half."

"Yes," I said. "He knows."

The *Iron Meridian* came around the last bend doing twelve knots, searchlight sweeping, loudhailer cracking warnings that the gorge shredded to confetti. And Mateo brought the *Mariposa* out of the eddy and put her squarely across the narrows — broadside, slow, deliberate, a man laying himself down on the tracks — and dropped anchor.

The collision, when it came, was almost gentle by the standards of the day. Steel met old wood at an angle, not head-on; Mateo had chosen his line with a pilot's precision even in surrender, giving his hull to their bow the way you give a shoulder in a doorway. The *Mariposa* rolled, shipped water, groaned like a living thing. The *Iron Meridian* shuddered, slewed, and grounded her port screw on the bar Mateo had spent three days learning, and suddenly the syndicate's finest vessel was spinning in circles in the middle of the gorge, screaming at a sinking fish-tender while we slipped past in the channel beyond.

I made the wheel hard over and took us through the gap between the *Mariposa*'s stern and the cliff wall — six feet, maybe eight — and as we passed, Mateo looked up at me across the closing water.

There was blood on his face. He was smiling anyway. He raised one hand — not waving, not saluting, just open, palm out, the way you show a stranger you carry nothing — and he shouted something the river took from me, and I will spend the rest of my life wondering which word it was.

"Don't stop," Wren said, and their hand closed over mine on the wheel, warm and steady. "Whatever you're going to feel, feel it downstream."

So I felt it downstream. I felt it through the Lower Gates, where the gorge finally exhaled and the walls fell back and the sky opened like a wound healing. I felt it while Tomas bound my side with sailcloth and the last of the whiskey. I felt it looking back once — once — at the *Mariposa* settling by the bow in the narrows, at the grey figures of the syndicate crew scrambling onto the bar, at the smoke beginning to rise from the *Iron Meridian*'s deck where something had caught.

The ledgers rode dry under their canvas. Forty pounds of paper. Dozens of families. Everything the river owed and everything it had stolen, riding in my leaking boat toward the lower landing, where the syndicate's shore agents would already be waiting, where the choice I had been carrying since the ice cave would finally be laid out in front of me like cards on a table.

Wren glanced at me, and then at the canvas, and I saw the same thought move behind their eyes that moved behind mine.

"Astrid," they said. "When we get there — what are you going to do?"

Ahead, the lower landing came into view through the rain, and standing on it, rank upon rank, were men with lanterns, and the river ran dark and fast between us and them, and I put my hand on the wheel and told the truth for the first time in ten years:

"I don't know yet. But nobody else is going to decide it for me."
