## 20. Aftermath of Ash

The smoke smelled like every funeral Wren had ever attended, all rolled into one gray shroud over the lower landing.

They stood at the rail of the *Kestrel's Wake* — Astrid's boat, though Wren was only now learning to think of it that way instead of *the vessel* or *the target* — and watched the last charred pages lift off the blackened iron basin and scatter across the water. The ledgers had burned for the better part of an hour. Names, claims, deeds, inheritances — decades of theft rendered down to ash and cinder, each flake a family set loose into uncertainty. Wren had watched their own surname curl and vanish in the heat, and had felt nothing like grief. It felt more like setting down a weight they'd carried so long they'd forgotten it wasn't part of their body.

The syndicate boat sat low in the water downstream, listing slightly to port where Mateo Vasquez had rammed it with his own vessel in the throat of the gorge. Its crew worked in silence now, sullen as beaten dogs, running lines and patching planking under the flat gray light of a monsoon that was finally, grudgingly, loosening its grip. Rain still fell, but softly, apologetically, the way a storm behaves when it knows it has lost its argument.

"They're leaving," Wren said.

Astrid didn't answer right away. She stood with her arms crossed, her left one bound tight from shoulder to elbow where the patrol boat's grapnel had torn through her jacket and the flesh beneath. The wound had been cleaned and stitched by Marta, the deckhand who'd argued for selling the ledgers and then, when the burning came, had wept openly and helped feed the fire anyway. People were like that, Wren was learning. Contradiction wasn't a flaw in a person. Sometimes it was the whole architecture.

"Syndicates don't leave," Astrid said at last. "They retreat. There's a difference. Retreat means they'll be back when they've counted their losses and found someone new to blame."

"Then let them come back to ash." Wren nodded toward the basin. "There's nothing here for them anymore. No ledger, no leverage, no proof of what they own. You can't enforce a claim you can't prove."

"You can if you have guns." But there was no real bitterness in it. Astrid's eyes stayed on the retreating vessel, tracking it the way she tracked everything — sandbars, weather, debts. "And they know something else now too. They know the river saw them. Half the landings between here and Kettle Landing watched us run the Throat with their stolen paper burning on our deck. Scandal travels faster than gunboats."

Wren turned that over. "You planned that."

"I planned to survive." A ghost of something moved at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile, but related to one, the way a distant cousin is related to you. "The rest was the river's idea."

* * *

Mateo Vasquez came aboard two days later, when the floodwater had dropped enough to expose the landing's lower steps and the syndicate's main vessel had limped away upriver toward whatever reckoning waited for it in the boardrooms of the coast.

He came alone. That was the first thing Wren noticed. No escort, no enforcers, no show of authority. Just a broad-shouldered man in a soaked canvas coat, moving carefully, favoring his ribs where his own ship's collision had broken something, standing at the foot of the gangplank like a man waiting to be told whether he was allowed to exist.

Astrid stood at the top of the plank. Wren hung back near the wheelhouse, close enough to hear, far enough not to crowd whatever this was going to be. Marta hovered in the galley doorway with a knife she was pretending to clean.

"Mateo." Astrid's voice gave away nothing. Ten years of practiced nothing.

"Astrid." He looked up at her, and Wren saw it then — what power had done to him, how it had hollowed him out and left the shape of a man standing where the man used to be. His eyes were the same brown as hers. That was the cruelest part. "I'm not here to ask for anything."

"Good. Because I don't have anything to give."

"The ledgers—"

"Burned. You were there. You blocked the patrol boat with your own hull so we could get clear. You watched me burn them." She paused. "Why did you do that?"

Mateo's jaw worked. Rain dripped off the brim of his hat onto the wet boards. "Because I spent eleven years thinking I'd traded our family's ruin for a place inside the machine that ruined us. And then I watched my superiors look at me the way they looked at the families in those books. Like inventory." His voice cracked, just once, and he mastered it. "I thought if I got close enough to power, it would count me as a person. It never does. It counts you as a tool until the tool breaks, and then it counts the cost of replacing you."

"So you broke yourself instead."

"I blocked a gunboat with a salvage barge, yes. My math was bad." Almost, almost a laugh. "I'm not asking you to forgive me. I'm not even asking you to believe me. I just—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "Papá used to say the river forgives everything except stopping. I stopped, Astrid. For eleven years. And everything I touched while I was stopped went rotten."

Astrid came down two steps of the gangplank. Not all the way. Two steps — close enough to speak without shouting, far enough that no one could call it an embrace.

"You sold our name," she said. "You sold the river rights Papá bled for, and then you enforced the same theft on forty other families so you could keep your cabin warm."

"Yes."

"And I burned the ledger that could have given us all of it back. Our rights. Our name. Everything." She held his gaze. "So understand me, Mateo: I didn't do it for you. I did it because freedom that costs other people their chains isn't freedom. It's just a bigger cell."

"I know."

"Do you?" Her voice didn't rise. That was the frightening thing about it. "Then start walking. Upstream, downstream, I don't care. Start moving again. And maybe in ten years, if the river's kind, we'll pass each other on the water and nod. That's more than you've earned. It's also more than I can afford to give."

Mateo looked at her for a long moment. Then he took off his hat — a small thing, an old courtesy from a childhood neither of them had mentioned — and pressed it briefly to his chest.

"Astrid Vasquez," he said, saying her full name aloud like a man reading his own sentence. "Thank you for the nod you haven't given yet."

She gave it then. A single inclination of her head, cold and precise as a compass bearing. Nothing in it but acknowledgment: *you live; we are not nothing to each other; we are also not anything yet.*

Mateo put his hat back on, turned, and walked up the landing road without looking back. Wren watched him go until the rain folded him into the gray.

"That's it?" Marta muttered from the galley door. "Eleven years of betrayal and she gives him a *nod*?"

"It's more than I expected," Wren said.

"It's exactly enough," Astrid said, coming back up the plank. Her hand brushed the rail where the burn scars from the basin had marked the wood. "Everything else would have been either too much or a lie."

* * *

The scandal arrived before the syndicate finished retreating, which was, Wren reflected, exactly how scandals work.

It came upriver by packet boat first — a newspaper three weeks old, already soft at the edges from being passed hand to hand along the landings. Someone in the syndicate's coastal office had talked. Or someone had been paid to talk. Either way, the story ran across four columns: *INHERITANCE CARTEL EXPOSED — LEDGERS OF STOLEN CLAIMS DESTROYED IN DEVIL'S THROAT — FAMILIES NATIONWIDE COME FORWARD.*

Forty families. Then sixty. By the end of the week, lawyers were arriving at river towns like migratory birds, and magistrates who'd spent years not noticing the syndicate's boats were suddenly, urgently noticing them. The company's local agents resigned in a cascade. One of them, Wren heard, had tried to flee with a strongbox and been relieved of it by his own dockworkers before he cleared the pier.

Wren read the paper twice, sitting on the *Kestrel's Wake*'s roof with rain dripping off the eave onto the pages, and felt the strange vertigo of watching a thing you'd been part of become a thing everyone was talking about. They had been sent to recover a map. Simple contract, simple fee, simple extraction. Somewhere between Kettle Landing and the glacier cave, the simplicity had died, and Wren had killed it themselves, standing in the bilge with wet hands, choosing a stranger over a paycheck.

"You could go back," Astrid said from below, not looking up from the engine she was rebuilding with one working arm and an impressive vocabulary of curses. "The syndicate's scattered. Your old employers will be scrambling to distance themselves from the whole affair. If you move fast, you might even collect on the original contract. Irony's free this month."

"I read Thorn's letter," Wren said. "That was the contract. Everything after was just freight."

Astrid glanced up, grease on her cheekbone, hair tied back with what appeared to be a strip of sailcloth. "That's a stupid way to run a business."

"It's the only way I've got left."

Something passed between them then — brief, unspoken, the kind of current you feel through a hull before you see the water move. Astrid went back to her engine. Wren went back to the roof and the rain. Neither of them mentioned it again, but both of them knew it was there, the way you know a sandbar is there before the channel shifts to reveal it.

* * *

On the fourth day after the burning, Wren found themselves repairing the *Kestrel's Wake*'s nameplate.

It was Marta's suggestion, or maybe an accident — she'd left a tin of white paint and a brush on the foredeck, and Wren, who had never in their life been able to leave a broken thing unrepaired, picked it up without deciding to. The old name had faded to a ghost: letters worn nearly smooth, readable only if you already knew what they said. *Kestrel's Wake.* Beneath it, older still, almost invisible under years of repainting, another name showed through where the sun had stripped the varnish. A different boat's name, from a different life. The *Vasquez Star*, maybe. Wren had heard the stories secondhand, pieced together from Thorn's ravings and Marta's gossip and the things Astrid said at three in the morning when she thought no one was listening.

Wren scraped the plate clean. Sanded it. Primed it. Worked through the afternoon while the monsoon's last clouds shredded themselves against the ridgelines and the first honest sunlight in weeks came slanting gold across the water.

Astrid found them there at dusk, paint-spattered, the nameplate gleaming wet and white.

"You didn't have to do that," she said.

"No." Wren set down the brush. "But I've spent a year doing things I was paid to do. I wanted to try doing something I wasn't."

Astrid studied the plate. Then Wren. Then the plate again, as if comparing the two and finding them similarly repaired.

"There's cargo space," she said, in the tone of someone discussing ballast weights. "If you're staying. Honest freight only. Passengers who ask questions pay triple and get thrown off at the next landing."

"That's a terrible business model."

"It's kept me alive." She paused. "Mostly."

Wren looked out at the river. The flood had dropped. New channels had opened where old ones had closed; sandbars had migrated like slow animals overnight. The whole map of the water had rearranged itself while no one was watching, which was, Wren supposed, what rivers did after floods — what people did after ruin. Nothing stayed where it was. Everything found a new way through.

"I don't know how to be anything but a stranger," Wren said quietly. "It's the only trade I've ever learned. You pay me, I disappear. You stop paying, I was never there."

"I know." Astrid leaned against the rail beside them, careful of her bandaged arm. "I spent ten years perfecting the same trick. Never stop moving. Never take passengers who ask questions. Never stay anywhere long enough to be known." She was quiet a moment. "Here's what I learned in Devil's Throat. Stopping doesn't kill you. What kills you is stopping somewhere you hate, for reasons that aren't yours, with people who'd sell you by the pound." She turned her head, and in the last of the light her eyes were the exact brown of her brother's, and nothing like his at all. "So pick your stopping place carefully. That's all. Pick it like it matters. Because it does."

The river murmured against the hull. Somewhere upstream, ice groaned in the high gorge — the calving season winding down, the mountain giving up its winter one piece at a time. Downstream, the world was rearranging itself around forty families' worth of burned paper, and none of it, none of it, could be put back.

"I'm staying," Wren said. "Not because you need crew. Because I want to find out who I am when nobody's paying me to be anyone."

Astrid nodded — the second nod of the day, but nothing like the first. This one was slow, and warm at the edges, and cost her something visible.

"Then you'd better learn to paint," she said. "This boat needs a full refit before the dry season, and my arm's useless for another month." She pushed off the rail and headed for the wheelhouse, then stopped, half-turned. "Wren."

"Yeah?"

"Thorn's letter named you. In the ledger — your family's claim. I saw it before I burned it." Her voice was gentle in a way Wren hadn't heard from her before, careful as a hand extended across dark water. "Whatever name you decide to be, when you decide — that page is gone now. Nobody owns it. Nobody can take it back or hold it over you. It's yours to write however you want."

Wren looked down at their hands — paint-flecked, scarred, steady for the first time in longer than they could remember.

"I know," they said. "That's why I'm staying. I want to write it somewhere it won't wash off."

Astrid smiled then — fully, finally, the expression arriving like sunrise after the long dark of monsoon — and went inside, and the lamp in the wheelhouse glowed yellow against the blue dusk, and the river ran on.

* * *

They cast off at first light.

Marta took the bow line, coiling it with the satisfaction of a woman whose arguments had all, in the end, lost to her own heart. The engine caught on the second try — Astrid's rebuild holding, coughing once and settling into its old familiar rhythm, a heartbeat the whole boat seemed to organize itself around. The landing slid away behind them, gray wood and gray water, and ahead the channel opened wide and clean, the flood having scoured it into something new.

Wren stood at the rail and watched the banks unspool. Fields waking. Smoke from cookfires. A child on a dock waving with both arms at nothing in particular, or possibly at them.

"You're smiling," Astrid observed from the wheel, not looking over.

"I'm not."

"You are. It's terrible. Do it more."

Wren laughed — actually laughed, a sound that surprised them both — and moved to stand beside her at the wheel, close enough that their shoulders touched, neither of them remarking on it, both of them allowing it.

Ahead, the river bent south into country neither of them had run before. Behind them, ash had settled into the current and gone wherever ash goes, feeding something, maybe, in the silt of some future bank. The scandal was still spreading downstream, the syndicate still bleeding, Mateo still walking somewhere on a road with a hat pressed to his chest. None of it was finished. None of it was theirs to finish.

But the boat was sound, and the day was clear, and for the first time in ten years, Astrid Vasquez was steering toward something instead of away.

"What's the next landing?" Wren asked.

Astrid checked the chart, then folded it closed, unnecessary.

"Don't know yet," she said, and sounded, for the first time since Wren had met her, entirely glad of it.
