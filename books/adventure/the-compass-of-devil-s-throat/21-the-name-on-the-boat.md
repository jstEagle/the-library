## 21. The Name on the Boat

The ash took three days to wash out of the river.

Astrid knew this because she watched it happen from the deck of the *Marta Cielo*, moored at the lower landing where the current ran slow and brown-green and forgiving, carrying the last gray ghosts of the ledgers down toward the delta and the sea. Flecks of it clung to the pilings. It dusted the railings. When she dragged her thumb along the gunwale on the second morning, it came away smudged, and she stood there a long time with her thumb held up to the light like a man reading his own fortune.

Paper burned at four hundred and fifty-one degrees, Wren had told her once, back when they were still strangers pretending not to be curious about each other. She hadn't asked how they knew. She suspected now that everything Wren knew had come from salvage work, and most of the salvage work worth knowing about had come from the syndicate, and all of that was ash too, or would be soon enough, once the inquiries finished picking through the confluence camp like gulls through a gut pile.

She turned her hand over and looked at her palm. Soot in the lifeline. Blister across the base of two fingers where the wheel had fought her through the Throat's last mile. A thin white scar she'd carried since she was nine years old, from the summer her father let her hold the tiller of the family barge while he netted whitefish, and the river had bucked without warning, and she had learned her first lesson about ownership: the river lets you hold it only as long as it pleases.

*Vasquez*, she thought. Even the shape of the name in her head felt like a boat coming off a sandbar — a grinding, then a lift, then open water.

* * *

On the fourth morning, Wren found her sitting on the foredeck with a coffee can full of turpentine and a rag, working at the faded nameplate above the pilothouse window.

They didn't say anything for a while. That was one of the things Astrid had come to treasure about them — the way Wren understood that silence could be a shared labor instead of an absence. They lowered themselves onto the deck beside her, cross-legged, close enough that their shoulder nearly touched hers, and watched her work.

The old lettering had been painted over so many times it had become geology. She could feel the strata under her rag: the white paint she'd applied herself eleven years ago when she'd bought the boat cheap off a widow in Kettle Landing; beneath that, something blue, someone else's optimism; beneath that, the original gold leaf, flaking, stubborn, gorgeous even in ruin. And beneath all of it — she was almost sure, though she hadn't dared scrape deep enough to confirm — the name she'd covered first. Her own.

"You're going to sand your fingerprints off," Wren said finally.

"Good. Then nobody can prove anything."

"That's not how fingerprints work."

"It's how mine work." She passed them the rag. "Hold this."

Wren held it. They were good at being handed things lately — tools, rope ends, burdens. In the week since the burning they had developed a rhythm around each other that Astrid recognized with a small private astonishment: it was the rhythm of crew, which was harder-won than the rhythm of lovers and lasted longer. Wren had slept aboard every night since the landing, in the berth behind the engine room that still smelled faintly of bilgewater and char, and every morning they appeared on deck before she did, kettle already on, as if the boat had simply decided to keep them and informed them of the arrangement.

"Scraped down to bare wood yet?" Wren asked.

"Almost." She blew dust off the plate. "You know what's funny?"

"Tell me."

"I spent ten years making sure no one could read this boat. No name anyone could trace, no registry I couldn't abandon, no port I visited twice. I told myself it was prudence. Good rivercraft." She worked the scraper into a corner groove. "It was just the same theft the syndicate ran, except I was running it on myself. Stole my own name and buried it under paint."

Wren was quiet a moment. "Mateo did the same thing," they said. "Just in the other direction. Painted their name over everything he touched."

The scraper stopped.

Downriver, past the last bend, the syndicate's black hulls were gone. Two days after the burning, the patrol boats had weighed anchor and run for the main channel, and the rumor that came upriver behind them said the home office had disavowed the entire Throat operation — too much heat, too many questions, a ledger vault discovered empty and a regional chief who couldn't explain what had happened to forty years of paper. Mateo had been left standing on the confluence dock holding nothing but his own name, which was suddenly worth exactly what hers had been worth on the day their father signed away the water rights: whatever a stranger was willing to pay for it.

She had seen him once since. He'd come down to the lower landing at dusk, alone, walking with the careful gait of a man whose body had learned new limits in the floodwater, and he had stood at the end of the pier looking at the *Marta Cielo* for a long time without coming closer. She had stood at her rail looking back. Neither of them had waved. He had touched the brim of his hat — the smallest gesture, barely a movement, the kind of thing you'd miss if you blinked — and turned and walked back up the levee road into the dark.

It wasn't forgiveness. She wanted to be honest with herself about that, because dishonesty was the family trade and she was done trading. But it also wasn't nothing. It was a door left unlocked in a house neither of them lived in anymore, and maybe someday one of them would walk through it, and maybe the house would be gone by then, and both of those futures were survivable. She had learned that much from the ice.

"He'll be all right," Wren said, as if reading the drift of her thoughts. "Not good. But all right. Men like him don't drown. They just get redeposited downstream."

"Like silt."

"Like silt."

She went back to scraping. The turpentine smell rose sharp and clean, cutting through the green rot-smell of the post-monsoon river, and somewhere below decks the old diesel engine ticked as it cooled, a sound so familiar it had become a kind of heartbeat she only noticed when it changed.

* * *

By noon the nameplate was bare wood, pale as bone, and Astrid sat back on her heels and looked at it and did not pick up the brush.

That was the moment, later, she would think of as the true hard part — not the burning, not the rapids, not the night the glacier calved and the whole gorge roared like a struck bell. The hard part was this: a blank rectangle of wood, a can of paint mixed that morning at the hardware store in Kettle Landing (the proprietor had raised his eyebrows and said, "Vasquez? Like the old river family?" and she had said, "Exactly like," and paid in cash), and the knowledge that once the letters went on, they could not come back off. Not cleanly. Not without another decade of hiding.

Ten years ago she had stood in almost this exact position with almost this exact can of paint, and her hand had shaken then too, and she had told herself the shaking was cold. It hadn't been cold. It had been grief wearing work gloves, the same costume it always wore.

"You could leave it blank," Wren offered from the rail, where they'd perched with the particular balance of a person who'd spent years on moving decks. "Anonymous boats go where they please."

"Anonymous boats also get boarded whenever anyone feels like it. Turns out a name is papers enough, if you're willing to stand behind it." She dipped the brush. "Besides. I'm done going where I please. I'm going to go where I'm going."

"That's either very wise or very tired."

"Both. Hold the light."

Wren held the lantern against the gathering afternoon shade, and Astrid set the brush to the wood.

*V.* The first stroke went on thick and sure, white over bare grain, and something in her chest unclenched that she hadn't known was clenched — some muscle she'd been flexing since she was nineteen years old and standing in a courthouse hallway listening to a lawyer explain, in the gentlest possible terms, that the Vasquez Water Rights had been transferred, legally, irrevocably, to the Cordillera Salvage and Navigation Company, and that her father's signature at the bottom of the document was genuine, and that there was nothing, nothing, nothing to be done.

Her father had signed. That had been the wound under the wound — not the loss itself but the surrender, the sense that the man who taught her to read water had read his own future and given up. It had taken Elias Thorn dying in her bunk with blood on his lips to tell her the truth: the signature was genuine, yes. The choice was not. There had been debts, threats, a daughter to protect, a syndicate that collected inheritances the way other men collected stamps. Her father hadn't surrendered. He'd been robbed with a pen, and the ledger of that robbery had sat in a glacier for thirty years, and she had burned it rather than trade it, and the burning had freed forty families including her own into a freedom that looked, from certain angles, exactly like loss.

*A.* The second letter. Her hand was steady now.

*S.* Somewhere upriver, in the high country where the Throat narrowed to a stone gullet, the glacier was still calving. She could hear it sometimes at night, when the wind came down the valley right — a long report like artillery, then the groan, then the splash, the ice giving up pieces of itself into water that would carry them to the sea. The river was rearranging itself. The sandbars she'd memorized were dead; new ones were being born; the charts in her cabin were already lies. Every pilot on this stretch of water would have to learn the channels again from scratch, and for the first time in ten years, Astrid Vasquez was not afraid of that. A shifting river was a fair river. It stole from everyone equally.

*Q.* The paint smelled of linseed and mineral spirits and, faintly, of the turpentine still on her hands, and underneath all of it, when the breeze came off the water, the clean mineral breath of glacial melt.

*U.* Wren had not moved. They watched each letter go on with the attention of a witness, which was its own kind of gift. Anyone could watch a woman paint. It took a particular person to understand they were watching a christening.

*E.* The letters of her first name done, she cleaned the brush in the coffee can and started again at the left, lower, where the family name belonged. This was the part she'd been dreading, and it went easier than breathing.

*A.*

*S.*

*Q.*

*U.*

*E.*

*Z.*

She stepped back. Wren stepped back with her, and together they looked up at the nameplate of the *Marta Cielo*, and beneath the smaller, older name, in fresh white letters slightly too large, slightly imperfect, the second line declared itself to the whole brown length of the river:

**VASQUEZ**

"Well," Wren said.

"Well," Astrid said.

Her eyes were wet. She let them be. There was no one on this stretch of the landing she needed to hide from — the syndicate was gone, the debt collectors had scattered with their claims dissolved into ash, and the few fishermen working the shallows below the pier were too far off to see anything but a woman and her crewmate admiring fresh paint, which is what they were, and also what they weren't.

"Marta would've liked it," Wren said. Marta Cielo — the widow in Kettle Landing, eleven years gone now, who had sold her the boat for less than it was worth and made her promise, hand on the rail, to "keep her honest." Astrid had kept the boat. Honesty was newer.

"She'd have liked it better if I'd done it a decade ago."

"She'd have liked it best today. Today's the day it's true."

Astrid looked at them. Sunlight came off the water and broke against Wren's face, and she saw — not for the first time, but with a newness that startled her — that the wariness was gone from their eyes. All those weeks at the wheel, all those nights dividing watches, all the suspicion sharpening into fascination sharpening into trust sharpening into whatever this was, this quiet thing with no name yet, which she supposed was fitting, since names seemed to be the season's business. Wren had told her their family name once, in the cave, by lamplight, voice cracking on it. She had never repeated it back. Some names you carry for people until they're ready to say them themselves.

"Coffee?" she said.

"Thought you'd never ask."

* * *

They drank it on the aft deck, watching the light go long and amber across the water. The river was low and clear now, monsoon spent, running over gravel bars that gleamed like spilled coins. Downstream a log jam had formed against the old ferry piling, and the current curled around it in a long silver question mark.

"The charts are wrong," Wren said. "All of them. Mile Forty's moved fifty yards east. There's a new bar below the confluence you could grow corn on."

"I know."

"You're smiling."

"I'm a pilot with a blank map and a boat with her real name on it. Of course I'm smiling." She turned the tin cup in her hands. "I used to think the river owed me something. My father's rights, our moorage, the life we lost. I spent ten years collecting on a debt the river never acknowledged." She nodded at the water. "But look at it. It doesn't owe anybody. It never did. It just carries what it carries and takes what it takes, and the only inheritance it gives is the knowing how to move on it. That's mine. Nobody signed that away. Nobody could."

Wren was quiet, then said, softly: "Your father taught you that."

"My father taught me that." The words went through her like warm water through cold. "And Thorn gave me back the reason to use it. And you—" she glanced sideways, "—you patched a hole in my hull at Mile Forty with a shirt and a bucket and more nerve than skill, and I decided somewhere in there that you're allowed to stay as long as you want. Consider that your inheritance. It's not much. It comes with a leaking boat and a bad engine."

"It comes with you," Wren said. "It's plenty."

Below them, the river answered — a long, unhurried sound, water over gravel, the oldest voice there was. Astrid closed her eyes and let it say what it said.

* * *

She was rinsing the cups at dusk when she heard footsteps on the pier — light ones, uncertain, the walk of someone young. She came around the pilothouse with the dish towel still in her hand and found a girl standing at the head of the gangplank, sixteen maybe, with a canvas pack and river mud to the knees and a look on her face that Astrid recognized the way you recognize your own handwriting: hunger dressed up as practicality.

"Captain?" the girl said. Her eyes went up — Astrid watched her do it — to the nameplate, to the fresh white letters, and widened slightly. "They said at the landing there was a boat heading upriver. Taking cargo. Maybe passengers." She swallowed. "I need to get to Kettle Landing. And I need to ask you something about Devil's Throat."

Astrid set down the dish towel. Above her, the new paint dried in the evening air, white on old wood, a name over a name, history reclaimed one letter at a time.

"Come aboard," she said. "Ask."
