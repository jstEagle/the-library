## 22. Compass Points

The monsoon ended the way a long argument ends—not with thunder but with a slow, exhausted quiet that neither side quite trusted.

Astrid felt it first in the hull. For weeks the *Kestrel* had groaned under rain like an old man complaining of his joints, and then one morning Astrid woke to silence so complete she thought for a moment she'd gone deaf. She lay in her bunk listening to the absence, cataloguing it: no drumming on the deck above, no water chattering through scuppers, no river swollen to a roar. Just the creak of rope and, far off, a bird calling three notes that sounded almost like laughter.

She climbed out into a sky scrubbed clean and blue as kiln-fired porcelain. The air smelled of wet stone and green things growing too fast, everything lush and half-drowned. The river ran brown-gold with silt, high but no longer angry, carrying whole trees on its back like driftwood pilgrims.

The channels had changed. She could see it from the wheelhouse even before she took the *Kestrel* out—sandbars rearranged overnight like furniture in a house where a giant had slept badly. The deep channel past Miller's Bend had migrated thirty yards east. The old snag field below Kettle Landing was either gone or buried; she couldn't tell yet, and wouldn't until she sounded it herself.

This was the part of the river most captains hated—the after-monsoon reshuffling, when every chart went soft as wet paper and the water kept its own counsel. Astrid had always loved it. There was something honest about a river that refused to be known once and for all. You earned your passage fresh each season or you didn't have it.

Wren found her at the rail with two tin cups of coffee strong enough to strip varnish.

"You're smiling," Wren said. "I want it noted for the record that I witnessed this."

"Don't get used to it."

"Too late." Wren leaned beside her, shoulder almost touching hers, and looked out at the rearranged world. "So which way's the channel now?"

"Ask me tomorrow. Ask me every day. That's the only answer the river gives."

Wren drank their coffee and didn't push. That was another thing the monsoon had taught them both—how to stand next to a person without demanding they be anything other than what they were, right then, in that moment.

* * *

They took on cargo at Kettle Landing three days later, and it was the most boring cargo Astrid had ever loved.

Crates of dried fish. Bolts of canvas. A piano, God help her, crated in pine and destined for the schoolhouse at Upper Ferry, because the people there had passed a hat for two years to buy one secondhand from a hotel that was closing down. The manifest was honest, the payment was honest, and the shipper—a round, sweating merchant named Okafor who counted his crates twice and apologized for both counts—asked not one single question about where she'd been or what she'd done.

"This is it," Astrid said, watching the piano swung aboard on a cargo boom, the whole crew holding their breath until it settled on deck padding without cracking. "This is the life. Fish and pianos."

"You say that like it's a small thing," Wren said. They'd taken to wearing their hair tied back now, sleeves rolled, hands calloused from honest rope work. "It used to be a small thing. Before."

Before. The word sat between them gently, the way all their shared history did now—not avoided, not dwelt upon. The ledgers were ash. The syndicate's black boats had withdrawn upriver in disgrace, their chiefs recalled to face inquiries, their name spoken in dockside taverns with contempt instead of fear. Half the families along the valley had learned within a month that their stolen inheritances were unbound—no paper, no claim, just the land and the water and whatever courts and neighbors made of it. Some were fighting over it already. Some were simply standing on ground that had never legally been theirs and feeling, for the first time, that it might be.

Mateo had sent nothing. No letter, no word. But a week after the burning, a crate had appeared on the *Kestrel*'s deck at dawn, no note, containing a ship's compass in a brass housing, old-fashioned, beautifully made. Astrid had recognized the engraving on the case immediately—it was their father's compass, the one Mateo had taken when he left home at nineteen, the one their mother had given him saying *so you can always find your way back.*

Astrid hadn't decided yet whether to keep it or throw it in the river. So far it sat in the wheelhouse drawer, and every time she opened the drawer for charts, there it was, needle swinging patiently north, asking nothing.

She kept it. She wasn't ready to examine why.

* * *

They ran the new channel past Miller's Bend on a morning so clear the mountains stood out sharp as cut paper. Astrid took her time with it, sounding ahead with the pole, reading the water's surface the way other people read faces—the smooth glassy tongue where the current ran deep, the faint upstream V of a wave train marking the bar's edge, the telltale dirty swirl where silt rose off a submerged point.

"There," she said, pointing. "Channel's hugging the east bank now. See how the water wrinkles off that gravel spit? That's your edge. Give it ten feet."

Wren stood at her elbow with the chart, marking corrections in pencil. "You see all that from here?"

"I see it because I stopped assuming I already knew it. That's the whole trick. Most wrecks happen to captains who trusted last year's river."

Wren wrote that down too, though Astrid suspected they weren't noting it for the chart.

At Mile Forty—the same stretch where the hull had split against a submerged log in the rain, where Wren had knelt hip-deep in bilgewater proving themselves with tar and canvas and stubbornness—Astrid slowed out of respect rather than need. The log was long gone, swept downstream or buried. The patch on her hull held true, a slightly paler plank among the weathered ones, like a scar that had healed cleaner than expected.

"Funny," Wren said. "Doesn't look like much from outside."

"Nothing does. That's why you sound the hull every season." She glanced at them. "People too."

Wren smiled and said nothing, and the *Kestrel* slid on through water that asked nothing of her but attention.

* * *

The young traveler boarded at Upper Ferry, going downriver.

He couldn't have been more than twenty, with a canvas pack too big for him and the particular eager exhaustion of someone who had been walking for days and had just discovered that walking was not, in fact, the only way to move through the world. He paid his fare in small coins counted out carefully, took a place on the cargo deck among the fish crates, and spent the first hour staring at the gorge country rising to the west with an expression Astrid recognized from mirrors decades old.

Devil's Throat was visible from the river even at this distance—a dark seam in the mountains, the upper route still closed by ice, the lower rapids glinting white where the sun caught them. He stared at it the way people stare at fires.

Astrid let him stare. She steered. It was late afternoon, light going gold, the river running smooth as poured honey between willowed banks, and she was in no hurry anywhere, which was itself still a novelty she was learning to enjoy.

Finally he worked up his nerve and came to the wheelhouse door. She saw him hovering there in the glass, working himself up to it, and she felt the old reflex rise—the decade-old rule, *never take passengers who ask questions*—and watched it pass through her like weather and go.

"Captain?" he said. "Is it true about Devil's Throat? That nobody comes back?"

"Some come back." She kept her eyes on the channel, hands easy on the wheel. "Fewer than set out."

"They say there's treasure in it. And ghosts. My uncle swears he knew a man who—"

"Your uncle's man is probably your uncle," Astrid said, and the boy laughed, startled, and some of the awe went out of him and left curiosity behind, which was better company.

"So what's it actually like? In there."

Astrid considered the question. She thought about black water running between walls of granite, about the sound the glacier made at night, like the mountain clearing its throat. She thought about a dying man's blood on her deck and a letter addressed to a daughter who had been a grave. She thought about fire, and ash on the water, and her brother's face across a table, hollowed by the very power he'd sold his family to hold. She thought about Wren kneeling in bilgewater, and about a brass compass in a drawer, needle steady, waiting.

"It's loud," she said finally. "And cold. And it doesn't care what you came for."

The boy nodded solemnly, absorbing this. Then, because he was twenty and couldn't help himself: "Would you go back? If you had the chance?"

Astrid looked at the river ahead—the new channel, the shifted bars, the whole rearranged world that would shift again before summer ended. She looked at Wren on the cargo deck below, laughing at something with the fish-crate man, sleeves rolled, belonging to the boat as naturally as the railings did.

"No," she said, and found it was true. "I don't need to go back. I brought what I needed out."

"What was that?"

"My name." She said it lightly, but the boy must have heard something underneath it, because he went quiet and thanked her and drifted back to his crates, and later she saw him writing in a little journal, glancing up at the Throat, then at her, then at the Throat again.

Near dusk, as the light turned the water to hammered copper, he came back one more time. He stood in the doorway a moment, gathering courage again—he was the kind who always had to gather it twice—and asked the question she supposed everyone eventually would, the one the story would carry downriver ahead of her, growing in the telling:

"Why do you run this river, Captain? All these years. Everyone says you never stop moving. Never stop anywhere long enough to—" He caught himself, blushing. "Sorry. That's a question, isn't it. You don't have to—"

But Astrid was already smiling, small and real, the smile Wren had promised never to get used to and got used to anyway.

"It costs less than stopping used to," she said.

The boy frowned, turning it over, sure there was a riddle in it somewhere. Astrid let him think so. Some answers you only understand when you've paid the price yourself, and she wished him a long life of never needing to.

He went aft. The river carried them on. Behind them the Throat darkened into evening shadow, keeping its own counsel, holding whatever it held—ice, ledger-ash, the bones of old bargains—and Astrid found she could look at it now the way you look at an old scar: knowing exactly how you got it, and no longer flinching.

Ahead, the channel bent east toward open water, toward the wide slow miles where the fish and the piano and the rest of the honest world waited. Wren came up and stood beside her without a word, and she moved over half a step to make room, which was, she was beginning to understand, the whole of what love asked at the start—just room, and the willingness to steer.

The compass in the drawer swung gently north, patient as ever, asking nothing.

Astrid steered west anyway, into the gold light, choosing it.
