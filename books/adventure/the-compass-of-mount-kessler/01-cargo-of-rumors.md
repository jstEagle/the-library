## 1. Cargo of Rumors

The rule was simple, and Rafael Brightwater had built his whole life around it the way a man builds a house around a stove: never stop moving. Land, unload, load, lift off. No lingering over coffee, no second night in any town, no names exchanged that weren't already printed on a manifest somewhere. Movement, he'd found, was the only honest currency left to him. Everything else—trust, history, the truth about what he'd carried in the years before he'd gone straight—tended to devalue the moment you stopped to count it.

So when the Kessler Landing strip came up out of the morning mist looking wrong, his first instinct was not curiosity. It was arithmetic.

The strip itself was fine—two thousand feet of mowed grass along the river terrace, good enough for the Cessna Caravan he flew, bad enough to keep out anyone with sense. What was wrong was the traffic. Three white pickups sat at the edge of the field where usually there'd be one rusted Ford belonging to Marta Quill, who ran the clinic and considered parking a form of self-expression. The pickups were new. They had antennas on them like porcupine quills, and men in orange vests were walking the riverbank below with tripods and long yellow measuring sticks, staking little flags into the mud every fifty yards or so.

Rafael brought the Caravan down anyway, because the insulin in his cargo bay didn't care about his instincts, and because a man who broke his own rules over a few flags wasn't much of a rule-keeper at all.

The tires kissed the grass. He taxied to the fuel drums by Odell's hangar—a lean-to, really, more tarp than architecture—and cut the engine.

Odell Fennick emerged from under a Cessna 180 that hadn't flown since the previous administration, wiping his hands on a rag that had once been red. He was sixty-something, built like a rain barrel, and possessed of the particular calm of a man who had decided decades ago that most news was somebody else's problem.

"You're late," Odell said.

"I'm early," Rafael said. "You're slow."

"Both true." Odell squinted up at him. "You see the gentlemen down by the water?"

"Gentlemen." Rafael looked at the riverbank. "Those are surveyors."

"Those are surveyors *today*," Odell said. "Tuesday they were geologists. Monday they were fisheries consultants. Man asked me yesterday if I knew the subsurface composition of my own hangar floor. I told him gravel and secrets. He wrote it down."

Rafael laughed despite himself. That was the thing about Odell: he made trouble sound like weather. "Whose badges?"

Odell fished the rag into his back pocket and nodded toward the nearest pickup, where a man in a clean shirt was pinning something to a clipboard. "Crest on the door. Some kind of mountain with a pickaxe through it. Kessler Consolidated Mining, or Kessler Resource Development, or whatever they're calling themselves this month. They bought the old company store building last week. Paid cash. Nobody pays cash for a building with a roof like that unless they want something under it."

"Under it's just the cellar. I've seen the cellar."

"Then you've seen more than they have," Odell said mildly, "because they're not interested in the building. They're interested in everything south of it to the gorge. Every flag you see down there is a claim stake. And every claim stake is a question nobody's answered yet."

Rafael felt the familiar itch between his shoulder blades, the one that said *this town is about to become a story, and stories need witnesses, and witnesses get remembered.* He had spent six years being unmemorable. It was a craft, like anything else.

"I'll deliver and go," he said.

"Didn't think you'd stay for the parade." Odell patted the Caravan's cowling affectionately. "Insulin?"

"Cold box, plus penicillin, plus Marta's catalog order, which I did not open, and which better be yarn."

"It's yarn. It's always yarn. Last time it was yarn and a romance novel she made me swear never to mention."

* * *

Marta Quill's clinic occupied the front half of her house, separated from the living quarters by a bead curtain that had lost its argument with gravity years ago. She met Rafael at the door with a clipboard of her own and the expression of a woman who had been rehearsing a complaint since dawn.

"You're late."

"So I'm told."

"The cold chain held?" She was already past him, hauling the insulated box off his shoulder before he could offer. Marta accepted help the way rivers accepted bridges—grudgingly, and only where absolutely necessary.

"Held fine. Compressor cycled twice over the pass, but the thermometer never moved."

"Good." She cracked the lid, counted vials with a fingertip, and nodded once, which from Marta was a standing ovation. Then she looked up at him, and the ovation was revoked. "You should take coffee before you go."

"I don't drink coffee."

"You do today." She set the box down inside the door and folded her arms. "Sit."

Rafael had flown medicine into eleven settlements along this river, and in ten of them, an invitation to sit meant hospitality. In Marta's, it meant interrogation. He sat, because the alternative was standing in a doorway arguing with a woman who delivered babies in canoes when the flood came up, and he'd seen her win arguments with the flood.

She poured two cups from a pot that had clearly been waiting for him, which meant she'd known his schedule, which meant she'd wanted him here before word got around. That was the second thing wrong with the morning.

"Talk to me," she said.

"About what? The surveyors? Odell briefed me. Badges, flags, cash offers."

"That's the part they let people see." She sat across from him, cup untouched. "Three weeks ago, a prospector named Callum Reeve came through here buying batteries and complaining about his knees. You remember him? Big fellow, red beard, talked like a radio with the volume stuck."

"Vaguely. Loud hat."

"Loud hat. He went upriver past the gorge, said he'd found color in a creek nobody's bothered to name. Came back through Tuesday—or was going to. His canoe came down instead. Empty. Wedged in the shallows at Miller Bend, hull stove in on one side."

"Drowned, then. River does that."

"River does that," Marta agreed. "Except Callum swam the gorge rapids every spring for fifteen years, and except the constable—who is also the postmaster, the weighmaster, and the only man in town with a stamp—filed it as accidental without going to look at the bend. And except that Wednesday morning, two of those clean-shirt gentlemen walked into the trading post and asked, very politely, whether Callum had left any papers behind. Maps. Notes. A journal."

Rafael turned his cup slowly on its saucer. Outside, through the window, one of the orange vests planted another flag, precise as a man sewing a button.

"What did the trading post tell them?"

"That Callum kept his papers in his head, which was true as far as anyone knows." Marta finally drank her coffee. "Here's the thing, Rafael. I've been here nineteen years. I've buried four people the river took and one the mine took, back when there was a mine. When the river takes someone, the town talks about it for a month—the eulogies, the theories, the fool who goes out to look at the bend. This time, nobody talks. People cross the street when the surveyors come by. Old Semyon took down his fish racks from the public bank like he was hiding them. The whole landing has gone quiet the way a house goes quiet when there's a stranger in it."

Rafael looked at his coffee. Somewhere in the last few minutes, the itch between his shoulder blades had become a low, steady hum.

"Marta. I fly boxes. Whatever's happening under your quiet is above my altitude."

"Everything's above your altitude. That's why I'm telling you." She leaned forward. "Because you're the only one who leaves. Everyone else here has to live with whatever's coming. You get to see it from up high, where things look like what they are. If you're flying the gorge next week and you see something—a boat that shouldn't be there, a camp, a burn pile—you'll know it's not nothing."

"And if I see something, then what? I report it to whom? The constable-postmaster-weighmaster?"

That got the ghost of a smile out of her. "To me. Radio channel nine. I keep the lists now. Names, dates, who saw what. Somebody has to."

"Why you?"

"Because I'm too old to scare and too stubborn to leave." She stood, collecting his cup, conversation closed. "Go on, then. Odell will want help loading. And Rafael—" she paused at the bead curtain, "—the yarn's actually for a blanket. For Callum's mother, downriver. In case they find him. Someone should be knitting either way."

* * *

He was cinching the last tie-down when the clean shirt arrived.

The man was maybe forty, boots unscuffed, badge—a mountain with a pickaxe through it, just as Odell said—pinned to a vest that had never been near actual work. He had the friendly, unhurried walk of someone whose job was to make you feel that leaving quickly would be rude.

"Pilot," the man said. It wasn't quite a greeting.

"Brightwater." Rafael checked a tie-down that was already checked.

"Whitcomb. Site logistics, Kessler Development Group." He produced a card. Rafael took it, because refusing seemed like the kind of memorable act he avoided professionally. The card was heavy stock. Heavy stock, in a town where the paper was newsprint and birchbark. "We'll be running regular barge traffic up to the gorge come spring. Survey flights, too. You operate this route?"

"Medicine run. Biweekly. In and out."

"In and out," Whitcomb repeated, smiling like the phrase pleased him. "Efficient. Listen—if you ever happen to be upriver, and you happen to see debris, old workings, anything historical, we'd consider it a professional courtesy if you flagged it. There's compensation for useful reports."

"There's compensation for medical deliveries," Rafael said. "I stick to the manifest."

"Of course." Whitcomb's eyes drifted, casual as weather, over the cargo bay—the med crates, the cold box, the tied-down bundles—and back to Rafael's face. "Course you do. Well. Safe skies."

He walked away toward the riverbank, boots still clean, stepping around the mud with a delicacy that told Rafael everything: the man had read the cargo bay the way Marta read a pulse, and he'd filed Rafael under *harmless, possibly useful.*

Rafael finished the tie-downs. Odell appeared at his elbow with a fuel receipt and no comment, which was its own kind of comment.

"He offered you money," Odell said.

"For sightseeing."

"Everybody's getting offers. Semyon got an offer for his bank. The trading post got an offer for their ledger books—said it was for 'records modernization.' Marta says no. I say no. But offers are like rain, boy. Sooner or later something gives."

Rafael climbed into the cockpit and ran his checks. Below, the flags rippled in rows down the riverbank, fifty yards apart, patient as stitches in a wound. He thought about Callum Reeve's loud hat, and a stove-in canoe, and a constable who couldn't be bothered to walk to Miller Bend, and a knitting needle clicking somewhere downriver for a body that might never surface.

Never stop moving. It was a good rule. It had kept him alive through years he didn't talk about, and it would keep him alive through whatever was stitching itself along this river.

He lifted off into the mist, and the Landing shrank beneath him—until, climbing the far side of the valley, he glanced back out of habit and saw the whole pattern at once: the flags, the pickups, the white buildings, all of it arranged along the water like fingers feeling downstream. Feeling toward the gorge.

Toward whatever Callum Reeve had found, and died for finding.

Rafael put his nose west and flew. Two hours later, storm clouds rolled up the river valley behind him, black and boiling, and by nightfall the river he'd left would be three feet higher and full of everything it had ever swallowed.

He didn't know that yet. All he knew was that for the first time in six years, the rule—*never stop moving*—had developed a hairline crack, and something on the other side of it was knocking.
