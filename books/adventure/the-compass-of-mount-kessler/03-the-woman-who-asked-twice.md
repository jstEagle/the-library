## 3. The Woman Who Asked Twice

Elena Vasquez had learned three things in fourteen years of investigative work, and the first was that nobody lies well before ten in the morning. The second was that liars always over-explain. The third—learned the hard way, at the cost of her career, her pension, and her name on a plaque that had been quietly unscrewed from a wall in the capital—was that institutions do not bury mistakes. They bury people who find mistakes.

She stepped off the mail barge at Kessler Landing wearing a relief worker's canvas jacket two sizes too big and a smile she'd practiced in a riverboat mirror until it looked like something a person might actually feel.

"Vasquez," she told the dockhand, shaking his hand with the firm grip of someone who hauled crates for a living. "Regional Relief Coordination. We're doing a needs assessment on the upper river settlements."

The dockhand, a leathery man named Brannic who had seen every kind of official come up this river and trusted none of them, squinted at her clipboard. "Relief," he said, in the tone of a man tasting a word he suspected of being spoiled.

"Medical supplies, mostly. Insulin, antibiotics. Cold chain logistics." She'd done her homework. She'd done so much homework that she could probably pass a licensing exam in cold chain logistics, which struck her as one of life's small ironies: it had taken the destruction of her career to teach her how to be thorough.

Brannic grunted, which from him, she would later learn, constituted a warm welcome.

The town itself was a string of weathered buildings along a muddy crescent of shore, hemmed in by green mountains that seemed less like scenery and more like an audience waiting to see what she'd do. She counted four new faces within her first hour—men with clean boots and surveyor's badges from the Kessler Consolidated Mining Consortium, walking the riverbank with instruments and the particular confidence of people whose paperwork has been pre-approved by someone powerful.

Elena had spent eleven months learning everything there was to know about the Kessler Consolidated Mining Consortium, most of it from files she was no longer authorized to open. The fact that their surveyors were here, staking claims along a watershed officially designated as protected wilderness, told her more than any document ever had.

Protected watersheds didn't need protection from miners unless there was something under them worth protecting from the truth.

* * *

She found Rafael Brightwater where everyone said she would find him, which was nowhere, and then where nobody said she would find him, which was the back room of Odell's repair shop, arguing with a carburetor.

He was taller than his file photograph, leaner, with the sun-browned forearms of a man who worked for a living and the watchful stillness of a man who expected the world to require explaining. He didn't look up when she came in. He finished whatever delicate violence he was committing against the carburetor, wiped his hands on a rag with great ceremony, and then looked up.

"Can I help you?"

"I hope so. Elena Vasquez, Regional Relief Coordination." She offered the practiced smile. "We're assessing medical access along the river corridor. You're the pilot who runs supplies upriver, I understand."

"I fly things places," he said, which was technically true and completely unhelpful, and she liked him immediately, which annoyed her.

"That's what I said."

"Is it?" He tilted his head. "Sounded different."

"The settlement at Two Rivers Bend hasn't received insulin in six weeks. A diabetic boy there nearly died last month. You're the only pilot making that run."

Something flickered across his face—not guilt exactly, but the neighborhood guilt lives in. "Weather's been bad."

"Weather's been fine. I checked the logs."

"You checked my flight logs?" Now there was genuine surprise, and beneath it, briefly, something sharper. Alarm, maybe. Or its cousin.

"Public record," she lied smoothly. Flight logs were not public record. She was enjoying herself more than she should have been. Fourteen years of interrogating men in windowless rooms, and it turned out the skill transferred beautifully to arguing with a bush pilot over a carburetor.

"They're not public record," he said.

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "They're not."

He studied her then, really studied her, the way a man studies a river before crossing it. And Elena watched him arrive at the same conclusion she'd arrived at about him somewhere around the word *logs*: that they were both lying, both knew it, and both were deciding whether it mattered.

"So what are you actually here for?" he asked.

"Insulin distribution statistics."

"And you checked my personal flight logs for insulin distribution statistics."

"People die when pilots don't deliver medicine, Mr. Brightwater. I take that seriously."

"And I'm sure that's all it is." He picked up the rag again, folded it, unfolded it. A man buying time. "Because you've got the look of somebody who does this kind of thing professionally."

"What kind of thing?"

"Asking questions you already know the answers to."

Elena laughed—a real laugh, startled out of her—and saw him register it with visible suspicion, as though laughter might be some kind of trap. Honestly, it might have been. She'd used worse.

* * *

Over the next two days she did what she did best, which was be everywhere and seem harmless. She interviewed the nurse at the landing clinic, who confirmed that Brightwater's deliveries had become irregular since spring. She interviewed Brannic, who confirmed nothing and offered her coffee anyway, which she suspected was the local equivalent of a medal. She interviewed three consortium surveyors, who confirmed that they were measuring water levels for "environmental compliance purposes," a phrase so transparently false that she wrote it down just to have it on record as evidence of contempt.

And everywhere she went, she heard the same story wearing different clothes: a prospector named Halloran, drowned in the spring flood. Found snagged in the shallows below the gorge. Except Halloran had prospected these rivers for thirty years and knew them like his own kitchen, and drowned men don't usually drag themselves upstream against current to die in water barely knee-deep.

Nobody said *murder*. Nobody in a company town says murder out loud. But the word moved through conversations the way cold moves through a wall—present, patient, undeniable.

On the third day, she cornered Rafael Brightwater on the dock at dawn, because dawn was when honest people were weakest and liars were busiest, and Rafael Brightwater struck her as both.

"You found something in the river," she said.

He was loading crates into his plane, and he did not stop loading crates into his plane. "I found a lot of things in the river. It's a river."

"A chest. Sealed. With a crest on it."

His hands paused—one full second, maybe less, but she'd built a career on seconds. Then they resumed. "Folklore," he said. "Fishermen talk."

"Fishermen talk about fish."

"These ones are ambitious."

She walked around to face him, and up close she could see the tiredness under his calm, the specific exhaustion of a man who has been running for years and has begun to suspect the running is the trap. "Mr. Brightwater. I'm going to tell you something, and you can decide what it's worth. Three years ago I was a senior investigator with the Federal Commission on Historical Accountability. I opened a file concerning a place called Vessania."

The name landed. She watched it land—the way his shoulders changed, the way the river suddenly seemed very loud between them.

"There is no such country," he said carefully.

"There isn't now. There was once. It existed for sixty-one years, and then it stopped existing, and everyone who could make that happen got very rich in the process, and everyone who couldn't got buried in a valley behind Mount Kessler." She kept her voice level, almost gentle. "I filed a report. My report was classified. I was reassigned. Then investigated. Then dismissed, for 'unprofessional conduct,' which is what they call it when you find the thing you were hired to find. So when I tell you I know what's in that chest, Mr. Brightwater, understand that I lost everything I had to a cover-up already in progress. I'm not here for the consortium. I'm not here for the agency. I'm here because those people in that valley deserve to have happened to them *out loud*."

For a long moment Rafael Brightwater said nothing. A heron stalked the shallows behind him with the patience of a professional. Somewhere in town, a dog was losing an argument with a squirrel.

"You're not a relief worker," he said finally.

"No."

"You're not even a very good liar."

That stung more than it should have. "I'm an excellent liar. I'm just out of practice lying to people I don't entirely dislike."

"Two days," he said. "You've known me two days."

"Three. I count."

He looked past her, down the river, toward the gorge, toward whatever he was carrying that he shouldn't be. And Elena recognized the expression on his face because she wore it in mirrors: the arithmetic of a person calculating whether one more secret could fit inside a life already full of them.

"If I told you I hadn't found anything," he said slowly, "you wouldn't believe me."

"Not even slightly."

"And if I told you I found something and threw it back, you also wouldn't believe me."

"Correct."

"And if I told you the truth—" He stopped. Shook his head. Almost smiled, and it transformed his whole face into something younger and more reckless. "You'd want to go upriver."

"Yes," Elena said simply. "I would."

"Well." He hoisted the last crate into the plane and turned to face her fully, and she understood that whatever he decided next, he had already decided not to decide it in front of her. "I appreciate your concern for the region's medical access, Ms. Vasquez. If you'll excuse me, I have a delivery run."

"To where?"

"Places," he said, climbing into the cockpit. "It's what I do."

The engine coughed, caught, roared. Elena stood on the dock and watched the plane lift off the river in a spray of brown water and climb away north, toward the gorge, toward the fog, toward the valley she had spent three years being professionally erased for believing in.

She took out her notebook and wrote one line:

*He's flying it somewhere. Which means he hasn't decided yet.*

Then, underneath, after a moment's thought:

*Neither have I. Ask twice.*

Behind her, at the far end of the dock, one of the consortium's clean-booted men lowered a pair of binoculars and reached for a radio.
