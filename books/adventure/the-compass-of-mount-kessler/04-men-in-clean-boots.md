## 4. Men in Clean Boots

Rafael Brightwater had a theory about boots. In twenty years of landing on mud strips from the coast to the headwaters, he'd learned that you could read a man's intentions by his footwear before he ever opened his mouth. Prospectors wore boots that had given up arguing with the river. Trappers wore boots held together with wire and stubbornness. Missionaries wore sandals and called it humility.

But men who arrived in clean boots—white soles still factory-bright, leather creased in neat parallel lines like they'd been ironed—those were men who had never intended to stay anywhere long enough to get dirty. Men whose work was done to other people, at a distance, on paper.

So when the floatplane came chugging upriver at seven in the morning and disgorged four of them onto Kessler Landing's dock, Rafael was already moving before their leader finished stepping ashore.

* * *

The chest sat where it always sat now: strapped into the med-evac crate marked FRAGILE — VACCINES — KEEP COOL, wedged between two blocks of dry ice and a case of penicillin bound for the Toma River clinic. It was, Rafael reflected as he cinched the last strap, the most secure location in the entire interior, because no one in the history of aviation had ever voluntarily opened a cold-chain medical crate. Not customs officers, not bandits, not even the doctors themselves. You did not open a vaccine crate for the same reason you did not look directly at an eclipse. Some things demanded respectful ignorance.

He'd spent two sleepless nights arriving at this solution, lying in his bunk above Odell's workshop while the chest sat under a tarp in the corner radiating guilt like a woodstove. He'd considered burying it. Considered sinking it back in the river. Considered, at three in the morning with particular seriousness, mailing it to himself somewhere far away, care of nobody.

But every option ended the same way: eventually someone would find it, and whoever found it would either be looking for it or would sell it to someone who was. The only safe place for a secret, Rafael had concluded, was inside something so boring that curiosity itself died of exposure. Hence the vaccines.

"You're talking to a box," said Odell from the doorway, coffee mug steaming. "Just so you know. I've been watching through the window."

"I'm checking the straps."

"You're checking the straps *narratively*. There's a difference." Odell came out and squinted up at the sky, then downriver, where the floatplane's engine note was growing from a mosquito whine into something with opinions. "Company plane. That's the third one this week."

"Surveyors?"

"Surveyors don't land twice." Odell took a slow sip. "And surveyors don't wear shoes you could eat off of."

Rafael looked down at the dock. Four men in clean boots were fanning out across the landing with the unhurried confidence of people who owned whatever they were standing on. Their leader was a broad, gray-templed man in a canvas jacket too new to have earned a single scuff, and he carried himself the way Rafael had seen certain men carry themselves in ports from here to the coast—the way of a man who had never once been told no by anyone who mattered.

"That's Merck," Odell said quietly. "Dain Merck. Consortium security chief. He came through two years ago when they bought up the dredge leases. Shook my hand like he was testing it for ripeness."

Rafael's stomach performed a slow, practiced maneuver he thought of as the smuggler's waltz—one turn of fear, one turn of calm, ending in a neutral position. "I've got a delivery run at nine," he said. "Toma River. Insulin."

"Take the long way."

"Odell—"

"Take the *long way*, Rafe." The old mechanic wasn't looking at him. He was looking at the dock, at Merck, and there was something in his face Rafael hadn't seen there before. Not fear exactly. Something older and more tired. "Some men you talk to. Some men you wait out. That one you wait out."

* * *

They waited him out badly.

It began politely enough, which was how Rafael knew it wouldn't end politely. Merck walked the length of the landing introducing himself to everyone—Hollis at the trading post, the Ferreira brothers mending nets, old Marta selling smoked fish—and shook each hand while his men drifted behind him doing nothing in particular, which is to say everything. One examined the fuel drums. One photographed the moored boats. The fourth stood near Odell's workshop with his hands clasped behind his back, admiring it the way a cat admires a birdfeeder.

Rafael kept his hands busy. Busy hands were invisible hands. He re-greased the Otter's wheel struts that didn't need greasing, checked tie-downs that didn't need checking, and listened with the whole surface of his skin.

"—drowned prospector," Hollis was saying, jerking his chin toward the river. "Week back now. Fella name of Corben. Came down from the headwaters acting strange, bought a map off nobody knows who, went back up and never come down again. Boat washed up empty past the narrows."

"Unfortunate," Merck said, in the tone of a man reading a grocery list. "The interior's full of unfortunate men. Was he working for anyone?"

"Not that anybody knew."

"Mm." Merck smiled, and it was a good smile, white and even, the kind of smile that had been fitted rather than grown. "Well. If anything of his turns up—papers, equipment, personal effects—the Consortium would take a respectful interest. We're thorough about our people. Even the ones who stop being ours." He let that sit in the air a moment, then moved on toward the workshop, and Rafael felt the morning tilt beneath him like a deck in weather.

Because Corben had been carrying something. Rafael was sure of it now, sure in the way you're sure about weather coming over a ridge—the chest hadn't drifted loose from nowhere. A drowned prospector upstream, a sealed chest downstream, and a security chief asking after papers. The arithmetic was simple and ugly. Whatever was in that chest, Corben had died keeping it moving, and these men had come to finish the job the river started.

Which meant it wasn't treasure. Treasure got you a quiet offer and a handshake. This—searching a whole landing, photographing boats, sending clean boots into mud country—this was what you did about *evidence*.

Rafael tightened a bolt that was already tight and tried to look like a man thinking about bolts.

* * *

They came to Odell's workshop at noon.

Rafael watched it happen from the Otter's cockpit, thirty yards away, hating every yard of that distance. Merck's men entered without knocking—not because they were rude but because the concept of doors having authority over them had evidently never been introduced. There was some conversation Rafael couldn't hear. Then more conversation, with a different texture. Then Odell came out onto his own stoop with his hands spread, saying something, and one of the clean boots put a hand flat against the old man's chest and pushed, gently, almost courteously, the way you'd move furniture.

What happened next took eleven minutes. Rafael timed it, because timing it was the only thing he could do, and because later—he already knew there would be a later—he wanted to know exactly how long it took four men to destroy forty years of another man's life.

They didn't smash anything at first. They *inspected*. They opened drawers and set things back neatly. They unrolled Odell's drawings of engine mounts and weighed down the corners with washers, considerately. And then, having established that there was nothing to find, they found reasons anyway. A fuel rag left too near a solvent shelf. A heater, faulty, surely. An accident waiting patiently for years for its moment.

The first smoke came out gray and apologetic. By the time it turned black, Odell was being held—not roughly, just *held*, two men on his arms like ushers at a wedding—while the fire found the lacquer thinner and the varnish and the forty-year collection of parts that existed nowhere else on earth, and the workshop that smelled forever of machine oil and pipe tobacco began to roar.

Nobody in Kessler Landing moved to stop it. That was the worst part. Hollis stood in his doorway. The Ferreira brothers stood in their boat. Marta stood with her fish going warm in the sun. Everyone understood, in the wordless way small places understand large money, that this was not a fire but a message, and messages are not interrupted.

Merck walked over to the Otter while his men worked. He stopped at the strut Rafael had been greasing and studied it with interest.

"She's a good ship," Merck said. "De Havilland?"

"Otter. Yes."

"I flew in one of these during the border surveys. Bumpy old girl, but honest." He patted the fuselage twice, companionably. "You're Brightwater. The pilot. You do the medicine runs."

"Some medicine. Some freight."

"Mm." Merck looked out at the burning workshop, and his face did something complicated and brief—not quite regret, but a cousin of it, the face of a man noting a cost he'd budgeted for. "Terrible thing, fires. Dry season. Everything's kindling if you're careless." He turned back, and now the smile was fitted on again. "We're staking the upper watershed next month. Big equipment coming through. A lot of traffic on this river, Brightwater. Traffic makes people curious, and curiosity around heavy machinery is how accidents happen." He extended his hand.

Rafael took it. The grip was firm and dry and perfectly calibrated, and it told him nothing except that Merck had decided, for now, that Rafael Brightwater was a small man worth leaving alive.

"Safe flying," Merck said, and walked back toward his plane, his boots spotless, having never touched the mud of Kessler Landing at all.

* * *

They were gone by two o'clock. The workshop was gone by four, or the part of it that had been walls; the rest smoldered into the evening like a bad memory refusing to leave. Odell sat on a crate across from the ashes with a blanket over his shoulders that someone—Marta, probably—had put there without asking, staring at the wreck with an expression Rafael couldn't read until he got close enough to see it wasn't grief.

It was arithmetic. Odell was doing sums.

"They weren't searching," the old man said, without turning. "You saw. They knew there was nothing to find before they walked in. That was theater." He looked up. "For whom, Rafe? Who's the audience for burning an old man's shop in front of the whole town?"

Rafael crouched beside him. Across the landing, the Otter sat in the gold light, and inside her cabin, strapped between the dry ice and the penicillin, a dead nation waited in a crate marked KEEP COOL.

"For anyone holding anything of theirs," Rafael said. "Corben's papers. Corben's effects. Anything that floats up."

"And do we?" Odell asked it mildly, terribly, the way he asked whether an engine had compression—already knowing the answer, wanting only to hear it said honestly.

Rafael was quiet for a long moment. He thought about lying, and heard himself lie badly in his own head, and remembered Elena Vasquez's eyes yesterday on the dock, seeing straight through him like a window with the curtains open. He was apparently done lying to people he liked.

"Yeah," he said. "We do."

Odell nodded slowly, unsurprised, and fed a stick into the embers of his own life like a man tending a hearth. "Then I guess," he said, "you'd better make damn sure they never find it. And I guess—" he looked up, and there was something fierce in the old ruin of his face, something the fire hadn't touched, "—I guess you'd better not just keep it, boy. Things like that aren't for keeping. They're for *delivering*."

Rafael looked at the Otter. At the crate. At the darkening gorge upstream, where a drowned man's truth lay under wildflowers nobody had counted yet.

At nine tomorrow he had a delivery run to Toma River. Insulin, penicillin, vaccines.

And one passenger more, riding cold and silent, bound for a place no one searches.

He just hadn't yet decided where, exactly, he meant to deliver it—or whether the woman who'd asked twice was going to be on board when he did.
