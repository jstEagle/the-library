## 5. A Bargain at Altitude

Elena Vasquez had planned the conversation for three days. She had rehearsed it in the mirror of the Kessler Landing boarding house, where the glass was cracked down the middle so that her reflection always looked like two people arguing. She had written talking points on a napkin and burned the napkin in the stove because Odell's eyes were everywhere and his memory was better than any filing cabinet she'd ever met.

None of it mattered now, because Rafael Brightwater had taken off with her still buckling her seatbelt, and the moment the wheels left the gravel he said, cheerfully, "Medicine run to San Isidro. Should have mentioned—there's no road back."

"You're kidnapping a federal investigator."

"Ex-federal," he said. "I read the newspapers too."

The De Havilland climbed through morning haze, the river unspooling below them like a silver wire someone had dropped from heaven. Elena gripped the armrest and did the arithmetic. Two hours to San Isidro. Two hours back, if he bothered to bring her back at all. She decided that if she was going to be abducted, she might as well make it productive.

She unbuckled, hauled herself forward against the shudder of the plane, and dropped into the second seat beside him.

Rafael glanced sideways. "That seat's for cargo."

"So am I, apparently."

He almost smiled. She catalogued it—the way the smile started on one side of his mouth like it was testing whether the other side could be trusted—and then she opened the leather folder she'd carried against her ribs for four years, through three cities and one very humiliating tribunal, and laid it on the console between them.

"What's that?" he asked, though his eyes flicked down and stayed there.

"The truth about Vessania," Elena said. "Or as much of it as survived the shredders."

* * *

He didn't touch the folder. That was the first thing she noticed, and the first thing she respected. A guilty man would have grabbed it. An innocent man would have pushed it away. Rafael Brightwater flew with both hands and let the folder sit there between them like a third passenger, radiating its quiet weight.

"Vessania doesn't exist," he said finally.

"No. It exists so little that the government spent eleven million dollars making sure nobody remembered it existed. Border redrawn in '61. Census records sealed. The name struck from every atlas printed after 1963." She turned a page toward him—a black-and-white photograph, a valley, wildflowers. "There was a town upriver behind Mount Kessler. Four hundred people. Miners, mostly. They found gold, and the state found them, and in the spring of '64 a paramilitary unit arrived to negotiate."

"And?"

"And there is no record of what happened next, because the unit filed their report directly to the Ministry of Mines, and the Ministry of Mines burned it. But the payroll records survived. Forty-one men drew combat pay for six days in a valley that officially contained no one." She watched his jaw tighten. "The gold those miners pulled out of the mountain never went to the treasury. It went into private hands. Those hands incorporated. Today we call them the Meridian Consortium, and they hold mining rights to every mile of this river, and they are very, very careful about what washes out of the water."

The engine droned. Below them a heron lifted off a sandbar, unhurried, indifferent to history.

"I ferry medicine," Rafael said.

"You ferry medicine, yes. You also once ferried things that didn't have inoculation schedules, out of Puerto Cielo, under a different name, until your partner took a fall you didn't and you've been flying straight ever since—or flying at all angles but calling it straight." She kept her voice level, almost gentle. "I'm not here to arrest you, Rafael. I couldn't if I wanted to. I was dismissed from the Inspectorate General eighteen months ago for asking exactly the questions I just asked you. My clearance was revoked. My case files were seized. My supervisor told me, with real kindness, that I had a bright future somewhere far away from this river."

"That why you came back as a relief worker?"

"That's why I came back as whatever gets me on planes." She looked at him. "You pulled a chest out of the river, Rafael. I know because Odell talks when he's nervous and drinks when he's not, and you've made him do both. A chest with a seal on it that hasn't been stamped anywhere in sixty years. And three nights ago, men with clean boots burned his workshop over a piece of scrap metal they'd already searched twice. Nobody burns a workshop over treasure. Treasure you steal quietly. Evidence you burn loudly."

Rafael was quiet for a long moment. The mountains slid past the windshield, blue and enormous, patient as old men waiting outside a courtroom.

"If half of what you're saying is true," he said slowly, "then everyone who touches that chest ends up dead or ruined. You're proof of the second one."

"Yes."

"And you want me to fly you upstream anyway."

"I want to offer you something first." She reached into her jacket, past the folder, and produced a single sheet—not official letterhead, nothing so foolish, but a handwritten agreement, signed by a journalist named Tomás Reyes at the capital's last independent paper, and witnessed by a retired judge who still owed Elena her career. "Immunity. Not the government kind—they'd feed you to the consortium by Friday. This kind: full disclosure to the press before anyone can touch you. Your smuggling years, published as context, forgiven as history. Once the story breaks, prosecuting you becomes a distraction the Ministry can't afford. They'll need you quiet and cooperative, which means alive and free."

"You're offering me my own past as a shield."

"I'm offering you the only version of your past that stops being a debt." She leaned closer over the console. "You've been running for nine years, Rafael. I've watched you do it. You refuel facing the wind so you can leave faster. You park your plane nose-out even at a friend's strip. You sleep in the aircraft. I know what that looks like because I did the same thing with hotel rooms for a year and a half. Running isn't freedom. It's just fear with a schedule."

"That's very poetic for an ex-investigator."

"I had a lot of time to write poetry after they fired me."

He laughed—one short, surprised note—and she felt something in the cockpit shift, some pressure equalizing, like a door opening between cabins.

* * *

They crossed the ridge above San Isidro and he began the descent without asking her to move, which she chose to take as a good sign. On final approach he spoke again, eyes on the strip.

"My partner, Mateo. When they caught us, I got out with a full tank and he got twelve years. I told myself it was luck. Then I told myself it was planning. It took me about five years to admit I'd flown out and left him holding the crates." He flared the plane; the wheels kissed gravel soft as a confession. "So I know what evidence costs. I know what it does to the people near it."

Elena waited while he taxied to the fuel drum, while a boy ran out waving a manifest, while Rafael signed for insulin and quinine with the ease of a man performing his own alibi. Only when they were airborne again, climbing back into the light, did she say, "And yet."

"And yet," he agreed.

"Is that a yes?"

"It's a ride upstream." He glanced at her, and this time the smile arrived on both sides of his mouth. "Tomorrow, weather permitting. Upstream and nothing else—I'm not promising heroics, testimony, or a future. I'm promising altitude and a destination. What happens after the wheels stop is negotiable."

"Negotiable," Elena repeated. "Between whom?"

"Between you and me. Over coffee. At Odell's, assuming he rebuilds a table." He banked gently around a thunderhead, and the afternoon sun poured through the canopy and lit her folder on his console, gilding the photograph of the flowering valley. "One condition of mine."

"Name it."

"When we find whatever's behind Mount Kessler—you document it, all of it, every grave and every coin—but the names go in first. Before the gold, before the conspiracy, before anything. Somebody wrote down four hundred people once, and somebody else erased them. If we get one page of that ledger back, the names come home before the money does."

Elena looked at him for a long moment, at this smuggler-turned-pilot who had just, unprompted, stated the entire moral architecture of her ruined career in one sentence.

"Deal," she said.

Below them the river bent north toward the gorge, and beyond the gorge, faint even at ten thousand feet, Mount Kessler stood with its head in the clouds like a witness refusing to testify.

Then Rafael's hand stilled on the yoke. Downriver, at the bend near the landing, a boat was moving against the current—white hull, no registration markings, a mounted fixture on the bow that had no business being on a fishing vessel.

"That's new," Rafael said quietly.

Elena followed his gaze and felt the bargain they'd just struck grow heavier in her hands. "How fast can this thing go?"

"Faster than that boat." He pushed the throttle forward. "But not faster than whoever sent it."
