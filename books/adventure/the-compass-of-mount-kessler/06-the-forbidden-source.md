## 6. The Forbidden Source

Fog on a river gorge is not weather. It's a decision the mountain has made about you, and the decision is usually no.

Rafael flew into it anyway, because the alternative was sitting at Kessler Landing with a chest of evidence strapped in his cargo bay and a woman in his passenger seat who had spent the last two days dismantling every excuse he'd built in eleven years of running. Elena Vasquez didn't argue. She just asked questions twice, in that flat, patient voice, until the lies got tired and lay down.

"River's your road," she'd said at dawn, buckling in. "Fog's your cover. The patrol boats can't see you, and you can't see them, so we're even."

"That's not how I'd put it."

"How would you put it?"

"Suicidal," Rafael said, and pushed the throttle forward.

The Kessler River gorge ran like a green scar through the flank of the mountain, two hundred feet of water wedged between walls of granite and spruce. In clear weather it was a corridor. In fog it was a coffin with good acoustics. Rafael flew it the way his father had taught him to read a river in the old days, before the old days got complicated: watch the water, watch the walls, trust the sound of your own engine over the sound of your own doubt.

Elena sat beside him with the ledgers from the chest open on her knees, protected from the damp by a plastic map sleeve she'd requisitioned from his first-aid kit without asking. She'd been up since four cross-referencing shipment numbers against dates, and every few minutes she made a small sound — *hm* — that Rafael was learning to dread.

"Hm," she said.

"What?"

"March of the year before Vessania 'never existed.' Fourteen shipments of what the consortium's books call *aggregate stone*." She looked up at him. "Aggregate doesn't get armed escorts, Rafael."

"No," he agreed. "Aggregate doesn't."

The gorge bent north and the walls closed in. Rafael dropped lower, skimming above the white water, and the fog swallowed them whole. His hands were steady. His hands were always steady. It was the rest of him that had been shaking since the night he and Odell hauled that chest out of the river and his quiet little life of insulin runs and fuel debts had ended.

"There," Elena said suddenly, pointing left. "Smoke."

He saw it a half-second later — a gray smudge in the gray air, and beneath it, glinting when the fog tore for an instant, the black hull of a patrol boat. Consortium colors. A mounted gun on the foredeck, and a man beside it swinging something long and optical toward the sky.

"They can't hear us over the rapids," Rafael said, mostly to himself.

The first shot proved the rapids wrong.

It wasn't aimed — it was a question, a tracer arcing up through the fog like a rude firework — but the second one was an answer, and it cracked off the rock wall twenty feet to their right and came back at them as a spray of stone.

"Down," Rafael said, and put the plane down with it, hugging the water so close the propeller wash flattened the river into a white road beneath them. Elena didn't scream. She braced one hand on the instrument panel and kept the other flat on the ledgers, and Rafael thought, absurdly, in the middle of everything: *she's protecting the paper. She's like Odell with his tools.*

The gorge forked ahead — the main channel swinging east, and a narrower slot to the north that wasn't on any chart he owned. It was on the chart folded in the bottom of the chest, though. The Vessanian chart. *The source road*, someone had written on it in a careful, dead hand, and drawn a little mountain with a star above it.

He took the slot.

The walls came at them like hands closing. The fog thickened, then — miracle of miracles, or maybe just mountain weather doing what it pleased — thinned to a pale veil as they climbed through the notch, and the gunfire fell behind them, muffled, then gone, then replaced by a silence so complete Rafael could hear his own heartbeat arguing with the engine.

And then the valley opened.

* * *

He'd flown over a lot of country in eleven years. He'd seen rivers the color of hammered copper and forests that went on past the edge of what a man could want. But he had never seen anything like the valley behind Mount Kessler.

It was a bowl of green maybe three miles across, ringed by cliffs, walled off from the world so thoroughly that the storm that had drowned the chest in the river hadn't touched it. Wildflowers covered the valley floor in drifts — blue and white and a deep wine-red that Rafael had no name for — running right up to the foundations of stone houses. Dozens of houses. A church with its steeple fallen but its walls standing. A schoolhouse with a bell still hanging in its frame, green with age. Orchards gone wild, apples small and hard and everywhere.

Nobody had told him there had ever been a town here. Nobody had told him there had ever been a country.

Elena made a sound beside him that Rafael would remember for the rest of his life. It wasn't a word. It was the sound a person makes when grief and wonder arrive at the same door and have to share it.

"Set us down," she said quietly. "By the church."

He found a meadow firm enough to take the wheels and brought the plane down soft. The engine ticked into silence. Through the windscreen the valley lay in the morning light, and the flowers moved in a wind that had traveled over no road to get there.

They walked among the houses. Elena moved slowly, stopping at each threshold, and Rafael found himself doing something he hadn't done in years: he took his hat off.

At the third house there was a child's wooden horse on the porch rail, weathered gray, one ear chewed round by decades of weather. Elena picked it up and held it in both hands like something that might still be warm.

"They were farmers," she said. "Dairy, mostly, from the ledgers. The gold was never mined here — it was *moved* here. Through here. And when the shipments were done, when the last of the state treasury had been laundered through the consortium's accounts..." She set the horse back down, gently, facing the valley. "They didn't want witnesses to the arithmetic."

Rafael said nothing. There was nothing in him equal to it.

They found the graves at the far end of the valley, where the wildflowers grew tallest and sweetest. That was the thing that undid him — that the graves were beautiful. Long, low mounds in rows, hundreds of them, sinking gently into the meadow, and the flowers had taken all of it, every mound and every margin, so that the dead lay under a sea of blue and wine-red and the whole field hummed with bees. A single stone marker stood at the head of the rows. The Vessanian words had weathered away, but the carving at the top remained: a mountain with a star above it.

Elena knelt in the grass with her camera and her notebook and began to work, and Rafael watched her do it, and understood that this — the kneeling, the careful photographs, the names she read aloud from the ledgers to match against the stones — was her version of his flying. The thing you did instead of running. The thing that kept you human.

"Vasquez," he said, after a long while.

"Elena," she said, without looking up. "You've been flying me around for two days. You can manage my first name."

"Elena." He crouched beside her. "Why you? Out of everybody they could have buried this with. Why did they have to ruin *you*?"

Her hands paused over the notebook. For a moment he thought she wouldn't answer, and he was preparing to let it go, because he of all people knew about questions a person wasn't ready for.

"Because I was good," she said finally. "Because I'd closed eleven cases nobody else could close, and when the twelfth one pointed upstream — toward the consortium, toward the gold, toward *this* — they knew I wouldn't stop. So they didn't fire me. Firing me would have been a story. They disgraced me instead. Slowly. A leaked memo here, a falsified expense inquiry there, until the word *Vasquez* meant *problem* instead of *investigator*." She looked at him then, and her eyes were dry and furious and, underneath the fury, tired in a way he recognized from his own mirror. "They didn't take my badge, Rafael. They took my *name*. You'd be amazed how much of a person that is."

"I wouldn't," Rafael said. "I changed mine twice."

Something happened to her face then — not a smile exactly, more the place a smile would live if it ever decided to move in. "I know. I found the records. Brightwater wasn't the name you were born with."

"No," he admitted. "It wasn't."

"Are you going to tell me the real one?"

He looked out over the graves, at the flowers, at the fallen steeple catching the light. Somewhere in the last forty-eight hours the arithmetic of his life had quietly changed. For eleven years the equation had been simple: keep moving, tell no one, outlive the past. Now there was a woman kneeling in the grass beside him reading the names of the dead back into the world, and a chest of evidence in his plane, and a patrol boat somewhere behind them in the fog, and the strange discovery that being seen — truly seen, records and all — didn't feel like drowning. It felt like landing.

"Maybe," he said. "When we get out of here."

"That's the second time you've promised me something conditional."

"I'm a pilot. Everything's conditional. Weather, fuel, engine temperature—"

"Rafael."

"Elena."

"Thank you," she said, "for coming upstream."

He nodded, because his voice had temporarily left the building, and stood, and offered her his hand, and she took it, and they walked back down through the flowers toward the church, and if their hands stayed joined a little longer than the terrain required, neither of them filed a complaint.

* * *

They spent the day documenting. Elena photographed the marker, the graves, the houses, the child's horse, the schoolhouse bell. Rafael, who had planned to sulk usefully by the plane, instead found himself clearing brush from the church steps with a piece of driftwood, and then prying open the schoolhouse door, and then standing very still inside it, because on the wall someone had painted, long ago, a map of Vessania — the whole country, its rivers and towns, its little star-crowned mountain — in colors that had faded to ghosts but had not, despite everything, disappeared.

"Somebody should fix the roof on this place," he heard himself say.

Elena appeared in the doorway behind him. "That's a strange thing for a man who never stops moving to say."

"I'm just saying. It's a good building. It'd be a shame."

"Uh-huh." She came and stood beside him, looking at the painted country that no longer existed anywhere except on this wall and in her ledgers. "You know what happens next. The story breaks, the arrests come, the historians arrive. And then one day there'll be a road up here, and a fence, and a plaque. And it'll be right, and it'll be official, and it won't be ours anymore."

"Whose is it now?"

"Ours," she said. "For today."

They flew out at dusk, climbing through the notch into the last of the light, and Rafael looked back once — just once — at the valley glowing green and gold behind them, the wildflowers burning color in the low sun, the whole lost country holding its breath under the mountain.

He was halfway back down the gorge, fog rising to meet them, when he saw it: a column of dust on the far bank, vehicles moving fast toward Kessler Landing, and at their head a black truck he recognized, because he'd watched it burn Odell's workshop two nights before.

Elena saw it too. She went very still.

"Odell," Rafael said. "The chest is at the landing. The *spare* fuel drums are at the landing. Everything we have left is at the landing."

The radio crackled. Odell's voice came through, ragged and low, and Rafael had known the man for nine years and had never once heard him sound like this.

"Rafe. You'd better get back here. They've got my girl, and I told them everything."
