## 7. Betrayal at the Landing

Elena had learned to read a town the way other people read weather. Kessler Landing, on the morning they returned for fuel, was reading *storm*.

The signs were small at first. The bakery was shuttered, though it was barely nine and Marta Okafor had never in her life missed a morning of selling bread to men who complained about the price while buying three loaves. A dog that usually slept in the middle of the main street like it owned the deed had moved under a porch. And Odell's workshop — usually loud by now with grinding and swearing and radio gospel — stood silent, its doors open, its interior dark as a throat.

"Fuel first," Rafael said, but his voice had gone flat, the way it did when he was doing arithmetic he didn't like.

"Odell first," Elena said.

They found him sitting on an oil drum behind the workshop, hands wrapped around a coffee mug he wasn't drinking from, watching the river like it owed him an apology.

"You're back early," he said.

"We're back on time," Rafael said. "You said the drums would be ready."

"They are." Odell didn't look up. "Raf. You should take your fuel and you should go."

The words landed wrong. Not rude — Odell had never been rude in his life; his insults were a form of affection, delivered with a wrench in one hand — but *careful*. Every word placed like a man walking across ice he already knew was cracking.

"Odell." Elena crouched so she was looking up into his face. He was sixty-something, built like a stove, with forearms that could torque a propeller bolt by hand and eyes that had seen every kind of trouble the interior could ship downstream. Right now those eyes looked like a man reading his own obituary and finding it accurate. "What happened?"

"Nothing happened."

"Your shop is dark."

"I'm resting."

"You don't rest. You told me once that rest is how rust gets in."

A ghost of a smile crossed his mouth and died there. "I say a lot of things."

Behind them, a vehicle door closed. Then another. Elena turned, and her stomach performed the slow, professional drop she'd trained it to do over fifteen years of investigations — the drop that meant *you are standing in the photograph*.

Four men were walking up from the dock. Clean boots. That was always the tell, out here — men whose boots hadn't learned the mud yet, which meant they'd arrived recently and intended to leave soon, and neither arrival nor departure was going to be pleasant. The tall one in front wore a consortium badge on his jacket the way other men wore wedding rings: prominently, and with the assumption that it settled all further questions.

"Mr. Brightwater," the tall one said pleasantly. "And Ms. Vasquez. We've been hoping you'd come back. You made quite a trip upstream. Very scenic this time of year, I'm told. The wildflowers."

Nobody had followed them to the valley. Elena was nearly certain nobody had followed them to the valley. But certainty, she knew, was a luxury item, and someone had clearly paid retail for information.

"We're just refueling," Rafael said.

"Of course you are." The tall man smiled. His name, according to the badge, was Corver. According to the way he held himself, he was the kind of man who'd been given authority young and had never once wondered whether he deserved it. "We'll need the chest, Mr. Brightwater. And we'll need whatever copies or notes or photographs Ms. Vasquez has produced. She does love her documentation. It's in her file."

*Her file.* Her old agency's file, opened like a menu. Elena felt the cold move up her spine. They weren't guessing. They'd been briefed.

"And if we decline?" she asked.

Corver gestured, almost gently, toward the workshop door. Two of his men went inside and came out dragging Odell's daughter's photograph — no. Elena corrected herself. They came out dragging *Odell*, who had risen off the oil drum with the speed of a much younger man and gotten exactly two steps before a third enforcer, whom Elena realized she'd never even registered arriving, put a hand on his shoulder and sat him back down like a dog being told *stay*.

"Dad—" Rafael started forward, and Corver raised one finger, and the whole dock seemed to lean in around them.

"Here's the arithmetic," Corver said. "You give us the chest, everyone walks away, and Kessler Landing stays the quiet little town it's always been. You don't, and Mr. Odell comes downriver with us to discuss some debts. His daughter's debts. Medical ones. Expensive." He clicked his tongue. "Hospitals. Terrible things. Practically legal robbery."

Elena watched Odell's face, and what she saw there broke something in her chest — not fear, but shame. He wouldn't look at Rafael. He was staring at his own boots, muddy, honest boots, like they'd failed him too.

"It wasn't money," Odell said suddenly, hoarsely, to no one and everyone. "I want you both to know that. I didn't do it for money. I did it because they've got Amara's paper and they'll take the house and she'll lose the clinic placement and—" His voice cracked. "I did the math, Raf. I hate myself for it, but I did the math, and the math says one old mechanic against everything they've got."

"The math's wrong," Rafael said quietly.

"The math is *always* wrong," Odell said, "and it still adds up. That's the trick of it."

* * *

They gave up the chest because there was no version of the morning where they didn't. Corver's men took it from the med-evac crate with gloved hands and a reverence that told Elena more than any confession — these men knew exactly what they were carrying, and exactly how much it was worth to their employers that it never be read again. One of them photographed the crate itself, documenting the hiding place, and Elena understood that too: somewhere, a report was being drafted about *how close it came*.

Then Corver made his mistake. Or perhaps it wasn't a mistake; perhaps it was simply the next line of the script, and she'd been cast in it without her consent.

"Ms. Vasquez," he said, as his men loaded the chest onto their boat. "There's someone here to see you. Arrived an hour ago. She's waiting at the landing office."

"She?"

"You'll want to sit down," Corver said, with what might have been genuine kindness, which was somehow worse. "Director Reyes always has that effect."

* * *

Marisol Reyes had not changed. That was the first obscenity of it. Three years since Elena had last seen her — since the hearing where Elena's career had been disassembled bolt by bolt in front of a committee, with Reyes presiding over the demolition and calling it due process — and the woman looked exactly the same. Silver-streaked hair pinned with military precision. Reading glasses pushed up like a headband. The same cardigan, Elena would have bet money on it, the same soft gray cardigan that said *harmless administrator* to everyone who didn't know better.

"Elena." Reyes spread her hands, warm as fresh bread. "Look at you. You're thin. Are they feeding you out here?"

"There's a bakery," Elena said. "It's good."

"Marta's. I remember." Reyes sat, gestured to the chair opposite. Elena remained standing, arms crossed, and Reyes absorbed the refusal without a flicker, the way water absorbs a stone. "Three years. You look well, considering."

"Considering what?"

"Considering that you've spent three years chasing a case that doesn't exist, in a country that doesn't exist, for victims who are, officially, a rounding error." Reyes folded her hands. "I'm here to help you stop, Elena. That's the whole of why I came. I flew eleven hours in a turboprop to help you stop."

"The agency sent you."

"The agency sent me." Reyes nodded, unbothered by confirmation. "Which should tell you how seriously they take this. They don't send deputy directors for rounding errors." She leaned forward. "Sit down, please. What I have to say goes better seated."

Elena sat. Not because she was told to. Because her legs had opinions.

"Here is what happens now," Reyes said. "In approximately six hours, a federal team arrives at this landing. They will be thorough, competent, and completely misdirected. They will investigate the torching of Mr. Odell's workshop — which, incidentally, we regret; that was local initiative, poorly supervised — and they will find nothing else, because by then every document, every ledger, every gram of gold connected to Vessania will have been processed through channels that make it legally invisible. The massacre becomes a historical footnote. The gold becomes an asset reconciliation. And you—" Reyes's voice softened into something that in a kinder woman might have been tenderness— "you become eligible for reinstatement. Full clearance. Back pay. Your name cleared in the very hearing room where it was dragged through the mud. All of it, Elena. Everything you lost. In exchange for one signature and one silence."

"And if I don't sign?"

Reyes sighed, and for a moment the cardigan slipped, and underneath it Elena saw the woman who had once taught her everything she knew about institutional power: that it never threatened, it only *arranged*.

"Then you stay disgraced," Reyes said. "That's all. No threats. No dramatics. You stay exactly what you are now — a relief worker with a fabricated résumé, squatting in a town that can't protect you, attached to a pilot with a smuggling record, holding evidence that no longer exists. You stay a footnote yourself." She rose, smoothed her skirt, and paused at the door. "For what it's worth — and I know it isn't worth much — I argued for you. Three years ago. I argued that you were right and the numbers were wrong."

"What did they say?"

"They said the numbers are the country, Elena. The numbers are the *country*." She opened the door. Sunlight fell across the threshold like a verdict. "The offer stands until the federal team lands. Six hours. Think about what you actually want. You wanted justice. Justice is a story people agree to tell. We're offering you the pen."

She left. Elena sat alone in the landing office, listening to the river, doing her own arithmetic.

Six hours. A federal team. A chest on a boat heading downriver. An old man in debt. A pilot outside, probably rechecking fuel lines with hands that wanted to hit something.

And one thing Reyes hadn't counted on — one thing Elena herself had barely believed until she'd watched the file upload bar crawl to completion over the valley, night before last, while Rafael held the plane steady in wind that wanted them dead:

The evidence was already gone. Already moving. Already, at this moment, traveling through relay towers and borrowed satellite phones toward a journalist in the capital named Priya Chandrasekaran, who did not work for anyone's numbers.

Reyes thought she was offering Elena a choice between disgrace and redemption.

She was offering her a head start.

Elena stood, straightened her jacket, and walked out into the sunlight to find Rafael — because in roughly six hours, Kessler Landing was going to learn what it meant when the country finally paid attention, and somebody needed to be standing next to that stubborn, beautiful fool when it did.
