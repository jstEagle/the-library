## 8. The Long Way Down

The strip at Kessler Landing had never been much—a shelf of gravel Odell graded with a tractor that ran on prayer and baling wire—but Rafael had taken off from worse. What made it impossible now was the fuel truck parked sideways across the middle of it, and the four men standing around the truck with the patient confidence of people who owned the night.

Rafael crouched in the treeline, the med-evac crate heavy against his back, and did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding since sundown.

"Tell me you have another idea," Elena whispered beside him. She'd tied her hair back with a strip of the Vessanian flag, which was either practical or profane, and he hadn't decided which.

"I have a worse idea."

"Those are usually your best ones."

He pointed upriver, to where the mountains folded together behind Mount Kessler and the river gorge narrowed into a slot of black water and blacker stone. Somewhere up there was the pass—Kessler's Notch, the old trappers called it—a gap barely wide enough for regret, let alone a thirty-year-old de Havilland Beaver flying at night without lights.

"That's not an idea," Elena said. "That's a suicide note with wings."

"It's a shortcut." He shifted the crate. "They're watching the strip because the strip is the only sane way out. So we go out the insane way."

"And the engine?"

The engine. Right. The engine that had been coughing since the patrol boat put three rounds through the cowling at the gorge, one of them nicking something important that Odell had patched with a soldering iron and optimism. Odell, who was currently sitting in the back of a consortium pickup with his hands zip-tied, wearing an expression that said *I'm sorry* and *don't do anything stupid* and *do something stupid anyway*, all at once.

"We'll be gentle with it," Rafael said.

"You've never been gentle with anything in your life."

"First time for everything."

She looked at him then—not at the plane, not at the pass, but at him—and whatever she saw made her nod once, sharp, like signing a document she knew would ruin her. "Okay. But if we die, I want it on record that I told you."

"Noted for the record."

* * *

Getting the Beaver into the air meant going out the far end of the lake, where the water ran long and flat between the hills, and praying the floats didn't find a sandbar in the dark. Rafael taxied out with the navigation lights off, running on moon-glare off the water, and Elena sat in the right seat with the radio unit balanced on her knees—the little shortwave rig they'd pulled from Odell's workshop before the fire ate it, its antenna strung out the window on fishing line.

"Frequency?" he asked.

"Marisol Okafor, capital bureau." Elena's voice went flat and professional, the voice of a woman reading charges. "She runs the only newsroom in this country that isn't bought. I sent her a letter six months ago from a pay phone in Veracruz and she wrote back with a question mark and a post office box. That's trust, where I come from."

"And tonight?"

"Tonight I send her everything. The ledgers. The photographs from the valley. The names." Elena's thumb moved over the ledger pages wrapped in oilcloth on her lap. "The transmission will take twenty minutes if the sky cooperates. Longer if it doesn't."

"The sky," Rafael said, easing the throttle forward, "hasn't cooperated all week."

The Beaver came up off the lake heavy and reluctant, floats trailing silver, and Rafael turned it away from the strip, away from the men and their truck and their clean boots, and pointed the nose at the black wall of the mountains.

Behind them, a light flickered on at the landing. Then another. Someone shouting, thin across the water. A rifle shot that hit nothing but the dark, which Rafael chose to interpret as encouragement.

"Climbing," he said. "Start talking."

Elena keyed the transmitter. "Marisol, if you're there, this is the question mark from Veracruz. I'm about to make you famous. Or get us both arrested. Possibly both..."

Her voice settled into the rhythm of transmission—coordinates, dates, ledger numbers read twice for accuracy—and Rafael flew.

* * *

The gorge swallowed them whole.

By day it was beautiful; he'd flown it a hundred times, the river a green ribbon two thousand feet below, walls rising sheer on either side. By night it was a throat. The moon sat behind Mount Kessler now, so the world existed only in fragments: the pale stitch of whitewater below, the occasional grey shoulder of rock sliding past close enough to count lichen, and above, the ragged black teeth of the ridgeline.

The engine coughed.

Not the small, apologetic cough it had been making all evening—a real one, a full-body shudder that dropped the nose and made the airframe groan.

"Elena."

"One second, Marisol—" She covered the mouthpiece. "How bad?"

"Ask me after the next one."

There wasn't a next one. There were three. The engine caught, lost, caught again, running rough as a washing machine full of rocks, and Rafael found himself doing the thing he'd sworn off years ago in another life, in another country, with cargo in the hold that wasn't medicine: talking to the machine.

"Come on, girl. Come on. You've been lied to and shot at and fed nothing but bad fuel your whole career. One more valley. One more."

"What are you doing?"

"Negotiating."

"With the plane."

"She's the only one listening."

Elena almost laughed—he heard it catch in her throat and get swallowed by the work—and then she was back on the radio, steady as a metronome, reading the massacre dates aloud into the night while Rafael flew the impossible route with his heart going like a drum corps.

The pass opened ahead like a wound in the ridge. Kessler's Notch. In daylight, maybe eight hundred feet wide. Tonight, a suggestion, a place where the stars showed through instead of stone.

He climbed. The engine climbed with him, complaining, each foot of altitude purchased at interest. Two thousand feet above the river. Three. The walls narrowed. Wind came through the notch and shoved the Beaver sideways like a hand on the shoulder, and Rafael corrected, overcorrected, corrected again, flying by feel and fury.

Halfway through, the engine quit.

Full silence. The kind of silence that has weight. The propeller windmilling, the mountains suddenly very interested in them, gravity resuming old arguments.

"Rafael—"

"I've got it." He didn't have it. He pushed the nose down, trading height for speed, hunting for airflow, and the altimeter unwound like a confession. Below the notch, he knew—every bush pilot knows—the river bent west into a chain of lakes. Long, flat, cold. Landable, if the engine gave him even ten seconds of warning.

"Elena. How much longer on the file?"

"Four minutes." Her voice didn't shake. He'd remember that later, in the hospital and after: it didn't shake. "Maybe five."

"We don't have five."

"Then I'll send faster." And she did, her voice doubling tempo, numbers spilling out, and Rafael held the dead-stick glide through the notch with the ridgeline streaming past on either side close enough to touch, and somewhere in the middle of it the engine caught—one glorious, sputtering, half-hearted catch—and died again, and caught again, and stayed caught, running like a man with a bullet in him who refuses to sit down.

"There's a lake," he said. "Two miles. Maybe less."

"I'm sending."

The trees came up fast. He picked the widest gap in the blackness, flared too high, dropped too hard, and the floats hit the lake with a crack like the world breaking in half.

* * *

Afterward, there was water coming through the torn float, and the Beaver slewing sideways, and Rafael hauling the yoke full over to swing the tail into the wind, and the second impact when the left float's seams gave and the wingtip kissed the lake and cartwheeled them around in a slow, grinding arc that ended with the plane beached on a shingle bar, listing, hissing steam, alive.

Alive. Both of them.

Rafael became aware of this fact in stages: first the cold, then the taste of blood where he'd bitten his lip, then Elena's hand gripping his sleeve hard enough to leave bruises, and finally her voice, hoarse:

"—copy? Marisol, confirm receipt. Confirm receipt."

Static. Long static. Then a woman's voice, tinny and exhausted and awake at three in the morning, saying words that Elena would repeat under her breath for weeks afterward:

*"Received. All of it. God help me, received. Get out of there. Get out of there and stay alive—I'm putting it on the wire at dawn."*

Elena set the radio down in the flooded footwell as if it were made of glass. She was shaking. Rafael realized he was shaking too, and that the shaking had started somewhere over the notch and hadn't stopped.

"Well," he managed.

"Don't." She turned to look at him. In the starlight her face was wet—lake water, he told himself, definitely lake water—and there was a cut above her eyebrow and she was laughing, actually laughing, low and disbelieving. "Don't say anything smug. We crashed."

"We landed."

"The plane is in the lake."

"The plane is resting in the lake. There's a difference. It's a subtle distinction, but it matters to pilots."

She looked at the Beaver—the bent propeller, the wingtip folded like a broken finger, the floats full of the lake—and something crossed her face that wasn't laughter anymore. "You could have gone around. Taken the strip odds. Flown south and been in another country by Thursday with your anonymity intact."

"Yeah."

"Why didn't you?"

Rafael looked at the wreck of his plane, his escape route, his whole strategy for staying alive for eleven years, and found that the answer had been sitting in his chest for days, waiting for him to land hard enough to knock it loose.

"Because I spent my whole life moving so nobody could pin me down," he said. "Turns out some things you can't outrun. You just have to deliver them."

Elena reached over and took his hand, blood and lake water and all, and neither of them said anything else for a while, because the stars over the lake were doing what stars do after you've nearly died, which is burn like they mean it.

* * *

They walked out at first light along the shore, carrying what mattered: the ledgers in oilcloth, the flag, the coins wrapped in a sock, Elena's notebook swollen with river water but legible. Behind them the Beaver sat on the shingle bar with the dawn gilding its wounds, and Rafael did not look back, because looking back is how you turn a sacrifice into a wound.

Ahead, twelve miles down the lakeshore, there was a logging road, and on the logging road, eventually, a town with a bus depot and a telegraph office and a newspaper stand already stacking its morning editions.

And ahead of all of it—already moving faster than any plane, faster than any consortium truck, faster than fear itself—was the story. On the wire at dawn. Rolling toward the capital like weather.

Elena stopped at the top of the rise where the road showed below, and shaded her eyes, and smiled the smile of a woman who has just fired a shot she can't take back.

"They'll know we're coming now," she said. "Both sides."

"Good," Rafael said, and meant it for the first time in his life. "Let them set the table. We're bringing guests."

Far to the south, faint as a rumor, the first morning train whistled through the valley—and somewhere beyond it, in the capital, a pressroom was waking up to the end of the world as certain powerful men knew it.
