## 2. Miles, Not Years

People ask how old I am sometimes — usually drunks in cantinas, usually kindly, the way you'd ask a dog its age. I never give them years. Years are a currency I stopped carrying. I tell them I'm eleven thousand kilometers old, or fourteen, depending on which stretch of steppe we're standing on, and they laugh, thinking it's a joke, and I let them laugh because it costs nothing and because the truth is heavier than anyone wants at closing time.

The truth is that I measure my life in miles walked. Kilometers, technically — this is Argentina, and Argentina does everything in kilometers except forgive. Every morning since the winter of 2009 I have laced my boots and added to the total, and every night I have lain down knowing that the number is bigger than yesterday's and that this is the only arithmetic that has never lied to me.

Distance doesn't ask anything of you. Distance doesn't say *you should have turned back*. Distance just accumulates, patient as salt.

* * *

Here is what people think they know about the Whitlock avalanche: that a guide named Wendell Whitlock took six clients onto the Marconi Glacier approach in late September against all sense, that the slope above Camp Two let go at 6:14 in the morning, and that one of the dead was her own brother, and that no one who signs off on a route like that deserves to work in this country or the next one.

That version is mostly true, which is the cruelest thing about it. Mostly true is worse than a lie. A lie you can fight.

Let me give you the parts that don't make it into the bar-room version.

The client was a man named Dietrich Ohlsson, a Swedish shipping heir with more money than patience and a mountaineering résumé assembled from brochures. He had booked the traverse twice before and been turned back by weather both times, and he had decided — in the way rich men decide things, quietly and irrevocably, over expensive whisky — that the third attempt would happen on his schedule or not at all. The forecast window was narrow. The snowpack that season had built in layers, warm spells sandwiching cold ones like pages in a book, and every guide on the ice knew what kind of book that makes: one waiting for someone to open it.

I told him no. I want that on the record, even if the record is just me talking to a dark cabin ceiling. I stood in the refugio at El Chaltén with the avalanche bulletin printed out and pinned to the table under my finger, and I said, "Not this week. Not this route. We go low, we go slow, or we go home."

And Tomás — God help me, Tomás — said yes.

Not because he disagreed with me. Because he was twenty-six and charming and he believed that mountains reward the honest, and he'd watched me fight with Ohlsson for two days and seen the deposit slip on the refugio desk that represented four months of our operating budget. We were young. The company was Tomás's idea originally — *Whitlock Brothers Patagonia*, which is what everyone called it even after I corrected them for the hundredth time, because "brothers" sounded better than "brother and sister" and Tomás said accuracy was for maps. He handled the clients' hearts; I handled their ropes. It worked, for three years. It worked so well.

That night he came to my bunk and sat on the edge of it, the way he used to when we were kids sharing a room in Bariloche and he couldn't sleep before a ski race. "Wen," he said. "We take the lower line. We rope wide spacing. We turn around at the col by noon no matter where we are. I'll tell Ohlsson myself."

"You already told him we're going."

"I told him we're going *carefully*." He grinned, and it was the grin that got us free meals in half the towns in the south, and I was tired, and I loved him, and I wanted the season to be over and the books to balance. So I signed the route card. My signature. My hand. That's the part I carry — not the avalanche, which was nobody and everybody, which was weather and physics and bad luck stacked like those snow layers — but the pen moving across the paper.

At 6:14 the next morning the slope above Camp Two opened its book.

* * *

They found me two hundred meters downslope, half-buried, alive, which the rescuers called a miracle and I have since come to regard as a clerical error. They found three of the clients the same day. They found the fourth two days later. They did not find Tomás for nine weeks, and then only because a melting serac gave him up at the toe of the glacier, and I will spare you what nine weeks of meltwater does, because I have never once managed to spare myself.

The inquiry took five months. Five months of men in good jackets asking me to walk them through my decisions, and I did, honestly, all the way through, including the part where I signed. Hale countersigned too — Marcus Hale, my old mentor, the man who taught me to read slope angles the way other people read faces. His lawyer arrived with better lawyers than mine. When the findings came down, the report read like it had been written by someone who'd only heard the story secondhand: *the lead guide proceeded despite documented instability.* One name in bold. Mine.

Hale's name appeared once, in a footnote, as *countersigning authority*, in type so small you needed a young man's eyes to find it.

I went to see him afterward. He met me at a café in El Calafate with his coat still on, which I understood immediately as choreography — a man dressed to leave. He put his hand over mine on the table and said, "Wendell, you know I argued for postponement," and I said, "Then say it to the commission," and he looked at me with such genuine, bottomless sorrow that for one stupid second I almost comforted *him*. That is the trick of Marcus Hale. He believes his own sorrow. He has never once had to live inside anyone else's.

Two weeks later my guiding license was suspended pending review. The review concluded. The suspension became permanent. Chile followed Argentina's lead the way small countries do, and just like that, the only thing I had ever been good at was taken away and the taking-away was stamped official, with a case number, so that it looked like justice instead of what it was, which was a rich man's lawyers and an old man's cowardice dividing up a dead woman's reputation between them.

Tomás got a plaque. I got a blacklist. Between the two of us, I sometimes think he got the better deal.

* * *

You want to know what grief looks like from the inside? It doesn't look like anything. That's the swindle. You expect it to look like the movies — weeping at windows, clutching sweaters. Mostly it looks like getting up. It looks like remembering, mid-paragraph of some ordinary task, that your brother is dead, and putting the task down, and picking it back up, because there is nothing else to do with your hands. Grief isn't a wave. Waves end. Grief is a climate. You dress for it every day without noticing you're dressing for it, and one day you realize you haven't been warm in fifteen years and can't remember what warmth was for.

For the first year I stayed in El Chaltén and drank in a manner I can only describe as professional. For the second year I walked. I didn't decide to walk; walking decided for me, the way it decides for animals that can't stay in a burned forest. I walked north along the Carretera with a pack and no plan, and somewhere around Villa Cerro Castillo I noticed that for whole hours at a time I wasn't thinking about anything except the next ridge, the next water, the next kilometer. And I thought: *this*. This is the anesthesia. Not forgetting — nobody forgets — but covering. Laying distance over the wound like gauze, layer after layer, until the walking itself became the point and the wound was something that happened to a different woman, a woman with a company and a brother and a signature she couldn't take back.

So I made it a discipline. Eleven thousand kilometers and counting. I could tell you what I was thinking at kilometer 3,400 (nothing; a river crossing near Lago Posadas; cold to the sternum). I could tell you about kilometer 7,900 (a dog that adopted me for two days outside Perito Moreno and left me for a butcher with better smells). The kilometers have texture. The years don't. Years just sit there being evidence.

* * *

Fifteen years of this teaches you things. It teaches you that the dry side of the Andes — the rain shadow, the steppe, the country the mapmakers shaded yellow out of laziness — is the best place in the world to be a person who doesn't want to be found. On the wet side there are parks and rangers and tourists who recognize your face from the newspapers. On the dry side there is wind, guanaco, estancias spaced a day apart, and roads that appear and disappear according to some private treaty between the land and God. I've crossed it eleven times. I know which springs run in February and which lie. I know the smell of a puma's kill before you ever see it. I know that the steppe looks flat and isn't, that it hides canyons deep enough to swallow a truck, and that the people who live out there — gauchos, mostly, and the widows of gauchos — will feed a stranger who knocks politely and forget her by Thursday, which is exactly the hospitality I spent a decade shopping for.

I work when I need money, at the edges of the trade. Ferrying gear for geologists. Walking fence lines for ranchers. Once, memorably, escorting a German birdwatcher with a heart condition forty kilometers to a road because he insisted on seeing a rhea and then insisted on chest pains. I never guide. Guiding means responsibility. Responsibility is a word I've developed an allergy to, the way some people develop allergies to shellfish — fine for years, then one exposure and your throat closes.

Except that the body keeps its own ledger, underneath mine. Some mornings I wake up in whatever shed or tent I've rented and my hands are already moving through the motions — checking knots that aren't there, reading a sky that isn't there — and I understand that the guide didn't die in the avalanche either. She just got buried. And buried things, given enough time and enough meltwater, have a way of coming back to the surface.

* * *

Which brings me, reluctantly, to the cave.

I've thought about whether to tell this part, and I've decided that a confession with holes in it isn't worth the paper, so: three days ago, near Puerto Deseado, the tide went out further than any tide in living memory — the radio said so, fishermen said so, the exposed black rock said so in a language older than either — and I walked out onto seabed that hadn't seen air since before my grandfather was born, and I found a cave mouth.

Inside, on the walls, in lamp-black and scratched lines: survey markings. Route symbols, elevation notes, dates. The handwriting of someone trained the way I was trained, because there's really only one school for it out here. And beside the main cluster of figures, repeated like a signature, like a man practicing a name he was afraid to forget:

*T.W.*

I stood in that cave for a long time. Long enough that I started calculating the tide's return, which is the guide in me, always keeping one hand on the exit. And I photographed the walls with the cracked phone I keep for emergencies, and I walked out, and I climbed to the road, and I told myself the thing I have gotten very good at telling myself:

Legends are for tourists. Dead men don't leave initials in caves. The sea gives things back sometimes, and most of what it gives back is junk.

I told myself that all the way back to my cabin, forty kilometers inland, walking the whole way because the bus cost money and because, well. Old habits.

Eleven thousand four hundred and six kilometers. I counted them off under my breath like a rosary, and for the first time in fifteen years the number wouldn't sit still. It kept sliding sideways, toward the coast, toward the rock, toward two letters cut into stone by a hand I'd know anywhere, because I learned to write watching it.

This morning there was a knock at my door. A woman stood on my step holding a folder against the wind — city clothes wrong for the country, eyes wrong for the city — and she said her name was Elena Vasquez-Roth, and that she had photographs I needed to see.

I hadn't told anyone about the cave.

I hadn't told anyone, and yet here she was, opening the folder on my table with hands that weren't quite steady, and the first photograph was of my brother's handwriting, and the second was of a face I hadn't seen in fifteen years and had hoped never to see again.

But that's the next chapter, and I've walked far enough for today.
