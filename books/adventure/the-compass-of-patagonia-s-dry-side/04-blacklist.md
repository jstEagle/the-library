## 4. Blacklist

I told her no.

I want it on the record that I told her no with a certain dignity. I didn't shout. I didn't throw her photographs back at her or accuse her of being one more in a long line of people who thought a Whitlock could be bought for the price of a story. I stood at my own kitchen counter, in my own rented cabin, with my own kettle going cold on the stove because I'd forgotten about it mid-boil — a small domestic failure that Elena Vasquez-Roth, to her credit, pretended not to notice — and I said, "No."

She said, "May I ask why?"

"You may not."

"Then I'll ask anyway." She folded her hands on the table like a woman settling in for a long winter. Historians, I would learn, are built exactly like bad weather. They don't argue. They simply remain. "Is it the money? Because the figure is negotiable."

"It's not the money."

"The timeline? We could push the departure—"

"It's not the timeline, Doctor."

"Wendell." She said my name the way you'd set down something fragile on an uneven shelf. "You looked at those photographs for eleven minutes before you said a single word. You counted the carvings twice. Whatever is in that cave, you already believe it's real. So this isn't about whether the expedition existed. This is about why you won't be the one to take me there."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Outside, a chucao bird was making its idiotic racket in the coihue scrub, and I remember thinking that even the birds down here had opinions about my life.

"Guiding means responsibility," I said finally. "Responsibility means someone else's life in your hands. And I've held enough lives to know how they slip."

"That's not an answer either."

"It's the only one I have."

She looked at me for a long moment — that archival look, the one that files things away against future need — and then she gathered her photographs into their folder, squared the edges, and left them on my table. All of them. The cave mouth. The initials. T.W., cut into stone by a hand I would have known anywhere, because I'd watched it learn to write.

"I'll be at the Hostería del Pescador until Friday," she said from the doorway. "In case the answer changes. Answers do that, down here. The wind gets into everything."

Then she was gone, and I was alone with a cold kettle and my brother's initials staring up at me from a stranger's photograph, and I did what any sensible woman does when cornered by her own past.

I cleaned the cabin.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about disgrace: it doesn't end. It just changes addresses.

For fifteen years I had been a rumor moving north to south along the dry side of the Andes — the Whitlock woman, they called me in the guide houses of Bariloche and Coyhaique, the one who walked her brother into a slide. The details shifted depending on who told it. In some versions I'd ignored the signs out of greed; in others, out of pride; in the kindest ones, out of simple error, which somehow made it worse, because error meant it could happen to anyone, and no one wanted to hear that. What never changed was the ending: Tomás Whitlock, twenty-six years old, taken off the face of Cerro Catedral Sur under four meters of consolidated snow while his sister stood close enough to hear it happen and far enough to survive it.

The association pulled my certification within the month. Argentina followed Chile's lead, because Argentina always follows Chile's lead in matters of professional shame, and after that I stopped being a guide and became something smaller: a walker. A measurer of distances. A woman who knew every unmarked pass on the leeward side of the range and had no legal right to lead so much as a school group through a parking lot.

I lived on odd work. Fence repair for estancias that didn't ask questions. Survey assistance for mining outfits that asked fewer. Once, memorably, a season counting guanaco for a wildlife census, during which I learned that guanacos are the only creatures in Patagonia with a better talent for looking unbothered than I have. I kept a notebook — not a diary, God forbid, nothing so soft — but a ledger of kilometers. Forty-one thousand, two hundred and six, as of that morning Elena knocked on my door. I added the distance from the cabin to the hostería and back that afternoon, because she'd made me walk it twice in my head, and got two hundred and fourteen more.

That was the arithmetic of my life. Not years — years were just weather. Kilometers. Every one of them a step further from the sound a mountainside makes when it decides.

So I cleaned the cabin. I swept a floor that didn't need sweeping. I reorganized a gear shelf that hadn't changed since the last time I needed to not think about something. And around midnight, sitting in the dark with a cup of tea I'd finally remembered to make, I caught myself doing the thing I always did, the unforgivable thing, the thing that had kept me alive and hollow for fifteen years:

I was planning the route.

Not guiding it. Just — solving it. The way a man who's sworn off cards still shuffles. From Puerto Deseado inland along the old drover track, then west where the steppe starts remembering it used to be seafloor, then the long climb through the badlands to the rain shadow line, and after that the country only a handful of living people had crossed on foot, and I was one of them, and another one was dead, and the third was Marcus Hale, and I hated myself a little for knowing that.

I put the notebook away. I went to bed. I dreamed, for the first time in months, of snow.

* * *

The border official came on Thursday.

His name was Inspector Fuentes, and he arrived in a gray Toyota Hilux with government plates and the particular posture of a man who has driven six hours of gravel to deliver news he could have delivered by phone but preferred to deliver in person, watching your face. He was maybe forty, polite in the way bureaucracy is polite — which is to say, thoroughly — and he took off his hat before he knocked, which I grudgingly respected.

"Señora Whitlock."

"Inspector."

"May I?" He nodded at the table without waiting for permission, which told me everything about how the conversation was going to go. He sat. He produced a folder of his own, thinner than Elena's but considerably more ominous, and laid it open between us like a card player showing his hand one card at a time. "You are Wendell Anne Whitlock. Formerly certified guide, Asociación de Guías de Montaña de Chile, revoked 2010. Also listed with the Argentine federation, same year. Currently residing—" he checked, though we both knew he'd checked weeks ago, "—here. Seasonally. Is that accurate?"

"It's accurate."

"We've received a flag on your name." He turned a page. "Routine. But routine flags require routine follow-up. There is activity associated with your file. Inquiries. Someone has been asking questions about you in Comodoro Rivadavia, and again in Puerto Deseado. An American party, we believe. Well funded. They requested copies of your certification history."

My stomach did something slow and cold. "Marcus Hale."

"You know him."

"He taught me to read avalanche terrain when I was nineteen."

Fuentes wrote that down, which offended me more than anything else he'd done. "This party has applied for scientific survey permits covering a substantial area of the western cordillera. Their application references historical expedition routes." He looked up. "It also references, in an appendix, certain prior incidents involving named local guides. Your name appears in that appendix, Señora Whitlock. As context."

"As context," I repeated.

"A cautionary note. Regarding reliability."

I laughed. I couldn't help it — it came out of me like something escaping a trap, sharp and graceless, and Fuentes had the decency to look briefly uncomfortable. Fifteen years of walking, forty-one thousand kilometers of penance, and somewhere in a filing cabinet in Comodoro I was a cautionary note. An appendix. Footnote material in Marcus Hale's grant applications.

"What is it you're here to tell me, Inspector?"

He closed the folder. That was the strange part — he closed it, and softened, and for a moment he was just a tired man in a government truck who'd driven too far on gravel. "I'm here to tell you that staying in one place has become complicated for you. The flag will renew. It will grow. Men like this American — they don't inquire once. They inquire until the file says what they want it to say." He stood, put his hat back on, and paused at the door. "My advice, and it is only advice: a person whose name keeps appearing in other people's paperwork should consider being somewhere the paperwork isn't."

"And where is that?"

"West," he said. "Where the paperwork runs out." Then he tipped the hat, actually tipped it, and drove off in a rooster tail of dust toward a world where names meant what they said.

* * *

I sat with it all evening, the way you sit with a broken fence post, testing how deep the rot goes.

Here is the truth I'd been avoiding since Elena spread those photographs across my table: I hadn't said no because of responsibility. Responsibility was the respectable reason, the reason I served to visitors, the reason that sounded like grief instead of what it actually was. The real reason was smaller and meaner. The real reason was that if I guided her — if I took a client, a real client, across real terrain with real stakes — then I would be the guide again. And the guide is the person who decides. And the person who decides is the person who buried her brother.

Tomás died because I read the slope and called it good. That's the whole story. Not the client who pushed, not the warm snap nobody forecast, not the cornice that had been hanging there since the previous century waiting patiently for a Whitlock. I read the slope. I said go. My brother, who trusted me absolutely, because I was the older one and I was the one with the eye, stepped onto thirty-one degrees of loaded snow and the mountain answered inside of four seconds. They found him in June, when things melted. I signed the identification papers with a hand that didn't feel attached to me, and I have not felt attached to anything since, except distances.

Because here's the thing about kilometers: they can't die. You spend them and they're gone, clean transactions, no debt carried forward. A client is different. A client trusts you the way Tomás trusted me, and trust is the one currency I've been bankrupt in for fifteen years.

But.

But there was a compass in my head now, and it wouldn't stop pointing. T.W. carved into sea cave stone by a dead man's hand. A historian with her grandfather's ghost in her voice, saying *a survivor* like it was a door she was holding open. An inspector telling me my name was metastasizing through other people's files, and that the only direction left was the one direction I'd sworn off. And underneath all of it, patient as groundwater, the route itself — solved, complete, sitting in my skull like a tenant who pays no rent — insisting that it existed to be walked.

Cornered. That's what I was. Pride had walled me in on three sides and the past had walled me in on the fourth, and every exit led the same place: west, into the dry country, carrying someone else's life in my hands again.

I took out the ledger. Forty-one thousand, four hundred and twenty. I looked at the number for a long time, and I understood, with the clarity that only comes at the bottom of a bottle or the top of a decision, that the number had never been saving me. It had been hiding me. Every kilometer was a place Tomás wasn't, and I had spent fifteen years mistaking absence for peace.

I got out a fresh page. I wrote, at the top, in the survey hand I'd inherited from the same father who'd taught it to my brother: *Puerto Deseado to the rain shadow line.* Below it, the stages. Below those, the water sources, the escape routes, the places where the wind funnels and the places where it forgives. My handwriting moved faster as it went, and I let it.

Around two in the morning I stopped, and sat back, and admitted it to the empty room:

"I'm going to hate this."

The room, sensibly, said nothing.

Friday came up gray and clean over the water. I packed the ledger, the gear I'd swear I'd sold years ago, and the photograph Elena had left — the one where the light catches the carved initials just right, T.W. sharp as the day some frostbitten hand cut them. I put on my boots, which fit the way old sins fit, perfectly.

The Hostería del Pescador smelled of diesel and fried fish, and Elena Vasquez-Roth was at a corner table with three cups of coffee in front of her, as if she'd known exactly how long this negotiation was going to take and had provisioned accordingly.

I sat down across from her.

"My terms," I said, "are not negotiable."
