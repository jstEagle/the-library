## 6. Provisions and Ghosts

Comodoro Rivadavia in October smells like diesel and salt and the particular despair of a town built to service oil rigs that no longer love it back. I'd been there maybe six times in fifteen years, always passing through, always buying the same things: rope, fuel, dried meat, the kind of optimism you can fit in a pannier.

This time I was buying for two.

Elena had given me a list written in pencil on the back of an envelope, her handwriting small and precise, like everything else about her — the kind of script that belonged to someone who'd never had to write against wind. *Stove fuel, four canisters. Rope, sixty meters minimum. Dehydrated anything. A second sleeping bag, she runs cold.* She'd underlined that last one twice, which told me more about how much research she'd done on me than on herself. Historians. They archive everything except their own weaknesses.

The problem was money. The problem was always money, but this time it had teeth, because gear for a crossing of the dry side wasn't a purchase, it was a mortgage. I stood outside the ferretería on Rivadavia Street doing arithmetic I already knew the answer to, the way you check a locked door three times hoping it's changed its mind.

I had eleven hundred pesos and a reputation that made credit a rumor. Elena had wired me half her payment up front — generous by any standard, suspicious by mine — but half of a historian's grant is still less than what a proper expedition costs, and we were going to do this properly or not at all. There was no cheap way across the rain shadow. That was rather the point of it.

So I did what I'd done my whole life when the numbers didn't work: I walked into the shop anyway and started lying to myself about what was essential.

* * *

Old Man Ferreyra ran the outfitting store the way his father had, which is to say he treated every customer as a potential thief and every item as a personal favor being extended at great cost to himself. He was seventy if he was a day, with hands like tree bark and eyes that had priced a thousand liars.

"Whitlock." He said my name the way you'd spit out a seed. "Heard you were back in the country."

"I'm never in the country, Ferreyra. I'm just briefly not somewhere else."

"Hmph." He rang up rope without looking at me, which was his version of hospitality. "Sixty meters?"

"Eighty."

His eyebrows went up a centimeter. Eighty meters of rope says something about a route, and Ferreyra knew routes the way priests know sins. "Dry side," he said. Not a question.

"You sell maps too, or just opinions?"

"Opinions are free. Maps cost." He wrapped the coil in brown paper, slow as erosion. "You're guiding someone."

"A historian."

"A woman." He said it flatly, and I let him have it, because arguing with Ferreyra about what he could read off you was like arguing with a scale about your weight. "Corvera," he added, quieter.

My hands went still on the counter. "What did you say?"

"The expedition. Nineteen seventy-nine. Everyone who comes asking about the dry side lately is asking about Corvera." He finally looked at me, and there was something in his face I didn't like — not mockery, worse. Pity. "You're not the only one buying rope this month, Whitlock."

The shop went very small around me. "Who else?"

He shrugged, magnificent in his indifference, and quoted me a price for the rope that was robbery even by Patagonian standards. I paid it. Outside, the wind came off the harbor carrying the smell of fish meal and money changing hands, and I stood there holding eighty meters of future and understood that whatever race I'd thought I was entering had already started without me.

* * *

I spent the afternoon provisioning like a woman being chased, which, in a sense, I was — by the calendar, by the season, by the memory of Ferreyra's face. Dried beef, hard cheese, yerba mate in quantity, chocolate because Elena had mentioned once, casually, that she thought better with sugar, and I had filed that away with an attentiveness that embarrassed me. Fuel canisters. A repair kit. Spare bootlaces, because the dry side eats laces the way the wet side eats boots.

By five o'clock I'd spent money I didn't have at four different establishments, and the shopkeepers' expressions had progressed from suspicion through resignation into a kind of communal wager about whether I'd ever come back to settle up. I would. Probably. The blacklist had never stopped me paying debts; it just stopped anyone trusting me enough to extend them.

I was loading the last of it into the truck bed behind the hostería when I heard my name spoken in a voice I hadn't heard in fifteen years and had heard every day since, in the privacy of my own skull, where grudges keep their offices.

"Wendell Whitlock. As I live and breathe badly."

Marcus Hale stood at the corner of the building with the late sun behind him, and for one treacherous second he was exactly the man I remembered: broad, silvering, with the easy physical confidence of someone who has never once doubted that the world would hold his weight. Then the light shifted and I saw the rest. Seventy now, maybe older. The shoulders still wide but carried differently, like furniture moved to a new house. And the eyes — the eyes had gone from warm to polished. Warmth you can fall into. Polish you slide off.

"Marcus," I said. Fifteen years, and that was all I had. It was probably fifteen years too many.

"You look well." He came closer, arms half-open in the international gesture of men who want credit for affection they're not prepared to spend. "Thinner. Desert-thin. Still walking everywhere, I hear. Still measuring your life in kilometers."

"Still talking about other people's lives like you own shares in them."

He laughed, and the laugh was genuine, and that was the worst part. Marcus Hale had always laughed genuinely. He'd countersigned the report that ended my career with the same sincerity he brought to toasts and funerals. Some men lie with their faces. Marcus lied with his paperwork.

"They told me you were in town," he said. "Bought rope from old Ferreyra. Eighty meters." He clicked his tongue. "That's a serious purchase. That's a glacier-grade purchase."

"It's a rope, Marcus."

"It's a statement." He glanced at the truck bed, reading my provisions the way Elena read archives — fluently, hungrily. "Fuel for two. Two sleeping bags' worth of space in that arrangement, unless you've taken up sleeping diagonally. Chocolate." His gaze came back up, and the warmth in it had acquired a temperature below zero. "You're crossing."

I said nothing. Silence is the only currency the blacklisted can spend freely.

"To the dry side," he went on, softly now, almost tenderly, and I watched the transformation happen in real time, the mentor draining out of his face and something older and colder settling in behind the features, something that had been waiting fifteen years for exactly this conversation. "Following the Corvera markers. With the Vasquez-Roth woman."

"You've been asking about me."

"I've been asking about everyone. It's what money is for." He smiled, and it was the smile from the inquiry hearing, the one that had said *I'm sorry, Wendell, truly, but the signature stands* while his hand rested on the counter beside the document that buried me. "Elena Vasquez-Roth. Brilliant, apparently. Well-funded. Grandfather was radio operator on Corvera — did she tell you that part yet? No?" He read my face and something flickered in his, satisfaction maybe, or its cheaper cousin. "Ah. She hasn't. Well. Historians curate."

"That's a strange thing for you to know."

"It's a strange thing for you not to." He stepped back, and just like that the warmth returned, full strength, seamless, and I understood that I had been watching a man change masks, not moods. There was no mood underneath. There was only the next mask. "Wendell. Old student. Favorite failure." He spread his hands. "Come talk to us. We're at the Hotel Español. My team — twelve people, satellite comms, helicopters on retainer in Coyhaique. We have permits, funding, insurance, lawyers whose names make judges sit up straight. You could guide for us. Proper wage. Proper equipment. And—" he paused, delicately, a surgeon indicating the incision point, "—proper company. You've been alone a long time. It shows. It shows in the way you buy chocolate for someone and don't even know why you're smiling."

Heat crawled up my neck. I hated him for seeing it. I hated myself for doing it.

"No," I said.

"You haven't asked what we're offering."

"I remember what you offer. I've been paying it off for fifteen years."

Something moved behind the polish then — fast, quickly buried, but real. Hurt, maybe. Or the memory of hurt, fossilized. "That was a bad season," he said quietly. "For both of us. You think I slept after that inquiry? You think I chose—"

"I think you signed."

"And you didn't?" The words came out sharper than he meant them to, and for a half-second I saw the crack in the polish, the thing underneath that wasn't a mask at all but a wound he'd dressed so long ago he'd forgotten it wasn't healed. "You signed the route assessment, Wendell. Your name was on it first."

"My name was on it because you told me to put it there. Because you said the client's insurance required a lead-guide signature and yours would follow. And when the mountain came down, yours followed all right — straight onto the report as a formality, while mine followed me out of the country." I kept my voice level. Fifteen years of practice. "I don't want your wage, Marcus. I don't want your helicopters. Get out of my way."

"I'm not in your way." He stepped aside, actually did it, gesturing toward the street with a courtesy that was somehow more threatening than blocking the road would have been. "I'm ahead of you. I have been since before you knew there was a race. Ask yourself why the cave was found this year, Wendell. Ask yourself who paid for the survey vessel that charted that stretch of coast. Ask yourself—" and here he leaned in, and his voice dropped to something almost kind, which from Marcus Hale was the most dangerous register there was, "—what a man does with forty years of guilt when he finally has the money to go dig it up."

Then he straightened, clapped his hands together once like a man closing a ledger, and the warmth came back up over his face like a tide over rocks.

"Dinner's at eight, Hotel Español, if you change your mind. Bring the historian. We'll treat her well either way — that's not a threat, Wendell, it's a promise. We're not barbarians." He turned, then paused, half-smiling back at me. "She should know what she's hired, though. For her own safety. People deserve accurate records. Isn't that the whole profession?"

He walked off down Rivadavia Street, and men at the corner touched their hats to him, because money gets greeted in this country even when it isn't liked.

* * *

I sat in the truck for a long time after that, engine off, watching the harbor lights come on one by one like a slow confession.

*Grandfather was radio operator on Corvera.*

She hadn't told me. Or — no. Be fair. She'd told me things, in her careful historian's way, and I'd taken what I needed and filed the rest under *later*. Had she said it plainly? Had I chosen not to hear it? There's a difference between being lied to and arranging your attention so the truth slides past, and I couldn't swear which one I'd done. Both of us negotiating honesty like smugglers, trading in partial cargoes.

And the other thing. The thing that sat in my chest like swallowed ballast.

*Ask yourself who paid for the survey vessel.*

I tried to reconstruct it. The freak low tide. The cave mouth open for the first time in decades, exposed by water pulled back like a held breath released. Coincidence, I'd called it. Luck. The sea does strange things. But tides are not random — they're arithmetic, moon and coast and pressure, predictable to the minute by anyone with tables and a reason to look. Someone had known when that cave would open. Someone had been watching when it did. And the story had traveled — through Puerto Deseado, through the fishing gossip, through channels I didn't monitor because I'd spent fifteen years making sure nobody monitored me — until it reached a historian with a grandfather's ghost and a grant, and separately reached a rich man with a father's sin and a fleet.

Two expeditions, converging on the same empty place. Except the place wasn't empty. I'd seen the walls. Initials. Dates. A survey hand I'd have known anywhere, because I'd learned my own letters copying his.

Tomás.

I got out of the truck and carried the gear upstairs past the landlady, who looked at my purchases, then at my face, and wisely charged me double for the night without comment.

Elena was at the little table by the window with her papers spread out in their tidy constellation, and when I came in she looked up, and whatever she saw in my face made her set down her pen.

"You met someone," she said.

"How could you possibly—"

"Because you have the expression of a woman who's just been handed a bill she thought was already paid." She pushed the teapot toward me, an offering, a treaty. "Sit down, Wendell. Tell me who."

I sat. I poured. And I found, to my considerable irritation, that my hands weren't steady, and that the steadiness I'd spent fifteen years manufacturing out of distance and routine had come apart in one afternoon on a street in Comodoro Rivadavia, undone by brown paper and rope and a warm voice saying my name like it owned stock in it.

"Marcus Hale," I said. "My old mentor. The man who taught me to read terrain." I took a breath that tasted like diesel and old tea. "And the man who buried me."

Elena didn't flinch. She opened a fresh page in her notebook instead, and wrote the date at the top in her small precise hand, and waited, and I understood that I was about to become part of somebody's archive — and that for the first time in fifteen years, some stubborn surviving piece of me wanted to be recorded accurately.

"He knows where we're going," I said. "He was there before us. And Elena — he knows about your grandfather."

Her pen stopped moving.

Outside, the wind picked up off the harbor, rattling the window in its frame like a fist testing a door, and somewhere down the street a dog began to bark at nothing, or at everything, or at the shape of the season turning against us all.
