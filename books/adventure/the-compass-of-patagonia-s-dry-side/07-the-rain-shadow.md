## 7. The Rain Shadow

The rain started before dawn, which was fitting, because the whole point of this crossing was to leave the rain behind.

We broke camp under dripping coigüe forest on the Chilean side, the kind of green that gets into your lungs and stays there. Everything dripped. My pack dripped. Elena dripped, and pretended she didn't mind, which was the first thing I learned about her that morning: she would rather suffer in silence than admit a single discomfort, and I would spend the next three hundred kilometers watching her do it.

"Beautiful," she said, looking up into a canopy that was actively raining on her face.

"That's not beautiful. That's wet."

"It's the wettest place on earth, Wendell. The western slope catches everything the Pacific has. Four thousand millimeters a year in some valleys. That's why we're crossing."

"Most people fly."

"Most people," Elena said, tightening a strap with fingers gone white at the knuckles, "are not interested in what the mountains keep on the other side."

I let that stand. It was the thesis statement of the whole expedition, and she'd delivered it on day one, which told me she'd rehearsed it, which told me she was nervous. Good. Nervous people check their knots twice, and I'd already watched her check hers twice.

We climbed out of the fjordland through forest so dense the trail existed mostly as rumor — blazes on trunks, mud the color of old tea, the constant sound of water going somewhere else. By midmorning we'd gained the first shoulder of the range and I made us stop where the trees began to thin, because I wanted her to see it.

"Look west," I said. "Then look east. Then tell me why anyone lives on the west side."

She looked. To the west: cloud, forest, water, green stacked on green like a debt compounding. To the east: nothing. A brown so vast and dry it didn't read as landscape so much as absence — the steppe running out to the horizon in long pale folds, treeless, going gold at the edges where the grass had given up for the season.

"That's the rain shadow," I said. "The mountains take everything out of the clouds before they get across. The wind comes off the Pacific carrying a river, and by the time it clears these peaks it's carrying dust. Everything on that side lives on what's left over. Which is to say: almost nothing."

Elena was quiet a moment. "It looks like another planet."

"It looks like the truth," I said. "Everything else is decoration."

* * *

We dropped down the eastern flank that afternoon, and the transition happened faster than it ever does in photographs. There's no border. No sign saying WET ENDS HERE. There's just a moment, somewhere on the second day, when you realize the mud has become dust, that the moss has become lichen, that the sound under your boots has changed from a suck to a crunch. The forest quits all at once, like a man walking out of a meeting, and then it's ñire scrub and coirón grass and wind.

I have made this transition maybe forty times in my life, and it never stops feeling like a door closing behind you.

"Tell me about the water," Elena said, on the third day, when the first guanaco appeared — a lone female watching us from a ridge with the contempt only guanacos can manage.

"Water on the dry side is a debt," I said. "Everything owes somebody. The estancias own the springs. The springs own the trails. The guanacos know every one of them and will die before telling you. You plan your days around water the way other people plan around money."

"And you know where it is."

"I know where it was. Water moves. Springs silt up, get fenced, get claimed. Fifteen years is a long time for a map to stay honest." I said it lightly, but she caught the edge in it, because Elena caught everything. Fifteen years since I'd guided this country. Fifteen years since the last time I'd been responsible for another human being's water, and her food, and her life, and her route home.

"You're worried," she said.

"I'm recalculating."

"Those aren't different things."

"No," I admitted. "They're not."

She didn't push. That was the second thing I'd learned about her: she collected information the way the steppe collected dust — patiently, everywhere, all at once — but she knew when to close the notebook. Historians, maybe. Or maybe just her.

* * *

The steppe proper took us on the fourth day.

I want to be fair to it, because I've spent half my life out there and I'm tired of people calling it empty. The dry side isn't empty. It's austere. It's a country that has decided, with total conviction, what it is willing to support, and it will not be argued with. The grass grows in gray-green tussocks a meter apart, each one defending its circle of ground like a tenant with a lease. The sky is enormous — bigger than the wet side's sky, because there's nothing to interrupt it, no trees, no ridgeline clutter, just cloud and distance and the occasional condor turning a circle so wide it takes ten minutes to complete.

And the wind. Everyone warns you about the wind and nobody believes them. It comes off the ice fields to the west, pours over the passes, and hits the steppe with nothing left to slow it. It blows at you for days. It blows the words out of your mouth. By the second day on open ground Elena had stopped trying to hold conversations on the move and started writing in her notebook with her body curved around the pages like a mother around a child.

"¡El viento!" she shouted once, apropos of nothing, and laughed — actually laughed, bent double in it, her hair whipping across her face. "It's like being argued with by a continent!"

I found myself smiling. I killed the smile. There was no future in it. She was a client. Clients were cargo. Cargo got you killed, or got you blamed, or both — usually both, if the cargo had a famous name and the guide had a famous disgrace. I put my head down and walked.

But I'll tell you what I noticed, because I notice everything, it's the curse of the profession: Elena Vasquez-Roth was tough.

Not fit, exactly — though she was fitter than her soft hands and city boots had advertised. Tough. There's a difference. Fitness is a body's capacity. Toughness is a will's. On the second day, when the trail climbed through a field of broken basalt and I offered to take some of her weight, she said no with a flatness that closed the subject. On the third day, when her boots — beautiful boots, wrong boots, museum-quality boots — had raised blisters on both heels, she said nothing at all, and I only found out because I saw the way she was walking.

That was the limp.

She was hiding it. Badly. She'd shifted her gait to compensate, shortening the stride on the left, favoring the outside of the foot, and she'd done it gradually enough that she probably thought she was getting away with it. She was not getting away with it. I had spent fifteen years reading terrain, and terrain includes people. A limp on day three of a twenty-day crossing is not a detail. It's a fuse.

I said nothing. Not because I was kind, but because I was calculating. If I raised it, she'd deny it, and then we'd both be liars, and the whole expedition would rest on two lies instead of one. Better to watch. Better to know. Better to adjust the route in my head — shave the day six climb, reroute the descent — without telling her I'd done it.

That was the third thing I learned about her: she would make me complicit in her concealments, and I would let her, and we would both pretend this was a professional arrangement.

* * *

We camped that night in a hollow below a ridge of red scree, out of the worst of the wind, with a view east across the steppe going violet as the light died. I got the stove going — white gas, the small blue flame hissing like something alive — and we ate rehydrated something that claimed to be pasta, and Elena got out her notebook and worked by headlamp while I studied my maps.

"Ask me," she said suddenly, not looking up.

"Ask you what?"

"Whatever you've been not-asking me for four days. You have a face like a locked drawer, Wendell. Ask."

I turned the map over. Turned it back. "Your leg," I said. "The left one. What happened to it?"

Her pen stopped.

"It's an old fracture," she said, after a while. "Tibia. I was twenty-six. A fall — nothing dramatic, no story worth telling. It healed wrong, and sometimes, on long days, it reminds me it's there."

"Every day is a long day out here."

"I'm aware."

"It changes the arithmetic. If it worsens—"

"It won't."

"You don't know that. Nobody knows that. That's the whole problem with bodies." I heard my own voice harden and didn't entirely correct it. "I need to know what I'm carrying, Elena. That's not cruelty. That's the job."

She looked at me then, across the stove flame, and her face did something complicated — pride and exhaustion fighting for the same ground.

"My grandfather," she said, "walked out of these mountains in 1979 with a broken radio and frostbitten feet, and nobody believed a word he said for the rest of his life. He died in 1998, and the last thing he asked me was whether I'd found the proof yet. I was nineteen. I said I would. I have spent eleven years saying I would." She closed the notebook. "So yes. My leg hurts. And yes, I will walk on it until the job is done, and if you want to turn back over a limp, then you should have hired a healthier historian."

The wind came over the ridge and flattened the flame and we both sat in the sudden dark, and I thought: there it is. Not a client. A woman with a debt of her own, walking on a broken leg to pay it.

"Day six," I said finally. "There's a climb. I can reroute around it. It adds half a day."

"I don't need—"

"It adds half a day," I said again, "and I've already added it. You don't have to tell me when it hurts. Just don't lie to me when I ask. That's the deal. That's the whole deal."

She was quiet long enough that I thought she was going to argue. Then: "That's fair."

"That's the deal," I said, and turned back to my maps, and felt something in my chest I refused to name, because naming it would have meant admitting that for the first time in fifteen years I was responsible for someone again — and that some buried, unforgivable part of me was glad.

* * *

On the fifth day the land changed again, and I understood, watching Elena see it, why I'd never been able to leave this country for good.

We'd come down off the scree onto a plain of yellow grass running unbroken to a horizon so distant it curved. No fences. No trails. Just the wind combing the coirón in long silver waves, like something breathing under the ground. And in the middle distance, a line of darker green — a river valley, invisible until you were almost on top of it, cut thirty meters down into the plain, a secret the steppe kept from the sky.

Elena stopped walking.

"It's alive," she said.

"It's what?"

"The land. Everyone calls it dead — the desert, the wasteland, the empty side. But it's not dead, it's just..." She gestured at the grass, the waves, the hidden river. "It's withholding. It knows exactly what it's doing."

I looked at the plain and I thought: yes. That was the dry side exactly. A country that gave you nothing you hadn't earned, kept every secret in a cut valley or a fenced spring, and watched you the whole time with the patience of something that had outlasted every expedition ever sent into it — including, possibly, one in 1979, and including, if I wasn't careful, this one.

"Come on," I said. "The valley's got water. And water out here is a debt."

"A debt to whom?"

"To whoever got here first," I said, and started down toward the green line, and did not tell her that as we crested the last rise before the descent, I had looked back — the way I always look back, the habit of a woman who once lost someone by not looking back — and seen, far to the south, at the very edge of the haze, a smudge of dust that might have been wind.

Might have been.
