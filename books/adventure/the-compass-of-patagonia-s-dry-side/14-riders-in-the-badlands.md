## 14. Riders in the Badlands

The waterhole was a lie told by a map and believed by two tired women.

I'd marked it on my own hand-drawn chart years ago — a blue smudge in a fold of badlands where the terrain looked like God had started sculpting something ambitious and then lost interest. Eroded clay towers, ribs of sandstone, gullies that went nowhere with great conviction. In a wet year the hollows might hold water for a week after rain. This had not been a wet year.

We found it at midday, Elena limping worse than she admitted and me pretending not to notice, and the blue smudge turned out to be a crust of alkali over damp mud. Not nothing. You can dig a seep hole in alkali mud, let it fill, let it settle, drink it filtered through your bandana and pretend it doesn't taste like the inside of an old boot. I've drunk worse. I once drank out of a hoofprint in the Chubut steppe because the alternative was not drinking, and I'm still here to be wry about it.

So we dug. Elena worked the trowel while I rigged the filter, and neither of us talked much, because by day nine of a crossing you save your words like coins, spending them only when necessary.

That's why I heard them before I saw them. Or rather, heard the absence — the birdsong stopping, the way it does when something large moves through country that knows better than to sing.

"Company," I said.

Elena's head came up. "How many?"

I listened. Hooves, more than one animal, moving at a walk, which is either polite or confident. "Three riders. Maybe four."

She stood, brushing alkali off her knees, and looked at me with a question she didn't ask out loud. We both knew who rode this country now. We'd been watching dust plumes on the southern horizon for three days, paralleling us, patient as weather. Marcus Hale didn't hurry. Men with permits and lawyers and money never have to hurry. They just arrive.

"Keep digging," I said. "Water first. Whatever happens, we leave here with water."

She knelt back down without argument, and I'll confess something: watching her work that trowel, steady and uncomplimentary about the taste of the mud, I felt a fierce and inconvenient pride that had no business showing up in the middle of what was about to happen. I put it away. There'd be time for feelings later, if there was a later, and if later turned out to include feelings.

* * *

They came out of a gully to the northeast, single file, riding the way gauchos ride — like they were part of the horse and mildly bored by the arrangement. Three of them. Two young, one older, faces leathered into topographic maps of their own. The lead rider wore a canvas jacket with a logo stitched on the breast: a stylized compass rose and the word *AUSTRAL*, which was Hale's outfit, because of course it was. The man couldn't even send his scouts out anonymously. Branding. That was always Hale's religion — make sure everything you touch announces you.

I walked out to meet them. Not because I wanted to, but because there's a rule in the badlands: you meet riders at a distance, on neutral ground, standing up, with your hands visible. You don't let them ride into your camp while you're crouched over a waterhole looking exactly as vulnerable as you are.

"Afternoon," I said, when they were close enough to hear.

The lead rider reined in. He looked at me for a long moment, and I watched him do the arithmetic — woman, fifties, sunburned, gear worn but maintained, standing like someone who'd done this before. I watched him fail to place me, and then watch him decide it didn't matter.

"You're camped on Estancia La Fortuna," he said. His Spanish had the flat vowels of the interior. "This is private land."

"It's a waterhole," I said. "In badlands. I've seen cattle claim territory with more paperwork."

One of the younger riders smiled before he could stop himself. The lead rider didn't.

"The estancia leases exploration rights," he said. "To Austral Expediciones. Señor Hale has permits from the provincial government covering this whole drainage." He said *Señor Hale* the way some men say *the bishop*. "You're trespassing on his claim."

"I wasn't aware claims worked that way," I said. "I thought claims were for mining."

"They're for whatever Señor Hale says they're for." He shifted in the saddle, and his horse sidestepped, and behind him the other two sat very still in that particular way of men who are paid to be scenery until they aren't. "He asks that you turn south at the next ridge. There's a track. It will take you around."

"Around his claim."

"Around everything he has an interest in."

"And how far does his interest extend?"

"All the way to the cordillera," the rider said, and there it was — the whole shape of the thing, delivered flat as a receipt. Hale hadn't just been paralleling us. He'd bought the ground under our route. Every drainage, every pass approach, every waterhole between here and the mountains, wrapped up in leases and permits and whatever handshake arrangements he'd made with estancia owners who'd never refused money in their lives.

Behind me, I heard Elena stop digging.

"How much did all that cost?" I asked.

The rider almost laughed. Almost. "More than you have," he said, not cruelly, which somehow made it worse. It was just information. A fact about the world, like rainfall.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being blacklisted: it teaches you a very specific kind of patience. When every official door in two countries closes in your face, you learn to read the small print of other people's power. You learn what a permit looks like, what a lease means, whose signature outweighs whose. Fifteen years of being the name that gets flagged taught me more about bureaucracy than any lawyer ever learned about mountains.

So I knew exactly what that rider was telling me, and exactly how little of it was bluff.

Hale had spent real money fencing off the dry side. Not with wire — with paper. And paper, in Patagonia, is stronger than wire. A fence you can cut. A permit you can't.

"Tell Señor Hale," I said, "that we'll take the track around his interest."

The rider nodded, satisfied, and turned his horse.

"And tell him—" I paused, and the pause did its work; all three riders turned back. "Tell him the Whitlock girl says hello."

The change in the lead rider's face was worth the price of admission. It started as confusion, moved through recalculation, and arrived somewhere near alarm. Because everyone in this business knew the story of the Whitlock avalanche, and everyone knew the name, and none of them had expected it to come out of the mouth of the sunburned woman by the waterhole.

"Whitlock," he repeated.

"The one and only. Blacklisted in Chile and Argentina, if that helps. Ask your señor — he knew me when I still had a reputation." I smiled, and I made sure it was the smile that clients used to complain about, the one that meant I was about to say something true. "And tell him this: paper fences are like snow bridges. They hold fine right up until they don't."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Somewhere behind me, water dripped slowly into our seep hole, patient as anything.

"We're not here to make trouble," the lead rider said finally, and I noticed his hand had drifted to rest on his thigh, near but not on the knife at his belt. Not a threat. An availability. "Señor Hale wants no one hurt. He said to tell you that too, if we found you. He said you'd understand what he meant."

"Oh, I understood it the first time," I said. "Fifteen years ago. Tell him his message arrived long ago and I've been living in it ever since."

They turned and rode out the way they'd come, single file down the gully, and the birds waited a decent interval and then started up again, embarrassed at having doubted themselves.

* * *

Elena had the water boiling over a twig fire when I got back to the hole, which was either optimism or defiance, and with Elena those were usually the same thing.

"So," she said. "We're trespassing."

"On paper, yes."

"On paper that covers every drainage to the cordillera." She poured water over the grounds, and the smell rose up, absurdly domestic against the alkali flats. "Wendell. If he owns the approaches—"

"He doesn't own the high country. Nobody owns the high country. Above a certain line it's all provincial reserve and national park, and permits don't reach up there because no lawyer wants to hike." I took the cup she handed me. "But he owns everything below it. Every valley floor. Every track. Every estancia that could sell us food or put us up for a night."

"Then we stay high."

"For days. Maybe a week. On rations calculated for valleys." I drank the coffee, which tasted like punishment, which is to say like coffee. "He's not trying to catch us, Elena. You understand that? If he wanted to catch us, he'd have sent those riders yesterday, or tomorrow, or with ten men instead of three. He's herding us. He wants us up high, cold, hungry, burning calories we don't have, so that when we finally come down — if we come down — we come down slow and stupid, right where he's waiting."

Elena considered this. She had a way of considering things that made you feel like the thing itself should be flattered. "Or," she said, "he's bluffing. Leases on land he'll never develop, permits for a route he hasn't found yet, all of it theater to scare two women off a trail."

"Do you believe that?"

"No," she said. "But I wanted to hear you say it wasn't true, so I'd know how scared to be."

"That's a lot of emotional labor to assign to a person holding a tin cup."

"You're the guide," she said. "Scared is your department. I just fund it."

Despite everything — despite the riders and the leases and the arithmetic already running in the back of my skull like a meter in a taxi — I laughed. It came out cracked from disuse, but it came out. Elena looked pleased with herself in the specific way of a woman who has just discovered she can make a mountain guide laugh and intends to abuse the discovery.

* * *

We broke camp an hour later, carrying eleven liters of gray-tasting water between us, and climbed north out of the badlands toward the line where the clay gave up and the scree began.

I want to record, for honesty's sake, what I thought about during that climb. Not the route. Not the water math. I thought about Marcus Hale at twenty-nine, teaching me to read wind patterns off lenticular clouds, laughing at his own jokes before he finished them, calling me *muchacha* in that fake-Spanish accent he used because he thought it charmed people. I thought about the countersigned route report, his signature neat beside mine, both of us knowing what lay under that slope, and only one of us paying for it. I thought about how a man becomes what Hale became — how you don't fall all at once, you just keep making the convenient choice until convenience is all you're made of.

And then I thought about the rider's face when I said my name, and I felt something I hadn't felt in fifteen years, and it took me a mile of scree to identify it.

It was appetite.

Not fear. Not dread. Appetite — the old animal hunger of competition, the thing that used to make me race storms and out-think other guides and love every minute of it. Hale had spent a fortune trying to wall me out of these mountains, and instead of crushing me, the wall had done something else entirely. It had reminded me there was a game on.

That should have worried me. Appetite makes guides stupid; I'd seen it kill people. But there it was anyway, warm in my chest like a coal carried over from a dead fire, and I found I didn't want to put it out.

"Wendell." Elena had stopped below me, one hand on a boulder, breathing hard and hiding it badly. "You've got a look."

"What look?"

"The look of a woman about to do something the itinerary doesn't cover."

I looked west, where the badlands fell away into the golden evening and beyond them, faint as a promise, the white teeth of the cordillera. Somewhere past that line was Tomás's archive. Somewhere behind us was Hale, patient as winter. Between us and both: ridgelines no permit covered, and a season running out faster than any of us liked.

"There's a shelf," I said. "High route. My father described it once — he called it the long balcony. Nobody uses it because nobody's confirmed it in forty years, and the men who tried came back with opinions."

"Opinions?"

"Mostly that it doesn't exist. One came back without his partner, and his opinion was shorter."

Elena was quiet for a moment. Then: "How high?"

"High enough that Hale's paper burns off. High enough that the only law up there is weather and gravity, and neither of them takes bribes."

"And low enough that we freeze."

"Everything's a trade," I said. "That's the whole curriculum."

She looked at the peaks a long time. The light was going amber and then copper, and it caught the scar along her jaw, the one she'd never explained and I'd never asked about, and I thought: she'll say yes. I don't know how I knew, nine days into knowing her, but I knew it the way you know weather coming — not from evidence, from pressure.

"Show me the route," she said.

So I spread the map on a slab of sandstone, weighted the corners with rocks, and drew the line with my finger: up through the clay towers, onto the scree shoulders, then west along a contour that existed on no survey, following a description given to a girl by a dying man thirty years ago, toward a country where Marcus Hale's lawyers had never been and never would be.

Below us, far to the south, a dust plume hung in the last light, motionless, patient, certain.

Let it wait, I thought. Let him count his permits. Some things don't show up on paper — and I was about to spend the next week proving it, one impossible ledge at a time.
