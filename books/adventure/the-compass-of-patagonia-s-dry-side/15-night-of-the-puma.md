## 15. Night of the Puma

I had been keeping a list, in my head, of things I would never say to Wendell Whitlock. It was a long list. It was, by that point in the crossing, practically a novella.

*Item one:* You walk like a woman being followed.

*Item two:* When you sleep, you sleep like a soldier — on your side, boots within reach, facing the door of the tent even when the tent has no door, only a flap and a view of stars and the enormous indifferent dark of the steppe.

*Item three:* I think about your hands more than is professionally appropriate for a historian.

The list existed because saying any of these things out loud would have required a conversation, and conversations between Wendell and me were conducted in a language we were both still learning: half English, half Spanish, half silence. Yes, I know that's three halves. The arithmetic of that camp never added up either. We were two women, four weeks of rations stretched across six, one tent pitched with the optimism of people who had not yet met the weather, and somewhere behind us — two valleys, maybe three — Marcus Hale moving through the country like a debt coming due.

That night we made camp in a shallow fold of ground below a ridge of red scree, because Wendell said the fold would break the wind, and because by then I had stopped asking how she knew things and started simply believing her, which was either trust or laziness or love wearing a field jacket. We ate noodles with the last of the dried peppers. She told me a story about a client she'd once guided who insisted on bringing a full set of encyclopedias across the steppe because he "didn't trust local knowledge." She did an impression of him. It was devastating. I laughed until my ribs hurt, which I noted, distantly, was a sensation I'd been having more often around her, and which I filed under *do not examine.*

Then the horses — no, we had no horses; then the wind changed.

*

You learn to hear a place before you can read it. My grandfather taught me that, or rather his journal taught me, secondhand, decades after his voice had gone wherever voices go. *The country speaks first,* he wrote, in 1979, in handwriting that got shakier as the pages turned. *Listen for the change in its grammar.*

The grammar changed at roughly eleven o'clock. The guanacos that had been drifting along the ridge all evening — casual, bored, chewing their way through eternity — went still. All of them at once, like a radio station cutting to static. Then they flowed away westward, unhurried but total, the way water leaves a room it has decided against.

Wendell put down her spoon without any hurry at all. That was the first thing I noticed: the absence of hurry. She tilted her head, listening, and I watched her do something I had only ever seen described in old expedition accounts — I watched her *read*, the way I read archives, except her archive was the dark itself.

"Puma," she said.

My spine performed a small architectural renovation. "Here? There's nothing here. There's no — there isn't anything for it to eat."

"There's us."

"Thank you. That's very comforting."

"It wasn't meant to be." She was already moving, low, unhurried, feeding another branch onto the fire and then, deliberately, knocking down the little rock wall we'd built to shield the flames. The fire leapt up, doubled its light. "They don't want trouble. They want an easy question answered. Is this thing dangerous or not." She glanced at me, and there was something in her face I hadn't seen before — not fear, not bravado. Attention. Total, undivided, almost tender attention, aimed outward at the night. "So we answer it."

"And what's the answer?"

"That we're expensive."

*

I want to be honest about what happened next, because this is a book about honesty, or it became one, though at the time I thought it was a book about maps.

The puma came to the edge of the firelight maybe twenty minutes later. I never saw it arrive. One moment the darkness beyond the flames was just darkness, and the next it had a shape in it — low, tawny, liquid, a suggestion more than an animal, the way a rumor is more frightening than a fact. Its eyes caught the light and threw it back flat and green.

Every instinct I owned screamed at me to run, which, as Wendell had explained over dinner earlier that week with grim humor, is precisely the wrong answer to give a cat. Instead I sat very still and watched Wendell stand up.

She stood the way she does everything — without wasted motion, without theater. She didn't shout. She didn't wave her arms or throw anything. She simply stood, made herself tall and slow, and began to speak. Not to me. To the puma. A low, steady stream of Spanish, conversational, almost bored, the tone you'd use with a neighbor who'd wandered into your garden.

"You're a long way from the cliffs," she said to it. "Nothing down here for you but bad noodles and worse company. Go on. Go on, vieja. This isn't your night."

And the extraordinary thing — the thing I have thought about every day since, the thing I will think about when I am old and lying in some comfortable bed in Buenos Aires inventing this whole story into better shape — is that the puma *listened*. Or seemed to. It held there at the edge of the light for a long minute, weighing us, running whatever calculus cats run, and Wendell stood in the firelight talking to it like a landlady, and I understood suddenly and completely that I was looking at the guide. Not the disgraced woman, not the blacklist, not the file with the countersigned report that had ended her career. The guide. The one Tomás must have followed anywhere. The one clients must have trusted with their lives and been right to.

The animal turned, unhurried, offended perhaps, and poured itself back into the dark. The guanacos' grammar resumed some minutes later, farther off.

Wendell sat back down, picked up her spoon, and said, "Cold now," as if nothing whatsoever had occurred.

*

"You talk to them," I said, later, when my heart had climbed down out of my throat and reinstalled itself somewhere near my stomach.

"They talk first. I'm just answering." She was banking the fire, building it up for the night watch. "Tomás was better at it than me. He used to say they could hear a lie in your voice. You couldn't bluff a puma. You had to actually not be afraid, or at least be afraid of the right thing."

"Were you afraid?"

She considered this with the seriousness she gave all questions, which is one of the hundred things on my list. "Of the right thing, yes."

"Which is?"

"That it wouldn't be alone." She looked at me then, and the firelight did something unfair to her face. "A mother with cubs doesn't ask questions. But that one was old. Solo. Just checking the ledger." She shrugged. "We're in everyone's ledger out here. Guanacos, pumas, Hale. Everything wants to know what you cost."

"And what do we cost?"

"More than we look," she said, and smiled, and I had to look away at the fire because there are limits to what a person can witness without consequences.

*

I did not sleep. I want that on the record. I lay in my bag listening to the wind work the scree above us and thinking about the shape of her standing between me and the dark, and I conducted a rigorous internal seminar on why this was unprofessional, impractical, ill-timed, and quite possibly doomed, and the seminar concluded unanimously that none of these findings mattered in the slightest.

Because here is what fifteen years of archival work teaches you that no one warns you about: documents lie, but patterns don't. And the pattern was undeniable. It was in the way she carried my pack over the salares without mentioning it. In the way she'd started telling me stories — real ones, ones with her brother alive in them — instead of the polished deflections she'd offered in Comodoro. In the way that when I limped, she slowed down, and pretended she hadn't, and I pretended I hadn't noticed her pretending, and this elaborate mutual fiction was somehow the most honest conversation either of us had managed in years.

Love arrives sideways. Nobody writes that in the expedition accounts because nobody survives the admission. It doesn't come through the front gate with banners. It comes in through the kitchen window while you're busy watching the horizon for rivals, and by the time you notice it, it's already made itself tea and learned where everything is kept.

Somewhere past midnight I heard her shift outside the tent, on watch, and I heard her murmur something — to the fire, to the country, to Tomás, I don't know — and I thought: *my grandfather walked out of this range with a survivor's name in his radio log, and I walked into it with a liar's secret in my pack, and neither of us ever planned to fall in love with the terrain.*

But the terrain doesn't care what you planned. The terrain just asks what you cost.

*

Near dawn I finally slept, and dreamed of the cave at Puerto Deseado, the carvings, the initials T.W. cut into stone by a man the world had buried twice. In the dream Wendell was reading the wall aloud in her low firelight voice, translating, and every line she translated was addressed to her, and I woke with wet eyes and blamed the wind, which is the oldest lie on the steppe.

When I crawled out, she had coffee going — real coffee, hoarded, saved, produced now without comment — and she handed me the cup and looked east, where the sky was doing its extravagant pink thing over the badlands, and she said:

"We need to talk numbers today. Days, food, distance." A pause. Her jaw did the thing it does when she's decided something and hasn't yet admitted it to herself. "I've been avoiding the arithmetic. That's not like me."

"No?" I said, wrapping both hands around the cup, watching her profile against the sunrise, knowing — with the certainty of a historian who has spent her life recognizing the moment a record turns — that whatever number she was about to calculate, we were already past the point where either of us would have accepted the sensible answer.

"No," she said. "And I think you're going to vote forward anyway."

She was right. But neither of us knew yet what forward would cost.
