## 16. The Dry Season Ledger

I did the arithmetic by firelight, the way my father taught me, which is to say honestly and with a pencil I was already resenting.

The ledger itself was nothing but a page torn from Elena's notebook, ruled into columns in her tidy historian's hand before she'd turned in. Rations down one side, days across the top. My job was to fill in the numbers without lying to them, and lying to numbers is harder than lying to people — they don't flatter you back.

Here is what the pencil said.

We had walked eleven days out of Comodoro Rivadavia. Eleven days of steppe and badlands and three days of salt flat that had eaten Elena's boots and half her reserves of patience. We had lost two days at the collapsed canyon — the pass that wasn't — and gained nothing back since, because the country east of it had decided to be generous with distance and stingy with everything else. The second cave had cost us most of a day, and it had given us Tomás's journal, and the coordinates for the archive, and a final page torn out like a promise someone else intended to keep.

The archive lay four days' hard travel west, maybe five if the moraine country was as bad as Tomás's survey suggested. His handwriting got smaller there, the way handwriting does when a man is writing cold.

Behind us: six days to the estancia, eight to anything resembling a road, assuming weather.

Ahead of us: snow.

I looked at the peaks while I thought about it. They'd been white-capped since the canyon detour, but that was cosmetic snow, decoration, the kind that burns off by noon. Three nights ago the white had come down below the rock bands and stayed. Last night it had reached the scree. That's not decoration anymore. That's a door beginning to close, slowly, at the speed of geology until suddenly at the speed of weather.

The dry season on the leeward side runs October through early December, mostly, and "mostly" is a word that has killed better guides than me. It was the last week of November. Every year the front comes a little different — some years it holds until Christmas, some years it arrives like a creditor. You plan for the creditor.

So: four days out. Six days minimum to get back over this ground with tired bodies and short rations. Ten days against a margin I'd normally call three, four if I was feeling lucky, and I have never once in my life felt lucky. Fifteen years ago I felt *competent*, which is a different animal and a much more dangerous one, and the mountain ate my brother for it.

The pencil hovered over the column.

There was a third number I hadn't written down yet, because writing it would make it real. It was the number that said: you cannot do both. You cannot reach the archive and come back. Not both before the snows. The math didn't allow it. The math hadn't allowed it for two days, probably, and I'd been carrying the page around like it might change its mind if I fed it enough firelight.

One-way or turn around. Winter on the far side, or walk home empty-handed with a journal full of coordinates we'd never use, because Hale had the second cave's contents and all the time in the world, and empty-handed Whitlock returning empty-handed Vasquez-Roth would be a footnote in his victory lap.

I fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch.

Elena was asleep sitting up, the way she'd learned to on the salares — back against a boulder, knees drawn, hands folded like a woman waiting in a train station. Even asleep she looked like she was taking notes. Her limp had gotten worse since the salt; she hid it well in motion and badly at rest, and tonight she'd sat down the way you sit when your body has filed a formal complaint. She'd carried her own pack anyway. I'd stopped offering to take it after the flats, not because she refused — she never refused, refusing would be an admission — but because carrying it silently had become its own kind of sentence between us, and sentences like that are how the two of us talk.

*Tomás,* I thought, *you absolute bastard.* Four days further into the driest country on the wet side of the world's driest rain shadow, and he'd picked a spot behind a collapsed moraine that no one could reach in a season that was ending. He'd had years to choose somewhere sensible. He'd chosen somewhere that required faith, or stubbornness, or a death wish with cartographic ambitions. All three ran in the family, so I couldn't exactly fault him. I could just resent him, which I did, thoroughly, and then immediately felt guilty about, because the man had died alone in a fissure waiting for someone who took fifteen years to show up.

The wind came off the western peaks and moved through the coiron grass in a long sigh, the sound the dry side makes when it's thinking.

I woke her because she'd want to be woken. That's another thing we'd learned about each other: neither of us can stand being protected from information.

"Ledger's done," I said.

She surfaced the way she always did — instantly, completely, no groggy middle ground. Historian's reflex. She looked at the page in my hand and then at my face, and whatever she saw there made her sit up straighter.

"It's bad," she said. Not a question.

"It's arithmetic." I held it where the firelight hit it. "Four days to the archive. Six back, minimum, and that's if nothing goes wrong, and something always goes wrong — I've got a professional reputation built on things going wrong. The snow line's dropping maybe two hundred meters every three days. We're ten days from safe and we have nine."

"So we're one day short."

"We're one day short *today*. Tomorrow we'll be two." I turned the page toward her. "There's no version of this where we see the archive and sleep again on this side of the range before winter. There's the version where we go through, and the version where we go home."

She read the columns for a long time. Longer than the numbers deserved. She wasn't reading numbers; she was reading what the numbers meant, the way she read everything — looking for the document underneath the document.

"Wintering on the far side," she said finally. "What does that actually mean?"

"It means we find the archive, we take what we can carry, and then we descend the western drainage until we find timberline and shelter, and we stay there until the passes open. February, maybe March. Four months. On whatever food we're carrying plus whatever the country gives us, which on the far side means fish if we can find a lake, and huemul if we're very good, and hunger if we're honest."

"And the storm risk going in?"

"Real. But storms going in you survive. Storms coming back you die tired." I crouched by the fire and drew a line in the dirt with a stick — the range, the two sides, our little mark east of the divide. "Going forward, every day takes us lower into timber, into water, into country where you can build a roof. Turning back, every day takes us higher into scree and open ground with the season closing behind us like a trap. People think retreat is the safe choice. Out here, retreat is often just dying in the direction you came from."

"That's cheerful."

"I'm a guide. Cheerful is extra."

She almost smiled. Then she looked west, at the peaks holding their new snow like a verdict, and I watched her do the same arithmetic I'd done, except in her currency. Hers wasn't food and meters. Hers was a grandfather's last transmission, a survivor nobody believed, a book she'd crossed an ocean to write and a family silence she'd crossed a continent to break.

"My grandmother used to say," Elena said quietly, "that the worst thing you can do with a door is stand in front of it deciding. Because while you decide, it's closing either way."

"Eloquent. Also a way of avoiding the actual decision."

"No." She turned back to me, and the firelight did something to her face I didn't have a name for yet — I've been slow about naming things lately, deliberately slow, the way you walk slow past a dog you want to trust. "The actual decision is easy. I'm telling you mine now so you can argue with it properly instead of pretending to weigh it later. I'm going forward. Alone if I have to. I'd rather not, because alone I'll die of the limp inside three days, and also because—" she paused, and rerouted, the way she does when a sentence gets too close to something, "—because you're the only person alive who knows this country, and I intend to exploit that shamelessly."

"You realize what you're asking. Four months. No rescue. No road. If your leg goes bad—"

"If my leg goes bad, you'll carry me or you'll leave me, and either way that's a problem for December." She said it flatly, without drama, and somehow that was worse than drama. "Wendell. I spent six years finding you. Do you think I flew across an ocean to stop one day short?"

"One day short today. Two tomorrow."

"Then we should start walking."

I should say here, for the record, for the ledger, for whoever finds this account and wonders how two reasonably intelligent women talked themselves into wintering above the snow line of the Patagonian dry side: it wasn't courage. Courage is a story people tell afterward. What it actually was, was simpler and more terrible. It was that turning back had become, somewhere in the last eleven days, unthinkable — not because of the archive, though the archive mattered, and not because of Hale, though Hale mattered, but because of the small accumulating weight of shared fires and carried packs and the particular quality of silence between two people who have stopped performing for each other. Turning back meant walking away from the only direction my life had pointed in fifteen years. And I had learned, at great cost, what happened when I let other people's timetables make my decisions.

But I still made her hear the whole cost first. That much of the old guide survived in me.

"Say it back to me," I said. "All of it. So I know you understand."

She said it back. One-way crossing. Winter on the western slope. Rations cut to two-thirds immediately. The limp reported honestly every evening, no heroics, because heroics on the far side of the divide get people buried in spring thaw with a note in their pocket. If one of us went down permanently, the other continued or turned back per her own judgment, no debt, no argument. And the photographs — the memory card sewn into her jacket lining, the whole expedition reduced to a stitched seam — got checked twice daily like ammunition.

"Agreed," she finished. "And you?"

"What about me?"

"Your terms. You always have terms. It's how you keep the world at arm's length."

I looked at her. The fire had burned down to that red, honest light that shows people as they are.

"My term," I said, "is that you stop calling it arm's length. It's a compass length. There's a difference."

"Is there?"

"Not anymore," I said, before I could stop myself, and then we both sat there in the wind with that, neither of us picking it up and neither of us putting it down.

* * *

We broke camp in the dark, because the ledger said every hour belonged to the snow now, and hours were the only currency left that mattered.

The steppe at four in the morning is a country of gray suggestions. The coiron grass silvered under a thinning moon, and the peaks ahead had gone from white-capped to white-shouldered overnight, the way a man goes gray in a photograph you look at twice. I set a pace that was honest rather than heroic — fast enough to buy us margin, slow enough that Elena's leg wouldn't file for divorce by midmorning — and she matched it without complaint, which from Elena was practically a love letter.

By full light we'd left the last reliable water behind. I marked it in my head the way my father marked things: *spring, east-facing, flows till first hard freeze, then nothing.* After this, every liter we drank was a liter carried, and every gram carried was a minute of margin spent. The country changed under our boots too — softer now, rising toward the divide, the grass giving way to ñire scrub and then to bare ground with bones in it, guanaco skeletons bleached and arranged by wind into poses that looked deliberate, like the steppe was curating an exhibition about everyone who'd ever misjudged it.

Around noon we found the first marker.

Not one of Tomás's — older, cruder, a cairn with a rusted tin sheet nailed to it, hammered flat and scratched with a date: 1979. Corvera's route. My brother had walked this exact ground, thirty years before me, heading the same direction with the same clock running out behind him. I stood there longer than the schedule allowed, and Elena, who understood schedules better than anyone I've ever traveled with, stood there with me and said nothing, which was its own kind of gift.

"He was faster than us," I said finally. "Younger. No limp."

"He was also alone," Elena said. "Alone is fast. It isn't better."

We walked on. In the afternoon the wind rose, the west wind, the one that carries the smell of snow even before there's any to smell, and I caught myself checking the sky every few minutes like a woman counting money she can feel running out of her pocket. The clouds building over the divide weren't storm clouds yet. They were the *idea* of storm clouds, the rough draft. By morning they'd be edited.

It was near dusk, with the light going copper and long across the grass, that Elena stopped walking and pointed south.

"Dust," she said.

I'd seen it twenty minutes earlier and said nothing, hoping it was wind off a dry lakebed. It wasn't. It was vehicle dust, organized, moving with purpose along the distant track that paralleled our route — the track we'd abandoned days ago to shake them. Except they hadn't been shaken. They'd been patient. Hale's team, or part of it, rolling toward the second cave with permits and lawyers and all the time in the world, and between us and them nothing but forty miles of open ground.

They hadn't seen us. Not yet. But they were between us and retreat now — or close enough that retreat had just gotten complicated in ways the ledger hadn't planned for.

Elena looked at the dust, then at me, and I watched her arrive at the same conclusion I did, arriving the way she always arrived at conclusions: quickly, completely, and with her chin up.

"Well," she said. "That settles the vote."

Behind us, the dust. Ahead of us, the snow. And somewhere in the high country between, a fissure behind a collapsed moraine, and a missing page, and a brother who had waited years for an answer that was now four days out and closing.

I tightened the straps on my pack and turned west.

"There's no path back through that," I said. "So we'd better make sure the way forward is worth it."

The first flakes wouldn't come for two more days. But the wind already knew, and it walked beside us the whole afternoon, whispering the ledger's final entry: *no returns.*
