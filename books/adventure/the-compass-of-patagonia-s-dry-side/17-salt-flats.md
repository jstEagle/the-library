## 17. Salt Flats

The salares began the way bad ideas usually do: gradually, then all at once.

One hour we were walking on earth — actual earth, with its gravel and its tufts of coirón grass and its honest, forgiving irregularity. The next, the ground had turned white and flat as a page no one had written on yet, and the horizon dissolved into a glare so complete that I stopped being able to tell where the land ended and the sky began. We were walking inside an eggshell. Inside the light itself.

"Three days," Wendell said, squinting east, then south, then east again, checking her bearing against a sun that seemed to come from everywhere at once. "Maybe three and a half if the crust is soft."

"And if it's soft?"

"Then we find out what the phrase 'post-holing through sodium chloride' means in practice." She adjusted her hat brim. "It means we walk slower and swear more."

I had read about the salares, of course. I had read everything about this country — that was my profession, my obsession, my particular form of cowardice: to know a place thoroughly on paper before trusting it with my body. The dry side of the Andes was famous for these salt pans, these vast blinding floors where ancient lakes had evaporated down to their final confession. On paper they were beautiful. On paper they were traversable.

On paper, I noted now, there had been no mention of the way the light came off them like a physical blow.

We walked.

* * *

By noon of the first day I had learned three things about the salares that no archive contained.

First: the mirages are not occasional. They are constant, patient, and cruelly inventive. I saw water perhaps forty times that morning — silver sheets of it lying seductively on the white ahead, trembling with promise, complete with reflections of clouds that did not exist. Each time, the water retreated as we approached, maintaining a fixed and mocking distance, like a rumor that keeps just ahead of confirmation.

"Don't name them," Wendell advised, when I pointed out the fourth or fifth lake. "If you name the mirage, you start negotiating with it."

"That's absurd."

"It's true. Old desert rule. You start calling them things — *the lake*, *the good water* — and pretty soon you're making deals with them. *Just to the lake and back.* The desert hears you." She spat, dryly, expertly, a skill I had not previously appreciated as a skill. "Name nothing out here."

Second: the silence has texture. On the steppe there had always been wind, birds, the small industrious sounds of a living country. Here there was nothing but our footsteps — a crisp, glassy crunch-crunch-crunch that sounded enormous in the emptiness, as though we were two insects walking across a chandelier. I found myself listening to my own breathing. I found myself hearing my own heartbeat, which is a sound I recommend only to people with very clean consciences.

Third: the salt gets everywhere. Into the boot seams, into the pack straps, into the corners of the eyes, where it dried to a fine glittering residue that made every blink feel like a small accusation. By midday my lips had begun to crack, thin lines of tightness at the corners that deepened each time I spoke. I rationed my words accordingly. This, Wendell observed with what I chose to interpret as approval rather than amusement, made me better company.

We stopped at midday in the shadow of our own packs — the only shade available in the entire universe, apparently — and ate cold food and drank warm water measured out in careful sips.

"How's the leg?" Wendell asked.

It was the first time she had asked directly. I had been waiting for the question since the rain-shadow chapter of our acquaintance, since the first evening when she'd watched me descend from the truck and said nothing, and I had known she'd seen whatever there was to see.

"Fine," I said.

"Mm," said Wendell Whitlock, who could extract more skepticism from a single syllable than most historians manage from a full footnote.

"It's an old fracture," I admitted, because lying to her had become progressively harder, and because the salt flats seemed designed to strip a person down to inventory. "Tibia. University years. A climbing fall in — it doesn't matter where. It healed badly. It's fine on flat ground. It complains on anything else."

"And today?"

"Today it's filing complaints."

She nodded, once, and said nothing else, and I understood that this too was a kind of expertise. She did not offer to slow down. She did not offer sympathy, which would have been unbearable, or advice, which would have been worse. She simply filed the information away in whatever ledger she kept behind those gray eyes, and we walked on.

I should say something here about my limp, since it will matter later, and since I have spent years pretending it doesn't exist. The truth is that I broke my leg badly at twenty-three, in the Cordillera Blanca, doing something ambitious and underprepared, and the bone set crooked by a millimeter or two — enough that long days exact a toll, enough that by evening of a hard march I walk like a woman negotiating with her own skeleton. I hid it for fifteen years because historians are not supposed to be fragile. Because my grandfather crossed this same country at fifty-eight with a radio on his back and a war in his lungs, and who was I to complain about a millimeter?

Because admitting the limp felt like admitting I might not be equal to the thing I had started.

The salares, I discovered, do not permit such accounting tricks. There is nowhere to hide a limp on a surface that is perfectly, mercilessly flat. Every favoring hitch in my stride was visible for half a mile in any direction, to anyone looking — which meant, mostly, to Wendell, who was always looking, and who never once mentioned it again.

That was its own kind of eloquence.

* * *

The second day the wind arrived.

It came off the western peaks as a hot, dry, freight-train wind — the *zonda*, Wendell called it, though she said the locals on this side had other names for it, less poetic ones. It blew steadily at first and then in gusts that leaned against us like large polite men trying to move past in a narrow corridor. The salt crust, where it was thin, lifted in white veils and swirled around our legs, so that we appeared to be wading through smoke. The temperature climbed. My lips split properly now, a small hot wire of pain at the corner of my mouth that flared every time I spoke or ate or smiled, which limited me to two of the three.

We walked bent into it, and the day became a single continuous act of putting one foot in front of the other, which I suppose is the definition of a crossing, and possibly of most lives.

It was somewhere in the middle of that afternoon that I noticed the weight.

Not my weight. My pack's absence of it.

I had been so occupied with the wind and the glare and the arithmetic of my own leg that I hadn't registered the transfer. But somewhere in the last hour — I couldn't pinpoint the moment; there had been no moment, no announcement, no transaction — the familiar drag on my shoulders had vanished. My pack rode light as a daypack. And Wendell, ahead of me, leaning into the wind with her head down and her stride metronomic, was carrying two.

"When did you—" I started.

"Don't," she said, without turning around. "Don't make it a thing."

"It *is* a thing. That's thirty pounds."

"It's twenty-six. Your scale lies."

"Wendell."

"Elena." She matched my tone exactly, a perfect archival reproduction of my own reproachful note, and I would have laughed except that laughing hurt my lip. "You can argue with me about it tonight, at camp, using energy you won't have. Right now arguing costs water."

So I shut up, and we walked, and I spent the next several hours conducting a furious internal seminar on the subject of Wendell Whitlock.

Here is what I knew about her by then, or believed I knew: that she had spent fifteen years walking away from one bad afternoon; that she measured her life in kilometers the way misers measure gold; that she trusted terrain more than people because terrain, whatever else it does, does not pretend to be your friend before it breaks your heart. Here is what I was beginning to suspect: that all of that armor had a specific function, and the function was not to keep the world out, but to keep her hands free — free, always free, to carry someone else's load without ever having to explain why.

Because that was the thing about the pack. She hadn't asked. She hadn't offered. She had simply, at some unmarked moment in a featureless white afternoon, taken it — the way you'd take a chore off a tired friend's list, quietly, without witnesses, so that no debt would need acknowledging. No debt, no gratitude, no intimacy. Except that of course it *was* intimacy, the most elementary kind. There is no more honest statement between two human beings than *I will carry some of your weight and pretend I'm not*.

My grandfather used to say that the Corvera Expedition survived as long as it did because of load-sharing — that in the mountains, affection is logistical. He wrote it in his journal, in his cramped telegraphist's hand: *"R. took half my radio batteries today without a word. This is how we say what we cannot say."* I had read that line a hundred times and thought it quaint. Walking the salares with twenty-six pounds redistributed onto another woman's shoulders, I finally understood it as engineering.

* * *

We camped that night in the lee of a salt hummock — a low ridge of crusted brine, wind-carved into something like a wave frozen mid-break. Wendell got the stove going in the sheltered pocket behind it, and we ate rehydrated something-or-other while the light died over the salar in bands of pink and violet so theatrical they seemed embarrassed to be real.

"My grandfather hated open country," I said. It was the first time I'd spoken of him aloud on the trip, and saying it felt like opening a sealed letter. "He was a radio man. Mountains, valleys — places where signals bounce, where you can hide. He said the flats were the one landscape that terrified him, because there was nowhere to crouch. Nowhere to be small."

"He wasn't wrong," Wendell said. "You can see a person on the salar from ten kilometers. Maybe farther, with glass." She said it mildly, but I caught the undertone, and I knew she was thinking what I was thinking: Hale. His team, his permits, his gauchos, his dust plumes on horizons both real and imagined. Out here we were ink marks on white paper, legible from anywhere.

"You think he's behind us?"

"I think he's somewhere," Wendell said. "On the salar, *somewhere* is every direction." She fed another pinch of fuel to the stove. "Get in the bag. Your leg needs elevation."

"My leg needs—"

"Twenty minutes above your heart. Reduces the swelling. I've carried enough limping clients to know the routine." She said it flatly, factually, and then, catching herself, added: "Sorry. That was — I didn't mean—"

"It's all right." And strangely, it was. "How many?"

"Limping clients?" She looked into the stove flame rather than at me. "Over fifteen years? Dozens. Everyone comes to Patagonia with something broken. Usually it isn't the leg." She banked the flame. "Elevate it. Doctor's orders. Well — guide's orders. Same authority, lower fees."

I got into the bag and propped my ankle on the rolled-up jacket she handed me without being asked, and lay looking up at the first stars while the wind combed the salt plain around our hummock with a sound like sand poured over silk.

"Wendell," I said, into the dark.

"Mm."

"Why did you take the pack?"

A long pause. Long enough that I thought she might not answer, might deploy one of her deflections — the weather, the route, the tactical situation. When she spoke, her voice was quiet and slightly rough, as though the words had been stored a while.

"Because you were limping," she said. "And because asking would have made it yours instead of mine. If I ask, then you have to decide whether to accept, and accepting costs you something — pride, or whatever you're hoarding it for. But if I just take it, then it's already done, and you don't owe me the decision." A pause. "It's not generosity. It's just — logistics."

"Your grandfather's journal says otherwise," I said, and heard her breath catch, faintly, in the dark. "Affection is logistical. He wrote that. About his expedition partners."

Another silence, longer. When Wendell finally answered, she said only: "Go to sleep, Elena."

But she said it gently, and in the morning the pack was still on my side of the tent, still empty of everything but my sleeping bag, and I understood that the conversation would continue in the only language either of us trusted — the language of weight borne and not discussed.

* * *

The third day the salar tried to kill us politely.

It began with the mirage to end all mirages: a lake so perfect, so detailed, so criminally beautiful that even Wendell stopped and studied it through her binoculars for a full minute. Water, real-looking water, maybe four kilometers off, ringed with what appeared to be flamingos.

"Flamingos," I said. "Mirages don't have flamingos."

"These do." She lowered the glasses. "Which means it's not a mirage. Which means it's real, and it's four kilometers off our line, and we don't have the water budget for a four-kilometer detour and back." She was quiet a moment, running numbers I couldn't see. "Unless."

"Unless?"

"Unless the spring's where your grandfather's journal says it is." She looked at me. "You've been sitting on that page since the waterhole. Don't think I haven't noticed."

I had, in fairness, been sitting on it. Somewhere in my grandfather's papers — copied into my field notebook, translated from his shorthand — was a notation I hadn't fully trusted: a mark on the eastern edge of the salar, a symbol I'd taken for a survey point but which, in context, might be something else. An eye. A source. *Agua que no se ve* — water that isn't seen.

"Two kilometers north," I said. "There's a mark. I thought it was a benchmark."

"Show me."

I showed her. She studied my notebook for a long time, longer than the information warranted, and when she looked up something in her face had shifted — some door opened a crack.

"Your grandfather walked this exact line," she said. "Seventy-some years ago, give or take. He stood where we're standing, and his leg hurt, and his lips were cracked, and somebody he loved was carrying too much weight, and he wrote down where the hidden water was so that somebody — someday — could find it." She closed the notebook and handed it back with a care that bordered on ceremony. "That's not a benchmark, Elena. That's a hand reaching across a century. Walk."

We walked north, off our line, spending water we couldn't afford on faith we could afford even less. For two hours the salar gave us nothing — white, glare, wind, the crunch of our steps, my leg complaining in a register only dogs and guides could hear. Then, at the base of a low lava rise where the salt thinned to a crusty fringe, Wendell dropped to her knees beside a shallow depression that looked like every other depression, scraped away the crust with her knife, and went still.

Water seeped up into the hole. Slow, cloudy at first, then clear. Cold, when she touched it. Real.

She sat back on her heels and laughed — actually laughed, a short rusty sound like a gate opening after years, and I thought: *there it is, there's the woman who guided before the fall, there's the laugh the blacklist froze.*

"Your grandfather," she said, filling the bottles, "is officially promoted from 'historical figure' to 'patron saint.'"

"He'd have hated that. He was an atheist."

"So am I, mostly. Doesn't stop me praying to springs." She handed me a bottle and watched me drink, and there was something in the watching — a attentiveness, a gravity — that I chose not to examine too closely, examination being dangerous to us both. "Drink slow. All of it. We just bought ourselves the rest of the crossing."

* * *

We camped that night within sight of the salar's far edge, a dark seam of basalt on the eastern horizon that promised, if nothing else, an end to the flatness. My leg had swollen despite the elevation ritual, and I lay in the tent listening to Wendell move about outside — the small competent sounds of her evening: stakes checked, rope coiled, boots knocked clean.

When she came in, she didn't lie down immediately. She sat with her map across her knees, studying it by red headlamp, and after a while she said, without looking up:

"The gaucho estancia is a day and a half past the edge. The family your grandmother remembered — the one that sheltered 'the man who walked out of the ice.'" She tapped the map. "If Tomás made it this far, they'll know. Forty years is a long time, but grandmothers keep longer ledgers than archives do."

"I know," I said. "That's why we're going."

"There's something else." She folded the map, slowly, along its worn creases. "Hale's team found the second marker cave. We know that. What we don't know is whether they found the journal — Tomás's survey journal — or whether he moved it. Whether there's a third site." She paused. "And whether the gaucho family knows where."

"You think they might."

"I think a man who lived alone in the ice for months doesn't walk out and tell strangers everything. I think he tells them one thing — the thing that matters — and asks them to hold it." She clicked off the lamp, and in the sudden dark her voice came quieter. "Get some sleep. Tomorrow we leave the salar, and after the salar, Elena, the country starts keeping secrets again."

I lay awake a while after that, my leg throbbing its dull complaint, listening to the wind die by degrees. And in the last thought before sleep, I found myself thinking not of journals or caves or rival teams, but of the weight on my shoulders all day — absent, carried elsewhere, borne silently by a woman who would deny to her grave that it meant anything at all.

My grandfather wrote: *this is how we say what we cannot say.*

Tomorrow, I decided, I would learn to say it back.
