## 20. Running the Scree

There is a particular sound a mountainside makes when it decides it doesn't like you. Not a roar, not a rumble — a whisper. A dry, granular hiss, like the steppe clearing its throat before it spits. I had heard it twice in my life before that night. Once before the avalanche that took my brother, and once on a scree slope in Aysén that I still don't talk about. That night at the estancia, listening to horses being saddled in the dark and Marcus Hale's voice pitched low and courteous through the wall, I heard it a third time, and I understood it was aimed at me.

"We go now," I said, "or we don't go at all."

Elena was already stuffing the journal — Tomás's journal, the one we'd bled three weeks to find — into a waterproof roll. Her hands were shaking, but they didn't stop. I've seen guides freeze with less cause. I've been the guide who froze. There was something in watching her that I couldn't afford to look at directly, the way you can't look straight at the sun or at your own worst year.

"They'll hear us on the gravel," she whispered.

"They'll hear us either way. Better when we're moving."

The old gaucho, Anselmo, pressed a canvas bag into my arms — hard bread, a slab of fat, a water skin. He didn't say good luck. He said, in the flat way of men who have outlived every kind of weather, "The talus is awake this year. The frost got under it in June." Then he went back inside and closed the door, and that was the whole ceremony.

We went north, because south was Hale and east was open ground you could cross on horseback, and west was the cordillera doing what cordilleras do, which is not care. North was the talus fields — miles of broken rock shed off the high shoulders of the range, stacked in slopes that obey no law but gravity's bad temper. Nobody travels the talus at night. Nobody travels the talus at all, if they can help it. Which is exactly why we did.

* * *

I want to explain what scree is to people who've only met it in photographs, because the photographs lie. Scree looks solid. It looks like a mountain wearing a coat of gravel, and you think: a coat, fine, I'll walk on the coat. But it isn't a coat. It's a argument the mountain has been having with itself for ten thousand years, and every step you take, you're asked to referee. The rock moves under you in sheets. It moves in silence until it doesn't, and then it moves all at once, a hundred tons of it, and the only thing you can do is what I learned at nineteen from my father, who learned it from a man who died learning it: keep your weight over your feet, keep three points of contact, and when the slope starts to go, go with it a little, like dancing with something that wants to kill you.

I knew all of this. I had taught all of this. What I had forgotten, in fifteen years of flat ground and long detours, was how much of it lived in my left knee.

It happened a kilometer out from the estancia, on the first big slope, where the moon came out from behind cloud just long enough to show us the bad line and dare us to take it. I stepped down on a block the size of a stove, and the block did the honest thing and rolled, and my left leg went into a gap between two others and torqued. The sound my knee made was small — a wet click, nothing cinematic — but I knew it the way you know a familiar voice on a bad telephone line. The old fracture. The one from Aysén. The one I'd told Elena about in the way you tell someone about a scar on your back: technically true, carefully framed, and never once in focus.

I went down on one hand. The rock under my palm was cold and it was moving, very slightly, the whole slope breathing.

"Wendell." Elena's hand on my shoulder. "Talk to me."

"Give me a second."

"Is it the leg?"

"It's the leg."

I got up. That's the truth and I want it on the record: I got up, and I put weight on it, and it held, the way a bridge holds with a cracked pier — technically, and with a sound like a promise being made by someone who intends to break it. We moved. Slower now. The moon went back behind its cloud as if it had seen enough.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being the strong one: it's a job, and jobs end. I had been the strong one so long I'd mistaken it for a personality. I was the one who read the terrain, who counted the water, who decided when to camp and when to run, who carried the extra weight without mentioning it. I had carried Elena's pack across the salt flats without a word, because that was the arithmetic I understood — you measure love in kilograms, in kilometers, in the things you absorb so the other person doesn't have to. It was the only ledger I trusted. My brother had taught me the columns. The mountain had audited them.

So when my knee folded the second time — two hours in, on a slope of gray shale that hissed like a kettle under our boots, and went out from under me entirely, no warning, no negotiation — the fall was not just a fall. It was a bankruptcy. I lay on my side in the dark with the shale whispering past me in little rivulets and thought, with a clarity that felt almost funny: *oh. This is what I am without the walking. Let's find out.*

"Wendell." Elena was beside me, hands on my face, turning it toward her. The moon was back, thin and mean, and in it her eyes were very wide and very steady. "Can you stand?"

"Not on it. Not on that kind of slope."

"Then we don't stand on that kind of slope." She said it the way you'd say *then we don't order the fish.* No drama. She was already looking uphill, reading the slope — and I realized, with a jolt that hurt worse than the knee, that she'd been reading it all night. That she knew the line we'd taken was the line I'd chosen to avoid the worst of the running rock, and that she'd known, and had said nothing, because she trusted my reading and because saying it aloud would have cost me something she wasn't willing to spend on my behalf.

"There," she said, pointing. A rib of solid rock ran up through the shale — an old dike, harder than everything around it, standing out of the slope like the spine of a buried animal. "We go up the rib. It won't move."

"You can see that?"

"I grew up in the Sierra de la Ventana. It's not a real mountain range. It's a rumor of one. But my grandfather took me walking on it every winter of my childhood, and he taught me one thing, over and over, until I hated it: *find what doesn't move, and put your faith there.*" She shrugged, embarrassed, in the dark, where no one could see her shrug. "I thought it was about rocks. I think maybe it wasn't."

We went up the rib. I went on one leg and two hands and her shoulder, which is a configuration I had never once in my professional life permitted a client, because a client's shoulder is a liability, not an anchor. But Elena was not a client. I had stopped filing her under that heading somewhere on the salt flats, and I had not updated the paperwork, and now the audit had come due on a slope of singing shale at two in the morning.

She was stronger than she looked. That's a thing people say about her and it's wrong — she was exactly as strong as she looked, if you knew how to look, and I hadn't, not for weeks, because I'd been busy being the strong one. She took my weight in a low crouch, hips under it, the way you're supposed to, and she moved when I moved, and she stopped when I stopped, and she never once said *are you all right*, which is the question you ask people when you want them to reassure *you*.

* * *

The bad stretch came an hour before dawn, and it came the way bad stretches always come: all at once, and after you'd started believing you were through.

The rib ended. Below it the talus opened into a bowl — a great gray amphitheater of broken rock, two hundred meters of it, running rock the whole way, the kind of slope where the mountain holds a referendum on every traveler and the vote is rarely close. Around it, the detour would cost us four hours we didn't have, because behind us, faint but persistent, came the sound I'd been dreading: horses. Hale had found the empty estancia, and he was angry, and angry men on horses move fast across open ground.

"We cross it," Elena said.

"No."

"Then what?"

I sat on the last solid block of the rib and did the arithmetic out loud, because saying numbers aloud is a superstition of mine — numbers said aloud can be argued with. "We cross it and the slope may take us. We go around and the horses take us. The slope," I said, "is at least honest."

"That's the most romantic thing you've ever said about a mountain."

"I'm not romantic. I'm cornered."

She knelt in front of me. "Then we do it your way. But you don't lead. You can't lead. You know you can't." And when I opened my mouth to say the thing I had been saying for fifteen years — *I'm fine, I've got it, I'm the guide* — she put her hand flat on my chest, over the sternum, and said, "Wendell. Let me."

Two words. I have been given keys to houses, offers of money, a pardon once, almost, in Bariloche, dangled and withdrawn. None of it cost what those two words cost me, or bought what they bought. Because *let me* was an admission on both sides: that I was broken in a specific and inconvenient place, and that she was whole in the exact place I was broken, and that the two facts, put together, made a shape neither of us had planned.

"Three points of contact," I said, because I had to say something, and instruction is the only language I have for tenderness. "Small steps. If it starts to run, don't fight it — ride it downhill and left, toward the big blocks at the bottom. And if I go down—"

"You won't go down."

"Elena."

"If you go down," she said, "I will be down with you, and then we will both be extremely annoyed, and then we will get up. Now move."

We moved.

I don't remember most of the crossing. That's the honest truth, and it's the truth I'd have withheld from any client report, because a guide is supposed to remember everything, memory being the first tool of the trade. What I remember is fragments. Her hand on my wrist, setting the rhythm — step, pause, listen, step. The slope beginning to hiss under us, a long soft terrible sound like sand through an hourglass the size of a valley, and her voice, low, not panicked: *ride it, left, with me.* The world tilting and moving and me moving with it, weight over feet, three points, dancing with something that wanted to kill me, except this time I wasn't leading. The moon going out. The sound of her breathing, close, even, deliberate — a metronome I set my own breath against. And once, in the worst of it, when a whole sheet of shale let go somewhere above us and came down in a dry avalanche of its own, missing us by ten meters and filling the night with a sound like the mountain clearing its throat for good, her hand finding mine in the dark and gripping it, hard, and neither of us letting go for a long time after the danger had passed.

We came off the bottom of the bowl into the black gravel of an old streambed, and I sat down, and I laughed. It came out of me sideways, ugly and relieved, the laugh of a person who has just been told she doesn't have a terminal illness. Elena sat down next to me, and after a moment she laughed too, and then we were both laughing in the dark like a pair of fools, two women on the run from a well-funded man with lawyers, sitting in a dry riverbed at four in the morning, holding hands because it was easier than letting go.

"I have never," I said, "in fifteen years, let anyone lead me."

"I know," she said. "It was getting embarrassing to watch."

* * *

Dawn found us in a fold of the hills north of the talus, a green crease with a thread of water in it, and we stopped because we had to, not because we wanted to — the horses' sound had faded an hour back, but faded is not gone, and Hale does not lose things. My knee had swollen to the size of a grapefruit inside my boot, a fact I disclosed with the reluctance it deserved. Elena bound it with a strip of her shirt and the last of our tape, her fingers quick and unsentimental, and then she sat back on her heels and looked at me with an expression I couldn't file.

"What," I said.

"You're going to hate this."

"I already hate it."

"You can't walk on that today. Maybe not tomorrow. And we have maybe a day, day and a half, before Hale's scouts sweep this valley, because he knows the talus spat us out somewhere and he knows where the talus goes." She pulled the journal from its roll and set it on her knees. "But we're close. Closer than he thinks. Anselmo's valley, the second marker — the coordinates in the last entry put the cave two ridges north of here. Two ridges, Wendell. One hard morning, if we were both whole."

"If we were both whole," I said, "we'd have been there last week."

"So one of us isn't." She said it without pity, which is why I could stand it. "So we do what you'd tell any client to do. We rest today. We move at first light, on the good leg, with the bad one taped, and I carry your pack and you carry the knowledge, because that's the division of labor now, and you can be angry about it or you can be alive for it, but you don't get to be a martyr, because I've seen what you look like as a martyr and frankly it's worse than the knee."

I looked at her for a long time. The light was coming up gold behind her, the way it does on the dry side, all at once, like a door opening. Fifteen years I had organized my entire existence around the principle that needing people is how they die — that every rope team is a grief in advance, that the safest number is one. I had built the whole cold cathedral of my life on that stone. And here was this woman, this historian with her bad hip and her steady hands, telling me the stone was wrong, and telling me with a strip of her own shirt tied around my leg.

"Elena," I said. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me." She was already turning to the journal, businesslike, hiding whatever crossed her face. "Just be here tomorrow. Both legs or one."

I slept, or something near it, in the sun. And in the gray hour before we moved again, I woke to find her awake, reading the last intact page by touch in the dark, her lips moving over Tomás's handwriting, and I lay still and watched her and understood that the arithmetic had changed somewhere on that slope and I had not felt it happen. I had spent my whole life measuring in miles because miles are honest — they don't love you, they don't leave, they just accumulate. But there in the fold of the hills I caught myself measuring in something else entirely. In the weight of her hand on my sternum. In the sound of her breathing on the shale. In *let me*.

The knee held at first light, barely. We went north, up the first ridge, into country no map admits to. And at the top of it, in the cold morning wind, Elena stopped and pointed, and there it was — a black seam in the hillside, half-hidden behind a curtain of fallen rock, exactly where a dead man had written it would be.

And below us, on the trail we'd left, small and patient as ants: riders.
