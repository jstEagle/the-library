## 24. The Trap Springing

I woke before dawn because the birds had stopped.

That's a thing you learn out here that no guidebook bothers to print: silence has a texture, and the texture changes when something large moves through it. The austral thrushes that had been quarreling over the coirón grass all evening had gone quiet in the way birds go quiet for a fox or a puma — not startled, just *attentive*. I lay in the hollow of the boulder with Elena's breathing warm against my shoulder and listened to the dark decide what it wanted to be.

Wind. Wind was normal. Wind came down off the ice fields all night and combed the steppe flat. But under the wind there was another sound, low and rhythmic, that my body recognized about four seconds before my brain did: hooves. Many of them. Moving not toward us but *around* us, a slow orbit at perhaps half a kilometer's distance, patient as water finding its level.

"Hale," I said to the dark.

Elena stirred. "What time—"

"Dawn's in forty minutes. We have a problem."

We'd made camp in a shallow fold below the second cave, high enough to see the valley we'd climbed out of, low enough to be invisible from it — or so I'd believed when I chose the spot at two in the morning, running on adrenaline and half a tin of cold beans. Now I crawled to the lip of the fold and looked back the way we'd come, and the first gray light showed me exactly how wrong I'd been.

Fires. Four of them, strung across the pass behind us like beads on a wire. Tents. Mules hobbled in a picket line. Men moving between the fires with the unhurried economy of people who have arrived where they intended to arrive and are in no hurry whatsoever to do anything else.

They hadn't split up. They'd never split up. While Hale sat in the estancia drinking our host's wine and making his courteous offers, his team had swung wide — south, then east, then north through the badlands I'd thought too rough for mules — and closed the circle behind us while we slept with Tomás's journal under my head like a pillow.

"They were circling," Elena said beside me. She'd come up without a sound; she'd gotten good at that on the shelf. "The whole time. It was theater."

"It was arithmetic." My voice came out flatter than I meant it to. "He knew we'd go for the archive. He knew we couldn't resist reading what we found. All he had to do was let us do the hard part and stand at the door when we came out." I laughed, and it wasn't a laugh anyone would want to hear. "God. Fifteen years I've prided myself on reading country, and I walked straight into the oldest trap in the book because I wanted the ending more than I wanted the exit."

Elena put her hand on my arm. Her fingers were cold. "Options?"

I made myself look at the pass again, properly this time, counting instead of despairing. Eight men I could see, probably ten or twelve total. Rifles slung, not drawn — this wasn't going to be violence, not unless someone made it violence. Hale didn't need violence. Hale had permits, lawyers, gauchos on retainer, and now he had altitude and daylight and every route down except the ones that ended in glaciers.

"One option," I said. "Downhill, fast, before they finish their coffee. We might make the scree field before they saddle up."

"And then what? We're on foot with no supplies on the wrong side of the range in a season that's ending early." She said it gently, the way you tell someone their plan is dead. "Wendell. If they wanted us dead, we'd already be dead."

She was right, which I hated. I hated it the way you hate any true thing said kindly at five in the morning.

* * *

They let us come to them.

Of course they did. We got as far as the edge of their camp — close enough to smell woodsmoke and frying onions, which is a cruel thing to do to two people living on melted snow and willpower — and one of the guides stepped out from behind a rock with his hands visible and his rifle slung backward, and said, "Señorita Whitlock. El señor Hale asks that you join him for breakfast," and the whole performance of escape collapsed into the simple fact that we were expected.

Hale had a camp table. That was the detail that undid me, more than the rifles or the numbers. A folding aluminum table, set with a thermos and enamel plates, positioned to face the eastern light coming gold over the shoulder of the mountain, as if he'd rehearsed the tableau. Marcus Hale sat behind it in a canvas chair with his boots crossed, silver-haired, ruddy, looking for all the world like a man enjoying a well-earned holiday, and when he saw us he spread his hands in welcome with such unfeigned warmth that my stomach turned over.

"Wendell." He stood, actually stood, and offered his hand. "You look terrible. Both of you. Sit down, eat something, please. You've earned a meal if anyone ever has."

"I'd rather stand."

"You'd rather stand," he repeated, and sighed, and sat back down himself, and gestured at the chairs anyway. Elena took one. After a moment, so did I, because there is a kind of pride that costs calories and I had stopped being able to afford it somewhere around the salt flats.

He poured coffee without asking. Real coffee, not the chicory-sawdust we'd been rationing. He pushed the plate of eggs toward Elena first — a small courtesy, deliberate, the move of a man who reads people for a living — and waited until she'd eaten three forkfuls before he spoke again, which told me everything about how long he'd planned this conversation.

"The journal," he said.

"Good morning to you too, Marcus."

"Wendell." He said my name the way he used to say it across a briefing table fifteen years ago, when I still trusted the sound of it: patient, reasonable, a man inviting you to see sense. "Let's not do the scene where you pretend you don't know why I'm here. You spent last night reading your brother's survey notes. This morning I'm offering to buy them. Name a figure."

"It's not mine to sell."

"Whose is it, then? His?" And here he did something I wasn't prepared for: he softened. Genuinely, I think. He looked out at the light on the steppe and his voice dropped into something almost gentle. "Tomás was the best of us. You know I've always said that. What happened in '79 wasn't his failure, and it wasn't yours either, whatever the record says. But the record is the record, Wendell, and records only change when someone pays for the changing. I can pay. I'm the only one who can."

"The record," I said, "says eight men died in an avalanche."

"The record says what my father wrote home." He said it without flinching, which was its own kind of confession. "And my father was a liar. I've known it since I was nineteen and found the letters he burned and failed to burn completely. Do you imagine I funded this expedition for the scenery?" He leaned forward, elbows on the aluminum table, and for a moment the mask of the genial patron slipped and I saw the thing underneath, which was hunger — old, inherited, secondhand hunger, a son starving on a dead man's stolen meal. "Everything my family has came out of that lie. The company. The name. Me. I am the last page of a forgery, Wendell, and I would very much like to know how it ends before I die."

For one obscene half-second, I understood him. That was the worst part. There in the gold morning light with Tomás's journal weighing against my ribs like a second heart, I looked at Marcus Hale and saw a man as haunted as I was, chasing the same ghost from the opposite direction, and some traitorous corner of me wanted to hand him the book and say *there, now we're both free.*

Then I remembered the estancia. I remembered him offering to expose my record to Elena like a man laying cards face-up, courteous and lethal. I remembered that courtesy and lethality can share a face indefinitely.

"No," I said.

He exhaled through his nose. Not surprise — disappointment, the way a teacher is disappointed by a student who was supposed to be smarter. "Then we do this the other way." He nodded to one of his men, who came over carrying a canvas satchel and set it on the table between us with a care that made my skin crawl, because I knew what was in it before it opened: rope, good rope, sixty meters of it. Two liters of water. A tin of biscuits. A small first-aid kit, even a foil blanket.

"A day's provisions," Hale said. "Rope for the descent on the north side, which is the only way down that isn't suicide this time of year. Water for one day, because after one day you'll either have reached the river or you won't need it anymore." He folded his hands. "The journal stays with me. The photographs —" he glanced at Elena, and I watched her face do nothing at all, beautifully, absolutely nothing, and prayed her jacket lining was as good as she'd claimed — "you're welcome to keep. Paper burns. Memories are lighter anyway."

"You're leaving us to walk out," Elena said. Not a question. Checking the shape of it.

"I'm leaving you the means to walk out. There's a difference, and the difference is that I'll sleep tonight." He stood, and buttoned his jacket, and became once more entirely the civilized man. "You should understand something, Wendell, because I suspect you've spent the last hour composing the speech where you tell me I'll answer for this. I won't. Not legally — every permit I hold is real, every claim is filed, and your name opens doors in this country the way a crowbar does, and not favorably. Not morally, either, because morality is a luxury of people who weren't abandoned on a mountain by their fathers." He picked up the journal from where I'd set it on the table — I let him, because his men had rifles and my anger, however cold, was not yet stupid — and held it a moment, weighing it.

"He mapped all of this," Hale said quietly. "Your brother. Every pass, every spring, every marker. Fifteen years I've been reading fragments of his handwriting in archives on three continents, and I never once connected it to the disgraced guide named Whitlock until Elena's letter crossed my desk." He smiled, and it was the awful sincere smile, the one that meant it. "You're the best mountaineer I ever worked with. It runs in the family, apparently. Which is why I know you'll make it down. And why I know that if you don't, it won't be the mountain that kills you. It'll be the arithmetic."

He tucked the journal inside his jacket, over his heart, like a man pocketing a letter rather than stealing a relic, and walked away toward the fires, and his men began striking camp around us with the brisk indifference of weather.

* * *

They left us the table.

I don't know why that detail broke something loose in me, but it did. The aluminum table, the thermos, the plates with egg residue — they packed everything, loaded the mules, formed up in the col above the pass, and rode west without a backward glance, and the folding table stood alone on the bare stone like furniture in a house where everyone had died. Maybe it was contempt. Maybe it was efficiency — weight is weight. But standing there in the full morning with a day's water and sixty meters of rope and nothing else in the world, I found I could bear the theft of my brother's journal better than I could bear the table.

Elena touched it. "We could sell it," she said, and I barked a laugh that scared a condor off the ridge.

"There's a joke with legs."

"We could carry it." She said it seriously, testing the idea's weight the way she tested everything. "Sixty meters of rope is eleven kilos. The table's four."

"We're not carrying a table across the dry side."

"We're not crossing the dry side, Wendell. Look at it." She turned a slow circle, and I turned with her, and the country laid itself out with terrible honesty: west, the passes, white-capped now past the point of argument, the storm front sitting on the ice fields like a creditor. South, the badlands, Hale's territory, watched. North, the scree and the long descent to the river country — the way Hale had *generously* provided rope for, the way he'd calculated we'd go, because he'd read my maps the same way I'd read his moves, and he knew that a guide with a day's water walks toward water.

East, the steppe. Empty, waterless, forty kilometers of nothing to the gaucho estancia, and beyond it the rail line, and beyond that the world.

"He expects us north," I said slowly.

"He built us a road north. Rope, water, a route he's already scouted." Elena's eyes had gone bright and hard, the historian's eyes, the ones that lit up over a forged signature. "So the question is whether we take the road he built or the road nobody believes exists."

I looked east again. Forty kilometers of rain-shadow nothing, land so dry the very stones seemed to have given up on the concept of shade. With one day's water, for two people, in warming weather. It was impossible. It was exactly the kind of impossible my father used to describe around the stove, the high routes and the dry cuts, the country that only a fool or a Whitlock would cross — and I heard Tomás's voice in it suddenly, from the journal pages I'd read by headlamp twelve hours ago, entries in that careful survey hand: *W. would try it. W. would count the miles and laugh.*

I had counted miles for fifteen years. Miles as penance, miles as anesthesia, miles to keep the avalanche at a safe distance. And here was the country handing me one final sum, the cruelest and cleanest I'd ever been asked to do: forty kilometers east with a day's water, or the comfortable road north into whatever Hale had arranged at the bottom of it.

Helplessness is a strange fuel. It burns cold. I felt it settle into me there beside the abandoned table — the specific, clarifying rage of a person with nothing left to lose and one thing left to prove — and I found that my hands had stopped shaking, which they had not done since the second cave.

"Elena," I said. "How do you feel about walking east?"

She followed my gaze out onto the steppe, all that blazing empty gold, and I watched her do her own arithmetic — the limp, the water, the distance, the man on the western horizon growing smaller — and arrive at the only answer that had ever really been on the table for either of us.

"I feel," she said, "like someone who sewed a memory card into her jacket for exactly this kind of morning."

I picked up the rope. Eleven kilos. I'd carried worse for worse reasons.

Behind us the pass stood empty, the fires kicked cold, Hale's dust already fading westward with my brother's journal in it. Ahead of us lay the driest country on God's green — well, brown — earth, and one day of water, and no road at all.

I shouldered the coil and started walking, and for the first time in fifteen years, I wasn't measuring the miles.

I was spending them.
