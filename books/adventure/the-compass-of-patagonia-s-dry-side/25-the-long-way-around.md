## 25. The Long Way Around

They left us rope and a day's water, which was either generosity or an insult, and I hadn't yet decided which. The rope was good rope — Hale's money always bought good rope — and the water was in proper expedition bottles, the kind with the wide mouths and the measurement graduations printed on the side, so you could watch your hope go down in liters.

Elena sat on a rock with her hands wrapped around a bottle she wasn't drinking from, staring west, where the storm had chewed the horizon into something soft and gray and hungry.

"He wished us luck," she said.

"He did."

"I keep trying to decide if that was worse than shooting us."

"It was worse," I said. "Shooting us would have been honest."

We had one tent between us, half a stove's worth of fuel, a handful of ration bars, Elena's camera with its stitched secret, and my head, which contained — if I was lucky — the shape of a route nobody had walked in forty years, described to me once, by a dying man, in the kind of language dying men use, which is to say: loosely, and with too much poetry.

* * *

My father talked about the shelf the way other men talk about a woman they almost married. He'd been a surveyor for the oil companies in the fifties, back when the dry side was still mostly rumor and guanaco, and he'd spent one summer staring at it through binoculars from a ridge above the Río Blanco: a band of pale stone running east-west along the face of the cordillera, two hundred meters wide in places, pinched to nothing in others, hanging above the steppe like a ledge on God's own mantelpiece.

*La cornisa del viento,* he called it. The cornice of wind. Not because it was a cornice — it wasn't, it was honest bedrock, an old wave-cut bench from when all this had been seafloor, which is a sentence I love saying to people who think Patagonia's dry side has always been dry — but because the wind up there, he said, never stopped. Never. It came off the icefields and poured down the leeward face like water off a weir, and it blew from the west, always from the west, day and night, and a man walking east with his back to it would feel like he was being pushed gently toward salvation the whole way.

He never walked it. He said nobody had, not since a pair of sheepmen tried it in the thirties and one of them came back and the other didn't, and the one who came back wouldn't talk about it except to say the shelf was *there*, you could see it, it was *real*, it just didn't care whether you were.

I was nine when he told me this. I asked him why anyone would walk it at all, and he looked at me for a long time with that expression he had, the one that meant he was deciding how much truth a child could carry, and he said: *Because it goes where the maps don't, mija. And someday you'll want to go where the maps don't.*

He was wrong about that, for a long time. I spent fifteen years going exactly where the maps did, measuring everything, keeping to the marked routes like a woman walking on the painted lines of a crosswalk. But he'd been right about the shelf. I'd checked. Every time I crossed this country, some part of me — the part I didn't let out in daylight — would look up at that pale band and do the arithmetic.

The arithmetic went like this: the normal route to the archive, the one Tomás's journal described, the one Hale's team was on right now with their mules and their satellite phones and their forty liters of fuel, ran south around the shoulder of the range and up the valley beyond. Four days, maybe five, for a team moving well. We had no mules, no fuel, no food to speak of, and a storm coming down our necks.

The shelf ran straight. Two days, if it went at all.

If.

* * *

I laid it out for Elena that evening, drawing in the dirt with a stick, the way my father had drawn it in my notebook with a pencil, back when I still had notebooks and a father to draw in them.

"Here's the honest option," I said. "We go back down the valley, resupply at the estancia — assuming the gauchos will still feed us after Hale got to them — and take the southern route. We arrive four days behind Hale. We arrive to a site already picked clean, documents already in his hands, and our only play is the memory card and a legal fight in Buenos Aires that could take ten years."

"And the dishonest option?"

"The option that isn't honest is the one where I tell you the shelf is safe. It isn't safe. It's probably not even walkable. It's been untested in forty years, the rock is wave-cut and rotten in places, there's no water on it, no escape off it for thirty kilometers, and if the weather closes in we will be standing on a ledge in a hundred-kilometer wind with nowhere to go but over."

Elena looked at my map in the dirt for a while. The wind, down here at the bottom of the valley, was only doing its usual forty.

"You said my grandfather's journal mentioned it," she said.

"It's mentioned. One line. *El camino alto existe pero nadie lo quiere.* The high road exists but nobody wants it."

"That's not a warning."

"No?"

"It's a review." She almost smiled. "My grandfather wrote reviews of everything. He was a radio operator, he had strong opinions about equipment. If he'd thought it was impossible, he'd have said impossible. He said *nobody wants it.* That means somebody tried it and survived to not want it."

I looked at her — this woman who'd crossed a salar on a leg that should have kept her in Comodoro, who'd stared down Marcus Hale in an estancia kitchen with nothing but a historian's patience and a liar's nerve — and I felt the old thing stir in me, the thing I'd buried under fifteen years of kilometers. Not confidence. Confidence is what you feel before you know the terrain. This was older and less comfortable. It was the feeling of a route assembling itself behind my eyes, the way it used to, the way it did before Tomás, before the avalanche, before the word *blacklist* became the shortest biography of me in two countries.

"The guide question," I said, "is whether I'm willing to bet your life on a line my father described through binoculars in 1957."

"The guide question," Elena said, "is whether you're willing to spend the rest of your life knowing Hale read Tomás's last page before you did."

That was cheating. She knew it was cheating. I let her cheat.

* * *

We started before light, because the first hour of the approach was scree and I wanted it underfoot while it was still frozen solid. The valley floor gave way to moraine, the moraine to a wall of broken rock that we climbed in the blue dark by the sound of each other's breathing, and by the time the sun came up we were standing at the base of the headwall, looking at the place where the shelf began.

It was there. My father hadn't lied. A pale band of stone, tilted very slightly, running west to east along the face of the range as far as the haze would let me see — thirty kilometers of it, give or take, a road built by an ocean that had left the building sixty million years ago.

It was also, up close, exactly as rotten as I'd told her. Wave-cut rock is layered rock, and layered rock at altitude, freeze-thawed for sixty million years, is rock that wants to be gravel and is taking its time about it. I put a hand on the first section and a piece the size of a dinner plate came away and went down the face with a sound like a dropped book.

"Well," Elena said.

"We test every step. We rope up on the bad stretches. We don't talk when the wind talks." I was already re-rigging the rope, and I noticed my hands were doing it without my supervision, coils and knots arriving in the right order, and something in my chest that had been a locked room for fifteen years quietly unlocked. "And we keep moving. The shelf doesn't care about us, so we don't care about it. We care about the next three meters. That's the whole religion."

She looked at me for a second. "There she is," she said.

"There who is."

"The guide. The one from before." She shouldered her pack. "I've been reading about her for a year. Nice to finally meet her."

I didn't have an answer for that, so I turned and put my hand on the rock and started up, and she followed, and for the first time in fifteen years the country behind me felt more dangerous than the country ahead.

* * *

The wind found us at mid-morning, the way my father had promised it would. It came off the icefields to the west and poured over the lip of the range and down the face of the shelf, and it was — he'd been right about this too — relentless in a way I'd never felt anywhere else. Not gusting. *Pouring.* A river of air with us standing in it, leaning into it, and the strange mercy of it was that it blew from behind. Every kilometer we walked east, it pushed us east. It was the first time in my life the wind had been on my side, and I distrusted it completely, the way you'd distrust a loan from a man who'd ruined you.

We made eleven kilometers the first day, which was either excellent or terrible depending on how you counted, because eleven kilometers on the shelf was eleven kilometers of nothing else: no water, no shelter, no exit. We slept in a hollow behind a fin of standing stone, roped together, lying on the rope like a shared blanket, and ate half a ration bar each, and I lay awake doing the arithmetic I'd promised myself I'd stop doing, and the arithmetic said: two more days like this, and then the shelf descends at the eastern end into the upper valley, and the archive is one valley beyond, and we will have beaten Hale's mules by a day and a half on foot, on no food, on a route that doesn't exist.

Somewhere in the dark Elena said, "You're calculating."

"I'm resting."

"You're calculating. I can hear it. It sounds like a small animal turning a wheel."

I almost laughed. "My father used to say the same thing about my mother."

"Tell me about him. Your father. The one who found the road nobody wanted."

So I did. There on the shelf of wind, in the dark, with the rope between us, I told her about a man with binoculars and a notebook full of routes that went nowhere anyone wanted to go, and how he'd died ordinary — a heart, in a kitchen, in Puerto Deseado, no drama at all — and how I'd inherited from him a head full of impossible roads and from the mountains a terror of using them, and how those two inheritances had been fighting each other for forty years.

"Which one's winning?" she asked.

I listened to the wind pour over us, pushing us gently toward the place where my brother had died waiting for someone to come.

"Ask me tomorrow," I said.

* * *

The second day the shelf pinched.

I'd known it would — my father had said it pinched, *se estrecha a la mitad*, and I'd been telling myself since Puerto Deseado that a man who described a pinch through binoculars might be underestimating it, the way men underestimate everything they haven't touched. He hadn't underestimated it. For two hundred meters the shelf narrowed to less than the width of my shoulders, the wave-cut layers tilting outward like the pages of a book opening, the steppe a kilometer of pure air below, and the wind — the wind that had been our friend — now came over the lip and hit us broadside, trying to peel us off the rock like paint.

We roped at six meters. I went first, because that was the deal, the old deal, the deal I'd made with every client and then broken exactly once, at a cost I'd been paying interest on ever since. Three points of contact. Move one limb at a time. Face the rock, don't look down, don't look at the drop, look at the next hold, and the next, and let the body do what the body knows, because the body knows and the mind is only there to carry the fear.

Halfway across, a hold I'd weighted came off in my hand.

Not a plate this time. A slab, the size of a door, and it went down the face and I heard it hit the slope below — once, twice — and then just the wind, and my three remaining points of contact, and Elena's voice behind me, very steady, saying my name.

"I'm here," I said.

"I know. I'm saying it for me."

I found the next hold. And the next. And the shelf widened, meter by meter, until it was a road again, and I brought her across, and we stood on the far side shaking in the wind, and she put her forehead against my shoulder for three seconds, which on the shelf of wind was the equivalent of a long embrace.

"Two days," she said. "You said two days."

"One more night. Then we come off the east end."

"And the archive."

"And the archive. And Hale's mules still two days south of it." I looked back the way we'd come — thirty kilometers of pale stone hanging in the sky, unwalked for forty years, walked now. "Nobody wants this road," I said. "My grandfather's journal was right."

"Your father wanted it," Elena said.

"Yeah." I shouldered the pack, and the wind pushed us east, and I let it. "He was the only one."

* * *

We came off the shelf at dusk on the third day, down a ramp of talus at the eastern end that was almost — almost — kind, and stood in the upper valley with our boots on real ground and the cordillera at our backs, and I did the thing I hadn't let myself do on the shelf: I looked at the map in my head, and then I looked at the country, and the two matched.

Below us, the valley ran north toward the collapsed moraine, toward the fissure, toward the crates and the documents and the skeleton sitting upright with the last page folded in his jacket, waiting forty years for someone with a compass and a reason.

And far to the south — a day and a half south, maybe less if Hale had pushed his team hard, and he would have, Hale always pushed hard when he was close to a thing he wanted — I could see, or imagined I could see, dust. A thread of it, rising off the southern route, moving north.

We had beaten them. We had beaten them on a road that didn't exist, on no food, with one rope and a day and a half of water, and we had perhaps six hours of darkness to get down this valley and find a door my brother had hidden behind a collapsed glacier.

Elena was looking at the dust too.

"How fast can you walk?" she asked.

I tightened the pack straps and felt the old arithmetic come up one more time — and this time it wasn't miles. It was minutes. It was my brother's minutes, forty years of them, and how few were left before the man who'd abandoned him once came to do it again.

"Faster than that," I said, and started down.
