## 29. Tomás's Last Page

The skeleton had been waiting for us, and I suppose in a way it had been waiting for me.

Elena stood very still at the mouth of the fissure, her breath making small clouds that the wind took and shredded. Behind us the collapsed moraine looked like nothing — a heap of grey rock among ten thousand identical heaps of grey rock — and I understood suddenly why no one had found this place in forty years. You could walk past it every day of your life and the mountain would keep its mouth shut.

"Is it him?" Elena asked softly. "Do you know?"

I didn't answer right away. The chamber was larger than the light suggested from outside, a hollow where the glacier had once ground through and then thought better of it, leaving a floor of fine dry silt. Crates lined one wall, stacked with a care that hurt to look at — someone had wanted these found. Someone had believed they would be. And against the far wall, seated upright on a flat stone with his back straight as a surveyor's plumb line, was the man himself.

Jacket buttoned. Boots together. Hands resting on his knees like a man waiting for a bus that was forty years late.

"I don't know," I said finally. "I won't know until—"

But I already knew. That was the terrible thing. I knew by the set of the shoulders, by the way he'd arranged himself to be read rather than mourned. Tomás Whitlock had never once in his life sat down without deciding what message the sitting conveyed. When he was eight he'd broken his arm falling out of a coihue tree and climbed back up to show our father exactly which branch had failed him before he'd let anyone drive him to the clinic.

I crossed the silt floor. My boots sounded enormous. The dust rose around my ankles and hung there, undisturbed for decades, and I thought: nobody has breathed this air since you, and now it's me, and isn't that just like you, saving the last thing in the room for me.

The jacket was waxed canvas gone stiff as bark. In the breast pocket, folded into quarters, was a page torn from a survey journal — the same journal we'd found in the second cave, minus its final leaf. The paper had yellowed to the color of old bone but the ink held, brown-black, patient.

My hands were shaking so badly I nearly couldn't unfold it.

* * *

Elena knelt beside me without touching me. She'd learned that about me somewhere on the salares or the shelf of wind — that grief in my family is handled like a loaded rifle, passed carefully, muzzle down. She held the headlamp steady while I read, because my own hands wouldn't do it.

The handwriting was Tomás's survey hand, the tight vertical script he'd used for coordinates and never for letters home. But this wasn't coordinates.

*If whoever finds this is a Whitlock, stop reading standing up.*

I made a sound I didn't recognize as coming from me. I sat down in the dust beside him.

*Wendell. If it's you — and the compass told me it might be, because Dad gave us each one and yours always pointed a half-degree west of true and you trusted it anyway, which was either faith or stubbornness, and I know which — then you're older than I am now. That's strange to write. I've done the arithmetic four times and it keeps coming out wrong, or right, depending on how you look at it.*

*I'm going to tell it properly, once, in order, because the report they'll never let you see tells it crooked.*

*March 1979. We summited the east shoulder on the ninth. Corvera was ecstatic; Hale — Marcus Hale senior, the sponsor's man, not a climber, never a climber — stayed at Camp II "for radio relay." On the descent the weather closed. Corvera ordered a bivouac at the col. Hale ordered us down the north couloir over the radio, said the window was closing, said the sponsors needed the route documented by month's end. Corvera refused. Hale said the words I have thought about every day since: "Then the expedition continues without you."*

*He means it literally. He took the two strongest climbers, the rope, most of the food, and the satellite position papers, and he went down the south side. He reached Puerto Deseado in eleven days. He told the press the mountain took us. He filed a route report under his own name. It was accepted. There were no survivors to contradict it.*

*There were survivors.*

*Four of us came off the col alive. Corvera died on the third day trying to reach the estancia to the east. The others are names you'll find in the crates behind me. I walked out of the ice in June. An old woman fed me at a fire and I wept like a child, and if you ever meet her family, Wendell, thank them for me again.*

*And then I tried to come home. And I learned what a buried report does. Every official channel said the same thing: the Corvera Expedition is lost, all hands, tragedy, closed file. A man walking out of the ice claiming otherwise is either a fraud or a madman. I wrote thirty-one letters. I have copies. Nobody opens a file that powerful men have paid to keep shut.*

*So I stayed. I mapped. If I couldn't bring the truth out, I'd leave it where truth keeps well — in stone, in caves, in the dry side where paper rots but carving doesn't. Three sites. This is the last. Everything is here: the original route notes in Corvera's hand, the radio log with Hale's orders transcribed verbatim, the affidavits of the four of us who lived, signed and dated. And this page, which is the key, because a page can be carried where a crate cannot.*

*One more thing, and then I'll let you go.*

*I know what happened to you. Not the details — I've been dead to the world too long for details — but I know my sister, and I know she did not sign that route because she was careless. Whatever they told the world, whatever they made you carry, put it down. Put it down, Wen. Fifteen years is enough miles. Come sit by a fire. Measure something in years for once.*

*Your brother,
T.*

*P.S. The compass points a half-degree west of true. So did I, all my life. Toward you.*

* * *

I don't remember how long I sat there. Long enough that the light through the fissure changed twice, long enough that Elena built a small stove flame and heated water and pressed a cup into my hands and I drank it without tasting it. Long enough that the cold came up through the silt and into my bones and I welcomed it, because it was the first honest sensation I'd felt since unfolding the page.

The whole story clicked shut. That's the only way I can describe it — not like a door opening but like a lock turning, every tumbler dropping into place at once. The avalanche hadn't killed him. The avalanche had been the beginning of the lie, and the lie had a name, and the name had a son who was currently two valleys away with my brother's journal in his pack and my reputation in his pocket like a coin he planned to spend.

Hale. Both Hales. Father and son, forty years apart, countersigning the same kind of lie. The old one abandoning men on a mountain to steal a route; the young one letting a twenty-six-year-old guide hang for an unstable slope so his signature would stay clean. It wasn't coincidence. It was inheritance. Some families pass down land, or money, or eye color. The Hales passed down the art of surviving beautifully at other people's expense.

"He waited," I said. My voice came out rusted. "Forty years. He mapped the whole dry side waiting for someone to come."

"He wasn't waiting for someone," Elena said quietly. "Look at the page again. Look at the P.S."

I looked. The compass. Our father had given us each one — I'd lost mine in the river the day everything ended, or thrown it, I've never been sure which. Tomás had kept his. And Tomás had written that his compass pointed toward me, which was nonsense, compasses don't work that way, except that everything else my brother had ever said had sounded like nonsense right up until it turned out to be survey-grade true.

"He knew you'd come," Elena said. "Not when. But he carved those markers knowing that eventually a Whitlock would walk the dry side, because a Whitlock always walks the dry side. He left a trail only you could read." She paused. "He left it for you, Wendell. The tide, the cave, the estancia carvings, the compass in the ruins — none of it was luck. It was a fifteen-year invitation, and you finally opened the door."

I looked at the skeleton then, really looked, and the horror I'd braced for didn't come. What came instead was something worse and better: recognition. He'd buttoned his jacket. He'd squared his boots. He'd folded the page into quarters and put it in his breast pocket, over the heart, because he was a man who understood that the finder matters more than the finding. He'd made his death into a letter with an address on it.

"You stubborn bastard," I whispered. "You absolute stubborn bastard. Thirty-one letters and then you just — sat down and waited for the mail."

Elena laughed, a wet, cracked laugh, and then she was crying, and then somehow I was crying too, the first time in fifteen years, both of us kneeling in forty-year-old dust holding a dead man's page between us like a candle flame we were afraid to let gutter.

* * *

We worked after that, because work is what my family does with feeling — we convert it to load-bearing structure. Elena photographed the page from six angles with two backup lights. Then the crates: Corvera's route notes in a leather folio gone green with age. The radio log, transcribed in Tomás's careful hand, every one of old Hale's orders dated and timed. Four affidavits, four signatures, four dates spanning three weeks after the world had declared them dead. Photographs. A press clipping from Puerto Deseado, 1979, EXPEDITION LOST, ALL HANDS, with a photograph of Marcus Hale senior accepting condolences at the dock with the correct expression on his face.

"The same face," Elena said, holding the clipping beside her phone screen, where a recent photo of the younger Hale smiled his courteous smile. "Forty years and it's the same photograph."

"Families," I said. "They pass things down."

We repacked the crates as we'd found them. It mattered to me, though I couldn't have explained why — some instinct that said: he stacked these to be found intact, and we will honor the stacking. The page itself went into the inner pocket of my jacket, over my heart, because I am also a Whitlock and we understand addresses.

It was full dark when we finished. Outside the fissure the wind had risen, and when I crawled out and stood up, I saw it immediately and my stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

Snow. Not the dusting from days ago — real snow, fat flakes coming down out of a black sky in earnest, already furring the rocks, already erasing the moraine, already erasing our back-trail. The dry season wasn't ending early. It was ending now.

"Elena," I said. "How long were we inside?"

She checked her watch and then the sky and did the arithmetic, and I watched her arrive at the same number I had. Two valleys south, Hale's team was closing. Behind us, the storm was closing faster. And between us and any kind of safety lay the glacial river, which an hour ago had been a braided silver ribbon and was now, with the warm storm rain feeding it from the heights, becoming something else entirely.

"We can't stay here," Elena said. "Above the snow line, in the open, with what we're carrying—"

"No. We move tonight. Down-valley, toward the river crossing, while the trail still exists." I touched my jacket pocket, felt the fold of the page through the fabric. "He waited forty years to be heard. I'm not letting a blizzard interrupt the delivery."

We struck downslope into the dark and the rising snow, and somewhere below us, faint through the wind, I heard it — the deep, grinding roar of a river that had stopped being a river and become a verdict.

The crossing was going to happen tonight, whether we chose it or not.
