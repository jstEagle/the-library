## 40. The Dry Side of Morning

The guanacos were the first to know. They always are.

I was standing outside the estancia at Puerto Deseado with a cup of coffee going cold in my hands when I saw them come over the rise — six of them, long-necked and insolent, moving east with that trotting gait they use when they've decided somewhere else is better. Elena says it's migration. I say it's judgment. Either way, when guanacos start walking toward spring, you'd be a fool to argue with them.

It was October. The steppe had done its annual trick of pretending to be dead all winter and then exploding into green overnight, as if it had been holding its breath since September and couldn't hold it one more day. The coiron grass was silver-headed. The calafate bushes had put out their little yellow flowers, which meant berries by December, which meant jam if anyone had the patience, which nobody ever did. The snow on the western cordillera had retreated up into the couloirs where it belongs, leaving the passes open like doors left ajar in a house where everyone finally went outside.

And I was standing there with cold coffee, watching guanacos, feeling something I hadn't felt in fifteen years of measuring my life in kilometers.

I felt like staying.

That's not how it used to work. For most of my adult life, staying was the thing I was allergic to. A town lasted me two weeks, three if the laundry took longer. I had a system: I never unpacked past the second layer of the pack, never learned more than the bartender's name, never let a view become familiar enough to miss. Distance was the drug. Every mile walked was a mile put between me and a slope above Lago Argentino where the snowpack failed on a Tuesday morning in November and took my brother with it, along with my name, my license, and every version of myself I'd been before.

You can outrun a lot of things on foot. I tried grief for fifteen years. It has better endurance than you'd think.

* * *

Elena came out of the estancia carrying two cups and handed me one without asking whether I wanted it, because she stopped asking around year two and I stopped minding around year three. She stood beside me and followed my gaze to the guanacos.

"They're early," she said.

"Everything's early this year. The melt came down fast. Passes will be clear by mid-month."

"You've been doing arithmetic again."

"I have not."

"You have. You do the face. The face where you're adding days and subtracting weather." She sipped her coffee. "I married a woman who claims she doesn't measure anything."

"We're not married."

"Not yet," said Elena Vasquez-Roth, who had proposed eleven months ago on a knife-edge ledge four hundred meters above the steppe floor while the wind screamed hard enough to peel the words off her lips, and who had never once let me forget that I said yes between breaths, which she considers binding and I consider the only honest way anyone agrees to anything.

"Fine," I said. "I was doing arithmetic. But it wasn't the old kind."

"What kind was it?"

"The kind where the answer doesn't matter."

She looked at me then, properly, the way she looks at primary sources — with suspicion first and delight second. "Say that again slower. I want to write it down."

"Don't you dare put me in the book."

"Too late. You're chapter nine through thirty-nine."

"I'm aware. My lawyer has read it."

"You don't have a lawyer."

"I'm aware of that too."

* * *

Here is what we were actually doing out there, standing in the green with our coffee, because it still surprises me when I say it plainly:

We were waiting for clients.

Not clients the way I once had them — the ones who paid, the ones whose insistence on an unstable route I signed off on because the alternative was losing the season, the signature Marcus Hale countersigned and then hid behind while I took the fall for Tomás's death. Those clients are a closed ledger now. Hale's empire cracked open under its own weight last spring, the way bad ice does — quietly, then all at once. His father's abandonment of the Corvera Expedition made the papers. His own countersignature made the worse papers. He faces questions he cannot talk his way across, and I have stopped checking the news for his name, which is its own kind of mile behind me.

No — these clients were different. There were four of them arriving on Thursday's bus from Comodoro: a retired schoolteacher from Bahía Blanca, two sisters from Wales who had read Elena's book and decided the dry side sounded like their kind of country, and a quiet young man from Trelew who had emailed us eleven times asking increasingly precise questions about condor thermals until I realized he knew more about them than I did and told Elena we should hire him instead of guide him.

We weren't charging them much. We weren't really charging them at all — enough to cover the horses and the food, Elena insisted, because people value what they pay for, and because she's right about most things and insufferable about half of those. The point was never money. The point was that the country is beautiful, and we know its secrets, and secrets kept too long go stale.

Tomás taught me that. Posthumously, from a cave, via a skeleton and a journal page folded in a dead man's jacket pocket. It's not the teaching method I'd recommend, but it sticks.

* * *

I want to tell you about the morning itself, because mornings on the dry side in October deserve telling, and because I spent so many years not noticing them that I feel a professional obligation now to notice on behalf of everyone who's still running.

The light comes up flat and gray first, the way it does near the coast, and then the sun clears the eastern rim of the plateau and the whole steppe catches fire — not orange, exactly, but that pale gold that lives in dry grass and nowhere else, the color of things that have learned to live on almost nothing. The wind gets up out of the west, smelling of ice and distance, and moves through the coiron in waves you can watch travel for kilometers, like the land breathing after holding its breath all winter. Somewhere a choique — the lesser rhea, the bird that runs like it's late for everything — goes past at speed for no reason, which is the most Patagonian thing any creature here ever does.

Elena and I walked the route the evening before, just the first day of it, out to the ridge where you get your first look back at the coast. It's a guide's habit: walk the ground before you walk people over it. But it isn't only a habit anymore. It's also the thing I do instead of leaving.

That's the change, if you want it in one sentence. I used to walk to get away. Now I walk to arrive.

On the ridge, Elena pointed south, where the land folds down into the badlands we'd cut through years ago with Hale's dust plumes on the horizon behind us. "Do you remember which route we took?"

"I remember every route I've ever taken. It's a curse."

"Which one?"

"The one through the red canyon, then north along the dry wash, then the mistake."

"There were several mistakes."

"The second mistake, then. The one where you fell and blamed the geology."

"The geology was complicit." She smiled, and the wind took her hair sideways, and I had the thought I have most mornings now, which is that I don't deserve this and have decided to accept it anyway, the way you accept unexpected good weather — gratefully, and slightly braced.

* * *

The bus came Thursday. The five of us — me, Elena, the schoolteacher, the Welsh sisters, and the condor boy, whose name is Mateo and who had already reorganized my gear shed by merit alone — loaded panniers onto four horses and set out west under a sky so clean it looked rinsed.

I won't bore you with the whole crossing. Some of it you already know if you've read Elena's book, and some of it is ours, and some of it belongs to the country, which shares nothing willingly except views. But I'll give you the pieces that matter, the way Tomás's journal gave me pieces that mattered — not the whole map, just the marks that keep you alive.

The schoolteacher, whose name is Irma, had never ridden further than a beach parade and turned out to be made of iron and curiosity. She asked questions the entire first day — about the grass, the rocks, the guanaco social structure, why the fences run at angles no surveyor would endorse — and by the third day she'd stopped asking and started seeing, which is the whole education, really. On the fifth morning she stood at the edge of the salar remnant, the white scar of it shining under the sun, and said, "It's like walking on the sky's mistake," and Elena wrote it down, and I let her.

The Welsh sisters argued cheerfully about navigation the entire time, each convinced the other couldn't read a map, both entirely correct. Mateo found a condor roost on day four and sat below it for two hours in silence, and when he came back he didn't say anything for a while, and when he did speak his voice had changed key. That's the country working on him. It worked on me at his age. It nearly killed me, and it worked anyway, and I wouldn't trade it, which is either love or Stockholm syndrome and out here the distinction gets blurry.

And Elena — Elena rode point some days and sagged back others, and on the afternoon we crossed the high shelf, the impossible one, the shelf of wind that everyone said couldn't be done and that we did twice now, she stopped in the middle of it, where the drop falls away on both sides and the wind comes up from the steppe like the whole continent exhaling, and she looked at me and shouted over the noise:

"This is where you said yes!"

"It's where I said yes between breaths! Legally distinct!"

"It counts!"

"It counts!" I shouted back, and the wind took it south, and somewhere over the badlands a guanaco probably heard it and judged us both.

* * *

There's one more thing, and I've been circling it the way you circle a campfire, warming your hands at the edges before you commit to sitting down.

On the sixth night we camped at the waterhole — the spring marked on no map, the one from her grandfather's journal, the debt the land paid us when we had nothing left to pay it with. The clients were asleep. The fire was down to its red arithmetic. And Elena, who reads me better than any contour line, said, "You're quiet."

"I'm always quiet."

"You're always quiet differently. This is the quiet where you're about to say something true."

I fed the fire a stick. Watched it catch. Out on the dark steppe something small moved and stopped, moved and stopped — a fox, probably, conducting its own small audit of us.

"When I was twenty-six," I said, "I had a system. Walk far enough, fast enough, and the past can't catch up. Kilometers as anesthesia. Fifteen years of it. I could tell you to the meter how far I'd walked in any given season. It was the only number I trusted."

"And now?"

"Now I couldn't tell you how many kilometers we've done this week. Not roughly. Not at all. And it doesn't frighten me, which frightens me slightly, but mostly it just feels like—" I looked for the word. Words are Elena's department; mine are switchbacks and water sources. "Like putting down a pack I forgot I was carrying. You know how it is. You wear a heavy pack so long your shoulders learn its shape, and when someone takes it off you, you keep walking hunched for an hour before you realize you can stand up."

Elena was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "Tomás would have liked hearing that."

"Tomás wrote it first. 'Stop punishing yourself, Wen. The mountain didn't take you. Don't finish its job.' Word for word. I've got it memorized now, which took me fifteen years and a cave."

"He was patient with you."

"He had time," I said, and it came out darker than I meant, and she took my hand across the fire, and neither of us said anything else, because some silences are load-bearing and you don't renovate them.

* * *

We came down off the passes on the eleventh day, into the green, with the cordillera behind us wearing its snow like a collar it can't quite shrug off. The clients were transformed — sunburned, horse-sore, quieter in the good way, the way people get when a landscape has gotten inside them and started rearranging the furniture. Irma hugged me at the trailhead, which I tolerated, and then hugged Elena, which Elena encouraged, and the Welsh sisters shook hands all around like closing a business deal, and Mateo asked, carefully, the way you ask about something fragile:

"Can I come back? Next season? To learn?"

I looked at Elena. Elena looked at the sky, which is her way of saying it's your call and she already knows the answer.

"Come back," I said. "Bring better boots. And stop knowing more about condors than me — it's demoralizing."

He grinned like sunrise and caught the bus east, and the steppe swallowed the road behind him, and Elena and I stood in the gold light with empty panniers and nowhere in particular to be.

"So," she said. "What comes next?"

It's the question she asks every few months now, gently, like testing ice. Because for fifteen years the answer was always the same — next is away, next is further, next is the next hundred kilometers — and she knows the answer changed, and I think part of her still checks, the way you check a healed bone in cold weather.

I looked west, where the passes stood open. I looked east, where the tide was pulling back off the rocks, getting ready, in a month or so, to lie again — to expose the cave mouth with my brother's initials on the wall, where I'd leave the compass facing east forever, and where my own mark now sits beside his, cut shallow and clean, continuing rather than erasing. I looked at Elena, wind-blown and sunburned and reading my face like an archive.

"I don't know," I said, and it was the best answer I've ever given anyone. "For the first time in my life, I genuinely don't know. And I'm not measuring the not-knowing in miles."

"No?"

"No. Maybe in seasons. Maybe in mornings." I shouldered the empty pack — light, so light, the shoulders barely remembering their old shape. "Maybe in guanacos."

"That's a terrible unit."

"It's the only honest one. They always know which way to go."

We turned toward home, which is a word I use now without flinching, and the steppe ran gold ahead of us in every direction at once, and for once in my life every direction was the same direction — hers.

Behind us, far off on the southern horizon, a single plume of dust rose where no road runs, and I noted it the way you note weather: calmly, professionally, with a full larder and a whole heart, ready for whatever the dry side brings next.
