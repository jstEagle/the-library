## 1. The Closed Lines

The town of Port Salakan announced itself the way old men announce themselves at parties: with a cough, a smell, and no apology whatsoever.

I came in on the morning ferry from the mainland, standing at the rail while the harbor resolved out of haze — a crescent of rust-brown water, a breakwater held together more by habit than concrete, and behind it a skyline of corrugated roofs the color of old pennies. Somewhere in that jumble was a railway that hadn't run in thirty years, and somewhere in my breast pocket was a contract I'd signed because refusing it would have cost me everything my grandfather hadn't already spent.

"Engineer?" said the man beside me, not looking up from his phone.

"That's me."

"You're late."

"I'm early," I said. "The survey doesn't start until tomorrow."

He finally looked up. He had the smooth, well-fed face of a man whose money did his sweating for him — Voss, the financier, though he'd introduced himself over email as "a partner in regional infrastructure renewal," which is the kind of phrase that means nothing and costs somebody everything. "Ms. Okonkwo. Good. You'll want to see the line before the light goes."

"The light has barely arrived."

"It leaves early here." He smiled like a man quoting terms. "Monsoon season. We'll discuss the scope tonight. My boat is at the pier — the white one, you can't miss it. Everything else in this town is brown."

He was right about that. Everything else was brown: the warehouses, the hills, the river delta spreading inland like spilled tea. I watched him go up the gangway to a launch so clean it looked embarrassed by its surroundings, and then I turned and looked at what I'd actually come to do.

* * *

The railway started — or ended; dead lines don't much care about direction — at a shed two kilometers east of the harbor, where the mangroves had been patiently eating the embankment for three decades and were winning. The track ran west along the coast, or had once. Now it was a spine of rusted rail on rotted sleepers, disappearing into green so aggressive it seemed personal.

My grandfather would have loved it.

That's an ugly thing to admit about a man you loved, but there it was. Emile Okonkwo had spent forty years building railways across half of West Africa and the last twenty of those complaining that nobody built them properly anymore. He'd carried a brass compass everywhere — not a surveyor's instrument but a sailor's pocket compass, heavy as a knuckle-duster, with a needle that wobbled before settling like a bird deciding whether to trust a branch. When he died he left me two things: the compass, and a leather folder of maps marked in his cramped hand with lines through the Coral Triangle. Closed lines. Abandoned lines. Lines that had never been finished at all.

And one map, folded into eighths until the creases went soft as cloth, with a route inked up a river I now stood within smelling distance of, annotated in his handwriting: *Ask about the high route. Ask who closed it, and why.*

I'd asked. For six years I'd asked, in archives and ministries and the offices of men who owned things. The answer was always the same polite version of *no*, until Voss found me — or rather, found my debt. A bridge contract gone bad in a cyclone season, insurance that evaporated like morning fog, and a signature on a loan with terms that read like a threat dressed in a suit. Voss bought the paper. Then he offered me a job.

*Survey the decommissioned coastal railway between Salakan and Tanjung Rhu. Condition assessment, reinstatement feasibility.* Straightforward. Honest work, even. Except for three things.

First: nobody reinstates a coastal railway. The road parallel to it carries every truck on the island, and the towns it served have moved inland. There is no economic case. I could prove that in a week with a calculator and a bicycle.

Second: the contract's fine print gave Voss's company rights to "all cartographic materials produced or consulted during the engagement." Consulted. Which meant my grandfather's maps, if I used them — and how does an engineer survey anything without consulting her references?

Third: the expedition. Because I wasn't the only person Voss had hired this season. Three weeks ago a scientific party had gone upriver — geologists, a glaciologist, someone's cousin doing hydrology — funded by the same infrastructure-renewal partner, following the same general direction as the inked line on my grandfather's map. They'd checked in by radio every evening at nine.

I knew all this before I arrived. What I didn't know — what I learned my first afternoon, walking the dead line with a measuring wheel and my grandfather's compass riding my hip like a holstered opinion — was how much the town knew, and how carefully it wasn't saying.

* * *

The station master's office still stood at the old Salakan halt, which surprised me until I met the woman inside it. Her name was Ibu Rahayu, and she was perhaps seventy, and she had kept the office swept and the timetable current — the 1987 timetable — framed on the wall behind her desk, because, she informed me, "the trains are only delayed, not cancelled. You cannot cancel a promise."

"I'm surveying the line," I told her. "For reinstatement."

She looked at me the way you look at a child who has announced they will personally dig to the other side of the world. Kindly. With arithmetic happening behind her eyes.

"Reinstatement," she repeated. "Who wants the trains back?"

"The money wants them back."

"The money." She nodded slowly and poured me tea from a thermos that had outlived several governments. "Money came through here before too. Before the line closed. Men with papers. They wanted something further upriver, and the railway was how they meant to take it. When the taking got difficult, they closed the line and called it finished." She set down my cup. "You are measuring a corpse, Nona. But corpses tell you how they died, if you know where to look."

I looked. God help me, I looked, because that's what I'm for.

The rails themselves were sound — good steel, laid by people who knew their trade, and I say that with professional affection. But the closures told stories. The points at the junction weren't worn out; they'd been removed, bolts backed off clean, hardware salvaged deliberately. The culverts at kilometer four hadn't failed; they'd been filled with graded stone, a proper engineering job, done fast, by people who wanted that section impassable *specifically*. And at kilometer seven, where the line bent inland toward the river gorge, the ballast was disturbed in a pattern I recognized from bridge forensics: heavy equipment, tracked vehicles, something hauled out under load toward the water.

Somebody hadn't just closed this railway. Somebody had extracted something from it, or blocked access to something beyond it, and they'd done it with competence and haste.

I stood in the green silence with my wheel stopped and my compass out, more from habit than need, and watched the needle swing, settle, swing again. Northeast. Toward the gorge. Toward the river. Toward the same coordinates my grandfather had circled twice and starred once, in a hand that shook only near the end.

*Ask who closed it, and why.*

"Find something?" said a voice behind me, and I nearly put the compass through my own teeth.

* * *

The girl — young woman, I corrected myself, though she had the coiled stillness of someone older than her years — stood on the embankment with a duffel over one shoulder and an expression that suggested she'd been watching me longer than I'd have liked. She wore field clothes that fit badly and hiking boots that fit worse, and she held herself like a person who had rehearsed this conversation and resented needing to.

"You're the engineer," she said. Not a question.

"And you're standing on protected track," I said, because deflection is also a skill. "Structurally speaking."

"There's no structure left to protect." She came down the embankment with a sureness that contradicted her boots. "Mara Lindqvist. My father runs the expedition upriver. Ran. Past tense is doing some work today."

Something cold walked down my spine wearing small boots. "Past tense?"

"They missed the check-in. Last night, channel nine. No radio since." She said it flatly, each word placed like a stone in a wall. "Voss's people are saying equipment failure. Weather moving in. Nothing to worry about yet." Her jaw did something complicated. "I've heard 'nothing to worry about' my whole life. It's usually the loudest warning there is."

"Mara Lindqvist," I repeated. "Your father is Henrik Lindqvist? The glaciologist?"

"You know his work."

"I've read his ice-core papers. Everyone in my field who works near water has." I hesitated. "You said *ran*. You're estranged. That's not a question either — you flinched when you said 'my father.'"

Her chin came up, and for a second I saw the flinch confirmed and hated myself for the accuracy. "We don't speak. Haven't in four years. That's why I'm here instead of there — I came to fix it and I waited too long and now the monsoon's coming and the radio's quiet and all I can do is stand on your dead railway bothering the surveyor." She exhaled. "Sorry. That was — I've been practicing being calm for two days and it isn't holding."

"It rarely does." I looked north, where the green thickened toward the hills and the river ran somewhere underneath it all, patient as debt. The first clouds were stacking up over the ridgeline, gray going bruise-colored, and the air had that pressure-cooker weight I knew from cyclone coasts — the weather loading itself like a gun. "When's the next check-in window?"

"Nine tonight. Channel nine." She almost smiled, bitterly. "They named the channel after the hour. Henrik's idea. He thought it was tidy."

"And if they miss it?"

"Then Voss mounts a search, eventually, when it's cheap enough to bother." She looked at me directly then, and I saw she'd done her homework too. "You're contracted to him. So am I, effectively — he paid my passage, thinking I might be useful leverage someday. He's a man who keeps tools." A beat. "So am I asking you a favor, engineer, or comparing inventories?"

Above us, thunder cleared its throat somewhere over the interior, a long rolling syllable in a language older than either of our countries. The compass on my hip felt suddenly heavy, the way inherited things do when they start meaning something.

"Neither," I said. "Yet. Let's hear the nine o'clock silence first."

But I already knew, the way you know a span is overloaded before the crack shows — reading stress lines in steel, in contracts, in the faces of strangers on dead track. The job was a pretext. It had always been a pretext. And whatever Voss wanted from the closed lines, my grandfather's map pointed at the same place the silence did: upriver, toward the gorge, toward the high route.

At nine that night, channel nine stayed quiet.

By ten, Voss was knocking on my door with a rescue contract in one hand and a pen in the other, and Mara was already standing in the hallway, duffel packed, as if she'd known before any of us.

"We leave at dawn," Voss said. "There's a pilot. A local. Try not to frighten him."

I signed, because I had no choice, and because some part of me — the part that had walked a corpse of a railway all day listening to it name its murderer — had already decided.
