## 2. Silence on Channel Nine

The radio went silent on my third day of measuring dead track, which is the sort of coincidence that makes an engineer suspicious and a superstitious person religious. I was neither, exactly, but I had learned to read patterns the way my grandfather read weather — by the feel of things against the skin of the ordinary.

I was kneeling at kilometer fourteen with a surveyor's chain and a notebook gone soft with humidity, counting sleepers that had rotted to the consistency of biscotti, when the port office boy came pelting down the embankment waving a message flimsy like it was on fire.

"Madam engineer. Madam. The office says come now. The radio — the *Kestrel* —"

The *Kestrel* was the expedition boat. I knew this because everyone in Port Salakan knew it by then, the way small towns absorb the business of outsiders and make it common property. A scientific survey upriver, financed by a man named Voss, hunting for — nobody could say precisely what. Old mineral seams. A navigable high route. Something worth a contract with clauses thick enough to choke on.

I rolled my chain, shouldered my kit, and followed the boy at a pace that pretended to be professional and was actually fear. Because here is the thing about being hired to survey a decommissioned railway: the contract had specified, in clause eleven, that my survey would "accommodate any operational requirements arising during the engagement period." I had read that clause four times on the flight in, and each time it had looked less like boilerplate and more like a hook.

Voss was waiting in the port office, and Voss was not a man who waited well. He stood at the window with his back to the door, a tall, dry arrangement of a man, all angles and pressed linen, like a filing cabinet that had learned to walk. The radio sat on the desk between a cold cup of coffee and a stack of contracts, hissing static into the room.

"Channel nine," he said, without turning. "They've missed three scheduled check-ins."

"Radios fail," I said. "Batteries die. Antennas bend. Rivers eat electronics for breakfast."

"Dr. Reyes doesn't miss check-ins." He turned then, and I got the full force of his attention, which was like being measured for a coffin. "He is a man who broadcasts his position when he stops for lunch. Three silences in a row is not equipment failure. It is a decision, or a disaster."

"Or a monsoon," I said, nodding at the window, where the sky over the harbor had gone the color of wet slate. The first rain bands were still out at sea, a smudge like a bruise on the horizon, but the air had that pressed, waiting quality that every sailor and engineer in the tropics learns to dread. "You hired me to survey track, Mr. Voss. Clause eleven or no clause eleven, I'm not a rescue service."

"You're an engineer." He said the word the way some men say *doctor* and other men say *lawyer* — with a weight that was half respect, half accusation. "The *Kestrel* is a forty-year-old river boat with a hull designed for calm coastal water. If she's holed, if her engine's flooded, if her crew is alive and immobile upriver, then what they need is not a hero. It's someone who can keep machinery alive long enough to bring them home. That is you, Ms. Okonkwo."

I looked at the radio. The static hissed on, patient and enormous, like the sound the world makes when it's decided not to answer you.

"And if I refuse?"

"Then clause eleven becomes clause twelve, breach, and the debt comes due in full." He said it without cruelty, which was somehow worse. Cruelty you can fight. Arithmetic you can only obey. "I'd rather not. You come well recommended. Your grandfather's line, in fact — he surveyed the original coastal extension. The compass is yours, I understand."

That landed somewhere below my ribs. I touched my jacket pocket without meaning to, where the brass compass sat with its needle that had never once pointed at anything I wanted. My grandfather had left me two things: the compass, and the map of the closed lines, and the map was the reason I was standing in a port office at the edge of a monsoon owing a stranger money I couldn't count.

"Fuel," I said. "Boat, fuel, a crew who knows the river, and my debt reduced by half for the rescue work. Not waived. Reduced. I don't work for free and I don't work for gratitude."

Something flickered at the corner of Voss's mouth that on a warmer face might have been a smile. "Half. Done. There's a pilot — Tuan, he runs the upper reaches. He'll meet you at the north jetty in an hour. Pack for cold. The river climbs."

* * *

The north jetty was a toothless thing, pilings black with salt rot, and the boat tied to it looked like a joke someone had told forty years ago and never finished. She was maybe twelve meters, wooden, patched in a dozen places with metal that didn't match, and she sat in the water with the resigned air of an old woman at a bus stop.

The pilot was already there, checking a fuel line with hands that moved like they'd been born knowing that engine. He was younger than I expected, maybe thirty, with the stillness of someone who had grown up on water and didn't need to prove it. He looked up when I came down the planks, took in my kit, my face, and offered nothing in the way of welcome.

"You're the engineer," he said. Not a question. His English had the flat, careful precision of a man who had learned it from manuals and chose his words like rivets.

"Elodie Okonkwo."

"Tuan." He went back to the fuel line. "You've done river work?"

"Rail work. Water's just track that moves."

That got a flicker of something — not quite a smile, more like the memory of one — before it was gone. "The *Kestrel* went up past the second gorge. Three days' run if the weather holds. It won't." He nodded at the sky. "Monsoon's early this year. We'll be running ahead of it, and if we're slow, we'll be running inside it."

"Then we'd better not be slow."

"We," he repeated, and looked at me again, longer this time, and I understood I was being weighed against some private ledger of outsiders who had come up this river and taken things. I didn't blame him for the arithmetic. I'd have run the same numbers.

That was when I heard the footsteps on the jetty — fast, uneven, the walk of someone who had decided to run out of patience before she ran out of jetty.

"I'm coming."

She was perhaps twenty-five, rain jacket over clothes that had been packed in a hurry, a duffel bag over one shoulder and a expression that had clearly survived several arguments already and intended to survive more. Dark hair cut short and practical. Eyes that were furious and frightened in equal measure, and I recognized the mixture because I'd worn it myself for years.

"Mara." Tuan said the name like a warning.

"Mara Reyes," she said, to me, holding out a hand as if we were at a garden party and not on a rotting jetty under a bruised sky. "Dr. Reyes is my father. And before you say it — yes, I know he's upriver. No, I haven't spoken to him in two years. No, I don't need your sympathy, and no, you can't leave me here, because I'll charter something worse and follow you anyway."

I looked at Tuan. Tuan looked at the sky, the boat, and the fuel line, in that order, with the expression of a man recalculating a load distribution.

"Two years," I said. "And you drop everything when his radio goes quiet."

"Family is like that." Her voice didn't waver, but her hand on the duffel strap had gone white at the knuckles. "You can stop speaking to someone and still know, in your bones, the exact moment they need you. I've known for three days. I've been at this jetty for two. Voss wouldn't put me on the manifest. So I'm asking you." She turned the full weight of those eyes on me, and I felt the hook of clause eleven find some new purchase. "You're the one Voss actually hired. Say yes."

Here is what I knew: I knew nothing about this woman except that she was angry and loyal in the specific, contradictory way that gets people killed. I knew the river was climbing into a monsoon. I knew my grandfather's compass was in my pocket and my grandfather's debts were in my name, and that both of them had a way of pointing at things I hadn't chosen.

I also knew that if my father's radio had gone silent on some dead line in some drowned country, I would have wanted someone to say yes to me.

"Yes," I said. "But you bilge when I tell you to bilge, you hold what I tell you to hold, and you don't touch the engine. I run the boat if it comes to that. Deal?"

"Deal."

Tuan exhaled through his nose — the closest thing to a vote I was going to get — and cast off the lines. The engine caught on the third try with a cough like a smoker waking up, and we slid out past the jetty into water that had already gone the color of gunmetal.

The first rain band caught us twenty minutes out. It came across the river like a moving wall, and Tuan took us into it without slowing, and I stood at the bow with the spray hitting my face and the compass in my pocket and the static of that silent radio still in my ears.

Three silences. Three days of a man broadcasting his lunch stops going quiet all at once.

Ahead of us the river bent north, toward the gorges, toward the climbing cold, and behind us Port Salakan shrank to a gray smear on the water. Mara stood at the stern watching it go, and I watched her watch it, and thought: *we are all of us running toward something we said we were done with.*

The rain thickened. Tuan opened the throttle.
