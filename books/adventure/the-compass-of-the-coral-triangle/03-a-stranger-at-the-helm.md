## 3. A Stranger at the Helm

The boat was older than all of us, and I told her so before we'd even cast off.

"Forty-one years," I said, patting the gunwale like you'd pat a stubborn grandfather. "She was hauling cargo up this river before your father learned to walk. She leaks when she's nervous and she lies about her fuel gauge. Treat her right and she'll get you there. Treat her wrong and the river will decide for you."

The woman — Okonkwo, the engineer, the one with the debt and the dead railway in her eyes — looked at me the way outsiders look at everything here: like it was a problem that could be solved with a schedule.

"I've worked on older equipment," she said.

"You haven't worked on *her*."

Mara Voss stood at the dock's edge with her arms crossed, wearing grief like a coat two sizes too small. She hadn't said ten words since we'd loaded the supplies, and every one of them had been a question about river depth, current speed, travel time. Efficient questions. Questions from someone who'd decided feelings were ballast to be thrown overboard.

I resented them both, if you want the truth. The whole expedition, the whole enterprise of outsiders coming up my river with their radios and their contracts and their money that arrived wet with other people's trouble. Voss senior had hired me through a man named Calloway, who paid half up front and smiled like the other half was a formality. Men like Calloway smile like that because they've never once been on the losing end of a contract. My village had. That was a story for another day, or no day.

But they were paying, the monsoon was four days out by the smell of it, and my mother needed the roof finished before the sky came down. So: strangers at my helm, and my opinions kept below decks where they belonged.

* * *

We left at first light, which on the coast means gray light, which means light the way an apology is an explanation. The river took us the way it takes everyone — gently at first, all patience and brown silk, hiding its elbows.

Okonkwo stood at the bow for the first hour without speaking. I noticed because most passengers talk. They narrate. They point at herons and say "look" as though I might have missed forty years of herons. She just watched the water, and watched it the way I watch it — reading, not sightseeing. When we came to the first set of standing waves above the old ferry crossing, she shifted her weight before the boat did. Small thing. Most people never learn that trick. Most people never learn anything a river teaches.

"You've run this stretch before," she said, coming back to the helm. Not a question exactly, but not flattery either. Just an observation, laid down flat like a plank across mud.

"Since I was nine."

"And the channel markers?"

"Gone. Government pulled them when the line closed. Said the river wasn't worth marking anymore." I shrugged. "The river disagreed. It's still here. The markers aren't. Draw your own conclusions about who won."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. More like the beginning of an agreement between her face and mine that neither of us wanted to sign yet.

* * *

It was near midday, running the slow water below the first rapids, that I saw the compass.

She'd taken it out to check our heading against the bend — unnecessary, since I could run this bend blindfolded and have, once, though I don't recommend it — and the sun caught the brass case as she opened it. Old brass. Hand-worn brass. And on the inside of the lid, when it swung open, a repair seam. A solder line running neat and fine along the hinge plate, the kind of work nobody does anymore because nobody alive knows how to do it anymore except—

I cut the engine.

The silence after was enormous. Okonkwo looked up sharply. Mara came forward from the stern with her hand already halfway into her pack, whatever she kept in there.

"What is it?" Okonkwo asked.

"That compass." My voice came out lower than I intended. "Where did you get it?"

"It was my grandfather's. Why?"

"Open the lid again. Slowly."

She did. There it was. The solder seam, fine as a spider's leg, and beneath it — I leaned close, not touching, never touching another man's compass, that's a rule older than my boat — the tiny stamped mark. A fish hook and a star. My father's mark. He put it under every repair, hidden where only another repairman would think to look, because he said a mended thing should carry its mending proudly but privately.

"My father repaired this," I said. "Twenty years ago, maybe more. A foreign surveyor brought it in. Said the needle had gone false after a fall in the gorge country."

Okonkwo went very still. The kind of still that has weather underneath it.

"He fell in the gorge country," she repeated. "That would have been — yes. Yes, that would have been him. He was surveying closed lines upriver. He never talked much about the people he met. But he used to say—" She stopped. Started again. "He used to say the best hands he ever saw were on a boy who fixed his compass in a village whose name he could never pronounce properly."

"Kampung Selat," I said. "Nobody pronounces it properly."

"No," she said softly. "He said it wrong for thirty years. *Selat-ah.* Like that."

My father died six years ago with calluses no glove ever fit and a toolbox I keep under my berth and cannot bring myself to sort. He fixed things for whoever came up the river, and he charged outsiders double and then quietly gave half of it back to the ones who were polite, and he taught me that a thing that has been broken well and mended well is worth more than a thing never broken at all. I had forgotten that last part until a stranger opened a brass compass on my deck and showed me my father's handwriting in metal.

"Well," I said, because I am not a man made for moments, "he charged your grandfather double."

Okonkwo laughed. Actually laughed, short and surprised, like the sound had escaped custody. "He mentioned something about that."

"He would have." I restarted the engine. The old girl coughed twice and settled, grumbling. "Hold your heading. We'll make the second camp by dark if the rain holds off."

"The rain isn't going to hold off," Mara said from the stern. First words in an hour, and of course they were correct.

"No," I agreed. "It isn't."

* * *

The first rain band caught us an hour later, and it caught us the way monsoons do — not as weather but as a decision the sky had made about us personally. One minute the air was thick and waiting; the next, the world went white and loud and the river's surface vanished under a million fists.

I've run rain before. This was different. This had weight behind it, weight gathering somewhere north in mountains we couldn't see, and every hour it fell meant the river would rise tomorrow and the day after, rising faster than any of us could pretend to plan for.

"Get the tarp over the cargo!" I shouted. "Tie down anything that floats away with regrets!"

They worked. I'll give them that. Mara moved like someone who'd grown up around boats despite her city voice, and Okonkwo — Okonkwo tied knots I didn't teach her, tensioned lines against the swell instead of against the wind, and when the tarp ripped loose at the grommet she didn't panic, she re-rigged it around the winch post in about ninety seconds flat.

"You've done this," I yelled at her over the noise.

"Railroads flood too!" she yelled back. "Water doesn't care what it's ruining!"

There it was again. That agreement, signing itself a clause at a time.

By full dark we'd tucked into the lee of a bluff where the current slackened, and I let the boat swing on her anchor while the rain drummed the canopy like impatient fingers. Nobody slept much. Mara sat wrapped in a blanket staring at nothing with the particular intensity of someone staring at a memory. Okonkwo checked the bilge twice, found it manageable, and sat beside me under the wheelhouse awning with a thermos of coffee she'd apparently brought from a civilization with standards.

"For the pilot," she said.

I took it. It was good coffee. I hated that it was good coffee. It felt like being bribed with competence.

"Your father," she said, after a while. "The repair. Did he say anything else? About my grandfather?"

I turned the cup in my hands. Rain ran off the awning in ropes.

"He said the man was stubborn. That he argued with the price and then paid it without arguing, which my father considered the mark of an honest opponent." I paused. "And he said the man was going somewhere he shouldn't go, and my father told him so, and your grandfather said that was why he was going."

Okonkwo looked out at the black water. "That sounds like him."

"The expedition leader — Voss. Your grandfather knew him?"

"They surveyed together. Before the falling-out. Before Mara stopped speaking to him." She said it carefully, glancing toward the stern, making sure the blanket-shape there was still asleep or pretending. "This whole river is full of things people stopped doing. Stopped speaking. Stopped marking channels. Stopped answering radios."

I followed her glance. "You're worried about the silence."

"Aren't you?"

I was. I had been since Calloway handed me the charter with that smile. Radios fail, sure. Batteries die, antennas snap, men forget to check in because they found something interesting. All true. But three days of silence on Channel Nine, in monsoon season, from a party that included a radio operator who'd once sent weather reports hourly like clockwork — that wasn't failure. That was a door closing from the inside.

Or from the outside.

"There's something else," I said, keeping my voice low. "Two days ago, a boat came through my village heading upriver. Fast, heavy-laden, crewed by men who bought fuel and asked no questions and tipped like men spending someone else's money. They weren't fishermen. Fishermen ask about fish."

Okonkwo set down her cup. "Calloway's men?"

"I didn't ask. Men like that don't answer, and asking tells them what you know." I looked at the rain hammering down, at the river swelling already beneath it, brown and patient and full of elbows. "But if Voss's expedition went quiet, and someone else went upriver right after, quiet—"

"Then the silence isn't an accident," she finished.

The rain filled the pause between us, drumming, insistent, like a countdown neither of us had agreed to hear.

I should have turned the boat around then. I want that on the record, wherever records go. I knew the river was rising, knew the money was dirty, knew that men who tip like that tip for silence. Every sensible bone in my body voted for home, for my mother's unfinished roof, for the warm dry ordinary life of a man who fixes engines and minds his own water.

But my father had mended that compass. And a mended thing, he always said, carries its mending proudly but privately — and maybe that goes for people too. Maybe that's why I stood up, checked the anchor line, and started calculating fuel for a longer run than the one I'd been paid for.

"Get some sleep," I told Okonkwo. "Both of you. Tomorrow we pass the last marked landing, and after that it's my river and no map."

From the stern, in the dark, Mara's voice came quiet and awake:

"She was never anyone's map to begin with."

The rain said nothing back. It just kept falling, harder now, the way it does when it knows something you don't.
