## 4. The Leaking Hull

The monsoon didn't arrive so much as it moved in, the way a debt collector does — politely at first, then all at once, and then it owns the furniture.

I'd been below deck cataloguing Tuan's tools when the first real band hit. The boat lurched like a horse stepping in a hole, and everything I'd so carefully organized became a percussion section. A wrench found my shin. The compass — my grandfather's brass compass, riding in my breast pocket where it had ridden for three days now — pressed cold against my sternum like a small disapproving hand.

"Get forward!" Tuan's voice came down through the deck boards, flattened by rain into something almost calm. "Weight forward! Now!"

I hauled myself up the companionway into a world that had stopped pretending to be anything but water. The sky wasn't falling; it was simply *here*, occupying the same space as the air, and the river beneath us had gone from brown to the color of wet iron. Rain came sideways with enough force to sting. Somewhere ahead, thunder rolled along the gorge walls like freight cars coupling.

Mara was already at the bow, hauling crates aft of her feet, shouting something I couldn't hear over the drumming on the canopy. I joined her, and we worked without words — she pointed, I lifted, we shifted two hundred kilos of expedition supplies toward the front of the boat while Tuan fought the wheel behind us. It was the kind of work I knew how to do. Weight distribution is weight distribution, whether you're ballasting a locomotive or keeping a forty-year-old riverboat from broaching in floodwater.

The problem revealed itself an hour later, when the problem always reveals itself: too late and from below.

Tuan killed the engine in the lee of a bluff — a shelf of black rock that broke the wind if not the rain — and went quiet in the way that engineers go quiet, which is to say he listened. I listened too. Under the rain hammering the deck, under the river grinding against the hull, there was another sound. A thinner one.

A pump running constantly.

"That bilge pump," I said, "is rated for a boat twice this size?"

"It's rated for this boat." Tuan's jaw was tight. "It's not rated for what's coming through."

We opened the floor hatch together. The bilge was half full and rising, brown water sloshing around a tangle of fuel line, and along the port side, just above the turn of the hull, a seam had opened — not dramatically, nothing cinematic, just a dark line weeping water in a steady pulse timed to the waves. The caulking had given up somewhere in the last decade and nobody had told it.

"How long?" Mara asked from the hatch rim, her face pale in the grey light.

"At this rate? Hours." Tuan said it flatly, the way you say a fact about weather. "Less if we run the engine hard. The vibration works the seam wider."

"So we don't run hard," I said.

"We don't run at all, we drift onto something that opens it *wide*." He looked at me then, really looked, the assessment naked and unembarrassed. "You're the engineer. Tell me you have an idea that isn't prayer."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about engineering school: they teach you to design things correctly, with correct materials, correct tolerances, correct everything. Nobody teaches you what to do when correctness is four hundred kilometers away and all you have is a leaking boat, a monsoon, and whatever's in the hold.

What was in the hold, as it happened, was rail plate.

Three plates of it, salvaged off the decommissioned line I'd spent two weeks surveying before the radio went silent — dead track I'd measured and photographed and been paid (badly) to document. The financier hadn't wanted them aboard; they were "dead weight." I'd argued they were samples. In truth I'd argued because I liked having them near me, the way some people carry a stone from home. Steel from a line my grandfather helped close. Steel that had lain in the jungle for thirty years doing nothing, waiting, apparently, for exactly this.

"You want to weld?" Tuan crouched beside the plates, skeptical. "In this?"

"I want to bolt. Welding needs dry metal and stable current and neither exists within a hundred miles." I was already measuring the seam with my tape, already doing arithmetic in my head. "We cut the plate to overlap the seam by fifteen centimeters each side. Drill through the hull skin into the frame members — the frames are sound, I felt them when we loaded. Bolt it tight with a rubber gasket made from—" I scanned the deck. "Those fenders. Cut them into strips. And seal the edges with—"

"There's no marine sealant aboard."

"There's bitumen paint in your locker. I saw it when I was cataloguing. It's not elegant, but coal tar has been keeping boats alive longer than either of us has been alive."

He stared at me. Then he laughed — one short bark, surprised out of him. "You've done this before."

"I've patched a locomotive boiler with a truck inner tube and hose clamps at forty degrees Celsius. Boats are just trains that got confused." I was already pulling the angle grinder from his toolbox. "Give me your generator and get me light."

What followed was six hours of the best work I've ever done, which is to say the worst conditions and the most necessary outcome. The generator coughed and died twice; Tuan coaxed it back both times with his hands moving like he was soothing an animal. Mara held the work lamp and learned to anticipate where I'd need it next, shifting the beam before I asked. The rain never stopped. The river kept rising — you could see it climbing the bluff, a hand-width an hour, patient as interest accruing.

I drilled the first hole through the hull myself and hated every second of it. There is a specific horror in deliberately putting holes in a floating thing. My hands shook until the first bolt went through with its rubber washer and bitumen-smeared plate behind it, and then the shaking stopped, because the water slowed, and slowing water is the most persuasive argument in the world.

By the time we set the second plate, I'd stopped noticing the rain. That's the secret fear doesn't advertise: it can be used. Fear is energy with nowhere to go, and if you give it a task — measure, cut, drill, torque, check — it becomes something else entirely. Competence is just terror that found employment.

The bilge pump, when Tuan finally switched it from continuous to intermittent, sounded almost smug.

* * *

We ate cold rice and tinned fish in the cabin while the storm worked itself into a rhythm outside, long rolling pulses of wind like breathing. The lamp swung overhead. My arms ached in a way I intended to remember fondly later.

Mara sat across from me, turning her spoon over without eating much. She'd held that lamp steady for six hours without complaint, and now her hands wouldn't quite settle.

"The last time I saw my father," she said, to the spoon more than to us, "we fought about the map."

Tuan glanced up. I kept still. You learn on expeditions — even short, doomed ones — when someone is building toward something and needs you not to move.

"He had this theory about the closed lines. The high routes. He thought the old survey teams found something up there and buried the findings — literally buried them, reports sealed, lines decommissioned as cover." Her mouth twisted. "I told him it was conspiracy thinking. I told him he'd sacrificed everything real — Mom, me, the house, every birthday since I was nine — chasing a story he invented so the abandoning would feel like destiny instead of choice."

The lamp swung. Shadows crossed her face.

"He didn't argue back. That was the worst part. He just said, 'You'll understand when you find it,' and I said, 'I'm not going to find anything, I'm not going anywhere with you,' and I meant it. Three years I meant it." She finally looked up. "Then the radio went silent and I was on a plane in nine hours. So apparently I lied."

"You didn't lie," I said. "Anger is true when you say it. It just isn't permanent."

She studied me like I'd spoken in a language she half remembered. "Why are you here, Elodie? Really. The financier pays you to survey dead track, and suddenly you're welding his rescue boat together in a typhoon. That's not a contract. Contracts don't bleed."

I touched the compass through my shirt, an unconscious gesture I was starting to resent in myself.

"My grandfather worked these lines. Closed them, some of them. When he died he left me his compass and a map with annotations — closed routes, sidings, places the official record says don't exist." I heard my own voice go careful. "And debts. His debts. The man who hired me holds most of them. So I tell myself I'm working off an obligation. But Mara — the map matches your father's theory. The lines he thought were buried. My grandfather annotated them like a man leaving instructions."

Tuan had gone very still by the wheel. I caught him looking at my breast pocket, at the shape of the brass under the fabric, with an expression I couldn't read yet.

"So we're all chasing the same ghost," Mara said quietly. "My father, your grandfather, and us."

"And possibly whoever sent a second boat," Tuan said.

The cabin went cold in a way the rain couldn't account for.

"What second boat?" Mara asked.

Tuan's eyes stayed on the black water sliding past the porthole. "Fuel drums on the dock, the day we left. More than the manifest called for. And a man asking questions about our departure time who claimed to be a fisheries inspector and wore shoes no inspector ever owned." He turned back to us. "I didn't say anything because I wasn't sure. Tonight, checking the bilge, I checked the wake trail on the radar replay too. Something's been holding station behind us since noon. Matching our speed. Staying just inside the storm clutter."

Outside, the thunder rolled again, closer, and under it — or maybe I imagined it, maybe fear was inventing overtime — I thought I heard an engine that wasn't ours, idling somewhere out in the dark, patient as the rising water.
