## 5. What the River Keeps

My father taught me three things about rivers before the river took him. First: the water always tells you where it's been. Second: it never tells you everything. Third: if you find something on a riverbank that shouldn't be there, stand still and count to ten before you touch it, because whatever put it there might still be watching.

I was counting to ten when I found the boot.

It sat half-buried in the silt of the forward camp, sole up, laces still tied, as though its owner had simply stepped out of it and walked into the canopy. Nobody steps out of a boot on purpose. Not here. Not with the monsoon two days behind us and closing like a fist.

"Camp," I said, because Elodie and Mara were already climbing the bank behind me, and there was no gentler way to say it.

The expedition's forward camp sat on a shelf of gravel above the high-water line, or what passed for one this far upriver. Three tents, two of them collapsed inward like exhaled lungs. Crates pried open. A tarp shredded into ribbons that snapped in the wind like small flags surrendering. And everywhere, that particular disorder that isn't theft — theft is tidy, theft takes the good things — but flight. A primus stove left standing lit-out. A tin of biscuits punctured cleanly by rain, swollen fat. A pair of reading glasses folded on top of an open journal whose pages had gone to pulp.

Nobody leaves their glasses. You leave money, you leave tools, you leave people you're angry at. Glasses you wear until the end.

"They went upstream," Mara said. She'd gone very quiet, which for her meant she'd gone somewhere worse than loud. She picked up the journal, turned it over, set it down again without reading it. "The coordinates were another twelve kilometers past this point. He wouldn't have stopped."

"He didn't stop," I said. "He ran."

Elodie crouched by the fire ring. She had a way of looking at things — head tilted, eyes narrowed, like she was reading stress lines in steel — that I was beginning to trust more than my own instruments. "Fire's cold but the ash isn't scattered. Wind would've taken it apart in a day." She looked up at me. "Two days? Maybe less."

"Then we're close."

"Or they're gone."

* * *

We spent an hour salvaging what the river hadn't claimed. That was the arrangement now, unspoken: I handled the boat and the water, Elodie handled anything mechanical, and Mara handled her father's ghost, which seemed to be the heaviest cargo aboard.

She worked through the camp in silence, folding tarps that belonged to men she hadn't spoken to in four years, stacking crates marked with an expedition name she pronounced like a word in a language she'd renounced. Once, she stopped at a tent flap embroidered with initials — R.V., her father, Rafael Voss — and stood there so long I thought the monsoon wind had frozen her. Then she packed the tent without comment, harder than necessary, and said nothing else for twenty minutes.

I know that silence. It's the silence of someone carrying a debt they haven't decided how to pay.

It was Elodie who found the second thing. She called me over with just her voice pitched low, which I was learning was her way of shouting.

"Tuan. Look at this."

At the downstream edge of the camp, in the mud where the bank shelved into the water, there were prop-wash scars. Two sets. One old, furred at the edges by settling silt — that would be the expedition's own boat, days ago. The other was crisp. The mud at its margins still held the sharpness of fresh displacement, and a film of diesel rainbow shimmered on the water where it had pooled against the bank.

"Somebody beached here," Elodie said. "After them. Recently."

I knelt and pressed two fingers into the scar. The silt beneath was still soft, still bleeding water. A day. Maybe eighteen hours. The rain coming down the valley had blurred the finer details, but not enough.

"Outboard," I said. "Big one. Sixty, seventy horsepower. They came in fast and didn't care who heard." I looked at the spacing of the footprints that led up from the landing — long strides, heavy heels, at least three men, maybe four. "And they didn't come to help. Helpers land slow. They walk careful. These ones walked like they owned the ground."

Mara had come to stand over us. Her face had done something complicated. "Salvage rights," she said quietly. "That's what the contract said. My father signed a salvage-and-survey clause with Halloran's company. If the expedition located anything of commercial value, Halloran's people had right of first recovery."

"And if your father found something," I said, "and decided he didn't want Halloran to have it—"

"Then Halloran sends people to take it."

The rain chose that moment to arrive properly — not the polite preliminary drizzle we'd been threading through for two days, but the real thing, the monsoon's vanguard, dropping out of the sky in gray sheets that erased the far bank. We loaded what we could carry and got back on the water because staying on that bank felt like standing in an open mouth.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being a pilot on these rivers: you spend your whole life learning to read water, and then one day the water reads you back.

I ran us upstream through the rising brown flood, working the throttle in the short stabs my father had taught me — never steady, never predictable, because a steady engine in shallow water finds the one rock that ends you. Elodie sat forward with the compass open on her knee, calling depths off the sounder, and Mara sat in the stern with her father's salvaged tent between her boots and her arms wrapped around herself like she was holding her own ribs together.

And all the while I kept looking at the banks.

Every fallen tree was a boat hull. Every shadow under the overhanging fig was men waiting. Twice I cut the engine and let us drift, listening over the rain, and twice the river gave me nothing but itself — the hiss of water on stone, the groan of some giant buttress root shifting in the current, the far-off artillery of the storm moving up the valley toward us.

Paranoia, my father would have said, is just attention with nowhere to go. He'd also say it keeps you alive longer than courage does. Courage gets you counted among the dead; paranoia gets you counted, period.

Because here was the arithmetic I couldn't stop doing. Halloran had hired Elodie before the expedition went silent. Had paid her debts, bought her signature, put her on a decommissioned railway line that any surveyor could have walked. Why does a man buy an engineer he doesn't need? Because he needs one later, somewhere worse, and wants her already obligated. And why does a man keep a second boat crewed with heavy-footed men upriver of the party he's already funded?

Because he doesn't trust the first party. Because he never intended to share.

"You're doing the thing," Elodie said beside me. She'd moved up to the console without my noticing, which bothered me more than it should have. "Where you stare at the treeline like it owes you money."

"It might."

"Talk to me."

So I told her. About the prop scars, the diesel, the stride of the footprints. About the arithmetic. She listened the way she did everything — completely, without blinking, as though distraction were a mechanical fault she'd never tolerate in herself.

"Halloran told me the rescue was insurance-mandated," she said slowly. "Standard liability coverage. But he had the charter papers drawn before the expedition missed its second check-in. I remember thinking the paperwork was fast." Her jaw tightened. "Fast paperwork means the paperwork was written in advance."

"Now you're doing the thing."

"Yes," she said. "And I hate it."

Ahead, the river bent hard around a bluff of black rock, and beyond the bend the valley narrowed — I could see it even through the rain, the walls drawing in like a throat deciding whether to swallow. The gorge. Every pilot on this coast knew it the way sailors know a reef: the water that runs through it during monsoon rise isn't rapids, it's a verdict.

But between us and the gorge, on a gravel bar at the bend, there was a boat.

Not ours. Big-hulled, aluminum, riding low with the weight of too many men and too much equipment, tied off with a painter that somebody had anchored with three stakes instead of one because they expected to stay. A tarp rigged over the stern. Under the tarp, the dull geometry of fuel drums.

No one visible. No fire. In this rain, no smoke either — but the tarp was rigged taut and new, and men who rig tarps well intend to come back.

I killed the engine and let the current hold us against the bend, hidden by the rock, and the three of us looked at each other across the console while the rain hammered the deck like a demand for entry.

"That's them," Mara whispered. "Halloran's people. They're staging here — blocking the route to the gorge."

"Or waiting for your father to come back down," I said. "Either way, they're between us and everyone we're looking for."

Elodie was already calculating — I could see it, the tilt of the head, the reading of stress lines. "Their boat draws more than ours. There's a channel on the far side of the bar, along the bluff — I saw the eddy line from the last bend. Shallow, fast, ugly. But passable."

"Passable," I repeated.

"I didn't say pleasant."

I looked at the other boat, then at the black throat of the gorge beyond it, then at the two women in my boat — one running from a father, one running from a debt, both of them, I realized with a feeling I hadn't budgeted for, mine to get through this alive.

My father's third rule. Count to ten before you touch it.

I'd counted. Whatever watched from that gravel bar, we weren't going to wait for it to finish watching us.

"Stay low," I said, and fed the throttle.
