## 6. The Financier's Shadow

My father taught me one rule about rivers, back when he still spoke to me in full sentences instead of radio silences: *the water tells you what's coming before the sky does.* He meant it literally — debris riding a current that shouldn't be there, birds lifting off a bank all at once, the particular hush a river makes when something big is displacing it upstream. I'd spent eleven years pretending I hadn't learned anything from him, and here I was, three days upriver with two strangers and a leaking boat, reading the water like his daughter.

Because there was debris where there shouldn't have been.

A plastic jerry can, dented, riding the current seam. Then a coil of polypropylene rope, waterlogged, moving too fast for the slack eddy it should have drowned in. Tuan saw it at the same moment I did — I knew because his hands went still on the wheel, and Tuan's hands never went still. He was always adjusting, trimming, coaxing the old boat like a man arguing gently with a stubborn relative.

"Second boat," he said quietly.

Elodie looked up from the engine hatch, grease to her elbows, brass compass hanging from her neck on its lanyard like some engineer's rosary. "You're sure?"

"The rope is new." Tuan nodded at it as it slid past. "Expedition rope is yellow. That's green. Someone bought supplies recently."

"Or the expedition resupplied," Elodie said, but her voice had the flatness of someone doing arithmetic they don't like.

"Voss doesn't resupply," I said. Both of them turned. It was the first time I'd said my father's name out loud since we left the port, and it sat in the air between us like a fish left out too long. "He buys everything himself, packs everything himself, trusts no one else's knots. I used to think it was discipline. Now I think it's just that he doesn't believe anyone else will show up."

Nobody answered that. The river did, in its way — a long roll of thunder from the southwest, though the sky above us was still holding its blue like a lie.

* * *

We made camp that night on a gravel bar shaped like a sleeping animal, the boat pulled up and half-deflated against the bank, and I took first watch because I didn't trust myself to sleep anyway. This is what estrangement does: it leaves you with all this vigilance and nowhere to point it. Eleven years of not-calling my father had trained me to listen for a phone that never rang. Now the phone had gone silent for real, and I was pointing eleven years of listening at a black river.

The moon was down. The stars were the thick tropical kind, crowded and humid, and the water made no sound at all, which was wrong. Rivers murmur. This one held its breath.

I heard the engine before I saw the light — a low, four-stroke idle, throttled down to almost nothing, the sound of someone trying to be polite about arriving. I woke Tuan with a hand on his shoulder and he came up without a word, which told me he'd been sleeping the way soldiers sleep, which told me things about his village I hadn't asked yet.

"Kill our light," he whispered. "Get low."

Elodie was already awake. Of course she was. She moved like someone who'd spent her life around machinery that punished carelessness — economical, quiet, hands knowing where things were in the dark. She crouched beside me at the gunwale and we watched the far channel together.

The second boat came around the bend with its running lights off, navigating by memory or by instrument, and that alone told me it wasn't local. Nobody ran this river dark unless they had money to burn and no intention of being remembered. It was longer than ours, aluminum, new enough to squeak. It slowed as it passed our gravel bar — slowed, and hung there for three heartbeats, a searchlight's glow bleeding faintly under its canvas canopy — and then continued upstream and killed its engine somewhere beyond the point.

"They saw us," Elodie said.

"Everyone sees everyone," Tuan said. "River's a hallway."

"Then who are they?"

And because I was tired, and scared, and because keeping the name in my mouth any longer felt like holding a hot coal, I said it:

"That'll be Renard's people."

* * *

They came at dawn, which I suppose I should have expected. Men who do their work by paperwork prefer daylight; it photographs better.

There were five of them, in matching dry bags and non-matching expressions of authority, and the one who stepped onto our gravel bar first was a compact, sunburned man with the cheerful menace of a regional manager. He introduced himself as Okafor, which I doubted, and presented papers, which I also doubted, and smiled at each of us in turn like we were line items.

"Salvage and recovery rights," he said, tapping the folder. "Issued through Meridian Extraction Partners. Anything abandoned above this point — equipment, cargo, specimens, documents — falls under claim. We're authorized to recover it. You're welcome to assist, within reason."

Elodie read the papers. She actually read them, top to bottom, lips moving slightly, while Okafor watched her with the patience of a man who'd watched a hundred people read papers that didn't matter. When she finished she handed them back and said, "This covers salvage of *abandoned* property. The expedition isn't abandoned. They're alive."

"You don't know that."

"I know you don't know that," Elodie said, "or you'd have written different papers."

I liked her enormously in that moment, and I want it on the record that I liked her before the gorge, before the glacier, before any of the things that would make liking her inevitable. I liked her standing on a gravel bar with grease still under her fingernails, dismantling a lawyer with an engineer's patience.

Okafor's smile thinned by a millimeter. "Ms. Voss," he said, turning to me, and hearing my surname in a stranger's mouth, attached to a stranger's agenda, made my skin crawl in a way I hadn't anticipated. "Your father's expedition is operating on Meridian capital. Outstanding capital. The recovery of assets is simply... fiduciary prudence."

"My father," I said, "would rather sink his own equipment than let you touch it."

"Then he's been expensive company." Okafor shrugged, and behind him his men shifted, and I understood suddenly that the papers were not the point, had never been the point, that the papers were the polite version of a thing that had a less polite version waiting underneath. "We'll be upstream. If you come across expedition property, leave it. If you come across the expedition—" he paused, and the pause was the whole speech— "advise them that cooperation shortens everyone's stay in this weather."

The monsoon had been building for two days, stacked gray towers over the western ridgeline, and he gestured at them now like a man citing a footnote.

Then they went back to their boat, and I thought — briefly, stupidly — that was it. A warning delivered, a boundary drawn, the bureaucratic version of a knife laid on the table. Tuan was already muttering about pushing off before the rain, and Elodie was checking the fuel gauge with a frown she tried to hide, and I stood there feeling the strange vertigo of having heard my father described as a debt.

It was Tuan who noticed.

"Line," he said softly. "Look at the line."

Our boat was tied to a root-buttress with thirty meters of anchor line, and the line was wrong. It was taut at the wrong angle, running not to our anchor but sideways, down into the water along the bank, and it was moving — slowly, steadily, paying out through a cleat in small deliberate jerks, the way a line moves when someone below the waterline is hauling on it.

"They're siphoning," Elodie breathed. "They've got a hose on our tank. They tied our own line to their boat so we couldn't feel the drag."

We ran. There's no dignified way to describe it — three adults sprinting down a gravel bar at six in the morning, shouting at a boat that was already pulling away, Okafor raising one hand in a wave that managed to be both apologetic and final. By the time we reached the mooring, the second boat was around the bend, and when Elodie opened the tank cap and peered in with a length of tubing, her face told us everything before her mouth did.

"Enough to reach the gorge," she said. "Maybe. If I lean the mixture out and we don't fight the current more than we have to."

"They want us to follow," Tuan said. His voice was very quiet, and very flat, and I was learning that flat was how Tuan got when he was angriest. "They take just enough fuel that turning back is impossible. Only forward. Into the gorge. In monsoon season."

"Why?" I asked, and then answered myself, because eleven years of silence had made me very good at answering my own questions about my father. "Because they don't want to find him first. They want *us* to find him. We're cheaper than a search party. We're a divining rod with a family member attached."

The rain started then — not the dramatic kind, just a fine gray sift, the opening movement of something much larger — and the three of us stood around a fuel gauge like mourners around a grave that hadn't been dug yet.

* * *

Here is the thing nobody tells you about betrayal: it clarifies.

I had spent eleven years telling myself a story about my father — that he'd chosen the river over me, chosen dead languages and closed lines and other people's emergencies over birthdays and graduations and the hospital room where my mother waited three extra days for him and then stopped waiting. It was a good story. It had kept me warm. And now a man with borrowed papers had described my father as a line item, and I had felt something rise in me that was not the old cold anger but something hotter and more embarrassing, and I understood that I had never actually finished the argument with him. You can't finish an argument with someone by refusing to speak. You just carry it, and it gets heavier, and eventually somebody with a salvage claim comes along and tries to pick it up.

Not this time.

"We go forward," I said.

Tuan looked at me. Rain beaded on the canvas above us and dripped between us like a metronome. "Into the gorge."

"Into the gorge."

"With maybe enough fuel."

"With maybe enough fuel," Elodie confirmed, already elbow-deep in the engine again, carburetor jet in her teeth, talking around it. "I can get us another ten percent if I rejet for altitude and we run her hot. She'll hate it. She'll do it."

"She sounds like my aunt," Tuan said, and for the first time in three days he smiled, small and crooked, and it changed his whole face the way weather changes a coastline.

I untied the line myself. Coiled it. Stowed it. Small domestic motions with my hands while the decision settled into the rest of me. Somewhere upstream, past the bend, past the abandoned camp we hadn't reached yet, past the gorge that every pilot on this river named the way you name dangerous animals, my father was alive or dead, and either way he was mine — not Meridian's, not the ledger's, not the story I'd been telling to keep myself comfortable.

"Mara," Elodie said quietly, not looking up from the engine. "For what it's worth. Whatever's up there — you don't owe him the finding. But you won't regret the trying."

"How would you know?"

"Because I'm doing the same thing," she said, touching the compass at her throat, "for a man who's been dead nine years."

The rain thickened. The river rose a hand's width while we watched, brown and muscular, pulling toward the gorge with the confidence of something that had eaten better boats than ours.

Tuan took the wheel. Elodie fed the engine its lean, hot, hateful mixture. I stood at the bow with the rain starting to sting, watching the canyon walls gather ahead of us like a door deciding whether to open.

Behind us, faint under the rain, I could have sworn I heard the second boat's engine. Waiting. Making sure we committed.

We committed.
