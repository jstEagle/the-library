## 7. The Gorge

The engine died for the third time a kilometer below the gorge mouth, and I did what any reasonable engineer would do: I swore at it in three languages, two of them learned from my grandfather and one from a fitter in Lagos who'd taught me that profanity is just diagnostics with feeling.

"Tell me it's the fuel line," Tuan said, not looking back. He had both hands on the wheel, reading the water ahead the way I read blueprints — fast, total, no second chances.

"It's the fuel line *and* the impeller *and* whatever that grinding noise is, which I refuse to name because naming it makes it real."

"So it's everything."

"It's everything," I agreed, elbow-deep in the engine housing while the boat wallowed in an eddy behind a boulder the size of a cathedral. "Which is fine. Everything is fixable. It's only nothing that isn't."

Mara leaned over my shoulder, dripping, gray-faced, her hands still shaking from the night on the black water when the salvage crew took our fuel. She hadn't slept. None of us had. But she'd stopped asking whether we should turn back somewhere around dawn, and that was its own kind of answer.

"What can I do?"

"Hold this." I pressed the fuel pump housing into her hands. "And don't think about your father. Thinking about him makes you squeeze."

She almost smiled. Almost. "You're a terrible therapist."

"I'm an excellent engineer. Same skill set, worse bedside manner."

The truth was uglier than my banter. The impeller was shot — cavitation scars all over the bronze, chewed like something had been eating it — and running rapids with a failing cooling system was how engines became sculptures. But we had maybe forty minutes of run time left if I babied it, and the expedition's last coordinates were upstream through the gorge, and behind us was a salvage boat full of men who thought a fraudulent map gave them rights to other people's survival.

So: forward. Everything is fixable.

I patched the fuel line with a strip of rubber and a hose clamp Tuan produced from a toolbox older than he was, and I looked at the water while I worked.

That's the thing nobody tells you about rivers. They're load-bearing structures. Every rapid is a stress diagram drawn in white water — standing waves are compression, eddy lines are shear, and where the current piles up smooth and green before dropping away, that's your fracture point, the place where the river has decided to fail catastrophically and take anything floating on it along for the failure. My grandfather taught me to read rail steel the same way: hairline cracks singing under load, the way sound metal rings and tired metal goes dull. Water does the same thing. You just have to listen with your eyes.

"This one," I said finally, pointing at the tongue of green water threading between two holes near the left bank. "There's a seam there. The river's telling us where it bends."

Tuan studied it for a long moment. "My father would have taken the right side."

"Your father didn't have a dying engine. Right side means punching through two laterals. Left side means momentum carries us." I wiped my hands on my trousers, which were beyond saving anyway. "Trust me. I've spent my whole life listening to things that want to break."

He looked at me then — really looked, the way he had back at the dock when he saw the compass, and something passed between us that wasn't quite trust yet but had stopped being suspicion.

"My father said something once," he said. "About the man who repaired your compass. He said some things are worth fixing even when everyone says replace them."

"I'm choosing to believe that was about me."

"It was about the compass."

"Noted," I said, and climbed to the bow.

* * *

The first rapid nearly killed us inside ninety seconds.

Not because I misread it — I'll defend that read until I die — but because the engine chose that exact moment to deliver its resignation letter. It coughed, caught, coughed again, and we came off the entry wave sideways, the whole world tilting into brown chaos, Mara screaming something I couldn't hear over the roar, Tuan wrestling the wheel like a man fighting a live cable.

"LEFT!" I bellowed. "HARD LEFT!"

He threw his weight against it. The hull groaned — a deep structural complaint I felt in my teeth, the sound of planking being asked a question it couldn't answer — and then the current took pity or took interest, and we shot through the seam I'd picked, so close to the rock face that I could have touched it, spray hitting us sideways like thrown gravel.

Then the drop.

I have been in derailments. I have stood next to a bridge pier when a flood took out a span. Nothing prepared me for the drop. The bottom fell out of the world, my stomach stayed at the top, and we hit the trough so hard that everything not lashed down went airborne — including most of our gear.

I heard it happen more than saw it: the crate of spare parts tearing free, the jerrican of drinking water bouncing once and vanishing, Mara's bag — her father's letters, the whole estranged history of their family in paper form — skidding across the deck and over the gunwale like it had always meant to go.

"MARA'S BAG—"

She was already moving. No hesitation, no calculation. She went over the side after it.

The river took her instantly. One moment she was there; the next the water closed over her like a door.

I have never moved faster in my life. I got a line off the stern cleat, looped it around my waist, and went in after her, and the cold hit me like a train — glacial melt, high-altitude water, the kind of cold that stops your heart's paperwork mid-form. I found her by collision, actually colliding with her in the churn, and she shoved the satchel at me instead of grabbing my hand, which told me everything I would ever need to know about Mara Voss.

"The *bag*—" she gasped.

"Forget the *bag*—"

"It's the last thing he *wrote* me—"

Tuan hauled us both in on the line, arms shaking, the boat pinned against the bank below the rapid, and we lay on the deck coughing up half the river while it went on roaring past us, indifferent as arithmetic.

"You idiot," I told her, when I could talk.

"You went in after me."

"Yes, well, I'm trained for structural rescue. You're trained for *sulking*."

She laughed — a wet, cracked, furious laugh — and then she was crying, and then Tuan put a blanket around both of us because apparently pilots are also therapists, and none of us spoke for a long time except the river, which never stops speaking.

* * *

We lost the crate, the water, the spare impeller, two paddles, and the tarp. We kept the boat, the compass, the letters, and each other, though I wouldn't have phrased it that way at the time. Phrasing comes later. Survival first.

The engine ran for another hour on prayer and my thumb jammed into a bypass fitting, and then it died for good in a stretch of flat water beneath walls so tall the sky was a rumor. We drifted. There was nothing else to do.

"We walk from here," Tuan said.

"How far?"

"To the mouth of the high route? Half a day, if the ice hasn't moved. A week, if it has."

"And if the salvage crew comes through the gorge?" Mara asked quietly.

Tuan was silent. Then: "Their boat draws too much water for the seam. They'll have to portage. That buys us time."

"Time for what?"

"Time for the glacier to do what glaciers do." He nodded upstream, toward a notch in the canyon wall where the water came down milky with rock flour, cold as a held breath. "Calving season. When the ice breaks, it opens the high route for a few days. Days, not weeks. After that, the mountain closes the door."

I looked at the compass in my hand. My grandfather's brass compass, the needle steady even here, even now, pointing not north exactly but true north, the difference between them being the whole discipline of navigation. Magnetic north lies to you politely. True north doesn't care what you want.

Somewhere up that valley was her father. Somewhere behind us was a man with a fraudulent map and a crew of hired patience. And somewhere in between was a debt I'd signed my name to in a port town that smelled of rust, a contract that said I owed a financier my skills whether or not his claim was built on sand.

I'd been reading the map wrong, I realized. Not the coordinates — those were fine. The *claim*. My grandfather hadn't marked those closed lines because they were valuable. He'd marked them because they were *false*, a surveyor's quiet confession, a record of where the money wanted the railway to go and where the land refused to let it. He'd spent his life measuring the gap between ambition and earth.

And I was carrying the evidence in my pocket like a talisman instead of using it like a weapon.

"Elodie." Tuan's voice pulled me back. He was looking at me with an expression I was starting to recognize as the one I wore when a structure surprised me by holding. "You're smiling. We just lost everything we own and you're smiling."

"I figured something out," I said. "Something about the map. About why the financier really sent us."

Mara sat up. "Elodie."

"Later. When it counts." I tucked the compass away. "Right now we climb."

* * *

We beached the boat in a side channel and hid her under cut branches, Tuan whispering an apology to her like she could hear, and started upriver on foot. The trail — if you could call it that — clung to the canyon wall above the milk-white water, and the air got colder with every switchback, and the light got stranger, that high-altitude silver that makes everything look like it's already a memory.

Mara walked beside me. Her satchel hung across her chest now, strapped tight, and every few minutes her hand went to it, checking, the way you check a wound.

"He wrote me every month," she said suddenly. "For six years. I never opened them. They're all in there. Sixty-some letters, unopened."

"Why carry them, then?"

"Because I couldn't leave them, and I couldn't read them, and I couldn't do either without deciding who I am." She laughed, short and bitter. "A bag of unopened mail. That's my inheritance."

"No," I said. "Your inheritance is that you went into a freezing river for them. The mail's just paper. What you did today — that's the part that's yours."

She was quiet for a long stretch of trail. Then: "You'd have made a terrible therapist."

"But an excellent engineer."

"Same skill set," she said, and bumped my shoulder with hers, and we walked on.

Near dusk we rounded a shoulder of rock and stopped, all three of us, at the same instant.

The valley opened ahead of us into a bowl of stone and ice, and at its head stood the glacier — vast, blue-veined, ancient, groaning like a ship changing its mind. And as we watched, with a crack that rolled down the valley like God clearing His throat, a wall of ice the size of a building calved off the face and thundered into the meltwater lake, and the spray rose white and enormous, and Tuan said, very softly:

"There. That's the door opening."

Behind us, far downstream, an engine coughed in the dark — the salvage crew, fighting their way up the gorge, hours behind us and closing.

Ahead, the ice groaned again, and began, slowly, to move aside.
