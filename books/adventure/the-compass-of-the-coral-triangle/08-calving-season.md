## 8. Calving Season

The mountain spoke first.

I want to be clear about that, because later — when Mara tells the story at dinner tables in cities I will never see, and Elodie tells it in the careful language of engineers, all load paths and failure modes — people always assume the humans acted first. They did not. The glacier groaned like an old man getting off a boat, and then it boomed, a sound I felt in my back teeth before I heard it with my ears, and the whole face of the ice let go.

We had camped below the snout of it, in a bowl of grey rock the river had spent ten thousand years arguing with. The water was milky with rock flour, cold enough to make your bones file complaints. I was on watch because I hadn't been able to sleep, which is normal for me above two thousand meters, and because Elodie had fallen asleep sitting up against her pack with her grandfather's compass open in her lap like a book she kept rereading from the beginning.

The calving started small — a crack, a hiss, a chunk of ice the size of a truck sliding into the meltwater lake with a sound like a door slamming in an empty house. Then the big one. The whole lower tongue shrugged, and a wall of blue-white ice taller than the church in my village sheared away and went down, and the wave it threw across the lake came ashore twenty meters below our tents and soaked everything we owned.

Mara screamed. Elodie was awake instantly, compass in hand, already moving, already counting heads — three, three, all present — and I stood there in the spray with my heart going like a bilge pump and laughed.

Because I had seen what the wave left behind.

* * *

Here is the thing about glaciers that no one who lives near them forgets and everyone who reads about them never learns: they are not walls. They are rivers that happen to be made of time instead of water. And when a river drops a load, it opens a channel.

The calving had taken the tongue's lowest lip clean off. Behind it, where for as long as anyone in my father's generation could remember there had been nothing but a wall of ice climbing toward the pass, there was now a ramp — a broad, ugly, debris-strewn ramp of old ice exposed to the sun for the first time in who knows how long, running up along the eastern flank toward the notch in the ridge that every map called impassable and every map was wrong about, this week only.

"Days," I said. "Not weeks."

Elodie looked at me. Water dripping off her chin, hair plastered flat, and she still managed to look like someone taking minutes at a meeting. "Explain."

"The ice is melting as we watch. That ramp exists because the calving removed the wall. In four days, maybe five, the melt undercuts it and the whole thing slumps. After that, nobody goes over until next season." I pointed up. "Your father's coordinates are on the other side of that notch. If he's alive, he's alive on a clock now. Our clock."

Mara had gone very quiet, the way she does when something matters too much for her mouth to risk touching it. Then she said, "So we climb today," and started repacking her wet tent without waiting for an answer, which was answer enough.

I should say here what I have not said anywhere else: I did not want to go up that ramp. My duty runs downhill, not up. There is a village three days behind us that expects its pilot home before the monsoon finishes with the coast, expects him to run the mail and the medicine and the schoolteacher's monthly trip to see her mother. Every day on this mountain is a day my boat sits untended in a river that eats untended boats. Every day is a promise to forty families thinning like smoke.

And yet I found myself checking Elodie's bootlaces.

* * *

We climbed through the afternoon, and the mountain climbed with us, in the sense that it kept rearranging itself under our feet. Old ice is treacherous in a way new ice isn't — it has opinions. It creaks and settles and occasionally sighs, and every sound makes you stop and do arithmetic about how much of the mountain is directly overhead.

Elodie read it like she reads everything, which is to say like an engineer reading a bridge report. She'd put her palm flat against the ice, count seconds between sounds, and announce things like "that one's thermal, we're fine" or "that one's structural, move left." Mara learned fast. By mid-afternoon the girl was calling the thermal ones herself, and Elodie wore the particular pride of a teacher whose student has stopped needing her, which is a complicated pride, half joy and half grief.

I brought up the rear, which is where the person who knows the mountain belongs, and also where the person who wants to watch Elodie Okonkwo walk can do so without being caught at it. Not that I was hiding well. Mara caught me twice and said nothing both times, which from that girl is practically applause.

At the shoulder below the notch we made camp in a hollow the wind couldn't quite reach, and the glacier behind us began its evening argument — long groans rolling down the valley like thunder that had gotten lost. Somewhere below, another piece went into the lake. The boom arrived late, flattened by distance.

"We're on borrowed ground," I said.

"Everything is," Elodie said. "Bridges especially. You just try to be off them before the loan comes due."

She sat beside me at the mouth of the hollow, where you could see the whole valley laid out below — the milk-grey lake, the black river leaving it, and far down, a brown thread of water that was ours, that held our boat, that held my village at the end of it like a knot holding a rope together. The light was doing that thing high mountains do at dusk, turning everything the color of old brass.

Her grandfather's compass was in her hand again. She wasn't navigating. There was nothing left to navigate; the map's line ended at the notch and the expedition's coordinates were a dot on a scrap of paper in Mara's pocket. She just held it, thumb moving over the glass, the way you touch a thing when you are deciding whether it is a tool or a wound.

"My grandfather took this contract debt seriously," she said, to the valley more than to me. "Not the money. The obligation. He believed if a man draws a line on a map, he owes the truth to whoever walks it after him." A pause. "I used to think inheriting that meant I owed the same debts he did. Same lines, same promises, same closed railways."

"And now?"

"Now I think he meant it smaller than that. Or bigger. I haven't decided." She turned the compass over. On the case back, worn nearly smooth, were repair marks — solder scars, a replaced hinge pin. Your father's work, though she doesn't know that yet, or pretends not to. "I think he meant: carry what people give you carefully, and give it onward, and don't confuse the carrying with the keeping."

The wind moved. Down-valley, the glacier groaned again, longer this time, and neither of us pretended it was only thermal.

"Tuan." She said my name the way she says measurements — precisely, like it matters that it come out right. "If we get through tomorrow. If we find them and come down and the river lets us go. What happens to you?"

It was a fair question and a cruel one, and I respected it for both.

"I go home," I said. "That's the shape of my life. The boat, the runs, the village. My mother's garden needs a wall rebuilt before the next wet season. There's a widow named Ibu Rahayu who cannot carry her own firewood and pays me in cassava and gossip. Forty families who plan their weeks around when my engine noise comes around the bend." I watched the last light leave the lake. "Duty isn't a chain, Elodie. It's a keel. Without it I don't know what keeps me upright."

"And if you stayed?"

"Stayed where? Here? With you?" I laughed, but it came out wrong, too honest. "If I stay, I break the keel. And if I go—" I stopped, because finishing that sentence out loud would cost more than I had budgeted for the evening.

But she waited. She is very good at waiting. It may be her single most dangerous quality.

"If I go," I said, "I spend the rest of my life listening for a radio that isn't transmitting."

For a while she said nothing, and the mountain filled the silence with its own slow speech, ice settling, water running somewhere inside it, the whole mass of frozen time grinding one meter closer to whatever it was becoming.

Then: "My duty is a debt," she said. "Yours is a keel. Mine I can burn — I've already decided that, whatever happens up there. Yours I wouldn't ask you to break. I've watched what it costs you to be away from it for nine days. I won't be the reason it costs more."

"That's a very generous way to say goodbye."

"It's not goodbye." She finally turned and looked at me, and in the dark her eyes were just weather. "It's an engineering problem. Two loads, one structure. We haven't solved it yet. But I don't accept that it's unsolvable, Tuan. I have never once accepted that." Her hand found mine in the cold, calloused palm against calloused palm, two people who fix things for a living. "A man can run a river and keep a village. Boats have crews. Rivers have mouths. Somewhere downstream of here there is a version of this where I learn Ibu Rahayu's name and help rebuild a wall and my letters come to a dock instead of an office. I'm not asking you to choose tonight. I'm telling you I believe the structure holds."

Above us, the notch was a black bite in the starfield. Behind us, the glacier gave a long, low moan, and a crack ran through the dark like a struck match.

"You should sleep," I said, because my voice needed a job that wasn't trembling.

"So should you."

"I'm on watch."

"You're always on watch."

"Somebody has to be," I said, "when the person I'd trust most with it refuses to sleep."

She smiled — I heard it more than saw it — and lay back against her pack, and within minutes her breathing went slow and even, the way it only does in people who have finally decided, for one night, to trust the world.

I sat up alone with the glacier talking behind me, and I did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since the coast. Four days up and over. Three days down, if the river behaved, which it would not. Nine days past the point where my absence became a broken promise and eleven days past the point where it became a different kind of promise entirely.

Somewhere below us in the dark, on the far side of the notch, a radio that hadn't spoken in twelve days was either silent because it was dead or silent because its batteries were being saved.

At dawn we would find out which.

I checked Elodie's bootlaces one more time, told myself it was about safety, and knew, the way you know a hull is taking water before the bilge tells you, that I had already chosen.
