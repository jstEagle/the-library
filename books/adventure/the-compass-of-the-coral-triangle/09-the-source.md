## 9. The Source

I had spent eleven years not thinking about my father, which is a skill, and I want you to know it takes practice. You build a little room in your head and you put him in it and you shut the door and you tell yourself the door is load-bearing. Then a glacier calves open a high route that only exists for days, and you climb for two days on no sleep and cold rice, and at the top of the world there is a notch in the rock like a keyhole, and through the keyhole comes a voice you would know anywhere, even muffled by ice and distance and eleven years of not-thinking.

"Whoever's out there — we can see you! We have nothing worth taking!"

My father's voice. Still performing for an audience. Some things survive anything.

Elodie put her hand on my shoulder — she'd been doing that more lately, small anchorages in a storm — and Tuan went ahead low along the scree with his rifle slung and his face doing something complicated. We came around the last shoulder of moraine and saw them.

The narrowest point of the route was exactly what Tuan had promised: a shelf of old ice and rock barely wide enough for three abreast, walled on one side by a cliff that went up into cloud and on the other by a crevasse field that went down into blue dark. The expedition's survivors were bivouacked against the cliff wall — six figures, one of them lying still under a tarp that someone had rigged into a shelter with an engineer's economy. And across the shelf, blocking the way down, stood four men from the second boat with the financier's salvage paperwork folded in a plastic sleeve like a psalm.

And my father was standing up to meet us, which he should not have been able to do, because his left leg was splinted with what looked like a tent pole and zip ties, and he was leaning on a trekking pole, and he was white as the ice underfoot, and he said, "Mara."

Not *you came*. Not *I'm sorry*. Just my name, like I was a fact he'd finally gotten around to checking.

"Hi, Dad," I said. "You look terrible."

"You always did inherit my diplomacy." He tried to smile and it cost him something visible. "The leg's broken. The radio drowned on day nine. We've been walking out since."

"We heard the silence," Elodie said, stepping up beside me, and I watched my father do the thing he did with strangers, which was assess them in one pass — boots, hands, eyes, competence — and arrive at a verdict. Whatever verdict he reached on Elodie Okonkwo, it made him straighten slightly, the way men of his generation straightened for competence the way other men straightened for rank.

"You're the railroad engineer," he said. "Vasquez hired you to survey the closed lines."

"I was hired to survey dead track," Elodie said. "Then your daughter hijacked me upriver."

"She does that." My father looked at me again, longer this time, and I felt eleven years of the little room in my head strain against its door. "Mara. I need to tell you—"

"Not now," I said, because the four men across the shelf had started moving, and because some conversations deserve better than an audience of armed strangers and a crevasse field.

* * *

The man in front was called Reyes, or called himself Reyes, and he had the specific patience of someone who has never once needed to raise his voice because paperwork does it for him. He held up the plastic sleeve.

"Salvage claim," he said, "registered to Meridian Extraction Partners, covering all equipment, specimens, and mineral rights within the concession boundaries defined by survey map 44-C." He smiled at Elodie. "You'd be the engineer. Your grandfather drew that map. Family keepsakes are exempt, of course. We're not monsters."

"The map's mine," Elodie said.

"The map is collateral," Reyes said pleasantly. "Against debts incurred. Mr. Vasquez extends his regards and his invoice."

Here is what I knew, standing on that shelf with the wind coming off the glacier like a debt collector: I knew Vasquez had funded my father's expedition for three years. I knew my father had taken the money because no one else would fund research that far off any road. I knew that when the money ran short, Vasquez had sent a second boat without telling anyone, and that boat had followed us upriver through the gorge season, and its crew had siphoned our fuel in the night and left us to run rapids on fumes and nerve. I knew all of it, and I had spent the whole climb composing speeches about it, and now the moment was here and my mouth was dry as ballast.

It was Elodie who spoke first.

"Show me the map reference," she said.

Reyes blinked. "What?"

"Map 44-C. Show me the boundary coordinates. If you're going to enforce a claim in front of witnesses, I want to read it."

Something in her voice made him actually do it — unfold the sleeve, extract the map copy, hold it out. And I watched Elodie Okonkwo take her grandfather's map and hold it beside the salvage claim in the flat grey light, and I watched her face do what it had done in the engine room of Tuan's leaking boat when the water was winning: go very calm, very precise, the calm of a woman reading stress lines in steel.

"This isn't my grandfather's survey," she said.

"It's a certified copy—"

"It's a forgery built on a forgery." She turned the paper so we could all see it, though most of us couldn't read it, and didn't matter, because her voice carried the way truth carries, flat and clear across ice. "My grandfather surveyed these lines forty years ago for the coastal railway authority. His coordinate system used the old datum — the pre-war benchmark at Kilometer Zero. Every authentic sheet he drew carries his datum note in his own hand. This one doesn't. Worse — whoever redrew it converted the coordinates to the modern datum but forgot the datum shift itself. Every boundary on this claim is offset by roughly two hundred meters northeast." She tapped the paper twice, almost gently. "Which means every 'concession boundary' here sits over empty ice. The actual route, the actual find, whatever my father-in-law's expedition located — none of it falls inside this claim. You're holding legal title to a hole in the air."

The silence on that shelf had texture. I could feel it against my skin.

Reyes recovered fast; I'll give him that. "Datum errors get corrected—"

"Not retroactively they don't. A claim registered on fraudulent coordinates was never valid, which means it can't be amended — it has to be refiled, from scratch, with the original survey documentation attached." Elodie looked at him the way she'd looked at the failing bilge pump, measuring exactly how broken it was. "And here's the part you should have checked before you climbed a glacier: the original survey documentation doesn't exist anymore. My grandfather burned his field notes the year the lines closed. There is no original. There is no valid claim. There is only a piece of paper Vasquez paid someone to draw badly, so he could send four men up a mountain to take things that were never legally his."

One of the four men behind Reyes shifted his weight, and Tuan's rifle came off his shoulder with a sound like a sentence ending, and Tuan said, quietly, in the voice of a man whose village was downstream of everything that happened next:

"Nobody needs to fall in anything today."

* * *

Here is what I will remember about the next five minutes, when I am old: not the argument, though there was one, Reyes trying to reassert the paper and Elodie dismantling it clause by clause with the cheerful ruthlessness of an engineer finding the flaw in a bridge. What I'll remember is my father.

He had lowered himself onto a supply crate while she talked, leg out straight, and he was watching her work, and then he started laughing — a cracked, exhausted, delighted laugh that turned into coughing.

"What?" I said.

"That's how I lost the funding," he wheezed. "Year two. Vasquez wanted me to sign the survey over as collateral and I told him his maps were drawn wrong. I just — never had the proof. Never had the coordinates. I told him his empire was built on a two-hundred-meter error and he cut us off inside the week." He wiped his eyes. "Three years I sat on that. And his own hired engineer walks up a glacier and proves it in ninety seconds."

"He's not my—" Elodie started, and stopped, because my father was looking at her with an expression I recognized from photographs of myself at age seven, and she decided, visibly, to let it stand.

Reyes looked at his crew. His crew looked at the crevasse field, the weather, the rifle, and the arithmetic of a salvage bonus that had just evaporated into a datum error. Men like that don't fight for nothing; they fight for percentages, and the percentage had died on the ice.

"We'll want passage down," Reyes said finally, folding the plastic sleeve with great dignity.

"You'll walk behind us," Tuan said. "Where I can see you. And you'll carry the injured man."

They did. That's the thing nobody tells you about truth: it doesn't win arguments so much as it reassigns labor.

* * *

We moved down off the shelf before the weather window closed, my father riding the improvised litter between Tuan and one of Reyes' reluctant crew, shouting survey corrections at the route the whole way like a man who couldn't help himself. At the first rest halt he caught my hand.

"Mara. What I was going to say, on the shelf—"

"I know why you took the money," I said. "Tuan found your letters. In the forward camp. To me. Unsent."

His face did something I had never seen it do: it stopped performing. "Eleven years," he said. "I kept writing them because sending them meant admitting I'd chosen the work over you, and I wasn't ready to say it out loud where you could hear."

"You just did."

"Altitude," he said. "Makes a man honest."

"Then we should climb more often," I said, and he laughed again, and this time it didn't cost him.

Ahead of us, Elodie walked beside Tuan, and at some point she took out her grandfather's brass compass and held it open in her palm, not reading it — just carrying it, the way you carry something you've finally decided what to do with. Tuan glanced at it, then at her, and whatever passed between them passed without words.

Behind us, the glacier groaned once, long and deep, like a door deciding whether to close.

We had maybe two days left of the high route. And below us, somewhere past the ice line, the river was waiting to take all of us home.
