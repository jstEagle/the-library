## 10. Compass Resting

The river going down was not the same river that had tried to kill us going up. That one had been brown, furious, full of trees traveling sideways. This one ran green and apologetic, the way a man does the morning after he's thrown a punch at a wedding. The monsoon had spent itself somewhere over the mountains and left the valley rinsed, dripping, and embarrassingly beautiful.

I sat in the bow of Tuan's boat with my boots off and my feet in the water, which Mara said was reckless and Tuan said was tradition, and I have learned in my thirty-four years to trust pilots over accountants on matters of rivers. The hull no longer leaked. My patches — four rectangles of salvaged rail plate, riveted and tarred in the middle of a storm while Mara held a lantern and told me the story of the last time she'd spoken to her father — held like they'd been born there. There's a satisfaction in that which I can't fully explain to people who don't work with their hands. The metal remembered what it was for. I'd just reminded it.

Behind me, Mara sat with her father's head against her shoulder. Dr. Yusuf Adeyemi — I had learned his full name only that morning, which tells you something about how we'd met — was asleep, his splinted leg propped on a coil of rope, his face in sleep ten years younger and twenty years kinder than it had been in the photographs Mara kept folded in her jacket like a letter she couldn't decide whether to send. She had finally sent it, in the way that mattered. She'd read him the riot act for forty minutes straight as we came down through the gorge, and he'd taken it with his eyes closed, and when she finished he'd said, "You always did have your mother's timing," and she'd laughed and then cried and then laughed again, and Tuan had steered through all of it with the fixed politeness of a man pretending not to hear anything, which is a skill I intend to steal.

The salvage crew's boat was somewhere behind us, or more accurately, somewhere behind nothing, because it wasn't going anywhere. That was the thing about the financier's grand play — and I'll give Halloran this much, he thought in straight lines, the way a bad engineer does, all load and no tolerance. He'd built his claim on the map my grandfather had carried, on those closed lines across the Coral Triangle, on a survey certificate that said the expedition's route crossed land his company held mineral rights to. What he hadn't counted on was that I could read a survey certificate the way he read a balance sheet, and that my grandfather, bless his stubborn ghost, had never actually owned what he'd sold.

The map was a copy. The original — with the true provenance, the one that traced those lines back to a joint colonial commission that had lapsed in 1953, leaving the corridor to the villages along it, to *Tuan's* villages — had been sitting in a records office in the port town the whole time, filed under a mislabeled folder, waiting for someone with a grudge and a magnifying glass. I had a grudge. I'd acquired the magnifying glass in chapter three, so to speak.

I'd laid it out for Halloran on the black water while his men held rifles they were suddenly unsure about. "Your claim runs through land that was never closed," I'd told him. "Your financier's insurance is void the moment I file this. And the men you sent upriver to enforce a fraudulent claim are now accessories." He'd looked at me for a long moment, and I'd watched the arithmetic run behind his eyes, and then he'd done the only honest thing I ever saw him do: he'd turned his boat around.

The map itself I burned that night on the shingle below the gorge. Mara fed it to the fire page by page, and Tuan watched the closed lines curl and go, and I felt — I want to say I felt nothing, because that's the fashionable thing, but the truth is I felt my grandfather's hand lift off my shoulder for the first time since I was nine years old. It wasn't a loss. It was a hand coming to rest.

* * *

The debts settled themselves with less ceremony than you'd expect. Halloran's lawyer, a tired woman named Okafor who had clearly been waiting years for someone to hand her an exit, negotiated the release of my contract in exchange for my silence about the insurance angle — which was generous of her, since I'd have traded my silence for a sandwich. The expedition's insurers, confronted with survivors, paid out. Dr. Adeyemi, from a cot in the mission clinic at Tuan's village, spent his first coherent hour not thanking us but dictating corrections to his survey notes, which made Mara laugh until she had to leave the room.

"You could go home now," she said to me that evening, when she came back. "Contract's dead. Nothing holding you."

I was repairing the clinic's hand pump at the time, because a pump that gurgles is a pump that's asking for help, and I don't know how to say no to machinery. I looked at my hands, black to the wrist, and thought about the apartment in the city I'd been subletting, the one with the radiator that clanked in a rhythm I'd never bothered to learn. "Nothing was holding me there either," I said. "That's the problem with nothing. It holds you anyway, if you let it."

Tuan was on the porch, mending a net, because apparently the man did not own a single object that was allowed to remain broken. He didn't look up. "The boat needs an engineer," he said, to the net.

"The boat needs a new engine," I said.

"The boat needs *you* to say the engine is fine."

"The engine is a war crime, Tuan."

"Then it's settled," he said, and pulled a knot tight, and that was the whole conversation, and I understood it completely. Some people propose with rings. Pilots propose with maintenance schedules.

* * *

We went back up to the source at the end of the month, when the trails had dried enough to walk and the glacier had settled back into its long, indifferent work. It was a pilgrimage of four: me, Tuan, Mara, and her father, carried part of the way on a stretcher and part of the way on pure stubbornness, arguing about glacial moraine the entire ascent.

The high route had closed again behind us, as Tuan said it would. The ice that had opened for three days and let us through had ground shut like a door in a house where nobody lived. But the source itself was unchanged — a lake the color of a held breath, fed by the glacier's slow weeping, so clear that the stones at the bottom looked closer than they were, the way the past does.

Tuan knelt at the water's edge and took a small bundle from inside his jacket. His father's compass — the one his father had carried on every run up this river, the one that had been in the shop for repair the same season my grandfather's had, two instruments passing through the same hands in some port town thirty years ago, which is the kind of coincidence I'd have rejected as a novelist and accepted as an engineer. He'd kept it wrapped in oilcloth through the whole expedition, through the storm and the gorge and the standoff, and he'd never once said why, and I'd never once asked, because some cargo you don't inventory.

"My father brought boats up this river for forty years," he said. "He said the compass didn't point north. It pointed home. He said that like it was a joke." He unwrapped it. The brass was worn bright along one edge, where a thumb had rested for four decades. "It wasn't a joke."

I took out my grandfather's compass. The needle swung, settled, swung again — the source plays havoc with magnetism, all that iron in the rock, and I think my grandfather would have loved that, a place where the instrument admits it doesn't know everything. I'd carried it through two continents and one monsoon and a gunfight I still maintain was more of a heated negotiation. I'd inherited it at nine years old along with a debt I didn't understand and a map I couldn't read, and I'd spent twenty-five years assuming the inheritance was the *obligation*.

It wasn't. It had never been. The obligation was Halloran's invention, a lien on a dead man's name. What my grandfather actually left me was the habit of paying attention — to water, to metal, to the people standing next to you in the rain. The compass was just the receipt.

I set it on the stones beside Tuan's father's. Two brass faces catching the same light, needles turning lazily, disagreeing with each other and the mountain and everything. Mara put a stone on each of them, the way Tuan showed her, and Dr. Adeyemi — propped against a boulder, leg out straight, notebook balanced on his knee — said nothing at all, which for him was a eulogy.

"Your grandfather would have hated this," Mara said. "Leaving a working instrument."

"My grandfather would have understood it," I said. "He spent his whole life on closed lines. Somebody has to show him where the open ones are."

We stood there a while. The glacier calved once, far across the lake — a sound like the world clearing its throat — and the water carried the ripple to our feet, arriving exactly as unannounced and exactly on time as everything else on this river.

Then Tuan said, "The boat's due for its survey," and I said, "The boat's due for a funeral," and Mara said, "You two are going to be insufferable," and her father said, "Already are," and we started down.

I looked back once, at the two compasses on the shore of that bright water, and I felt the strangest thing, which was lightness. Not emptiness — lightness. The difference, I've come to believe, is what you put in the space you've cleared. I'd put a boat with a criminal engine, a village that needed its pump fixed and its ferry kept running and its records office taught to file things properly, a woman who laughed like the storm had never scared her, and an old man who was going to teach me glaciology whether I liked it or not.

My grandfather carried his compass for me. Tuan's father carried his for him. And now, it turned out, we carried each other — which is the only inheritance that appreciates.

The trail went down and the river went with us, and somewhere below, a boat older than all of us was waiting, leaking slightly, needing everything.

I couldn't wait to get my hands on it.
