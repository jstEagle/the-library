## 1. The River Keeps Its Accounts

The Kessel bend had flooded again, which was the river's way of filing a complaint about our existence. I'd learned to read water the way other people read faces—impatiently, and mostly for signs of trouble—and this year's complaint ran four feet over the survey stakes and had opinions about our culvert.

"Log it," I said to Tomas, who was already logging it. Tomas logged everything. He'd have logged his own heartbeat if I let him near the instruments.

We were three days into flood-season inspection of the decommissioned spur that ran along the old Vharskan corridor—thirty miles of rail that hadn't carried a paying customer since before I was licensed, and officially didn't exist at all anymore except as a line item in the commission's maintenance budget, which is to say it existed exactly as much as money required it to. Decommissioned spurs are like retired soldiers: nobody remembers why they're on the payroll, but nobody wants to be the one to discharge them.

I stood on the ballast shoulder with my boots sinking into silt and watched the river do its slow arithmetic. The Kessel took what it wanted every spring. Fence posts, culvert grates, once an entire section house that the previous surveyor had sworn was anchored to bedrock. Rivers keep accounts. They just don't itemize them.

Which is why, when Petrosky came down the embankment at a pace that could only mean either injury or discovery, I assumed injury. Injury was simpler.

"Ashgrove." He caught his breath against a tie pile. "You're going to want to see this."

"I never want to see anything," I said. "That's the job."

He didn't laugh, so I followed him.

* * *

The flood had undercut the bank forty yards downstream of the old mile marker 112, peeled back a layer of clay like a lid off a pot, and left sitting in the exposed face of the earth a chest the size of a coffin's smaller, more secretive cousin. Lead-lined—the edges showed dull grey where the water had scoured them—and bound in iron straps gone orange with rust. Stamped into the lid, above the hasp, was a seal: a double-headed eagle with its wings folded, standing on a wheel.

I knew the wheel. Every surveyor on this corridor knew the wheel; it was on the old bridge plaques and the rusted signal housings we catalogued and forgot. But the eagle—

"That's Vharskan," said Petrosky, unnecessarily. He was from up-country and had opinions about history the way he had opinions about weather: loudly, and without any practical application.

"It's scrap metal in a riverbank," I said.

"It's got a lock."

It did. A small brass thing set flush into the lead, green with age, and beside it, half-buried in the silt where the flood had sorted debris by weight, a key on a ring with no other keys. The river had delivered both together, which struck me then as coincidence and strikes me now as something else entirely.

Tomas arrived with the shovel and the logbook and the particular energy of a young man who believes every object wants to be found. "Should I log coordinates?"

"You should log it as salvage," I said. "Scrap. Unidentified ferrous material, lead-lined, no historical significance." I heard myself say *no historical significance* and something in my chest shifted, the way track shifts under a train you can't see yet—a vibration arriving ahead of its cause.

"But the seal—"

"The seal is on a lot of junk in this country, Tomas. Half the farmhouses out here use Vharskan eagles for target practice." This was true. It was also not the reason I gave the order, and I knew it, and I hated knowing it.

Here is the thing about me, the thing I had spent ten years building with the patience of a man laying rail through swamp: I am nobody. Not tragically, not dramatically—efficiently. Astrid Ashgrove appears on commission rosters, insurance forms, and the occasional disciplinary notice, and nowhere else. No photographs. No hometown listed beyond *provincial*. My file at the commission office is thinner than my instrument case, and I have personally seen to it. People remember gradients. People remember schedules. People do not remember the person holding the level, and I made sure of that the way other people make sure of their pensions.

So when a sealed box rose out of a drowned bank wearing the crest of a nation that had been erased from three atlases and one very thorough treaty, every instinct I owned stood up and said: *log it as scrap, walk away, let the river take it back next spring.* The past is heavy cargo. I don't haul freight.

"Scrap," I said again, and wrote it myself in the logbook in my own flat hand, because if I wrote it, it would be true, and if it was true, I could stop looking at it.

Petrosky looked at me the way men look at a supervisor they've decided not to argue with until later. "Whatever you say."

"Get two of the crew to pull it out before the bank slumps. We'll stack it with the culvert grates. Salvage yard takes it end of the month."

They pulled it out. It was heavier than it should have been—heavier than lead and iron account for—and when they dropped it on the grate pile it landed with a sound like a full stop.

* * *

That night the crew slept in the section house at mile 109, a building that smelled of kerosene, mouse, and the accumulated despair of a hundred inspectors before me. I took my usual bunk by the door, nearest the exit, farthest from the windows. Old habit. Everything about me was old habit; that was rather the point of me.

I lay there doing what I always did—running tomorrow's schedule through my head like a train over welded rail, click-clack, click-clack, stations and sightlines and the exact hour the light would be right for leveling the curve at 114—when I became aware of a small hard shape in my coat pocket.

The key.

I want to be precise here, because precision is the only honesty I've ever been good at: I did not decide to take the key. There was no moment, no internal argument, no dramatic little scene of conscience. Sometime between ordering the chest logged as scrap and walking away from it, my hand had simply done a thing my orders said shouldn't be done, and I had noticed only now, in the dark, with my thumb moving over the teeth of it like braille.

Ten years ago I would have gotten up and put it back. Ten years ago I still believed I was the kind of person whose hands obeyed.

But ten years ago I was also someone else, someone with a different name in a town I don't name, and I had learned since then that the self is a thing you build the way you build embankment—one load at a time, compacted, graded, until the ground holds the shape you've given it. I had built myself into a straight line. Straight lines don't pocket keys.

Except this one had.

I turned it over in the dark. Brass, cold, older than me. On the bow of it, worn nearly smooth, was stamped the same folded-winged eagle, and beneath the eagle, tiny enough that I needed the moonlight through the window and a certain stubborn squinting, a serial number: **K-112**.

Mile marker 112. The chest had been buried at its own address.

I lay there a long time after that, listening to the river work the bank in the dark, patient as a clerk going through files. Somewhere out there in the black water country was a box full of whatever a dead nation had thought worth sealing in lead, and somewhere in my coat was the only sentence that could open it, and I had told six men it was scrap.

*No historical significance.*

I closed my hand around the key. It didn't warm up. Things like that never do; that's how you know they're real.

Tomorrow we'd move camp downstream. End of the month the salvage yard truck would come, and the chest would go to be weighed and broken and forgotten, and the ledger of the world would balance, and I would remain precisely nobody, which was safe, which was the whole design.

Click-clack, went the schedule in my head. Click-clack.

But the river keeps its accounts, and it had just posted one to mine, and I fell asleep knowing—with the flat certainty of a man reading a grade off a level bubble—that I was not going to give the key back.
