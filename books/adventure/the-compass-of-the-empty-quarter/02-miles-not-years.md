## 2. Miles, Not Years

There is an arithmetic to a life spent on rails, and I have gotten very good at it. A mile is a mile whether you're happy or not. A gradient is a gradient. A timetable does not care what you did in the town you left, or who you were when you did it, or what name you were wearing at the time. I have trusted this arithmetic for ten years the way other people trust God, which is to say: completely, and mostly in the dark.

The morning after we pulled the chest from the Kessel bend, I woke at five as always, drank coffee that tasted like boiled gravel as always, and went over the day's figures as always. Chainage. Elevations. The sag in the old trestle at mile forty-one that I would have to report before someone's freight train found it the educational way.

The chest sat in the corner of the survey car under a tarp, logged as scrap iron, and I did not look at it. I looked at it the way you don't look at a loose tooth.

"You were up late," Tomas said, handing me the theodolite case. He's nineteen, all elbows and questions, the kind of crew hand who reads the instrument manuals for pleasure. I find it alarming.

"Figures don't check themselves."

"Neither do you, apparently." He grinned. I gave him the look I give the horizon, and he went off to stake out the sightline, and the morning proceeded the way mornings do when you've arranged them properly: in increments, in miles, in nothing that could ambush you.

* * *

Ten years ago I had a name that fit worse than this one, and a town that fit worse than the name.

It was a small town—you'd know the type. One main street, two churches that hated each other for reasons older than the congregations, and a gossip economy more robust than the actual one. I was twenty-two and working for the county engineer, which in a town that size meant I was the county engineer, and I was good at it, and I made the mistake of being good at it *visibly*, in a body the town had already decided how to read.

The scandal, when it came, was almost impressively small. A dance. A borrowed dress that fit me better than it fit its owner. A kiss behind the grange hall that lasted eleven seconds and cost me a decade.

You'd think eleven seconds wouldn't have the mass for it. But a small town is a closed hydraulic system; pressure goes in and has to go somewhere. It went into sermons, into a letter to the county that used the word *unsuitable* four times, into my mother's face at the market, into the particular silence that falls when you walk into a store and the conversation resumes a half-beat too late, like a train coupling that doesn't quite catch.

I left on a Thursday. That part I remember with total clarity, because it was the last thing in that town that ran on schedule. I took the morning mixed train north with one bag and no plan, and somewhere around the second junction a foreman asked if I could run a level, and I said yes, and he asked my name, and I gave him one that was nearly mine—Astrid, but a different last name, one off a gravestone I'd always liked—and that was that. A person, I discovered, can be rebuilt out of a skill set and a schedule. Nobody on a survey crew asks where you came from. They ask if you can shoot a bearing in wind, and if the answer is yes, you are, for all practical purposes, born that morning.

The first year I moved so often I stopped unpacking the bag. The second year I stopped writing home. The third year I stopped writing at all, because a journal is evidence, and I had learned what evidence does when it falls into the wrong hands. I kept figures instead. Miles surveyed. Miles walked. Miles between me and a grange hall.

People say distance doesn't fix anything. People say it with such confidence, too, usually people who've never needed it to. Distance fixed plenty. Distance gave me a profession, a competence, a self that was legible on paper—*A. Ashgrove, P.S., ten years' experience, references available*—and references, unlike neighbors, never ask what you were wearing at the dance.

What distance did not give me was anybody who knew the whole story. But that wasn't a bug. That was the design.

* * *

By afternoon the heat had come up off the empty quarter like something with intent, and we were running levels along the decommissioned spur, and I was thinking about gradients, which is my favorite thing to think about, because a gradient never lies and never leaves.

"Chief." Tomas, crouched by the trestle abutment, holding up something on a stick. "You want to see this?"

I did not, particularly. I went anyway, because that's the job.

It was a date, cut into the timber when it was green and grown distorted as the wood dried: a year, and under it, in a hand that had pressed hard, a word in a script I didn't recognize—cyrillic-looking, all teeth and fences. Vharskan, I'd know later. That day it was just a scar in the wood, and scars in old timber are a dime a dozen out here. Half the closed lines in the corridor were built by crews whose names went into the ballast with the ties.

"Old line, old scar," I said. "Log it if it makes you happy."

"You logged the chest as scrap," Tomas said, conversational, eyes on the timber. "Seemed heavy, for scrap."

The wind moved through the sage. Somewhere a crow made its opinion known.

"Heavy scrap," I said, "is still scrap. You want the commission asking why we spent crane hours recovering a box of somebody's dead paperwork? You want the paperwork? It's mold and rat leavings."

"Didn't say I wanted it." He stood, brushed off his knees, and gave me a look that was friendly and entirely unfooled, which is the worst kind. "Just noticed it wasn't in the log. The key, either."

"The key?"

"You had it in your pocket this morning. When you bent over the instrument case." He shrugged, easy as weather. "I notice things. It's why you keep me."

I keep him because he's cheap and accurate, but I let that stand. We walked back to the sightline and I shot the rest of the afternoon's bearings with my heart going a little faster than the work justified, and I told myself it was the heat.

It was not the heat. It was the fact that I had lied—well, not lied, *edited*—and the edit had come out smooth, unhesitating, with a little wry shrug built in, and some part of me had stood back and admired the workmanship. Ten years of practice, apparently, waiting for an application.

* * *

That night the crew slept in the railcars at the work camp, and I sat alone in the survey car with a lamp turned low, and I took out the key.

It was brass once, now the color of an old bruise, and it was wrong in a way I couldn't initially name. Then I named it: it wasn't a railway key. Railway keys are honest things—switch keys, box keys, all flat steel and function, stamped with the company that owns them. This was ornamental, with a bow shaped like a bird or a double-headed something, and it was heavy for its size, and it had been made for a lock that somebody had cared about.

I turned it over. And over. The way you'd worry a coin, if you were a person who worried coins, which I am not, because coins are for fares and fares go one direction: away.

Here is what I knew, and I knew it the way a surveyor knows things, which is to say in elevations and contours rather than words. The chest had been sealed—soldered, not just locked, which is what you do when you never intend to open it again. It had been sunk, or buried, or both, at a bend of the Kessel that no timetable had touched in forty years. It bore the crest of a country that had done itself out of existence before I was old enough to vote. And somebody, at some point, had cared enough about what was inside to make it heavy, make it lead-lined, make it *permanent*—and had then, evidently, thought better of permanence and drowned it.

People don't drown things they're proud of.

I thought about the town I'd left. I thought about how the whole machinery of it—the sermons, the letters, the silences—had run on one fuel, which was the pleasure of other people's exposure. I had spent ten years making sure there was nothing of mine left to expose. I had gotten so good at it that I sometimes forgot there had ever been anything. That was the trick of it. That was the *safety*: not that nobody knew me, but that there was so little of me on the surface that knowing me was a poor return on the effort.

And here was a box full of somebody else's surface. Somebody else's names, presumably, somebody else's eleven seconds behind the grange hall, magnified to the size of a nation. I should have put it on the next supply wagon to the commission office and let the clerks have it. That was the procedure. That was the arithmetic. Scrap in, report out, miles continue.

Instead I sat there turning a dead country's key in my fingers, feeling it catch the lamplight like it wanted something, and told myself the only reason I hadn't logged it properly was bureaucratic tidiness. I am a tidy man. I am a tidy person. I am tidy the way a grave is tidy.

The lamp hissed. Out past the railcars the empty quarter went on being empty, which is its whole personality, and I understood, sitting there, that I had chosen my whole life the way you choose a seat on a train—by what you're passing, not by where you're going.

Tomorrow we'd work the far side of the bend. Tomorrow there'd be figures.

I put the key in my breast pocket, over the heartbeat, which was a sentimental place to put a key, and I told myself it was just so I'd know where it was.

I knew exactly where it was. That had never been the problem.
