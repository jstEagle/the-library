## 3. What the Ledger Names

The Hotel Meridian in Corvane had two virtues: it was cheap, and the locks on its doors were older than I was, which meant they were honest about what they could do. A brass key the size of a chisel, a deadbolt that required actual commitment. Nobody could slip a credit card into that gap and let themselves in to find out what I kept under my bed.

What I kept under my bed, as of Tuesday, was a nation.

I want to be precise about that, because precision is the only religion I've ever practiced. The chest itself was maybe thirty inches long, lead-lined, iron-banded, heavy enough that hauling it up two flights of stairs had given me a new opinion about my lower back. It was stamped on every face with a crest: a double-headed eagle holding a compass rose instead of an orb, wreathed in wheat that had been rendered with loving detail by some engraver who presumably got paid whether or not his country continued to exist.

Vharska. I'd heard the name the way you hear weather reports from another hemisphere. It had been on maps when my grandmother was young. It had been off them by the time I learned to read one. Nations are like railroads that way—somebody builds them, somebody abandons them, and twenty years later teenagers are spray-painting their names on the culverts.

I'd logged the chest as scrap. That was the professional move. Salvage from flood zones goes through channels; channels have paperwork; paperwork has eyes. I wrote *ferrous container, water-damaged, no manifest value* in the logbook in my flattest hand, and then I did something I hadn't done in ten years of keeping immaculate records.

I lied by omission. And I pocketed the key.

The key had been wired to the chest's hasp inside an oilcloth wrapping, which meant someone had wanted it found eventually, but not easily, and not by accident. Someone had planned for decades of river silt and the possibility that the only person who ever opened this thing would be a surveyor with a shovel and too much curiosity. I resented being predictable.

So there I sat on the Meridian's lumpy bed at nine in the evening, door bolted, lamp turned to the wall, a towel shoved along the sill against the light leaking out like I was doing something shameful instead of something merely illegal-adjacent. I told myself it was due diligence. A surveyor doesn't leave unexplained mass sitting on a decommissioned spur; unexplained mass affects load calculations; load calculations affect everything downstream. This was simply the thorough version of checking a culvert.

I am very good at telling myself things. Ten years of practice.

The lock was stiff. The key turned with a sound like a knuckle cracking, and the lid came up on a smell I wasn't expecting—not rot, not river, but oil and paper and something faintly sweet, like the inside of an old violin case. Whatever had been done to seal this chest, it had been done well. Things had been kept.

Maps. Dozens of them, rolled in waxed linen sleeves, and I did what any railroad surveyor would do before reading a single word of text: I unrolled one across the bed and felt my stomach drop through the mattress, through the floor, through the geology of the whole town.

It was a line profile. Elevation versus distance, the bread-and-butter diagram of my entire working life, drawn in ink gone brown at the edges. And I knew the corridor immediately—the Kessel bend, the dry washes, the long climb toward the pass—but the numbers were wrong. Not wrong. *Unofficial.* A mainline grade where the official record showed nothing but open range. Stations marked where no stations existed. Sidings, water stops, a tunnel bored through rock that every government map since had insisted was solid.

I unrolled another. Same corridor, different year, more detail. Another. The lines accumulated like sediment, decade over decade, and every single one of them showed infrastructure that officially had never been built.

Except it had been built. You don't fake a tunnel. You don't fake ballast depth and drainage cuts and the little marginal notes in a hand that got shakier as the years went on: *water table high here, men refused to dig past noon in summer.* Men. Plural. Digging tunnels through a desert that appeared on no map, along a line that appeared in no ledger, for a country that no longer existed.

My hands had gone cold. I noticed this the way I notice all bodily complaints—with mild irritation and a plan to address it later.

Under the maps, wrapped separately, was the ledger.

*

It was a proper ledger, the kind I grew up seeing in freight offices: bound in green cloth gone gray, columns ruled in faded red, entries in three different hands. The first forty pages were boring, and I mean that as a compliment. Boring ledgers are honest ones. Coal tonnage. Water rights. Wages paid out—and there I stopped, because the wage column didn't say wages. It said *rations issued,* and next to it, in a narrower column, *laborers accounted for.*

Accounted for. Like tonnage. Like ties.

I read backward, because that's how I read everything—start from the anomaly and work toward the cause. The entries got older and the language shifted from bureaucratic to something worse, which is to say it stayed bureaucratic but the subject changed. *Transfer of corridor population, per directive 44-C. Transport allocation: forty cars. Losses en route to be reported separately.* Losses en route. Reported separately. Somebody's job, somewhere in Vharska's ministry, had been counting the people who died between being taken and arriving, and filing those counts in a different book so the totals would look clean.

And at the bottom of each quarterly page, signatures. Authorizations. The names of officials who had approved, funded, signed off on the movement of an entire border region's population into labor camps along those unofficial lines—fed by those unofficial sidings, watered at those unofficial stations, buried, I understood now, somewhere near that tunnel where the men wouldn't dig past noon.

I sat on the edge of the hotel bed with a dead nation's accounting book open on my knees and I did the thing I always do when the world stops making sense: I looked for the numbers. And the numbers were meticulous. Whoever had kept this ledger had been good at their job. That was somehow the most obscene part. Somewhere in Vharska there had been a clerk—I imagined him exactly like me, lamp turned to the wall, sleeves rolled, hating errors—who had recorded atrocities with perfect decimal accuracy, because that was the work, and you do the work.

Then I reached page sixty-one, and the work acquired a signature I knew.

Not the man. The hand. Halvard Crane signs his correspondence with a long descending stroke on the H, a flourish he probably thinks of as charming, and I've processed enough of his commission's paperwork to recognize it the way you recognize a voice through a wall. There it was, dated thirty-one years ago, authorizing "expedited transport allocation" for the third quarter, followed by a disbursement figure that even adjusted for era made my eyes water.

Crane. Whose money had built the Ashford Valley Commission. Whose letterhead sat in the office where I'd signed my own contract ten years ago, under the name I'd chosen off a list of acceptable spellings. Whose portrait hung in the depot at Corvane with a plaque reading *VISION BUILDER OF THE NEW FRONTIER.*

He'd been young then. Young men do terrible things and get called ambitious. Old men who did terrible things get called founders.

I set the ledger down on the bedspread very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off, and I stood at the window with the towel still blocking the light and looked out at the yard lights of the Corvane yards, at the commission's rolling stock, at the survey flags I'd planted myself along the extension route—all of it built on Crane money, running on Crane schedules, measured by instruments Crane had paid for.

Here is the vertigo, and I want to describe it accurately because I've never managed to describe anything else accurately since. My career is a map. Every mile I've surveyed, every grade I've calculated, every promotion earned by being the person who never makes waves—it all sits on top of a substrate, the way track sits on ballast sits on earth. And tonight I learned the substrate was crime. Not metaphorically. Literally. The corridor I'm extending, the very ground my theodolite has kissed, runs parallel to and in places directly over lines laid by deported labor, financed by a signature I've seen on my own pay vouchers' ancestry.

The map of my life was drawn over someone else's grave, and nobody had told me, and the worst part—the genuinely unbearable part—was that the map was *good.* Clean grades. Sound engineering. Everything I'd ever admired about the work was true, and it was also built on this.

You can't un-know a thing like that. Believe me, I spent a while trying. I put the ledger back in the chest. I closed the lid. I sat on the bed and ate a stale roll from my pack and rehearsed the professional response: log it, report it, let the channels handle it. Channels have procedures. Procedures have jurisdictions. Jurisdictions have lawyers, and lawyers have clients, and I know exactly whose client owns the jurisdiction here.

If I reported the chest, it would reach Crane's people within a week. It would be appraised as scrap. It would disappear into a warehouse or a furnace, and I would keep my invisibility, my clean record, my miles. That was the safe move. That was the only move, according to every rule I'd lived by for a decade.

But I'd already pocketed the key, hadn't I. Three days ago, standing in the mud at Kessel bend, some part of me had known before the rest of me caught up. The chest had been sealed to be found by someone specific—not me specifically, but someone like me: literate in grades and gauges, able to read those maps and understand that they were real, able to look at a ledger and know that columns don't lie even when nations do.

There was one more item in the chest, wedged under the ledger's spine. An envelope, brittle with age, addressed in that same shaking hand I'd seen in the map margins. Inside, a single sheet:

*To whoever opens this: the lines are real. The men were real. The books balance. Find the clerk if he lives—he kept the second copy, and he knows where the graves are. I was the engineer. I could not stop it. I could only write it down. Forgive the weight of this box. Truth is dense.*

No signature. Just a small inked compass rose in the corner, the same one from the crest, pointing north like it still believed directions meant something.

I sat with that for a long time. Outside, a freight train came through Corvane on the through line, and I counted the cars out of habit—forty-one—and hated myself for counting, and counted anyway.

The clerk. *Find the clerk if he lives.* The commission's historical files, which I helped organize during a slow winter two years ago, mention a Station Nine on the closed desert lines—a water stop, decommissioned, listed in one dusty memo as having a "resident caretaker, non-salaried." Non-salaried caretakers don't exist. People who don't exist do.

Station Nine was three hundred miles out, most of it across the empty quarter on rails nobody had inspected since the closure. Three hundred miles of nothing, carrying a chest full of evidence that a living magnate would burn a country to keep buried.

I should log it as scrap. I should sleep. I have a schedule tomorrow, and schedules are how I've survived, and survival is the thing I'm best at.

Instead I lay in the dark listening to the yard, and I thought about a dead engineer who couldn't stop what he saw, so he wrote it down and weighted it with lead and trusted it to a river. And I thought: he chose the current. He let the water decide who would find it.

The river decided me.

God help me, I started planning the route.
