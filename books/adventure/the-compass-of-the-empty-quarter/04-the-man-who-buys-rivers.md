## 4. The Man Who Buys Rivers

I have been called many things in my life — visionary, vulture, the man who buys rivers — and I have answered to all of them with the same pleasant smile, because a name only wounds you if you flinch at it. Halvard Crane does not flinch. I learned that at nine years old, when my father's mill burned and the town called us cursed, and I stood on the scorched foundation and decided that if people were going to talk about me anyway, they might as well say something useful.

So when my secretary knocked twice — her signal for trouble, as opposed to her single knock for coffee and her three knocks for journalists — I set down my pen and said, "Send them in," before she'd even announced anyone.

"Sir," she said through the door, "there's a telegram from the Kessel district."

Kessel. The name arrived in my chest like a stone dropped down a well. I waited for the splash. It took longer than it used to.

"Read it," I said, because my voice is steadier than my stomach, and always has been.

She read it. A salvage crew working flood inspection on the decommissioned spur had pulled a chest from the river bend. Lead-lined. Stamped with the old seal. Logged as scrap by the surveyor in charge — an Ashgrove, of the commission — but the local stationmaster, who drinks with my people and reports to them, noted that the chest had not gone to scrap. It had gone to the Grand Meridian Hotel, room 214, in the personal luggage of the same surveyor.

A key had been pocketed. A log entry had been quietly amended. And a stranger was sitting alone in a hotel room with forty years of my history under their bed.

* * *

People assume men like me keep secrets out of guilt. This is the first thing they get wrong about me, and it is the error I have built half my fortune upon. Guilt is for people who believe the past can be changed. I believe no such thing. The past is a sunk cost. What I did in Vharska — what was done *in my name*, which is the same thing to a tribunal and a very different thing to me — happened in a country that no longer exists. You cannot prosecute a ghost. You cannot extradite a man to a nation that has been carved into three successor states, two of which owe me money.

The ledger exists. I know it exists because I signed its sister copy, once, in an office in Belovar that smelled of ink and lilies, while a clerk named Brandt watched me with eyes like wet slate. I was thirty-one. I told myself then what I tell myself now: that the corridor would have emptied anyway, that the deportations were policy, that policy is weather and I merely sold umbrellas. That the men who profited most never touched paper at all, and I, who kept accounts, am somehow the criminal for having written things *down*.

There is a version of this story where I am a monster. There is another where I am a bookkeeper. I have spent forty years making sure the second one is the one that gets printed.

And now some surveyor with a pocketed key wanted to open the book.

* * *

I did not panic. Panic is for amateurs and poets. I poured myself the good brandy — the one I save for decisions rather than celebrations; there are fewer occasions for it than you'd think — and I sat by the window of my office on the fourteenth floor and looked down at the rail yards, my rail yards, the steel spine of everything I'd built since I came back across the water with nothing but a name too common to trace and enough capital to make honesty unnecessary.

Here is what I actually felt, and I will admit it here because no one will ever read this: I felt tired. Not afraid — tired. Forty years of clean ledgers, charitable foundations, a hospital wing with my mother's name on it, and still the past sits in the river like a coin at the bottom of a well, waiting for some fool with a dredge.

History is a debt, people say. Nonsense. History is a creditor, and creditors can be paid. I have paid. God knows I've paid — in lawyers, in endowments, in the quiet purchase of archives and the quieter unpurchase of others. I paid Belovar's provisional government a sum that built two courthouses, on the understanding that certain records were "lost in the transition." I paid a journalist's widow's pension for eleven years after her husband got curious about the corridor. Every payment was legal. That is the trick of it. If you do it right, restitution and concealment look identical on a balance sheet.

But a ledger in the hands of a stranger — that is not a debt. That is a trial.

* * *

I sent for Maren Voss at seven o'clock, which was early for her and precisely on time for me.

Voss came into my office the way she comes into every room: like weather that has already decided what it will do to your picnic. She is not a large woman. She is large the way a coupler is large — all function, no ornament, capable of holding enormous weight together or tearing it apart depending on which way the strain runs. She had been military police in the border garrisons during the last bad years, though her file says "logistics." Her file says a great many comforting things.

"You've read the telegram," I said.

"I've read the telegram."

"A salvage crew on the Kessel spur pulled up a chest. Lead-lined. Old seal. The surveyor logged it as scrap and then didn't scrap it."

Voss stood with her hands behind her back and let me finish, which is more courtesy than I usually get from her. "Ashgrove," she said. "A. Ashgrove. Ten years on the commissions. No family listed. No address before the rails. No debts, no vices, no politics." She paused. "No history."

"No history," I agreed. "Which used to mean safe. Now it means unreadable."

"You want them read."

"I want the chest read. I want to know what's in it, how much of it survived the water, and whether any of it names me." I turned from the window. "And then I want it gone, Voss. Not stolen — stolen things get hunted. Gone the way things go when they were never there. Scrap. River. Whatever's cleanest."

"And the surveyor?"

The question hung there. I made myself consider it honestly, because self-honesty is the one luxury I permit before breakfast. Did I want Ashgrove hurt? I did not. I want that recorded somewhere, even if nowhere records it. I have never enjoyed hurting people. I have simply never once allowed that preference to interfere with arithmetic.

"The surveyor gets a promotion," I said. "Or a settlement. Or a very good reference to a railway in a country with poor mail service. People who dig up the past usually just want to be paid to bury it again. Find out the price. Everyone has one. It's rarely money — it's whatever they're missing."

"And if they're not missing anything?"

"Then they're missing nothing," I said, "and that's worse. Those ones you can't buy. Those ones you have to *outlast*."

Voss nodded slowly. "You want me aboard the commission."

"I want you *assisting* the commission. Insurance audit. Loss prevention. My name on the paperwork as a concerned stakeholder with a financial interest in the line's security." I smiled, and I felt the smile work, because it always works; it worked on ministers and it worked on Brandt's supervisor and it has worked on every tribunal clerk I've ever sat across from. "You'll be helpful, Voss. You're always helpful. Helpfulness is the best disguise there is — nobody checks the papers of a woman carrying a clipboard and a fire extinguisher."

"What's the actual cover?"

"Flood damage assessment. The Kessel bend flooded; the commission will need to certify the spur's condition before the season closes. You'll be the insurer's eyes." I slid the folder across the desk. "You'll also be mine."

She took it without opening it. She never opens anything in front of me. "And if the chest is already empty? If the contents are already moving?"

"Then we find out who they're moving toward and get there first." I said it lightly, but the well in my chest dropped another stone, and this time I heard the water. "There's a name I want you to hold in your head, Voss. Brandt. Ilya Brandt. Clerk, border corridor administration, Vharskan civil service. If he's alive he'll be old, and old men who buried their names don't dig them up — but they recognize them when someone else does. If our surveyor goes looking for a witness instead of a buyer, that's who they'll find."

"Where?"

"Station Nine. Or whatever Station Nine calls itself now. The desert lines. Nobody lives out there unless they're hiding or dying, and at his age it's probably both."

Voss wrote nothing down. She never writes anything down either. "When do I leave?"

"Tonight. First train." I came around the desk and shook her hand, and my grip was warm and firm and entirely steady, and I hated how much practice that had taken. "Maren. One thing. The surveyor is an employee of a commission I fund, in a country with laws I respect, more or less. Nothing happens to them that couldn't happen in a flood. Am I understood?"

"Understood," said Voss, in the tone of a woman who understands perfectly and has already filed my instruction under *considerations*.

* * *

After she left I sat alone with the brandy I hadn't drunk and the city lights below, and I did the thing I do perhaps twice a decade: I opened the bottom drawer, the locked one, and took out the photograph.

Belovar, spring, forty years ago. A young man with dark hair standing on a platform beside a private car, shaking hands with a regional administrator whose face history has mercifully blurred. Behind them, freight cars. Behind the freight cars, people boarding them — I have looked at this photograph so many times that I can no longer see whether they are boarding or being loaded, and I suspect that ambiguity was the photographer's gift to me, whether he knew it or not. In the corner, half out of frame, a thin young clerk holds a ledger against his chest the way a soldier holds a rifle.

Brandt. Even then. Watching me. Writing me down.

I put the photograph away and locked the drawer, and I told myself the story I have told myself ten thousand times: *the country is gone, the law is gone, the debt is paid, the past is a closed account.* And for the first time in years, the story didn't take. It sat in my mouth like river water.

Because here is the flaw in my arithmetic, the error no auditor ever caught: I assumed the past stayed buried because I kept paying for the shovel. But rivers move things. Rivers keep their own accounts. And somewhere east of Kessel bend, a stranger was turning a small brass key between their fingers, and I did not yet know their price —

— and the ones with no price, I reminded myself, pouring the brandy at last and drinking it like medicine, the ones with no price are the ones you have to reach before they reach Station Nine.

I rang for my secretary. "Get me the night schedule," I said. "All lines west."
