## 5. Voss Comes Aboard

She arrived on the Tuesday morning local with a leather case and a smile that had been ironed flat, and she introduced herself to my crew before she introduced herself to me, which I later understood was not an accident but a doctrine.

"Maren Voss," she said, shaking hands down the line of us like a bishop blessing a slow parade. "Continental Indemnity. Insurance audit. Your commission's underwriters want a full risk assessment of the Kessel corridor before they renew."

Nobody questioned it. Why would they? Insurance men descend on railway projects the way gulls descend on a fish dock—noisy, inevitable, and gone as soon as the pickings thin. What none of them expected was that she'd stay. Auditors visit. Voss *settled*, the way weather settles in.

I watched her from the instrument tripod while she worked the crew, and I'll admit my first read was wrong. I took her for what she appeared: a mid-level functionary with good boots and a company expense account, someone who would fill out forms about tie rot and culvert clearance and be gone by Friday. She was maybe forty, gray at the temples in a way that looked maintained rather than earned, and she carried herself with the particular economy of a person who has learned never to take two steps when one will do.

Then she asked Petrosky how long the flood season ran.

"Six weeks, give or take," he said. "River peaks twice. Once on snowmelt, once on the autumn rains."

"And the second peak—the water comes up fast?"

"Overnight, sometimes. You can lose a mile of grade in a night if the bend shifts."

Voss nodded and wrote something down. It was the nod that snagged me. An insurance auditor hears "you can lose a mile of grade" and writes *erosion exposure*. Voss heard it and wrote something else entirely—I could see it in her face, the small private satisfaction of a woman confirming a fact she already owned. She wasn't gathering information. She was checking my crew's answers against a list.

I swung the transit back toward my benchmark and told myself I was being paranoid. I am very good at telling myself things. Ten years of practice.

* * *

The trouble with paranoia is that it doesn't announce itself. It arrives quietly, like water finding a seam, and by the time you notice the damp it has been inside your walls for weeks.

The chest sat in my hotel room in Corven's Landing, wrapped in an oilskin tarp, wedged behind the wardrobe where a chambermaid would have to be both curious and committed to find it. The key lived in my breast pocket, against my sternum, where I could feel it when I breathed too deep—a small cold fact pressing on a warm one. I had logged the salvage honestly enough at first: *one sealed strongbox, lead-lined, crest illegible, contents unknown, retained pending assessment.* That entry sat in the field book like a landmine with my initials on it.

So on Wednesday night, after Voss had spent the day asking Petrosky about drainage and asking the chainmen about access roads and asking everyone, casually, over dinner, whether anything interesting ever washed up out of the river, I went back to the survey office and did something I had never done in ten years of meticulous, blameless record-keeping.

I altered the log.

*One sealed strongbox, lead-lined, crest illegible, contents unknown — reported to commission office, remanded for disposal as scrap.* I dated it three days earlier than the truth. I forged my own handwriting doing it, which is harder than it sounds; my hand knows its own habits and resents being lied through. Then I burned the original page in the stove, fed the ash into the grate, and stood there watching the fire eat the only honest sentence I'd written all week.

Here is what I want on the record, since apparently I'm keeping one now: I had decided, by then, that I was going to open the chest. I hadn't admitted it yet even to myself, but the decision was made. Everything else—the forged log, the key against my ribs, the lie I was building plank by plank—was just scaffolding around a choice I refused to look at directly. Astrid Ashgrove, who had spent a decade constructing a life so transparent that anyone could see straight through it to nothing, was learning carpentry in the dark.

And the worst part, the part I still don't like writing down: the lying came easily. Smoothly. I lied to Petrosky about why the log smelled faintly of smoke ("stove's drawing badly"). I lied to the hotel clerk about needing the room until the end of the month ("commission's extending the survey"). I lied to myself most fluently of all, standing at the window with the key in my fist, telling myself I was only keeping it safe. Safe from whom? From the woman downstairs who asked questions in the shape of small talk? From Halvard Crane, whose name I hadn't yet spoken aloud but whose signature I had now read four times, each time hoping the ink would rearrange itself into somebody else?

Ten years without a single lie worth the name, and then six of them in one evening, each one sliding out clean as a fish through weeds. I hated it. I hated how natural it felt, the way you hate discovering you have a talent for something shameful—that some locked room in you had been waiting all along for permission, and the permission turned out to be fear.

* * *

Thursday, Voss came out on the line with us.

"Mind if I walk the bend?" she asked me, and there it was again—not *a* bend, not the nearest section, but *the* bend. Kessel. The flooded spur where a scrap crew with a dredge winch had pulled a lead-lined box out of the silt eleven days ago.

"Insurance doesn't usually care about geography," I said.

"Water damage cares about geography." She smiled. "Underwriters care about what water damage finds."

So we walked it together, the two of us, along the old spur bed with the floodwater still standing in the cuts, brown and patient. She asked good questions. That's what I kept circling back to all day—they were *good* questions, the kind a real auditor would ask, which meant either she was a real auditor or someone had briefed her extremely well. When did the dredge start? What depth were you cutting? Did you pull up anything besides rail and ballast?

"Scrap iron. A wagon axle. Some brick," I said. All true. All beside the point, which is its own kind of lie.

"No containers? Sealed boxes? The river moves a lot of material downstream after a flood—people lose whole sheds."

"People lose sheds," I agreed. "We found no sheds."

She laughed at that, a short genuine-sounding note, and for one vertiginous moment I thought: she believes me. And then I thought: no. She's letting me think she believes me. And then I thought, with a weariness that surprised me: does it matter? If she already knows, the lying is theater. If she doesn't, the lying is armor. Either way I keep lying, because stopping now would be a confession delivered in person.

We stood at the edge of the cut where the chest had come up. The water there was calm, holding the sky upside down, and Voss looked into it for a long time the way people look into wells—not seeing anything, seeing everything.

"Strange place for a spur," she said. "Dead end. No town within eight miles. Somebody built twelve miles of track to reach nothing."

"Somebody built it to reach the river," I said. "Timber loading, probably. Before my time."

"Mm." She turned from the water. "You've been with the commission ten years, Ms. Ashgrove?"

"Astrid," I said, because correcting her felt like giving her something else to write down. "Yes. Ten years in the fall."

"Loyal. That's rare in this work." She said it kindly. That was the thing about Maren Voss—everything came wrapped in kindness, and you only discovered the wire underneath when you tried to carry the package any farther than she wanted it to go. "Crane Industrial likes loyal people. Mr. Crane says loyalty is the only asset that appreciates."

"I wouldn't know. I've never met him."

"No," she said. "Not yet."

* * *

It was Tomas who nearly ended everything, and he did it without knowing, the way young men do—by being honest in the wrong room.

He was twenty-three, chainman, quick with numbers, the kind of kid who reads the field books for pleasure and asks questions that are half ambition and half genuine hunger to understand. On Thursday evening, in the mess tent, with Voss twenty feet away eating her supper with the tidy attention of a woman cataloguing the menu, Tomas said, loudly enough to carry:

"Ashgrove—the salvage manifest. The strongbox we pulled at the bend. Where'd it go? It's not in the commission returns. I checked, because I thought maybe we'd get a salvage fee split."

The tent did not go silent. Tents never do; that's the cruelty of them. Forks kept moving, someone laughed at the far table, the lamp hissed. But I felt the silence anyway, the way you feel a coupler take up slack—all that weight suddenly hanging on one link, and the link is you.

"Remanded for disposal," I said. "Lead-lined scrap. The commission office took it."

"That fast?" He frowned. "Usually they sit on salvage for weeks. There's a form. Salvage Disposition 12-B. I filed one for the wagon axle."

"The office handled it," I said, and gave him a look I hoped read as *drop it* rather than *please God drop it*. "Ask me about the benchmarks, Tomas."

He asked about the benchmarks. Across the tent, Voss ate her supper and did not look up, and did not look up, and did not look up, and the not-looking was louder than any question she'd asked all week.

Afterward I caught Tomas outside, in the dark between the tents, where the desert starts making its night noises. He was embarrassed, apologetic, sixteen kinds of sorry. I told him it was fine, no harm done, and then—because he was standing there looking at me with that open young face, waiting to be dismissed—I heard myself say:

"Tomas. If anyone asks you about the salvage again—and I mean anyone, including me, especially if I seem to be asking strangely—you remember exactly what I told the tent. Scrap. Remanded. Form or no form, the office took it. Understood?"

He blinked. "Is something wrong?"

"No."

That was the seventh lie of the week, and it cost me more than the others, because his face fell by a single degree—not suspicion, just hurt, the small wound of realizing the person you admired keeps a door closed somewhere. He said goodnight and went to his bunk, and I stood alone in the dark with the key against my sternum and the desert ticking around me, and I understood the shape of my situation with perfect clarity for the first time.

Voss was here for the chest. Whether Crane had sent her or she'd simply followed the river's rumor, it didn't matter; she was here, she was patient, and she had time. My forged log would hold against clerks. It would not hold against her. Sooner or later she would ask the commission office about a Salvage Disposition form that didn't exist, or ask the dredge foreman what the winch had really brought up, or simply wait for me to make the mistake every liar eventually makes, which is believing their own cover story longer than anyone else does.

Which meant the chest couldn't stay in Corven's Landing. Which meant it couldn't stay anywhere near me, near the line, near the neat little grid of my ten safe years—which meant, though I didn't let the thought finish itself, that it had to move, and soon, and far, and I would have to be the one to move it.

I walked back past the mess tent. Through the canvas I could see Voss still sitting at the table, alone now, writing in a small black notebook by lamplight. She wrote for a long time. Then she stopped, and without turning her head—without any reason in the world to know I was there—she said, clearly, to the empty tent:

"Goodnight, Ashgrove."

I stood in the dark with my heart going like a loose driving rod, and began, calmly and in detail, to plan a journey across three hundred miles of country that officially had no railroad on it at all.
