## 6. The Empty Quarter

I have stolen exactly two things in my life, and both of them were keys.

The first was a bicycle, when I was sixteen, which I rode out of a town that had decided what I was before I'd finished deciding myself. I left it in a ditch forty miles on and walked the rest of the way to the rail yard. The second was the little brass thing in my pocket now, salvaged from a drowned chest stamped with the seal of a country that no longer existed to be insulted by my theft.

The difference between the two thefts was that this time nobody would be looking for a runaway kid. They'd be looking for a surveyor with eleven years of unblemished service records and a habit of being precisely where the timetable said they'd be. Which was why, at 2:14 in the morning, I was not where the timetable said I'd be.

The handcar sat on the old maintenance spur behind the equipment shed, oiled, loaded, and — I'll admit it — decorated. A tarp over the chest. A water can. A lantern with a fresh wick. Three days' rations arranged with the compulsive neatness of a person who has never once been spontaneous and intends to fake it badly.

Tomas's bunk had been empty when I passed through the crew car. That was the part that sat wrong in me, worse than the lying, worse than the logbook entry I'd falsified with my own tidy hand. He'd noticed the gap in the inventory. He'd said nothing. And then tonight he'd been absent from his cot at the exact hour he was always in it, which meant either coincidence or complicity, and I didn't know which frightened me more. If he'd gone to Voss, I had hours. If he'd gone to sleep somewhere else out of ordinary young-man reasons, I had until dawn.

Either way: miles, not years. I pumped the lever and the handcar rolled out onto the closed line like a secret leaving a mouth.

* * *

The empty quarter earned its name honestly.

By the third hour the last telegraph pole had fallen behind me and the rails ran straight as a ruled line across hardpan the color of old bone. No stations. No sidings. Just the two threads of steel going west toward a horizon that kept promising to arrive and never did. The sun came up and did nothing kind with it — just laid itself flat across the desert and dared everything living to argue.

I pumped and counted. Pumping a handcar is the only prayer I've ever trusted: one-two, one-two, the body too busy to think. The chest shifted under its tarp at every dip and I found myself reaching back to steady it, the way you'd steady a sleeping animal, or a passenger.

*You could still turn around,* said the reasonable voice, the one that sounded like ten years of clean service records. *Log it as scrap. Bury it deeper. Let the river keep its accounts.*

But the ledger had a signature in it that matched the man whose money paid my wages, and once you've read a name like that you can't unread it. Halvard Crane had built half the railways in three provinces, and somewhere under all that philanthropy was a line of ink connecting him to trains that officially never ran, carrying people who officially never existed, to labor camps that appeared on no map except mine — except the dead nation's, which I now carried strapped to a handcar in the middle of nowhere.

Station Nine lay a hundred and ninety miles west. Records said a clerk named Ilya Brandt had signed for water deliveries there as recently as four years ago. Records also said the line was condemned past Mile 120, which is the sort of sentence that means different things to an accountant and a surveyor. To an accountant it means don't go. To a surveyor it means the ballast is soft after Mile 140 and the second culvert is suspect, but the rails themselves will hold if you respect them.

I respected them. Mostly.

The exhilaration hit me around noon, somewhere past the last junction marker, and I want to be honest about it because honesty is apparently the theme of my late career: it felt wonderful. Ten years of schedules and gradients and being nobody in particular, and here I was doing something that mattered, alone under an enormous sky, outrunning — well, nothing yet. Outrunning the possibility of pursuit. It was the freest I had felt since a bicycle in a ditch.

Then the wind changed, and the exhilaration curdled the way milk does, slowly and then all at once.

Because the open country offers no cover. That's the joke of it. In town you can be invisible — a face in a crew, a name on a roster. Out here there was one handcar, one set of tracks, one line of travel, and anyone following me wouldn't have to find me. They'd only have to follow the rails. I had chosen the most legible escape route in the province. Every mile I gained, I also announced.

I stopped at the Mile 90 water tank — dry, its legs rusted to lace — and ate hard bread and looked back down the line I'd come by. Empty. Heat shimmer. Nothing.

Which proved nothing. Heat shimmer proves nothing; it's the desert's own forged document.

I got back on the car and pumped, and the counting started again, one-two, one-two, and somewhere inside mile 100 I admitted the thing I'd been not-admitting since the shed: I wasn't running toward Brandt so much as away from Voss, and those are not the same direction, even when the rails only offer one.

* * *

Maren Voss had worked enough missing-person cases to know that the first hour tells you almost everything and the first lie tells you the rest.

She stood in Astrid Ashgrove's quarters aboard the commission car and let the room talk. People's rooms always talked. This one said: disciplined, ascetic, nothing out of place. Clothes folded to regulation angles. Boots polished. A shelf of timetables going back a decade, spines cracked at the same pages — the ones with the long through-runs, the routes that crossed emptiness.

And one thing out of place. The bed had been slept in, but the blanket was folded back over the pillow in a way that suggested haste dressed up as tidiness. Under the mattress edge, a single thread of blue tarp canvas. The same weave as the salvage tarps issued at Kessel bend.

Voss took the thread, sealed it in an envelope, and wrote the time in her log: 05:40. Then she went and woke her driver.

"Handcar," she told him. "Westbound on the closed line. They'll have maybe six hours on us. We take the motor quad."

"The line's condemned past 120, ma'am."

"So is everyone, eventually." She checked her watch. "Pack water for three days and bring the rifle we're not supposed to have."

Her orders from Crane had been characteristically elegant. *Assist the railway commission. Ensure any recovered historical materials are properly catalogued.* He hadn't said *retrieve the chest*, because Halvard Crane never said the incriminating thing out loud; he said the thing adjacent to it and let you do the arithmetic. Voss had done the arithmetic for eleven years. She was very good at it. What she hadn't expected, packing the quad in the gray light, was the small cold thought that arrived uninvited: *the surveyor lied to me.*

She'd liked Ashgrove. That was the annoyance of it. Precise, courteous, the rare subject whose eyes stayed level while lying — and they had been lying, about the bend, about the log, about the weight of the crate on the manifest versus the weight on the crane scale, a discrepancy of forty pounds that Voss had clocked and filed and almost forgiven, because she'd assumed incompetence, not intent.

Intent changed the calculus. Intent meant the surveyor knew what was in the chest. And knowing meant the quiet solution — silence, a promotion, a clean new name, the standard package — might not hold. People who ran were people who'd already decided the package wasn't for sale.

The motor quad took the spur at speed, Voss braced against the rail plate, the desert opening ahead of them in the early glare. Somewhere out there, a handcar moving at pumping speed — call it eight miles an hour, sustained, if the arms held, and arms always stop holding eventually. Six hours' lead, closing at twenty miles an hour. Arithmetic again. She was good at arithmetic.

What she couldn't put numbers on was the other thing, the thing she'd learned years ago watching men run across open ground: that people fleeing toward something were harder to catch than people fleeing from something, because they'd accept damage to keep going. She needed to know which kind Ashgrove was.

She'd find out at Station Nine, or wherever the rails gave out first.

* * *

Mile 141. The culvert.

I saw it too late to stop cleanly — the handcar doesn't stop, it negotiates — and brought it to a grinding halt with the front wheels hanging over a gap where the culvert beneath had folded like wet cardboard, taking a twelve-foot stretch of track down with it into a dry wash.

I sat there breathing hard, hands blistered through my gloves, and made myself laugh, because the alternative was screaming. Condemned line. Right. The surveyor's report had been optimistic by one culvert.

Twelve feet. The chest weighed forty pounds. The handcar weighed four hundred. There was a way to do this — there was always a way to do this, that was the whole of my profession — but it would cost an hour, maybe two, and every hour was a mile Voss gained, if Voss existed, if Tomas had talked, if the shimmer on the horizon behind me was heat or pursuit.

I got off the car and walked the wash, measuring with my eyes, already solving it. Rail as lever. Ballast as ramp. One-two, one-two.

Behind me, far back down the straight silver line, the heat shimmer moved.

It moved against the wind.

I stood very still in the enormous empty quarter, the only person for a hundred miles in any direction, entirely visible, entirely exposed, holding a dead nation's maps and a live man's crime, and understood with perfect clarity that the exhilaration was over.

I picked up the crowbar and started working.
