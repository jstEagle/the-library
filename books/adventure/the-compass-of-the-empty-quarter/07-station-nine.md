## 7. Station Nine

The handcar and I arrived at Station Nine an hour past midnight, which was fitting, because everything about Station Nine seemed to have arrived an hour past its own schedule and simply never caught up.

I'd pumped the last forty miles on rails that the commission's own books called "conditionally abandoned," a phrase I had personally typed into a report three years ago and now understood to mean: *we stopped looking, so officially it isn't there.* The quarter at night is not empty the way people say it is. It's full — of wind working the telegraph wires like a man tuning an instrument nobody will ever hear, of stars stacked up with the shameless abundance of things that have never been audited. My arms were jelly. My shoulders had filed a formal complaint. But the chest rode behind me on the car's bed, lashed down with rope I'd stolen from my own crew stores, and every jolt of the track reminded me it was real.

I had told myself, for two hundred miles, that this was still salvage. Recovery of materials. A logistical errand with paperwork attached. I am very good at telling myself things; I've had ten years of practice, and like all long practice it has made me worse, not better.

Station Nine resolved out of the dark the way stations do in the empty country — first the water tower, black against blacker sky, then a scatter of buildings that might once have been a town and were now more of a rumor with roofs. No lights except one, low and yellow, in what I took to be the station house. The tower leaned over everything like a chaperone who has outlived everyone she was supposed to be watching.

The records said one resident. The records, for once, were right.

* * *

He heard the handcar from half a mile out — of course he did; in country that quiet you can hear a man swallowing — and when I came up the station platform he was already standing in the doorway with a lantern held high and a shotgun held low, which is the posture of a man who has been expecting trouble for thirty years and would be almost disappointed if it didn't come.

"Railway business," I said, hands open, because it was true in the way most useful lies are true.

"There hasn't been railway business here since before you were born." He was old the way driftwood is old — bleached, hard, shaped entirely by what had been done to him. His accent had been sanded down but not removed; there was a Vharskan vowel hiding in there, rounded and stubborn, like a coin under floorboards. "You're off the Kessel line. Surveyor's coat. Commission boots." He looked me over the way I look over a gradient — measuring, dismissing, filing. "So either the world got interested in Station Nine again, or you're lost. And surveyors don't get lost."

"Ilya Brandt?"

The shotgun didn't move, but something behind his eyes did — a door closing, or trying to. "Nobody by that name."

"That's a remarkable thing," I said, "because your name is on a manifest I've read, dated forty-one years ago, clerk third class, border corridor office."

For a long moment the only sound was the wind in the dead telegraph wires, playing its tuneless tune. Then he lowered the lantern and, with the air of a man conceding a point in a game he'd hoped was over, said: "You'd better come inside before the cold does."

* * *

The station house interior was a museum of one man's refusal to leave. Timetables from defunct lines framed on the walls like saints' icons. A stove burning low and clean. On the desk, under a paperweight made from a rail spike, a single sheet of paper covered in columns — water levels, pump pressures, dates going back years. He had been keeping records of a water tower that served no trains, for no company, in a town with no people.

"You keep the tower alive," I said.

"I keep the *records* alive." He said it sharply, then heard himself, and something bitter moved across his face. "There's no difference anymore. There wasn't, after a while. You pump the water, you log the water, you log the logging of the water. It gives the day edges." He sat down heavily across from me. "Who sent you? Say the name and let's have done."

"No one sent me."

"Everyone is sent. I was sent. Forty-one years ago I was sent east with a crate of documents and instructions to burn them, and instead I buried them, and I buried my name with them, and I have been no one ever since, and no one is a very comfortable thing to be, so whoever sent you can go to—"

I set the ledger on the desk between us.

I watched him stop talking. I watched him recognize it — the binding, the stamp, the particular institutional ugliness of Vharskan government stationery, which even in reproduction has the look of something designed by a committee that hated beauty as a matter of policy. His hand came out toward it and stopped, hovering, the way you hover over a stove you know is hot.

"Where did you get this."

"Kessel bend. Flood scoured the bank. It was in a lead-lined chest with maps of lines that don't exist."

"They exist," Brandt said quietly. "That was always the trick. The lines existed. The people went down them. Only the *paperwork* doesn't exist, and paperwork is what a country is, in the end. When the country dissolved, the paperwork dissolved, and everyone agreed to pretend the trains had never run." He finally touched the cover, one finger, like testing ice. "I catalogued this. I know this ledger. I carried it. I was supposed to burn it and instead I—" He stopped. Recalculated. Looked up at me with eyes gone suddenly, terribly shrewd. "Why do you have it? Who are you?"

"Astrid Ashgrove. Surveyor, grade two, Corridor Commission."

"And who sent you, Astrid Ashgrove, Surveyor, grade two?"

"No one." I heard how thin it sounded. I decided to stop spending thin words. "I found it. I read it. One of the signatures in it belongs to Halvard Crane, and Halvard Crane funds the commission I work for, and tomorrow — today, now — his security chief is probably following my tracks down this same line. That's who I am. That's the whole of it."

He sat back. The lantern hissed. Somewhere outside the tower creaked against the wind, keeping its own kind of time.

"Burn it," he said.

"What?"

"Burn it. Bury it deeper. Take the next freight out and forget the bend exists. I did the arithmetic you're doing right now, forty-one years ago, and I'll save you the trouble: the sum comes out to *survive*. You put your name on paper next to that ledger, and Crane's people will erase you the way they erased the corridor — thoroughly, quietly, and with excellent documentation." He tapped the ledger without opening it. "I knew men who testified. Do you know where they are? Neither does anyone else. That's the answer. *Neither does anyone else.*"

"It's evidence," I said. "It names names. There are descendants—"

"There are *graves*," he said, and his voice cracked on it, just once, a hairline fracture in all that driftwood. "Descendants is a word for people who got to keep living. There are graves along three hundred miles of that corridor, unmarked, unmapped, and you want to know the worst part? I helped draw up the manifests. Clerk third class. I stamped them. I was very good at stamping. It gave the days edges." He laughed, and it was the worst sound I'd heard in years. "You see? I kept the ledgers and then I kept the water levels and it's all the same discipline. You write the number down so you don't have to think about what the number means."

* * *

We sat with that for a while. I poured us both coffee from his pot, which tasted like it had been brewed sometime during the previous administration, and neither of us complained, because complaining would have required admitting we wanted something better, and neither of us was in a mood to want things.

"Why did you bury it?" I asked finally. "The ledger. If surviving was the whole arithmetic — why keep the evidence that could kill you?"

He was quiet long enough that I thought he wouldn't answer. Then he got up, went to the wall of timetables, and stood in front of them like a man addressing a jury of departed trains.

"Because I was the last one who remembered their names," he said. "Not the officials'. The deported. You spend months typing a manifest — four hundred souls, five hundred — you learn the names whether you mean to or not. Anselm Petrik, wheelwright. The Dvorak sisters, weavers. A boy listed only as 'the Kessel fiddler,' no given name, because whoever compiled that page couldn't be bothered." His finger moved along a timetable frame, touching nothing. "Everyone else forgot on purpose. Somebody had to forget by accident. So I kept the paper. Not to use. Just so the forgetting wouldn't be total." He turned around. "And now you've dug it up and it's not a memorial anymore, it's a weapon, and weapons require a hand willing to be burned."

"I'm willing."

"You're a child in good boots."

"I'm a person who spent ten years making sure no one knew my name," I said, and heard my own voice do something it hadn't done in a decade — crack. "Do you know why? Because back home there was a scandal, small and stupid and local, the kind that eats a person alive in a town of nine hundred people, and I ran, and I took a job where nobody asks questions because the timetable answers everything, and I told myself invisibility was safety. Ten years. I measured my life in miles because miles don't ask who you are." I pulled the key from my pocket — the chest key I'd pocketed that first day at the bend, the first theft of my entire careful career — and set it on the desk next to the ledger. "I'm not brave. I'm a coward who finally ran out of track. But I carried this three hundred miles through the quarter on a handcar with my arms shaking, and I'm telling you: I'd rather be erased standing up than survive sitting down. I've tried the other way. It doesn't hold weight."

Brandt looked at the key. At me. At the ledger, which had waited forty-one years under lead and dirt and one coward's guilty devotion to be exactly what it was.

"Cowardice recognizes cowardice," he said at last, and there was something in it that wasn't quite contempt — something older and tireder and, God help me, almost gentle. "That's how you catch one. Nobody else can spot us. We blend in." He lowered himself into the chair, opened a drawer, and took out a pen — a real pen, ink bottle and all, kept oiled and ready for forty-one years of documents that never came. "A sworn statement needs a witness besides yourself. Is there a justice of the peace within two hundred miles?"

"No."

"Then I'll swear it to the tower," he said. "She's seen more than any judge." He pulled a sheet of paper toward himself, squared it against the desk edge with the precision of a man for whom squaring paper had once meant life and death, and began to write. "My name is Ilya Brandt," he said aloud, testing it, rolling the Vharskan vowels back into place like coins recovered from under floorboards. "Clerk third class, border corridor office. And I am going to state, for the record, everything I spent forty-one years pretending I'd forgotten."

He wrote until the lamp burned low. Outside, the eastern sky had started to go grey at the edges, and with it came a sound I had been dreading since Kessel bend, faint but unmistakable, riding the rails themselves like a message through a wire:

An engine. Coming from the south. And gaining.
