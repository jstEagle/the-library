## 8. The Last Train Through

Maren Voss had sealed enough lines in her career to know that a railway is never really closed. You can spike the switches, chain the gates, post men with rifles at every milepost, and still the thing breathes under your hands like something buried alive. Rails remember. That was the trouble with rails. They remembered everything, and they told anyone who knew how to listen.

She stood on the platform at Junction Kessel with her coat collar up against the river damp and watched her men work. Halvard Crane had given her forty of them and one instruction, delivered in that warm boardroom voice he used for unpleasant things: *Nothing leaves the valley.* Not the chest. Not the ledger. Not the surveyor who'd pulled it out of the river like a splinter from an old wound.

"North line's spiked," reported Drescher, her second, a former customs officer who'd learned to treat orders as weather—inevitable, impersonal, occasionally destructive. "South line's chained. The freight from Station Nine comes through at 0400. We hold it at the junction, search it top to bottom, and let it roll after."

"And if it doesn't roll?"

Drescher shrugged. "Then Crane owns a dead line and a very expensive excuse."

Voss almost smiled. She liked Drescher. He understood the arithmetic. Everything in this business was arithmetic: how many men, how many hours, how many ways a person could be made to disappear into a schedule. She had spent eleven years making people disappear into schedules for Halvard Crane, and she was good at it, and she had stopped asking herself what that made her somewhere around year four.

The surveyor was the variable. Ashgrove. Voss had read the file twice on the ride down. Ten years of clean, unremarkable service. No debts worth mentioning, no lovers worth mentioning, no politics worth mentioning. A person built entirely out of gradients and timetables—the kind of employee Crane's world manufactured by the thousand and consumed without noticing.

And yet.

Voss had been doing this long enough to know that the quiet ones were the ones you feared. The loud ones wanted something you could price. But a person who had spent a decade learning to want nothing—that person could not be bought, because there was nothing on the shelf to hang a price tag on. Voss had offered Ashgrove silence, promotion, a clean new name, all the currency of their trade, and had watched the offer land like rain on stone.

So now it came down to a freight train and a junction, which was fitting. In the end, everything on a railroad came down to a train and a junction.

"The clerk," Drescher said. "Brandt. You think he signed anything?"

"I think old clerks keep carbon copies of their own funerals," Voss said. "I think if Ashgrove went three hundred miles across the empty quarter to find him, it wasn't for the conversation."

She looked north, up the line, where the rails ran straight as a ruled line into the dark and then bent away toward Station Nine and whatever waited there. Somewhere up that line was a handcar, probably abandoned now, sitting on a siding like a shed skin. And somewhere behind it, moving south on foot or by bribery or by some means Voss hadn't yet imagined, was a surveyor carrying a dead nation's accounts payable.

"Double the watch on the tunnel road," she said.

"There's no tunnel road," Drescher said. "That whole stretch is condemned. Hasn't seen traffic since before the war."

Voss turned to look at him. "Condemned," she repeated. "You know what I've learned about this country, Drescher? Nothing here is ever condemned. It's just waiting."

* * *

Astrid crouched in the signal box above the junction, watching Voss's lanterns move along the platform below, and did the arithmetic of despair.

One scheduled freight train, due through at 0400, bound south with ore and, if Astrid had anything to say about it, one sworn statement sewn into a mail sack. One junction, held by forty men with rifles and chains and the calm professional patience of people paid by the hour to be immovable. And between the junction and the tribunal clerk in Corvane City, three hundred miles of track that Voss controlled from end to end.

The statement couldn't go around. It couldn't go over. It had to go through—and Voss owned the through.

Astrid checked the pocket watch by feel, its face too worn to read in the dark but its tick familiar as a heartbeat. 0230. Ninety minutes. Below, a lantern swung twice: shift change. Men moved, coughed, stamped their feet against the river damp. Chains clinked against switch stands. A machine of flesh and iron, running exactly as designed.

*They built it,* Astrid thought. *Crane's money, Voss's men, the commission's seals. They built a machine to stop one piece of paper.*

And that was when the thought arrived—not as inspiration, which would have been flattering, but as memory, which was worse. Because Astrid had spent the evening in Brandt's station house reading maps, and not the commission's maps. The other ones. The ones stamped with the double-headed eagle of Vharska, drawn by surveyors who had worked these same grades sixty years ago for reasons their government had never entered into any public record.

Lines that officially never existed. Chapter three of Astrid's life, except it had happened to someone else first.

There was a tunnel. Nine miles east of the junction, bored through the Karst ridge in '09, used for four years, then abandoned when the border moved and the corridor it served became somebody else's country. Officially: collapsed. Condemned. Struck from every timetable and every commission chart, because a nation does not like to advertise the roads it built and then lost.

But nations fall. Their paperwork survives them. And a collapsed tunnel, according to the Vharskan survey sheets folded inside Astrid's coat, had not collapsed at all. It had been sealed—both portals walled, ventilation shafts capped, the interior left dry and sound, because the army engineers who closed it had done the job properly, expecting to come back someday. Nations always expect to come back.

Ninety minutes to reach the east portal. Nine miles of condemned grade beyond it, rejoining the main line south of Voss's net. If the walls were as the map promised. If the drainage hadn't flooded the bore. If the air inside wasn't poison after sixty years of standing still. If, if, if—the whole plan hung on ifs the way a bridge hangs on piers, and any one of them failing meant the statement died in a mail sack at a guarded junction while Astrid died somewhere more inconvenient.

Astrid found Tomas's face rising unbidden—young Tomas, who had noticed the missing chest entry and said nothing, whose loyalty was a debt Astrid hadn't asked for and couldn't repay from hiding. *Tell no one,* Astrid had written, and left it unsigned, because signatures were the one thing being rationed this week.

Well. Almost the one thing.

Astrid slung the pack, took the last pull of cold coffee, and went down the back stairs of the signal box into the dark, walking the ties east with the Vharskan map open against their chest like a second heartbeat.

* * *

The east portal was exactly where a dead empire had left it: behind a curtain of birch scrub and a wall of mortared stone that some soldier had chiseled shut with more care than most soldiers gave to living. Astrid put a shoulder to the loose keystone the margin notes described—"third course, left of center, packed sand behind"—and felt the wall exhale sixty years of stale, mineral-smelling air into their face.

Dry. Cold. Breathing, faintly, through the capped vents somewhere overhead. The engineers had done it properly, just as the map promised.

Astrid stood in the black mouth of the tunnel and felt the fear arrive on schedule, right on time, like a train you'd been dreading all day. There were rats down there, probably. Standing water, maybe. Air that might be thin or foul or simply old. And nine miles of absolute darkness with nothing but a hand lamp, a compass, and the confidence of men who had been dead for two generations.

"You're measuring this in miles," Astrid said aloud, to nobody, because saying it aloud made it true. "Not years. Miles. Walkable miles."

It was the litany of a decade. It had gotten them across the empty quarter and out of every corner they'd ever painted themselves into. It got them through the portal.

The tunnel was a cathedral nobody had prayed in since before the war. Astrid walked the center of the bore where the ballast was firmest, lamp beam swinging, counting culverts off the survey sheet—culvert seventeen, culvert eighteen—each one a small confirmation that the dead surveyors had known their trade. Water lay in the side ditches, shallow and clear. The air tasted of stone and rust but not of rot. At mile six, a section of lining had cracked and dumped a fan of rubble across the trackbed, and Astrid climbed it hand over hand with the pack straps cutting their shoulders, and stood at the top breathing hard in the dark, and laughed—one short, disbelieving note that the tunnel swallowed whole.

Because this was the joke, and it was a good one: Vharska had built this tunnel to move deported people and stolen goods along a corridor it pretended didn't exist. And now it was going to carry the account of those crimes to daylight, because the empire's lies had been so thorough, so well-engineered, so beautifully documented, that they'd survived as a secret road for the truth. The ledger had come out of a river. The route was coming out of a lie. The dead nation was paying its debts at last, in the only coin it had left: information.

At 0340, Astrid came out of the south portal into a night full of stars and the smell of sagebrush, and stood blinking like something exhumed.

* * *

The mail hook was the last problem, and it was almost embarrassingly simple, which Astrid chose to take as the universe apologizing.

The southbound freight didn't stop at the little flag stop below the ridge—it took the exchange pouch off the arm-mounted hook at thirty miles an hour, same as it had for fifty years, same as it would tonight, because even a line under siege kept its schedules. Schedules were holy. Astrid had staked a life on that article of faith, and tonight it finally paid a dividend.

The pouch went up on the hook at 0412, weighted with Brandt's sworn statement wrapped in oilcloth, addressed in block letters to the tribunal clerk in Corvane City, with a duplicate already riding in Astrid's coat toward a journalist who owed Brandt a favor from another lifetime.

Astrid stood beside the track and watched the headlamp swell out of the south, watched the engine pass close enough to feel the heat of it, watched the catcher arm snatch the pouch off the hook with a sound like a door closing. Then red taillights, shrinking, gone.

Somewhere behind, at Junction Kessel, Maren Voss was opening an empty mail sack and learning that the line she'd sealed had a hole in it shaped like history. Let her do the math. She seemed good at math.

Astrid should have felt triumphant. Instead they felt the way the tunnel must have felt for sixty years: hollow, sound, and waiting for someone to notice the walls were load-bearing.

Because the statement was loose in the world now, and statements had consequences, and consequences had names attached. To deliver evidence was one thing; anyone could mail a package. But evidence needed a witness. Tribunals wanted faces. Journalists wanted bylines. Sooner or later—probably sooner, probably at a flooded crossing where it had all begun—someone was going to ask the only question that mattered:

*Who found the chest?*

Astrid looked south, where the taillights had vanished, and then west, toward Kessel Bend and the woman who would be looking for an answer.

"Not yet," Astrid told the empty quarter, and started walking. "But soon. And under my own name."

The words sat strangely in the mouth—a name, spoken aloud, in open country, where anything said could be heard. It felt less like safety than it ever had.

It felt, for the first time in ten years, like a beginning.
