## 1. Ten Years of Miles

I have logged eleven thousand, four hundred and six miles on foot in the last ten years. I know this because I write it down, because a man who measures his life in miles doesn't have to measure it in anything else.

The morning I picked up Corben Voss's trail for the last time, the desert outside Port Moresby's reach was doing its impression of an oven that had given up on cooking and settled for slow roasting. The red dirt held the night's cold for maybe an hour after dawn, and then it surrendered it, the way everything out here eventually surrenders something. I was three days behind Voss and gaining at a rate that would have depressed a mathematician.

Let me explain what I do, since I've had a decade to get good at explaining it and I still haven't found a way to make it sound like anything. I'm a ranger. Not the park kind—the contract kind, licensed through a federal office in the capital that has never once, in ten years, sent anyone out to see whether the work was real. Landowners, mining outfits, a few cattle stations too poor to hire real security, hire me to track down the people who take things from them. I find them. I bring back what they took, or I bring back proof of what they took, and the law does the rest, or doesn't, depending on the season and the mood of whatever magistrate drew the short straw.

Mostly it's cattle. Mostly it's men who stole cattle because they were hungry or stupid, and usually both, and usually the two conditions fed each other until the theft seemed like the only reasonable act left in their lives. I understand those men. I've eaten with them, in the shade of a windmill, before cuffing them and walking them back to whatever passed for justice in their district. I have never once hated the work. I have also never once loved it. There's a difference between a job that's empty and a job that's full of nothing in particular, and I've spent ten years pretending I couldn't tell them apart.

Corben Voss was not a cattle thief.

The file on him was thin in the way that files are thin when someone has paid to keep them that way. Forty-one years old. Former surveyor, which meant he could read country the way I read it, which meant catching him would be a conversation between two people who spoke the same language and were both trying to lie in it. Six months ago he'd walked into a records annex outside Port Moresby—a building so forgotten it didn't even rate a guard dog, just a padlock and the assumption of heat—and walked out with a sealed brass tube containing survey papers from 1911 through 1919. Colonial-era documents. The kind of thing that sits in a drawer for a hundred years doing nothing but aging.

He hadn't taken the cash box. It was sitting right there, three hundred kina in it, and he'd stepped over it.

That detail had bothered the clerk who filed the report, and it bothered me, and it bothered my employer, a man named Ossler who represented interests he described, always, as *regional stakeholders*, as if the region were a patient and he its concerned family. Ossler had hired me with a speed that told me the tube mattered more than the money, and he'd paid a retainer that told me the same thing louder.

"A hundred years of paper," I'd said to him, across a desk in a Port Moresby hotel bar where the ice melted faster than the conversation. "What's in it that's worth this?"

"Boundaries," Ossler said. "Old boundaries. You wouldn't understand the implications."

He was right about that, in the sense that I didn't understand them yet. He was wrong about it in every other sense, but I didn't know that yet either, and there's a version of my life where I never find out. I think about that version sometimes. It's a smaller life. It fits in a saddlebag.

* * *

Voss traveled like a man who'd done it before, which is to say badly in ways that looked good and well in ways that looked like nothing. He camped cold—no fire, ever, which in that country meant eating tinned food at room temperature and sleeping in a man's own stink, and I respected him for it the way you respect a toothache: thoroughly and without affection. He watered at rock holes instead of stations, which meant he'd memorized the hydrology of a desert most people couldn't find on a map. And he left almost nothing.

Almost.

Here is what ten years of miles buys you. It buys you the ability to read a pressed grass stem and know which way a man's weight was rolling when he passed. It buys you the patience to sit for forty minutes watching a spider rebuild a web across a game trail, because if the web is intact, nothing has come through since midnight, and if it's broken at shoulder height, your man came through running, and if it's broken at knee height, your man came through drunk, and either way you've just read a page of a book that was written while you slept. It buys you the knowledge that every tracker in the world is a liar about one thing: we call it following a trail, but what we're really doing is following a man's decisions, and a man's decisions leave a wider, clearer track than his boots ever will.

Voss's decisions said he was in no hurry, and then, on the ninth day of my pursuit, they said he was.

The country changed the way it always does out there—gradually, then all at once. The red dirt went to gravel, the gravel to stone, and the spinifex gave ground to acacia scrub, and the acacia scrub to something greener, and I came over a rise at midday and saw, laid out below me, the first creek I'd seen in two hundred miles. Dry, but recently dry. The clay in its bed still held the ghost of water, cracked into plates the size of dinner plates, and the ghost was less than a week old.

And Voss's track went straight down the bank and along the creek bed, heading east.

East was the coast.

I sat on the rise for a long time with my hat brim low and my canteen warm in my hand and I did the arithmetic that I had been refusing to do for nine days. Every mile of this pursuit had been pointed, in the loose way that weather is pointed, generally eastward, generally downhill, generally toward the one thing in this country I had arranged my entire adult life to avoid. I'd told myself it was coincidence. I'd told myself the desert was big and Voss was in it and the geometry worked itself out. But a man's decisions leave a wider track than his boots, and Voss's decisions had just resolved into a bearing, and the bearing was water.

I had not stood at the edge of the sea in ten years. I want to be precise about that, because precision is the only honest thing I own: not the coast, not a river, not a billabong deeper than my knees. I had built a career, a reputation, a whole dry architecture of a life, in the one part of the country where water was a rumor. People assumed it was temperament. Rangers are desert people, they said, it's a type, like greyhounds and priests. And I let them assume it, because the truth was a door I'd nailed shut from the inside, and you don't discuss the doors you've nailed shut. You just make sure the house you build has no windows facing them.

My brother drowned. That's the whole of it, and it's less than the whole of it, the way a grave marker is less than a man. It happened in a water dispute—officially a dispute over allocation rights on an irrigation channel, unofficially a rigged auction, officially an accident, unofficially nothing at all, because the men who rig auctions are also the men who write the reports. I was nineteen. The file on his death was thin in the way that files are thin when someone has paid to keep them that way. I have spent ten years not thinking about that sentence, and I have just now, for the first time, written it down in my head in exactly those words, and I notice that the file on Corben Voss is thin in the same way, and I notice that I noticed, and I decide not to notice that I noticed.

This is how avoidance works. It's not a wall. It's a hallway with all the doors painted the same color as the walls.

* * *

I caught sight of him on the eleventh day, and the catching was mine, not his.

He'd made a mistake, the first one, and it was the kind of mistake a good man makes when he's tired: he'd cut a corner. Instead of swinging wide around a granite outcrop with its approaches in plain view, he'd gone close along its base, trusting the rock to hide his line. It did hide his line—from anyone coming behind him at ground level. But I had climbed the outcrop at first light, because climbing things is free and walking around them costs water, and from the top I watched him come down a drainage line three miles east, a small dark figure moving with the unhurried economy of a man who believes he's alone.

I could have taken him. That's the thing I want on the record, in whatever court of myself convenes over this. I was above him, downwind, with a rifle I'd never once fired at a human being in ten years and a pair of legs that had eaten eleven thousand miles of country. I could have come down that drainage, called his name, and had the cuffs on him by noon. The tube would have gone back to Ossler. Ossler would have paid me. I would have logged the miles—two hundred and forty, this one, door to door—and I would have driven back to the desert and waited for the next thin file.

Instead I sat on the granite and watched him until he was gone, and I told myself I was waiting for a better position.

I was not waiting for a better position. I was watching a man carry a brass tube of hundred-year-old paper toward the sea, and I was wondering, for the first time in a decade, what it was that made a man step over a cash box. Thieves take money. That's the covenant, that's the whole contract between my profession and its prey. A man who takes paper instead of money is not a thief. He's a reader. And I found, sitting on that hot rock with the sweat drying salt on my forearms, that I did not want to catch a reader. I wanted to know what he'd read.

So I followed him at a distance that would have embarrassed my instructors, and I told myself it was tactics.

* * *

The country kept its promise. Greener every day, then wetter, then loud—the desert is silent in a way that gets into your teeth, and the country east of it is loud in a way that does too, insects and birds and wind in leaves, all of it talking at once like a market. I drank water that hadn't come out of a tank. My hands, I noticed, were not entirely steady, and I attributed this to diet.

Voss reached the coast on the fifteenth day. I know because I was a half mile behind him, close enough now that I'd stopped pretending I was tracking and admitted I was following, the way you admit you're not lost, you just don't know where you are.

The coast came up out of the trees all at once: a line of white sand, a line of green water, and beyond it the flat hammered silver of the sea going out to a horizon that had no edges, no fence lines, no survey posts, nothing a man could measure. I stopped at the tree line the way you'd stop at a border post, and I stood there with eleven thousand four hundred and twenty-one miles in my legs and I looked at all that water, all that unmeasurable water, and my heart was doing something I refused to name.

Voss's track went along the beach to a village—a scatter of stilt houses over the tide line, canoes pulled up, smoke from cooking fires. And then his track did something strange. It went to the water's edge and it stopped. It stopped so completely, so deliberately, that I knew he had stood there a long time. There were two impressions of his heels, deep, planted, facing the sea, and beside them, pressed into the wet sand, the round butt-print of a brass tube set down on end, the way a man sets down a thing he's about to stop carrying.

Then his track turned north along the beach, lighter, quicker, a man who had made a decision.

I followed it north. The tide was going out—out hard, out wrong, the water pulling back from the sand in a long silver sheet, exposing reef flats and sandbars and things that I had no names for, and the villagers had come out of their houses to stand at the edge of the retreating sea and not one of them took a single step after it. They stood the way people stand at the edge of a grave.

A mile north, where the beach bent toward a headland of black rock, Voss's track left the sand and climbed toward a cave mouth in the headland's flank, a dark opening that the falling tide was uncovering inch by inch, and I stood at the tree line with the sea air in my throat and thought: whatever is in there, he went in after it, and whatever it costs, the bill comes due.

I had spent ten years measuring miles. The tide was going out, and for the first time in my life, I was about to measure water.
