## 2. The Tide That Lied

The sea did something impossible on a Tuesday, and by Wednesday the whole coast was talking about it.

I heard about it from a woman selling smoked fish at the last crossroads before the water, who told me the tide had gone out and forgotten to come back. She said it the way people say things they don't quite believe themselves—half gossip, half prayer. I bought two fish I didn't want because she looked like she needed someone to buy them, and asked her which way to the village of Wagu-something-or-other, the name having been mangled beyond recognition between her mouth and my ears.

She pointed with her chin, the way people do out here when pointing with a hand would be too much commitment.

I'd been tracking Corben Voss for eleven days at that point, and I'll be honest about what that means: it means nothing. Tracking Voss was like tracking weather. The man left signs the way a ghost leaves footprints—just enough to keep you moving, never enough to catch him. Ten years of chasing petty thieves across empty miles had taught me every trick there was, and Voss used all of them, competently, without flair. He walked on rock when he could. He backtracked through his own camp. He'd once, I was fairly sure, tied his bootlaces in a pattern designed to make me waste forty minutes measuring stride length against a print that wasn't even his.

But he was heading for the coast. That much was certain. And men like Voss don't go to the coast for the view.

The village sat where the land gave up pretending to be solid—a scatter of stilt houses over grey-green shallows, canoes pulled up on mud the color of old bruises, nets drying on racks like the skeletons of enormous fish. It had maybe sixty residents and one of everything: one trade store, one church, one dog with opinions. The kind of place where a stranger is noticed before his boat touches bottom.

Which mine did, within four minutes of arrival.

"You're the fifth," said a boy of perhaps ten, poling alongside me as I waded ashore. "Looking for the tall man."

"Fifth?"

"Three came yesterday. One this morning." He considered me with the frank appraisal of someone who hadn't yet learned to pretend not to stare. "You're the first one with dusty boots."

I looked down at my boots. They were, admittedly, spectacularly dusty. Eleven days of inland walking will do that. "Did the tall man say where he was going?"

"He didn't talk." The boy poled a slow circle around me. "He just watched the water. Then he went out to the point when the tide went strange, and he didn't come back."

"Strange how?"

The boy grinned, showing a gap where a tooth had recently surrendered. "Come see."

* * *

Here's what I knew about tides before that morning, which is roughly what any man who's spent ten years in dry country knows: the sea comes in twice a day, goes out twice a day, and doesn't take requests. It is the one appointment the world keeps without fail. Sailors build their lives around it. Fishermen set their clocks by it. Entire economies rise and fall on its punctuality.

So when I crested the black basalt point at the village's northern end and saw what lay below, my first instinct was not wonder. My first instinct was to check whether I'd been drugged.

The sea was gone.

Not retreated—*gone*, hauled back a quarter mile or more, leaving exposed a landscape that had no business existing: ribbed sandbars, pools steaming in the early heat, forests of coral dead or dying in the air, and everywhere the littered floor of the ocean itself—anchors, a wrecked dinghy's ribs, bottles green with age, things I couldn't name and didn't try to. Villagers stood along the tideline in a loose, silent row, and none of them were taking photographs, which told me more than anything else could have. People photograph wonders. These people were standing the way you stand at a funeral.

And there, at the base of the point, where the cliff met the exposed seabed, was a cave.

It wasn't hidden. That was the unsettling part—it stood open like a doorway in a house you'd walked past your whole life without knowing was there. A tall arch of black stone, its floor swept clean by whatever violence of current normally kept it drowned, running back into the cliff farther than the light cared to follow.

"That's where the tall man went," the boy said beside me. He'd followed me up without invitation. "Nobody goes in there."

"Why not?"

He shrugged with the elaborate indifference of a child concealing genuine fear. "It doesn't have a name."

"Everything has a name."

"Not here it doesn't." He turned his canoe around and paddled away from me with more urgency than the statement warranted, and I stood there on the hot black rock with my dusty boots and my two unwanted fish and felt the first cold thread of something I couldn't yet name work its way under my collar.

I should mention—I'm a careful man. Ten years of ranger work makes you careful the way a scar makes you careful; it's not philosophy, it's anatomy. I check knots twice. I count my rounds. I don't walk into caves that frighten children.

I walked into the cave.

Not because of courage. Let's be clear about that. I walked in because Corben Voss had gone in, and because eleven days of pursuit had built up a kind of momentum in me that I no longer knew how to stop, and because—and this is the part I've thought about since—the alternative was standing on that beach with the vanished sea and admitting I had no idea what I was actually chasing anymore. A tube of survey papers. Old ones. Worth, apparently, enough for a careful thief to burn a decade of careful invisibility. I'd stopped asking myself why somewhere around day six, because the answer kept coming back *because it's the job*, and *the job* had started sounding thinner each time I said it.

The cave floor was damp but walkable, shelving gently down into the dark. Ten meters in, the daylight behind me thinned to a grey memory, and I clicked on my torch.

Voss's camp announced itself thirty meters in, and it was wrong in ways that took me a moment to catalogue.

First: it was comfortable. A sleeping roll, unfurled and abandoned mid-use. A small spirit stove. An opened tin of corned beef with the spoon still standing in it, upright, like a flag planted in conquered territory. Nobody abandons a meal mid-bite unless something happened fast—or unless they wanted whoever found it to think something happened fast.

Second: the walls. By torchlight I could see marks scratched into the black stone at shoulder height—not graffiti, exactly. Tallies. Rows of them, some fresh, some furred over with mineral stain so old they'd nearly healed shut. Someone had been counting days in this cave across years, decades, generations. Hundreds of tallies. Thousands.

Third, and worst: the smell. Under the salt and wet stone there was something else, faint but unmistakable to anyone who's worked recovery details—old smoke, old cloth, the particular sweetness of places where people have lived and, possibly, died. The cave remembered occupants the way a bottle remembers wine.

I stood there with my torch beam shaking slightly—my hand, not the light; the light was fine—and made myself do the job. Camp inventory. He'd left the stove, the bedroll, most of his food. He'd taken the papers. Of course he'd taken the papers; the whole point of Voss was that he never left the thing being chased. But near the back wall, wedged under a flat stone with the deliberateness of a man leaving a message rather than losing a possession, I found a scrap of chart.

It was old. Hand-drawn, ink gone brown, the paper soft as cloth. Most of it was missing—torn raggedly, one corner surviving—and what survived showed a stretch of riverine coastline rendered in the confident strokes of someone who'd actually surveyed it, with depth soundings in an archaic hand and one notation that stopped my breath:

*Entrance opens at extreme ebb — once in a generation. Within: the Magistrate's Ledger.*

Below that, smaller, in different ink, newer: a string of characters that meant nothing to me. Not coordinates. Not words in any language I knew. A cipher, or a key to one.

I crouched there holding a hundred-year-old piece of paper in a cave that shouldn't exist, listening to water drip somewhere in the dark with the patience of a clock, and I understood—with the clarity that sometimes arrives too late to be useful—that I had crossed some line I couldn't see. This wasn't a theft anymore. This was a door, and I'd put my hand on the handle.

Behind me, gravel shifted.

I came around with the torch in one hand and the other nowhere near a weapon, because rangers in these parts carry citations, not sidearms, and the silhouette in the cave mouth resolved into an old man. Small, straight-backed, barefoot on the wet stone, dressed in a shirt that had once been white and had since achieved a kind of philosophical grey. He looked at me the way you look at weather you've been predicting.

"You're the fourth one today," he said. His English was slow and precise, learned somewhere formal, decades ago. "The others, I sent home. You I could not send home."

"Why not?"

"Because you already touched the paper." He nodded at my hand. I looked down. I was still holding it. "The others only looked. Looking is cheap. Touching costs."

I set the chart down on the stone between us, carefully, the way you set down something that might be live. "I'm looking for a thief. Tall man, careful walker, came through yesterday. Did he take anything else from here?"

The old man lowered himself onto a rock with the economy of someone whose knees had negotiated a permanent settlement with gravity. "He took the papers he came for. He left the rest." He studied me. "You are police?"

"Ranger. Land patrol. Out west."

"Desert man." Something moved through his face—not quite a smile. "That is why you came in. Desert people think water is honest."

"And it isn't?"

"Water keeps everything," he said. "That is not the same as honest." He gestured, one hand taking in the cave, the dark, the tallies on the walls. "My grandfather's grandfather counted days in here. There was a magistrate then. Aldous Crane. A white government man who wrote down everything—who paid him, who he paid, whose land he stole with a pen instead of a gun. Everything went into his book. Every lie, written down in his own hand, because—" the old man paused, choosing the words with visible care— "because he wanted God to have evidence. Or because he was proud. Those are often the same thing."

"The ledger," I said. "On the chart."

"You read the chart?"

"A fragment."

"Then you know more than the three men yesterday, and less than the tall one." He leaned forward. "Listen to me, desert man, because I will say this once and I am old and my voice tires. This cave does not open when it should. Twice a century, sometimes less. When it opens, it is because the water has decided something, and the water does not decide for free. The year it opened before, we lost nine canoes and a girl who walked in on a dare and was found three days later at the river mouth, forty miles away, alive, speaking a language nobody knew." He said it flatly, without theater, which made it worse. "The cave always costs someone. That is not superstition. That is accounting."

"I don't believe in accounting like that."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "You believe in miles. I can see it on you. You measure everything—how far, how many days, how many men. You think if you measure enough, nothing can surprise you." He rose, joints cracking like green firewood. "The sea went out four days ago and has not come back. Four days. The fish are dying in the pools. The elders say the rivers upstream have been changed—someone is drinking the basin, diverting it, and the sea has come looking for its water. That is why the cave stands open. Not for you. Not for the tall man. For the water's own reasons." He started toward the entrance, then paused. "The tall man asked me the same question you're about to ask."

"Which is?"

"Where does the ledger lead." The old man smiled then, fully, and it transformed his face into something both kind and terrible. "I told him: it leads downstream. Everything here leads downstream. Even you."

He walked out into the light, and I stood alone in the dark with a dead man's counting marks on the walls and a fragment of chart in my head that I could not unsee.

* * *

I did the sensible thing. I want that on the record, wherever records end up. I walked out of the cave, I climbed the point, I stood in the sun among the silent villagers, and I told myself the sensible thing was to radio ahead, report Voss's direction of travel, and let the matter pass into larger hands.

Then I looked at the sea.

It still hadn't come back. Four days, the old man said, and the pools were steaming, and the fish lay silvering in the shallows, and the horizon had a bruised look in the southeast that my desert-trained eye read instantly, because a storm wall looks the same whether it's crossing sand or water: *something big is coming, and it is in no hurry, which is how you know it's serious.* Monsoon season, arriving early, arriving angry.

And beneath that, quieter, the other thing. The thing I'd been not-thinking about for eleven days, which now stood in front of me wearing an old man's voice: *someone is drinking the basin.* Rivers diverted. Water stolen at scale. And a tube of survey papers worth killing for, and a ledger full of forged deeds, and a mining consortium's fingerprints, probably, on all of it—though I didn't have the consortium yet, just the shape of it, the way you feel a storm before you see it.

Ten years I'd chased thieves across empty country, and I'd told myself it was service. Standing on that dead seabed, I finally admitted what the old man had seen in five minutes: it wasn't service. It was motion. Miles as anesthesia. As long as I was measuring distance, I never had to measure anything else—like why a desert ranger who grew up two streets from a flooded creek won't sleep within earshot of running water. Like why I took the transfer inland the same year my brother drowned in a dispute over water rights that every document in the case file said was settled, fair, and legal, and every person who'd actually been there said was robbery.

Paper had killed my brother. Paper, signed and stamped and filed. And now a piece of hundred-year-old paper in my pocket said that somewhere upstream, in a cave that cost someone every time it opened, there was a book that proved the paper was always like this—that the rot went down to the founding charter, to the first pen stroke.

Voss was heading upstream. Toward the basin. Toward the source.

There was exactly one seaworthy boat left in the village before the monsoon closed the coast—a broad-beamed, long-tailed workboat the color of rust, owned by a fisherman who named a price that assumed I was richer than I looked and dumber than I felt. I paid half up front and promised the rest on return, and he looked at me with the pity people reserve for men buying tickets to the wrong destination.

"You going upriver?" he asked. "In this season? Alone?"

I opened my mouth to say yes.

"No," said a voice behind me, in an accent that had crossed an ocean to get here. "Not alone. He's taking me."

I turned. A woman stood at the top of the boat ramp with two instrument cases and a expression that suggested she had already decided the boat was hers and was merely informing me of the arrangement. She was sunburned, mud-spattered, and radiating the specific fury of a scientist whose data has personally offended her.

"You're the ranger," she said. It wasn't a question. "The one asking about flow rates. We need to talk. Preferably before you buy that leaky coffin."

The fisherman, sensing either a sale or a spectacle, began untying the mooring line.

The sea, behind us, still had not come home.
