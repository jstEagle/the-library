## 3. A Stranger With Instruments

I have been thrown out of better bars than the one in Karawari Landing, but never by a man holding a salinity meter.

Let me back up. My name is Ilse Marchetti, and for eleven years I have made a living measuring water and telling people truths about it that they did not want to hear. Water is honest. It is the only thing in my professional life that has never lied to me. Rivers don't fudge their numbers. Aquifers don't pad their reports. When a river changes its mind, it does so in public, in parts per thousand, and you can stand on the bank with a forty-dollar instrument and catch it in the act.

Which is what I was doing when the tide lied.

The anomaly had shown up first in satellite data — a coastal salinity drop of nearly four practical salinity units over six weeks, in a basin where the seasonal swing should have been less than one. Then the tide gauge at the fishing village downriver went strange: an extreme low that no lunar table predicted, exposing seabed that local memory said had not seen air in a generation. The fishing village wouldn't talk about it. The mission station wouldn't talk about it. Everyone kept glancing upriver as if the answer were a relative they were ashamed of.

So I came upriver to find out who was embarrassing the ocean.

My working hypothesis was mining. It is almost always mining. Somewhere in the headwaters of the Sepik's tributary system, somebody was moving water — diverting flows, dewatering an open pit, dumping tailings into a catchment — and the whole estuarine balance was paying for it downstream. If I could get flow measurements from three or four points along the middle reaches before monsoon sealed the basin shut, I'd have enough to write a paper that would make certain people very unhappy. Making certain people very unhappy is, if I'm honest, the part of the job I enjoy most.

What I needed was a boat. What I found was a tall desert-ranger type standing next to the only seaworthy boat left at Karawari Landing, having a conversation with its owner in the low, patient voice of a man who negotiates for a living.

I should describe him properly, because first impressions matter and mine was wrong in every particular. He looked like a man assembled from spare parts: sun-cured skin, a jaw that had been broken at least once and set by someone in a hurry, hands too big for the rest of him. He wore a ranger's canvas jacket gone soft with age, and he stood the way men stand when they've spent years watching horizons — weight even, shoulders loose, nothing wasted. His name, I would learn, was Rafael Brightwater. At the time, all I knew was that he was between me and a boat, and that the monsoon wall was five days out and closing like a door.

"Excuse me," I said, in the tone I reserve for men negotiating over things that are already mine. "Is this boat spoken for?"

"It's spoken for," he said, without turning around.

"By whom? You?"

"Me and Tambo here are finalizing terms." He nodded at the owner, a wiry old fisherman who was grinning at me with the frank delight of a man watching weather come in from two directions.

"I'll double whatever he's offering."

That got his attention. He turned, and I got the full effect of eyes the color of dust — not brown exactly, more the color of the land itself after a dry season, everything burned down to essentials. He looked at me the way you look at a river gauge giving a reading you don't believe. Not hostile. Recalibrating.

"You're the hydrologist," he said. "From the coast. The one asking about the tide."

Word travels fast in small places. "I'm the hydrologist from everywhere. Who told you about me?"

"The elder at Wagu. He said a woman arrived with instruments and started sticking them in the water." He glanced at the case slung across my shoulder. "He also said you'd be trouble."

"Elders flatter me constantly."

"He meant it as a compliment."

"Then we agree." I stepped past him and put my hand flat on the gunwale of the boat — a long aluminum skiff with an outboard older than both of us combined, patched at the seams with something that had once been epoxy and now resembled scar tissue. "Here are my terms. I need to run transects at four points upstream over the next four days. I'll pay half the hire, half the fuel, and I won't ask what you're doing."

"That last part's generous," he said, "since what I'm doing is following a thief."

"Then we're both chasing something upriver. Perfectly compatible."

"Nothing about boats is perfectly compatible. Boats are where compatibility goes to die." But he was looking at me differently now — the recalibration resolving into something like interest, which I chose to find flattering rather than alarming. "Why do you care about some tide?"

"Because tides don't do that." I opened my instrument case partly to show him, partly because talking with my hands near my instruments steadies me the way other people are steadied by prayer. "A once-in-a-generation extreme low, uncorrelated with lunar phase, coinciding with a sustained salinity collapse across sixty kilometers of coastline. That's not weather. That's plumbing. Somebody upstream is rearranging the basin's water budget, and the sea is filing a complaint."

"And you think it's miners."

"I think it's miners the way you think your thief went upriver. It's the probability-weighted guess."

Something moved behind those dust-colored eyes — not agreement, exactly. Recognition. The look of a man hearing his own reasoning come out of a stranger's mouth and not enjoying the experience.

"Tambo," he said. "We take her."

Tambo's grin widened until I worried about structural failure. "Two fares, one boat, monsoon coming. Good luck to both of you," he said, which I later understood was less a blessing than a eulogy delivered in advance.

* * *

We loaded at dawn, which is when all bad decisions load, because dawn makes everything look provisional and forgivable. His gear: a bedroll, a rifle wrapped in oilcloth, a canvas satchel he kept within arm's reach at all times, and a battered notebook whose pages, when the wind flipped them, showed columns of figures — distances, headings, days logged. My gear: the meter, a refractometer, sample vials, a depth sounder, a laptop I had already accepted might die out here, and a change of clothes compressed into a space smaller than his ammunition.

He watched me stow the science and asked, "How long have you been doing this?"

"Hydrology? Eleven years. In this country? Four months."

"Where before?"

"Everywhere with water problems. Which is everywhere with people." I cinched a strap. "You?"

"Desert ranger. Ten years. Out east, past the government's attention span."

"Chasing what?"

"Thieves, mostly. Small ones." He said it the way a man describes a job he stopped finding meaningful somewhere around year six and kept doing anyway because stopping required a reason and inertia didn't. "One specific thief right now. Corben Voss. Stole a sealed tube of old survey papers off a government transport. Worth nothing on any market I know of, which means it's worth something to someone."

"And he ran toward water."

"Toward the coast. Then the trail went strange." He paused, and I watched him decide how much to tell a stranger, and watched him decide wrong — or rather, watch him tell me more than he'd planned, which I've learned is what happens when people haven't talked to anyone in a while. "There's a cave the tide opened. Locals won't go near it. Voss camped inside it. Left in a hurry."

"What was in the tube?"

"If I knew, I'd have caught him by now."

We pushed off into a river the color of strong tea, carrying enough sediment to make the light itself feel thick. Within an hour I had my meter out and was taking readings every ten minutes, logging coordinates, calling numbers aloud mostly to myself. He tolerated this for approximately ninety minutes before curiosity beat professionalism.

"What are the numbers telling you?"

"That the river is running heavy and getting heavier, and that the conductivity profile doesn't match rainfall." I held up the meter so he could see the display, because there is a particular pleasure in showing a layman raw data — it either bores them or converts them, and I wanted a convert. "This much suspended load this early in the season means someone's cut a new channel or blown a diversion upstream. Natural erosion doesn't spike like this unless there's a storm, and there's no storm above us yet."

"So the water's being moved."

"The water is being *stolen*," I said, and heard my own voice sharpen. "Sorry. It's a sore subject. Where I worked before this, a consortium diverted a river for a smelter and three villages lost their fishing season permanently. The compensation package included a school building with no teachers. I testified. It went badly for everyone except the consortium."

He was quiet for a stretch, reading the banks with that horizon-watcher's attention, cataloguing things I couldn't see. Then: "Your testimony. What happened to you?"

Ah. There it was. The question under the question. "I was reassigned," I said lightly. "To a post with excellent views and no equipment. I resigned. Now I work for whoever will fund actual measurement, which currently is a research trust that pays me late and asks no questions. Why? Does my file interest you?"

"Everything files interest me lately." He adjusted the throttle, and the old outboard coughed and caught. "Ten years of chasing petty thieves teaches you that nobody steals nothing. Every crime has a customer. Somebody wanted that survey tube, and somebody wants this river rearranged, and I'd bet my commission those somebodies shake hands at the same board table."

It was my turn to be quiet. Because underneath the wry delivery, the man had just described my entire career thesis in two sentences, and I felt the strange vertigo of being understood by a stranger — which is either the beginning of trust or the beginning of trouble, and on rivers it is usually both.

* * *

Trouble arrived on the second day, wearing an unmarked boat.

We had put in at midday to take a proper cross-section measurement at a confluence — me wading with the meter, Rafael standing in the shallows with the rifle unslung but pointed at the mud, which he claimed was habit. I was ankle-deep in data when he said, very quietly, "Don't turn around. Boat coming down the far channel. No registration, no markings, four men, and they're hugging the bank like they're looking for something."

"Fishermen hug banks."

"Fishermen don't have binoculars raised when they pass a woman standing in a river."

I kept my eyes on the instrument and read the numbers upside down in my memory, which is a skill I recommend to anyone planning a life of inconvenient honesty. Salinity still anomalous. Flow still wrong. And four men in an unmarked boat studying me with optics, in a basin where I had told exactly one person my itinerary.

"They're gone," Rafael said after a minute. "Heading downstream. Fast."

"Friends of yours?"

"No. Yours, maybe. Or friends of Voss's buyer." He waded back, and up close I saw he wasn't relaxed anymore; the loose horizon-watcher had folded away and something tighter stood in his place. "Question. Your research trust. Who funds it?"

"A consortium of universities and one anonymous endowment. Why?"

"Because anonymous endowments are how companies launder their curiosity. They may not be following *you*. They may be following anyone with instruments." He looked at my meter case the way he'd looked at the boat on the first day — assessing it as a variable, a risk, a thing that could be taken. "Who else knows you're on this river?"

"My trust. A colleague in Port Moresby. And now you."

"Keep it that way." He slung the rifle. "And from here on, we take readings fast and move faster. Monsoon's four days out, and I don't like the company."

"We're not partners," I reminded him. "We're co-lessees of a boat."

"Sure," he said, and the ghost of a smile crossed that broken-jaw face, quick as a fish rising. "Co-lessees. Best kind of partnership. Nothing to dissolve but the lease."

We ran the rest of the afternoon against a sky going the color of a bruise, and I logged readings that made less sense the further upstream we went — the river's arithmetic refusing to add up, as though someone upstream were cooking the books of the water itself. By dusk I had a hypothesis forming that I didn't say aloud, because hypotheses spoken too early become hostages.

At nightfall we tied up at a stilt village whose name Rafael seemed to already know, and while he negotiated our welcome with a man who greeted him like weather returning, I stood at the rail of the boat and looked upriver into the dark, where the numbers said something enormous and deliberate was happening to the water.

The tide hadn't lied, I realized. The tide had told the truth, early and loudly, the way honest witnesses do — and somewhere ahead of us, whoever had rearranged the basin's plumbing was about to discover that a hydrologist with a meter and a desert ranger with a grudge were coming to audit the books.

Behind me, Rafael struck a match to light the cook stove, and in its flare I saw him glance at my instrument case, then at me, recalibrating again.

"Tomorrow," he said, "we're going to meet the river's new owners. I can feel it."

I believed him. What I didn't yet believe — what I wouldn't believe until it was far too late to matter — was how well he already knew them.
