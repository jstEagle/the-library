## 4. Upstream Against the Season

The boat was called *Patience*, which struck me as either a joke or a prayer, and by the second hour on the river I suspected both.

She belonged to a fisherman named Tomas who wanted too much money for her and got it, because Ilse Marchetti did not negotiate so much as she out-stubborned people. She'd counted out the kina with the flat expression of a woman settling a debt she resented, and Tomas had handed over the keys to an outboard older than my career along with a warning delivered in Tok Pisin that Ilse translated for me in three words: "It leaks. Slowly."

"Everything leaks slowly," I said. "That's just entropy with good manners."

Ilse looked at me like I was a data point she intended to discard. "I meant the boat."

So did I. But I'd learned already that conversation with Ilse Marchetti worked best when you kept your metaphors in a separate compartment from your facts, because she would come after the metaphors with a knife.

We pushed off an hour before dawn, ahead of a storm wall stacked up over the interior like a debt collector who knew where you lived. The Sepik spread itself brown and muscular under a sky the color of wet concrete, and I sat in the stern with the outboard's tiller in one hand and Corben Voss's trail in my head, running upstream against the current and the calendar both.

Tracking a man through water is not like tracking him through sand. In the desert, every footprint is a sentence; you read the ground and it tells you what happened, when, how fast, how scared. On a river there is no ground. There are only the banks, and the villages, and the things people remember. Voss had passed through two days ahead of us—Tomas confirmed it without being asked twice, which told me Voss had paid well or paid badly enough to be memorable—and after that, the trail became a rumor with legs: a white man alone, buying fuel at Wagu Bend, asking questions about the old survey lines, heading up into the hill country where the river braided itself into a dozen arguments.

He was going to the cave. The chart fragment in my pocket said as much—the inland entrance, marked in faded ink with a symbol the elder at the coast had refused to look at directly. *The cave opens once a generation,* he'd said, *and it always costs someone.* I'd filed that under superstition at the time. Two days later, sitting in a leaking boat named Patience while a hydrologist took notes on water that was behaving like it had somewhere better to be, I was starting to move it to a different file.

"You're doing it again," Ilse said.

"Doing what?"

"Squinting at nothing. You've been squinting at the same bend for ten minutes." She didn't look up from her logbook. She wrote in it constantly, small precise figures in a hand so neat it looked printed, and every few minutes she'd frown at the numbers like they'd personally insulted her. "Either tell me what you see or stop performing vigilance. It's exhausting to watch."

"I'm reading the bank."

"And?"

"And the reeds are bent wrong." I cut the throttle and let us drift. "Somebody dragged a boat through there recently. Into the cut on the left. Heavy boat, recent—stems still wet above the waterline."

Ilse finally raised her head. She had the kind of face that made its decisions before her mouth caught up, and right now the decision was skepticism. "Fishermen drag boats."

"Fishermen drag boats toward the water. This was dragged away from it, upstream of a village, and hidden." I let us drift another few meters, watching the cut where the reeds stood crushed in a fan. "Somebody didn't want their boat found."

"Somebody like Voss?"

"Somebody like whoever's following Voss." I restarted the engine and swung us back into the main channel, and I felt her eyes stay on me for a long moment before she returned to her logbook. I'd have bet money she wrote something unflattering about me in it. I chose not to ask.

* * *

By midday the storm wall had eaten half the sky, and the river began telling us things we didn't want to hear.

It started as a feeling more than a fact—the water moving wrong, eddies spinning where they shouldn't, the current stuttering like a bad radio signal. Then Ilse started saying numbers out loud, and the numbers were worse than the feeling.

"That's impossible," she said, for the third time in an hour.

"Which part?"

"The stage height." She held up her gauge readings like evidence at a trial. "This stretch should be running at two-point-one meters this season. Historical mean, thirty years of records. We're at one-point-four and falling, upstream, during monsoon buildup. Rivers don't fall before a flood, Brightwater. They rise. Unless—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening, and stared upstream at the brown water as if it might confess.

"Unless somebody's holding it back," I finished.

She didn't answer, which was an answer. I thought about the salinity anomalies she'd been chasing on the coast, the tide that had lied low enough to open a cave that should have stayed drowned, and I felt the shape of something assembling itself behind all these disconnected facts—a shape with money and lawyers and, if the men in the unmarked boats were any indication, guns.

Because there were men in unmarked boats. That had become clear around noon, when we'd passed a side channel and seen a long aluminum hull pulled into the shade, no registration, no name, an outboard worth more than Tomas's entire village. And then again an hour later: two men on the bank under a tarp, scanning the river with binoculars, and when our wake reached them they moved with the unhurried urgency of professionals rather than the scrambling panic of fishermen surprised by weather.

"They're looking for us," Ilse said quietly.

"They're looking for anyone heading upstream. We're just the only idiots doing it."

"No." She shook her head, and there was something underneath her irritation now, something that might have been fear wearing irritation as a coat. "The man on the bank had a list. I saw him check it. You don't need a list to find fishermen."

I turned that over. A list meant names. Names meant someone upstream had described us—Voss, probably, or whoever Voss had talked to—or, less pleasantly, someone downstream had. Kessler's name had surfaced once on the coast, attached to a consortium and a rumor about water rights, and rumors about men like that traveled faster than boats.

"We should put in," Ilse said. "Wait out the front, travel at night."

"Night travel on an unfamiliar river in rising flood is how you die tired."

"And being found by those men is how you die quickly. Pick your pace of death."

"Wry," I said. "You're getting wry. I'm rubbing off on you."

"Don't flatter yourself. I was wry in three languages before you were born." But some of the tension went out of her shoulders, and I noticed—I'd been noticing, damn it, all morning—that when she wasn't glaring at me she was easy company, the way a sharp wind is easy once you stop fighting it and just lean.

* * *

The leak announced itself properly at four in the afternoon, with the modesty of all disasters: a soft sloshing sound under the floorboards that I first mistook for the river slapping the hull.

Then I put my hand down and came up wet.

"How slow did Tomas say?" I called forward.

Ilse looked back, saw my hand, and her expression performed a full audit. "Slowly," she said. "He said slowly."

"Well, entropy's picked up the pace." I found the source without much drama—a seam near the transom weeping in a thin steady line, the caulking gone brittle with age. Not fatal. Not yet. But the bilge pump was a hand pump, the storm wall was close enough now that I could smell it, ozone and wet vegetation rolling downriver ahead of the wind, and every hour we ran the engine hard was another hour of vibration working that seam wider.

I pumped. Ilse bailed with a plastic bucket when my arm gave out, and we settled into a rhythm that neither of us commented on, because commenting on it would have made it something, and neither of us was ready for whatever it was. The rain arrived in stages—first a hiss in the canopy far upstream, then a gray curtain walking across the water toward us like something with intent—and we ran ahead of it with the light dying and the seam weeping and the river dropping beneath us in ways rivers do not drop.

"There," Ilse said suddenly, pointing.

A village, or the bones of one, on a bluff above a cutbank: stilt houses, maybe eight of them, dark against the last light. Smoke from one cooking fire. As good a harbor as we'd get between here and the hill country, and better than most, because the bluff meant floodwater couldn't reach us.

I brought *Patience* in through the cut, past a mooring post carved into the shape of a bird whose beak had rotted away, and tied off below a house where an old man watched us with the patient attention of someone who had seen every kind of stranger the river could deliver.

He spoke to us in Tok Pisin. Ilse answered fluently—of course she did; the woman collected languages the way I used to collect miles—and within minutes we had permission to tie up, a roof to sleep under, and a bowl of sago that tasted like wet cardboard but was the best thing I'd ever eaten, because I was hungry enough to lie to myself.

His name was Ambrose. He was maybe seventy, built like the mooring post, and when Ilse explained we were tracking a man who'd passed through two days ago, he nodded slowly and said something that made her go very still.

"He says the man asked about the old boundaries," she translated. "The survey lines. Where the government says the land ends." She paused. "And then he says—"

Ambrose spoke again, longer this time, gesturing upriver with a chin that had spent a lifetime pointing at things. Ilse listened with her head tilted, and I watched her translation stall halfway up her throat.

"What?" I said.

"He says other men came through yesterday. Four of them, in a fast boat. They showed a photograph." Her voice had gone flat, careful. "Of you."

The fire crackled. Somewhere out on the black water, thunder cleared its throat.

"Ambrose says they offered money for information. He told them he hadn't seen you." A pause. "He says he lies well, because his father taught him, during the war, when lying well was the difference between a village and a memory."

I sat with that. Ten years of chasing thieves across empty miles, and I had never once been the one on the photograph. The reversal sat strangely, like a boot on the wrong foot.

"They're not searching the banks anymore," I said. "They're ahead of us."

"Yes." Ilse fed a stick to the fire, and the light moved across her face and showed me something I hadn't seen before—not fear exactly, but its cousin, the one that looks like anger and works harder. "Brightwater. If they know your face, they know mine. Someone described us. Which means someone we talked to sold us, or someone we haven't met yet will." She looked at me across the fire. "How much further to the cave entrance?"

"Two days. Three, if the river keeps misbehaving."

"And the storm hits tomorrow."

"Probably tonight."

She nodded slowly, and I could see her running the same arithmetic I was: a leaking boat, a falling river, hunters ahead and behind, and a window closing over all of it like water over a drowned thing.

"Ambrose," she said, turning to the old man, and asked him something in Tok Pisin that I only half followed. His answer came quick and certain, and whatever it was, it made her smile—the first real smile I'd seen from her, brief and crooked and gone too fast.

"What did he say?"

"He says there's a man in the village with a faster boat than ours, and that he owes Ambrose a favor, and that the favor is due." She looked at me, and the firelight did something complicated to her eyes. "He also says we should leave before dawn, take the eastern channel where the searchers won't think to look, and that the river will decide whether we're worth the trouble."

"The river can get in line," I said. "Everyone else already has."

But I didn't sleep much. I lay under Ambrose's roof listening to the rain arrive in earnest, hammering the thatch like a fist on a door, and I thought about photographs and lists and a seam weeping water into a boat named Patience, and somewhere past midnight I understood, with the clean clarity that only comes at the worst possible hour, that the chase had turned itself inside out somewhere back downriver.

For ten years I had been the one following. Now something was following me, and it had my face on a piece of paper, and it was willing to pay for it.

The rain kept falling. Upstream, in the dark, the river kept dropping, held back by hands we hadn't seen yet. And two days ahead, a cave waited under the hill country with a dead magistrate's secrets in its throat, costing someone, just like the old man at the coast had promised.

I just hadn't expected the cost to start collecting so soon.
