## 5. The Fixer's Offer

They took me at the fuel depot in Angoram, which I will say for them was efficient. One moment I was arguing with a man about the price of diesel and the integrity of his jerry cans—both, in my professional opinion, compromised—and the next there was a hand on my elbow, polite as a maître d', and a voice saying, *Dr. Marchetti? There's someone who'd like a word about your instruments.*

I want it noted that I considered running. I also want it noted that the man holding my elbow had the relaxed grip of someone who had caught runners before and found them boring. And the boat—the only seaworthy boat left on this stretch of river before the monsoon ate everything—was behind me, not in front of me. So I did what any sensible hydrologist does when outnumbered by men with corporate haircuts: I picked up my salinity meter, held it to my chest like a child, and went.

*Someone who'd like a word.* In my experience, the people who want a word never want one word. They want an hour, and your signature, and your silence for the rest of your natural life.

The boat they put me in was white, clean, and had an outboard worth more than my entire grant history. It smelled of new fiberglass and faintly of money, which is a smell I recognized from conference hotels: air conditioning pretending to be oxygen. We went upstream, against the current, at a speed that made the banks blur into green smears. Nobody spoke. The man with the grip sat across from me and smiled at intervals, like a screensaver.

I used the time productively. I catalogued.

Whoever these people were, they knew my name before I'd introduced myself to anyone but a customs officer and a very drunk missionary. They knew I was measuring salinity anomalies. Which meant they knew about the tide—that impossible low tide at the coast, the sea pulling back from the land like a dog that's been kicked. Which meant they knew their own rivers were misbehaving, and they knew why, and they were worried that I knew too.

Good, I thought, hugging the meter. Let's all be worried together.

* * *

The camp was up a side channel where the water ran the wrong color—not the tea-brown of the Sepik but a pale mineral gray, like something had been chewing on the riverbed upstream and spitting it back out. Barges. Dredges. A floating compound of prefabricated buildings arranged around a helipad with the tidy arrogance of a chessboard set down in the middle of someone else's game. MERIDIAN RESOURCE GROUP stenciled on the barges in letters you could read from orbit.

They took my instruments. All of them. They did it gently, with little padded cases, the way you'd handle a museum piece or a hostage's jewelry, and that gentleness frightened me more than shouting would have. People who are careful with your equipment intend to keep it a long time.

Then a corridor, a door, air conditioning that actually worked, and a man standing at a window looking at the river like it owed him rent.

"Dr. Marchetti." He turned. Mid-fifties, silver at the temples deployed with surgical precision, a shirt ironed past the point of decency. He had the kind of face that had been handsome once and had since been promoted. "Dain Kessler."

"I know," I said. "You're the reason the rivers are lying to me."

Something flickered behind his eyes—amusement, maybe, or the reflex of a man who'd learned to enjoy being disliked because it meant he'd been understood. "Would you like coffee? Real coffee. Not the apology they serve in Angoram."

"I'd like my instruments back and a straight answer about flow rates on the upper tributaries."

"Coffee first," he said pleasantly. "Negotiation is thirsty work."

He poured it himself from a French press, which told me the meeting had been staged down to the crockery. This was theater. I've sat through enough funding panels to know theater when it's served in good china. I took the cup anyway. I'm principled, not stupid.

"You've been in-country eleven days," Kessler said, settling into a chair across from me with the ease of a man reading his own file about me. "Port Moresby, then Wewak, then down the coast. You chartered a boat at the fishing village—" he paused delicately, "—the one where the tide went out. You've taken salinity readings at fourteen stations. Your dissolved-solids numbers are, frankly, excellent work. Better than anything our own contractors have produced, and we pay them embarrassingly well."

"Flattery is cheap," I said. "So are you, apparently, if your contractors can't find a diverted river."

His smile widened by a millimeter. There it was. I'd said *diverted* out loud, and he hadn't flinched, hadn't denied, hadn't reached for the word *conspiracy* the way guilty men do. He just let it sit between us like a stone on the table.

"You think we're moving water," he said.

"I think the tide at the coast dropped two meters below any recorded low in sixty years of records. I think the sediment load in three tributaries has tripled in a season. I think somebody upstream is rearranging the plumbing of an entire basin, and I think the sea noticed before anyone bothered to tell the people whose villages sit at the end of those channels." I sipped the coffee. It was, infuriatingly, excellent. "So yes. I think you're moving water."

Kessler looked at me for a long moment, and then he laughed—a real laugh, short and genuine, which was somehow worse than the smile.

"They said you were direct. The people at the institute in Brisbane. They said you were asked to leave after the Murray-Darling report." He said it kindly. That was the trick of him; I could see that already. Every knife came sheathed in sympathy. "You published findings that embarrassed a state government, and instead of defending you, they restructured your department until your job didn't exist anymore. Am I close?"

"You've read my file."

"I read everyone's file. It's a hobby. Cheaper than golf." He leaned forward. "Here's what I admire about you, Dr. Marchetti. You got it right, and you got punished for it, and you kept going anyway. You came eight thousand kilometers to a basin most hydrologists couldn't find on a map, on savings and stubbornness, because the data mattered more to you than comfort. That's rare. That's *valuable*. And it is being wasted."

"On what, exactly?"

"On being a tourist in a war zone." He spread his hands toward the window, toward the gray water and the barges and the green immensity beyond. "Do you know what Meridian is building here? Not a mine—a *system*. Water management infrastructure for the entire basin. Flood control. Dry-season storage. Hydrology that works *for* people instead of drowning them every monsoon and abandoning them every drought. Somebody has to do it. The government won't. The NGOs come, take photographs, and leave. We're the only ones staying."

"And the villages downstream of your diversions?"

"Will have better water than they've ever had." He said it smoothly, and I realized—he believed it. That was the terrible thing about Dain Kessler. He wasn't lying. He had simply performed the arithmetic where the rounding errors were made of people, and he'd slept fine afterward. "There are costs in any transition. Managed carefully, minimized, compensated—yes. But the alternative is the status quo, which is children dying of waterborne disease and whole communities losing their houses every wet season. You've seen the flood lines on the stilts. Don't tell me you haven't."

I had seen them. That's the trap of talking to a man like Kessler: he builds his argument out of true things.

"So here's my offer," he said, and now the warmth in his voice went up half a step, the register of rescue rather than negotiation. "Come work for us. Full funding—for life, if you want it. Your own lab. Satellite telemetry on every gauge in the basin, which is more data than you could gather in twenty years with a meter and a canoe. A salary that would make your former department weep. And the chance to do the thing you actually care about: honest measurement, at scale, that shapes policy instead of getting you blacklisted for it." He paused, precisely. "All I ask is that you stop asking questions about flow rates."

I nearly choked on the coffee. "That's—you understand that's the entire job. Flow rates are the job. That's like hiring a doctor and asking her to stop looking at patients."

"It's like hiring a doctor to run a hospital," Kessler said mildly, "and asking her to trust the diagnostics her own institution provides, rather than conducting unauthorized examinations that upset the staff." He set down his cup. "Dr. Marchetti. I'm going to be candid with you, because I think you're someone who respects candor even when it's inconvenient. There are things happening in this basin that are larger than you, larger than me, and considerably older than either of us. If you poke at them alone, in a leaking boat, ahead of a monsoon, you will become a statistic. A drowned researcher, regrettable, footnote. Or—" the smile again, warm as lamplight, "—you can be inside the room where the decisions get made, with resources, with protection, with influence. Those are the options. I genuinely prefer the second one. I'd hate to see the first."

And then, casually, the way a man mentions the weather:

"You're traveling with Brightwater. Rafael Brightwater."

The room got very quiet, or I did. I kept my face still—I've had practice; I once presented a failing dam assessment to a room full of engineers who owned the dam—but something in my chest pulled tight, like a mooring line coming taut.

"What about him?"

"He was a ranger. Ten years, desert patrol, out past the end of the paved world. Good record. Commendations." Kessler examined his nails. "Also a brother. Daniel Brightwater. Died six years ago in a water dispute up in the highlands—a dispute over allocation rights that was, shall we say, adjudicated in favor of parties with better lawyers. Rafael spent two years trying to reopen the case. He got nowhere. The file, I'm told, is sealed." He looked up at me, and his eyes were kind, and that kindness was the coldest thing in the room. "A man like that carries a great deal of anger, Dr. Marchetti. Anger needs somewhere to go. I'd hate for it to go somewhere that gets him hurt. Or gets *you* hurt, standing next to him. Because make no mistake—if you stay on this river with him, that's exactly where you'll be standing."

I set down my cup. My hands were steady. I was almost proud of them.

"You've done your homework," I said.

"It's a hobby," he repeated, and smiled.

* * *

They gave me a cabin for the night. Of course they did. A bed, a working light, a bottle of water with the seal unbroken. Temptation dressed as hospitality, hospitality dressed as rescue. Through the porthole I could see the gray channel and, far off, the dark line of storm building over the basin like a bruise coming up through skin.

I sat on the bunk and thought about it honestly, because I promised myself years ago I'd always look temptation straight in the face before refusing it—it's the only way to be sure you're refusing it for the right reasons.

Full funding. For life. A lab. Legitimacy restored, the Murray-Darling humiliation retroactively erased, my name attached to the largest water-management project in the southern hemisphere. Every night for six years I had woken up at 3 a.m. in rented rooms doing the arithmetic of exile—how many months of savings, how many more seasons of borrowed boats—and here was a man offering to make the arithmetic stop.

And all it cost was closing my eyes. Stop asking questions about flow rates. Trust the institution's numbers. Be a good doctor who never examines patients.

I thought about the flood lines on the village stilts, yes—Kessler was right, I'd seen them, black bands of old water stacked like tree rings. But I'd also seen, in the last two days, the new marks. Marks above the old ones. Fresh scars on wood that had stood fifty years without ever recording water that high. Whatever Meridian was managing, it was managing the basin toward something, and the something was arriving downstream at the speed of a rising river, and the people at the bottom of the gradient were going to meet it first.

And I thought about Rafael Brightwater, whom I'd known for four days and mostly argued with, a man who tracked thieves across empty miles because measuring miles was easier than measuring grief. Kessler knew about his brother. Kessler knew about the sealed file. Which meant Kessler—or whatever he served—had been within arm's reach of that rigged water dispute, or something shaped exactly like it, and had watched a ranger spend two years beating his fists on a locked door and had filed the man under *manageable*.

Manageable. Like flow rates. Like villages. Like me.

I lay back on the bunk and listened to the rain start—fat, preliminary drops on the roof, the monsoon clearing its throat—and I planned.

In the morning I would tell Dain Kessler that I needed time to consider. That I was a scientist, and scientists weigh evidence. I would let him believe the offer was working its way through me like a slow poison, because a man who believes he's winning stops watching the door. I would ask for my instruments back—"so I can finish my station logs before deciding"—because a lie works best when it's built out of true things, and I truly did want my instruments back.

And then I was going to steal a boat, find a certain ranger, and tell him that the fixer knew his brother's name.

Somewhere downriver, thunder rolled along the basin like a drum being carried closer.

I closed my eyes and started counting the hours until dawn.
