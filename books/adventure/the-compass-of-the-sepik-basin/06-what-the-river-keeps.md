## 6. What the River Keeps

The stilt village announced itself the way secrets do—quietly, and only when you were already too close to pretend you hadn't seen it.

We'd been running the boat on fumes and prayer since midday, Ilse up front with her instruments balanced on a plank across two crates like a surgeon operating in a canoe, me at the tiller pretending I wasn't watching the fuel gauge the way a man watches a wound. The Sepik had widened into something less like a river and more like a slow brown argument with the land, and the banks had gone soft and green and endless. Then the smoke appeared—cooking fires, thin and domestic—and around a bend of sago palms there it was: thirty houses on stilts above the water, connected by walkways that sagged like tired shoulders, dogs barking from porches, kids hanging off railings to stare at us with the frank appraisal of children who have seen every kind of stranger arrive by river and have opinions about most of them.

"We should keep moving," I said, because I'm an idiot with good instincts.

"With what?" Ilse didn't look up from her salinity meter. "We've got maybe ten liters of fuel and a hull that's drinking more than we are. Unless you plan to paddle four hundred kilometers against the current, we stop here."

She was right, which she'd been discovering was a habit, and which I was discovering I hated. I nosed us toward the village landing—a platform of blackened poles where three men sat mending nets—and cut the engine. The silence afterward was enormous. Water slapping wood. A rooster somewhere, confused about the hour or just committed to chaos.

One of the net-menders looked up. He was old in the way river people get old early, weather worked into his skin like grain into timber, and he looked at me for a long moment before he looked at the boat.

"Leaking," he said. Not a question.

"It's got opinions," I admitted.

He almost smiled. "Everything on this river has opinions. Tie it there. If it sinks overnight, that's its business."

* * *

They fed us before they asked us anything, which is a courtesy I've found mostly dead everywhere else in the world. Fish smoked over coconut husk, sago in a gray slab that tasted like nothing and filled you like a promise, sweet potato boiled in a pot older than the village probably. We ate cross-legged on the floor of the headman's house while a dozen people watched us eat with the patience of people who understand that hunger comes before conversation.

Ilse talked first, of course. She'd been talking since Wewak—about flow rates, about the gauge readings that made no sense, about how the river was running low in places it shouldn't be low this close to monsoon, like something upstream was holding water back behind cupped hands. She asked questions the way other people fire rifles, rapid and aimed, and the villagers answered some and deflected others, and I sat back and did what I do best, which is watch.

What I watched was this: every time she mentioned Meridian—the consortium, the survey teams, the men in unmarked boats we'd dodged two days ago—the room changed temperature. Not fear exactly. Something older and wearier than fear. The look of people who have heard a promise before and know how it ends.

It was the old woman who broke the pattern.

She came in late, small and bent, carried on a grandson's arm to a place of honor near the wall, and when she spoke the room went quiet in a way that told me everything before she said a word. Her name, I learned later, was Yawan. Her voice was dry as seed husks.

"You are looking for the paper-thief," she said. To me, not Ilse. "The one who took the survey tube."

I set down my food. "You know about Voss."

"I know about everyone who passes through the water." She folded her hands. "I know he went upstream with his tube. I know the men in the fast boats went after him. And I know you—" she pointed one crooked finger at me "—are not one of them, because those men do not eat sitting down. Those men stand. They stand like they are afraid the ground will make claims on them."

There was laughter in the room, brief and careful, and I felt myself relax half a degree, which for me is practically a hug.

"Why does it matter to you?" I asked. "Voss, the tube, any of it?"

Yawan looked at me for a long time. Then she said, "Because your face is asking the wrong question, ranger. You are asking *where*. You should be asking *what*." She turned to the headman. "Show them."

* * *

What they showed us was a map that wasn't a map.

It hung on the back wall of the house, under a sheet of plastic gone cloudy with age and mold: a piece of plywood, maybe a meter square, painted in faded ochres and whites. Rivers in white. Boundaries in ochre—but not straight lines. Curves and loops and clusters of dots, organic as coral, following things no surveyor would ever follow: a grove where a certain palm grew, a bend where the floodwater always pooled first, a stone that had been moved three generations ago and whose absence was itself a marker.

"My grandmother painted it," the headman said. "Her mother taught her the boundaries. Her mother learned them from hers. Nobody wrote them down because nobody needed to. The river knew them. We knew them. That was enough."

"And then?" Ilse asked softly.

"And then the Germans came, and then the Australians, and then the paper." He said *paper* the way you'd say a disease. "A magistrate named Crane drew lines on his maps. Straight ones. Lines that gave our land to plantations, to missions, to companies. Lines that said our families were living on property belonging to men in Melbourne who had never seen this river except through gin glasses." He tapped the plywood. "When we protested, they showed us documents. When we showed them our memory, they said memory is not law."

Yawan spoke again from her corner. "My family's ground is on that chart. Four hundred hectares along the Yuat tributary. My father fished it, his father fished it, back past counting. In 1962 a deed was produced saying a man named Hargrave owned it. Hargrave. I met his son once, in town. He wore shoes even on boats." Her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "Shoes. On boats. And the court said his paper was true and my father's lifetime was not."

The room was silent. Somewhere below us, the river kept moving, indifferent and patient, the way rivers are.

"Now the new men come," Yawan continued. "Meridian. They bring contracts instead of deeds, but it is the same paper wearing different clothes. They say: sign, take the money, move to the settlement. They say the land will be mined and the water diverted and it cannot be helped, progress is a flood and you cannot argue with a flood." Her eyes found mine. "But now there is a flood coming that is not a story. And their machines are pulling the water out of the rivers upstream—I have lived eighty years; I know when a river is being stolen. So tell me, ranger. You chase the thief with the tube. What is in the tube that makes rich men send guns?"

I thought about lying. It would have been easy—they'd have accepted it, filed me away as another outsider chasing his own small purposes. But Ilse was watching me, and so was Yawan, and there's a kind of honesty that gets pulled out of you by old women whether you consent to it or not.

"A key," I said. "The tube holds survey papers from the colonial administration. Codes, reference numbers, the system Crane used to write his records. There's a rumor—just a rumor—that Crane kept a private ledger. Every bribe he took, every deed he forged, every verdict he sold. And the ledger can't be read without the cipher in that tube."

The headman translated for the older ones in a language I didn't catch, and I watched the words move through the room like current through water, touching everyone.

"If it's real," Ilse said quietly, beside me, "it proves the deeds were forged. All of them. It would undo sixty years of land titles in this basin."

"It would also prove," I added, because someone had to, "that the company Meridian grew out of was founded on forged land. Which means Kessler isn't just buying water rights. He's buying silence."

* * *

Later, when the lamps had burned low and most of the village slept, I couldn't. I climbed down the ladder to the walkway and stood over the black water, listening to the frogs run their endless audit of the night, and I thought about files.

Here's the thing about erased records: they have a shape. People think destruction leaves nothing, but that's wrong. Destruction leaves a hole with edges, and the edges tell you everything. I spent six years of my ranger career, early on, trying to get access to a file. My brother Tomás's file—the dispute file, the water rights case in the Atacama-adjacent concessions where he'd worked as a hydrology tech, the case that ended with him drowned in a canal that official records said held no water that day. I requested the file eleven times. Eleven times I got either nothing or a version so redacted it read like a ransom note. And the shape of those redactions—the same three names blacked out in every copy, the same missing forty-eight hours, the same witness statements that existed as summaries but never as originals—that shape told me more than any document would have. It told me somebody had been thorough. Thoroughness costs money. Money means motive.

I never found the original. I found a transfer instead—to Papua New Guinea, a lateral posting nobody wanted, which I took because the alternative was becoming a man who camped outside government offices until he died of it. Ten years of miles since. Petty thieves, cattle rustlers, fuel smugglers. Small crimes, clean crimes, crimes where the paper matched the wound. I told myself I'd chosen the work. What I'd actually done was choose crimes small enough that the paperwork couldn't lie to me.

Until Corben Voss stole a tube of survey papers, and the shape of the hole it left looked exactly like the shape of the hole where my brother's truth used to be.

"You're very far away," Ilse said.

She'd come down the ladder without a sound, which should have annoyed me and instead felt like relief. She leaned on the railing beside me, arms crossed against the river damp, and for a while neither of us said anything. Below us, a fish jumped—some big lazy thing—and the rings spread out and out until the dark ate them.

"The old woman," she said finally. "Yawan. Eighty years old, reciting boundary markers from memory because some clerk decided paper beat people. Did you see her hands when she talked about the deed? She wasn't angry. That's what got me. She was *precise*. Like she'd rehearsed it for decades, waiting for someone to ask."

"She has been rehearsing it," I said. "People like her always are. The question is whether anyone ever shows up to listen."

"You listened."

"I recognized the shape," I said, and left it there, and she was smart enough not to push. Instead she pulled her notebook from inside her jacket, flipped it open to a page dense with her cramped handwriting, and showed me a sketch: a graph of flow rates from five gauging stations, plotted over the last month.

"Look at the tributaries feeding the middle basin," she said. "Every one trending down, seven to twelve percent, starting eight weeks ago. Natural variation doesn't synchronize like that. Somebody's impounding water upstream—small diversions, dozens of them, each one legal-looking on its own. And here's the part that keeps me up at night." She tapped the bottom of the graph. "If they hold that water back until peak monsoon, then release it all at once downstream—"

"The flood hits harder than anything in living memory," I finished.

"Floods erase things," Ilse said. "Boundary stones. Fences. Old gardens. Everything physical that says *this land is ours, this line is ours.* You don't need lawyers if the river moves the border for you. And afterward, when the villages are displaced and scattered, Meridian walks in with relief money and purchase contracts and nobody's in a position to argue about where the old lines ran." She closed the notebook. "Except people like Yawan. Who carry the lines in their heads."

I looked at her—at this woman I'd known nine days and argued with for eight of them—and realized I trusted her completely, and that this fact frightened me considerably more than Kessler's gunboats.

"Tomorrow," I said, "we go upstream faster."

* * *

I didn't sleep much. Around what I guessed was three in the morning, I heard movement on the walkway and found Yawan sitting outside her house with a lamp, wrapped in blankets, awake the way very old people are awake—on their own schedule, answerable to no one.

She patted the boards beside her. I sat.

"You knew my brother," she said, after a while. "Not yours. Mine. Anani. He went to Port Moresby in 1971 to fight the Hargrave deed in court. He was clever—spoke English, wore a tie, could read a survey map better than the surveyors. Everyone said he would win." She watched the river. "He never came back. The letters stopped. Years later a man from town brought word that he had died of fever. But there was no grave anyone had seen, and no belongings, and the court record of his case—" she made a small gesture, opening and closing an empty hand "—was missing. My brother became a hole in the paper. Do you understand? They did not kill him, perhaps. Perhaps it was truly fever. But they let him become nothing, and nothing is easier to dispossess than a man."

"I understand," I said, and my voice came out rougher than I intended.

"You are hunting the tube for yourself," Yawan said. It wasn't an accusation. It was diagnosis. "For some hole in your own paper. That is why your eyes look like that." She turned and studied me, and her eyes in the lamplight were pale and sharp as a fish knife. "I will tell you what my grandmother told me. The river keeps everything. Every boundary, every name, every wrong. Paper burns. Memory drowns. But the river keeps. Your job, ranger, is not to find the paper. Your job is to decide what the river keeps *through you*."

Downstream, far off toward the coast, thunder muttered—too early in the season, too heavy, the storm wall building itself out over the Bismarck Sea like something being assembled by hand.

I sat with an old woman above a stolen river and understood, finally and completely, that I was never going to hand that ledger to a federal office. Whatever protocol said, whatever my commission demanded, whatever neat chain-of-custody forms existed for exactly this situation—I had seen the shape of custody before. Custody was where holes in paper were made.

The thunder rolled again, closer.

And from upriver, faint but unmistakable, came the sound I'd been dreading for two days: engines. More than one. Moving fast, against the current, toward us.
