## 7. Leaking Toward the Source

*Rafael*

The boat told us it was dying about an hour before it actually died, the way old men and outboard engines do—complaining in a language only the patient bother to translate.

I'd been listening to that language for two days. A knock in the hull every time we came off a wave, soft at first, then insistent, then frankly rude. Ilse heard it too but had decided, in her way, to treat it as data.

"That's not a knock," she said, crouched in the bilge with her sleeve rolled up and her hand flat against the planking like she was taking its pulse. "That's a seam letting go. There's a difference."

"There is?"

"A knock is structural. This is negotiable." She looked up at me through the spray, hair plastered to her forehead, and I thought—not for the first time—that she was the kind of woman who would negotiate with a sinking boat and probably win, right up until she didn't. "We should put in somewhere and re-caulk before the rapids."

"The rapids are before anywhere worth putting in."

"Then we have a scheduling problem."

"We do."

The Sepik's tributaries don't so much flow as argue. The one we were on had spent the morning arguing with itself, braiding apart around sandbars and stitching back together, and the water had gone from tea-brown to something thicker, more opinionated. Behind us, stacked on the southern horizon like furniture under a sheet, the monsoon wall sat where it had sat for three days, getting no closer and no farther away, which somehow made it worse. A storm that chases you is honest. A storm that waits is thinking.

Voss's trail had gone cold at Wagu Bend—or warm, depending on how you counted, because what I'd found there wasn't Voss at all. It was Kessler's men. Three boats, unmarked, working the banks with the methodical patience of people being paid by the hour. They'd found his campsite before I did. They'd found my boot print next to it. After that, the river had stopped being a place where I was hunting and become a place where I was known.

So we'd gone upstream instead of sensible, because the chart fragment—the scrap of Crane's survey paper I'd pulled from Voss's abandoned camp on the coast—showed a second entrance to the cave. An inland one. Marked in the magistrate's tight colonial hand with a notation I'd been turning over for days: *approach at low water; the mountain drinks.*

Ilse had her own reasons for going up. Her salinity numbers kept getting worse. Every gauge she checked said the basin's rivers were running light—diverted, siphoned, stolen—and every kilometer upstream brought her closer to whoever was doing the stealing. She hadn't said Meridian out loud yet. She didn't have to. We'd both done the arithmetic.

Now the boat was negotiating the terms of our continued existence, and losing.

* * *

*Ilse*

Here is what nobody tells you about rivers: they are not scenery. They are infrastructure, and when someone tampers with them, they file a complaint. My whole career has been reading complaints.

The gauges were screaming by midday. Discharge down forty percent against seasonal norm. Stage height wrong. The flood pulse—the great breathing rhythm that defines an entire ecosystem—was arrhythmic, like a heart with a hand squeezing it. Somewhere above us, somebody had put their thumb on the basin, and everything downstream was compensating, badly, the way a body compensates for a blocked artery until the day it doesn't.

I told Brightwater some of this. He listened the way he does everything—completely, silently, as if your words were tracks he intended to follow later. It's unnerving. Most men listen the way they drive: waiting for their turn.

What I didn't tell him was the other thing the numbers said. The thing I'd been sitting on since the coast, because saying it would make it real: the diversion pattern wasn't sloppy. It wasn't a mine dewatering operation cutting corners. It was engineered. Timed. The flows were being held back now, ahead of the monsoon, which meant they could be released later—which meant someone intended to use the river itself as an instrument. I didn't yet know for what. I knew enough to be afraid of the shape of the question.

Then the boat made its position clear.

It happened between one argument and the next. The channel narrowed, the current stood up on its hind legs, and the hull hit something just under the surface—a log, a bar, the river's opinion of us—and the knock became a crack, and the crack became a fact. Water came in over the transom in a way that ended all discussion.

Brightwater didn't panic. I want to record that, because I've watched engineers with doctorates panic worse. He simply said, "Grab the dry bag," in the tone of a man asking you to pass the salt, and then he started bailing with a fuel can cut in half while steering with his knees toward the inside of the bend, because of course he knew that the inside of the bend is where the debris collects and the eddy gives you a fighting chance. Ten years of tracking things across empty country teaches a man where the world keeps its mercy. Apparently some of it transfers to water.

We hit the eddy. We hit the bank. We hit it hard enough that I bit my tongue and tasted copper and didn't care, because we were aground on a shelf of black mud beneath a wall of rainforest, and the boat was half-swamped beside us, and we were alive.

Mostly.

* * *

*Rafael*

We lost most of it. That's the short version. The long version is that we lost most of it in about ninety seconds, standing chest-deep in a flooded timber stand, hauling crates out of a dying boat while the river took inventory.

Gone: the spare fuel, most of the food, the tarp, both sleeping bags, my backup radio. Kept: the dry bag with the chart fragment, Ilse's instruments—she went back twice for those gauges, and I let her, because I understood. You don't leave the evidence. The evidence is the mission. Gone also, briefly, was my hat, which the river accepted as tribute, and then returned, snagged on a root ten meters downstream, which I chose to read as an omen and Ilse chose to read as hydrodynamics.

By late afternoon we had a fire going on the mud shelf—a smoky, miserable fire, because everything was wet including the concept of fire—and supplies laid out in a row that told the truth about our situation: two cans of meat, a bag of rice gone damp at one corner, a block of salt, Ilse's instruments, the chart fragment, one pistol, one machete, one hat.

"Well," Ilse said, surveying the row. "We're rich in salt."

"We're alive in salt."

"Don't make it poetic. I'm too tired to appreciate poetry."

She was wringing out her shirt with the mechanical efficiency of someone who had decided exhaustion was a scheduling problem, same as the boat. There was a scrape along her forearm from the timber, already going angry at the edges. Humid country eats small wounds. I had antiseptic in the dry bag—one luxury I'd guarded without knowing why—and I nodded at her arm and she held it out without ceremony, without flinching, watching the treeline while I worked, because she never entirely stopped watching the treeline anymore. Neither did I.

"You're quiet," she said.

"I'm always quiet."

"You're quieter than usual, which for you is a kind of noise." She flexed her fingers as I taped the dressing down. "You've been staring at that chart fragment since we landed. You haven't looked at the river once. For a tracker, that's a confession."

I didn't answer. The fragment lay on a flat stone where I'd weighted its corners, and Crane's notation sat in the firelight: *the mountain drinks.* Two kilometers inland, if the old surveyor's distances could be trusted, and colonial surveyors' distances could rarely be trusted, which was rather the point of everything we were chasing.

"So," Ilse said. "Do we go in, or do we turn back?"

"Turn back to what?" I gestured downstream, in the general direction of Kessler's boats and the rising water and the villages whose names I was starting to learn. "Back is a longer walk than forward."

"That's not an answer. That's geometry."

"It's the best kind of answer. Geometry doesn't lie."

"Geometry also doesn't explain why you want to go into a cave that opens once a generation and costs someone." She turned to face me fully, firelight on one side of her face, dark on the other, and I understood I was no longer in a conversation; I was in an interrogation, and the interrogator had instruments. "The elder warned you. You told me yourself. 'It always costs someone.' And here you are, soaked and starving, wanting to walk into it. So either you believe the warning and don't care, or you don't believe it, or—" she paused, and I watched her decide to spend the ammunition she'd been carrying since the coast, "—or you're not going in for the ledger at all. You're going in because it's the farthest point from wherever you're hiding from."

The fire popped. Somewhere across the river, a bird made a sound like a hinge that needed oiling.

"And what exactly," I said, "am I hiding from?"

"You tell me. I've shared a boat with you for six days. I know your brother died over water—I know it from the way you go silent at weirs, of all things, weirs, Rafael, who goes silent at weirs? I know you measure your life in miles logged because miles don't ask questions. I know you avoided the coast for a decade and followed a thief straight into it anyway, which means either fate has a sense of humor or you did." She leaned forward. "I'm a scientist. I don't need the mechanism. I just need you to admit there's a phenomenon."

I looked at the fire for a while. There are maybe four people alive who know the full story of my brother Tomás, and one of them is me, and I had planned to keep it that way indefinitely, right up until this woman with salt-cracked hands and impossible standards sat down across from me and refused to accept geometry as an answer.

"He drowned," I said finally. "Not literally. That's the joke of it. He drowned administratively." I turned the hat in my hands. "Tomás worked water rights in the south. Irrigation districts. He found out a land company was falsifying flow records to strip wells from smallholders—same shape as what your gauges are telling you, different decade. He filed reports. Correct channels. All of them." I smiled, and it wasn't a smile. "His own agency buried the reports. Then it buried him—transferred him, demoted him, ground him down until he quit, and then he drank himself into the Ord River one night, and the coroner wrote 'accident,' and the file closed, and I got a letter of condolence signed by the same director who'd killed the investigation. So I joined the service he'd served. I told myself I'd work it from inside. Ten years later I'd logged enough miles to reach the moon and I'd never once opened his case file. Because opening it would mean admitting the inside doesn't work. Miles are easier. Miles don't ask whose law you're enforcing."

The silence after that had weight. Ilse didn't offer comfort, which I was grateful for; comfort would have broken it.

"My career disgrace," she said at last, quietly, "involved refusing to falsify exactly the kind of records your brother filed truthfully. Different continent. Same disease." She reached over and tapped the chart fragment once, lightly, like knocking on a door. "So. The cave."

"The cave."

"We go in tomorrow at first light. Before the storm decides for us." She lay back on the tarp we'd salvaged—it had survived, folded small in the dry bag, the universe's single concession—and stared at the canopy. "And Rafael? When we find whatever Crane left in there, you're going to open your brother's file. Consider it peer review."

I lay back too. Above us the rainforest went on with its enormous indifferent conversation, and behind it, far to the south, the storm waited with its furniture under its sheet, patient as a creditor.

Sleep didn't come easy. Around midnight I woke to find Ilse already awake, sitting up, listening. Not to the forest.

To the water.

The river had dropped. I felt it before I measured it—wrongness in the air, the mud shelf exhaling. Ilse clicked on her headlamp and shone it at the bank, where a fresh line of wet mud gleamed a full handspan above the current.

"It's falling," she whispered. "Fast. At night. Rivers don't do this naturally." She looked at me, and her face in the lamplight was the face of someone watching a diagnosis confirm itself. "They're holding it back. Whatever's upstream—they're closing the gates. Which means when they open them—"

Behind us, two kilometers inland through the black trees, the mountain waited with its mouth full of paper and bones, and somewhere beyond it, hands we couldn't see were setting the river up as a weapon.

First light was five hours away. It suddenly seemed very far.
