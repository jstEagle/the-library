## 9. Voss, Cornered

I have been robbed exactly four times in my life, and every time it was by someone I'd already decided to trust. You'd think a man in my profession would learn. But there's a particular blindness that comes with the job: when you spend your days deceiving people, you start believing that everyone else is as honest about their dishonesty as you are. Kessler wasn't. Kessler smiled the way banks smile.

So when I say I saw it coming, understand that seeing a thing coming and being able to do anything about it are two different countries, and I was standing in the wrong one.

* * *

They left me at the edge of a sago swamp, which I want noted for the record was not even an efficient place to dump a body. If you're going to murder a man, at least choose somewhere his remains won't be discovered by a family of foraging pigs who will then become emotionally attached to him. The swamp was ankle-deep in black water that smelled like old coins and regret, and the mosquitoes had organized themselves into something between a choir and a militia.

My ribs were broken—I counted three, maybe four, on the principle that precision matters less than volume—and my left eye had swollen shut sometime during the boat ride, so the world had gone down to one channel, like a river in drought. My hands were zip-tied behind me until about an hour ago, when I managed to work them against a piece of razor grass that cut me worse than it cut the plastic. That's the swamp for you. Everything cuts both ways.

Kessler's men had taken the tube, of course. The survey papers. Ten weeks of planning, three false identities, one very expensive bribe to a shipping clerk in Port Moresby, all folded up and carried away in a waterproof cylinder by a man named Okafor who had shaken my hand at the handover like we were closing on a house.

"You did good work," he'd said. And then, almost kindly, which was the worst part: "Mr. Kessler says the arrangement is complete."

Arrangement. Complete. I lay in the mud and turned those words over like stones looking for what lived underneath.

Here's what I hadn't told anyone—not Kessler, not Okafor, not the bartender in Madang who thought he knew everything: the tube was only half the job. The survey papers were the key. Crane's cipher, the old magistrate's private shorthand, built off colonial survey coordinates because Aldous Crane trusted nothing else in this world except the mathematics of land. Without the key, the ledger in the cave was just two hundred pages of a dead man's beautiful, unreadable handwriting. With it, it was a confession signed by an empire.

And Kessler thought he'd bought a key.

I laughed, out there in the swamp, alone, with broken ribs, and it hurt so much I cried a little, and I couldn't tell you where the laughing stopped and the crying started, or if they were ever separate things at all.

* * *

Let me tell you about my grandmother, since apparently I'm dying and the dying get to be sentimental.

Her name was Yolanda Voss—born Yolanda Wagu, of the middle river, before the name meant anything to anyone but the people who fished that bend. She was dispossessed at nineteen by a deed she never signed, witnessed by a man she'd never met, filed in an office in a city she never saw. She used to tell the story the way women like her tell everything: quietly, while working, so you could pretend she was talking about something else. How the surveyor came with his instruments and his polite questions and measured their garden into someone else's property. How her father went to the magistrate to object and came back older. How the paper said what the paper said, and the paper was the law, and the law was the paper, and round and round until you understood that the circle was the point.

She kept one thing from the old life: a shell, a cowrie, worn smooth, that her mother had pressed into her hand the morning they walked away from the water. She gave it to me when I was nine, the year before she died, and said, "You'll know what to do with it."

I didn't. For thirty years I didn't. I became a thief instead, which is what happens to a lot of grandsons of stolen things—we grow up thinking the whole world is just property waiting to change hands, and if it's going to change hands anyway, it might as well be ours doing the changing.

Then, four years ago, in a dealer's back room in Darwin, I found a fragment of chart referencing the Magistrate's Ledger, and I read the name Crane, and I remembered my grandmother saying *the magistrate*, spitting it like a seed, and I sat down on the floor of that back room and understood, finally, what the cowrie was for.

It wasn't for selling. It was for returning.

* * *

The problem with wanting to return something is that returning requires a destination, and destinations require trust, and I had spent thirty years making myself untrustworthy on purpose. You can't walk into a village on the Sepik carrying stolen colonial documents and a résumé like mine and expect a warm reception. So I did what I knew how to do: I sold the job. I let Kessler believe I was a mercenary who'd found the ledger for a consortium that wanted proof the old deeds were forged—which, technically, they are, and technically the ledger proves it, and this is the trap of truth, that it serves whoever holds it regardless of what it means.

My plan was simple and stupid, which are the only plans that survive contact with reality: deliver the tube, let Kessler think he'd won, then steal it back and take it upriver myself, to the councils, to the elders, to whoever still remembered where the boundaries actually ran. Give them the key. Let them decide what door to open.

Simple. Stupid. It did not survive.

Because Kessler isn't stupid either, and men like him can smell a double-cross the way sharks smell blood—not because they're clever, but because they assume everyone bleeds the same way they do. He had me followed from the handover. They took the tube at the landing, beat me for information I genuinely didn't have—the location of the cave's inland entrance, which I'd memorized and then burned—and dumped me in the swamp with a warning delivered secondhand through Okafor, who stood at the edge of the mud in his clean boots and said:

"Mr. Kessler wants you to know there's no hard feelings. This is just business concluding."

Business concluding. I've been shot at twice, knifed once, and betrayed by everyone from a nun to my own brother-in-law, and I swear that sentence hurt more than any of it. Not the beating—the phrasing. The absolute certainty that I was a line item. That my grandmother's cowrie, my ten weeks of lies, my whole crooked pilgrimage toward one honest act could be summarized as *concluding*.

I lay in the mud after Okafor left and held the cowrie in my bound hands—it was on a cord around my neck; they'd searched me for weapons and documents and never once imagined the most valuable thing I carried was a shell worth nothing—and I made a decision that frightened me more than the swamp did.

If I got out of here, I was going to have to trust somebody.

* * *

The somebody arrived two days later, which in mosquito-time is roughly a geological era.

I heard the boat before I saw it—a leaking, laboring thing, engine coughing like a smoker climbing stairs—and I used the last of my functioning strength to drag myself up onto a root buttress where I might be visible, on the theory that dying unseen in a swamp is the single most undignified exit available to a professional, and I have standards. Low ones, at this point, but standards.

Rafael Brightwater stepped out of the treeline like a man stepping into a room he'd been avoiding for years. I knew him by reputation before I ever saw his face: the desert ranger who logged miles the way other men log grudges. There were stories about him in the ports—that he'd tracked a cattle rustler across four hundred kilometers of nothing on a hunch and a heel print; that he'd once turned down a promotion because it would have taken him off the trail. A man who measures. I'd spent my whole career avoiding men who measure.

He had a woman with him—tall, sunburned, carrying instruments like other people carry grievances. The hydrologist. I'd heard about her too, in fragments, from Kessler's men who complained about her the way you complain about weather.

Brightwater crouched over me, and I watched him take inventory the way I'd watch another thief work a mark: the ribs, the eye, the wrists, the dehydration. Professional assessment. Then something shifted in his face—not pity, exactly. Recognition. Like he was looking at a photograph of himself from some year he didn't talk about.

"Voss," he said.

"Brightwater." My voice came out like gravel being apologized to. "You're a long way from your desert."

"So are you." He cut my wrists free with a knife that moved like it had done this ten thousand times. "Whoever did this—how many?"

"Four in the boat. More upstream. They work for Meridian. Kessler." I coughed, and coughing with broken ribs is nature's way of teaching you humility. "They have the tube. The cipher key. And they know the ledger exists."

The hydrologist swore in what sounded like German crossed with pure exhaustion.

"They don't know how to use it," I managed. "Not yet. Crane's cipher needs the original survey coordinates, cross-referenced by hand. It takes days. Maybe a week. But they'll figure it out, because Kessler employs patient people, and patient people with money always figure it out eventually."

Brightwater looked at me for a long moment. One-eyed, I looked back. Somewhere in the swamp a bird made a sound like a hinge that needed oiling.

"Why?" he said finally. "Why steal it? Why any of it? You're a thief. Thieves sell things."

And there it was. The question I'd been carrying for four years, asked by the one man whose answer I couldn't lie to, partly because I was too broken to construct a decent lie and partly because lying to Brightwater felt like cheating at solitaire.

"My grandmother," I said, "was born on the middle river. Wagu bend. Her family's ground was erased by a deed with a forged signature, filed under a magistrate named Crane, and she died in a boarding house in Lae with a shell in her fist and no country to be buried in." I worked the cord over my head, clumsy with swollen fingers, and held the cowrie out to him. "I stole the key to give the ledger back. Not to sell. Not to trade. To return. There's a difference. It took me forty years to learn there's a difference, and I learned it too late to matter, because I handed the thing to the wrong man and now it's going to buy water rights instead of justice unless—"

I stopped. Because begging is a skill like any other, and I know exactly how it sounds, and I had sworn I wouldn't do it.

But then I did it anyway.

"Finish it," I said. "Please. I'm not asking you to forgive me. I'm not asking you to trust me—I wouldn't. I'm asking you to be the man who finishes things, because I've met a hundred men who start them and I've never once met one who finishes, and my grandmother waited her whole life for somebody to finish." The cowrie sat in my palm between us, smooth and stupid and worth everything. "Take it to the councils. Not the courts. The courts are where paper goes to die. The councils. The elders. The people who still remember where the lines really run."

Brightwater didn't take the shell. He looked at it the way you look at a live grenade—respectfully, and with a dawning understanding that it had been in the room the whole time.

"The file," he said quietly, mostly to himself. "Your grandmother's dispossession. What year?"

"Nineteen fifty-one. March."

Something closed behind his eyes—not hardness, but a door shutting on a room he kept locked. I'd seen that look before, in mirrors. Everyone chasing ghosts across empty country is chasing one specific ghost.

"I had a brother," he said, which was all he said, and somehow it was enough, because men like us communicate in fragments the way the swamp communicates in smells.

The hydrologist—Marchetti—crouched down beside me with a canteen and none of Brightwater's caution. "Can you walk?"

"I can walk," I said, "in the sense that a collapsed building can be described as architecture."

"She means can you travel," Brightwater said.

"I mean," Marchetti said, "that the barometric pressure has dropped fourteen millibars since yesterday, the birds have gone silent, and every hydrological indicator I own says there is a wall of water coming down these rivers within—" she checked something on her wrist "—thirty-six hours, possibly less. So yes. Can you travel."

I looked at her, then at Brightwater, then at the sky, which had gone the color of a bruise deciding whether to bloom.

Thirty-six hours. Kessler's men somewhere upstream with a cipher they'd crack in a week. A cave full of dead magistrate's sins. A monsoon the size of God's bad mood rolling in off the Bismarck Sea.

"Give me a stick," I said, "and point me at the bastards."

Brightwater pulled me upright, and the world went white and then came back, and I stood swaying in the swamp that had failed to kill me, holding my grandmother's cowrie, next to the man I'd spent ten weeks running from and one hour trusting.

Behind us, far off, the first thunder spoke—not a rumble but a statement, the kind of sound that doesn't discuss, that announces. Marchetti looked north and said, very quietly, "We're out of time."

The rain began before she finished the sentence.
