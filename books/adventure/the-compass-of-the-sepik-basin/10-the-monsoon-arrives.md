## 10. The Monsoon Arrives

I have spent my career being laughed at in conference rooms by men who thought hydrology was a hobby for people who couldn't do real engineering. I want it on the record that I have never once laughed at the water. The water always wins. The only variable is how many people refuse to notice until it's standing in their living room.

The monsoon arrived at 4:17 in the morning, and I know the time because I was awake logging it, because I am the kind of person who logs things, and because Rafael Brightwater was asleep sitting up with his rifle across his knees like a man who had made peace with the posture decades ago.

The first sound wasn't thunder. It was the river clearing its throat—a deep, wet, structural groan from the channel below the bluff where we'd made camp, the sound of a body of water deciding to become a different body of water. Then the sky simply opened, not like rain but like a verdict.

"Rafael," I said.

He was awake before I finished the second syllable. Of course he was. Ten years of chasing thieves across empty country will teach you to sleep like a tripwire.

"Storm's here," he said, stating the obvious with the flat calm I was beginning to find either deeply reassuring or deeply infuriating, depending on the hour.

"It was here yesterday. This is the arrival." I held up my rain gauge, which had filled past its capacity in forty minutes, which is a sentence I never expected to write about a gauge rated for a monsoon. "Rafael, this is wrong. I've modeled basin floods. This intensity, this early in the season—it's not weather. It's weather with intent."

He looked at me the way he always did when I personified water, which was the way a man looks at a colleague who talks to her instruments. But he'd been listening to me for two weeks now, and I'd watched the skepticism drain out of him degree by degree, the way a river drops when you dam it upstream.

"Twenty feet," he said. "That's your number?"

"Twenty feet in the main channel by tomorrow night. Give or take." I didn't tell him the error bars. There was no comfort in the error bars.

* * *

By noon we had lost the argument with the terrain and were moving.

The cave—the inland entrance, the one the chart fragment had marked with Crane's obsessive little cipher, the one we'd spent three days translating while the sky darkened like a bruise—was two kilometers downstream and thirty meters up a limestone face. Thirty meters had sounded like safety when we'd found it. Now I watched the brown water climbing the rock below us and did the arithmetic I didn't want to do: at this rate, the lower gallery would flood within the day. The ledger sat in the upper chamber, wrapped in oilcloth and Voss's shirt, and every hour the river took another meter of our margin.

We went down to it anyway. You don't leave a thing like that to the water. Water keeps things, but it keeps them the way a magpie keeps them—scattered, drowned, unfindable.

The climb down was Rafael first, me second, Voss last, moving on a leg that was healing wrong and a conscience that was healing slower. Inside, the cave breathed at us—that cold mineral exhalation that caves do, the smell of stone that has been keeping secrets longer than any of us have been alive. The water in the lower passage was already thigh-deep and moving.

"Upper gallery," Rafael said, and we climbed.

I'll say this for Corben Voss: the man could not be trusted with a boat, a map, or the truth, but he carried a ledger through a flooded cave like it was a sleeping child. He handed it up to me at the lip of the upper chamber and I took it and felt the weight of it—not paper weight, which is nothing, but the other kind, the kind you can't put down.

"We can't stay," I said, when we'd caught our breath in the torchlight. "The entrance floods, we're sealed in a limestone tomb with a dead magistrate's paperwork. That's not a plan, that's an epitaph."

"Every route out is underwater," Voss said. He was shivering. He'd been shivering for two days and I'd stopped pretending it was just the fever. "Every route out was underwater yesterday."

"Not every route." Rafael was at the cave mouth, looking out at the rain-hammered green. "The high ground. Northeast. The council house at the nine-tributary confluence sits on a ridge the old maps mark at sixty meters. The villages go there every flood season. It's a day's walk if the channels behave."

"If," I said.

"If," he agreed, and there it was, the whole of our situation, balanced on a conjunction.

* * *

Here is what I could not stop doing, even while we packed, even while we planned, even while Voss dozed against the wall with his bad leg stretched out: I kept running the numbers, and the numbers kept refusing to behave.

I've been a hydrologist for eleven years. I know what a natural flood curve looks like the way Rafael knows what a guilty man's trail looks like. A monsoon flood rises fast, yes—but it rises *broadly*. It fills the channels it has always filled. It is violent but it is honest. It has no preferences.

This water had preferences.

I'd been logging stage heights at six points along our route since we'd left the coast, and when I laid the readings out that afternoon on the flat of my field case, the pattern was so clean it was almost elegant. The main stem of the Sepik's tributaries were rising exactly as the storm demanded. But the distributary channels—the ones that fed the stilt villages, the ones that wound through the lowland gardens and past the boundary trees and the marker posts that had stood since before anyone in those villages was born—those channels were rising *faster*. Disproportionately. Engineeredly.

"Rafael," I said. "Come look at this."

He came. He looked. He didn't understand it, and to his credit, he said so.

"Upstream," I said. "Somebody has been tampering with the flows for weeks—that's what I came here chasing, the salinity anomalies, the stage data that made no sense. I thought it was mine dewatering. Sloppy mining, careless pumping. But look." I put my finger on the readings. "The diversions aren't random. They're not even primarily serving the mine. They're routing peak flows into exactly the channels that flood the village lands. Every boundary marker, every garden plot, every strip of ground the river people have farmed for generations—it's all in the lowlands, and all the lowlands are being fed water from every direction at once."

Rafael was quiet for a long moment. Rain hammered the cave mouth behind him like applause for something terrible.

"You're saying the flood has an author," he said finally.

"I'm saying the flood has a *contractor*." I heard my own voice go flat and cold, the voice I use when the data is worse than I can afford to feel about it. "Think about what Kessler needs. He needs the land. The deeds are forgeries—Crane proved that a hundred and fifty years ago, and the ledger proves the company that became Meridian was founded on it. But forged deeds can be challenged. Dispossessed villages can file claims. Unless—" I tapped the readings again. "Unless the physical evidence disappears. Boundary markers wash away in a flood. Gardens silt over. Villages that survive a flood like this don't come back to the lowlands—they relocate, they resettle, and when they do, the land is 'abandoned,' and abandoned land can be purchased. Cleanly. Legally. With paperwork that looks better than anything Crane ever forged."

"Kessler isn't fighting the villages," Rafael said slowly. "He's erasing them."

"He's letting the river do it. Deniable, cheap, and if anyone asks, it's an act of God." I laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "I spent three years being drummed out of my field for claiming someone was manipulating flows and having no proof anyone would accept. Rafael—I'm standing inside the proof. The proof is a *flood*."

He looked at me then, and I saw something shift in his face—not the ranger's flatness, not the avoidance I'd been cataloguing since the day we fought over that boat, but something rawer. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man who has spent a decade enforcing a law and is watching, in real time, the machinery of that law turned into a weapon.

"The villages," he said. "The stilt villages downstream. The old woman's people—the one who recited the boundaries from memory. How long do they have?"

I did the arithmetic. I hated the arithmetic.

"Lowland channels crest tomorrow night. If they haven't moved to high ground by tomorrow morning—" I didn't finish it. Finishing it would have made it real, and I needed it to stay a projection for another few hours, the way you need a diagnosis to stay a word.

"Then we're not just carrying a ledger anymore," Rafael said. "We're carrying a warning."

* * *

We left the cave at first light, or what passes for first light under a monsoon sky, which is to say the darkness turned a slightly different flavor of gray and the rain kept on. The ledger went into a waterproof satchel against my back, because Rafael carried the rifle and Voss carried himself, which was all he could carry.

The forest had become a canal system. What had been game trails two days ago were now brown rivers moving at walking pace and accelerating. We waded, we climbed, we waded again. Rafael navigated by a combination of the chart fragment, his compass, and some private sense of direction that I suspected was less a skill than a superstition that had never once failed him, which is functionally the same thing.

We were maybe two hours out when he stopped so abruptly I walked into his back.

"Boats," he said.

I heard it a moment later: engines. More than one. Small outboards, working the flooded forest the way we were, but moving, and we were wading.

"Kessler's men?"

"Who else sweeps a basin in a monsoon?" Rafael pulled us off the trail into the root tangle of a buttress tree, and we stood there, three people pressed against wet bark, while the engines passed—two boats, maybe three, men in dark rain gear with the patient, methodical air of people searching rather than traveling.

They knew we were here. That was the thing I understood as I watched them, my heart doing something arrhythmic against the ledger's satchel. They weren't patrolling. They were *sweeping*. Kessler knew the cave, knew the ledger, knew the three of us were somewhere in a drowning basin with no way out, and he had simply waited for the monsoon to close every door and then sent his men through the ones that remained.

"They'll work the high ground eventually," Rafael murmured, when the engines had faded. "The council house. It's the only dry ground for fifty kilometers. If the villages gather there—"

"Then that's where we're going," I said. "And that's where they'll come. Rafael. The ledger isn't just proof anymore. The readings are proof. The *flood pattern* is proof. If I can get my logs to the councils, to anyone with a functioning brainstem and a chart—"

"You're talking about accusing a consortium of manufacturing a natural disaster with a rain gauge and a notebook."

"I'm talking about the only kind of proof that's ever mattered," I said. "The kind someone was willing to stand in the water and take."

He looked at me for a long moment, rain streaming off his jaw, and something passed between us that I didn't have a word for yet. I'd have one later, in a flooded church ruin, between pages of a dead man's confessions. But not yet. For now it was just two people who had each spent a decade being disbelieved, standing in a conspiracy made of water, believing each other completely.

"Move," he said softly. "While their engines are downstream."

* * *

The river rose twenty feet in a day. I want to be precise about that, because I am precise about everything, and because someday, if there is a someday, someone will ask me for the data and I will be able to give it to them to the centimeter.

We reached the ridge path at dusk, the ledger against my spine, Voss half-carried between us, the basin below us transformed into a single heaving brown sea dotted with the crowns of drowned trees. Somewhere down there were the villages. Somewhere down there, if the elders had read the sky and the water the way their grandparents had, the people were already climbing toward high ground, carrying what they could, leaving the rest to a river that had been aimed at them like a weapon.

And somewhere down there, in an unmarked boat with a satellite phone and a clean conscience, Dain Kessler was watching his contract fulfill itself.

I stood on the ridge in the rain and felt the ledger's weight against my back and understood, with the terrible clarity that only comes from data, that we were not running from the storm anymore.

We were running *ahead* of it, carrying the only evidence that it had ever been aimed at all—and the men who had aimed it knew exactly where we were going.

The council house fires were visible through the rain, high on the ridge ahead, small and stubborn. Rafael counted them under his breath.

"Nine," he said. "Nine fires. The nine tributaries."

"Then let's hope," I said, "that they're expecting company."

Behind us, downstream, something in the drowned basin boomed—deep, tectonic, the sound of a channel wall giving way—and the water below us climbed a little faster, as if it had heard where we were headed too.
