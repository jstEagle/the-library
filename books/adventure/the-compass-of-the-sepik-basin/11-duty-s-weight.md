## 11. Duty's Weight

The radio sat in the bottom of my pack like a stone I'd been carrying for ten years.

That's not quite fair. Radios don't have weight unless you count the weight of what you're supposed to say into them, and I'd said a thousand routine things into this one—position reports, suspect descriptions, requests for backup that never came because there was no backup out where I worked, just me and the miles and the occasional jackrabbit with poor judgment. The radio was the tether. It was how Port Moresby knew I still existed, and how I knew I still belonged to something, even if that something mostly consisted of a dispatcher named Oka who pronounced my surname like a sneeze.

Now I sat in the flooded church ruin with the Magistrate's Ledger wrapped in oilcloth on my lap and Ilse Marchetti three feet away wringing out her socks, and the tether felt less like a lifeline than a leash.

"You're going to call it in," she said. Not a question. She'd watched me dig the radio out, watched me check the charge with the automatic tenderness of a man checking his own pulse.

"Protocol," I said.

"Of course." She hung her socks on a nail that had probably held a hymnal chart once. "And protocol is?"

I told her. Report discovery of historical documents of potential evidentiary value. Secure them. Await instructions from federal custody liaison. It sounded reasonable when I recited it. It had sounded reasonable for ten years, which should have been its own warning.

"And the federal custody liaison," Ilse said, "is the same office that signed off on Meridian Consortium's water survey permits. The ones that don't match the actual flow rates by about forty percent."

"I know."

"You know, and you're calling anyway."

"Ilse." I turned the radio over in my hands. "I've been a ranger for ten years. Everything I've done—every arrest, every report, every mile—I did it inside a system. The system isn't perfect. But the alternative is me deciding, alone, in a swamp, whose property this is. That's not justice either. That's just a man with a gun and an opinion."

She looked at me for a long moment, water dripping off her braid onto the church floor, and I could see her deciding whether to argue or let me find out for myself. She'd been doing a lot of that lately. Letting me find out for myself. It was either respect or strategy, and with Ilse it was usually both.

"Fine," she said. "Call your people. I'll be interested in what they say."

* * *

Getting a signal was its own small expedition. The monsoon had knocked out the repeater at Ambunti two days ago—we could tell because the static had changed character, gone from busy to empty—and so I climbed through the collapsed roof of the vestry, up onto what remained of the bell tower, and held the antenna toward the sky like a man offering a match to a storm.

The rain came down in ropes. Below me, the basin was unrecognizable. Three days ago there had been channels and banks and sandbars; now there was just brown water moving with intent, carrying whole trees, half a fence, somebody's canoe, somebody's roof. Somewhere under all of it were the boundary markers Kessler's engineers had spent months placing, and I understood now—they'd known exactly what the flood would do. They'd built their case on paper and then hired the sky to erase the evidence against the paper. It was elegant. It was the most elegant evil thing I'd ever seen, and I include the time a cattle baron burned his own barn for the insurance.

Static. Then, miraculously, a voice.

"Brightwater? That you?" Oka. Sneeze-pronunciation and all. Ten years of miles, and hearing her voice made my chest hurt in a way I didn't have time to examine.

"Brightwater, affirm. Position—" I gave it as best I could, coordinates that would put me somewhere in a drowned world where no coordinate meant anything anymore. "Oka, listen. I need to log a discovery. Historical documents. Colonial-era records, substantial volume, apparently intact. Contents indicate—"

I stopped. How did you say *contents indicate that every land deed in this basin is forged, including the founding charter of the company currently trying to drown the witnesses* into a radio during a monsoon?

"—contents indicate systematic fraud in original land titling. Extending to current corporate interests."

Silence on the line long enough that I checked the signal indicator. Still there. Oka was thinking, and Oka thinking was never good news; Oka was supposed to just write things down.

"Say again the nature of the documents."

"A ledger. A magistrate's ledger. Aldous Crane. He recorded everything—the bribes, the forged deeds, the bought verdicts. It's all in there, Oka. Names, dates, amounts. A hundred years of it, written down by the man who did the buying."

More silence. Rain hammered the tower. Somewhere below, Ilse had come up into the vestry doorway to watch me, arms crossed, socks forgotten.

"Hold," Oka said finally. "Stand by for command."

I stood by. I stood by while the rain tried to drown me and the wind tried to throw me off the tower and the river below ate another piece of somebody's life. And I thought about standing by, about what standing by had meant across ten years. Standing by while a dispute over a well went to arbitration and came back owned by a man who'd never seen the well. Standing by while eviction notices went out to families whose names weren't on any deed because nobody had ever offered them one. Standing by, always, because the paperwork was in order, and the paperwork was always in order, because the men who wrote the paperwork wrote it for themselves.

The radio crackled back to life.

"Brightwater. Command here." A different voice. Deputy Director Harrow, which meant this had already gone up three rungs in four minutes, which meant someone above Oka had recognized the word *Meridian*. "Confirm your last. You have physical possession of Crane-era documents?"

"Affirm. Originals. Condition good."

"Listen carefully. Effective immediately, those materials are designated federal evidence. You are to secure them and hold for retrieval by the Federal Custody Liaison Office. Do not permit access to any non-authorized party. Do you copy?"

"Copy," I said. "Who retrieves? FCLO is Port Moresby-based. Nobody's flying into this."

"Retrieval is already arranged, Ranger. Meridian Consortium has vessels in the basin. They've been granted temporary custodial authority given the flood emergency. A Mr. Kessler will coordinate handover. Confirm you understand."

The rain kept falling. It seemed important, suddenly, to notice that. The rain kept falling, the world kept turning, and somewhere in Port Moresby a deputy director had just told me, in a bored administrative voice, that the man who wanted this ledger destroyed was being deputized to collect it.

"Ranger Brightwater. Do you confirm?"

I looked down through the broken roof at Ilse, watching me from the vestry with an expression that said she'd heard every word, because of course she had, sound carries over water, and she wasn't even surprised. That was the worst part. She'd known. She'd sat there wringing her socks knowing exactly what my protocol would buy us, and she'd let me make the call anyway, because some lessons you can't teach a man. He has to hear the voice himself.

"Ranger Brightwater."

"I hear you," I said.

"Do you confirm the order?"

Here's the thing about ten years of obedience. It doesn't live in your head. It lives in your hands, in your mouth, in the muscles of your throat. My mouth was already shaping the word *confirmed*, had been shaping it since before Harrow finished talking, because that's what the word after *do you confirm* had been for a decade, the way *amen* is for a congregation. I stood on that drowned bell tower with the leash in my fist and felt the whole machinery of my compliance reach for the switch.

And then I thought about the old woman in the stilt village, the one who recited boundaries from memory—*the creek with the crooked pandanus, the bend where the white cockatoos nest*—boundaries that existed nowhere on paper except in a dead magistrate's cipher, boundaries that Kessler's contracts called vacant. I thought about Voss bleeding out in the mangroves with his grandmother's dispossession folded up inside him. I thought about my brother, eleven years dead, killed by a water dispute that was settled in a room he was never allowed into, by men whose signatures were legal and whose law was not.

Whose law is this?

It wasn't a dramatic question. That's what surprised me. I'd expected, if I ever broke, for it to feel like breaking—a snap, a fall, thunder. Instead it was quiet. It was a question arriving the way questions actually arrive, on a Tuesday, in the rain, while a bureaucrat waits on the line. Whose law is this? Not *is this lawful*—it was lawful, God help us, every letter of it lawful, that was the whole trick. Whose law. Who wrote it. Who it was written for. Who it drowned.

"Ranger Brightwater, confirm receipt of order."

"Receipt confirmed," I said.

Harrow exhaled, satisfied, a man closing a file. "Good. Kessler's team will reach your position within forty-eight hours. Protect those documents, Ranger. They represent significant—"

"I know what they represent," I said. "They represent the only honest piece of paper ever written in this basin. That's why you want it in a vault instead of in a courtroom. Good night, sir."

I switched off the radio before he could answer. Then I sat down on the wet bell tower in the rain and laughed, once, a short ugly sound, because I'd just committed a dozen offenses against the service that had fed me for ten years, and the first thing I felt wasn't fear. It was relief. Ten years of miles, and it turned out the road ended here, on a church roof, saying no to a voice.

* * *

Ilse didn't say *I told you so*, which took more discipline than anything else I'd witnessed from her, and I'd watched her take water samples in crocodile country.

"So," she said instead, when I came down dripping. "We're criminals now."

"We're nothing yet. We haven't done anything. I just... failed to confirm an order."

"You told a deputy director his custody chain was corrupt and hung up on him."

"That's not a crime. That's insubordination. There's a difference, and the difference is about six months of pay."

She almost smiled. Then she didn't, because outside the thunder rolled and we both looked at the waterline creeping up the church steps, and the joke died between us like everything else the flood touched.

"He'll send them," she said. "Kessler. Forty-eight hours means thirty-six, because men like that pad their promises backward. And he won't come politely. He knows you have it, he knows the villages are sheltering on high ground, and he knows the flood gives him cover. This church—" she tapped the wall, "—is on every survey map he owns. It's the highest structure for six kilometers. If we stay, we're a target with a roof."

"If we move, we're a target without one."

"Yes." She crouched beside the ledger, unwrapped a corner of the oilcloth, looked at the dense ciphered columns underneath the way other people look at sleeping children. "Then we'd better use the time. Your thief's tube deciphers maybe a third of this. If we're going to lose it—or lose each other—somebody besides us needs to know what's in these pages. Word for word. Before Thursday."

I looked at her. Rain on the tin roof, candle stub guttering on the altar rail, a woman who'd lost her career asking a man who'd just lost his to spend their last safe hours copying a dead colonial's confessions by candlelight.

"You realize," I said, "that if anyone had described my life to me a month ago, I'd have arrested them for public intoxication."

"Get the paper, Brightwater."

We worked. That's the part I'll remember, later, when people ask about the night everything changed—not the decision, which took four seconds on a bell tower, but the work after it, the two of us hunched over Crane's cramped handwriting while the century's worst flood climbed the walls around us. Ilse read the cipher aloud, translating, and I copied, and somewhere past midnight she read an entry dated 1911 describing a payment of twelve pounds to a village headman for land "acquired in perpetuity from persons having no concept of ownership," and her voice cracked on the word *perpetuity*, and I reached over and covered her hand with mine, and neither of us pulled away.

Outside, far downstream, an engine sound carried over the water. Too early. Too many engines.

Thirty-six hours, I'd guessed. I'd been generous.

"They're coming," Ilse said quietly.

I blew out the candle. In the dark, with the ledger against my chest and her shoulder against my arm, I understood that whatever happened next, I wasn't running. For the first time in ten years, there was nowhere I'd rather be than exactly here, holding ground.

"Stay behind the altar," I whispered. "And Ilse—whatever they offer me, don't let me listen."
