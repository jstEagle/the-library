## 12. Between the Pages

**Rafael**

The church had been Methodist once, back when the colonial administration still bothered to dress its greed in a collar. Now it was a roofless shell of coral stone standing in two feet of brown water, its bell tower leaning like a drunk against the night, and it was the best hiding place in the flooded forest because nobody prayed here anymore and therefore nobody looked.

We waded in at dusk, Voss's survey tube strapped across my chest under my shirt like a splint on a broken rib, the ledger wrapped in oilcloth inside Ilse's pack. The water was warm and smelled of drowned earth. Somewhere out in the dark, Kessler's boats were running their searchlights along the channels, patient as men checking traps.

"Cozy," Ilse said, hauling herself onto the altar slab, which was the only dry-ish surface left. She wrung out her braid with the efficiency of someone who'd stopped being precious about her hair somewhere around the third day of monsoon. "I've defended worse field stations."

"You've *defended* field stations?"

"Dissertations don't write themselves." She snapped a chemical light stick and set it on the altar like an offering. Green light washed up the walls, caught the ghost of a painted text above where the altar would have been—*GOD IS LOVE*, or what remained of it, the O's gone to mold so it read GD S LVE, which struck me as more honest than most scripture I'd encountered.

I unslung the tube. My hands were steady, which surprised me. They hadn't been steady since Wagu Bend, since Voss went down in the water with a hole in him and his eyes on mine, saying *finish it*, and I'd said yes the way you say yes to a dying man, which is to say cheaply, easily, without understanding that he intended to hold me to it.

Ilse watched me handle the tube. "You keep treating it like it might bite."

"It might. Everything in this basin bites. The river, the weather, the paperwork."

"The paperwork least of all," she said. "Paperwork is just other people's patience, preserved."

I slid the chart fragment from the tube—the piece Voss had stolen, the key—and laid it beside the ledger. Crane's cipher wasn't sophisticated. That was the first thing that struck me, kneeling there in a ruined church with floodwater lapping at my knees: the magistrate who'd documented every forged deed and bought verdict in the basin had hidden his record behind nothing more than a surveyor's grid. Coordinates. Numbers mapped to letters by a chart any licensed surveyor could read. He hadn't wanted the ledger to be impossible to open. He'd wanted it to be openable only by someone who understood land—who could read a plat the way a priest reads Latin.

He'd been waiting for a ranger. Or a thief with a grandmother. Either would do.

"Grid substitution," Ilse said over my shoulder, her breath close and quick. "Look—the marginal numbers correspond to the survey section lines. He indexed his entries by parcel. Every crime filed under the property it was committed to steal."

"He organized his sins by real estate."

"He was a magistrate. Of course he did." She laughed—a short, startled sound, the kind you make when something awful is also funny and your body can't decide which. In the green light her face was all angles and rain, and I thought, absurdly, that I'd known her nine days and trusted her further than I'd trusted anyone in ten years, and that this fact should frighten me more than Kessler's boats did.

I opened the ledger to the first page and began to translate.

* * *

**Ilse**

Here is what I know about confession: it goes easier if you're doing something with your hands.

My mother taught me that. She was a woman who could only discuss her marriage while kneading bread, only discuss her grief while weeding, and I inherited the trait whole. So while Rafael worked through Crane's crabbed nineteenth-century hand, letter by letter, I kept my hands busy—decanting water, checking the satellite phone's seals, drying pages with strips of my last clean shirt—and I listened to him read aloud in a low voice, like a man telling stories to a child afraid of the dark.

And between entries, because the night was long and the storm outside made the ruined church feel like the inside of a drum, we talked.

It started small. He asked about the salinity logs, and I told him about the anomaly that had brought me upriver—the flow rates that made no sense, rivers running thin when they should have run fat, the tide lying to the coast. Then, because he didn't interrupt, because he had the rarest skill of simply waiting, I found myself telling him the rest.

"I had a job before this one," I said. "A good one. Rhine Commission, then a consultancy in Darwin. I was the person ministries called when they needed to know whether a dam proposal was honest."

"And?"

"And I called one dishonest. Publicly. With data." I turned a page for him, though he didn't need me to; I needed to do it. "The minister's brother-in-law held the construction contract. My report was 'restructured' by people who'd never seen the river. When I objected, they produced emails suggesting I'd falsified my sampling. Forged, actually—which is funny, given our current reading material. Forged well enough that I spent two years unemployable in my own field. I came out here measuring anomalies for a conservation trust because it was the only work left that nobody thought worth corrupting."

Rafael was quiet a moment. Rain hammered the missing roof; the water in the nave rose a finger's width, slow and certain.

"They used paper to drown you," he said finally. "Same as Crane's forgeries. Same as Kessler's contracts. Different century, same weapon."

"That's why I couldn't walk away from this. Even when you were insufferable about the boat."

"I was *never* insufferable about the boat."

"You said, and I quote, 'a hydrologist should know her vessel sits lower in the water than her optimism.'"

He almost smiled. It was the first time I'd gotten anything close to a full smile out of him, and I noted, with the detached precision I brought to all my data, that it did something unreasonable to my pulse. I decided not to publish those findings.

"Your turn," I said. "Fair trade. You've heard my disgrace. Show me yours."

* * *

**Rafael**

There it was. The toll gate on the road I'd built around myself, decade-long, mile by measured mile.

Outside, lightning showed the flooded forest in negative—black trees, white water—and thunder rolled down the channel like something heavy being dragged. I could have deflected. I was good at deflecting; I'd had practice. But she'd handed me her worst thing, flat and unflinching, and there was a ledger open in front of us full of a dead man's honesty, and it seemed suddenly cowardly to be less brave than a colonial magistrate.

"My brother Tomás," I said. "Younger by two years. We grew up on the edge of the desert, the dry side, where water law is the only law anybody respects. He stayed when I joined the rangers. Bought into a small irrigation cooperative—forty families, a canal off the aquifer, nothing grand."

I turned a page I wasn't reading. Crane's cipher swam in the green light.

"A development company wanted the aquifer. They couldn't buy the co-op outright, so they bought the records instead—filed a competing claim with documents showing their rights predated the canal. Tomás fought it. He was good at fighting; he had our mother's stubbornness and none of my caution. He rode to the district office three times to contest the filing. On the fourth trip, coming home, his truck went off a culvert in a storm. Single vehicle. No witnesses."

Ilse had gone very still.

"The coroner's report listed rain as the cause. Six months later I pulled the file through a friend—ranger channels, quiet—and found the road maintenance log for that stretch had been altered. Culvert inspected and cleared two days *after* the date of death. And the claim documents that started it all? I've spent ten years half-knowing what they were. Forgeries. I felt it the way you feel weather coming, but I never proved it, because proving it meant going through courts owned by the same money, and because—" I stopped. Started again. "Because chasing it meant stopping moving. And moving was how I survived him. Ten years of petty thieves across empty miles, each one a reason not to sit still long enough to grieve. I logged forty thousand kilometers, Ilse. I could tell you the distance between every waterhole in the territory. I couldn't tell you the last thing Tomás said to me."

The rain filled the silence. When she spoke, her voice was careful and kind, which from her was somehow more devastating than sympathy.

"What was it?"

"I don't remember. That's the truth. I remember the truck leaving. I don't remember the words."

She reached over and put her hand flat on the ledger, over the entry I'd been failing to read, as if to hold the past down the way you hold down a map in wind.

"Then we find the words," she said. "In here, maybe. Crane documented everything. If the company that killed your brother's co-op traces back through Meridian's founding charter—" She didn't finish it. She didn't have to. The possibility sat between us on the altar slab, enormous and terrible, and for the first time in ten years I let myself look at it straight on.

* * *

**Rafael**

We worked until the chemical light died and Ilse cracked another, and then until that one guttered too, and the sky behind the broken tower went from black to the bruised grey that isn't dawn yet but is thinking about it.

Crane's ledger was worse than I'd expected and better than I'd feared. Worse, because the man had recorded everything—dates, sums, names, the specific verdicts purchased, the villages whose boundaries he'd redrawn with a ruler and a bribe, parcel by parcel, tribe by tribe, until the paper map and the living river had nothing left to say to each other. Better, because he'd done it systematically, with cross-references, with an appendix keyed to the founding charter of the New Guinea Development Company—Meridian's great-grandmother—listing every director whose signature sat atop a forged deed.

"He knew he'd lost," Ilse said quietly, near the end. "That's what this is. You can hear it in the later entries. The handwriting gets tired. He knew the empire would bury his reports, so he buried them deeper—in cipher, in a cave, in a generation's silence—and he trusted the river to give them back eventually."

"To someone who could read them."

"To someone who'd *bother*," she corrected. "Reading was always possible. Bothering was the scarce resource."

Somewhere in the smallest hours, wet and cold and lit green, we stopped translating for a while. Not because we ran out of ledger. Because she leaned her shoulder against mine—just leaned, the way you lean against a wall you've decided to trust—and I found I didn't want to move, and the not-moving felt like the bravest act of the night.

"I spent nine days thinking you were the most irritating man I'd ever shared a boat with," she murmured.

"Nine days. That's a record. Most people need eleven."

"Shut up, Rafael." A pause. Her head found the hollow below my shoulder, fitting there like it had been surveyed for. "Thank you for not shutting up, though. Earlier. About your brother."

I put my hand on her hair, damp and salt-stiff, and said nothing, because there was nothing to add and everything already said, and love—if that's what this was, arriving quietly between pages, without announcement, the way the tide comes—doesn't require commentary. It requires staying.

* * *

**Ilse**

Dawn came grey and mean, the storm down to a steady hard rain, the churchyard a lake with the graves submerged—we were sitting, I realized, on top of other people's dead, which seemed appropriate for a night spent among a dead man's words.

Rafael slept an hour against the tower wall, truly slept, his face losing for once that ranger's arithmetic, the constant tallying of distance and threat. I watched the water rise around the altar and did sums of my own: the flood cresting tomorrow, maybe the day after; Kessler's boats needing deep channels, which narrowed his approach routes to two; the council house at high ground reachable in a day's paddling if we left within the hour.

Then I did a different sum. The ledger's appendix, cross-referenced against the salinity data I'd been collecting since the coast. The diversion points Kessler's engineers had built. The boundary markers along the lower tributaries—the ones the old woman had recited from memory in the stilt village, the ones no paper acknowledged.

They weren't just hiding the flooding. They'd *designed* it. The flow rates I'd logged as anomalies weren't mining inefficiency. They were a schedule. Water released to drown the lowland markers before the councils could ever file a claim based on ground that existed. Erase the landscape, erase the evidence, erase the people's case—all with rain as the alibi.

I woke Rafael with my hand on his shoulder, and he came up out of sleep fast and silent, a knife of a man, then saw my face and stilled.

"You found something."

"In the ledger and in my own data. They match." I showed him, page against screen, Crane's parcel numbers against my flow readings, and watched him understand it the way I'd understood it—horizon-wide, all at once.

He closed the ledger, wrapped it in oilcloth with hands that were no longer quite steady, and looked out at the drowned churchyard, the drowned forest, the drowned world.

"The councils meet at high ground," he said. "Voss's promise, the elders' claims, your data, Crane's confession—it all lands in one place now." He slung the pack. "We move before Kessler figures out the same math."

Out in the channel, faint under the rain, came the sound I'd been dreading all night: an engine, idling, patient, closing.
