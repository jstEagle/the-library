## 14. The Betrayal at Wagu Bend

The guide's name was Tambe, and I should have known.

I should have known the way you know a card sharp in a border town—not by anything he does, but by how clean he looks doing it. Everyone else in that flooded village wore the monsoon like a second skin: mud to the knees, hair plastered flat, eyes red-rimmed from smoke and worry. Tambe came out of his stilt house in a dry shirt, pressed somehow, with a canoe paddle worn smooth as a preacher's manners, and he looked at Voss—gray-lipped, shivering under a tarp in the bottom of our wrecked boat—and said, "I can take you to Wagu Bend before dark."

Not *I can help*. Not *God, what happened to him*. Just the offer, priced and ready, like a man selling fuel at a crossroads.

Ten years of tracking thieves through empty country teaches you a few things, and one of them is that desperation makes everyone honest except the people who've already decided what their desperation is worth. Ilse saw it too—I watched her see it—but we had a dying man, a ledger wrapped in oilcloth against my chest, forty miles of drowned country between us and the council house, and Kessler's boats somewhere behind us closing like a hand.

So we took the offer. That's the thing about being out of options: they stop looking like traps and start looking like doors.

* * *

We loaded up under a sky the color of wet slate. The village had gone vertical with the flood—people hauling pigs and cooking pots into the rafters, kids perched on roof beams watching us with the flat appraisal of children who have seen strangers bring trouble before. An old man pressed three smoked fish into Ilse's hands without a word. She tried to pay him; he folded her fingers closed over the coins and walked away. She stood there holding the fish like they were evidence of something.

"He knows what's coming," she said quietly, when we pushed off.

"Everyone here knows what's coming."

"That's not what I mean." She shipped her paddle and looked back at the village shrinking behind us. "They know the water isn't natural. They've been saying it for days—the river rising wrong, rising from the tributaries first, like something upstream is pouring it down on purpose." Her jaw did the thing it does when she's holding back the full lecture. "Kessler isn't just buying land, Rafael. He's manufacturing the flood that makes the land worthless enough to sell. And these people are going to stand on their rooftops and watch him do it."

Voss coughed from the bottom of the canoe. It was a bad sound, wet and deep, the kind of cough that spends currency you don't have. The bullet had gone through his shoulder two nights ago at the church ruins—my fault, my route, my decision to hole up in a building with one door—and the wound had turned angry since, hot to the touch, streaking red lines toward his heart. Ilse had cleaned it with boiled water and the last of our antiseptic, but we both knew the arithmetic. Infection plus exposure plus no hospital equals a debt coming due.

"Hey," Voss said, when the coughing let him. His eyes found mine, fever-bright. "You still got it?"

I patted the oilcloth bundle under my jacket. "Still got it."

"Good." He lay back, gray face tipped up to the rain. "My grandmother used to say the river keeps its own records. Every lie ever told on its banks, written down in silt." A ghost of a smile. "Crane just wrote faster than the silt."

Tambe paddled in front, silent as a heron, and I watched the back of his dry shirt and told myself I was paranoid. Told myself that a man who ferries strangers through floodwater for kina is just a man making a living, same as anyone. Told myself that ten years of catching liars doesn't mean everyone is one.

It means you're right more often than you'd like.

* * *

Wagu Bend announced itself an hour before we reached it—a change in the water's voice, the river's mutter deepening where the channel swung wide around a bluff of black rock. The forest crowded down to the waterline there, half-drowned, the trees standing hip-deep and holding their branches like people wading with their arms up. Mist sat in the hollows. Everything smelled green and rotting and ancient.

"We rest here," Tambe said, steering for a strip of gravel that was mostly underwater already. "Past the bend, the current is too strong to fight in rain. We wait for the window."

Ilse checked the sky, then the water, then the sky again—the habit of a woman who reads rivers the way I read tracks. "What window?"

"The rain thins at dusk. Twenty minutes, maybe thirty. Then we run the bend fast and make the confluence by midnight." He said it smoothly, all of it, every detail pre-measured, and something cold slid down my spine because I recognized the cadence. It was the cadence of a man reciting a script someone else had written.

"How many times have you made this run?" I asked.

His paddle paused, just a fraction. "Many times."

"In this flood? With this current?"

"Rafael." Voss's voice, low, from the bottom of the boat. "Stop interrogating the man and help me sit up."

But Ilse had caught it too. I saw it happen in her face—the hydrologist's instinct, the sense for anomalies, for flow rates that don't match the channel. She looked at the gravel bar, at the mist, at the blind curve of the bend where the water disappeared around black rock, and she said, very quietly, "There's no village past the bend, is there. No reason to stop here unless you're waiting for someone."

Tambe didn't answer. That was the answer.

I had my hand on the ledger through the wet canvas of my jacket, and time went strange and slow the way it does in the last seconds before things break. I thought about pulling the rifle. I thought about the arithmetic of three against however-many, in chest-deep water, with a dying man. I thought about my brother, Daniel, eleven years dead, and how the last thing he ever said to me was *trust the paperwork, Rafe, the paperwork protects you*, and how the paperwork had protected everyone except him.

Then the far bank spoke.

"Tambe!" A voice through the rain, amplified by a megaphone or just by authority. "Bring them in!"

Two longboats slid out of the flooded timber on the far side, maybe twenty men between them, black rain slickers, rifles slung muzzle-down the way professionals carry them when they want something alive. Behind them, riding the current like it had been ordered to cooperate, came a barge with a canopy and a generator light burning yellow through the murk.

Dain Kessler's light. Dain Kessler's patience, arriving at exactly the right place at exactly the right time.

Tambe raised both hands toward the longboats—an old gesture, older than any of us, surrender or greeting or the end of a transaction. He wouldn't look at me. "Your family's debts," he said to the water, to nobody, to himself. "Four children. My brother's medical bills. You don't know what the company holds over people here. You come from a desert. What do you know about owing?"

And God help me, I understood him. That was the worst part. Ten years chasing petty thieves across empty miles had taught me that almost no betrayal is born wicked; most of them are just arithmetic done by desperate people, and the sum always comes out the same: someone else pays.

"You could have just left us at the village," I said.

"They'd have found you there anyway. This way my family eats." He finally looked at me, and his eyes were wet, and I couldn't tell if it was rain. "I'm sorry about your friend. Truly."

Voss started laughing. It turned into coughing halfway through, which somehow made it worse. "Sorry," he wheezed. "Everybody's sorry. Kessler's sorry. The magistrate was probably sorry. The whole basin runs on sorry and kina."

The longboats were fifty meters out and closing. Forty. The men in them weren't rushing—they knew the geometry of the trap better than we did, the bluff on one side, the drowned forest on the other, the current pushing everything downstream into their arms.

Ilse grabbed my arm. "The forest," she said. "Look at the forest."

I looked. The drowned trees stood in water four feet deep, five, their trunks spaced close enough to thread a canoe through if you were insane, their root systems making a lattice below the surface that would shred any hull that touched it wrong. No motorboat could follow in there. No longboat. It was a maze, and mazes only work if you know them.

"Do you know it?" I asked her.

"I've spent six years mapping channels in this basin." Her eyes were fierce and terrified and absolutely certain. "I know it better than the men who are trying to drown it."

Twenty meters now. A voice through a megaphone, bored and official: "Rafael Brightwater. Ilse Marchetti. Corben Voss. By order of the district administrator, you are detained for questioning regarding stolen cultural property. Come peacefully and no one will be hurt."

Stolen cultural property. Kessler had even gotten the language right. Somewhere a lawyer was earning his retainer.

"Voss," I said. "Can you swim?"

"I can float," he said. "It's basically the same thing."

"It's really not," Ilse said.

The first longboat's bow ground onto the gravel bar ten meters away, and a man stepped out into knee-deep water with a rope coil in his hands, and I made the decision the way you make all the decisions that matter—too fast to be proud of, too slow to undo.

"Now," I said, and put the canoe's nose at the trees, and dug the paddle in like I was trying to kill the river itself.

* * *

The next ninety seconds were the loudest quiet I've ever moved through. Shouting behind us, megaphone distortion shredding words into pure anger, the crack of a warning shot that echoed off the bluff like the sky splitting—and underneath all of it, the sound of our own breathing and the paddle biting water and the trees coming at us out of the mist like a crowd of witnesses.

Then the third shot wasn't a warning.

I heard it differently before I understood it—the flat slap of a round finding something soft instead of something hard—and then Voss, who had dragged himself upright against the gunwale despite everything Ilse and I had said, Voss who weighed nothing anymore and burned like a paper lantern, twisted sideways across the stern with his back to the open water and his arms spread over the oilcloth bundle in my jacket.

He took the second bullet high in the chest, on the side that was already ruined, and the impact folded him over the gunwale and into the flooded forest's brown water, and I stopped paddling because the world stopped, just for a second, just long enough to be unforgivable.

"GO!" Ilse screamed, and her hands replaced mine on the paddle, hauling us deeper into the trees, and I got Voss by the collar and hauled him back over the side—he weighed nothing, nothing, a man reduced to his skeleton and his grievances—and we went into the labyrinth with the shots cracking off trunks around us and the roots grabbing at the hull and Ilse calling turns in a voice stripped down to its wires: "Left—hard left—duck—right, take the right channel—"

Behind us, the longboats reached the edge of the drowned forest and stopped. I heard the engines idling, heard men arguing, heard the particular silence of professionals deciding that a maze full of submerged roots and snipers' angles wasn't worth the hulls. Kessler wanted the ledger alive and intact, but he wanted his boats intact too, and the math favored retreat.

For now.

We ran the channels until the shouting faded and the rain thickened back up and the light went from slate to charcoal, and then we ran them some more, because Ilse said the maze doubled back on itself twice before opening into a lagoon the maps didn't name, and I believed her the way you believe a person holding the only lamp in a cave.

When we finally grounded the canoe on a shelf of roots above the waterline, night had come down like a lid. Voss was lying in the bilge with his eyes open and his breath coming in short, expensive installments, and when I peeled back his shirt the wound was exactly as bad as I'd feared and worse than I'd let myself calculate.

"Hey," he said. His voice was a thread. "Did we lose them?"

"For tonight," Ilse said.

"Good." He smiled, and there was blood in his teeth, and he looked younger than I'd ever seen him, younger than the thief I'd chased across a desert, younger than the man who'd stolen a tube of survey papers and set all of this in motion. "My grandmother," he said, "kept a stone. From the river. When the government declared her land void, she kept the stone in her pocket for forty years and told everybody it was her deed." His laugh cost him; I watched it cost him. "She was right, you know. The stone. The river. All of it. Paper's just what they print when they're afraid of what people remember."

"Don't," I said. "Don't do the speech. Save it for the council house. You're going to be there."

"Rafael." He caught my wrist. His grip was astonishing, for a dying man—ten fingers with a thief's stubbornness in them. "Listen to me. If I don't—"

"You will."

"If I don't. The ledger goes to the councils. Not the courts. Not the feds. Not to whatever archive they'll bury it in and lose the key." His eyes held mine, fever-bright and clear at the center, the way eyes get at the end when everything false has burned off. "Promise me. Swear it on miles. You people from the desert swear on miles, don't you?"

I looked at him. Ten years I'd measured my life in miles logged, cases closed, thieves returned to jurisdictions that processed them like freight. And here was the last of them, bleeding out in a canoe in a drowned forest, asking me to swear an oath on the only currency I'd ever actually earned.

"On miles," I said. "I swear it."

He nodded once, satisfied, and let go of my wrist, and Ilse crawled to him and began the work of keeping him alive through the night with boiled water and pressure and the kind of ferocious tenderness that hydrologists apparently keep in reserve for emergencies.

I sat in the bow with my back against a trunk and the ledger against my chest and listened to the rain and counted the costs, because that's what trackers do when the running stops: we count.

One canoe, holed at the seam, held together with resin and prayer. Two days of food, soaked. One rifle, fourteen rounds. One guide's family fed and freed and one guide's soul, I suspected, going to be heavy for a long time. One bullet spent into Corben Voss's chest that had been aimed, with perfect professional economy, at the book.

That was the number that stayed with me. Not the ambush, not the betrayal—those were just weather in this country, the climate of what Kessler was buying. The number was the trajectory. A hired man on a longboat had drawn a bead through rain and dusk and fired not at the man holding the ledger but at the man shielding it, because Kessler's orders valued the paper over the people carrying it, and somewhere in a barge under a generator light, a man who had once believed development could be honest was sleeping fine with that arithmetic.

Ilse settled beside me near midnight, wrung out, her hands shaking in the way hands shake after they've been steady too long. She leaned her head on my shoulder, and neither of us said anything for a while, because the forest was listening and grief talks loud.

"He shielded it," she finally whispered. "He turned his body into a wall for a book."

"I know."

"People aren't supposed to do that, Rafael. Books are supposed to be for people. That's the whole point of books." Her voice cracked on the last word, and I put my arm around her and felt her breathe.

Somewhere downstream, the barge light burned. Somewhere ahead, through forty miles of flooded country, the council house waited on its high ground with nine communities sheltering inside it. And somewhere between those two points lay a gap we had to cross carrying a dying man, a sworn promise, and a ledger that everyone with power in this basin would burn the forest down to bury.

Voss's breathing evened out into something like sleep, or something like the country just past sleep. Ilse drifted against my shoulder. The rain kept on, patient as a creditor.

Forty miles. One night and part of another. I closed my eyes and did the only navigation left to me.

I swore again, silently, on miles.
