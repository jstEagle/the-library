## 15. Voss's Last Mile

The bullet went through Corben Voss somewhere below the ribs and above the hip, which is the kind of geography a body does not forgive. I knew it before I touched him. I've spent eleven years reading water—where it pools, where it drains, where it refuses to go—and blood obeys the same rules. His was pooling wrong.

We carried him into the mangroves because carrying him anywhere else was impossible. The flooded forest took us in like it takes everything: without opinion. Knee-deep water, roots like the knuckles of some enormous patient hand, and the light coming down through the canopy in green coins that trembled every time Rafael shifted his grip. He had Voss over his shoulders in a fireman's carry he'd clearly done a hundred times, and he didn't say a word for two hours, which told me more about him than any confession had.

I brought up the rear with the ledger wrapped in oilcloth against my chest, and I will admit—at the risk of ruining whatever dignity this account has—that I spent those two hours doing arithmetic instead of feeling anything. Blood loss by body weight. Time since injury. Distance to the council house at the high ground. I ran the numbers four times and got the same answer each time, and the answer was *no*, and so I stopped running them and started looking for dry ground instead, because a hydrologist who can't find a river person a dry place to die has no business calling herself one.

We found a root island—a tangle of mangrove buttress big enough for three bodies and a book—and Rafael laid Voss down on it with a gentleness that didn't match the rest of him at all.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about watching a man die in a mangrove swamp at the end of the world: it is mostly waiting, and the waiting has a sound. Water moving through roots. Insects conducting their indifferent business. Somewhere far off, Kessler's boats, still sweeping, their engines a dull heartbeat we all three pretended not to hear.

Voss surfaced from unconsciousness around what I judged to be four in the morning, though dawn in the basin during monsoon is less an hour than a rumor. His eyes found Rafael first. Of course they did. Whatever history those two had—the decade of pursuit, the thief and the ranger—it had apparently curdled into something else on the river, something neither of them had signed paperwork for.

"Still chasing me?" Voss said. His voice was a dry leaf.

"You're going the wrong way," Rafael said. "You always were."

"I was going home." Voss tried to laugh and it turned into a cough, and the cough cost him something visible, some coin he couldn't mint again. "My grandmother used to say that. Going home. She said it about a village she hadn't seen in sixty years. Wagu-side. Before—" He gestured vaguely, and the gesture seemed to encompass the ledger, the flood, the whole colonial century. "Before the paper people decided she'd never lived anywhere."

I looked away. Not out of delicacy. Out of recognition. I'd heard that sentence before, in other mouths, in other villages along these tributaries, usually delivered flatly, the way you deliver facts you've carried so long they've worn smooth. *The paper people.* Eleven years of salinity readings and flow gauges, and the most important data in this basin had never been chemical. It was this: who got to be written down, and who got written out.

"Marchetti." Voss had turned his head toward me. Even dying, the man tracked like a surveyor. "You know the rites."

It wasn't a question. It was also, I realized, not small talk. It was a request filed in advance.

"I know some," I said. "The old woman at Torembi taught me. For the flood season. When the ground won't take you, you give the body to the water, and you tell the water its name."

"The water knows my name already," Voss said. "It's been trying to collect me since the rapids." Then his eyes came back to Rafael, and everything casual fell out of his voice. "Brightwater. Listen to me. I don't have the time to make this complicated, so I'll make it simple."

Rafael crouched. I watched him do it—the deliberate lowering of himself, ranger to prisoner, ten years of miles collapsing into two feet of mangrove mud—and I understood that whatever Rafael Brightwater had been running from when I met him, he had just stopped.

"The ledger doesn't go to the courts," Voss said. "Promise me. Not Port Moresby. Not the federal archive. Not any building with a lock on it, because every lock in this country has a Meridian key. You take it to the councils. The nine tributaries. You put it in the hands of people whose names are *in* it, and you let them decide. That's the whole job. That's the last mile."

"The courts can prosecute—"

"The courts are where Crane sent the men he bought," Voss said. "You read the entries. Page forty. Page sixty-one. Every verdict he purchased was *certified*, Brightwater. Stamped. Filed. The system worked perfectly, every single time, for exactly the people it was built to work for. You want to hand the cure back to the disease and hope it feels bad?"

Rafael didn't answer right away. I watched his jaw work through the things he wanted to say—duty, procedure, the liturgy of the commission he still wore folded in his breast pocket like a saint's medal—and then I watched him put all of it down.

"I promise," he said. "The councils. Not the courts."

"Not the courts," Voss repeated, as if sealing it. And then, quieter, almost amused: "Ten years you chased me across the desert for stealing things. Now I'm making you steal one. Feels different from this side, doesn't it?"

"It feels the same," Rafael said. "That's the problem."

Voss smiled. It was a real smile, crooked and tired, and it was the last fully present thing he did for a while. He drifted after that, in and out, muttering sometimes in a language I half-recognized from the upper tributaries—his grandmother's language, kept alive in one mouth for sixty years like a coal carried under ash.

* * *

Dawn came the way it comes in the flooded forest: not as light but as a change in the quality of the dark, the black going green, the green going silver, the water turning from ink to pewter. The rain had thinned to a fine drifting mist. Somewhere a bird started up with a call like a hinge that needed oiling, and I thought, absurdly, that if I survived this week I would never again be able to hear that bird without my chest tightening.

Voss died at first light, between one breath and the next, without drama, the way a river changes course—not an event but a fact, noticed only afterward. Rafael was holding his hand. I don't think either of them planned it. I think Rafael reached out to check a pulse and then simply didn't let go for a while, because letting go would have made it official, and there is a version of mercy in delay.

I gave him five minutes. Then I said, "If we're doing this, we do it now. The water's rising and Kessler's sweep pattern puts him within two klicks by mid-morning."

Rafael looked up at me, and I saw something in his face I'd seen once before, years ago, on a colleague in the Kimberley who'd just gotten news from home and hadn't yet decided whether to fall apart or function. Functioning, it turns out, is the more common choice, and the worse one.

"We bury him," Rafael said. "Properly."

"There's no ground," I said. "There's water and roots. So we do it the way Torembi does it. Flood rite. Listen, because I'll only say this once and then we do it."

I had learned the rite eight years earlier, during a salinity survey up the Sepik's middle reaches, from a woman named Yolanda—no, that wasn't her name, her name was Mereane, but I have spent years translating her into forms I could carry, and I suppose this account is one more translation. She taught it to me half as anthropology and half as scolding, because I'd asked her, with the arrogance of a woman holding a clipboard, how the villages coped with losing people to the river. She said: *we don't lose them to the river. We return them. Losing is what your people do, with your files and your fences.*

So here is what we did.

Rafael waded out from the root island until the water reached his chest, and he unwrapped the canvas groundsheet from his pack—the same pack that had lost most of its contents in the rapids, the pack that had become, over six days, a metaphor I refused to examine—and he spread it flat on the water. We laid Voss on it. I had washed his face with river water, which sounds sentimental and was actually practical, and I had closed his eyes, which was harder than it should have been, because his eyes were the kind that seemed to keep working even shut.

Then I spoke the words Mereane gave me, in the trade tongue she insisted on, because, she said, the dead understand every language; it's the living who need the right one.

"*Old man of the water, we bring you one of ours. He came from the high country and he returns by the low road. Take him gently. Tell the current his name: Corben. Son of a daughter of this basin. He carried a heavy thing a long way, and he set it down. Let the water carry what his hands cannot.*"

And then, because Mereane had also taught me that a rite without truth is just theater, I added the part she'd called the *telling*—the public accounting of the dead person's debts and gifts, so the community could weigh them honestly.

"He stole," I said. "Papers, mostly. Things that belonged to other people. He lied to us both, at the start, and he'd have let us drown rather than lose the tube, I think, in the early days." Rafael glanced at me but didn't contradict it. "And he carried a book through a war zone to give it back to people who couldn't pay him for it. He took a bullet standing between that book and the men who wanted it. Both of those are true. Weigh them together."

The current took the groundsheet slowly, turning it once, twice, drawing it out past the root line into the brown floodwater, and the mist closed behind it, and that was that. A man becomes a direction. That's all a burial is, in the end—converting a body into a bearing.

Rafael stood chest-deep in the water long after the sheet was gone, and I thought he was praying, or composing himself, or doing whatever desert rangers do instead of crying. Then I heard him speak, low, and I moved closer because I am a bad person and a curious one.

"—logged every mile," he was saying. "Ten years. Forty-one thousand, two hundred and six miles of patrol. I know because I wrote them down. Every one. And I could not tell you a single thing about a single person I buried in all that time. Names, dates, cause of death. File numbers." His voice caught on something. "Corben Voss. Thief. Grandmother from Wagu Bend. Wanted to go home. Got there."

He waded back to me, and his eyes were wet, and he did not wipe them, and he did not apologize for them, and I understood that this was the bravest thing I would ever see him do, braver than the church ruins, braver than whatever was coming at the council house. A man who measures his life in miles had just measured it in a person instead, and the number had broken him open.

"I buried strangers for a decade," he said. "I'm not doing that anymore."

"No," I said. "You're not."

* * *

We moved within the hour. Grief is a luxury the flood does not extend credit for. But something had changed in the mechanics of us—I felt it in the way Rafael checked the oilcloth wrapping on the ledger before handing it to me, the way he now touched the book itself, briefly, like a man taking an oath with his palm instead of his hand.

We went upstream, against logic, toward the high ground and the council house of the nine tributaries, because the promise had a destination now and promises are the only cargo that gets heavier the farther you carry it.

Halfway through the morning, Rafael stopped on a root shelf, listening, and held up a fist. Through the rain I heard it too: engines, plural, downstream—but also, faintly, upstream, ahead of us, a sound I hadn't heard in days and had almost stopped hoping for.

A drum. The long-signal pattern, the one the villages use when the water speaks too loudly for voices. Three beats, pause, seven beats, pause. And every stilt village between us and the high ground answering it, relay by relay, the message traveling faster than any boat.

Rafael looked at me. "What are they saying?"

I translated, and my voice cracked on the last word, because I had spent eleven years measuring this river and had somehow missed the fact that it had been talking the entire time.

"They're saying," I said, "*the book is coming. Make the way ready.*"

Someone upstream already knew. Word had outrun us, the way it always does here—through the drum lines, through the flood-season kinship networks, through whatever quiet miracle moves truth through a drowned forest faster than men with engines can move lies. The councils were waiting.

The last mile, it turned out, was the only mile anyone had ever cleared for us.
