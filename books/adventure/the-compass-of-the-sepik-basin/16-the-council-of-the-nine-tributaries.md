## 16. The Council of the Nine Tributaries

### Rafael

The council house sat on a spine of high ground above the flooded plain, a long building of blackened timber and iron roofing, and it was already full when we arrived—full the way a drum is full, with nine river communities pressed into it and more coming by dugout every hour. I had seen refugee camps before. I had never seen a courtroom built out of a flood.

We came up the last stretch of the trail with Voss's blood still on my shirt and the ledger wrapped in oilcloth against my chest, and a boy of maybe fourteen stopped us at the door with a spear he held like he'd only recently been told which end was which.

"They're expecting us," Ilse said.

"Everyone's expected," the boy said. "That's the problem."

Inside, the smell was woodsmoke and wet clothing and betel nut, and the noise was tremendous—until it wasn't. It went quiet the way water goes still, all at once and from the edges inward, and then nine communities were looking at us and I understood that word had traveled faster than the flood. Word always does. It's the one current nobody can dam.

An old man rose from the center bench. He was small and straight-backed, with a white beard cropped close and a walking stick he clearly used for pointing rather than leaning. Behind him, arranged along the wall like the instruments of a trade, sat the others: nine elders, or what remained of nine elders' delegations, some communities having sent one man where they'd have sent three in a drier year.

"I am Yawan," he said. "Of the Keram. You are the ranger who burned his boat."

"I didn't burn it," I said. "The river did. I just didn't argue."

Someone near the back laughed, briefly, and was hushed. Yawan didn't smile. "You carry the Crane book."

It wasn't a question, so I didn't answer it. Instead I unwrapped the oilcloth and set the ledger on the table in the center of the room, and I watched nine communities look at a dead colonial magistrate's handwriting the way you'd look at a crocodile surfacing in your drinking water.

"This book," Yawan said, "has cost how many lives so far?"

"One that I know of," I said. "A man named Corben Voss. His grandmother was born at Wagu Bend."

The silence changed quality. Somewhere in the room, a woman began to weep, quietly, the way people weep when they've had practice.

"Then you understand," Yawan said, "why the first thing we must decide is not what is written in it. The first thing we must decide is whether it should have ever been found."

* * *

### Ilse

I had sat through enough academic conferences to recognize a debate about to go badly: the moment when everyone agrees the truth matters and nobody agrees what it's for. The council house filled with it. A man from the Sepik's middle reaches—Bumbu, they called him, a heavyset elder with a magistrate's own spectacles perched on his nose, which struck me as either irony or defiance—wanted the ledger copied and sent to every school in the basin. A younger woman, Lorna, who spoke for a community downriver whose name I'd seen on Kessler's purchase contracts, wanted it in the hands of lawyers in Port Moresby before the flood even receded. And at the far end of the bench sat an old woman who had not spoken since we entered, and who, when Yawan finally turned to her, said only:

"Burn it."

Her name was Kambiri. She was the one who had recited her people's boundaries from memory to Rafael in the stilt village, and I had thought, watching her then, that she was the strongest person in the basin. I thought it again now, for a different reason.

"Everything that book touches has bled," Kambiri said. "The magistrate bled us writing it. The thief bled carrying it. The ranger bled defending it. You want to know what's in it? I'll tell you what's in it. Our names. Our grandmothers' names, written down by the man who stole from them, in his language, for his purposes. And you want to pass it around like a gift." She looked at me, and I felt the look all the way down. "The water woman. You've read it. Tell me I'm wrong. Tell me there's a page in there where we are not property."

I couldn't. I had read it through the night in the church ruin with Rafael's shoulder warm against mine and the cipher resolving line by line, and I could recite the entries that had lodged in me like shrapnel: *Purchased the assent of the Keram headman, two pounds and a bolt of calico. Deed of the Wagu Bend lands executed in the name of a man who had been dead four months. Verdict in the matter of the drowning at Ambunti, bought, twelve pounds.* Aldous Crane had kept honest books about dishonest work, and every honest line was a wound dressed in neat colonial script.

"There's no page where you're not property," I said. "But there are pages where he admits it. In his own hand. With dates and amounts and the names of everyone he paid. That's not the same thing as nothing."

"It's the same thing with more paper," Kambiri said.

And the room murmured, because she was right, and everyone in that house had spent a hundred years being told that paper was truth, paper was law, paper was the thing that erased them—and here was more paper, arriving wet and bloodstained, promising to fix what paper broke.

* * *

### Rafael

They argued until the lamps burned low. I mostly listened, which was new for me; a decade of ranger work teaches you to talk first and listen on the move. But this wasn't my decision, and for once I knew it. I'd spent ten years believing that my job was to carry things to the proper authorities—stolen goods, stolen people, stolen time—and the proper authorities had already shown me, through a radio handset in the rain, exactly what they'd do with this book. So I sat on a bench with my ruined hands folded and I listened to nine communities decide their own future, and it was the hardest and easiest thing I'd ever done.

The arguments circled. Publish and be damned, or bury and be safe. Lorna's faction wanted the courts; Bumbu's wanted the schools; Kambiri's wanted fire. And underneath it all ran the fear nobody named until a young man near the door—too young to be an elder, old enough to have already lost land to a contract—stood up and said it plainly:

"Kessler's boats are still on the river. Whatever we choose, they're still on the river."

That was the truth of it, and the truth went through the room like cold water. The ledger was a weapon, but weapons don't aim themselves, and the men who wanted it back were upstream of every one of us, with radios and rifles and the patience of men who are paid by the month.

Yawan let the silence sit for a long time. Then he looked at me.

"Ranger. You carried this thing through a flood and a bullet. You've earned a voice, if not a vote. Speak."

I stood up. My legs were stiff from the mangroves and my shirt still had Voss's blood on it, and I was aware, distantly, that I was about to argue against both my training and my own worst instincts, and that neither of those things had ever stopped me before.

"I was sent to retrieve stolen government property," I said. "That's what this book is, on paper. Federal custody. Evidence. Chain of command." I looked at Lorna. "And I'll tell you what federal custody looks like. It looks like a warehouse in Port Moresby. It looks like a case number. It looks like ten years of motions, and at the end of it, a judge who owns shares, or a clerk who owes a favor, or a fire in the archive that nobody investigates. I've spent a decade delivering things to that system and watching it digest them. It digests everything. That's what it's for."

"Then the courts get nothing," Lorna said. "And Kessler's contracts stand."

"No," I said. "That's the trap. It's not courts or nothing. It's not publish or burn. Kambiri is right that this book was written to own you. So don't let it be a relic. Don't put it on a shelf and worship it and wait for it to save you—books don't save anyone, people do. And don't burn it, because the day after you burn it, Kessler's lawyers will stand in a courtroom and say there was never any proof, just rumors, just grieving people, and they'll be right, because you burned the proof."

I put my hand flat on the ledger. It was still damp at the edges. It smelled of the cave and the river and the church ruin and, faintly, of blood.

"Use it," I said. "That's the dangerous middle path, and it's dangerous because it means work. Copy it—every page, tonight, by every lamp in this house, and send the copies in nine directions, not one, so no single road or river or office can make it disappear. Read it aloud in every village, in your own languages, with your own names put back where Crane wrote you as property. Let the old women who remember the boundaries stand next to the pages and say *this entry is a lie, and here is the truth, and I am the proof.* Then take it to the courts if you want—flood them with it, from nine directions at once, so they can't bury nine copies with one fire. But don't hand them the original. The original stays here. In this house. Under this council's guard. Not because the paper is sacred. Because *you* are, and this is the only building in the basin where that's ever been the law."

I sat down. My heart was going like a drum, and I couldn't tell if it was the speech or the malaria I probably had.

* * *

### Ilse

For a long moment nobody spoke, and I watched Rafael misread the silence as failure, the way he misread most silences, and I put my hand over his on the bench and felt him startle like a man touched in his sleep.

It was Kambiri who broke it.

"The ranger talks like a man who has buried a lot of things," she said.

"Yes," Rafael said.

"And he is right." She said it the way you'd admit to a debt. "I wanted to burn it because burning is clean. Burning is one act and it's finished. What he is proposing is not finished. It's the beginning of a fight that will outlive everyone in this room." She looked down the bench at the other elders, one by one. "But I have been reciting my grandmother's boundaries from memory for forty years because there was no paper. My memory will die with me. The book will not. Fine. We use it. But I say this, and I want it entered into the record of this council, if we keep such a thing." Her voice didn't shake. "The book is evidence. It is not history. Our history is in the old women. Any copy that leaves this house goes with a speaker attached. Paper and voice together, or not at all."

Bumbu raised his hand. "Seconded."

Lorna, after a moment, raised hers. "Thirded. And the copies to Port Moresby go to the newspapers first and the courts second. The courts follow the newspapers here. Everyone knows it."

Yawan looked down the bench. "Those in favor of the ranger's path—copy, speak, distribute, and keep the original under this roof—raise your hands."

Hands went up along the whole bench. Kambiri's came up last, slowly, like something being unclenched.

"Carried," Yawan said, and struck the floor once with his stick, and the sound went through the house like a heartbeat.

Then the house became a workshop. Lamps were fetched, paper was found—a mission school's supply of exercise books, of all things, rescued from the flood—and the copying began, elders and teenagers bent over the ledger in shifts, transcribing Crane's neat colonial hand into nine languages' worth of notes, while runners were sent down to the canoes with oilskin-wrapped packets and instructions that sounded like a military dispatch: nine directions, no manifest, tell no one the routes.

I stood at the edge of it with Rafael, watching, and he said, quietly, "I gave a speech."

"You did."

"I've never given a speech. I've given *directions.*"

"It was a good speech."

"It was a dangerous speech. I told them to fight a war with a book."

"You told them to fight a war with themselves," I said. "The book is just the ammunition they already were."

He looked at me then, tired and soaked and half-smiling, and I thought: this man, this exact man, in this exact flood, and no other.

* * *

### Rafael

The copying went on past midnight. Around two in the morning, Yawan came and sat beside me on the bench with two cups of something hot and terrible and handed me one.

"My son works for the consortium," he said.

I didn't say anything. There wasn't anything to say that wouldn't be worse than silence.

"He drives a boat. He tells himself it is just a boat, just wages, just work. His children eat because of it." Yawan drank. "When your Kessler's men come up this hill—because they will come, the ranger; a man does not spend that much money to lose a book and then go home—he will be in one of those boats."

"Yawan—"

"I am not asking you to promise me anything. I am an old man on a hill. I am telling you so that you know the cost has already begun to be counted, and it is being counted in families, not in pages." He set down his cup. "You asked them to use the book and not worship it. Good. But understand what you have done. You have made every house on this hill a target and every copy a fuse. Sleep if you can. I would not."

He went back to the table, and I sat with my terrible drink and watched the lamps and the copying hands, and out past the door the rain had stopped, which I did not like at all. Rain I understood. The flood I understood. But a stopped rain in monsoon season meant the sky was reloading.

Ilse came and stood beside me, following my eyes to the door, to the black and listening dark beyond it.

"You heard him," she said. "About his son."

"I heard him."

"Then you know what's coming."

I did. And downriver, faint under the sound of the water, so faint I might have invented it, came the low, patient thrum of an outboard motor that was not in any hurry at all.
