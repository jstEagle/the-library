## 17. Flood War

The water told us first.

I'd spent ten years reading ground—sand, scree, the way a trail bent around a deadfall—but the river spoke a different language, and by the seventeenth day of the monsoon I was starting to dream in it. Ilse woke me before dawn, her hand on my shoulder, and said only: "It's coming up the wrong channel."

I was on the council house veranda in under a minute, boots in one hand, rifle in the other, feeling like a man who'd carried a gun his whole life and only just learned what it was for. The high ground at the Council of the Nine Tributaries rose maybe forty feet above the flooded plain, a knuckle of earth where the council house stood on stilts older than the colonial administration that had tried to erase it. Around us, the basin had become a single brown sea dotted with treetops, and the villages that had gathered here—nine communities, four hundred souls, one book worth killing for—lay in canoes and lean-tos like flotsam that had decided to organize.

The water was coming up the wrong channel. That was the thing. Ilse had spent three weeks mapping the flood's behavior the way other people read scripture, and she knew which channels should carry what. This surge—two feet overnight, cold, carrying red clay from highlands that shouldn't have been feeding this side of the basin—meant someone upstream had opened or closed something. A diversion gate. A levee cut. The same engineering Kessler had used to drown boundary markers was now being used to push a wall of water at us.

"They're not just coming for the ledger," Ilse said. She stood at the rail in the gray light, hair tied back, binoculars hanging from her neck like a stethoscope. "They're moving the river to do it."

"Can they?"

"They already did. Look at the bend." She handed me the binoculars. Two kilometers out, where the Wagu channel met the old river, the current had reversed. "That's not a flood. That's a decision."

I handed the binoculars back and looked at the ledger instead. It sat inside the council house in a waterproof drum, under guard of three elders and a fourteen-year-old boy named Tomas who had appointed himself to the position with the kind of absolute authority only a fourteen-year-old can muster. Crane's cipher had been cracked, the key pages photographed, the transmissions sent—Ilse's work, done in the chaos of the last assault's aftermath, and it had changed nothing about tonight. Kessler's leverage was dead in the open air, but Kessler's men were still in the basin, and a man with forty hired guns and nothing left to lose doesn't retreat. He doubles down. He burns the evidence he can no longer own.

The ledger was the evidence. And we were standing on the only high ground for twenty miles.

* * *

Old Serafim ran the defense the way he ran everything—slowly, and then all at once.

He was the eldest of the council, a man whose arms carried the faded geometry of tattoos that predated every map in Crane's ledger, and when I'd first suggested, days ago, that Kessler's men might come for the council house itself, he had listened, nodded, and said nothing. I'd taken that for disagreement. It was consideration. That afternoon, while the rain came down in sheets, he'd walked the flooded perimeter with me and pointed out, with the patience of a man describing his own hand, the channels beneath the water.

"You see the surface," he said. "I see the bones."

The bones were this: the knoll we stood on had once been an island, before the government road and the logging cuts changed the drainage. The old channels still ran beneath the flood—deep slots of faster water, invisible from above, winding through the brown sea like veins through a wrist. Serafim knew them the way I knew the desert's arroyos. Where the water looked calm, it was deep and slow. Where it looked dangerous, it was often knee-deep over a sandbar. And in three places—Serafim called them the throats—the old channels pinched together and ran fast enough to flip a loaded boat.

"They will come at night," Serafim said. "In the rain. They will come with engines, because engines are what men trust when they are afraid of the dark."

"And the water?"

"The water," he said, "will do most of the work. We will only have to finish it."

We spent the day preparing. Not fortifying—there was nothing to fortify, a stilt house on a mound in a drowned world—but positioning. Canoes at the throats, paddled by young men who'd grown up poling these channels before they could swim. Fishing nets weighted with stones, sunk across the two widest approaches where the water was shallow enough to run a boat's propeller into them. Ilse rigged her remaining instruments—a depth sounder, a current meter, a salvaged marine radio—into a listening post on the veranda, tracking the surge and calling out changes in the flow like a woman reading a pulse.

I posted myself where I was most useful, which was nowhere and everywhere: moving between positions, checking lines of sight, doing the thing I'd done for a decade in the desert. Waiting. Watching. Measuring.

But this time I wasn't measuring miles. I was measuring something that mattered.

* * *

They came at 2 a.m., in the rain, with engines.

Ilse heard them before anyone—three outboards, throttled low, running the Wagu channel toward the knoll's southern approach. Her instruments had already told us the surge had peaked; the water stood four feet up the council house stilts, chest-high in the shallows, and the flood plain between us and the tree line was a mile of open brown water.

"They're running the deep channel," she said, headphones half off one ear. "The one Serafim said flips boats."

"Kessler's men don't know the bones."

"Kessler's men have a map." She looked at me. "Somebody gave them one."

I thought about the guide at Wagu Bend, the man who'd sold us for his family's debt, and I thought about how many other men in the basin were holding purchase contracts over their heads. You don't need to betray a whole people if you only have to betray one channel.

"Doesn't matter," I said. "Serafim's map is older."

The first boat hit the throat at 2:17.

I didn't see it happen—I heard it. An engine climbing, climbing, then the sound every river man knows, the hollow *clunk* of a propeller biting air, then screaming, then the net, then the water taking the rest. The rain swallowed most of it. What came through was a single flashlight beam sweeping wild across the flood, a voice shouting in a language I half knew, and then nothing—just the current, doing the work Serafim had promised, dragging men and aluminum into the deep slot where the old river ran.

The second boat turned. Smart. They'd lost the surprise, and whatever Kessler was paying them, it wasn't enough to die blind in the dark. They fell back toward the tree line, and for twenty minutes there was nothing but rain and the sound of my own heart and Ilse's hand finding mine in the dark, squeezing once, letting go.

Then they came at the knoll itself—on foot, or what passed for it. Chest-deep water, moving in a spread line from the eastern shallows where the flood had made a beach of the old road grade. Twelve of them, maybe fifteen, wading with rifles held high, using the water the way they'd planned to use it all along: as cover, as noise, as a way to come at us without boats to net.

"Eastern shallows," Ilse called. "They found the sandbar."

I found Serafim in the dark and told him, and the old man smiled in a way that made my spine cold.

"Good," he said. "The sandbar ends."

* * *

I won't pretend it was clean. Nothing about that night was clean.

The fighting, when it came, was the ugliest thing I've ever been part of, and I include a decade of chasing men through country that wanted us both dead. It was chest-high water and muzzle flash and hands. It was Tomas—fourteen, armed with a fish gaff and more courage than sense—being dragged back from the veranda rail by two elders while he screamed at the men in the water. It was a Meridian man no older than my brother would have been grabbing my ankle from below the surface, and the two seconds of pure animal terror before I understood he wasn't trying to drown me, he was trying to *climb me*, use me as an island, and I put the rifle stock across his hands because the alternative was us both going under.

It was Ilse at the radio, calling coordinates in three languages, directing the canoe crews through the throats by instrument and memory while bullets chewed the veranda posts above her head. It was Serafim standing on the lowest step of the council house in the open, in the rain, in the line of fire, singing—actually singing, a low river song, a boundary song, the kind the old woman in the stilt village had recited from memory when the paper erased her land—and the men in the water hesitating, because no one had told them what to do about an old man singing in the dark.

The eastern shallows did what Serafim promised. The sandbar ended, and the lead men walked off the edge of the world into the old channel, and the ones behind them stopped, and in that stopping—knee-deep, bunched, blind—the canoe crews hit them from the flanks with paddles and gaffs and the flat, brutal efficiency of people defending the only dry ground in a drowned world.

I fired twice. I'm not sure at what. In chest-high water at night, in the rain, you don't fight a battle—you survive a series of accidents, and you pray the accidents happen to the other man.

It lasted forty minutes. It lasted a year.

* * *

Gray light came, and with it the count.

Three of Kessler's men dead, pulled from the throats and laid out on the council house floor under woven mats, because even for enemies these people had rites. Seven missing, presumed taken by the old channel—the bones keeping what the bones keep. Two villagers wounded, neither mortally. One canoe split. Tomas with a gash across his forearm he would describe for the rest of his life as a sword wound, and no one had the heart to correct him.

And one prisoner. The man who'd climbed me. Mid-twenties, shivering so hard his teeth chattered between words, sitting wrapped in a blanket by the cook fire with a bowl of something hot in his hands, because the council had decided that a man who'd been in the water that long had suffered enough before whatever came next.

I crouched in front of him. Up close, in daylight, he wasn't a Meridian man. He was just a kid with a company contract and a company rifle and the hollow look of someone who'd heard his friends go under the night before and couldn't stop hearing it.

"Kessler," I said. "Where?"

The kid looked at the fire. "Gone."

"Gone where?"

"He left at midnight. Before." His voice cracked. "He knew. He told us to hold the house till the water dropped, and he took the helicopter pad at the mine camp, and he—" The kid stopped. Looked up at me, and there it was, the thing I'd spent ten years seeing in the faces of men I chased: not evil. Just a man who'd finally understood the shape of the hole he'd dug. "He said the ledger doesn't matter anymore. He said it's already everywhere. He said—" the kid swallowed— "he said the only thing left worth taking is the person who sent it."

Ilse. The broadcast had come from her uplink, her hands, her name on the transmission logs.

I stood up. Across the compound, Ilse was at her instruments, logging the falling water, and the morning light caught her face, and I thought: *he's not running from the ledger anymore. He's running from her.*

Serafim came to stand beside me, following my gaze.

"The water is dropping," the old man said. "In two days, the channels open. In three, the boats run."

I looked at the flood plain, brown and vast and finally, grudgingly, beginning to fall. Somewhere out there, a helicopter pad. A man who'd moved a river and lost. And a woman on my left who had made herself the last loose end in the Sepik Basin.

"In three days," I said, "we'd better not be here."
