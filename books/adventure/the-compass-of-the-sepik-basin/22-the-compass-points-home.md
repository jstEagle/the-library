## 22. The Compass Points Home

**Rafael**

The survey office sits on stilts above the Karawari's north bank, which means that for three months of the year it is a boat and for the other nine it is a building with opinions about the weather. We built it ourselves—council timber, council labor, a roof Ilse insisted on pitching steep enough to shed the monsoon like an insult. Over the door, someone painted a compass rose. Not my request. The elders decided it was funny: the man who spent ten years measuring miles, given a compass that points at his own front door.

I have learned to let the river people have their jokes. They've earned them.

This morning the river is low and brown and moving with the unhurried confidence of something that has already won every argument it will ever be in. I'm on the veranda with a drafting board across my knees, redrawing the boundary between two garden plots that the flood rearranged last season. The old woman from the stilt village—the one who recited her family's boundaries from memory while the paper called her land void—sits beside me correcting my lines with a stick she uses for everything: pointing, scolding, stirring sago, and once, memorably, defending a doorway.

"You've got the bend wrong again," she says.

"I've got the bend right," I say. "The river's got it wrong."

"The river doesn't get things wrong. It gets them *new*." She taps the paper. "Draw what it did, not what you wish it had done."

Ten years ago I would have bristled. Ten years ago I measured distances the way a miser counts coins, and any correction felt like theft. Now I erase the line and redraw it, and she nods once, which from her is a parade.

Ilse comes down the ladder from the instrument loft carrying the flow gauge log against her hip like a rifle. She has the look she gets when the numbers have been talking to her all morning and some of what they said wasn't polite.

"Salinity's up four percent at the second confluence," she announces to no one and everyone. "Somebody upstream is pulling water again."

"Meridian?" I ask.

"Meridian's assets are frozen, their licenses suspended, and their fixer is providing very cooperative testimony to a commission he once tried to drown." She drops onto the bench across from me. "So either it's them through a shell company with a name like 'Sunrise Harmony Ventures,' or it's somebody new who read Kessler's playbook and thought, *there's a career.*"

"Cheerful."

"I'm a hydrologist. Cheerful is a sediment problem." But she's smiling when she says it, and her knee finds mine on the bench, and I remember—not for the first time—that love arrived for us between pages, in a flooded church, under threat of death, and has never once since required better circumstances than a bench and bad news.

The elders call this office many things. Officially it is the Joint Survey Office of the Nine Tributaries Councils. Unofficially, the young people call it *the house where the book lives*, because a certified copy of Crane's ledger rests in a waterproof case beneath the floorboards, and the original—Voss's dying wish honored—circulates among the villages on a schedule set by the councils themselves. Every community keeps it for a month. Every community reads it aloud, the entries translated now into four languages, the forged deeds read alongside the true boundaries they erased. Justice here isn't a verdict. It's a recitation, repeated until the lie forgets its own shape.

I burned my ranger's commission in a tin drum on the day the flood receded. I expected to feel lighter. What I actually felt was taller, somehow—as if I'd been walking bent for a decade and only noticed when I straightened. The badge didn't define me; it had just been pressing on me so long I'd mistaken the pressure for posture.

Now I map boundaries for people whose boundaries were never supposed to exist on paper. It turns out this is what I was always doing out there in the desert, chasing petty thieves across empty miles. I just hadn't known yet what the miles were for.

**Ilse**

I want to record, for whoever reads these logs—and Rafael teases me that I write even my grocery lists as field reports—that one year after the flood, the basin is teaching me humility at a rate I would once have described as unprofessional.

I came here believing water was a system to be corrected. Diverted flows were errors; anomalous tides were symptoms; my job was diagnosis and repair. Then I watched a consortium engineer a flood to wash away boundary markers, and I understood that water is never neutral. It carries whoever aims it. And I watched nine river communities aim it back—using channels their grandmothers knew by heart to break an assault by men with better boats—and I understood something else: the deepest hydrological data in this basin isn't in my instruments. It's in memory. In the old woman's recited boundaries. In the fishing calendars that predicted the cave-tide before my models did.

So now I do both. Gauges and grandmothers. The councils pay for half my instruments; I pay for the other half with the grant money that followed the scandal—three universities competing to be associated with "the Marchetti Method," which is a phrase I find embarrassing and which Rafael finds hilarious and deploys whenever I take myself too seriously.

Which is often. He'd want that noted too.

This morning I found the anomaly at the second confluence, and by noon I'd traced it to a gravel operation up the Yuat tributary—small, unlicensed, the kind of thing that would have been invisible a year ago and is now, thanks to the councils' monitoring network, visible within hours. I radio the council house. By evening there will be six men in a boat and a summons written in three languages. No guns. Just witnesses, and the ledger's precedent, and the simple revolutionary fact that the river people can now prove what was always theirs.

Kessler's trial begins next month in the capital. I am told the galleries are full. I will not attend. I said my piece in a deposition forty pages long, and besides, I have a salinity gradient to babysit.

But I'll admit—one small, unprofessional satisfaction: the prosecution's key exhibit is a photograph of page 114 of Crane's ledger, transmitted from a church ruin during a gun battle, by a woman standing chest-deep in floodwater. My hair looked terrible in every reconstruction photo they've used. Worth it.

**Rafael**

The boy arrives at dusk, poling a dugout against the current with the stubborn rhythm of someone who refuses to admit exhaustion. Young—nineteen, maybe twenty. Government-issue boots gone gray with river mud, a ranger's satchel riding too high on his shoulder the way mine used to, and eyes that sweep the bank in practiced arcs, cataloging exits. I know those eyes. I wore them for ten years. They're the eyes of a man who measures every stranger as either a suspect or a witness, and hasn't yet learned the third category, which is simply: *person.*

He beaches the dugout below our stairs and climbs them two at a time, then stops dead on the veranda when he sees the drafting boards, the gauge cables, the compass rose over the door.

"This the survey office?" he asks. Then, catching himself, squaring his shoulders: "Sir. Ranger Second Class Tomas Ellery, Wewak district. I'm tracking a fugitive."

"Are you," I say.

"Corben Voss."

The name lands in my chest like a stone dropped down a well. I hear it fall a long way before it stops.

Beside me, Ilse goes very still. The old woman with the stick looks from the boy to me and back, reading something in the air, and wisely says nothing.

"He stole documents from a government archive," Ellery continues, producing a warrant that has traveled badly—creased, sweat-stained, the photograph of Voss faded to a ghost. "Sealed survey materials. His trail ends at the coast, then picks up again here, on the river. Witnesses say a man matching his description came through last week heading upstream." He swallows. "My supervisor says the case matters. Something about the original Voss file being reopened, now that the Crane records are public. Turns out the grandfather was mixed up in it somehow. The dispossession lists."

I look at the warrant. Corben Voss is dead. I carried him dying through flooded mangrove forest and buried him at dawn with mud on my hands and a promise in my mouth, and Ilse sang the rites these villages taught her, and I grieved—for the first time in ten years—properly, for a man I'd chased across a country to arrest.

His grandson, apparently, is chasing him still. Chasing the file. Chasing the name. Chasing whatever piece of the story might make the running mean something.

"Sit down, Ranger Ellery," I say.

"I should—"

"Sit. Have water. You poled against the current for six hours; I could see your arms shaking from the garden." He sits, because nineteen-year-olds obey tone before they think about it, and I remember being exactly that obedient, exactly that certain that the next mile would contain the answer.

Ilse brings him water and a plate of sago, and watches him eat with the particular attention she pays to anything she's deciding how to understand.

"Tell me about your fugitive," I say.

Ellery talks. Voss—no relation he knows of, though the surname and the timing suggest otherwise and I keep my face still—worked as an archivist's assistant in Wewak. Pulled sealed colonial-era files using legitimate access codes, copied them, returned most of them, kept fragments. Fled when the audit caught the discrepancy. The boy recites it flatly, professionally, but his voice snags on one detail: "One of the fragments he kept was a survey chart. Coastal. References something called the Magistrate's Ledger." He looks up. "You'd know about that. Everyone does. It's in all the news feeds. The whole reason my supervisor reopened the file."

"Yes," I say. "I'd know about that."

"So you understand why it matters. If Voss's chart connects to the ledger cipher—if the grandfather was on the dispossession lists—then this isn't just theft. It's—" He struggles. "It's like he was trying to finish something. And nobody in the department sees it. They just see stolen papers."

I look at this boy with his gray boots and his careful exits and his hunger to have the case mean more than paperwork, and I feel the full strange weight of the circle closing. A year ago I was him. Ten years ago I was him. Somewhere ahead of him is a tin drum and a match, or maybe not—maybe he finds a different door entirely. That's not mine to choose. Mine is only to hand him what I wish someone had handed me.

"Your man came through eight days ago," I say. "Heading upstream toward the Yuat junction. He bought fuel from the council depot and asked about the old trade routes." This is true—or true enough; the councils' network logged a lone traveler matching the description, and whether he is Voss's grandson, a copyist, or a coincidence, the river will tell him in its own time. "Take the east channel past the second confluence—it's running shallow but passable. Avoid the west cut; the gravel operation there has made the water unpredictable, and the council monitors will stop you anyway if you don't check in." I pull a sheet toward me and sketch the channels quickly, the way the old woman taught me: draw what the river did, not what you wish it had done.

"Thank you, sir." He studies the map, then me. "They said in Wewak you used to be a tracker. Best in the district, before you—" He stops, embarrassed.

"Before I quit," I supply.

"Before you *stayed*, is what the radio piece said." He frowns. "I don't understand that part. Why stay? After everything—the flood, the fighting, all of it. Anywhere else would be quieter."

Ilse makes a sound that is almost a laugh. I ignore her with great skill.

"Can I give you some advice, Ranger Ellery? Unofficial. Off the record."

"Sir?"

"When you find your man—and you will, you're competent, I can see it—when you catch up to whatever it is you're chasing, ask yourself one question before you put the cuffs on. Ask what the chase was *for*. Because I spent ten years logging miles. Thousands of them. I could tell you the distance between any two points in the desert to within a hundred meters, and I couldn't have told you why any single one of them mattered." I tap the sketch I've drawn him, the channels bending around islands that won't exist in five years. "Out there you measure in miles because miles are easy. Miles just accumulate. But nothing accumulates out here except understanding. Measure in years, son. Measure in what changes. The miles will take care of themselves."

He looks at me for a long moment. Nineteen years old, and something behind his eyes shifts—a door unlatching, not opening yet. Doors like that open on their own schedule or not at all.

"Measure in years," he repeats, uncertain, filing it away the way you file a thing you don't understand until suddenly you do.

"And check in with the council monitors," Ilse adds. "All nine tributaries. It's not bureaucracy—it's how the river keeps its own records now. Better than yours, frankly."

"Yes, ma'am."

He stands, shoulders the satchel—still too high; I resist the urge to fix it, mostly—and pauses at the top of the stairs. "The man I'm tracking. If I find him, and if it turns out he's... trying to finish something. Like you said." He hesitates. "What would you do?"

The question hangs over the brown water. Behind me the compass rose catches the last light. Under the floorboards, a certified copy of a dead magistrate's confessions sleeps in its waterproof case, and somewhere upstream the original is being read aloud in a language its author never learned, to people he never imagined would inherit his honesty.

"I'd listen first," I say. "Arrest second. And if listening takes longer than your supervisor likes—" I shrug. "Let it take longer."

He nods slowly, and goes down the stairs, and poles out into the current, heading east toward the shallows, toward his own decade, whatever it becomes.

* * *

**Ilse**

We watch the dugout shrink until the dusk takes it. Rafael stands at the rail with his arms folded, wearing the expression he gets when he's pretending he isn't moved.

"You gave him Voss's trail," I say quietly. "Or something shaped like it."

"I gave him a direction. What he does with it is his." A pause. "Corben spent his whole life trying to give something back. Seems right that the name keeps doing it after him."

"Very tidy."

"I'm a surveyor. Tidy is a boundary problem."

I laugh, and lean into him, and together we watch the first stars come up over the water. Below us the river moves, patient and immense, carrying sago barges and gossip and the dissolved history of mountains, going wherever it decides, redrawing every line we draw for it.

"Salinity's still climbing," I murmur against his shoulder. "Someone's going to need to go up the Yuat tomorrow."

"Mm. Terrible. A whole day on the river."

"We'll need the big boat."

"Tragic." He kisses the top of my head. "First light, then."

Below the floorboards, the ledger sleeps. Above us, the compass rose points—at the door, at the river, at home, which turned out to be the same direction all along.

And far downstream, a boy with gray boots is learning to measure in years.
