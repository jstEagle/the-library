## 1. Six Tides

The river gave up its dead slowly, and it gave up this one grudgingly.

Ivo Okonkwo hung in the green murk forty meters beneath the surface of the Fly, his air hose whispering against his ear like a confessor who never had anything new to say. Below him, the hull of the *Vigilant* stretched away into darkness — a naval survey vessel that had gone down nine years ago with a complement of forty-one souls and a cargo manifest nobody would show him. Above him, the morning light came down in broken columns, bent and scattered by silt, so that the whole drowned world seemed lit by the memory of the sun rather than the sun itself.

He checked his depth gauge. Forty-one meters. The same as yesterday. The same as the day before that.

"Okonkwo, give me a reading." The voice came through the earpiece in his helmet, tinny and impatient — Supervisor Brann, a man who had spent so many years on the surface that Ivo suspected his blood had forgotten how to carry anything but coffee and grievance.

"Forty-one," Ivo said. "Same as yesterday. Same as the day before. The wreck isn't going anywhere, and neither is the number."

A pause on the line. He had learned to hear the pauses. In eleven years of contract diving for the navy's salvage arm, he had learned that the silences between orders carried more weight than the orders themselves. This pause was a long one, and it had a shape he didn't like.

"Log it at thirty-eight."

Ivo turned slowly in the water, though there was nothing to turn toward. The current tugged at his lines like a patient hand.

"Say again, Brann."

"Log it at thirty-eight meters. The survey needs the wreck sitting shallower for the insurance assessment. You know how it works. Deeper water, higher salvage fees, longer contract. The navy wants this wrapped up clean, and clean means thirty-eight."

Ivo looked at his gauge again, at the small brass needle resting honest and unmoving against forty-one. Three meters of water, three meters of paper. It seemed such a small thing. It was never the small things that drowned you; it was the way they accumulated, tide over tide, until one day you looked down and realized you were standing on the seabed of your own compromises and couldn't remember the surface.

"Forty-one," Ivo said.

"Okonkwo—"

"Forty-one is what the gauge says. The gauge doesn't take orders. That's why we use gauges."

Another pause, longer, colder. Then Brann's voice came back flattened, administrative, the voice of a man closing a ledger on a problem. "Come up, Okonkwo. Come up and we'll discuss your attitude on the surface."

Ivo began the slow ascent anyway, hand over hand up the descent line, watching the light strengthen around him, watching the wreck fall away below into the dark where it had kept its secrets for nine years. He thought, as he always did at this depth, of his brother. Of the last time he had seen Kofi — not in the water, not in the river, but on the dock at Port Meridian, seventeen years old and grinning, saying *I'll be back before the rains, Ivo, I promise*, and how the navy had come to the house eight months later with a folded flag and a document that said *lost at sea, circumstances classified*, and how their mother had read that document forty times and never once found the word *sorry* in it.

The surface broke over him in a rush of noise and heat. The barge *Meridian Star* rocked under his boots as the winch hauled him aboard, and there was Brann waiting with his clipboard and his sunburned neck, already talking.

"You're a good diver, Okonkwo. Best numbers I've got. That's why this is difficult." He said it the way men say everything difficult — quickly, so it would be over. "The navy doesn't need a diver who argues with his instruments. The navy needs a diver who *is* his instruments. You understand?"

Ivo pulled off his helmet. The river water ran out of the seams and pooled at his feet, brown and patient.

"I understand the wreck sits at forty-one meters," he said. "I understand you want me to write thirty-eight. And I understand that somewhere in that difference is a reason nobody will give me, the same way nobody would give my mother a reason for what happened to my brother."

Brann's face did something complicated and then settled into nothing at all. "That's not — you're not going to make this personal."

"Everything on this river is personal. That's what the navy's never understood about it."

The supervisor looked at him for a long moment, and Ivo saw something flicker behind the man's eyes that might have been envy — the look of a man who had made every compromise offered to him and could no longer remember what it felt like to refuse one. Then Brann wrote something on his clipboard, and the flicker was gone, and Ivo Okonkwo was unemployed.

"Clear out your bunk by evening tide," Brann said. "Contractor's office will mail your final pay. Don't come back to any navy zone, and don't talk about what you saw down there."

"I saw a wreck at forty-one meters," Ivo said. "That's all I ever saw. That's all anybody ever let me see."

He walked the length of the barge to the small cabin he had occupied for seven months, and he began, without hurry, to pack.

* * *

He counted his life in miles. It was a habit he had fallen into so gradually he couldn't name the year it started, the way you can't name the year a river changed course.

Twenty-two thousand, four hundred and six miles, give or take — the distance he had traveled since the day the navy men came with their folded flag. He kept the count in a waterproof notebook in the breast pocket of his shirt, and he updated it every night like a man saying his prayers. Kilometers he did not count. Kilometers were for surveyors. Miles were for men who were going somewhere, even if they had not yet decided where.

His bunk on the *Meridian Star* had held a sea chest, a diving knife, a photograph of Kofi at sixteen holding a fish nearly as long as himself, and a tin of his mother's tea that he had never opened because opening it would mean it was the last. All of it fit into one canvas bag. That was the whole arithmetic of seven months. He had lived aboard ships and in dockside shacks for eleven years, and everything he owned had fit in one canvas bag for all of them. He sometimes wondered whether he owned the bag or the bag owned him — whether he was a man who traveled or a man who fled, and whether, at this point, there was any measurable difference.

By evening tide he stood on the dock at the navy staging yard with his bag over his shoulder, watching the barges of the salvage fleet lie at anchor like sleeping animals, their floodlights just beginning to bloom against the coming dark. Somewhere in the administration shed, a radio was playing. Somewhere in the water below him, forty meters down, the *Vigilant* waited with whatever it was the navy wanted buried at thirty-eight meters of paperwork.

He had asked, once, early in the contract, why a survey vessel that sank in a charted channel needed eleven months of diving. The contractor's man had smiled and said *recovery of sensitive equipment*, and Ivo had nodded, and neither of them had believed the other for a moment. That was the arrangement. That was always the arrangement. The navy paid, the divers dove, and nobody asked what the river was holding.

A deckhand he half knew — Tomas, a boy with a lazy eye and an honest laugh — came up the dock with a crate of chain and stopped when he saw the bag.

"You're done, then?"

"I'm done."

"They're wrong, you know." Tomas set down the crate and wiped his hands on his trousers, glancing back toward the administration shed. "About the numbers. Everybody knows the wreck's deep. Brann just does what they tell him."

"Everybody knows," Ivo said. "And everybody says nothing. That's the whole navy in one sentence."

Tomas laughed, then didn't. "Where will you go?"

Ivo looked downriver, toward the sprawl of Port Meridian, where the river widened and the lights of the taverns were coming on one by one like a fever breaking out along the shore. He had no plan. He had not had a plan in seventeen years, only a direction and a count of miles.

"Downstream," he said. "For now."

He walked off the navy dock and did not look back, because looking back was a luxury for men who intended to return, and he had stopped intending to return to places a long time ago.

* * *

His shack stood on stilts at the edge of the working docks, three miles downriver from the navy yard, in the stretch of waterfront where the salvage men and the smugglers and the fishermen all drank at the same taverns and pretended not to recognize one another. He had built it himself four years ago from salvaged decking and a roof of corrugated iron, and he had told himself it was temporary, the way he told himself everything was temporary. The stilts had rotted and been replaced. The iron had rusted and been patched. The temporary had developed the deep, settled patience of the permanent, and Ivo had never once acknowledged it.

He lit the lamp, set the kettle on the spirit stove, and sat at his table with the waterproof notebook open in front of him.

Twenty-two thousand, four hundred and six miles. He dipped his pen and added nothing, because today he had traveled only three miles downriver, and it felt wrong to log a day that had ended rather than a day that had moved. He sat looking at the last entry — a run upriver to the navy yard, seven months ago, the day he'd signed the *Vigilant* contract — and he thought about what it meant that the longest entry in the book was the day he'd gone to work for the people who had folded the flag over his brother's empty coffin.

The kettle began to sing. He did not get up.

Outside, the night sounds of the docks assembled themselves: the slap of water against pilings, the groan of moored barges, a drunken argument two taverns down that would end the way all those arguments ended, with apologies and blood in roughly equal measure. Somewhere a ship's horn sounded, long and low, and Ivo felt the old ache in his chest that the horn always produced, the ache of a man standing still while the world moved.

He had been a boy who loved the water. That was the thing nobody understood, the thing the navy had never understood in all its years of employing him. He had not become a diver for the pay, though the pay was good, or for the danger, though there was plenty. He had become a diver because the first time he had gone under — fourteen years old, in the flooded quarry behind his father's house, with a garden hose and a borrowed mask and his brother counting his breaths from the rocks above — the world had finally, finally gone quiet. All the noise of a house with too little money and too much grief, all the noise of being the responsible son, the steady son, the one who stayed — it had all stopped at the surface line, and below it there was only the green silence and his own heart, and he had thought: *this is where I live now. This is my country.*

He had never found a way to say that out loud. So he had counted miles instead, as if distance could stand in for the thing he actually meant.

The kettle sang itself hoarse. He got up finally, made the tea from his own cheap tin — not his mother's, never hers — and carried the cup to the doorway. The dock stretched away into the dark, lamps burning at intervals, water sliding past the pilings with the sound of something being said over and over to no one.

And that was when he saw the old man.

He was coming along the dock in the stumbling, deliberate way of a man who was very sick and very determined, one hand on the rail, the other pressed to his side as if holding something in — or holding something *against* being taken. His shirt was filthy with river mud and something darker, and his white hair stood out around his head like smoke. Fifty meters away, then thirty. The dockhand lamps caught his face, and Ivo saw the fever in it from across the water, the bright, burning, glassy look he had seen on drowning men and dying men and once, memorably, on a naval officer who had just been told what his orders really meant.

The old man's eyes found Ivo in the lamplight of the doorway, and they fixed on him with a terrible clarity, the clarity of a man who has been looking for one particular thing for a very long time and has finally found it.

"You're the diver," the old man said. His voice was wrecked, gravel over rust. "The one they threw off the *Vigilant* job today. Word travels faster than boats on this river."

Ivo set down his cup. "You've got the wrong dock, old man. Go to the mission, they'll—"

"I've got exactly the right dock." The man reached the doorway and stopped, swaying, one hand braced against the post. Up close the fever was worse, and beneath it was something else, something that looked like fear that had been worn so long it had polished smooth. "I've been walking three days to find the right dock. I'm Eben Marsh. I was a prospector on the upper Fly for thirty years, and I am dying — not soon, not eventually, *tonight*, if the river has any say in it — and I have a map that the navy would burn this whole waterfront to get back."

Ivo said nothing. The river said nothing. Somewhere far off, the ship's horn sounded again.

"And I need you to find my daughter," Eben Marsh said, and swayed forward, and Ivo caught him by the arms as the old man's weight came against him, fever-hot, shaking, and lighter than a man his size had any right to be. "Her name is Ada. Find my daughter, diver. The map is yours. The map was never for me anyway. It was always going to be for whoever was stubborn enough to say yes."

The old man looked up at him, and Ivo saw that he was already half gone, his eyes swimming, his knees folding — and still he held on to Ivo's shirt with both fists like a man holding on to a rope over deep water.

"Say yes," Eben Marsh whispered. "Before the tide turns. Just say yes."

And Ivo Okonkwo — who had refused his supervisor that morning, who had refused the navy for eleven years in a hundred small unrecorded ways, who had said *no* so often and so quietly that it had become his whole personality — heard himself say the other word.

"Yes," he said. "All right. Yes."

The old man smiled, and let go, and Ivo carried him inside out of the river wind, not yet knowing that he had just agreed to everything — the map, the wreck, the manifest, the daughter, the six tides that would remake his life from the keel up.

The tide, as it happened, was already turning.
