## 2. The Dying Man's Bargain

The rain came off the Fly in sheets that evening, the kind of rain that made the river and the sky the same gray animal, and Ivo Okonkwo was on his knees under his shack re-caulking a seam that didn't need it, because caulking was better than thinking.

He'd been back from the *Vigilant* site for nine days. Nine days of waking at four-thirty to a silence where there should have been a compressor's heartbeat, of making coffee for one in a pot sized for a crew, of catching himself reading depth gauges in his sleep — numbers he hadn't logged, numbers command had wanted changed, numbers he'd refused to change with nothing more dramatic than a shrug and a *no, sir*. They'd taken his pass, his pay chit, and his berth on the barge with the same bureaucratic tenderness you'd use to put down a dog. No anger. Anger would have meant he mattered.

He measured his life in miles traveled. It was a habit older than his navy years, older than his brother's drowning, maybe older than grief itself: Port Moresby to Daru, Daru upriver, the long hauls out to the reef fields, the ferry crossings, the troop transports, the salvage runs. Somewhere north of forty thousand miles, he figured, if you laid all his traveling end to end. And what did it add up to? A shack on stilts at the edge of a dock nobody used anymore, a tool chest, a dory with a cracked transom, and a head full of wreck sites he was contractually forbidden to discuss.

The seam didn't need caulking. He caulked it anyway.

He heard the man before he saw him — a dragging, wet-footed shuffle along the boardwalk planks, then a cough like something tearing loose at the roots. Ivo set down the caulking iron and came out into the rain.

The prospector stood at the top of his stairs swaying like a mooring buoy in a swell. Old, but not old-old — sixty, maybe, worn down to wire and sinew by decades of river gold. His shirt hung open over a chest slick with fever sweat despite the cold rain, and his eyes had the glassy fixedness of a man looking at something three feet behind the world. A canvas pack hung off one shoulder by a single strap, too heavy for the body carrying it.

"You're the diver," the man said. Not a question. "Okonkwo. The one they threw off the *Vigilant* job."

Ivo went still. That name wasn't in any newspaper. "Who told you that?"

"River told me." The man smiled, and the smile cracked into another cough. He gripped the stair rail, knuckles white as driftwood. "River tells me everything. Been listening to it for thirty years." He took a step down, missed the stair entirely, and Ivo moved without deciding to — caught him under the arms as his legs quit, and together they went down the steps in a controlled collapse, the pack cracking against the boards, the two of them half-falling through Ivo's door into the lamplight.

Up close the fever was obvious — the flushed skin, the rattling breath, the tremor in the hands. Malaria, probably, or something worse riding on its back. There were sores on his shins gone green at the edges. This man had been walking for days on legs that had stopped agreeing to it somewhere back upriver.

"Bed," Ivo said. "There's a bunk."

"No time for beds." But he let himself be lowered onto the cot anyway, and when Ivo peeled the soaked shirt off him the man shivered once, hard, and then lay still with the terrible docility of the very sick. "Quinine," he said. "In the pack. If there's any left. There won't be."

Ivo dug through the canvas pack while the rain hammered the tin roof. Rope, a rusted gold pan, a billy can, dried fish wrapped in banana leaf, a coil of fuse cord — and wrapped in oilcloth, flat and heavy, something rectangular that clinked faintly. He set it aside unopened. Under everything, a small tin of quinine tablets, maybe a dozen left rattling in the bottom.

He crushed two into water and got the man to drink. The prospector drank like it was an assignment, eyes closed, throat working, and then lay back and looked at Ivo with sudden, unnerving clarity.

"Eben Marsh," he said. "Upper Fly country. You'll not have heard of me. Nobody has. That's rather the point of me."

"Ivo Okonkwo."

"I know." Marsh's mouth twitched. "I've been asking after you from Kiunga down. Three weeks. Everyone knows the diver who wouldn't lie about how deep the water is. You're famous, son. Famous the way a dead man's famous — everyone talks about you and no one will look at you."

Ivo sat on the crate across from the cot and said nothing. Outside, the river spoke in its low continuous voice, the voice it had been speaking since before either of them was born.

"They pulled you off the site," Marsh said. "The *Vigilant* site. Because you wouldn't write down false numbers."

"Because I wrote down true ones."

"Same thing, from where they sit." Marsh turned his head and looked at the ceiling, at the water stains spreading there like continents. "You know what's down there. You've been inside her."

"I know what my contracts say."

"Your contracts say nothing. That's what I'm telling you." Fever climbed back into his voice, gave it an edge. "I spent eleven years learning what your contracts don't say. Eleven years, Okonkwo. I panned every bar from D'Albertis Junction to the falls. I dove the shallows with a garden hose and a bucket on my head, God help me, when I was younger and stupider. And six years ago I found something that wasn't gold."

The lamp guttered. Ivo trimmed the wick and waited. He was good at waiting; it was most of what diving was.

"There was a clerk," Marsh went on, quieter now. "Naval procurement office, Port Meridian. Drunk, frightened, guilty — the whole catalog. He'd copied something he shouldn't have copied and couldn't live with it and couldn't turn it in either, because the men it named would eat him. So he sold it to me for forty ounces and a promise I'd never say his name. Forty ounces, Okonkwo. I paid it gladly." His hand crept toward the oilcloth bundle Ivo had set aside. "That's it. That's what's in there. And I've carried it up and down this river for six years like a stone in my chest, and I am done carrying it, because I am dying, and we both know it, so let's not be polite about it."

"You should see a doctor at—"

"At Kiunga? At Meridian?" Marsh laughed, and the laugh became a cough became a wet, awful rattle. "Son, I've seen doctors. They gave me six weeks in the spring. That was five months ago. I outran them, but the river's wide here, and I'm tired of swimming." He fixed Ivo with those glass-bright eyes. "Open it."

Ivo didn't move. "Why me?"

"Because you're the only man on this river who's been inside that wreck and walked away honest. Because they fired you instead of buying you, which means you can't be bought. And because—" Marsh stopped. Something moved through his face then that had nothing to do with fever, something older and softer and much more painful. "Because I need a diver. And you're the best one the river's got, even unemployed. Especially unemployed."

Ivo untied the oilcloth.

Inside was a leather document case, swollen with damp, buckled at the corners. And folded around it, pressed flat as a relic, a sheet of heavy paper gone soft and brown at the folds, covered in grease stains and pencil lines drawn and redrawn until the paper nearly wore through. A chart. Hand-drawn, hand-lettered, the work of someone who understood water deeply and drafting not at all. Depth soundings crowded the margins in a tiny crabbed hand. Current arrows crawled across the channel like insects. And at the center, rendered in careful cross-section, was a ship.

Ivo knew her silhouette the way a man knows his own hands. The *HMAS Vigilant*, patrol sloop, 640 tons, lost with partial loss of life in the upper reaches four years back — official cause, grounding and subsequent foundering during cyclonic conditions. He'd spent three months diving her hull for a contractor whose invoices went to a naval account with no name attached. He knew her plates, her seams, her flooded corridors. He knew the way her bow section lay canted into the mud at nineteen fathoms and the way the survey charts everyone worked from showed her holds empty, swept, stripped of anything worth recovering.

But this drawing showed something the surveys never mentioned. Aft of the engine room, forward of the shaft alley, a compartment marked in red grease pencil — SEALED. WATERTIGHT DOOR JAMMED AT TIME OF LOSS. NOT ENTERED. And beside it, in letters pressed so hard into the paper they'd nearly torn through:

**THE PROOF IS IN THE IRON BOX. THEY KNOW IT'S THERE. THAT'S WHY THE WATER IS FORBIDDEN.**

Ivo looked up. Marsh was watching him with the expression of a man laying his last card face-up.

"You've seen her insides," Marsh said. "Tell me I'm wrong. Tell me there's no compartment aft of the engines that isn't on any survey."

Ivo was quiet a long moment. Rain. River. The tick of the cooling lamp.

"There's a bulkhead door there," he said finally. "Painted over. Painted so many times it looks like part of the wall. I noticed it because paint doesn't belong on a pressure boundary that's already failed. Somebody maintained that seal, Marsh. After she went down, somebody sent divers down to maintain the seal on one compartment of a dead ship." He heard his own voice go flat and cold, the voice he used for reporting facts to men who didn't want them. "I asked about it once. I was reassigned within the week."

Marsh nodded slowly, as if Ivo had confirmed a diagnosis. "Six years I've tried to get back to her. Six years, and every approach watched, every permit denied, every boat I chartered suddenly needed elsewhere. They've got the whole reach locked up tighter than a widow's purse. And now—" he gestured at his own ruined body, at the sweat-soaked cot, at the whole collapsing architecture of his last months— "now I'm out of time entirely. Which brings us to the bargain."

"I don't make bargains with dying men. Nothing good comes of it for either party."

"This one's simple." Marsh pushed himself up on one elbow, and the effort cost him visibly — a fine tremor ran through his whole frame, and his breath went thin. "The map is yours. The case — whatever the clerk sold me — yours too. Gold, evidence, leverage, kindling, I don't care what you do with it. In exchange you find my daughter."

Something shifted under Ivo's ribs, some old plank groaning in a swell.

"Daughter?"

"Ada. Ada Marsh. Her mother's people are river clans up past Nukumba — the mother's dead, died when the girl was small, and I sent Ada to the mission school at Saint Brigid's because a schoolhouse was the one thing I could give her that the river couldn't take back." His voice caught and steadied. "She thinks I'm dead already, likely. Or worse — she thinks I chose the river over her, which is near enough the same thing to a child, and she stopped being a child angry at me and became a woman angry at me, and I let it happen because I kept telling myself next season, next season, after I settle this thing, after I make it safe, then I'll come for her and explain everything." He laughed, or tried to. "Men like us, Okonkwo. We always think there's a next season."

"What do you want me to tell her?"

"The truth. All of it. What I found, why I stayed away, why I'm dying alone in a stranger's shack instead of in a bed I built for her." His hand closed around Ivo's wrist — surprisingly strong, the grip of a man who'd hauled on ropes his whole life. "Don't pretty it. Don't make me noble. She'll see through it, she always could. Just tell her her father loved her and was a coward about everything except the truth, and the truth is in that compartment, and somebody has to raise it before they bury it forever."

"Bury it how?"

Marsh's grip tightened. "They're coming for her. The wreck. A salvage fleet, civilian flags, navy money — I've watched them fitting out at Meridian for a month. Demolition crews, Okonkwo. Not recovery crews. When they're done, there won't be a compartment, there won't be a hull, there won't be anything but a scatter field and a new survey showing debris. Six tides, maybe less. The river's running high and they'll move on the spring water." He sagged back against the cot, spent, his chest heaving. "So. The map for the promise. Find Ada Marsh. Tell her the truth. And if you've got any of the stubbornness they say you've got — raise the proof before they burn it out of the world."

Ivo sat with it. The rain eased off toward that softer midnight rain, and the river kept talking, and the lamp burned low, and the old man's breathing went shallow and fast, the tide of him going out.

"If I say yes," Ivo said, "it's not a favor. It's not charity. I'll find her because a promise given is a debt, and I pay my debts. But I'm not promising to dive that wreck. That's a separate decision, and I'll make it myself, on my own reckoning."

"That's all any man can promise." Marsh smiled, and for a moment, under the fever, under the ruin, there was a glimpse of the man he must have been at twenty — lean and laughing and greedy for rivers. "Deal, then. Shake on it, diver. My word's the only collateral I've got left."

Ivo took the offered hand. It was hot as a stove plate and dry as paper, and it held on a moment longer than a handshake needs to.

"Ada," Marsh murmured, settling back. Eyes closing. "Named her after my mother. Strong woman. Mean as a cut snake when crossed. She'll like you, probably. She likes stubborn things she can't break." A pause. The breathing slowed. "There's a photograph. In the case. So you'll know her face..."

He drifted. Ivo sat through the small hours with the lamp trimmed low, boiling water, forcing quinine and sips of broth between the man's teeth whenever the fever let him surface enough to swallow. Twice Marsh woke and talked — fragments, mostly, names of sandbars, the color of a particular morning on the Strickland, a woman's laugh described so precisely Ivo could almost hear it. Once he woke clear-eyed and terrified and gripped Ivo's arm and said, *they followed me, there were men at Obo village asking after a map, tell Ada to lock her doors,* and then sank again before Ivo could ask anything more.

Toward dawn the breathing changed. Ivo had heard it before — in a shipmate, in his father, in strangers on riverbanks in three countries. The rattle smoothing out, the intervals stretching, the body quietly standing down from its long watch. He lit the lamp fully and sat close and held the old man's hand, because no one should cross open water alone, and Eben Marsh's grip tightened once, reflexively, around his fingers.

"Ada," the prospector said, very clearly, to someone in the room who wasn't Ivo. "Coming home now, love. Coming home."

And then the river took him, the way it takes everything eventually, gently and without malice, at seventeen minutes past dawn with the rain stopped and the light coming up gray-gold on the water.

* * *

Ivo buried him that afternoon on the rise above the shack, where the ground was high enough to keep the grave dry and the view ran ten miles down the river. He dug the hole himself — four hours with a mattock in clay that fought him like a living thing — and lined it with the tarpaulin off his dory, and said the words he remembered from a Navy chaplain at his brother's committal, adapted for a man with no flag and no service record and no one present but a stranger and the birds.

*We commit the body of Eben Marsh to the deep... well, no. To the earth. Close enough. The river gets us all in the end.*

He marked the grave with a length of anchor chain set upright in concrete, because wood rots and crosses get burned for firewood out here, but chain lasts. Then he went inside, washed the clay from his hands, and opened the leather case on his table.

A photograph first, as promised: a girl of perhaps eight, gap-toothed and furious-eyed, standing rigid in a mission school uniform with her chin tipped up in open defiance of the camera. Even at eight, even in the stiff collar, something in the set of her shoulders said *I am not impressed by you and I never will be.* On the back, in pencil: *Ada, Saint Brigid's, age 8. Still arguing.*

Beneath the photograph, documents. Naval letterhead. A cargo manifest, partially copied in a clerk's cramped hand — crates listed by weight and contents that made no sense for a patrol sloop's stores: crated machinery with no serial numbers, medical supplies bound for ports that had no hospitals, ammunition lot numbers matching no naval inventory. And a single page, typed, initialed by three officers whose signatures Ivo recognized from the very contractor files he'd once been forbidden to read — an order authorizing the *deliberate scuttling* of HMAS Vigilant following completion of cargo transfer, casualties regrettable but acceptable, insurance and inquiry narratives to reflect grounding.

They hadn't lost her in a storm. They'd sunk their own ship on purpose, crew aboard, to drown the paperwork of a smuggling run — and then sealed one compartment to protect the original manifest, which some officer with a conscience had stowed in an iron box rather than destroy, betting that someday someone would come asking.

Ivo sat back. The lamp hissed. Out on the dark river, far downstream, he thought he could hear — though he knew it was impossible, miles were miles — the deep-throated churn of heavy barges moving against the current.

Six tides.

He looked at the photograph of the furious little girl, and at the map with its red grease-pencil compartment, and at his own hands, which had not done an honest day's work in nine days and were suddenly, terribly restless.

"Well, Eben," he said to the empty room. "You picked the right diver and the wrong month."

He began packing.
