## 3. What the Map Shows

The rum was the kind that came in bottles without labels, traded three-for-a-dollar off a barge out of Port Meridian, and it tasted like kerosene that had been apologized to. Ivo poured two fingers into a tin cup, set the lamp wick low so the flame sat blue and small, and unrolled Eben Marsh's map across the scarred planks of his table.

Outside, the river worked at its endless labor. The Fly never slept; it dragged silt and secrets down toward the delta in equal measure, and the pilings of Ivo's dock creaked with the patience of things that had learned to wait. A gecko ticked somewhere in the rafters. Rain had come through an hour before and left the air smelling of crushed leaves and hot iron.

He had buried Eben Marsh at dawn.

Not buried, exactly — there was no ground soft enough to dig on the stilt-side mudflats, no shovels to be borrowed at that hour, no time. He had wrapped the old man in his own canvas bedroll, weighted it with two of the diving stones from Ivo's own belt — stones he'd carried through forty meters of black water more times than he could count — and given him to the current where the channel ran deep past the second bend. The navy would call it improper disposal if they ever knew. Ivo thought the river was as good a church as any, and better than most. It took everyone eventually. At least Eben went in company.

*Find my daughter,* the man had said, or tried to say, the words coming apart like wet paper between his cracked lips. *Ada. She's at the mission school upriver. Tell her—*

He hadn't finished telling her whatever it was. He'd gripped Ivo's wrist with a strength that surprised them both, pressed the oilcloth packet into his palm, and then the fever took him under for the last time, and by first light his breathing had gone shallow as tide-water over a shoal, and then not even that.

Ivo drank. The rum burned a clean line down his throat and settled warm behind his sternum, which was the only kind of warmth he trusted anymore.

Now the map.

It was drawn on sailcloth, or something like it — heavy woven fabric gone stiff with age and grease, folded so many times along the same creases that the fibers had begun to separate, so that handling it felt like holding a bird's wing. In the lamplight the ink had faded to the brown of dried blood, but the lines were still sure. Whoever had drawn this had drawn it *from memory*, and memory that precise belonged to either a surveyor or a survivor.

Ivo spread it flat and weighted the corners with his cup, a brass cartridge casing, a coil of copper wire, and the flat stone he used to sharpen his dive knife.

The coastline came first: the great bend of the Upper Fly rendered in confident strokes, tributaries branching off like veins in a leaf. Then the elevations — hills hatched in careful rows, a ridge line marked with what looked like a mission cross, a village indicated by three dots and a name half worn away. This was country Eben Marsh had walked for decades. You could see the decades in it. The map wasn't drawn; it was *accumulated*, layer over layer, the way a pearl forms around grit.

And then, in the lower right quadrant, the reason a dying man had traded everything he had for a stranger's promise.

A rectangle. Drawn twice — once small, once blown large in an inset box — with dimensions written alongside in a cramped hand: numbers for length, beam, draft. A silhouette of a hull in profile, and Ivo's stomach dropped through the floorboards of his shack before his mind caught up with his eyes, because he knew that silhouette. He had been inside that silhouette. He had run his hands along those bulkheads in water so cold and dark it erased the difference between touching and being touched.

The *Vigilant*.

Officially she didn't exist. Officially the classified wreck zone at Kilometer 214 was a navigational hazard of unknown origin, and Ivo Okonkwo had spent eleven months diving her as a contractor for a salvage firm whose contracts were printed on navy letterhead and whose instructions arrived sealed and unsigned. Officially he had been mapping debris fields and recovering nothing, week after week, while command grew steadily more interested in what he *wasn't* finding than in what he was.

Unofficially, he knew every rivet of that ship. And now a dead prospector's grease-stained map was showing him a compartment on her that did not exist.

He turned the lamp closer and leaned in until his nose nearly touched the fabric.

The inset drawing showed the wreck in section — cut open lengthwise like a specimen on a table. Ivo recognized the layout instantly: the engine room aft, the boiler flats, the cargo holds amidships, the officers' quarters forward beneath the bridge. Delphine Okafor could have drawn this from the galley's vantage point; he drew it now from the diver's, from the memory of swimming those corridors with a torch whose beam died four meters out in the silt-hung water.

But between hold two and hold three, where his own surveys — the ones he'd submitted, the honest ones, the ones that got him dismissed — showed solid plating and a fuel bunker, the map showed a void. A compartment. Roughly six meters by four, positioned low in the hull, below the waterline even when the ship floated, accessible — according to a dotted line Eben had traced from the forward cargo hatch — through a maintenance crawlway that appeared on no schematic Ivo had ever seen.

Someone had penciled a single word beside it, in a different hand than the rest of the map. Younger handwriting. Sharper. The letters pressed hard enough to emboss the cloth:

*PROOF.*

Ivo sat back. The gecko had gone quiet. Even the river seemed to have lowered its voice.

He took another drink and made himself think like a diver instead of a man who had just watched someone die in his shack. Divers survived by refusing to let their imagination do their breathing for them. Fear underwater was a luxury; it consumed air faster than exertion ever could. So: facts. What were the facts?

Fact one: Eben Marsh had possessed detailed internal knowledge of a wrecked naval vessel that officially had no name, no registry, and no history. Not general knowledge — not the kind you picked up dockside from gossip. Dimensional knowledge. Structural knowledge. Knowledge of a crawlway that existed on no drawing Ivo had ever been shown, and he had been shown every drawing the salvage firm possessed, because checking them against reality had been his entire job.

Fact two: the navy's own surveys of the wreck — the ones conducted after the sinking, the ones Ivo's firm had been quietly instructed to contradict wherever convenient — recorded no such compartment. He remembered the survey summary he'd been ordered to falsify depth readings against. Clean hull. Standard configuration. Nothing anomalous. He had signed off on readings he knew were wrong and refused to sign off on one reading he knew was right, and that refusal had cost him his contract, his clearance, and his place in the only structure that had ever pretended to be a life for him.

They hadn't wanted him falsifying depths because of silt drift. They'd wanted him falsifying depths because the true readings pointed at something they needed not to exist.

Fact three: a man had written *PROOF* on a map of that compartment, in handwriting too young to be Eben Marsh's, and Eben Marsh had carried the map long enough to wear the creases soft, and had died rather than give it to anyone but a stranger with a diver's hands.

Ivo got up and paced the length of his shack — eight steps, turn, eight steps back. The floorboards complained. Out the window, the moon was coming up over the far bank, laying a silver road across the current that led, inevitably, toward Kilometer 214.

He tried the other direction first. He always tried the other direction first; hope was just dread wearing a disguise, and he'd learned to strip both of them before trusting either.

Suppose the map meant nothing. Suppose Eben Marsh had been a fever-mad old man who'd once salvaged a chart from a dead officer's effects and built a treasure story around it the way river mud builds around a snag. Old prospectors did that. The upper river was full of men chasing ghost gold from wrecks that had never carried any, and dying men were especially prone to leaving maps that promised everything and delivered rust.

Except.

Except that Eben hadn't asked him for gold. That was the thing Ivo kept circling back to, the splinter in his mind he couldn't work loose. A dying man trading a treasure map should have asked for rescue, for medicine, for a priest, for anything that might keep him alive to collect. Eben Marsh had asked for a daughter. He'd spent his last currency not on himself but on making sure a girl upriver learned *something* — and he'd chosen as his messenger a man whose hands still smelled of diving grease, whom he'd never met before collapsing on his dock.

Which meant Eben hadn't seen a treasure hunter. He'd seen a witness. Someone who went down into dark places and came back up and told the truth about what was down there. Maybe the only kind of man left on this river who would.

Ivo returned to the table and turned the map over.

The reverse held more writing — columns of figures, dates, initials. Cargo entries. His breath slowed without his permission. He recognized the format; it was a manifest layout, standard naval issue, the same template used on every supply run he'd ever audited as part of his contract paperwork. Most of the entries were smudged beyond recovery, but a few had survived the years and the grease:

*Ammunition, small arms — 400 crates.*
*Medical supplies, assorted — 60 crates.*
*Cement, bagged — 200 tons.*

And beside the cement, in that younger hand again, a note so small he had to hold the cloth an inch from his eye:

*No cement was loaded. Hold 2 sealed before departure. Manifest altered at source. Originals in the compartment. Ask the cook.*

*Ask the cook.*

Ivo read it three times. The words didn't change. Outside, a night bird called once across the water and got no answer.

Four hundred crates of ammunition and sixty of medical supplies — that was plausible, that was the kind of tonnage a patrol vessel like the *Vigilant* would legitimately carry on an upriver run. But two hundred tons of cement that was never loaded, entered on a manifest anyway? That wasn't clerical error. That was a hole dug in paper, big enough to hide a smuggling operation in — payoffs, kickbacks, cargo that existed only on documents so that money could exist only in certain accounts. And when a scheme like that grew too big to stay hidden, when someone aboard started asking questions, you didn't court-martial your way out of it. You scuttled your own ship in deep water, drowned the questions along with the questioners, and spent the next decade paying contractors to produce surveys confirming that nothing was there.

That the compartment wasn't on the surveys. That the compartment was full of the original manifests, the real ones, the ones that would hang somebody very highly placed.

And that the navy had hired Ivo Okonkwo — honest, thorough, inconvenient Ivo Okonkwo — to spend eleven months producing the paperwork that would bury it forever, and had fired him the moment he declined.

He sat down slowly. The tin cup was empty. He didn't remember drinking it dry.

The lamp flame bent in a draft from under the door, throwing his shadow huge against the wall, a giant hunched over a scrap of cloth. He thought about his brother, and stopped thinking about his brother, the way he always did — a door opened onto a cold room and shut again before the chill could settle into his bones. Tomás had gone down in navy waters too. The navy had sent a letter that explained nothing, in language designed to explain nothing, and their mother had died waiting for a truth that had been filed away in some archive marked *closed*. Ivo had joined the service afterward looking for that truth and found instead a machine for manufacturing its absence. He measured his life in miles traveled because miles, at least, never lied about their length.

And now here was a dead man's map offering him the one thing the machine could not abide: a document that would make the lie too big to hold underwater.

Six tides. That was how long the salvage fleet would need — he'd heard the radio chatter before his dismissal, the barges massing at Port Meridian under civilian flags, the talk of "final clearing operations" at Kilometer 214. Final clearing. Demolition charges laid, the wreck collapsed into the channel muck, every trace of that compartment compressed into geology. After that, the proof wouldn't be hidden. It would be *unmade*.

He looked at the map again. At the dotted line running from the forward cargo hatch down through the crawlway. At the word *PROOF*. At *ask the cook*.

The cook. There had been a cook aboard the *Vigilant* — he'd heard the name once, years ago, in a dockside bar in Meridian, attached to a story about a woman who'd fed the crew of a doomed ship and walked away from the service under a cloud nobody would describe. Delphine. Delphine Okafor. She cooked now on a riverboat out of the middle landing, if she still cooked at all, if she was still alive, if the cloud hadn't swallowed her the way the river swallowed everything.

Ivo stood, took the lamp in one hand and the map in the other, and held the cloth up so the light shone through it. The fibers glowed amber, and the lines of the dead ship showed through like veins in a leaf, like bones in a hand.

Somewhere down at Kilometer 214, in forty meters of black water, a sealed steel room waited with its paper heart still beating. Somewhere upriver, a girl named Ada didn't yet know her father was dead. Somewhere downriver, a fleet was loading demolition charges with his former employers' signatures on the work orders.

And somewhere in between, a disgraced cook who knew every corridor of that ship by heart was frying fish for strangers.

Ivo rolled the map along its soft old creases, slid it back into the oilcloth, and tucked it inside his shirt, against his skin, where the river couldn't reach it.

"Delphine Okafor," he said aloud, testing the weight of the name. The shack gave back only silence and the sound of the current.

He blew out the lamp. Through the window, the moon-road on the water stretched toward the wreck zone, silver and straight and patient, and Ivo understood, with the calm certainty of a man watching his last exit close behind him, that the promise he'd made to a dying stranger had just become the most dangerous thing he owned.

He would need a boat. He would need a crew. And he would need to reach a woman at the middle landing before anyone else asked her what she remembered about the night the *Vigilant* died.

He packed his dive knife, his spare regulator, and every dollar he had in the world — thirty-one of them — into a canvas bag, and stepped out onto the dock where the river waited, keeping its secrets, counting its tides.
