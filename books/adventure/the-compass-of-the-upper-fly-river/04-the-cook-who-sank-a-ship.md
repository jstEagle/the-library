## 4. The Cook Who Sank a Ship

The river town of Sable Landing smelled of diesel and frying plantains and the particular rot that came when the water dropped two feet in a week and exposed everything the current had been hiding. Ivo Okonkwo walked its single street with Eben Marsh's map folded against his chest like a second heartbeat, and he told himself he was only here for a meal.

He was lying to himself, but he was good at it. A man who spent his life underwater got fluent in holding his breath.

Delphine Okafor ran her kitchen out of the stern half of a half-sunk paddle steamer called the *Belle Amara*, which had been beached so long that mangrove seedlings had taken root in her wheelhouse and nobody had the heart to pull them out. The bow half sat dry on the mud at low tide, propped on cribbing, and someone had strung lanterns along her rail so that from the water she looked like a festival that had run out of money but not out of light. The sign over the gangplank said DELPHINE'S — NO RESERVATIONS, NO REFUNDS, NO NONSENSE.

Ivo had eaten there twice before, years ago, back when he still worked navy contracts and could afford to eat anywhere. He remembered two things about those meals: the food had been extraordinary, and the woman who cooked it had watched him the whole time with the flat, measuring stare of someone counting knives.

He climbed the gangplank at four in the afternoon, between services, when the kitchen would be empty of customers but not of Delphine. That was the trick with people like her. You didn't catch them off guard — you caught them working, because work was when they were most themselves.

The galley was hot as a held breath. Copper pots hung from hooks overhead like a school of polished fish, and the whole room smelled of scotch bonnet, smoked fish, and stock that had been going since before dawn. Delphine Okafor stood at the cutting board with her sleeves rolled to the elbow, and her forearms were the forearms of a woman who had hauled hundred-pound sacks of rice up gangplanks for twenty years: corded, scarred, absolutely certain of themselves.

She didn't look up. "Kitchen's closed."

"I'm not here for the kitchen."

"Then you're lost." The knife kept moving — onion falling into even dice, fast and hypnotic. "River's that way."

"Delphine Okafor," Ivo said. "Second cook, then chief cook, aboard the *Vigilant*, naval auxiliary survey vessel, three years of service. Discharged under circumstances the record doesn't explain. You've been feeding this river ever since."

The knife stopped.

She turned around slowly, wiping her hands on her apron, and gave him the full weight of her attention, which was not a small thing. She was perhaps fifty-five, broad through the shoulders, with gray threading through her close-cropped hair and eyes the color of strong tea — warm if you were her friend, and Ivo suspected you did not want to find out what they were otherwise.

"And who's asking?" she said. "Because the last man who stood in my galley reciting my service record was a paymaster's clerk, and I put him ashore at the next bend without his boots."

"My name is Ivo Okonkwo. I dive wrecks. Until nine days ago I dove one in particular, under contract, and then I refused to write down some numbers I'd been told to write down, and now I don't work anymore." He set his hands flat on the edge of her prep counter, an old diver's habit, steadying himself against solid things. "I'm not a clerk. I'm not navy. And I'm not here to take your boots."

"No," Delphine said. "You're here because you think I know the *Vigilant*."

"I know you know the *Vigilant*."

"Everybody knows the *Vigilant*. She's on the charts. She's a hazard to navigation, God rest whoever's down there." Her voice was pleasant and completely closed, like a door painted to look like a wall. "Sit down if you're staying. I don't cook for men who hover."

He sat on a stool by the pass-through window while she worked, and for a long time neither of them spoke. She reduced a mountain of peppers, fed a stockpot, tasted and adjusted with the economy of long habit. Ivo watched her hands. He had spent enough years around salvage crews to read people the way he read hulls — where the stress lines ran, where the paint had been patched over something underneath.

There was a photograph tacked above the spice shelf, gone soft and brown with steam. A ship's company lined up along a deck rail, maybe sixty faces, squinting into sun. Navy auxiliary, by the cut of the uniforms. Someone had drawn a small circle in grease pencil around one figure near the galley door — a broad-shouldered woman with her cap pushed back, laughing at something outside the frame.

Ivo looked at the photograph for a long time. Then he looked at the ship in it, and his stomach went cold and quiet, the way it did forty feet down when the light failed.

It was the *Vigilant*. Whole, afloat, alive. He knew her lines the way he knew his own hands — he had spent six weeks crawling over her drowned bones — but seeing her upright, riding the water instead of lying in it, rearranged something in his chest. Ships were supposed to be like that. Upright. Manned. It was the water that was wrong, not the ship.

"You circled yourself," he said.

"I circled myself," Delphine agreed. She didn't turn around. "Sixty-two souls aboard when she went down. Fifty-one came home. You want to guess what my job was, the night we lost eleven of them?"

"Cooking."

"Cooking." She said it lightly, terribly. "Midnight rations. The boys coming off watch cold and wet, and me with a pot of pepper soup going, telling them the weather would break by morning. Eleven of them ate in my mess that night, Mr. Okonkwo. I can name every bowl." Now she turned, and the tea-colored eyes were not warm at all. "So when a stranger walks into my galley and says *Vigilant* in that careful voice, you'll understand why I'm not reaching for the salt."

"The navy says she struck an uncharted shoal in the flood season," Ivo said. "Hull failure, rapid foundering, no fault assigned. That's the finding."

"That's the finding," Delphine repeated.

"I spent six weeks on her. Contracted salvage survey, restricted site." He chose the words carefully, laying them out like tools on a bench. "There are things about that wreck that don't match the finding. The damage pattern is wrong. The flooding sequence is wrong. And there's a compartment amidships — sealed, welded shut from the inside of the hull scheme — that appears on none of the surveys I've seen. Not the public ones. Not the ones they let me read."

Delphine had gone very still. Steam rose between them.

"Welded shut," she said.

"With the frames intact behind it. Nobody welded it after she sank. It was sealed before she ever left port."

For a moment the only sound was the stockpot breathing and, far below, the river slapping the *Belle Amara*'s hull. Delphine took off her apron, folded it once, and laid it across the counter with the deliberation of a woman setting down something heavy.

"Come with me," she said.

* * *

She led him aft, past the dining benches, through a door marked PRIVATE, into a cabin that had clearly once been the steamer's purser's office and was now part pantry, part shrine. Shelves of preserved goods lined one wall. On the other wall hung a framed certificate of discharge, a child's drawing of a boat done in crayon, and — Ivo's breath caught — a ship's bell, green with river patina, mounted on a bracket of dark wood.

He knew that bell. He had seen its mounting bracket empty, forty meters down, furred with silt.

"You salvaged that?"

"I asked a friend to look the other way for ten minutes, eight years ago." Delphine touched the bell's rim without ringing it, the way you'd touch a sleeping child's head. "They fined the wreck zone off before anyone could say goodbye to her properly. No memorial service. No list of names read aloud. Just a buoy and a fine and a line through her in the register." She looked at him. "Eleven men, Mr. Okonkwo. Two of them were boys from my own village who joined up because I wrote home that the food was good and the work was honest."

Ivo said nothing. There was nothing yet to say. He had learned that the most important moments in a conversation underwater were the ones where you hung in the water column and let the other person move first.

Delphine sat down heavily on a crate of condensed milk and studied him with new attention, the measuring stare from the photograph replaced by something rawer.

"Now you tell me why a fired diver walks into my kitchen talking about a compartment that isn't on any survey. And you tell me true, because I have buried enough lies for one life."

So Ivo told her. Not everything — not yet, not the map, not Eben Marsh dying on his dock with river water in his lungs and a promise in his fist — but the shape of it. The order to falsify depth readings. His refusal. The compartment that shouldn't exist. The way the navy's own contractors were already massing barges downriver, moving with the urgency of men cleaning up a crime scene rather than salvaging a wreck.

"They're not going down to raise anything," he said. "I've watched salvage fleets my whole working life. These men aren't rigging for recovery. They're staging for demolition. Six days, maybe less, and that compartment — whatever's in it, whoever sealed it — becomes a crater."

Delphine listened without interrupting, which he came to understand was her discipline; she listened the way she cooked, with total attention and no wasted motion. When he finished, she was quiet for a long moment, her thumb moving absently over the milk crate's stenciled lettering.

"There was a clerk," she said finally. "Aboard the *Vigilant*, her last commission. Paymaster's assistant, young fellow, nervous as a cat in a dog yard. Name of Whitcombe." Her mouth twisted. "He used to come into my galley at odd hours, not for food — for quiet. Said the wardroom made his skin crawl that trip. Said there were crates going into the hold at night that weren't on any manifest he'd signed, and men handling them who weren't on any crew list, and when he asked questions he was told his career would be very short and possibly his life with it."

"He talked to you? A cook?"

"A cook hears everything and is beneath suspicion, Mr. Okonkwo. That's the whole secret of my profession." She stood, restless now, moving among her shelves. "Three nights before we sailed on the final run, Whitcombe came to me shaking. Asked me — me, a chief petty officer's widow with a pension to protect — whether I thought a man could live honest after he'd stayed silent about something wicked. I told him the truth as I saw it. I said a man who stays silent drowns slower, but he drowns just the same."

"What happened to him?"

"He vanished the night before we made port. Desertion, the record says. They docked his pay and struck him from the list, and the officers joked about it at dinner, and I served them their dinner." Her voice didn't rise, but the temperature of it dropped several degrees. "Two weeks later the *Vigilant* went down with eleven souls, and the finding was shoal and hull failure, and every question I asked was answered with a form."

Ivo felt the pieces click together with the heavy, terrible satisfaction of a mechanism seating home. A nervous clerk. Crates off the manifest. A compartment welded shut before sailing. A ship sent to the bottom in flood season, in a remote reach, where the only witnesses would be the river and the dead.

And somewhere in that sealed steel box, if Eben Marsh's grease-stained map told the truth, lay a document that named names.

"Delphine," he said. "I need to go down into that compartment. Before the fleet gets there. And I can't do it alone, because I never served aboard her, and her insides are a maze, and the flooded sections will be black as sin and full of collapsed gear. I need someone who knows her decks the way you know this kitchen." He paused. "I can't pay much. I can't promise safe. I can't even promise we'll come back with proof worth the risk. What I can promise is that whatever's down there gets brought into the light, or we die trying, and either way it won't be buried quietly again."

Delphine stopped pacing. She stood with her hand resting on the ship's bell, and Ivo watched her weigh it — not the risk, he realized, but the arithmetic of the dead. Eleven bowls in her mess. One missing clerk. A bell that should have been rung at a funeral and never was.

"You said you can't pay much," she said at last.

"I said I can't pay much in money."

"Good. Because I wouldn't take it." She straightened, and something in her bearing changed — the shopkeeper falling away, the sailor standing up underneath. Twenty years of running a kitchen fell off her shoulders like an apron, and what remained was a woman who had ridden a warship into a flood and walked away from the water. "Here are my terms, diver, and they're not negotiable. First: we go for truth, not gold. If there's treasure in that compartment alongside your papers, it stays down there or goes to the tribunal, and if you argue with me I'll put you ashore at Kolo Bend without your boots. Second: when this is done — however it's done — the eleven get their names read aloud. Publicly. On the water, over the spot where they lie, if there's anything left to lie in. Third—" and here her voice caught, just slightly, the first crack in all that iron, "—if there's anything of Whitcombe down there, a button, a boot, a bone, it comes home to me. He deserves a grave with his right name on it."

Ivo stood and extended his hand. "Agreed. All three."

She took it. Her grip was a winch.

"One more thing," Delphine said, not letting go. "That map you're carrying. Don't insult us both by pretending you don't have one — a fired diver doesn't mount an expedition on conviction alone. Whatever it shows, whatever dead man drew it for you, I'll want to see it tonight, over food, because I'm not diving into a maze on half information." She released his hand and turned toward the galley, already moving, already cooking, the transformation somehow complete and seamless. "Now sit down. You look like a man who hasn't eaten since he buried somebody, and I don't plan expeditions on an empty stomach any more than I fight on one."

Ivo sat. Outside, the light was failing over Sable Landing, and the lanterns along the *Belle Amara*'s rail came up one by one, and for the first time since Eben Marsh died on his dock, Ivo Okonkwo did not feel entirely alone with the map against his chest.

Delphine set a bowl of pepper soup in front of him — the same soup, he understood suddenly, that she had ladled out to eleven men on the last night of their lives — and then she pulled a stool to the opposite side of the counter, produced a pencil and the back of a delivery invoice, and looked at him with eyes that had already begun charting flooded corridors.

"Now," she said. "Show me the map. And then you're going to tell me exactly how many days we have — because if it's fewer than five, we sail tonight, and you'd better pray the Magpie's engine is half as good as Ferro Lantz claims, because I am not drowning in that hull twice."

Ivo froze with his spoon halfway to his mouth. "How do you know about the Magpie?"

Delphine smiled for the first time, and it was not entirely comforting.

"Diver," she said, "a cook hears everything."
