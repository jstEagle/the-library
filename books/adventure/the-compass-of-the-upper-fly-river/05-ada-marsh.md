## 5. Ada Marsh

Delphine Okafor had fed admirals and fed prisoners, and she could tell you which of the two was harder to read. Admirals lied with their whole faces. Prisoners lied with nothing but their eyes. The woman in the nurse's uniform at the Kambiri Mission School did neither. She simply looked at Ivo Okonkwo across the dispensary table as if he were a symptom she hadn't yet diagnosed.

"You said you were friends of my father," Ada Marsh said.

"We said we knew him," Delphine corrected gently. "Friendship with Eben Marsh was a thing you earned in installments."

The dispensary smelled of carbolic and river damp, of boiled bandages and the green rot that came off the Fly in the wet season. Mosquito netting sagged from the rafters like old lace. Beyond the open shutters, mission children were singing something about a shepherd in pidgin, their voices thin and bright against the drum of rain on the tin roof. Delphine had cooked for men who would have paid a year's wages for this kind of quiet, and here it was, free, wasted on people who didn't know to want it.

Ada Marsh didn't look like her father. That was the first thing Delphine noticed, and the second thing too. Eben had been all weather — a face scoured down to grain and knot, eyes the color of creek water after a flood. His daughter was composed where he'd been eroded. Dark skin smooth as river stone, hair cropped practical-short, hands steady on the ledger she'd been writing in when they walked in. She had his stillness, though. Delphine recognized it now that she saw it again: the way Eben could sit motionless for an hour watching a heron, and you'd think he wasn't paying attention until he named every fish it caught.

"I knew him," Ada repeated. The words came out flat, pressed like flowers between book pages. "Knew. Past tense. Is he dead?"

"He was dying when we left him," Ivo said. "Fever. Lungs full of water that wasn't river water."

Something moved behind her face — quick, swallowed, gone. She wrote a final line in her ledger, closed it, squared it with the table's edge. A woman who put things away properly.

"Then I'll say the requiem myself. Father Anselm charges three shillings and talks too long." She stood. "If you've come for money, there isn't any. If you've come because he told you some story —" her mouth tightened — "then you've wasted a boat trip, and I'm sorry for it, but not sorry enough to feed you."

She turned to go, and Delphine did the thing she'd been doing since she was nine years old and her mother first put a wooden spoon in her hand: she read the room by its hunger.

The girl was starving. Not for food. For news. For one true sentence about the man whose blood she carried and whose name she clearly couldn't say without her jaw going rigid. You can hide hunger from anyone who hasn't spent twenty years watching men pretend they're full.

"Sister," Delphine said, using the title the way she'd use salt — just enough. "We watched your father die. He held my hand hard enough to leave marks. Do you want to know what he held on to say, or do you want to go file your ledgers?"

Ada stopped. Rain ticked on the tin roof. Somewhere a child laughed, then got shushed.

"What did he say?"

"Sit down," Delphine said, "and I'll tell you over tea. I make better tea than this mission does. I've made tea for two flag officers and one man who pretended to be a flag officer, and none of them ever sent a cup back."

* * *

It took an hour, and most of what cracked the girl open wasn't the dying at all. It was the small things. That Eben had asked twice whether the tide was coming in or going out, as if it mattered to him where the water meant to go. That he'd called Ivo by another name — "Tommy" — and gripped his wrist and said *you know how it is, Tommy, you know what they do with names*. That at the very end, when the fever had burned everything else away, he'd smiled and said *she'll have my mother's hands*, and died before anyone could ask him what he meant.

Ada listened with her hands folded on the table, and Delphine watched those hands. They stayed still through all of it except once — when Delphine described how Eben had pressed the oilcloth packet into Ivo's palm and said *find my daughter, tell her it wasn't debt, it was witness*. At that, Ada's fingers curled inward, knuckles whitening, as if catching something falling.

"It wasn't debt," Ada said finally. Her voice had gone strange. "That's what he always said. When the navy men came to our house looking for him, when we moved four times in two years, when he wouldn't let me keep a photograph of us together because photographs could be found — he'd say, *it's not debt, Ada, it's witness.* I thought it was madness. I decided it was madness, because the alternative was deciding my father had thrown our whole life away on purpose."

"And now?" Ivo asked.

"Now I don't know what I've decided." She looked out at the rain. "He wrote me letters. Twice a year, no return address, always different handwriting — he'd pay someone else to write them so they couldn't be traced. Like a man posting letters from inside a locked room." She turned back. "You said he gave you a map."

Ivo glanced at Delphine. This was the part they'd argued about on the boat ride upriver, Delphine steering and Ivo sulking over the tiller like a man asked to share his last cigarette. Show her or don't show her. Delphine had won, the way she usually won, by pointing out that a secret kept from the daughter was just another weight tied to the drowning man's ankle.

Ivo unwrapped the oilcloth and laid the map on the dispensary table between the teacups.

Ada didn't touch it. She leaned over it instead, close enough that her breath fogged the grease-stained paper, and Delphine watched her read it the way you'd watch someone read a letter from the dead. Her lips moved slightly. Her finger hovered above the ink without landing, a heron over fish.

"This is his hand," she said. "This is — he drew these depth numbers himself. He used to practice them on the backs of envelopes. I used to think he was doing sums." Her finger traced the coastline of the chart, the little cross-hatched rectangle marked in the margin with a symbol Delphine hadn't been able to name: a circle with a line struck through it, drawn over and over, obsessively, like a signature. "Do you know what this mark is?"

"No," Ivo said.

"I do." Ada sat back. All the color had gone out of her voice; what remained was dry as ash. "It's the seal on a strongbox. He kept his papers in one, under our floorboards, the whole time I was growing up. Circle with a strike through it. He told me once it meant *closed*. Sealed. Something shut that stays shut." She looked up at Ivo, and for the first time her composure showed its seams. "What's sealed on that map?"

"That's what we came to ask you," Delphine said quietly. "Because the coordinates are inside the wreck zone. The *Vigilant*. And the navy's own surveys say there's nothing there but cargo hold and mud."

* * *

They talked until the rain stopped and the light went the color of weak broth. Ada Marsh talked the way people talk when they've been holding a breath for ten years — in bursts, in fragments, doubling back, correcting herself, sometimes stopping entirely to stare at the middle distance while she reassembled a memory she'd spent a decade trying to disassemble.

Eben Marsh had not always been a prospector. That was the first thing. Before the upper river, before the claims and the pans and the lean years, he had worked the government side of the water — a survey contractor, licensed, respectable, the kind of job that came with a canvas satchel and a stamp. He'd run supply manifests for coastal steamers. Paperwork. The dullest work on earth, Ada said, and her father had loved it precisely because it was dull, because dull work let a man be home for supper.

Then, six years ago, he'd come back from a contract posting at Port Meridian a changed man. Quiet in a new way — not the quiet of tiredness but the quiet of a man listening for footsteps. Within the year he'd sold the house, taken Ada out of her school, and gone upriver into prospecting, which everyone understood to be a polite word for poverty.

"He never told me why," Ada said. "But I found things. Children find things; it's the one skill we have that adults forget. Papers he burned half of. A scrap he missed with a name on it — a naval clerk, a requisition officer, someone at Meridian. And once, drunk on palm wine, he said a sentence I've carried around like a stone ever since." She recited it slowly, the way you'd recite scripture learned unwillingly. "*They didn't lose her. They put her down like a dog and filed her under weather.*"

Delphine felt the words land in her own chest. She'd served aboard naval vessels for eleven years. She knew exactly what *filed under weather* meant. Ships lost to storms got sympathy and monuments. Ships lost to anything else got silence, and the silence got swept, and the men who swept it got promoted.

"The *Vigilant* was lost with all hands four years ago," Ivo said carefully. "Official finding: structural failure in heavy seas."

"Yes." Ada's mouth twisted. "And my father moved us again the month after. Deeper upriver. And started drawing maps."

"Maps of what?"

"Of one place. Over and over. I used to think it was a claim he was obsessed with — some gold seam he'd dreamed up. Now—" She stopped. Pressed her palms flat against the table as if steadying herself against a swell. "Now I want to see it again. Properly."

Ivo slid the map toward her. She bent over it, and this time she did touch it, fingertips light on the grease-stained margins, reading her father's obsessive little numerals like a language she'd forgotten she spoke.

"There," she said. "That rectangle. It's not on any chart of the *Vigilant* I've ever seen — and I looked, after he died, I mean, after he left, I went to the shipping office in Kerema and asked to see the public survey and they refused me, which itself was an answer." Her finger rested on the cross-hatched box. "A sealed compartment. Forward of the main hold. He marked the access — look, this line here, this is a crawlway, he's drawn it like a throat. And these numbers aren't depths. They're turns. Port, starboard, port. It's a route. From outside the hull to inside the compartment."

Delphine went cold in a way the river breeze couldn't account for. Because she knew those corridors. She had carried tea trays down those corridors for fourteen months aboard a ship she was legally required to describe as lost to weather. And the route Eben had drawn — port, starport, down through the trunk — matched the layout of the *Vigilant*'s forward section the way a key matches a lock it was cut for.

"He was aboard her," Delphine said. "Before. Wasn't he? Your father surveyed the *Vigilant* herself."

"I think he did more than survey her." Ada lifted her head. "I think he counted things onto her that were never in any manifest. Or counted things off. I don't know which. But I know this much, and I've known it since I was sixteen, and I have never said it aloud to another living soul—"

She stopped. Footsteps in the corridor outside. Ada rose smoothly, crossed to the door, and slid the bolt — a small motion, practiced, the reflex of a woman who had grown up bolting doors.

"Men have already come asking," she said, turning back. "That's the other thing you should know. Three weeks ago. Two of them, wearing civilian clothes that fit like uniforms. They had a photograph of my father and they asked the sisters whether he'd visited, whether he'd written, whether he'd left anything in the school's keeping. They were very polite. They left a donation." Her jaw tightened. "Father Anselm told them nothing, mostly because he knows nothing, and partly because he disapproves of their shoes. But I saw the car they drove. Nobody drives a car like that to a mission school out of charity."

"What did they look like?" Ivo asked.

"One was big and soft-spoken and did all the talking. The other never spoke at all. He just stood by the gate and watched the river." She wrapped her arms around herself, and for a moment she looked sixteen again, the girl who had found the scraps of burned paper. "Watching the river. Who does that?"

"Men waiting for a boat," Delphine said.

Silence. The mission children had gone in out of the wet; the compound below the dispensary window lay empty, steaming, washed clean and bright as a lie.

"They asked if he'd left papers," Ada continued. "Papers. Not gold, not a claim, not money he owed. Papers. And when I said he'd left me nothing, the soft one smiled and said, *people always leave more than they think.* Then they went." She looked at the map on the table, at the sealed compartment her father had drawn like a wound. "So either you two are exactly what you say you are, or you're the third visit, and I've been slow-witted twice already today."

"We're what we say," Ivo said. "Your father asked me to find you. He paid me with that map. I intend to earn it."

"By diving a forbidden wreck that the navy sank, to raise papers the navy wants burned, with a fleet arriving to do the burning?" Ada shook her head slowly. "My father traded a stranger a death sentence and called it a promise. That sounds like him. That sounds exactly like him."

"It does," Delphine agreed. "I only met him once and I could draw his portrait from it. But here's what I'd ask you, Sister, and I ask it as a cook, which means I ask it as a woman who knows that everything depends on what goes into the pot." She leaned forward. "You've spent ten years deciding your father was mad, because mad was safer than right. Now two strangers walk in carrying his map and his last words, and men in shoes too good for the mission have come sniffing after his papers. Mad doesn't bring the navy to your door. Right does. So the question isn't whether your father was a thief."

Ada's eyes had gone bright and hard, creek water after a flood. Eben's eyes. Delphine saw it plainly now — the resemblance had been there all along, hiding under the composure like a fish under ice.

"The question," Ada finished for her, barely above a whisper, "is what he witnessed."

The bolted door rattled. Once. Lightly. Almost courteous.

All three of them went still. Through the shutter slats, down in the compound, Delphine saw it: a launch at the mission jetty, grey hull, no flag flying, and two figures standing in the rain that fit like uniforms, looking up at the dispensary window.

Not asking this time. Just watching the river.

Ada moved first. She swept the map off the table, folded it into her apron with her father's own economy of motion, and said, in a voice gone suddenly calm and clinical as a surgeon's:

"Back door. There's a canoe behind the laundry shed, and I'm the best paddler on this stretch of the Fly. And you're both going to tell me everything on the water — because whatever my father sealed in that compartment, I am done letting other people decide what I'm allowed to know."
