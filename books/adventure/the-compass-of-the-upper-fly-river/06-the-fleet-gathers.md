## 6. The Fleet Gathers

Ada Marsh had learned to read the river the way other people read scripture — not for comfort but for warning. From the ridge behind the mission, where the morning fog still clung to the water like a held breath, she could see eleven miles of the Fly's broad brown shoulder before it bent away toward the coast. She had counted the bends a hundred times on her walks. She knew where the herons stood, where the current ran green over gravel bars, where the drowned trees lifted their arms at low water.

She had never seen barges there before.

There were six of them now, strung downriver in the haze like beads on a line that someone was slowly pulling taut. They sat low and heavy in the water, cranes folded against their decks like sleeping birds, and they flew no flag she could name — only plain white ensigns with a blue chevron, the kind of anonymous colors that meant somebody wanted very much not to be identified. Ada had spent three years at the mission school learning to catalog things: ferns, fevers, the faces of children brought in half-drowned from the rapids. She recognized this the way she recognized infection. Something was gathering, and it was gathering with intent.

"You'll strain your eyes," said Sister Bernadette behind her, setting a tin mug of tea into her hands without asking whether she wanted it. The old woman's voice was dry as river clay. "You've been standing there since first light."

"Do you know those boats?"

"I know they're not fishermen." Bernadette squinted downriver, her habit of forty years of looking at trouble without flinching. "Fishermen work close to shore. Those are sitting out in the channel like they own it." She paused. "They came up past Kolo Bend last night. The pilot at the trading post said they didn't answer his hail."

Ada wrapped her hands around the mug and felt the heat of it as something almost foreign — warmth that asked nothing of her. She had been cold since the day Ivo Okonkwo and his strange cook had come to the mission gate with her father's name in their mouths. Three days now. Three days of refusing to think about it, which was its own kind of labor, harder than nursing, harder than grief.

Eben Marsh. Her father. Dead by dawn in a dockside shack, and the first she'd heard of it was from a stranger with salt-cracked hands who had carried a grease-stained map four hundred miles of river because he'd promised a dying man he would.

*Find my daughter,* the man had told her father, and her father had believed him, and apparently the man had been fool enough or decent enough to keep the promise.

Ada had turned them away. She remembered the exact words — *my father was nothing to me* — and she remembered how they had sounded in her own mouth, rehearsed, load-bearing, the way a wall sounds when you knock on it and hear the hollow behind. Delphine Okafor had looked at her with eyes that missed nothing and said, gently, terribly: *He talked about you until the end. Said you liked guavas.* And Ada had said something about how any man could guess a daughter liked guavas, and Delphine had nodded as if agreeing, and gone, and Ada had stood in the dispensary afterward with a bowl of guavas some child had brought her that very week and understood that she had lied, and that everyone present had known it.

Now the barges were coming, and the map was somewhere out on the river in the hands of people she had refused to help, and her father's body was already two days in the ground at Port Meridian under a wooden marker with his name spelled wrong.

"Six," Bernadette said.

"What?"

"Barges. There were five yesterday evening. Six this morning." The sister took the mug back from Ada's nerveless hands and drank from it herself, an old liberty between them. "Whatever they're doing, they're doing it in numbers."

* * *

The mission kept a shortwave radio for medical emergencies, locked in the office of Father Anselm, who believed that God had given humanity radio so that missionaries would not have to die of treatable fevers. Ada had the key because she was the one who called in the malaria cases. At midmorning she sat in the office with the door shut and turned the dial through static, listening to the river talk to itself.

Most of what she heard was routine — traders arranging cargo, a church service relayed from somewhere down the coast, the endless chatter of pilots calling sandbars. But on a frequency just above the shipping band, voices were moving in clipped patterns that weren't trade. Numbers. Grid references. A phrase repeated twice that made the hair rise on her arms: *no surface activity prior to commencement.*

Commencement of what?

She wrote down what she caught in the margin of a dispensary ledger, in pencil, in her small clinical hand. Then she sat with the page for a long moment, staring at it, feeling the shape of something assemble itself in her chest the way a storm assembles over the estuary — pressure first, then wind, then the certainty of rain.

Her father had been obsessed with a wreck. That was the word everyone used, *obsessed*, as though obsession were a disease he'd caught rather than a thing he'd witnessed. He had come home once, years ago, soaked to the ribs and talking about a ship going down that shouldn't have gone down, and her mother had listened, and then her mother had died, and after that Eben Marsh had stopped talking about anything except the river and the ship and the men who put it there. Ada had been fifteen. She had decided her father was mad the way children decide things — completely, and for their own protection.

The men coming up the river in anonymous barges were not mad. They were organized. They were funded. They were flying flags designed to be forgotten.

Madmen did not send six barges.

* * *

In the afternoon she walked down to the trading post at the foot of the mission track, ostensibly to collect quinine, actually because Mr. Tamakloe saw everything that moved on the water and sold his observations as freely as his kerosene. He was a fat, shrewd, kind-eyed man who had known her mother's clan and always gave Ada credit longer than he gave anyone else.

"Those boats," she said, laying the quinine money on the counter. "Where are they from?"

Tamakloe glanced out the window at the water, then back at her, and lowered his voice in the way of a man who has survived several governments. "Registered out of Meridian. Salvage concern — Meridian Deepwater Recovery, or some such name. Paper company if ever I saw one. The crews wear civvies, but I sold rice to two of them Tuesday, and one of the men paid me with a chit." He held up a slip of paper. "Navy scrip, girl. I'd know it anywhere; my brother drew it for twenty years. They're navy men in borrowed shirts."

"Salvaging what?"

"That," said Tamakloe, "is the interesting part." He leaned on the counter. "Salvage outfits buy charts. Charts cost money. These fellows came up with no charts at all — just coordinates written on a board, like they don't need to look for anything. Like they know exactly where it is." He tapped the counter twice. "And they bought rope. Not lifting rope — cutting rope. Detonation cord, the supplier called it, though he wasn't supposed to sell it and I wasn't supposed to hear. Forty spools."

Ada's hands went cold around the quinine bottle.

"They're going to blow something up," she said.

"They're going to blow something *up*," Tamakloe agreed comfortably, "and whatever they're blowing up, they want nobody watching when they do it. Which tells you what's down there is worth more destroyed than raised." He studied her face, and something in it made him add, more quietly: "Your father used to sit on my porch, you know. Before. Telling me about that ship. I thought him a cracked old man. Now I watch six barges come upriver to make sure a dead ship stays dead, and I begin to think maybe he was the only sane man on this whole river."

* * *

That night Ada did not sleep. She lay in her cell in the nurses' quarters listening to the frogs and the water and, beneath both, a sound she couldn't place at first — a deep, rhythmic thrumming, felt more than heard, traveling up through the ground itself. Engines. Barge engines, idling downriver in the dark, holding station. Whatever they waited for, they were waiting close.

Six tides. That was what the strangers at the gate had said — no. That was what she had calculated herself, lying awake the night after they left, hating herself for calculating it. Six high tides until a fleet like that could reach the wreck site and do whatever it intended to do. Five remained.

She got up and lit the lamp and took out the one thing of her father's she owned: a letter, unsent, found among his effects at the shack and passed along to her by the mission office at Port Meridian with a note saying *we thought you should have it*. It was addressed to her and dated eight months ago and never posted, because Eben Marsh had never posted anything in his life; he saved things up the way the river saved driftwood, meaning always to sort it later.

*Ada,* it began, in handwriting that started strong and degraded as it went, as though the writing of it were a physical descent. *I know you don't want to hear from me. I'm not writing to ask for forgiveness. I'm writing so that somebody besides me knows the truth, because I am an old man and the river takes old men, and if I die with this in my head it dies with me, and then the men who did it will have won twice.*

*I saw the Vasa Star go down. You were fifteen. I came home talking about it and you looked at me like I was drunk and maybe I was, but I saw it. We were camped at the third bend when she came downriver listing hard, no distress signal, no flares, lifeboats still lashed to the davits. Lashed, Ada. A crew doesn't lash its own boats unless it isn't meant to use them. She went down in ninety feet of water in the middle of the channel where no reef and no rock has ever been. I dove the site myself two weeks later, on a borrowed line, and I'll tell you what I found and then never speak of it again: a hole blown in her hull from the inside. From the inside. Ships sink outward. This one sank inward.*

*There was a compartment aft, sealed, that isn't on any survey. I got into it once. There's paper in there — manifests, ledgers, a logbook — and the paper says the ship was carrying cargo she never declared, and that certain officers were paid to carry it, and that when the arrangement was done they killed the ship to kill the record. There was a man in that compartment, Ada. In uniform. He'd been shot before the ship ever flooded. I couldn't bring him up. I've thought about him every day since. Somebody's son. Somebody's brother. Left down there so the paperwork above him would rot quiet.*

*I stole a copy of the manifest location from a clerk in Meridian who owed me. That's the map. It's worth nothing as treasure and everything as truth. If you're reading this, I'm dead, and the map is with a diver named Okonkwo, who promised me he'd find you, and I'm sorry for putting that weight on a stranger, but I trusted his face more than I've trusted anyone since your mother died, and I was right to, I think. Go to him or don't. But if those ships come upriver — if men arrive to bury that wreck a second time — then everything I was too cowardly to say to your face is true, and I was not mad, and neither was your mother, who believed me to the end.*

*You liked guavas. Your mother grew them behind the house at Wabo. I remember everything, Ada. That's the curse of being the witness. Nobody wants the witness to remember.*

*Your father, Eben*

Ada read it four times. On the fourth reading she stopped crying, which surprised her, because she had not known she was crying. The lamp burned steady. Outside, the thrumming continued, patient as tide.

*If those ships come upriver.*

They had come upriver.

* * *

At dawn she climbed the ridge again, and the count was seven.

Seven barges now, plus a gray tender riding shotgun at the fleet's upstream edge, and a helicopter — a helicopter, on the Fly River, where the nearest landing pad was a rumor — had passed overhead twice in the night, its rotor-sound stitching across the sky. The white flags with the blue chevron hung limp in the still air. Down at the bend, the lead barge had begun lowering something over its side on cables: long, segmented shapes, dark and jointed, that Ada's nursing mind insisted on categorizing before her fear could stop it. Pipes? No. Too thick, too short, too many couplings. She had seen demolition cord described in a trauma manual once, in a chapter on mining accidents. Forty spools, Tamakloe had said.

Five tides.

Sister Bernadette found her there again, and this time said nothing at all, simply stood beside her and looked, and after a while said: "Well?"

"Well what?"

"Well, what are you going to do about it?" The old woman turned to her, and her eyes were the pale washed-out blue of a thing bleached by decades of sun, and they held no judgment whatsoever, which was somehow worse than judgment. "You've watched for two days. You've listened on the radio. You've interrogated Tamakloe. You've read your father's letter until I could recite it. You are a woman who gathers evidence, Ada Marsh — it's why you're the best nurse this mission has ever had. So. Evidence gathered. What is the diagnosis, and what is the treatment?"

"The diagnosis—" Ada's voice caught and steadied. "The diagnosis is that my father was telling the truth for thirty years and I let him die alone believing his own daughter thought he was insane."

"And the treatment?"

Ada looked downriver at the seven barges and the limp anonymous flags and the shapes going over the side on cables, and she felt something happen in her chest that she could only describe as a door swinging — not opening, exactly; doors open onto rooms, and this was more like a wall becoming a door, the way a thing you had decided was fixed revealed itself to have been movable all along.

"The treatment," she said, "is that those men are going to destroy the only proof that my father wasn't mad, and the only proof of how a sailor's son ended up shot in a sealed compartment at the bottom of the Fly River, and I am going to stop them. Or fail to. But I'm going to be there either way, and I am not going to watch from a ridge."

Bernadette nodded slowly, as though she'd known the answer before the question, which she probably had. "Then you'll need to leave today. The river is faster than regret."

"I don't even know where they are. Okonkwo. The cook. They left here three days ago—"

"They'll have gone downriver," Bernadette said. "Toward the wreck. Everyone goes toward the wreck eventually; it's the way this whole valley leans." She smiled, thin and dry. "And you have clan ties on the lower river, girl. Your mother's people. There is not a checkpoint between here and the delta that won't wave through a woman of your mother's line — or at least listen to her. I've been thinking about that since the day those two came to the gate. I think the old man chose well."

Ada stared at her. "How long have you known I'd go?"

"Since you asked me where the boats were from," Bernadette said, "instead of asking whether they were dangerous. Dangerous you can hide from. Origins you have to chase." She pressed the tin mug back into Ada's hands. "Drink this. Pack light. Take the quinine. And take this—" she slipped something from her sleeve, a small brass compass on a leather thong, worn smooth by forty years of another woman's pocket "—because every Marsh who ever went to the river went armed with one, and I'll not have it said the mission sent you blind."

Ada held the compass and felt its weight and thought: *this is what it feels like, then. This is what it feels like when fear stops being a wall and becomes a heading.*

* * *

By noon she had signed out of the mission register in her own hand — *Ada Marsh, departing, family business, duration unknown* — and Father Anselm had frowned at the entry and then, unexpectedly, blessed it. By one o'clock she stood at the trading post, and Tamakloe, who had expected her, had already arranged passage: a fuel skiff going downriver as far as Kolo Bend with a load of diesel drums and a pilot named Yaw who owed the mission more favors than he could count.

"Kolo Bend's as far as I go," Yaw said, not unkindly. "Past the bend there's a patrol post, and I've got cargo that doesn't love questions."

"Kolo Bend is fine," Ada said. "I can walk from anywhere."

She stood at the rail of the little skiff as it pulled away from the bank, and the mission slid behind her — the red roof, the guava tree she'd planted her first year, the ridge where she had stood watch like a sentinel over a war she hadn't yet admitted was hers. The river opened ahead, wide and brown and utterly indifferent, carrying seven barges' worth of intention toward a dead ship and one woman's worth of stubbornness toward the same coordinates.

Five tides.

Somewhere down there, a man who had kept a promise to a dying stranger was running out of river, and a cook who remembered corridors was remembering them again, and a smuggler's boat with a magpie's name was either going to save the truth or become part of the wreckage. Her father's map was aboard it. His letter was against her heart, wrapped in oilcloth, next to the brass compass.

At Kolo Bend the skiff tied off at a rickety jetty and Yaw pointed down the bank. "Patrol post is the other direction," he said. "Walk fast, walk quiet, and if anyone asks, you're visiting your mother's people at Wabo. Nobody argues with Wabo."

Ada stepped onto the mud and shouldered her pack and looked downriver, where the afternoon haze lay over the water like gauze, and beyond the haze, faint as a bruise, the masts and cranes of the fleet.

Then she heard it — an engine, upstream, working hard against the current, and around the bend above her came a battered white riverboat with patched plating and a wheelhouse like an old man's hat, and painted on her bow, faded but legible, a name that made Ada's breath catch in her throat:

*Magpie.*

She raised her hand and waved it, and kept waving, and did not stop.
