## 7. The Riverboat Magpie

The Magpie sat in Ferro Lantz's moorage like a drunk sleeping off a decade, half-sunk to her gunwales in tea-colored water, her white paint gone the grey of old bone, her paddle wheel furred with weed. She was ninety feet of rot held together by habit, and she was the fastest thing on the upper Fly River, and Ivo Okonkwo wanted her the way a drowning man wants air.

"You're looking at her wrong," Ferro said.

They stood on the sagging dock above her, morning mist burning off the water, and Ferro Lantz looked exactly like the kind of man who owned a moorage at the end of nowhere: small, neat, sixty-odd, with hands too clean for his trade and eyes that counted everything twice. He wore a linen jacket despite the heat, and beneath it, Ivo had no doubt, a pistol worn smooth by use.

"Wrong how?"

"Everyone looks at her and sees the rot." Ferro spat over the rail, a long brown arc into the water. "You're looking at her bones. That's either very smart or very stupid, and I haven't decided which."

"She was built for the mail run," Ivo said. "Before the war. Shallow draft, twin screws, an engine room big enough to dance in. Somebody re-plated her hull forward of the midship mark — badly, but it held. She'll do thirty knots light if the shafts are true."

Ferro's eyebrows went up a fraction, which Ivo understood was the equivalent of another man's open mouth. "And where does a navy diver learn to read a hull like a love letter?"

"That depends on what you charge for answers."

Ferro laughed — a dry, delighted sound, like paper tearing. He turned and walked the length of the dock, and Ivo followed, watching the boat instead of the man. Up close the rot was worse than it had looked from the water. The rail stanchions were soft as cheese. The wheelhouse windows were plywood painted to look like glass. But the davits were naval, surplus, good ones, and the engine-room hatches were the heavy kind men only fit when they intend to keep an engine alive through things engines should not survive.

"I'll be honest with you, Okonkwo, because honesty is cheaper than lawyers out here," Ferro said, stopping at the dock's end. "Three people have asked about this boat in two years. One wanted to strip her copper. One wanted to burn her for the insurance he didn't have. And you." He turned around. "You looked at her the way a man looks at a woman across a room when he already knows how the night ends. So before we talk price, you tell me what you're doing, because if it's stupid enough, I can't sell her to you. I need her back."

"Back?"

"She was mine once. Before." Ferro waved a hand that took in the moorage, the river, the whole crooked geography of his life. "I sold her to a man who ran her aground on purpose for the insurance, except there wasn't any insurance, and she's been sitting here dying ever since because I'm a sentimental fool."

"You want her running again."

"I want her *running*." Something moved behind Ferro's eyes, quick and hungry. "There's no joy in a fast boat at anchor, Okonkwo. None. A boat like this is a promise, and promises rot faster than planking."

Ivo looked down at the Magpie again. Through the scummed water he could see the shadow of her forefoot, clean-lined, unbroken. Whatever grounding she'd suffered, she'd been pulled off by people who loved her.

"Name your price," he said.

"Money?" Ferro smiled like a knife being drawn. "No. Money I can get anywhere, and money tells me nothing about you. I deal in secrets, diver. You want my boat, you pay me in something true."

* * *

They sat in Ferro's office above the moorage — a room that smelled of diesel, dried fish, and old paper — and Ferro poured rum into two glasses chipped at the rim, and waited.

Ivo turned the glass in his fingers. He thought about all the ways this could go wrong, and then he thought about Eben Marsh dying on the floor of his shack with a grease-stained map pressed into his hand, and about Delphine Okafor standing in the galley of her riverboat kitchen saying *I served on that ship, and nobody has ever asked me what happened to her*, and about Ada Marsh on the mission ridge counting salvage barges with her nurse's steady eyes. Six tides. Five and a half now. There was no version of this where he kept his secrets and also kept his promise.

"The wreck I was working," Ivo said. "The classified one. The one I was fired from."

"Mm." Ferro drank. "The one nobody names."

"It's not on any survey. Not the navy's, not the hydrographic office's, not the commercial charts. A ship that size doesn't sink in forty fathoms of shipping channel and vanish from the record unless someone makes it vanish." Ivo set the glass down. "Here's the secret: I've seen the survey sheets they don't file. There's a compartment on that wreck that appears on none of them. Sealed. Forward hold, port side, below the waterline. Every other space on that ship was mapped to the inch. That one was left blank on purpose."

Ferro had gone very still. It was the stillness of a man doing arithmetic at speed.

"And that's worth a boat."

"That's worth knowing what I know," Ivo said. "Because whoever blanked that compartment killed a ship to keep it blank, and they've hired a fleet to finish the job, and in five days there won't be anything down there but a smear on the sonar. I'm going to get to it first. Your boat is how."

For a long moment Ferro said nothing. Mist drifted past the window. Somewhere below, water lapped against the Magpie's flanks with a patient, eating sound.

"A sealed compartment," Ferro repeated softly. "On a ship whose name I could probably guess, given that I used to run cargo past her buoy line at two in the morning and got waved through every time by a patrol launch that never logged me." He laughed again, quieter this time, without much humor in it. "You know what I think, Okonkwo? I think you just paid me double. Because now I know the secret *and* I know you're the kind of fool who says things like that out loud to a smuggler."

"Are you selling me the boat or not?"

"Yes." Ferro stood, went to a cabinet, and came back with a ledger bound in cracked green leather. He opened it flat, licked a pencil stub, and wrote. "Condition of sale: she's yours as-is, where-is, ghosts included. Price: one secret, rendered, plus —" he wrote another line — "one favor, unspecified, callable within a year, payable in secrets, services, or silence. Non-negotiable."

Ivo read the page upside down. "That favor clause is a leash."

"It's a *relationship*," Ferro said. "Sign it or swim."

Ivo signed. His signature looked strange to him under the smudged lamp — he couldn't remember the last contract he'd signed that wasn't military paper, some form assigning his lungs and his judgment to men in offices far from any water. This one assigned him a rotting boat and a debt to a criminal, and it felt, absurdly, like the most honest document of his life.

Ferro blotted it, tore out the carbon, and handed him a key ring holding a single large iron key. "Engine room. Fuel barge is on my account until sundown — consider it a wedding gift. And Okonkwo." He paused at the door, and for a moment the smuggler's polish dropped and something older looked out. "Whatever's in that compartment — whatever they killed a ship to bury — when you bring it up, bring it up loud. Quiet things drown down here. Loud things make waves, and waves reach shore."

* * *

The Magpie came alive grudgingly, then all at once.

Ivo worked her alone through the afternoon, and the work was a language he'd forgotten he spoke. He pumped her bilges until the water ran clear instead of black. He found the fuel tanks three-quarters full of diesel gone sour, drained them, and took on fresh from Ferro's barge while the sun leaned west. In the engine room, kneeling between the twin diesels in a space that smelled of iron and old oil, he discovered that someone — Ferro, presumably, in his sentimental-fool years — had maintained them beautifully before abandoning her: fresh bearings, dressed valves, a spares locker organized with a surgeon's tidiness. The rot was skin-deep. Underneath, the Magpie was still the boat she'd been built to be.

By dusk she would run. By dusk she had to, because Delphine's message had come in on the mission radio at noon, four crackling words relayed twice upriver: *Have the girl. Hurry.*

He took her out on the flood tide with the last light behind him, and the old girl shook herself like a dog coming out of a river, and then she *ran*. Thirty knots was optimistic; twenty-eight was real, and twenty-eight knots on the upper Fly after dark was less navigation than prayer. Ivo stood at the wheel with the plywood-windowed wheelhouse rattling around him like a drum, charts weighted with a spanner, and felt something in his chest unlock that had been locked since the day command had handed him falsified depth readings and expected a signature.

He'd spent eleven years measuring his life in miles. Miles of channel, miles of decompression, miles between himself and whatever it was other men went home to. Tonight the miles were pointing somewhere, and it was not away.

* * *

He found them at the mission landing an hour past full dark, two figures on the mud with lanterns, and brought the Magpie in slow against the current with both screws backing so her wash wouldn't swamp the dugouts tied along the bank. Delphine came up the boarding ladder like she'd been born on it, took one look around the wheelhouse, and snorted.

"You bought a coffin."

"I bought the fastest coffin on the river."

"She's beautiful," Delphine said, contradicting herself without a flicker, and went below to inspect the galley with the gravity of a woman judging whether a house was fit to raise children in.

Ada Marsh came up the ladder more slowly. She stopped on the deck, wet to the knees, pack on her shoulder, and looked at Ivo with an expression he couldn't read — grief and fury and exhaustion braided together, and underneath it, faintly, something that might have been hope trying to remember how to work.

"My father drew that map," she said. "The coordinates on it. Did you check them?"

"Against every chart I have. They sit inside the forbidden zone, dead center, over the wreck."

"Then he knew." Her voice caught. "All those years he told us he was prospecting. He was sitting on that river the whole time, watching a hole in the water, waiting for—" She stopped. Pressed her lips together. Started again, flatter. "Waiting for someone to ask him what he saw."

Ivo didn't answer right away. Below them, Delphine's boots rang on the deck plates as she claimed the galley for civilization. The current pushed gently at the hull, patient as time.

"He waited for you," Ivo said finally. "He just ran out of tides."

Ada looked at him for a long moment, and then she nodded once, sharply, the way nurses nod when a treatment starts working, and went below to find Delphine, and Ivo rang the engine telegraph to *ahead slow*, and the Magpie turned her bow downriver into the dark.

* * *

They made nine miles before the fleet's glow showed on the horizon — a dirty orange stain low in the southeast, too steady and too organized to be a settlement, the light of a floating town of barges and generators and floodlamps burning all night because men with something to hide sleep badly. Ivo cut the running lights and took her in close under the northern bank, where the channel narrowed and the current did half their work, and set Delphine's nephew's boy — no; there was no boy, there was only the three of them, and that was the whole arithmetic of it — set the watch alone.

Delphine came up at midnight with coffee that could have stripped paint and stood beside him at the wheel, studying the orange stain on the horizon.

"Demolition charges," she said. "Ada heard the dockhands talking at the mission landing. Crates marked mining supplies, but the weight stencils are wrong. Nobody ships that many crates of *mining supplies* to a salvage job." She sipped her coffee. "They're not raising anything, Ivo. They're erasing it."

"I know."

"And you signed papers with Ferro Lantz to race them." She glanced sideways at him. "Do you know what he is? Actually know?"

"I know he sold me a boat and bought a secret, and that the favor clause means he'll come collecting before this is over."

"Then you know more than most people who sign papers with him." Delphine turned her face toward the dark water sliding past. "I cooked for the navy eleven years, Ivo. I know how men talk when they think the help is furniture. If those charges go off with proof still inside that hull—" She didn't finish it. She didn't have to.

Ahead, the river bent, and around the bend a single green navigation light blinked its slow, idiot reassurance, marking a channel that led straight toward the thing they'd all, in their different ways, been swimming from for years.

Behind them, far back in the dark, a searchlight swept the water once, twice, and went dark again.

Someone was checking the river behind the fleet.

Ivo eased the throttles down a whisper and watched the light's reflection die on the black water, and thought about Ferro's pencil scratching out that favor clause, and about how cheap a secret sounded until someone came to collect it.

Five tides left. And somewhere astern, moving quietly against the current, something else was already racing them to the wreck.
