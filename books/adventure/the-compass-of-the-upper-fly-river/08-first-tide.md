## 8. First Tide

The Magpie ran the river like a drunk running from his own shadow — lurching, swearing, but moving.

Delphine Okafor stood at the galley stove with the boat's motion in her knees and her hands steady on the knife, because that was the arrangement she had made with the world long ago: let everything else shake, keep the blade still. Below her feet, Ferro Lantz's old engines coughed and caught, coughed and caught, a rhythm like a man deciding whether to live. Above her head, Ivo Okonkwo worked the wheel with that silence of his, the one she had decided was not emptiness but discipline — a man who had learned that most of what a person might say was ballast.

They had cleared Port Meridian at dusk with forged papers and no lights, and now the Upper Fly unrolled before them black as spilled ink, the banks invisible, the current talking to itself in the dark.

"Chow," she called up the companionway, because a crew fed on schedule was a crew that thought clearly, and clear thinking was about to matter more than any of them wanted to admit.

* * *

She served it on the aft deck under a tarp so the lamplight wouldn't leak over the water — rice and river fish and a pepper sauce hot enough to make Ada Marsh's eyes water on the second bite. Good. A woman who could be made to cry by a pepper could be taught to cry for better reasons later, and Delphine had spent eleven years reading people across a serving line. You learned more about a soul from how it ate than from how it prayed.

There were four of them now, counting herself. Ivo at the head of the crate they used as a table, forearms scarred from lines and props, watching the river more than his plate. Ada beside him, sitting the way her father must have sat — Eben Marsh had eaten at her mess twice, years back, before the fever took his mind apart, and he'd had the same way of holding his spoon like a tool rather than an instrument of pleasure. And Ferro's boy, Tam, sixteen and all elbows, who had come with the boat the way rust comes with iron, and whom nobody had yet decided whether to keep.

"Tonight we work," Ivo said, when the plates were empty. "Delphine."

She wiped her hands on her apron out of habit, though there was nothing on them, and took the chalk Tam had found somewhere — Ferro had left half a case of it in the hold along with the ledgers he'd burned pages from, and Delphine tried not to think about what a man wrote down that he needed to burn.

She drew on the tarp's underside, stretched flat between two posts. First the river, then the wreck.

"The *Vigilant*," she said. "Nine hundred tons when she was whole. Gunboat hull, merchant fit-out, which is why she handled like a bishop trying to dance. I cooked aboard her for three years." Her chalk moved. "You're diving her stern section. The navy's charts will show you the cargo hatch off the main hold, port side. Forget it exists."

"Why?" Ada said.

"Because it doesn't anymore. It collapsed in the second year after she went down — I heard it from a diver who worked the survey before they classified it. The whole hatch frame came down and took the catwalk with it. What you want is here." She tapped the chalk against a point amidships, just forward of the engine room bulkhead. "Coaling door. Small, man-sized, hinged inward. On the surface plans it looks like nothing. Underwater, it's your front door, because the navy never bothered to seal what they never planned to use again."

Ivo leaned in. He studied drawings the way other men studied faces. "And inside?"

"Inside is where it gets honest." She drew the corridors freehand, and her hand did not shake, though something behind her ribs did. "Main corridor runs fore-aft, two meters wide, overhead at one-eighty. Flooded to the deck plates, silt to your shins in places. There are four cross-passages before you reach the compartment bulkhead. The third one is a liar — it looks like it goes through, but there's a collapsed frame halfway down. You go in thinking you'll come out the far side, and you won't. Divers have died in passages half that mean."

"How do you know all this?" Tam asked. "From the galley?"

Delphine smiled without warmth. "Boy, on a gunboat the cook knows the ship better than the captain does. The captain sees the bridge and the chart table. I saw everything — every corridor, because I walked them with provisions; every compartment, because I counted the mouths in them twice a day. When the pumps failed and the water came, I learned the rest." She paused. "I learned the rest in the dark, holding a lantern, listening to her die. You don't forget a thing you learned that way."

Nobody spoke. The engines coughed below. Somewhere off the port bow, a night bird cried once and thought better of it.

"The compartment itself," Ivo said quietly, "is sealed?"

"Welded, if the navy did it, which they did — I watched the welding crew come aboard six weeks before she sank. Official story was grain storage refit." She met his eyes. "We both know what gets stored in a welded compartment on a ship that's about to sink."

"Manifests," Ada said.

"Or men," Delphine said, and let that sit.

Ada's face went very still. Delphine had seen that stillness before, in sailors hearing hull sounds they didn't want to name. The girl looked at the chalk drawing — her father's coordinates translated into steel and floodwater — and then looked away at the river, and said nothing, and helped Delphine stack the plates, which was its own kind of answer.

* * *

The drill began at midnight.

Ivo rigged a mock-up in the hold using crates for bulkheads and rope for corridors, and Delphine walked them through it blind — literally blind, a rag tied over the eyes, because "you'll see nothing down there but what your hands tell you, so your hands had better know the way home." She called the turns. Ivo crawled the route first, once, slow, mapping it into his body. Then Ada. Then Tam, who complained until Delphine told him about the diver who'd died in the lying passage, and after that he complained differently, which was progress.

"Again," she said. "Third cross-passage is a liar. Say it."

"Third cross-passage is a liar," Tam muttered into the dark.

"Again."

"Third cross-passage is a liar."

"Good. Now the coaling door opens—"

"Inward," Ivo's voice said from inside the mock-up, muffled by canvas. "Against you. If the current pushes while you're pulling, you brace left shoulder and pull with the right hand only."

Delphine stopped. In the dark of the hold, with only the sound of the river through the planking, she felt something turn over in her chest — not surprise exactly. Recognition. "That's right," she said. "Who told you that?"

"No one." A pause. "A door is a door."

"A door underwater is a trap that hasn't decided yet," she said. "Remember that phrase. I invented it the hard way."

They drilled until three bells past midnight, until Ada could run the route in ninety seconds blindfolded and Tam could do it without swearing, and then Delphine called it and sat alone in the galley afterward with cold tea, looking at her own hands. They were steady. They were always steady. But tonight they remembered things she had not asked them to remember — the tilt of the *Vigilant*'s deck in her last hour, the way the lamps swung, the sound the men made in the flooded corridor below the galley when the bulkhead held and the air didn't.

She had testified, once, quietly, to a board that had already written its conclusion. She had been transferred within the month, and discharged within the year, and she had carried the layout of that ship in her skull for nine years like a stone she couldn't spit out.

Now someone finally wanted the stone. That was the strange mercy of it. That was also the terror.

* * *

The fight started near dawn, and Delphine heard all of it from the galley, because the Magpie was not a large boat and did not believe in privacy.

It began as navigation talk. She was on deck coiling line when Ada pointed at the chart Ivo had pinned by the wheel and said, "We should cut inside the shoal at Graywater. My father used to run that channel in a dugout. It saves us two hours."

"We run the marked channel," Ivo said.

"The marked channel adds two hours, and you told us the fleet makes the wreck zone by the sixth tide."

"I told you the fleet is fast. I didn't tell you I'd trade our keel for speed." His voice wasn't sharp. That was the thing about Ivo — even his refusals came out level, like depth readings. "Your father ran that channel in daylight, in a boat that drew a hand's width of water, with a man in the bow sounding every stroke. We're doing it at night in a hundred-ton wreck with a boy at the lead line."

"He knew this river better than any pilot the navy ever licensed."

"Then he'd be the first to tell you not to run Graywater blind."

"You didn't know him." Ada's voice changed — Delphine heard the seam split in it. "You've been talking about him like you knew him. Like you know what he'd say. You read his map for one night and suddenly you're the expert on Eben Marsh."

"I read his map," Ivo said, "and I read what was on it. Coordinates measured to a sealed compartment on a classified wreck. Tide tables copied in a clerk's hand. That's not a treasure map, Ada. That's evidence, collected carefully, by someone who understood what he was holding. Thieves don't document their thefts to the fathom."

"So now he's a witness. Last week you wouldn't say a word about him."

"Last week I hadn't studied the map."

"That's not it." Her voice cracked across the deck like a whip. "You don't get to decide what he was. You weren't there. You don't know what he stole or why, or what it cost my mother, or—" She stopped. Breathed. Started again, lower, worse for being lower. "He left us. Do you understand? Whatever noble thing he was doing with that map, he chose it over coming home. Nine years. And now he's dead, and everyone wants to make him a hero because it's convenient for the mission. Well, heroes don't leave little girls at mission schools wondering what they did wrong."

The river talked. The engines coughed. Delphine kept coiling line, slowly, giving the moment room the way you give a burn room.

Ivo was quiet a long time. When he spoke, it was quieter than before, and somehow that carried further.

"My brother drowned when I was nineteen," he said. "Navy patrol boat, upper delta. The report said navigational error. The report was eight sentences long and every sentence was a lie, and I know that because I spent eleven years pulling navy wrecks and learning how reports get written. Nobody ever told me the truth about my brother. Not once. Not a sentence." He turned from the chart and looked at her, and Delphine saw that his level voice had cost him something to maintain. "So when I say your father was a witness, I'm not making him a hero. Heroes are for statues. I'm saying he was a man who held the truth in his hands and refused to put it down, and that cost him everything, including you — and I think he knew exactly what it was costing, every single day, for nine years. That's not a hero, Ada. That's just a man in pain who wouldn't lie. I've wanted to be that man my whole life and mostly failed."

Ada didn't answer. Delphine watched her stand there with the dawn coming up gray behind her, and watched the fury drain out of her shoulders not because it was resolved but because it had nowhere left to sit. After a while she said, "Graywater at first light, then. With Tam on the lead line every stroke. Like my father would have done."

"Every stroke," Ivo agreed.

She went below. Ivo stood at the wheel a while longer, looking at nothing, and then Delphine came up and put a mug of tea in his hand without being asked, because that was the arrangement she had made with the world too: some things get said, some things get fed, and you don't confuse the two.

"She'll come around," Delphine said.

"She's already around." He drank. "She's fighting because trusting us means forgiving him, and she's not ready to forgive him. The anger isn't at me."

Delphine looked at him sideways. "For a man who says he's bad with people, you see them clear enough."

"I count miles," he said. "People are just the terrain."

"And what am I, then? Terrain?"

He almost smiled. It was the first one she'd seen on him, and it was crooked, and it suited him badly, which meant it was real. "You're the cook who sank a ship," he said. "Ferro told me that story the night I bought her. Said you once dropped a whole consignment of the captain's brandy over the rail rather than serve it at a table where a bribe was being discussed."

"Ferro exaggerates." She took back the empty mug. "It was two cases. And the captain deserved to lose them."

* * *

They ran Graywater at first light with Tam swinging the lead and calling depths in a voice that started shaky and ended sure, and the channel gave them their two hours and nearly took the rudder paying for it, and by midmorning the Magpie was making good time down the long straight reach below the shoals with the current helping and the wind quartering kind.

Which was when Tam, aloft on the jury-rigged platform Ferro had once used for spotting revenue boats, called down in a voice pitched wrong.

"Smoke. Two points off the starboard bow. Low, on the water."

Delphine climbed enough of the rigging to see it herself. A smudge against the far bank, gray-brown, deliberate — coal smoke, low and flat, the signature of a heavy boat moving under power. As she watched, a second smudge joined it, farther downriver, angled to converge.

Navy boats ran clean-burning oil these days. But civilian fronts flew civilian flags and burned whatever was cheap, and she had spent enough years in the service to recognize a picket line forming when she saw one.

Below her, Ivo had already seen it. He was standing at the wheel with the chart open, measuring distances with his thumb, and when he looked up at her his face had gone to the place he kept for depth readings — level, cold, exact.

"Kolo Bend," he said. "There's a patrol station at Kolo Bend, and we're going to run straight through it."

Delphine went down to the galley and began, quietly, to hide everything on the boat that had a name written on it.
