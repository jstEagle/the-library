## 9. Checkpoint at Kolo Bend

The river bent at Kolo the way a snake bends when it senses heat — slow, deliberate, coiling back on itself so that a boat traveling it could see both its own wake and what was coming. Ada Marsh stood at the rail of the *Magpie* and watched the bend open ahead of them, and what she saw made her stomach drop through the deck planks.

A patrol boat sat anchored across the channel like a cork in a bottle, gray hull, navy pennant snapping in the morning wind, a machine gun mounted on its foredeck with a young man behind it who looked barely old enough to shave.

"They're waiting for us," she said.

Ivo came up beside her, squinting against the light off the water. "They're waiting for everyone. That's what checkpoints do." But his hand had already gone to the wheel, easing the throttle down, and she saw the tension climb his neck like water rising in a lock.

Delphine emerged from below with a dish towel over her shoulder and flour to her elbows, as if she'd been baking bread instead of memorizing the layout of a drowned ship. She took one look at the patrol boat and said, "Kolo Bend. Of course. They always put them at Kolo Bend — narrow channel, no room to run, and the current does half their work for them."

"You've been stopped here before," Ada said.

"Twice." Delphine's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "Both times I was carrying rice and lying about it."

Ferro Lantz appeared from the shadow of the pilothouse, where he'd spent most of the morning pretending not to listen to everything. He held a leather folder against his chest the way a priest holds a missal. "Papers are ready," he said. "Charter vessel, licensed out of Port Meridian, cargo manifest lists agricultural machinery parts. Boring. Nobody searches boring."

"And if they search anyway?" Ivo asked.

Ferro's eyes flicked downward — toward the hold, where the dive gear was stashed under false floorboards, where Delphine's hand-drawn diagrams of the wreck were rolled inside a tube of oilcloth, where every single thing aboard that mattered was one careless pry bar away from discovery. "Then we're all going to learn something about each other," he said, "that I'd rather we didn't."

* * *

Ada had grown up on this river, or near enough to it. Her mother had been born two days' walk from Kolo Bend, in a village whose name Ada had never learned to write properly because her mother spoke it more than she spelled it. Her mother had died when Ada was eleven, and after that her father had gone quiet in the particular way of men who have lost the person who did all their talking for them. Eben Marsh had loved his daughter, she never doubted that. He had also loved a map, a grudge, and a story about a ship, and somewhere in the last years of his life those loves had tangled until she couldn't tell which one he was reaching for when he reached for her.

She thought about him now, watching the gray hull grow larger. She thought about the way he'd taught her to read water — *the river tells you where it's been, Ada-girl, you just have to ask politely* — and how she'd sworn, standing over his grave with wet eyes and dry anger, that she was done asking the river anything.

And yet here she was. On a stolen-smuggled-borrowed riverboat, running forged papers past a navy checkpoint, chasing the same obsession that had hollowed her father out like a rotten log.

Not chasing it, she corrected herself. Chasing *them*. The men on that boat, or men like them, the ones who had come to the mission school three months ago in civilian clothes with government manners, asking questions about her father's papers, his visitors, his last known movements. She hadn't understood then what they wanted. She was beginning to.

"Girl." Ferro's voice, low, at her elbow. "You've gone pale."

"I'm fine."

"You're not. And I need you to be, because here's the thing about my papers." He glanced forward, making sure Ivo was occupied with the wheel and Delphine with her flour. "They're good. They're very good. I paid a man in Port Meridian who is very good at this sort of thing. But good forgery only survives a bored inspector. If they get curious — really curious — paper won't save us. Only a person will."

"What person?"

He looked at her. Just looked, with those flat merchant's eyes that weighed everything, and Ada felt the weight of what he wasn't saying settle onto her shoulders before he said it.

"You," he said. "You have your mother's face. And your mother's people have fished this bend since before there was a navy to patrol it. Half the petty officers on these river boats grew up in villages that trade with your mother's clan. If an inspector looks up from that folder and sees a stranger, we're cargo to be searched. If he sees *kin* — even distant, even doubtful kin — we're neighbors passing through, and neighbors get waved along."

"My mother's clan doesn't know me. I was raised at the mission."

"They don't need to know you. They need to *recognize* you. There's a difference, and the difference is a scar, a way of tying a headwrap, a name spoken correctly. Do you remember your mother's clan name?"

"Of course I remember it." It came out sharper than she intended. "I say it every night. It's the last thing she made me promise to keep."

"Good," Ferro said. "Because in about four minutes, you're going to say it to a man with a machine gun."

* * *

The patrol boat's whistle sounded twice — the universal grammar of *heave to* — and Ivo brought the *Magpie* around into the current with a smoothness that Ada was beginning to recognize as his particular form of prayer. Lines went across. Fenders dropped. The hulls kissed, and a boarding party of three came over the gap: a lieutenant with a clipboard and a sunburn, a bosun's mate with a crowbar he carried less as tool than as suggestion, and a boy at the rear holding a rifle the way boys hold things they've been told are important.

"Papers," the lieutenant said.

Ferro handed over the folder with the easy boredom of a man who had done this a hundred times, and maybe he had. The lieutenant read. The wind moved the pennant. Somewhere upriver, a fish eagle screamed once and gave up.

"Charter vessel," the lieutenant read aloud. "Out of Meridian."

"That's the one," Ferro said.

"Carrying?"

"Agricultural machinery parts. Couplings, drive shafts, the unglamorous innards of progress." Ferro smiled. "You're welcome to look, Lieutenant, but I warn you, it's duller than sermons."

The lieutenant did not smile back. He looked up, and his eyes went across the deck — Ivo at the rail, arms crossed, wearing the studied blankness of a man with nothing to declare; Delphine in her kitchen apron, radiating the harmless authority of a cook; and then Ada, standing slightly apart, and Ada watched his gaze snag on her the way a hook snags on a submerged branch.

Something changed in his face. Not suspicion. Something older than suspicion. Recognition of a kind.

"You," he said. "Where are you from?"

"Upstream," Ada said, which was true and useless.

"No. Before that. Your mother's people." He switched languages without warning — the river tongue, her mother's tongue, the language Ada kept folded away like a dress too fine for daily wear — and asked her clan name.

And here was the moment, Ada thought later, where the whole expedition balanced on the head of a pin. Because she could lie, and liars got searched. Or she could speak, and speaking meant claiming something she'd spent eleven years keeping at arm's length.

She heard her mother's voice: *the river tells you where it's been.*

She answered in the river tongue, the clan name rolling out of her mouth with an accent she hadn't used since childhood, and she watched the lieutenant's whole posture change — shoulders dropping, clipboard lowering, the machinery of officialdom grinding down into something human.

"Sister-village," he said. "My grandmother was from the fishing camps below the falls. You have the look of the marsh-clan women. The tall ones."

"The tallest," Ada said, and heard Delphine make a small sound that might have been a suppressed laugh.

The lieutenant asked about her mother. Ada told him — some of it, the true parts, the death, the village upstream, the mission school where she nursed now — and as she talked she watched the crowbar-carrying bosun drift toward the hold hatch, and something cold slid down her spine.

"Lieutenant," the bosun said. "Standard procedure. Cargo inspection."

"It's a charter boat, Osei. Look at it. It's barely floating."

"Standard procedure," the bosun repeated, and there was something in his flatness that made Ada's skin prickle. This one wasn't from any sister-village. This one was from nowhere, from the placeless corps of men who follow orders the way water follows gravity.

The lieutenant sighed and waved permission, and the bosun went below with the boy and the crowbar, and Ada stood on the deck of the *Magpie* listening to boots on planks above the thing they were all hiding, and discovered that terror, properly concentrated, has a clarifying effect. Her fear burned off like morning mist and left underneath it something hard and bright and furious.

Her father had seen something on that ship. Men had hounded him to his deathbed for it. And now the same navy was stopping honest boats on an honest bend, prying at floorboards, because whatever Eben Marsh had witnessed was dangerous enough that six months after his death they were still hunting its shadows.

*Come on,* she thought at the floorboards. *Be boring. Be full of couplings.*

* * *

The wait lasted eleven minutes. Ada counted them by her heartbeat, which was poor timekeeping but excellent drama.

When the bosun came back up, his face wore the expression of a man who has found nothing and resents it. "Hold's full of scrap iron," he reported. "Machinery parts, like the manifest says."

"Told you," Ferro said pleasantly. "Duller than sermons."

The lieutenant was already stamping the folder. He handed it back to Ferro, then turned to Ada and, in the river tongue, said something that undid her: *"Your mother's people say a traveler who speaks the clan name is never lost. Go well, cousin."*

"Go well," she managed, and the lines came in, and the engines woke, and the *Magpie* slid free of the checkpoint like a splinter leaving skin.

Nobody spoke for a full mile. Then Delphine exhaled explosively, set down her dish towel, and said, "I have never been so happy to be boring in my life."

Ivo cut the throttle to a crawl and turned. "That bosun," he said quietly. "He wasn't checking cargo. He knew what he was looking for."

"He was looking for dive gear," Ferro said. "Or charts. Or—"

"He was looking for *us*," Ada said.

They all looked at her. She realized she was gripping the rail hard enough to whiten her knuckles, and made herself let go.

"At the mission," she said, "when the men came asking about my father, one of them had a naval bearing even out of uniform. Same posture as that bosun. Same nothing-face. They weren't investigating a dead prospector's paperwork. They were tracking the map." She swallowed. "They know someone has it. Maybe not who. But that checkpoint wasn't routine — the lieutenant was bored, but the bosun wasn't. Routine inspections don't bring crowbars onto charter boats carrying farm parts."

"So they're sweeping the river," Ivo said slowly, "ahead of the salvage fleet. Clearing the water of anyone who might reach the wreck first."

"Which means," Delphine said, and her voice had gone very quiet, the quiet of a woman remembering a galley at night, "they expect someone to try."

"There's more." Ada hesitated, then committed. "While the bosun was below, the boy with the rifle — he was nervous, chattering. I asked him, in the river tongue, what the fleet was doing downriver, why so many boats. He said—" she closed her eyes, replaying it, the boy's words in her mother's language, words she almost hadn't understood because they made no sense — "he said they weren't loading salvage gear. Cranes, yes, but also crates stenciled for demolition stores. Shaped charges. He thought it was strange too. He said, *why would you blow up a ship you're trying to raise?*"

The silence that followed had weight, like deep water.

"They're not salvaging the wreck," Ivo said at last. Each word came out flat and separate, laid down like stones across a current. "They're destroying it. And everything in it. Everything *under* it."

"The manifest," Delphine whispered. "If it's still aboard — if my captain sealed it in that compartment like Eben believed — then in six tides there won't be a compartment. There won't be a wreck. There'll be a gravel bank and an official report saying the site was cleared for navigation."

"Six tides," Ferro murmured. "Minus one. We lost half a day at that sandbar, and the checkpoint cost us the morning."

Ada looked downriver, where the bend straightened out toward the horizon and the forbidden zone beyond it. Somewhere down there, barges were massing under civilian flags, loading crates of shaped charges with navy hands. Somewhere down there lay the ship her father had spent his last years circling like a moth around a cold flame, and the proof that would burn the liars clean.

She had joined this crew angry — angry at her father, mostly, for dying with a map in his fist instead of a daughter in his arms. Standing on the deck now, with the checkpoint falling away behind her and demolition charges waiting ahead, she felt the anger transmute into something far more useful.

Purpose. Cold, specific, and aimed.

"Then we don't stop," she said. "We don't sleep, we don't haggle, we don't lose another hour. My father traded his life for what's in that hold. I'm not letting them bury it twice."

Ivo looked at her for a long moment — the first time, she thought, that he'd truly looked at her rather than merely watched her — and then he pushed the throttle forward and the *Magpie* leaned into the current like a bird taking wind.

"One problem," Ferro said from the shadows of the pilothouse, and his voice had an odd note in it that Ada couldn't name. "There's a boat coming up fast behind us. Flying no flag at all."

Ada turned. Far down the silver thread of their wake, a sleek hull was eating the distance between them, and she recognized it even at that range, because she'd seen it tied up at the mission landing once, three months ago, the day the men in civilian clothes came asking about her father.

It was the same boat.
