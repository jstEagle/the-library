## 10. Second Tide

The river turned against us an hour after midnight.

I felt it before I heard it — a change in the way the water spoke to the hull, the low mutter beneath the deck plates going taut and argumentative, like a man rehearsing a quarrel. I had the wheel, running dark with only the binnacle lamp's red glow and the sound of water to steer by, and when the current shifted a half-point to starboard I knew we'd crossed out of the main channel and into the braided shallows above the Kolo Narrows.

"Delphine," I said quietly. "Kill the stove."

She was below, keeping the galley lamp lit against regulations, warming coffee nobody needed at that hour because nobody intended to sleep. Her boots came up the companionway ladder in six seconds flat. She'd been a navy cook for eleven years; she understood what it meant when a man at the wheel asked for silence instead of explaining why.

"Bar?" she said.

"Maybe. Or the channel's moved." Rivers up here were living things. They dragged their beds around at night the way a sleeper drags a blanket. "The chart's three years old and Ferro's memory is older."

"Charming," she said, and went forward to the bow without another word, where she could lean over the rail and listen for the water going shallow. That was the old way, the way they'd run boats up this river before anyone thought to put an echo sounder in a wheelhouse: a woman at the bow with her ear cocked, reading the river's voice. Delphine had learned it on her first ship, the one that now lay forty fathoms down with its secrets bolted inside it.

Ada came up from the cabin, hair loose, eyes wide awake. She'd slept maybe two hours since Kolo Bend and it showed in her like a fever — not weakness but a kind of burning. She'd talked her way past a navy patrol two nights ago using her dead mother's clan name and a lie smooth enough to float a barge, and since then she'd been someone new. Or rather, someone who had always been there, underneath the mission-school starch.

"Where are we?"

"Six miles above the Narrows. Maybe less." I kept my voice level. "The current's pushing us toward the north channel and I don't trust it."

"Should I wake Ferro's pilot's manual?" she said, deadpan, and I almost smiled. She'd taken to reading the smuggler's annotated river guide by lamplight, memorizing soundings the way she'd once memorized pharmacology. She carried it up now, along with a hurricane lamp she didn't light.

The Magpie was a hundred and ten feet of rot held together by paint, prayer, and Ferro Lantz's talent for selling junk as history. Her engines were two marine diesels older than I was, rebuilt by my own hands in a boatyard that smelled of creosote and betrayal, and they ran sweet — that much I'd guaranteed when I signed for her. But sweet engines don't help you when the river decides to stand up.

"Depth's falling," Delphine called from the bow. "I can hear gravel."

I eased the throttles back. The diesels dropped from a growl to a murmur. Behind me, Ada held the unlit lamp like something she was guarding.

"Slack water's in ninety minutes," she said. "The Narrows run at six knots on the ebb. If we're not through before—"

"I know the arithmetic, Ada."

"I know you know. I'm saying it out loud so the river knows we know."

That was her way now. She narrated danger the way her father's map had narrated the river — every bend named, every depth penciled in, as if naming a thing gave you power over it. Eben Marsh had drawn that map with a dying man's shaking hand, and his daughter had inherited the habit of it. I was starting to understand that she wasn't afraid of the river. She was afraid of arriving at the wreck unprepared, of standing in front of her father's grave someday and having nothing to show for his last request.

The river made its decision for us.

It came as a shudder through the keel — a long, grinding, fingernails-on-slate shudder that ran from the stem aft and stopped dead beneath the wheelhouse, and then the Magpie stopped, all hundred and ten feet of her, as if the river had put its palm flat against her bow and said *no*.

The momentum threw us forward. Delphine caught the rail. Ada caught me. The diesels, still turning, complained, and I hauled the throttles to zero before the shafts could chew themselves against the sand.

"Sandbar," Delphine called back, unnecessarily. "Port bow, hard aground. We're on it like a fly on jam."

I stood in the red dark of the wheelhouse and listened to the river run past us, mocking.

* * *

We went over the side at first light, because there was nothing else to do.

The sandbar was a long gray tongue of the river's own making, maybe forty meters of wet sand and gravel lying just under the surface on the bar's seaward edge, and the Magpie had driven her port bow up onto it like a drunk onto a church pew. The current broke white around our stem and ran fast along our starboard side, and every minute it ran, it was packing sand in under the hull, welding us to the bar a little more firmly.

"Kedging," Delphine said, standing hip-deep in the shallows with her sleeves rolled, looking the situation over with the same expression she'd once given a galley full of spoiled stores. "Carry the kedge anchor out in the skiff, drop it in the deep channel astern, and winch ourselves off backward. I've done it a hundred times."

"On a ship with a working capstan," I said.

"On a ship with *you*," she said. "Which is better."

Ada waded out to join us, trousers rolled to the knee, the pilot's manual wrapped in oilcloth and tucked under her arm as if she might need to consult it while standing in a river. The morning was gray and warm and smelled of wet silt. Upstream, somewhere beyond the bend, the Narrows were waiting, and behind us, somewhere downriver and gaining, a fleet of barges flying honest civilian flags over dishonest intentions was making six knots toward the same coordinates we were dying to reach.

We had six tides. We had already spent two. And now the river had taken a third and was holding it out of reach.

"Options," Ada said. It wasn't a question. She'd learned that from watching me, I think — that when a thing goes wrong you don't mourn it out loud, you list what's left.

"Kedge off at the next slack, around noon," I said. "If the sand hasn't set like concrete. If it has, we lighten ship — run everything movable aft, maybe take the skiff and ferry weight ashore — and grind ourselves off on the next flood tide, tonight."

"Tonight's tide puts us at the Narrows in the dark, in the ebb, in a boat we've already proven can't read her own channel," Delphine said.

"Yes."

"Good," she said. "I hate it when the plan is comfortable."

We worked. There's a particular honesty in physical work that I've trusted all my life, more than charts, more than instruments: you put the anchor in the skiff and you row it out against the current and you drop it where the water goes dark, and then you come back and you put your hands on the winch handle and you pull, and either the ship moves or she doesn't, and the river doesn't care how you feel about it. By eight o'clock we had the kedge set and the winch cranked to a singing tension and the Magpie had shifted perhaps the length of my forearm, groaning, and then stopped, and the sand under her port bow had the final word.

We broke for water and hard bread on the foredeck, three of us sitting in a row with our boots steaming in the weak sun, and the river ran past, indifferent and beautiful, the way it always is when it's beating you.

It was Ada who broke the silence, and she broke it sideways, the way she did.

"You never talk about yourself," she said. "Delphine told me about the *Vigilant*, about the night she smelled the smoke in the galley and knew. You told us about the hatch, the collapsed cargo hatch, the compartment that isn't on any survey. You'll talk about the wreck for hours. But you — Ivo Okonkwo, salvage diver, fired for refusing to fudge a sounding — you're a closed hold."

"Some holds are sealed for a reason," I said.

"I know." She looked out at the water. "My father's was. It killed him."

Delphine said nothing. She was peeling an orange with a cook's knife in four unbroken turns of the peel, and she was watching me over it, and she was not going to rescue me. She'd made her own confession at Kolo Bend, in the dark, about the night the ship died and what the officers had eaten an hour before it happened, and she'd done it plainly, without drama, because that was her way: truth served like food, hot and without apology.

The sandbar held the ship. The fleet held the river ahead of us. There was nowhere to be except here, and no work to do until the tide turned, and I had learned — slowly, the way you learn anything that matters — that a crew is a thing you build out of what you're willing to hand over.

"My brother's name was Tomas," I said.

Delphine's knife stopped.

"He was older than me by two years. We grew up on the coast, south of here, in a fishing family — my father fished, his father fished. Tomas joined the navy at seventeen because the navy had engines and our village had sails, and he was the kind of man engines loved. He could hear a bearing going bad before the bearing knew it himself." I turned the hard bread over in my hands. "I followed him in at eighteen. He got me my first posting. He taught me to dive. He used to say the sea keeps better books than any clerk — everything you put in, it writes down, and everything it takes, it writes that down too, and one day it balances."

"What happened to him?" Ada asked. Quietly. She knew the shape of the answer already. People who've lost someone recognize the weight of it in another person's voice.

"His ship went down. The *Corvus*. A coastal patrol vessel, thirty-one souls aboard, lost with all hands in what the board of inquiry called a navigational error in heavy weather." I looked at my hands, at the rope scars across the knuckles, at the diver's calluses. "Tomas was the engineer. The *Corvus* was a sound ship. I know because I'd worked on her. Her steering gear had been overhauled six weeks before, and I overhauled it — my own two hands, every bolt. A navigational error in heavy weather doesn't explain why the survivors' raft from the *Corvus* washed up three months later with the sea cocks opened from the inside."

Delphine set the orange down.

"You dove it," she said.

"Off the books. Years later, when I had my own rig and no employer asking questions. The *Corvus* lies in ninety feet off Cape Serrano." I heard my own voice go flat, the way it does when I'm reading a gauge. "She was carrying cargo. Not navy cargo. Baled goods, crated liquor, sealed drums — a smuggler's hold in a warship's hull. And the sea cocks were open, and the engine room telegraph was at *full astern*, and there was a hull breach on the starboard side that no rock and no reef could have made, because there is no rock and no reef within two miles of where she lies."

Ada had gone very still.

"The navy scuttled her," Delphine said. Not a question. She'd served long enough to know the shape of the thing.

"The navy scuttled her," I said, "or the men using her to run cargo scuttled her when the run went wrong, and the board called it weather because the alternative was calling it murder, and murder has names attached. Tomas was below. He'd have gone to the engines. He'd have stayed at the engines, because that's who he was — the man who stays at the engines. And whoever opened those sea cocks opened them knowing he was there."

The river ran. Somewhere a bird cried out over the water and got no answer.

"I reported what I found," I said. "To the maritime authority. I had photographs. I had a piece of the hull plating cut from the breach — the edge was cut from inside, with a torch; I still have it, wrapped in wax cloth, in a box I've carried through six postings. You know what happened. Nothing happened. The file was closed. My dive permit was reviewed. My next contract was in a different district, and the one after that was farther, and I understood the lesson they were teaching, which was: the sea keeps books, and so do powerful men, and theirs balance too, as long as the evidence stays down where the water is dark."

"And so you count your life in miles," Ada said.

"Distances are honest," I said. "You travel them or you don't. Years lie — a year can be nothing, a year can be a man sitting in a wheelhouse running a classified wreck for men who order him to falsify soundings, and the year counts the same either way. But a mile is a mile. I told myself if I put enough water behind me, I'd eventually outrun the *Corvus*." I shook my head. "You can't. The *Corvus* is in every wheelhouse. She's under this one."

Ada set down the bread. She reached over and put her hand on my forearm — a diver's forearm, scarred and salted — and she didn't say *I'm sorry*, which would have been the easy thing, the thing people say when they want the confession to be over. She said:

"My father watched them sink a ship too."

"I know."

"He ran because he had a daughter. He drew the map because he was dying and a dead man can't testify, but paper can." Her grip tightened. "Tomas stayed at the engines. My father ran upriver. And the men who did both things are the same men, Ivo. The manifest in that compartment — if it's there — it names them. It could name the ones who sank your brother's ship."

"It might," I said. "Or it might name different men, and the *Corvus* stays a navigational error forever."

"Then we raise it and find out," she said. "For Tomas. For my father. For the thirty-one."

Delphine picked her orange back up and finished the peel in one long unbroken spiral and handed sections to each of us, and that was her benediction, her way of saying the confession had been received and the crew had absorbed it and we were now, all three of us, carrying one another's dead.

"That's why you refused the falsified soundings," she said. "On the classified wreck. That's why you wouldn't fudge the depth readings even to save your posting. You've already watched them bury one ship's truth. You weren't going to hand them the shovel for a second."

"I was tired," I said.

"You were *done*," she said. "There's a difference, and the river knows it, and now we do too."

* * *

The flood tide came at noon, and with it the river's forgiveness, grudging as all its forgiveness is.

We kedged again with the water lifting under the hull, and this time the Magpie moved — a foot, then a yard, then a grinding, shuddering slide as her keel broke free of the sand that had held her and the river took her back the way it takes everything, without apology and without memory. We ran her astern into the deep channel, swung her, and stood up the river toward the Narrows with the ebb still hours away and the sun falling west behind the ridge line.

Ada had the manual open on the chart table, reading soundings aloud. Delphine had the bow, reading water. And I had the wheel, and for the first time in longer than I could measure, the wheelhouse didn't feel like a sealed hold.

We made the Narrows at the turn, running the white water with the current at our back and both engines at full, and the Magpie — rot and paint and prayer — rose to it like she'd been waiting her whole rotten life to be asked. Delphine called the channel from the bow, voice steady over the roar. Ada called the soundings. And when we shot out the far side into the long dark reach below the wreck zone, all three of us were laughing, the ragged laughter of people who have been afraid together and come through.

Then the laughter stopped, all at once, the way it does.

Because there, anchored in the reach ahead of us, black against the last of the light, were the running lights of the salvage fleet — all six barges, arrived early, floodlights already sweeping the water over the wreck.

And tied alongside the nearest barge, its own lanterns lit and unmistakable, was a boat I knew the way you know a scar.

Ferro Lantz's boat. Ferro Lantz, who had sold us the Magpie and taken his payment in secrets — and who had just sold ours to the fleet.
