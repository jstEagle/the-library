## 11. Ferro's Price

Delphine Okafor had cooked for two hundred men on a ship that was murdered, and she had learned one truth in the galley's steam and grease: you could always taste a lie before you heard it. It came through in the way a man held his cup. In the pause before he said *no trouble here*. In the arithmetic of his eyes, tallying what you were worth against what it would cost to keep you.

Ferro Lantz drank his rum the way he did everything — like a man settling an account.

He came up on them at midmorning of the third day, when the river ran wide and brown and lazy between walls of cane, and none of them were watching the water behind because all of them were watching the water ahead. Delphine was at the rail of the *Magpie* scraping rust into the current when she heard the engine — not the Magpie's dying cough but something younger, meaner, a long-shaft outboard running half-throttled, the sound of a man who wanted to be heard.

"Company," she said, and didn't turn around. "And we know him."

Ivo came up from below with a wrench still in his hand, as if a wrench would help. Ada appeared in the companionway with her father's map rolled tight against her chest, and Delphine saw her knuckles whiten around the oilcloth and thought: *there it is. The girl already knows who it is.*

The boat that swung alongside was a patched skiff with a canvas dodger gone green with mold, and standing in it with one boot on the gunwale and a grin like a knife laid flat on a table was Ferro Lantz himself. He looked exactly as Delphine remembered him from the port markets — small, neat, wrong. A man built like a clerk who moved like a cat burglar, with rings on three fingers and a fourth finger bare where a ring used to be and wasn't anymore, and everyone on the river knew why: he'd lost it to a debt collector who'd wanted more than the ring, and Ferro had talked his way down to the finger. He was proud of that story. He told it often. That was the thing about Ferro — he mistook survival for cleverness, and he'd survived long enough to believe it.

"Ivo Okonkwo," Ferro called across the gap. "You're a hard man to find. No, wait." He tilted his head. "You're an easy man to find. You bought the slowest boat on this river and pointed it at the most forbidden water on this river. I found you by asking where a fool would go."

"Ferro." Ivo set the wrench down on the rail, deliberately, where the man could see it. "We're midriver."

"We're midriver," Ferro agreed pleasantly, "which means nobody's watching, which is my favorite kind of place. Throw me a line, Captain. I've come to collect."

* * *

They let him aboard because there was no sense in letting him circle. Delphine made tea because making tea was what her hands did while her mind worked, and she watched Ferro Lantz settle into the Magpie's rotting deckhouse like a rat inspecting a pantry he intended to own. He complimented the boat. He complimented the tea. He complimented Ada — "Eben Marsh's girl, I'd have known those eyes anywhere; your father owed me eleven pounds and died owing them, no offense meant, dead men are my favorite creditors" — and Ada looked at him the way a nurse looks at a wound she's decided not to treat.

Delphine poured four cups and gave Ferro the chipped one.

"You sold us this boat," Ivo said. "Paid in full. Secrets, you said. I paid your price."

"I did, and you did, and it was a fair trade, and I've come back because fair trades are just the opening bid." Ferro turned his cup, and Delphine marked how his eyes went past Ivo's shoulder, past Ada, to the chart table where the oilcloth roll lay under a tin plate as if a plate could hide anything from a man like this. "You see, Captain, I've been doing some thinking since Kolo Bend. Dangerous habit. And my thinking goes like this: the navy pays better than you do."

The word landed in the cabin like a dropped pan. Delphine kept pouring. Her hands did not shake. Twenty years in a naval galley taught you that the steadiest thing in a sinking ship is the cook, because if the cook runs, everyone runs.

"The fleet," Ferro went on, conversational, awful. "Six barges under civilian flags, and every flag a lie, and every barge carrying demolition stores instead of salvage gear — yes, I know about the charges, I sold the fuse wire to a quartermaster with a gambling problem, don't look so shocked, I sell everything to everyone, that's the whole of my philosophy. They'll reach the wreck inside three tides. When they do, whatever's down there stops existing. And whoever's been asking questions about it—" he sipped his tea, delicately, "—stops being a question."

"So you came to warn us," Ada said. Flat. Cold. Good girl.

"I came to *invoice* you." Ferro smiled. "The warning is free. Consider it a courtesy between business partners. The invoice is the map."

"No," Ivo said.

"You haven't heard the amount yet."

"There's no amount. The map isn't mine to sell. It belongs to a dead man's promise, and this boat doesn't carry cargo like that."

Ferro set down his cup and leaned forward, and for the first time the grin fell away and Delphine saw the actual creature underneath, the one that had talked a debt collector down from a hanging to a finger. What lived under the charm was hunger, patient and total, the hunger of a man who had spent his whole life one bad week from nothing and had decided somewhere along the way that he would rather be the wolf than the meat.

"Eben Marsh stole that map from a navy paymaster's clerk," Ferro said softly. "I know because I introduced them. I know because I took a cut. I know because when the clerk turned up floating face-down past the mission landing six weeks later, I asked myself who else knew what was in that tube, and I've been asking ever since. That map is the only paper on this river worth killing for, and I am the only man positioned to sell it — to the navy, to the tribunal, to the smugglers whose operation it buries, take your pick, they all pay. You're sitting on a fortune, Captain, and you're using it to hold a table level."

"It's not a fortune," Delphine said.

Everyone looked at her. She hadn't meant to speak. But the words were out now, and she was a woman who finished what she started, meals and sentences alike.

"It's a receipt," she said. "For a murder. And receipts don't spend, Ferro. They burn the hand that holds them. Ask the clerk."

Ferro's eyes flicked to her, and something moved behind them — recalculation, fast and fluid, a merchant repricing goods. "The famous cook," he said. "Okafor. You served on the *Vigilant*, didn't you? Third mess. I remember the name from the survivor lists. You fed the men the night she went down."

"I fed forty-one men breakfast the morning she went down," Delphine said. "Eleven of them are alive today. So when I tell you what that paper is worth, understand that I'm the only person at this table who has already paid the price you're haggling over."

* * *

He stayed another hour. That was the worst of it — the hour. A man who meant you harm and had decided would be quick about it; a man still deciding was the dangerous one, and Ferro lingered like smoke. He walked the deck. He admired the engine Ivo had coaxed back from the dead. He asked Ada questions about her father that sounded kind and were baited hooks — *did he ever mention a strongbox, did he ever write to anyone in Port Meridian* — and Ada answered each one with a nurse's perfect blankness, giving him nothing, and Delphine could have kissed her.

Delphine herself washed dishes. It gave her reason to stand at the little basin with her back half-turned and her ears wide open, and it let her think, and what she thought was arithmetic.

That was the beat she couldn't get out of her head, the one Ferro had named without knowing he'd named it: *loyalty tested by arithmetic.* Because here was the sum she kept arriving at, no matter how she arranged the figures. Ferro Lantz had sold them the boat. Ferro Lantz had sold fuse wire to the men coming to destroy the wreck. Ferro Lantz had introduced a thief to a clerk and taken a cut of a theft that ended in a drowning. Every column of the man's ledger read the same direction. There was no version of Ferro's history in which he rode with them to the end out of sentiment. Sentiment was not a currency he accepted.

But — and here the sum bent strange — he had come alone. One skiff, one man, no escort, no signal flags, no second boat lurking in the cane. If he'd already sold them, the fleet would have been here, or the patrol, or at minimum a pair of hard eyes taking notes from the bank. Instead: Ferro, tea, and a demand. Which meant either he hadn't sold them yet — or he was selling them right now, slowly, keeping them talking and afloat until his buyers arrived, and the thought slid into Delphine's chest like a fishhook.

She dried her hands. She carried the teapot out and refilled his cup, and while she poured she smiled at him, the smile she'd used on seasick admirals and drunken captains and once, memorably, on a mutinous chief steward, the smile that said *I am on your side* without committing her to any side at all.

"Ferro," she said, low, just for him, "walk with me to the stern a moment. I have something to show you, and I'd rather the children didn't see."

Something lit in his face — greed is a lamp, it shows through everything — and he followed her aft, past the wheel, past the rusting davits, to the stern rail where the wake unspooled brown and endless behind them.

Delphine reached into her apron pocket and brought out a roll of oilcloth, tied with tarred string, exactly like the one Ada carried. She put it in his hand.

His fingers closed on it and she watched the whole war happen in his face — the hunger, the triumph, the swift mental auction — and she watched him weigh it, feel its weight, judge its weight, and believe it.

"That's a copy," she said. "There are two. The original stays with us. You take yours to whoever pays, and here's what happens: they open it, they compare it against their own surveys within a day, and they discover it's a tracing on butcher's paper with the coordinates shifted half a mile into the falls. And then they ask you one question, Ferro, only one: *who gave you the copy, and where is the real one?* And you will have to answer, and the answer is a woman and a diver and a girl on a slow boat, and they will come for us either way — but they will come for *you first*, because you're the one holding the worthless paper, and you'll be standing right there when they ask."

Ferro's thumb moved along the oilcloth. His eyes had gone very still.

"But," Delphine said, and here she leaned close, close enough to smell the rum and the river on him, "if you sail away right now and never breathe a word — if you decide that the smart money is on the people who trusted you with a copy — then someday, when this is over and the officers are in irons and the papers are screaming, there will be a tribunal witness list. And names on that list get paid. Protection, pardons, prize money. The navy pays its informers, Ferro, but tribunals pay their *witnesses*, and witnesses eat at the captain's table."

She stepped back. She let him do the arithmetic. She watched him do it — she could nearly hear the abacus clicking behind his eyes, greed against fear, fear against greed, the only two weights he'd ever owned.

"You're bluffing," Ferro said at last, but his voice had lost its floor.

"Of course I'm bluffing," Delphine said pleasantly. "I'm a cook. We bluff constantly. Half of what I ever served was yesterday's soup wearing today's garnish. The question, Mr. Lantz, is whether you can afford to find out which part was the bluff." She tapped the roll in his hand. "Keep it. My gift. Consider the debt between you and Eben Marsh settled — eleven pounds and a daughter, both forgiven. And consider, on the long row home, which side of this river has ever actually paid you."

* * *

He left without another word about the map. He shook Ivo's hand — Ivo took it the way a man takes a snake he's decided not to kill — and he tipped an imaginary hat to Ada, and he stood in his skiff a moment longer than he needed to, looking back at the Magpie with an expression Delphine couldn't file anywhere. Not hatred. Not friendship. Something more like a man memorizing a card.

Then the outboard snarled and he was gone, downstream, toward the fleet, and Delphine stood at the stern rail watching the wake swallow him and told herself firmly that the oilcloth in his fist weighed almost nothing and was worth even less — butcher's paper, ink, a half-mile of deliberate error — and that she had done the right thing, the clever thing, the thing that kept them alive.

So why did her hands want to shake now, when they hadn't shaken in front of him?

Because she had seen his face when he took it. Because she had watched a starving man accept bread and understood that accepting bread was not the same as deciding not to rob the bakery. Because Ferro Lantz had survived thirty years on this river by always, always selling, and Delphine had given him a product and a price and a reason, and hope was not a strategy.

Ivo came and stood beside her. For a while neither spoke. The cane banks slid by. Somewhere ahead, three tides away, floodlights waited on black water.

"He'll sell us," Ivo said. It wasn't a question.

"He might not." Delphine folded her arms against a wind that wasn't cold. "I made it expensive to."

"You bluffed a copy of a map we haven't had time to make a copy of."

"I made it last night. While you slept. Off Ada's description and my memory of the survey charts I wasn't supposed to memorize." She glanced at him. "Twenty years feeding the navy, Captain. You learn what they check and what they don't. They'll check the coordinates. They won't check the paper."

Ivo looked at her for a long moment, and she saw him re-file her — not the cook, not the survivor, something new, someone who forged documents by lamplight while the crew slept — and she couldn't tell if what crossed his face was gratitude or alarm.

"And if he compares it against the original he knows exists?"

"Then we're already dead," Delphine said, "and it won't matter what he's holding."

She turned back to the wake. Far downstream, so far it might have been heat shimmer off the water, she caught it: a smudge of smoke where no village was, rising off the river in a straight gray line. Coal smoke. Barge smoke. Six ships' worth.

Three tides, Ferro had said. Maybe fewer now.

"Get below," she told Ivo quietly. "Both of you. We need to talk about what happens if Ferro's memory is better than his loyalty — because I think he counted our crew, Captain. I think he counted our guns. And I think he left here knowing exactly what we don't have enough of."

Behind them, unnoticed by any of them, Ada Marsh stood in the shadow of the companionway with the real map pressed to her chest — and in her other hand, hidden in her skirt, a page torn from her father's journal that she had not shown anyone, on which Eben Marsh had written, in a hand gone shaky near the end:

*If Lantz comes, trust him exactly as far as the horizon. He is not evil. He is for sale. Those are different things, and the second is worse.*
