## 12. Third Tide

The storm came out of the west like something with a grudge.

Ada felt it before she saw it — a pressure behind her ears, a hush over the water, the river birds going silent all at once, as if the whole sky had drawn a breath it meant to hold for hours. She was on the Magpie's foredeck, coiling line with hands that had learned the work in three days, when Ivo's voice came down from the wheelhouse, flat and urgent.

"Get below. Now."

She looked up. The western sky had turned the color of a bruise going bad, and beneath it the river had gone strange — glassy, oily, moving without wind. She had grown up on this river. She knew its moods the way she knew her own hands. This was the mood it wore before it tried to kill you.

"Is it the monsoon early?" she called back.

"It's worse than the monsoon." Ivo's face appeared in the wheelhouse window, rain already spotting the glass. "It's the kind of storm that doesn't show on any chart because nobody who charts rivers lives long enough to draw it twice."

Delphine came up from the galley wiping her hands on her apron, took one look west, and said a word in her grandmother's tongue that Ada suspected was not a blessing. Then the three of them moved — no one had to say it — battening hatches, lashing the dive gear, doubling the lines on the fuel drums. Ferro's forged papers fluttered in the wheelhouse until Ada weighted them with the brass lamp. The riverboat groaned under her feet like an old woman being made to run.

They had made better time than Ivo had dared hope. Two and a half tides from the mission, and the wreck zone lay less than a day ahead. Ada had begun, against her own will, to think of the manifest as something real — a thing with weight and edges, a thing that could be held. Her father's map rode in an oilskin pouch against her ribs, and she had stopped resenting its warmth there.

The sky opened.

Rain came not in drops but in ropes, in cables, in whole torn riggings of water, and the wind hit the Magpie broadside with a sound like a door slamming on a cathedral. The river, which had been glass, became a field of white teeth. Ivo fought the wheel, shouting courses to Delphine, who relayed them forward, and Ada clung to the rail and watched the banks disappear entirely, the world reduced to gray water and grayer air.

"We can't run the rapids in this!" Delphine screamed.

"We can't anchor in it either!" Ivo screamed back. "We'd be on the bar by dark!"

Ada wiped her eyes and squinted into the murk, and there — a flicker, a wrongness in the western bank, a place where the rain fell differently, where the wind broke and curled. She had spent her childhood in country like this, running from her father's moods, hiding in the green. She knew what a hidden thing looked like.

"There!" She pointed. "Left bank — there's an opening in the trees. A channel. I can see the current bending around something."

Ivo didn't argue. That was the thing she was learning about him: he argued with everything except a good pair of eyes. He put the wheel over, and the Magpie leaned into the turn with a shriek of old timbers, and for one long, suspended moment Ada was certain the river would simply pick them up and dash them against the bank like a child discarding a toy.

Then they were through.

The channel was barely wider than the boat, walled in by pandanus and strangler fig, and the storm's roar dropped away behind them as if a door had closed. The water here was black and still and smelled of leaf-rot and stone. Fifty meters in, the channel opened into a lagoon — a drowned cup of a place, ringed by cliffs furred with vine, its surface unbroken by the rain above because the canopy caught it and let it down in slow, silver threads.

Ivo brought the boat in under the trees, killed the engine, and dropped anchor into water so deep the chain ran out its whole length and hung slack.

For a long moment, nobody spoke. The rain drummed on the canopy overhead, a sound like distant applause. Somewhere a bird screamed once and went quiet.

"Well," Delphine said at last, into the silence. "The river gave us a room."

Ada stood at the rail, her heart still slamming, and looked at the shore. And that was when she saw it.

* * *

It was nothing, at first. A shape in the green that her eyes kept sliding off — a lean-to built of split bamboo and palm thatch, sagging, half-swallowed by vine. A fire ring blackened by old rain. A canoe, overturned, its hull gone soft and gray as driftwood.

But Ada's body knew it before her mind did. Her feet were moving. She was over the rail, down onto the mud, wading ashore, and she did not remember deciding any of it.

The lean-to had her father's hands all over it. She knew the lashings — he never trusted knots, he wired everything, and there, rusted brown at the joints, was the wire. She knew the arrangement of the camp: the fire downwind, the sleeping shelf raised off the ground because of snakes, the water still dug at the edge of the mud where a spring seeped. She had grown up in a dozen camps like this one, in a dozen green rooms like this lagoon, while her mother read to her by lamplight and her father paced and muttered over charts she was never allowed to touch.

"Eben," she said aloud, and her voice came out small in the enormous quiet. "You stubborn, impossible old man. You *camped here.*"

Behind her, boots in the mud. Ivo, then Delphine, then — after a pause — the soft slap of Ferro's deck shoes, because of course Ferro had come too; the man had a nose for other people's history the way sharks had a nose for blood.

"This lagoon," Ivo said quietly. "It's not on any survey I've worked. And I've worked all of them."

"It wouldn't be." Ada's throat was tight. "He found places like this the way other men find trouble. He said the river kept secrets for people who kept the river's." She stopped at the lean-to and put her hand on the bamboo, and the bamboo was soft with rot, and something in her chest cracked along an old seam.

Because the last time she had seen her father, she had called him a liar. She had told him that his stories about the navy, about the ship, about the men who watched him — that they were the fever-talk of a man who had spent too long alone with his own importance. She had said it in the mission's gatehouse, in a voice pitched to be overheard, because the mission's matron was standing right there, and Ada had wanted so badly to be a person with an ordinary father, a dead father, a father who was simply *gone* and not *hunted*.

He had looked at her for a long time. Then he had said, "You'll know I told the truth when you're standing in one of my camps with my map in your hand," and he had walked out into the rain, and that was the last time.

She was standing in one of his camps. The map was against her ribs.

"Ada." Delphine's voice, gentle, behind her. "There's a tin under the shelf. Is that his?"

Ada turned. Beneath the sleeping shelf, wedged into a cavity in the bank, wrapped in oilcloth and wired shut — wired, always wired — sat a biscuit tin the size of a hatbox. She knelt and worked the wire with fingers that would not stay steady, and Ivo crouched beside her and did not touch it, and did not touch her, and somehow that was the kindness she needed.

The oilcloth came away. The tin's lid was swollen with rust, but it gave.

Inside: three journals bound in oilcloth, their covers furred with mold. A bundle of letters tied with string. A pistol, wrapped separately, oiled and clean. And at the bottom, in a leather folder gone stiff as board, a sheaf of pages copied out in her father's small, furious hand — columns of numbers, cargo codes, dates.

Ada lifted the first journal. The cover read, in her father's block capitals: *FOR ADA, IF I DON'T COME HOME. FOR NO ONE ELSE. BURN IT IF ANYONE ELSE.*

"He wrote to me," she said, and was surprised to find her voice worked at all. "He knew I'd say no. He knew I'd want nothing to do with it. And he wrote to me anyway."

"Read it," Ivo said. "Or don't. It's yours either way."

* * *

She read it aloud, because her father had always said words kept better in the open air, and because she could not have read it silently with three strangers watching her face. She read it by lamplight in the Magpie's saloon while the storm worked the canopy above and the river ran high and brown beyond the channel mouth.

*My girl. If you are reading this, then either you came looking — which I never believed you would — or someone brought this to you, which means I am dead, and which means I am sorry, and which means I was right, and I would give anything not to be.*

*You called me a liar. I don't blame you. It was easier for you to have a mad father than a hunted one, and I built the madness myself, some of it, to keep you from being hunted too.*

*Here is what is not madness.*

*I was at Kolo Bend on the night the ship went down. Not ashore — on the water, in my canoe, running a cargo of river turtle that I will not pretend was legal. I saw the ship at anchor where no ship should have anchored. I saw a boat put off from her with no running lights. I saw her settle — not break, not burn, but *settle*, the way a man lies down when he has decided to die. And I saw, in the morning, the navy launch that came to write down that she had struck the old minefield, and the officers who did not once look at the hole in her side, which was in the wrong place for a mine and the right place for a scuttling charge.*

*I went aboard the next night. I know you'll be angry at that. I went because the hold was open and the cargo was wrong — crates stenciled with codes that don't exist in any naval inventory I could bribe out of any clerk I could find. And I found a compartment, sealed, that appears on no plan of the ship, and in it a man who had been dead before the ship ever sank, and a waterproof tube with a manifest inside, and I understood that the ship had not been lost. She had been *filed*. Filed away like a receipt, like evidence, and the manifest was the thing being filed.*

*I took nothing that night. I memorized the compartment. I came back twice more to be sure of my bearings, and the second time there were men on the wreck, and they were not salvagers, and I have been running since.*

*The location is not written anywhere. It is here —* (he tapped his own chest, she knew, in the writing; she could see the gesture) *— and now it is in yours. If men come for the map I stole from the clerk at Port Meridian, let them have it. It shows them nothing but water. The truth is in my head and now in yours, and the manifest is still in her hold, and the men who sank her will burn her bones before they let it rise.*

*You will want to know why I didn't go to the tribunal myself. Because I did, Ada. Two years ago. And the man who heard my testimony now sits on the salvage board that is licensing the fleet to pick her clean.*

*I don't ask you to finish this. I ask you to know it. That was always the whole of what I wanted from you — not forgiveness. Knowledge. I could die a liar in your eyes, but not a fool in my own.*

*Your father, who kept the river's secrets because the river kept his. Eben.*

Ada set the journal down on the saloon table. Her hands were shaking, and she let them.

Nobody spoke. Delphine had stopped pretending to cook an hour ago; she sat with her elbows on the table, her chin on her fists, her eyes bright. Ivo stood at the saloon's aft window with his back to them all, watching the rain, and Ada understood that he was giving her the privacy of his turned back, and that he had lost a brother to this same machinery, and that he was not watching the rain at all.

"He went to the tribunal," she said finally. "He *tried.* And they buried him in the paperwork instead of the ground."

"That's how it's done," Ferro said from the doorway. He said it without cynicism, which was somehow worse. "You don't kill the witness. You discredit the testimony. A dead witness is a martyr. A mad one is a cautionary tale."

"He wasn't mad." Ada's voice cracked on it. "He was the sanest man on this river and I told him he was mad to his face because it was *easier*—"

"Ada." Ivo turned from the window. "I refused to falsify a depth reading and told myself it was principle. It was also easier than asking what the navy was hiding. We all choose the easier cowardice until the day we don't." He crossed to the table and put one finger, lightly, on the copied manifest pages. "He memorized the compartment. He drew the map so that only someone who knew the wreck could read it. He was leaving you a trail, not a treasure. And you followed it."

"I followed it because I wanted to prove him wrong," Ada said.

"Does it matter?"

She looked at the journals, at her father's furious small handwriting, at the pistol he had oiled against a day he must have known would come. And she understood, with a settling in her chest like a keel finding water, that it did not matter. Motive was the navy's business — motive was what tribunals argued about. What mattered was that her father had told the truth, alone, in the rain, for years, with no one to witness him, and that she was here now, and she could witness him.

She stood up.

"I'm not going back to the mission," she said. "When this is done — whatever happens at the wreck, whatever happens with the manifest — I'm not going back to bandaging children and pretending my father was a fever-dream. He witnessed a murder. Nobody witnessed *him*. So I'll do it. I'll stand up at whatever tribunal will listen and I'll say: I am Ada Marsh, daughter of Eben Marsh, and everything he told you was true, and here are his journals in his own hand, and here is the manifest, if we get it, and you will look at me while I say it, because he never got looked at."

Delphine smiled — slow, and fierce, and proud, as if Ada were her own.

"That," Ferro said, "is going to be worth money someday, that speech. I mean that as a compliment."

Ada almost laughed. Then Ivo said, quietly, "There's a problem," and the room went still.

He tapped the copied pages. "The fleet. I've been thinking about their schedule. Six tides from when the first barge dropped anchor — that was two days ago, by Ferro's reckoning. Which means they begin clearing the hold on the fourth tide." He looked up, and the lamplight made his face into something carved and grim. "The storm bought us a hiding place. It also slowed us a half-day. We're no longer ahead of them, Ada. We're *level* with them. And they'll have demolition charges, because men who file a ship don't leave a file cabinet sitting in open water."

Ada looked at the journal in her hands — at the last page, where her father had written a single line she had not yet read aloud, pressed so hard the pen had torn the paper:

*The river keeps secrets for people who keep the river's. Tell it I kept mine.*

Outside, the storm was dying. Through the saloon window she could see the lagoon going silver, the canopy dripping, the channel mouth running fast with the flood tide — and far downriver, past the bend, a smudge on the horizon that was not cloud.

"They'll burn the wreck tomorrow," she said.

"Then we dive tonight," Ivo said.
