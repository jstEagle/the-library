## 13. The Wreck Zone

The buoy showed itself an hour before full dark, a lean black finger against the last copper band of the sky, and Ivo felt the river change around it the way a room changes when a dangerous man walks in.

*Restricted waters. Naval salvage operations. Keep clear by order of the Port Authority.* The words were painted on a steel plate bolted to the buoy's staff, and they had been repainted recently enough that the letters still held a wet shine. Somebody ashore had been busy. Somebody ashore wanted the message legible from a hundred meters, legible from the air, legible from anywhere a curious eye might drift.

"Slack water in forty minutes," Delphine said from the wheelhouse doorway. She had a dish towel over her shoulder like a bandolier and a pencil behind her ear, and she looked, as she always did, like the calmest person on any river in the world. "Maybe fifty. The falls are running heavy after the storm."

Ivo stood at the Magpie's bow rail with the engine below him idling down to a murmur, and he did the arithmetic he'd been doing since the lagoon, since Ada came running down the bank with her father's journals pressed to her chest like a wounded bird. The fleet had been six tides out when they'd first seen it massed at Kolo Bend. Three tides had burned since. The barges would have made the lower anchorage by now — the wide water below the wreck shoal, where the current slackened enough for heavy work. Which meant the fleet was not coming. The fleet was *there*.

"Kill the engine," he said.

The Magpie drifted. Behind them the river ran brown and swollen with storm water, carrying branches and whole trees south toward the delta, and ahead the channel bent north around a wooded point and opened into the long reach where the *Vigilant* — the ship with no name, the wreck his navy employers had paid him to measure and forbidden him to remember — lay folded into forty fathoms of cold green dark.

He had worked that wreck for eleven days. Eleven days of running lines and reading gauges and logging depths he knew were lies before command ever asked him to sign them. Eleven days of descending into a silence so complete it felt less like water than like held breath. He had told himself it was just another job. He had almost believed it.

Now the job was floating out there in the dark with floodlights on it.

* * *

They came around the point under oars, engine cold, running on the last of the light the way Ferro had taught him to run contraband — *never at night, boy, always at the edge of it, when a sentry's eye is tired and everything looks like what he expects to see.* The Magpie sat low in the water with her deck cargo of rusted drums and rotting net, dressed up to look like what she'd been for twenty years: a dead boat doing dead boat things. Ferro's forgeries said she was the *Cormorant Maid*, licensed for drift salvage out of Port Meridian, and Ferro had sworn the papers would pass a casual inspection. Ivo was counting on the inspection being very casual indeed, because the *Cormorant Maid* did not exist, and neither, officially, did any of them.

Ada crouched at the stern rail with the journals wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, and Ivo watched her watch the water. Three days ago she had been a stranger with her father's anger in her mouth. Now she moved around the boat like she'd been born on it — checking lines, reading the current, asking Delphine questions in a low voice and remembering the answers. Eben Marsh's blood, doing what Eben Marsh's blood did. The old man had spent thirty years reading that river for gold and for escape, and his daughter read it the same way, except she was reading it toward the truth instead of away from it.

"There." Her voice was barely breath. "Past the willow snag. Lights."

Ivo saw them. First as a smudge of wrongness against the treeline, then as individual points resolving out of the dusk: anchor lights riding high above the water in a line, and beneath them the white glare of work floods sweeping across the surface in slow, patient arcs. Four barges, maybe five, moored bow and stern over the deep hole. He could make out the silhouette of a crane barge with its boom folded, and the low boxy shape of a decompression chamber lashed to a work deck, and the running lights of a picket launch circling the perimeter like a shark that had learned to walk.

"Five hulls," Delphine murmured. She'd come up beside him without a sound, which was a habit from a galley that had fed three hundred men at four in the morning without waking the officers. "Two of those are the navy fronts from Kolo Bend. The crane barge is new. That's *Meridian Star* equipment — I loaded stores on her sister ship, before."

"Before you sank one," Ada said. It was not a joke. It was the flat way Ada said things that were true.

"Before any of it," Delphine said, and let it lie.

The floodlights swept. Ivo counted the rhythm — twelve seconds dark, eight seconds lit, the beam crawling across the water toward them and sliding past. A sentry's rhythm. A tired man's rhythm, or a confident one. Either way, it was a rhythm, and rhythms could be read, and anything that could be read could be slipped beneath.

They let the current take them past the willow snag and into the shadow of the point, and there Ivo shipped the oars and let the Magpie drift stern-first into a cut in the bank overhung with fern, where the black water was still and smelled of rot and clay. Above them the fleet's lights painted the underside of the clouds a dull orange, like a fire burning in a room below.

* * *

They laid the plan out on the deck plates by the light of a single shuttered lantern, the way soldiers lay out a battle on a map of a country they're about to burn.

Ivo unrolled Delphine's drawings — she'd drawn the *Vigilant*'s interior from memory over three nights, in pencil, on the backs of menu sheets, and the drawings were better than the navy's own surveys because the navy's surveys had never shown what Delphine's showed. She had walked those corridors twice a day for fourteen months. She knew where the pipes ran, where the hatches sat, where the deck plates were loose enough to catch a diver's line.

"Here." Her finger came down on a rectangle amidships, just forward of the engine room bulkhead. "Number three cargo hold. Forward bulkhead gave way when she went down — I felt it go, the whole ship shuddered like something had kicked her. The hatch above it was a cargo hatch, twenty feet wide, hinged at the forward edge. When the bow section folded, that whole deck structure collapsed forward. Which means—" she dragged her finger up and over, tracing the fold, "—the hatch didn't seal. It *tore*. It's lying open, angled down into the hold, like a mouth."

"The navy's surveys don't show it," Ivo said.

"The navy's surveys don't show *anything*," Delphine said. "I've seen their drawings. They show a ship with three clean compartments and a tidy break. They show a ship that sank the way ships are supposed to sink. I served on her for fourteen months, and I'm telling you she didn't sink like that. She was *opened*."

"And the compartment Eben marked." Ada's finger touched the grease-stained map, which lay beside Delphine's drawings like a confession beside an alibi. The old man's hand had been shaky but sure — a rectangle drawn into the overhead of the forward hold, with a notation in pencil so small Ivo had needed a lens to read it: *sealed. welded. manifest inside, in the tube, under the plate.* "Forward hold, overhead plating. That's above the collapsed hatch. That's *inside*."

"That's inside," Ivo agreed.

He let the silence sit for a moment, because there was a thing that had to be said and he wanted to say it plainly, the way he wished someone had said things to him eleven days ago when he'd still had a commission and a lie to sign.

"The dive is bad," he said. "I want everyone to hear how bad it is, and then I want us to do it anyway, and I don't want anyone pretending afterward that they didn't know. Forty fathoms on a single line, in black water, through a wreck that's been settling for two years and is going to be settling a lot faster with five barges parked on top of it running winches and charges. The collapsed hatch is the only way in that the navy doesn't watch, and the only way in at all, because every other opening on that ship is either silted shut or on the side facing their lights. I go down the hatch, forward through the hold, up into the overhead compartment, and back. Delphine's corridors, her memory, her pencil marks. If I deviate from the drawings, I die. If the line snags, I probably die. If I'm down there when they start moving heavy gear — and they will, because they didn't bring a crane barge to look at it — I definitely die."

"And if the manifest is gone," Ada said quietly, "if Eben was wrong, or if someone got there first—"

"Then I come back up and we decide what a man's promise is worth when the thing he promised is gone." Ivo met her eyes. In the shuttered lantern light her face was all planes and shadow, and she had her father's stubborn jaw, and he thought: *he chose right, the old man. He chose the one person alive who would not let this go.* "But it's not gone. Eben Marsh spent thirty years learning where rivers keep their secrets, and he spent the last year of his life keeping this one. It's down there. The dead are good at holding on."

Nobody spoke. The fleet's lights swept the clouds overhead, orange, dark, orange.

"I should go with you," Delphine said at last. "I know the corridors."

"You know them from *inside a lit ship with air in it*," Ivo said. "Down there it's my water. I've done eleven days on that wreck. You keep the boat, keep the watch, and keep her alive if I don't come back." He looked at Ada. "And you keep her honest about that. She'll want to dive after me. Don't let her. That's not a request, it's the promise I'm making you both: if I'm not up by the fourth hour of the flood, you cut the line, you run for the falls, and you put the journals in front of a tribunal without me. The proof matters more than the man. That's the whole point of it."

"That's a coward's arithmetic," Delphine said.

"It's the only arithmetic that beats theirs," Ivo said. "They count on people mattering more than the truth. That's how they buried a ship. Somebody loved the wrong thing at the wrong time, and the truth went to the bottom. Not this time."

Ada reached across the drawings and put her hand flat on the grease-stained map, over the little penciled rectangle, over her father's last careful handwriting. "Fourth hour of the flood," she said. "Not a minute more."

* * *

Slack water came at midnight, and with it the strange hush Ivo had learned to wait for on every river he'd ever worked — the moment when the current forgot which way it was going, when driftwood hung motionless, when the water went glassy and dumb and the whole river held its breath between exhale and inhale. Forty minutes, maybe fifty, of water that would not push a diver sideways or drag a line taut. After that, the flood tide would turn and run hard upriver, and the wreck would begin, in its slow bone-deep way, to move.

He dressed in the shadow of the cut bank. Wetsuit, weight belt, twin tanks on a manifold, the big torch lashed to his forearm, the cutting tool at his hip. The manifest tube — a brass cylinder Delphine had found in the Magpie's bilge, of all places, some smuggler's relic from a previous life — rode in a pouch on his chest, empty, waiting. He checked each piece twice and then a third time, not because he doubted his hands but because his hands were the only part of him that wasn't afraid, and he'd learned long ago to let the frightened parts do the counting.

The line was three hundred meters of braided cable on a reel that Delphine had mounted at the stern rail. From the stern rail to the hatch was a descent of forty fathoms along the wreck's port shoulder, through water so dark that the torch would be less a light than a rumor of light. Delphine would tend the line. Ada would run the watch and the air gauge and the slate signals. Ferro — Ferro was two miles downriver in the *Magpie*'s stolen skiff, doing whatever Ferro did, which Ivo had decided not to ask about, because there are questions you ask before you need a man and questions you ask after, and Ferro's usefulness lived in the space between.

"You come back up," Ada said. She stood at the rail with the line reel between her hands, and she didn't say it like a plea. She said it like a heading. "Fourth hour. Not a minute more. I mean it, Ivo. I've buried one man who wouldn't come up off this river. I'm not doing the vigil twice."

"You won't," Ivo said. And because it was true and because she deserved to hear a true thing before he went down into the dark, he added: "Your father gave me a map. You gave me a reason to read it. That's more family than I've had since I was a boy, and I don't leave family at the bottom of anything."

Her face did something complicated and then went still. "Go," she said.

He went over the rail without a splash — an old trick, fins first, arms folded, the body entering the water like a knife entering a sheath — and the cold closed over him and the river's hush became the sea's silence, total and absolute.

* * *

Forty fathoms is two hundred and forty feet, and at forty fathoms there is no up. There is no down, either, not really — there is the torch beam and the line in your hand and the numbers in your head, and everything else is a darkness so complete that your own body becomes the only proof the world exists. Ivo had worked in that darkness for eleven days once, and he had hated it, and he had also, in some part of himself he didn't examine, trusted it. The dark didn't lie. The dark didn't sign falsified depth reports. The dark just was, and a man in it was either competent or dead.

He descended along the port shoulder with the wreck rising beside him out of the black like a hillside made of iron. The torch found her plating in slabs and glimpses — a porthole with its glass gone, a mooring bollard furred with silt, the ghost-lettering of her false name still just readable under two years of river scum. *VIGIL—*. The navy had repainted her name twice in her life, Delphine said. Once when they gave her to the smuggling run, once when they decided she'd never surface again. The river was repainting her a third time, letter by letter, year by year, erasing her the way men erase things. But not fast enough. Not tonight.

The collapsed hatch was where Delphine said it would be.

He felt it before he saw it — a change in the wreck's silhouette, a ragged absence where plating should have been — and then the torch found it and his breath caught in a way that had nothing to do with the regulator. She'd been right. She'd been exactly, perfectly right. The forward deck structure had folded down and forward when the ship broke, and the great cargo hatch had torn along its hinges and now hung open into the hold below, a black mouth twenty feet wide, angled down into the dark at forty degrees, its edges curled back like petals of a flower made of rust. Silt hung in the water around it in slow, turning curtains. Beyond it, the torch beam simply stopped, swallowed.

*Number three hold,* he thought. *Forward bulkhead. Overhead plating. Sealed, welded, manifest inside.*

He checked his watch. Thirty-eight minutes of slack water gone. The flood would start in under twenty, and when it started, the river above would begin to move and the fleet above that would begin to wake, and somewhere up there a crane barge would lower its first hook into the water and begin the work of making the *Vigilant* — and everything she carried, and everyone she'd carried — into nothing at all.

Ivo Okonkwo folded his body through the torn hatch, into the mouth of the hold, and the darkness behind him closed like water over a stone.

Somewhere above, faint as a heartbeat through two hundred feet of green black, he heard the first winch of the crane barge begin to turn.
