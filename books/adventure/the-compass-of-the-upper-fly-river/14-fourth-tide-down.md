## 14. Fourth Tide: Down

The water of the Upper Fly at slack tide was the color of strong tea left too long in a tin cup — brown-black, opaque, patient. Ivo Okonkwo stood on the Magpie's aft deck in his rig, checking each buckle for the third time, because down there mistakes were not lessons. Down there, mistakes were just the last thing you did.

Delphine had drawn the corridors for him one final time that morning, in charcoal on the back of a flour sack, her thick fingers moving with the certainty of a woman who had walked those decks two hundred times and fled them once. The mess. The ladder well. The cargo hatch aft of the galley stores, the one collapsed inward at an angle no survey would ever have flagged, because no survey had ever looked.

"You go down," she'd said, "and you count. Not breaths — bulkheads. Three frames past the galley, turn to port, follow the overhead until it drops. If the overhead drops before the third frame, you've drifted into the engine room crawl and you come back up, do you hear me? You come back up."

"I hear you."

"And Ivo." She'd gripped his wrist then, hard enough to leave marks through the neoprene. "Whatever you find down there — whatever is wearing that uniform — you tell me first. Before Ada. Before anyone. A body is not evidence until someone who loved this ship says what it means."

He hadn't asked her what she meant by *loved*. He'd understood. Some people sink with a vessel and never surface; Delphine Okafor had surfaced and left half of herself below, walking those remembered corridors every night behind her eyes.

Ada stood at the rail, watching the fleet's floodlights sweep the water a quarter mile downstream, pale blades turning over black glass. She had braided her hair tight against the humidity and said almost nothing since dawn. When Ivo had finished checking his gauges, she crossed the deck and took his hand — not gently, but the way you take hold of something you intend to keep from being taken.

"My father drew that map," she said. "You're diving inside his handwriting. Come back and tell me if he was right."

"He was right," Ivo said. "That's what frightens me."

Then he spat into his mask, rinsed it, and went over the side without a splash, the way the navy had taught him, the way the navy now wished it never had.

* * *

Black water closed over him like a hand closing around a coin.

At six feet the last daylight died. At twelve feet his lamp found nothing but silt hanging in suspension, a blizzard of fine river sediment that had been falling for years and would fall for years more, burying the wreck a grain at a time while men in offices argued about whether she had ever existed. He descended along the shot line they'd dropped at slack water, hand over hand, feeling the pressure settle onto his shoulders like a passenger who intended to stay.

At forty feet his boots touched steel.

The *Vigilant* lay on her port side, canted maybe sixty degrees, so that her deck was a wall and her hull a roof and everything aboard had slid, in the long minute of her dying, toward the same corner. Ivo had worked classified wrecks for eleven years. He knew how ships died: the way compartments flooded in sequence, the way air pockets held and then didn't, the way loose objects became projectiles and then became part of the architecture. But knowing how a ship dies is not the same as knowing why, and the navy had always been careful to keep those two knowledges in separate rooms.

Tonight he was swimming between them.

His lamp cut a cone through the murk and found the hull plating, furred with silt and a grey-brown film of river life. He followed it aft, counting frames under his gloved palm the way Delphine had taught him — *one, two* — the rivet lines coming up regular as ribs beneath his fingers. The silt here was soft as flour. Every movement raised a slow cloud that hung in his lamplight like smoke in a sealed room, and he learned quickly to move the way the dead moved down here: slowly, deliberately, disturbing nothing.

*Three.*

The galley should be ahead and slightly above — which, on a ship lying on her side, meant off to his right and up. His lamp swept across a shape that resolved, after a moment, into a range hood, still bolted to what had been a vertical bulkhead and was now a floor. Beyond it, a great cooking kettle lay on its side, its lid sprung, filled with forty years of river mud. Delphine had stirred that kettle. Delphine had fed two hundred men out of it, and then watched the navy list her ship as lost with all hands except the ones it quietly reassigned, and herself among them, erased by a stroke of administrative ink.

Ivo turned to port where the overhead dropped, exactly as she'd said it would. The corridor mouth gaped there, half-choked by a collapsed frame, a triangle of darkness barely wide enough for his shoulders. He pulled himself into it.

Inside, the ship stopped being a wreck and became a throat.

* * *

There is a silence underwater that is not the absence of sound. It is the presence of everything unsaid. Ivo felt it press against his ears along with the depth — all the testimony this hull had been holding, all these years, in the dark, waiting for someone with a light and a reason.

He swam the corridor by touch as much as sight. His glove trailed the bulkhead on his left, reading it like braille: rivets, a doorframe, a doorframe frozen half-open, a nameplate corroded illegible. Twice the passage narrowed where plating had buckled, and twice he exhaled, flattened himself, and slid through with his tank scraping the steel above him — a sound like a fingernail dragged along the world's longest coffin lid, transmitted straight through his bones.

His air gauge read 2,100 pounds. Enough, if he was disciplined. Discipline was the only currency the deep accepted.

Delphine's voice ran in his head, counting with him. *Past the officers' washroom. Past the linen locker. The compartment is abaft the number three hold, behind a bulkhead the yard crew welded shut in drydock the spring before she sailed.* Welded shut. He had signed the survey report on that weld line four years ago — or rather, he had signed the report saying there was nothing behind it worth surveying, because command had told him what the report would say and he had written it, and told himself it was routine, and slept badly for a week, and then slept fine, because sleeping fine is what a man does when he decides the knot in his stomach is just the job.

Now he was going to open the knot.

The corridor ended at a junction where three passages met and none of them went anywhere anymore — the ceiling here had come down in a tangle of conduit and cable, a steel nest fused with corrosion. But Delphine's charcoal map showed a maintenance access low on the starboard side, a crawl space no wider than a man, running parallel to the sealed bulkhead. The navy's divers had never found it because the navy's divers had never looked for anything. They surveyed what command pointed at. That was the whole secret of the classified wreck program, and Ivo had been its instrument for a decade before Eben Marsh put a grease-stained map in his hand and made him a witness instead.

The access hatch was there, exactly where she said. A wheel lock, seized with rust. He set both hands on it and threw his weight sideways, and the wheel gave with a groan that traveled through the water like a whale's complaint, and the hatch swung inward, exhaling four decades of trapped water into his face — colder than the river around it, darker, tasting of iron and something older.

The sealed compartment.

* * *

His lamp found paper first.

Bundles of it, strapped and stacked on what had been shelving, wrapped in oilcloth that had done its work better than any coffin — the edges gone translucent with age but the bundles intact, lashed with wire, labeled in stenciled characters he couldn't read from here but recognized in his gut: manifests. Cargo documents. The paperwork of a smuggling operation dressed in naval uniforms, sealed behind a welded bulkhead on a warship, sunk by its own navy to drown the arithmetic.

Eben Marsh had been right. The old man's map wasn't treasure. It was testimony, drawn in grease pencil by a prospector who had understood, in the last fevered days of his life, that some things are worth more than gold precisely because they can hang a man.

Ivo's heart was hammering now, and he made himself stop, made himself hang weightless in the black water and breathe slow, four counts in, six counts out, the way they teach you so you don't die of your own excitement. The compartment was small — maybe eight feet by ten, a storeroom converted to a strongbox. And in the far corner, half-buried in settled silt, his lamp found what the manifest alone could never have proven.

A man.

He was seated, or had been placed seated, back against the bulkhead, and the silt had taken him the way the river takes everything — patiently, completely, keeping the shape and discarding the rest. What remained was a navy uniform, dress whites gone the color of old bone, and within them a skeleton whose posture was wrong for drowning. Drowning men thrash. This man sat with his hands in his lap, composed, deliberate, arranged — or arranging himself — with the terrible dignity of someone who knew what was coming and refused to give it the spectacle.

And in the center of his chest, punched through the breast pocket of his jacket, a hole the river had not made.

Ivo hung there in the black water, six fathoms down inside a murdered ship, and looked at a murdered man, and understood.

The officer had been shot before the scuttling. Shot in a sealed compartment with the ship's own contraband, and left to go down with the evidence, wearing the evidence, becoming the evidence — the one piece the cover-up could never fully account for, buried under forty years of official grief and a memorial plaque and mothers who were told their sons died with honor in a storm. This man had probably been the one who kept the ledgers. Or the one who'd threatened to. Either way, command had solved him the way they solved everything: with a bulkhead, a weld, and a story.

Ivo's breath rasped in his own ears. His gauge read 1,400 pounds. He had perhaps twenty minutes of working time, and the dead man in front of him had waited forty years, and would understand if Ivo needed every one of those minutes.

But first — Delphine's instruction, and deeper than instruction, something Ivo felt in his chest like a hand pressing down — first, witness.

He unclipped his slate from his harness. With numb fingers, in the cone of his lamp, he wrote:

*One officer, unidentified. Single gunshot wound, anterior chest. Positioned post-mortem or self-arranged. Sealed compartment, abaft No. 3 hold. Manifests present, oilcloth-wrapped, approximately [he counted] fourteen bundles. Ship scuttled. This was not an accident.*

He wrote the date. He wrote his name. Then he reached out and touched the dead man's shoulder — lightly, the way you'd wake a sleeping friend you hadn't meant to disturb — and said, aloud, into the water where no one could hear him and everyone could:

"I see you. I'm coming back for you. Your name goes on the record if I have to nail the record to the tribunal door myself."

The bubbles rose and were swallowed. The skeleton kept its counsel, hands folded, patient as arithmetic. Somewhere above, muffled through forty feet of tea-dark water and a steel hull, Ivo thought he felt — or imagined, or needed to feel — the distant pulse of the salvage fleet's engines, the sound of men coming to erase this room forever.

Six tides. They were on the fourth. There was no fifth tide for the *Vigilant*.

* * *

The manifest tube was where Eben's map promised: a waterproof document case, brass-capped, strapped to the shelving beside the paper bundles — the original shipping manifest itself, the captain's duplicate, kept apart from the rest because whoever hid it had known the bundles might be found and wanted one copy to survive even that. Inside the case, when Ivo cracked the seals with his knife, the pages lay flat and legible, ink faded to sepia but every figure standing: cargo entered as medical supplies leaving as scrap iron, weights that matched no weighbridge, signatures of officers whose names Ivo knew from the dismissal hearing that had ended his own career — names that went up like flares.

He rolled the manifest, sleeved it in the recovery tube, and clipped the tube high on his harness, where no snag could take it. Then he did the hardest thing a diver ever does: he left the rest. Fourteen bundles of corroboration, and he took one page only, torn free and folded into his thigh pocket — enough to authenticate the tube if the tube alone seemed too convenient, not enough to cost him the minutes he'd need to live.

On his way out through the crawl space, his lamp passed the dead officer one last time. And Ivo — who had counted his life in miles traveled rather than years lived, who had believed distance was the only honest unit of measurement a drifting man could keep — found himself thinking in a different unit entirely. One man. One name, unknown, owed. Some debts don't accrue interest; they just wait, and the waiting is the interest.

His air line — the umbilical he'd run in on, because a hard line was surer than tanks alone in a drowned maze — brushed the collapsed frame at the junction, and he heard it, felt it, the faint drag of hose over jagged steel, and froze, and eased it clear with exaggerated care, and told himself: *that was the warning. Don't need a second one.*

He started up. The silt cloud of his own passage closed behind him like a curtain, and somewhere in the black above, the river waited with its patience and its current and its fleet of lights, and Ivo Okonkwo climbed toward them carrying a dead man's testimony against his chest — not knowing that thirty feet up, in the dark where the water began to taste of diesel and searchlight, a patrol skiff's propellers were already turning toward the Magpie.
