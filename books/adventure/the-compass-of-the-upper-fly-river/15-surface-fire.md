## 15. Surface Fire

Delphine Okafor had learned to read a river the way other women read a husband's face — by the small betrayals, the things it tried to hide. So when the water off the Magpie's starboard quarter went glassy and wrong, flat in a current that had no business being flat, she set down her knife mid-onion and stood very still at the galley rail, listening.

Diesel. Faint, but there, riding the surface film like oil on soup. And beneath it, a sound she knew from six years in a navy mess hall: the low, disciplined thrum of an outboard run at half-throttle, the sound of men who did not want to be heard until they were already alongside.

She wiped her hands on her apron — a habit, armor, the costume of an innocent woman — and stepped out onto the deck.

The night was thick with river mist. Downstream, maybe four hundred meters, the salvage fleet lay at anchor like a herd of sleeping animals, their floodlights sweeping the water in slow, patient arcs. One of those arcs had just passed over the Magpie's bow and kept moving, which meant they'd seen her and weren't worried yet. That was the good news. The bad news was the skiff.

It came out of the mist on the starboard side, running dark, a navy patrol boat stripped of its numbers and painted civilian gray — the same trick Ferro used, the same trick everyone on this river used. Six men aboard. Rifles slung, not raised. A lieutenant's cap on the man at the bow, white and crisp as a lie told cleanly.

"Delphine," Ada said from the wheelhouse doorway, her voice pitched low. She'd seen it too. Of course she had; the girl missed nothing anymore. "Ivo's been down eleven minutes."

Eleven minutes. His line ran taut off the stern rail, disappearing into black water that suddenly felt like a crowded room. Delphine did the arithmetic she hated: the dive gear — the spare tanks, the compressor hose, the charts of the wreck's corridors drawn in Delphine's own hand — all of it sat in the open on the aft deck like evidence laid out for a tribunal. And Ivo was thirty feet down with his air line hooked to *their* compressor, which meant that when the boarding party came aboard, the first thing they would find was the umbilical cord of a diver who officially did not exist.

"Ada." She kept her voice pleasant, almost lazy, the voice of a cook annoyed at an interruption. "Take the gear ashore. Now. Through the mangroves, up the bank, behind the big dead fig. Don't run — walk fast. Running is a confession."

"And you?"

"I'm going to make them dinner."

For one heartbeat Ada looked at her — really looked, the way she'd started looking at all of them lately, as if memorizing faces against loss — and then she moved. No argument, no wasted breath. Delphine watched her slip over the port rail with a tank under each arm and the chart tube strapped across her back, wading into the shallows where the mangrove roots made a corridor of shadows, and she thought: *Eben Marsh, wherever you are, your daughter has more spine than half the officers I ever cooked for.*

Then she turned to face the skiff, and she put on her smile.

* * *

The lieutenant came up the ladder first, and Delphine assessed him the way she assessed every man who'd ever eaten at her table: by his hands and his hunger. Young — twenty-six, maybe less. Hands soft around the grip of nothing in particular. The kind of officer who'd been given a job slightly too large for him and was terrified someone would notice. Behind him came five ratings with the flat eyes of men ordered to be somewhere they didn't want to be.

"Evening," Delphine called down pleasantly, before he could speak. "You're lucky. I've got a pot of pepper soup on and I always make too much. Navy habit — you feed ten and nine show up."

The lieutenant blinked. Whatever script he'd rehearsed — suspicion, demand, threat — she'd knocked it sideways before he reached the top rung, and she could see him scrambling to find his footing on the new ground. Good. A man looking for his footing wasn't looking at her stern rail.

"Madam." He stepped aboard, touching his cap. "This vessel is inside a restricted zone. I'll need to see papers."

"Papers, of course." She swept toward the wheelhouse, talking the whole way, because talking was the tide she controlled. "Ferrous Lantz's boat, chartered — you know Ferro? Everyone knows Ferro. We're running charter for a survey outfit out of Port Meridian, fish-stock assessment, terribly dull work, mostly we sit still while instruments complain." She produced the forged papers from under the chart drawer where Ferro had stashed them, and handed them over with the confidence of a woman handing over a menu. "There's soup, if you want it. My mother always said the fastest way to end an inspection is to make the inspector hungry enough to leave."

One of the ratings snorted and caught himself. The lieutenant almost smiled, then remembered his cap and didn't.

He read the papers slowly. Too slowly. Delphine kept her body loose and her mind moving like water through the gaps: *the compressor is running, they can hear it, they can hear it humming through the hull.* She'd need to explain the compressor. She was already building the explanation — generator trouble, battery charging, a boat this old lived on its machinery's nerves — when the lieutenant lifted his head and looked past her, toward the stern.

"Your generator," he said, "seems to be breathing."

* * *

"It's a hookah rig," Delphine said, walking him aft with the unhurried gait of a woman showing off a garden. "Ferro's idea. He swears it catches more fish than nets — sends a man down to look at the beds directly. Honestly I think he just likes watching people do uncomfortable things."

The lie came out smooth because it was built on true bones: the rig *was* a fishing rig once, before Ivo rebuilt it, and Ferro *did* delight in other people's discomfort. The best lies were load-bearing walls made from real timber.

The lieutenant crouched at the stern rail, studying the air line where it vanished into the river. His shadow fell across the water, and Delphine saw — with a lurch she kept entirely off her face — that the line was trembling. Not slack. Not still. Moving with the small, rhythmic pull of a living man breathing at the end of it, thirty feet down, working his way back along a drowned corridor with a dead man's manifest against his chest.

"Your diver's been down a while," the lieutenant said.

"He comes up when the tanks say so. Fish don't keep schedules and neither does he." She leaned on the rail beside him, close enough that he could smell the pepper soup on her, and dropped her voice into conspiracy. "Between us — you lot scared everything with fins out of this stretch months ago. If your fleet wants fish surveys done properly, you should hire Ferro instead of chasing him."

"I don't know any Ferro."

"Nobody knows any Ferro. That's how he likes it."

The lieutenant laughed — one short bark, quickly strangled — and something in Delphine's chest unclenched half a degree. Then it clenched again, tighter, because downriver the floodlights stopped sweeping.

All of them. At once. Every arc of light on the water swung and held, fixed on the Magpie like a dozen eyes deciding.

And out of the darkness between the barges came a second skiff, larger, faster, cutting a white seam straight toward them.

* * *

"Company," Ada breathed.

She was flattened in the mangroves twenty meters up the bank, the dive gear buried under root-rot and palm fronds, watching through a gap in the leaves. The first skiff's engine idled. The second one bore down, and standing in its bow, arms folded, hatless, unmistakable even at distance in the searchlight spill, was Ferro Lantz.

Ada's stomach dropped. *He sold us.* It arrived whole and cold, the way conviction arrives — not a suspicion but a fact, already true, waiting only to be spoken aloud. He'd taken the copy of the map Delphine bluffed him with, and he'd gone straight to the fleet, and now he was coming back wearing their lights.

But then she watched him come alongside, and she watched what he did, and the fact began to wobble.

Ferro leapt aboard without asking permission, which was very Ferro, and said something to the young lieutenant — Ada couldn't hear it over the water, only see the shape of it, the shrug, the grin, the theatrical boredom. The lieutenant stiffened. Ferro said something else, longer, gesturing lazily at the Magpie, at the river, at the sky, a whole pantomime of *nothing to see here, son.* The lieutenant looked at his papers again. Looked at Ferro. Looked at the stern rail, where the air line still trembled its small mortal rhythm.

And Ferro — Ferro looked at the line too. Just once. Just a flick of the eyes, so quick Ada would have missed it if she hadn't been watching him the way you watch a card sharp. Then he looked away and lit a cigarette, exhaling smoke into the floodlight like a man with absolutely nothing better to do with his evening.

*Either mercy,* Ada thought, *or strategy.* Her father had taught her the difference, once, sitting on a riverbank with a fishing line between them: *A man who helps you for free wants something later. A man who helps you at cost means it.* Which was Ferro? She couldn't tell. That was the worst of it — not knowing whether the knife at your back was being sharpened or quietly put away.

Down on the deck, Delphine's voice floated up, warm and unbothered, offering soup to a second round of armed men. Ada pressed herself deeper into the roots and waited, counting her breaths the way her father had taught her to count tides.

* * *

The second skiff carried a different animal entirely. Delphine felt it before she identified it — the way the ratings on her deck straightened, the way the young lieutenant's hand drifted toward his cap brim. The man who climbed aboard wore commander's tabs and the particular stillness of someone accustomed to rooms going quiet when he entered.

"Commander Voss," he said, to no one in particular, which meant to everyone.

"Ferro Lantz." Ferro stuck out a hand. Voss did not take it. Ferro converted the gesture into adjusting his collar, smooth as shuffling cards. "Evening, Commander. Terrible night for paperwork."

"What's your business here?"

"Salvage survey. Civilian contract." Ferro produced a second set of papers from somewhere inside his coat — Delphine hadn't known he had a second set, and filed that away as either reassurance or terror. "Fish stocks, wreck hazards, the usual river gossip. You know how it is. Everybody counts everybody else's boats."

Voss wasn't listening to Ferro. Voss was looking at the stern rail, at the trembling line, and Delphine watched his face do something small and terrible: nothing at all. A man who knew exactly what a breathing air line meant, and had already decided what to do about it.

"Who's diving?" Voss asked.

"My diver," Ferro said.

"Name."

"You know how divers are, Commander. Superstitious. Won't give names to the river before a job."

"Cut the line," Voss said, to the nearest rating, "and we'll meet him when he surfaces."

The world went very narrow and very bright. Delphine heard her own pulse in her ears, and beneath it, absurdly, the simmer of the pepper soup below decks, and beneath that, thirty feet down, the slow respiration of Ivo Okonkwo, who had perhaps four minutes of air left in his reserve bottle and no idea that the rope keeping him alive was about to become the rope that killed him.

So she did the only thing left. She opened her mouth and let the river talk through her.

"Commander." Her voice came out lower than she intended, stripped of charm, and that was precisely why it landed. "That man down there is diving on a hull section your own surveys flagged as unstable three years ago. There's a cargo shift in B-hold sitting on a live scuttling charge — the navy's own ordnance, never recovered. You cut that line and he drops onto it. You want to explain to the tribunal why a civilian diver died on your charge, in your restricted zone, during your operation?" She let a beat pass. "Or would you rather I pull him up myself, nice and slow, and we all pretend this evening was about fish?"

Silence. The river slapped the hull. Somewhere in the mangroves, Ada stopped breathing.

Voss studied her. Really studied her, for the first time, and Delphine understood with cold clarity that he was trying to place her face — that some part of him, some old mess-hall memory or service record, was turning pages. Six years she'd spent making herself forgettable, and now forgettability was the one thing she couldn't afford.

"You were navy," Voss said.

"Twelve years, mess branch." She lifted her chin. "HMS *Vigilant*, among others. I cooked for men who are now admirals, Commander, and I remember every one of their table manners. Shall we compare memories, or shall I get your diver up?"

Another silence, longer. On the rail, the air line trembled its rhythm — quicker now, she noticed, quicker — and Ferro, smoking beside her, shifted his weight almost imperceptibly between Voss and the water, a man standing on a trapdoor he alone could see.

"All right," Voss said finally. "Bring him up. Slowly. And madam—" his eyes settled on her with the weight of a promise, "—when he surfaces, I'll want a word with him myself."

* * *

They hauled the line together, Delphine and two ratings, hand over hand, and every fathom of it was a prayer she didn't believe in. The mist thickened. The floodlights held their stare. And Delphine Okafor, who had fed captains and liars and one entire doomed crew, pulled a drowning man's lifeline and thought, with terrible tenderness: *come up, Ivo. Come up breathing. Whatever you found down there, whatever it costs — come up.*

The line went slack in her hands.

Not released — *slack*, sudden and total, the weight at the end of it simply gone, and her heart stopped with it. Then the water at the stern erupted, white in the floodlight, and a hand shot up out of the river and clamped the rail, and Ivo Okonkwo's head broke the surface streaming black water, eyes wide, lips gray, one fist locked around a metal tube he thrust upward toward her like a man passing a torch through fire.

Behind him, far downstream, the first demolition charge went off early — a muffled boom rolling up the river, and the whole wreck zone shuddering like a struck bell.

Voss spun toward the sound. Ferro crushed out his cigarette and said, softly, to no one: "Well. There's your fish survey."

And Delphine reached down and took Ivo's hand.
