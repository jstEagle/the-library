## 16. Fifth Tide: The Manifest

The fifth tide came in gray and breathless, the river lying flat as poured pewter between its banks. Ivo felt it in his ears before he saw it — that pressure change, that hush — the water holding still for a few merciful minutes while the moon and the sea argued over who would pull harder. Slack water. The only window they'd get.

He sat on the Magpie's stern rail in his diving dress, boots dangling over black water, and let Delphine check his valves without speaking. Her hands were quick and certain at his collar, at the manifold, at the line coiled on the deck like a sleeping snake.

"Air's good," she said. "Line's flaked clean. No kinks, no fouls." She said it the way a priest says a blessing — not because she believed it would hold, but because saying it was the ritual that made the next hour bearable.

"And if it fouls?"

"Then you cut free and come up the way you were born," she said. "Screaming."

Ada stood at the rail beside him, her arms crossed tight against the cold, the manifest's location burned into her memory now — third compartment forward of the engine room bulkhead, behind a plate welded shut with a seam Eben had drawn in grease pencil like a signature. She'd traced it three times with her finger on the map until Ivo told her to stop wearing holes in the paper.

"You don't have to do this," she said.

"Yes, I do."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I've got." He looked at her then, really looked — this thin, stubborn woman who'd hated him two tides ago for calling her father a thief, who'd kept him breathing through the night before last when his lungs filled with river, whose hands were steady now only because she'd decided they would be. "Your father gave me a map. You gave me a reason to use it. That's the whole arithmetic of my life, Ada. Somebody gives you something, you carry it the rest of the way."

She didn't answer. She reached out and gripped his forearm once, hard, above the wrist where the pulse ran, and held it for a count of five, the way nurses did — as if measuring whether he was alive enough to risk.

"Five minutes past slack," she said, "I start counting."

* * *

Down was a color before it was a direction.

Ivo went down through forty feet of green murk into forty more of brown, and after that there was no color at all, only the pale bloom of his lamp swinging ahead of him through water so thick with silt it seemed less liquid than weather. The wreck rose around him by degrees — first a shadow, then a wall, then a country. The *Vigilant*. Ninety meters of navy corvette lying on her port side in the mud of the Upper Fly, her name still legible beneath a fur of algae, her superstructure folded inward like a fist closing.

He'd been down here before. Not this ship — but every ship like her. The navy had taught him wrecks the way other men learned scripture, and he moved through the drowned corridors with the fluency of long habit, following Delphine's voice in his head the way she'd drilled it into them for six nights running: *Past the galley, port side, mind the steam line — it'll be grown over but the stub will cut you like a razor. Through the mess. The watertight door to C-3 sticks; hit it low on the hinge side.*

The galley was exactly where she said it would be. He put his gloved hand against the doorframe and felt, absurdly, the ghost of her — some cook's apprentice twenty years dead, stirring pots in this same room while the ship above them was being prepared for murder. A ladle lay fused to the deck plate by rust. He swam past it and did not look back.

C-3's door stuck. He hit it low on the hinge side, twice, and it groaned open a hand's width, enough.

The compartment beyond was a cathedral of silence. Silt had drifted in through the years and lain across everything like snowfall — across bunks, across lockers, across a card table still set with a game nobody finished. His lamp found the aft bulkhead and swept along it, plate by riveted plate, counting seams the way Ada had counted them on paper.

Third seam from the engine-room frame. There.

The weld was different. Even under twenty years of growth he could see it — too clean, too deliberate, laid in beads by someone with time and skill and something worth hiding. Eben's grease-pencil signature, rendered in steel.

Ivo hung in the black water and understood, suddenly and completely, what he was looking at. Not a strongroom. Not a cache of gold. A tomb with a filing cabinet built into it. Someone had sealed this compartment from the inside knowing the ship was going down, and had sealed the evidence inside it knowing that dead men keep secrets better than living ones.

His knife went under the weld seam. The torch-cut groove Eben must have started — or someone before him — was still there, a channel of weakness running the plate's height. The blade bit, levered, and a fist-sized piece of the weld cracked away with a sound he felt in his teeth rather than heard.

It took eleven minutes. Eleven minutes of working the blade, of prying, of bracing his boots against a bunk frame while his air hissed steadily into the helmet and his heart counted seconds he couldn't afford. The plate peeled back at last like the lid of a tin, and his lamp swung into the gap.

A sea chest. Brass corners, canvas wrapping gone to sludge, but the sealant — military-grade, applied by hands that knew their trade — had held. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth within rubber within more oilcloth, a tube. And beside it, in a leather case furred white with mold, a logbook stamped with the crest of the Upper Fly Naval Station.

Ivo took both. Stowed the tube inside his suit, against his chest, where it rode like a second heartbeat. The logbook he lashed to his belt. He put his palm flat against the sea chest for a moment — a diver's superstition, touching the dead to tell them they'd been found — and turned for home.

That was when the line went tight.

Not a snag's gentle resistance. A *yank* — sudden, total, the whole length of his lifeline snapping taut as a bowstring and hauling him backward off his feet into the dark. His lamp spun wild. He caught a stanchion, lost it, caught a pipe. Somewhere above, the line had fouled — on the mastfoot, on a spar, on twenty years of collapsed wreckage that shifted and settled the way ruins always did, patient and treacherous. And somewhere above that, Delphine was paying out slack or she wasn't, and either way the line ran taut across a jagged edge of torn plating, and every second it sawed there was a second closer to parting.

He didn't panic. Panic was a luxury priced in air. He worked backward along the line hand over hand, following it to where it bent around a shelf of collapsed decking, and found the foul: the line looped twice through a shackle on a fallen davit, cinched fast, and the davit itself pinned under a section of hull plating that no man's shoulders would move.

He could cut the line. Cut free, swim blind for the surface, gamble on the current and the dark and the fleet's floodlights sweeping the water a quarter mile downstream. Or he could stay, work the loops loose, and spend air he might not have.

He checked his gauge. The needle said twelve minutes, and lied, because gauges always lied at the end.

He stayed. Fingers numb inside the gloves, he fed the first loop back off the shackle, then the second, each movement small and precise, the way you undid knots in dreams. The line trembled against his fingers — Delphine feeling for him, testing, faithful as a pulse. Twice the davit shifted and settled with a grinding whisper that raised every hair on his body. On the third try the loops came free, and the line went slack, and slack had never felt like such a gift.

Twelve minutes became nine by the time he cleared the wreck's shadow. Eight by the time he found the ascent line. Seven when the current — the tide turning, the window closing — took him sideways and dragged him across a field of broken plating he should have seen and didn't.

He never knew what cut him. A plate edge, a spar, a bolt of rusted steel — something opened his suit along the left forearm and the river came in, cold as a verdict. The shock of it doubled him. His grip tore off the ascent line. The world rolled — mud below, black above, black below, black everywhere — and his helmet flooded to the chin, and he was breathing water before he understood he was drowning.

The last thing he did, deliberately, with the small clear corner of his mind that still answered to his name, was put his hand flat against his chest. Over the tube. *Still there.* Then the corner closed, and the river took all of him.

* * *

They pulled him out at the shallows below the wreck zone, four sets of hands hauling on the line Delphine refused to stop reeling even after it came up trailing blood-tinged water instead of a man. Ferro's skiff — Ferro, of all people, idling at the edge of the floodlights' reach with his engines muffled — had spotted the surface disturbance and run it down. What they brought aboard the Magpie was not quite Ivo Okonkwo. It was a shape wearing his diving dress, vomited half the river, lips gray, eyes rolled to show only white, one arm locked across his chest like a man shielding a wound.

Ada broke the helmet seals with a knife and got the faceplate off and found him not breathing.

What happened next, none of them ever described the same way afterward. Delphine said later that Ada simply took command, the way people do when the alternative is unbearable. She rolled him, struck his back, got a cupful of river out of him and nothing else. She pinched his nose, sealed her mouth over his, and blew — once, twice, three times — then folded her hands over his sternum and pressed, counting aloud in a flat nurse's cadence that did not waver even when her voice cracked underneath it.

"One. Two. Three. Four. Breathe, damn you. One. Two—"

He breathed on the fourth cycle. A wet, tearing, involuntary gasp that sprayed silt-water across her cheek, and then another, thinner, and then a rhythm — ragged, shallow, but a rhythm. Ada turned him onto his side and stayed there, one hand spread flat on his back, feeling it rise and fall, rise and fall, while Delphine cut away the ruined sleeve and found the gash beneath and bound it by lamplight.

"He's in the river's debt," Delphine murmured. "And ours."

"He's in mine," Ada said. "I intend to collect."

* * *

The night divided itself into watches, though no one slept in any true sense. They anchored in the reeds of a nameless island, engines cold, lights doused, the fleet's glow a bruise of orange on the southern horizon. And in the Magpie's cramped cabin, by the light of a single shielded lantern, Ada Marsh fought the river for possession of a man she'd known eight days.

Fever came with the small hours. It always did — the river's filth finding the blood, the blood carrying the argument to every corner of him. He shivered so hard the bunk frame rattled, then burned so hot Delphine stripped him to the waist and bathed him down with river water cooled in the night air. Between the shivering and the burning he talked.

He talked to his brother.

"Jonah," he said, clear-eyed and elsewhere. "Jonah, the line's fouled. Jonah, don't go down again, the survey's wrong, the chart's wrong, they know it's wrong—" And then, quieter, in a voice that made Delphine turn her face away: "They gave me his file. Sealed. Forty years sealed. I signed the paper, Ada. I signed the paper they handed me and I took the money and I measured my life in miles after that because miles don't ask you what you buried."

Ada held a cup of water to his lips. "You're not signing anything tonight," she said. "Drink."

He drank. His eyes found hers — present, for a moment, lucid as glass — and something passed between them that neither of them named. Gratitude, maybe. Or recognition. Two people who had each been handed a sealed file and had finally, at terrible cost, gotten it open.

"I've got it," he whispered. "Tell me I've got it."

She took his hand from his chest and felt beneath it the hard cylinder of the tube, still lashed inside the sodden undersuit, still sealed, still dry. She pressed his own hand back over it.

"You've got it," she said. "Now stay."

He stayed. Through the fever's worst hours, through the delirium that had him counting tides aloud — *first tide, second tide, third tide* — like a man tallying debts, through the moment near dawn when his breathing went so shallow she put her ear against his lips to be sure of it. Delphine relieved her twice; Ada refused to leave longer than it took to boil water. She sat propped against the bunk with his wrist in her fingers, counting his pulse the way she'd once counted her father's, and she thought about how strange it was that devotion arrived unannounced — that you woke one morning willing to drown for someone whose name you hadn't known a week ago, and there was no ceremony in it, no declaration, only the quiet fact of your hand on their wrist and your refusal, absolute and unreasoning, to let the number stop.

Near dawn the fever broke. Sweat came, and with it sleep — real sleep, deep and dreamless. Ada eased his wrist down onto the blanket and sat back and discovered her own hands were shaking for the first time since the river had given him back.

Delphine appeared in the doorway with coffee and a look on her face that Ada had learned to read as caution.

"There's smoke downriver," Delphine said quietly. "Off the flagship. Not cooking smoke." She set the mug down. "Somebody's burning papers. And Ferro's boat is gone from the anchorage — slipped out an hour before dawn, running dark."

Ada looked at Ivo, sleeping, breathing, the tube rising and falling beneath his folded hand.

"Wake him gently," she said. "Whatever's coming, he'll want to meet it awake."
