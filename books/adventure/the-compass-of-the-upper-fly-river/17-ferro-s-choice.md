## 17. Ferro's Choice

Ferro Lantz had spent thirty years learning the exact weight of a debt. He could feel one the way a pilot feels weather coming — in the pressure behind his eyes, in the way the river went flat and silent before it turned on you. And standing on the aft deck of the *Iron Meridian*, with Commander Voss's flag snapping above him and two marines who smelled of gun oil flanking the companionway, Ferro felt the heaviest debt of his life come due.

"You've been busy," Voss said.

The commander was younger than Ferro expected. That was always the way with men who'd risen fast — they wore their rank like a borrowed coat, still smoothing the shoulders of it. Voss had a narrow face, careful eyes, and hands that never quite stopped moving: adjusting his cuffs, touching the rail, finding the seam of his trouser pocket and worrying it. A man who counted things. Ferro knew the type intimately, because he was the type himself.

"I run a salvage consultancy," Ferro said. "I go where the work is."

"Your consultancy is anchored four hundred meters from a restricted wreck at three in the morning." Voss turned his head slightly, and one of the marines produced a canvas satchel and set it on the deck between them with a soft, final thump. "And your papers say you're contracted to survey silt displacement for the Port Meridian Harbor Board. Which is interesting, because the Harbor Board has no record of any such contract."

Ferro kept his face still. Thirty years of card games, customs officers, and men with knives had taught him that a face was just another ledger — you wrote what you needed into it and hoped nobody audited. "Paperwork gets lost on rivers. Rivers eat everything."

"They do." Voss smiled without warmth. "That's rather the point of this whole operation."

*There it is.* The confirmation Ferro had been dreading since he'd spotted the fleet's barges massing under civilian flags, since he'd sold Ivo Okonkwo a rotting riverboat and taken a copy of a dead man's map as payment. Demolition charges, Ada Marsh had learned at Kolo Bend. Not salvage gear. The navy wasn't coming to raise the *Vigilant*. It was coming to unmake her — to turn evidence into slag and slag into silt, and the river would eat everything, just like Ferro had said.

"So here is where we find ourselves," Voss said, stepping closer. His voice dropped into a register meant for two men alone, though the marines were close enough to hear every word, which was itself a message. "I've read your file, Lantz. All of it. The Kolo Bend run in '09. The arms crates you moved up the Fly for Colonel Adeyemi before he fell out of favor. The customs man in Port Meridian whose widow still receives an anonymous envelope every month — don't look surprised, we know about that too, and frankly it's the most decent thing in the file."

"Sentimental of me," Ferro said.

"It's leverage, is what it is." Voss's hands found his pockets again. "You owe money to people who frighten me, and I frighten most people. Fourteen thousand crowns to the Sese brothers, plus interest that compounds like a fever. Your boat impounded twice. Your license suspended once and forged twice more. You are, by any honest accounting, finished — except that finished men are useful men, because they have nothing left to lose except their skin, and skin can be priced."

Ferro said nothing. The river moved beneath them, black and patient. Somewhere across the water, floodlights swept the surface in slow arcs, and beyond them, invisible in the dark, the *Magpie* lay at anchor behind a spit of gravel with her lights cold and her crew — his crew, though he'd never signed anything that said so, though he'd told himself a hundred times they were just a means to an end — waiting for him to come back or not come back.

"The man who owns that boat," Voss said. "Okonkwo. Ex-navy diver, dismissed for insubordination six weeks ago. He has something aboard that belongs to the Crown. A document. Possibly two." He paused, letting the silence do its work. "Bring me his head — figuratively, I'm not a savage, though if it comes to blood the tribunal will call it salvage accident — and the document, and I will clear your debts. All of them. The Sese brothers included. You'll walk out of this river owing nothing to anyone, with a naval salvage contract in your pocket worth more than you've earned in a decade."

"And if I decline?"

"Then at dawn my boarding parties sweep every vessel in this anchorage, yours first, and you spend the rest of your short, expensive life explaining ledgers to a magistrate who owes me his appointment." Voss shrugged, a small, economical motion. "You're a businessman, Lantz. Run the arithmetic."

Ferro ran it. He was good at arithmetic; it was the only scripture he'd ever kept faithfully. Fourteen thousand crowns against one diver's neck. A lifetime of running against one night of standing still. Every column balanced toward betrayal the way water balances toward the sea.

But there was a second ledger, the one he kept in the place behind his sternum where he'd buried his mother's voice, and that ledger had different columns. In it were written things like: *the cook Delphine Okafor fed you when no one else would serve you after Kolo Bend, and never asked what was in the crates.* Things like: *the girl Ada looked at you across her father's map and saw not a smuggler but a man, and you could not remember the last time anyone had looked at you that way.* Things like: *Ivo Okonkwo paid you not in crowns but in secrets, honestly given, and shook your hand like a man shaking a hand instead of closing a deal.*

Thirty years of debts, and the only ones that had ever actually weighed on him were the ones no creditor held paper for.

"I'll need until slack water," Ferro heard himself say. "To get my boat clear of the anchorage. She draws too much to run in the dark with the floodlights sweeping — your picket boats would spot her inside ten minutes, and then you'd have a civilian casualty on your hands, and tribunals do love those."

Voss studied him for a long moment. The floodlight swung past and lit his face white, then let it fall back into dark. "Slack water," he said. "One hour. Then we begin, with you or without you." He extended his hand, and Ferro took it, and both men understood that the handshake sealed nothing, proved nothing, promised nothing — it was just two liars confirming the terms of a test.

Ferro walked down the gangway to his launch with his spine straight and his mind already burning.

* * *

The ledgers lived in a waterproof case bolted under the floorplate of the *Magpie*'s engine room, because Ferro had never trusted a safe and never trusted a shore. Three volumes, oilcloth-wrapped, twenty-two years of names and sums and favors: every crate, every bribe, every man he'd bought and every man who'd bought him. They were worth more than the boat. They were worth more than the boat, the launch, and the warehouse in Port Meridian combined. Men had killed for less than what was written in those pages, and men had offered him fortunes for exactly that reason.

He knelt on the engine room plates with the case open in front of him and did not touch them for a full minute.

This was the moment the arithmetic screamed at him. Because there was a third option, the clever one, the Ferro Lantz special: take Voss's money, warn the crew anyway, keep the ledgers, play all sides forever. He'd done it a hundred times. It was practically his signature move — never burn a bridge, just sell tickets to cross it from both banks.

But he thought about Delphine's face when she'd bluffed him with the copied map, sharp and unfooled, knowing he might sell them out and serving him dinner anyway. He thought about Ada keeping watch through the night over a half-drowned diver she'd known five days. He thought about the way the *Magpie* had started to feel under his feet these past tides — not like an asset, not like an escape route, but like a deck he was responsible for, with people on it who laughed at his jokes and trusted his charts and had never once asked him what he was really worth.

You couldn't run all sides forever. Sooner or later the river narrowed, and you chose a bank.

Ferro took the ledgers topside, to the stern, where the charcoal brazier they used for cooking stood banked and glowing. He unwrapped the oilcloth. The top page of the oldest volume caught the brazier light: *Kolo Bend, '09. 40 rifles, 6 cases cartridges. Col. A. Paid in ivory. Witness: none required.*

"Witness: none required," he read aloud, to the river, to himself. "That was always the lie, wasn't it. There's always a witness. It's just a question of whether he's for sale."

He fed the first volume into the brazier page by page, because a whole book smothered the coals and burned unevenly, and Ferro Lantz had never in his life done a job unevenly. The paper curled, browned, bloomed into orange, and the ink lifted off the pages in ghost-smears before the fire took it entirely. Names going up. Sums going up. Twenty-two years of leverage becoming smoke that the night wind carried out over the black water and scattered.

It should have felt like dying. It didn't. It felt like setting down a load he'd forgotten he was carrying — the peculiar lightness of a man who has finally made himself worthless to everyone who ever wanted something from him.

The second volume went faster. The third he paused over, because the last pages held the newest entries, and the newest entry was the *Magpie* herself — *sold below value to I. Okonkwo, payment in confidence, see notes* — and beneath it, in his own cramped hand, a note he'd written the night of the sale and never shown anyone: *First honest transaction in eleven years. Felt strange. Felt good.*

He burned that too. Some things shouldn't be evidence. Some things should just be true.

When the last page was ash, he raked the brazier clean, dumped the ashes over the stern, and went looking for a bottle he'd been saving for an occasion he'd hoped would never come.

* * *

Delphine found him in the galley, because Delphine Okafor could find anything on a boat by smell, and the brandy had a smell. She filled the doorway with her arms crossed, still in her apron, flour on one wrist, and looked at him the way she looked at a sauce that had gone wrong — assessing whether it could be saved or whether the whole pot had to go.

"You're back early," she said. "Voss didn't keep you long."

"He kept me exactly as long as he needed." Ferro poured a second glass and slid it down the counter to her. She looked at it, looked at him, and picked it up, which he took as a small mercy. "He offered me fourteen thousand crowns and a clean slate to hand over Ivo and whatever he pulled off that wreck."

Delphine drank. Set the glass down with a click. "And?"

"And I'm telling you instead of doing it, so draw your own conclusions." He turned the empty glass in circles on the counter, watching the ring of moisture it left, thinking about how even a glass leaves a mark, how everything leaves a mark, how he'd spent thirty years pretending otherwise. "But there's a complication. I told Voss I'd deliver at slack water. One hour. After that, his boarding parties start sweeping the anchorage, and they'll come here first, because I'm the known quantity, the weak link, the man with debts. Which means in fifty minutes this boat is going to have marines on her decks unless she isn't here anymore."

"We can't run," Delphine said flatly. "Ivo's dive line runs to the wreck. He's down there right now, or getting ready to go down. We move the boat, we cut him off, we kill him."

"I know."

"Then what are you proposing, Ferro? Because I've known you eleven years and I've never once seen you propose something that cost you."

"That," Ferro said, "is because you've never seen me own anything worth losing." He pushed off the counter and went to the chart table, where the river lay spread under a lamp in all its treacherous detail — the anchorage, the wreck zone buoy, the falls roaring somewhere off the map's upper edge like a threat printed in blue. "Here's what I can give you. Voss thinks I'm his. He'll hold the sweep until slack water on my word, which buys Ivo his time underwater. But the moment that word comes due, every picket boat points at us. So somebody has to make sure that when they point, they're pointing at the wrong thing."

Delphine's eyes narrowed. "What wrong thing?"

"A fire." He tapped the chart, on the little marked square that was his own impounded barge, the *Cormorant*, riding at anchor two hundred meters south with a hold full of kerosene drums he'd never gotten around to selling. "A spectacular, blameless, entirely accidental fire on Ferro Lantz's other boat, drawing every searchlight and every skiff in the fleet to the south bank while the *Magpie* goes dark and drifts north on the ebb. Nobody interrogates a crew during a fire. Nobody boards a boat during a panic. You get your diver back, you get your manifest, and you get gone upriver before the fleet remembers which direction north is."

Delphine was quiet for a moment. Then: "And you? Where are you during this fire?"

"On the *Cormorant*, lighting it." He said it lightly, the way he said everything, but his hand wasn't steady on the glass and he knew she saw it. "Someone has to be seen doing it, or it's not a diversion, it's a trick. And if I'm seen, I'm arrested, and Voss finds out his bargain partner played him, and there's no ledger left to buy my way out with, because—" he gestured vaguely at the stern, at the smoke that was already somewhere over the water— "I no longer possess one."

"Ferro." Delphine set her glass down. Her voice had changed — lower, stripped of the kitchen-command briskness, down to something older and plainer. "Those ledgers were your life."

"My life was a list of crimes with prices next to them." He managed a grin, and it almost worked. "I've decided to diversify."

She came around the counter and, to his lasting astonishment, embraced him — brief and fierce, flour transferring from her wrist to his jacket, the smell of her kitchen and the river salt in his nose. "You impossible, mercenary old fraud," she said into his shoulder. "Eleven years I waited for you to be worth knowing."

"Took me thirty," Ferro said, his throat tight. "Don't tell anyone. I have a reputation."

* * *

They woke Ivo's crew quietly — Ada at the wheel, young Tammo on the engine telegraph, the diver himself still gray-faced and hollow-eyed from the fifth tide but upright, stubborn, alive, the waterproof tube with the manifest lashed inside his jacket like a second heartbeat. Ferro laid out the plan in the chart room in the flat, fast voice he used for deals, and watched their faces as it landed: the fire, the drift, the window of darkness, the falls beyond the wreck zone that they'd have to shoot in the dark because there was no other way out.

"That's suicide," Ada said. "The falls at night, in a boat this heavy—"

"The falls in daylight, with the fleet behind us, is also suicide. This is just suicide with better odds." Ferro met her eyes. "I know what I'm asking. I'm asking you to trust a smuggler's river-reading one more time."

"It's not that," Ada said, and her voice cracked on it. "It's that you're not coming with us. You're staying on that barge."

"Somebody has to strike the match where the light can see it." He shrugged, and hated how casual he couldn't make it sound. "Besides — a man ought to end up owning the ruin he made. The *Cormorant* is the last piece of the old Ferro Lantz afloat. Seems fitting he goes down with it."

"You're not going to die," Ivo said. It wasn't a question, and it wasn't a comfort. It was a statement delivered in the flat certainty of a man who had spent his life in black water deciding what was true. "You're going to light the fire, put your skiff over the far side, and paddle north along the shadow of the bluff until you're past the pickets. Then you're going to follow our masthead light upriver, because we're leaving it burning low and visible specifically so you can find us. I've read your charts, Lantz. You always leave yourself an exit. It's the only consistent virtue you have."

Ferro stared at him. Then he laughed — a real laugh, startled out of him, the first one in longer than he could reckon. "You magnificent suspicious bastard. You've been planning my escape since before I planned my sacrifice."

"Nobody sacrifices on my boat," Ivo said. "We've got enough dead men on this river. Go light your fire and come home."

*Home.* The word landed in Ferro's chest like a coin dropped down a well, falling a long way before it touched bottom.

* * *

The *Cormorant* burned beautifully. Of course she did; Ferro had chosen her cargo personally, years ago, with exactly this kind of contingency in the back of his mind, because Ferro Lantz never did anything without a contingency, and it turned out that all his contingencies had been leading here, to this: standing on his own deck with a lantern of kerosene, feeding his past to the flames while the fleet's searchlights whipped toward him and voices shouted across the water and the night turned orange from the south bank to the bluff.

He struck the fire in three places, made sure the picket boats saw him doing it — a lone figure, arms raised, screaming about a spontaneous combustion in the fuel stores, a tragedy, an act of God — and then, as the first boarding skiff ground against the *Cormorant*'s hull and boots hit her deck, he went over the far side into the cold black river with nothing but the clothes he stood in, a knife, and a paddling motion he hadn't used since he was a boy on the delta.

The current took him. The shouting receded. Behind him the *Cormorant* went up like a second dawn, and every light in the fleet bent toward her, and somewhere in that blaze of attention the *Magpie* slipped her anchor and ran dark for the falls.

Ferro swam for the shadow of the bluff, hauled out on the rocks, and looked back once at the fire that had been his fortune, his freedom, and his name — all of it climbing into the sky in a column of sparks that the wind carried north, over the river, toward the boat where his family was.

Every debt paid. Every ledger ash. Worth exactly nothing to every powerful man on this water, and — he grinned through chattering teeth, wringing the river out of his sleeves — completely free.

Then he saw the skiff. Low, dark, running without lights along the base of the bluff, straight toward the rocks where he crouched — and standing in its bow, rifle leveled, a figure in navy gray who called out in a voice like a blade being drawn:

"In the water! Hands where I can see them!"

Ferro raised his hands, and began, very sincerely, to lie.
