## 18. Sixth Tide: The Hold

The falls announced themselves before we saw them — a low thunder rolling up through the hull of the Magpie, through the soles of my boots, through the bones of my hands where they gripped the wheel rail. Below us, spread across the black water like a string of lit lanterns, the salvage fleet had finally closed its fist.

"Count them," Ivo said quietly.

I counted. Seven barges and two gunmetal patrol launches, floodlights raking the river in long white blades. Somewhere among them was Ferro Lantz's boat, and I could not decide whether that thought was a splinter of hope or a nail in a coffin. He had warned us — burned his own ledgers to do it, Delphine said, paid his price in ruin — but warnings only travel so fast, and the fleet had been faster.

"They've boxed us," Delphine said from the engine hatch, her voice flat as a cleaver on a board. "Falls behind, fleet ahead, and the channel narrows to nothing between. There is no river left for us."

Ivo stood at the bow with the current pulling at the boat like a hand at our collar, drawing us backward toward the roar. He did not look afraid. That frightened me more than anything else, because I had learned on this voyage that Ivo Okonkwo wore fear the way other men wore weather — you only saw it when it changed.

"There's one place left," he said.

Delphine turned. Even in the dark I could see her face go still. "No."

"The wreck."

"You want to hide inside a grave while they drop demolition charges on top of it." She said it not as a question but as an indictment, and she was right to. We had watched them load the charges at Kolo Bend. Crates stenciled DEMOLITION, STORES, NAVAL — crates that had nothing to do with salvage and everything to do with erasure.

"I want to be alive when the charges are done falling," Ivo said. "The stern section sits in forty feet against the channel wall. The old cargo hold has an air pocket if the bulkheads hold — Delphine, you told me yourself the *Vigilant* was built to survive a mine strike. Compartments, watertight doors, the whole doctrine. Her builders meant her to take punishment."

"She took it," Delphine said. "That's rather the problem."

Above us the sky was going the color of wet slate, and the first rain came down in fat, warm drops that hissed on the deck plates. Six tides. This was the sixth. My father's map had counted them out in grease-pencil strokes, and I had carried that map against my ribs for two hundred miles of river, and now the last tide was running out beneath a boat too slow and a crew too small and a truth too heavy to swim with.

"We dive or we die on the surface," Ivo said. "Those are the choices. The fleet will board anything floating. They'll find the manifest, and they'll find us, and by morning there won't be enough left of any of it to float a rumor."

"And if the bulkheads fail?" I asked. It was not a rhetorical question. I wanted him to say it plainly, because I had spent my whole life watching people refuse to say things plainly — my father most of all, dying in a shack with a map in his fist and a daughter's name in his mouth and never once saying *I am sorry* or *I was wrong* or even simply *this is what I did.*

"If the bulkheads fail," Ivo said, meeting my eyes across the rain, "we drown together. Which is more than the navy would give us."

It should have been a terrible answer. It was a terrible answer. But there was something in it — some cold, clean honesty — that steadied me the way my father's journals had steadied me in the lagoon. Eben Marsh had watched the *Vigilant* die from a hillside and had run instead of speaking, and it had cost him everything, and in the end he had chosen to spend his last breath buying back the chance to speak. I was not going to run. Whatever else happened in the next hour, I was not going to be my father in that one particular way.

"Do it," I said.

Delphine looked at me for a long moment. Then she spat over the side — an old river habit, a blessing or a curse, I never learned which — and threw herself down the engine hatch. "Then we do it properly," she called up. "Air bottles first. Lines coiled. And somebody get me that wreck layout before I have to remember it drunk, because I only ever drew it sober and I don't trust sober memory in a flooding ship."

* * *

We went into the water on the landward side of the hull, in the shadow between the wreck and the channel wall, where the fleet's floodlights couldn't reach without sweeping the shore. The rain had thickened to a steady drumming roar, and above it all the falls thundered like a congregation that would not stop singing.

The *Vigilant* lay on her starboard side, half-buried in silt, her port rail rising out of the water like the rib of some drowned animal. Up close she was bigger than any drawing could make her — Delphine's charcoal plans, drawn from memory in the Magpie's cabin, had prepared me for the shape of her but not the weight. Forty years of river lived in her hull. Weed streamed from her plates like hair. A school of small silver fish scattered from my light and reformed behind me, following, curious, as though we were something worth following.

Ivo went first, towing the manifest tube lashed inside his jacket, then me, then Delphine, who moved through the water with the unhurried economy of a woman who had done this a hundred times and resented every one of them. We swam down along the port rail to the collapsed cargo hatch — the one the navy's surveys never mentioned, the crack in the world that my father's map had found and Ivo's eye had confirmed.

"Inside, follow my line," Ivo said through the regulator, his voice thin and strange in my ears. "Hand over hand. Delphine leads after the third frame. Nobody lets go of anything."

The hatch was a jagged mouth. Going through it was like being swallowed — the light died all at once, and the water changed from river-water to ship-water, colder, older, tasting of iron and rot and something faintly sweet that I decided not to think about. My own breath sounded enormous. Somewhere ahead Ivo's lamp made a trembling yellow coin in the black, and I fixed my eyes on it and hauled myself along the guide line through corridors that had once been passageways and were now cathedral arches of silt and silence.

And then Delphine's remembered geometry took over. She stopped at a junction — I felt more than saw her glove touch the frame, counting frames the way a cook counts knives — and turned us down a companionway that sloped steeply, and suddenly the water around us was shallower, and then there was air.

It hit me like a wall. Not air exactly — a pocket of it, trapped under the upturned hull, foul with the smell of rust and dead ship and decades of stagnation, but air, real air, and we broke into it gasping, three heads bobbing in a black lake inside a steel belly, our lamps throwing wild reflections off a ceiling that was really a floor.

We clung to a pipe run that ran the length of the compartment. Below us, somewhere under the black water, was the rest of the hold. Above us, beyond maybe ten feet of hull plate, was the river, and the fleet, and the charges.

"Now," Ivo said, "we wait."

* * *

Waiting inside a sinking ship is its own particular discipline. You learn the sounds. At first there was only dripping — a hundred small drips echoing off steel, arrhythmic, maddening. Then the drips organized themselves into a rhythm, and I understood with a lurch that the rhythm was the falls, transmitted through the water, through the mud, through the hull, the whole drowned ship humming with the same note the river had been singing since before any of us were born.

We tied ourselves to the pipes. Ivo checked our lines twice, then a third time, and Delphine told him to stop fussing, and he didn't, and she let him, which was its own kind of tenderness.

"How long?" I asked.

"Charges go on the tide," Ivo said. "They'll want slack water for placement. An hour, maybe less. Then—" He didn't finish. He didn't need to.

I found I wasn't thinking about the charges. I was thinking about the lagoon, three days ago, when I'd knelt in my father's abandoned camp and read his journals by lamplight. *I saw them set the scuttling charges myself,* he had written, in a hand that deteriorated page by page from clerk-neat to drunken scrawl. *I saw the officer pay the bosun. I ran. God forgive me, I ran, and the ship went down at dawn with all hands who didn't know.* He had memorized the manifest's location because he hadn't dared carry paper. He had spent thirty years carrying it in his head like a stone.

*I'm sorry,* I had whispered to the empty camp, to the man who had died in a dockside shack holding my hand. *I understand now. I forgive you. Come with me anyway.* And I had taken the journal, and the map, and the mission, and made them mine.

Now I sat in the belly of the ship he had watched die, waiting for the navy to finish the job, and I found that I was not afraid in the way I expected to be afraid. I was angry. Anger is a better fuel than fear. It burns longer and it doesn't make your hands shake.

The first charge went early.

* * *

There was no warning. One moment the ship was humming with the falls' single note; the next, the note shattered. The detonation came through the water as a blow — not sound so much as pressure, a fist of it that slammed through the hull and through my chest and knocked the breath I'd just found back out of me. Silt rained from the overhead. Our lamps swung wildly. Somewhere far aft, steel groaned like a living thing in pain.

"Second charge," Delphine shouted over the ringing. "They're walking them down the hull. They mean to break her back!"

"Hold position!" Ivo's arm locked around my harness. "If we move in the water now we lose the pocket — we ride it out here!"

The second blast was closer. The third closer still. Each one came as that terrible fist, and each time the black lake around us shuddered and the air pocket compressed — I felt it in my ears, a squeezing, and I worked my jaw and swallowed and did the things you do to keep your head from splitting. Debris ticked against the hull like hail. A pipe joint somewhere let go with a shriek and a jet of bubbles boiled up through the water below us, and for one vertiginous moment I understood that the pocket was venting, that our air was escaping into the river in silver strings, and that every minute we stayed we were breathing a smaller room.

Then the big one hit.

It was aft, close, devastating. The whole ship lurched — actually lurched, the way a boat lurches, which no sunken thing should do — and the groan that followed went on and on, deepening, a sound like the end of the world played at quarter speed. Water came into the compartment. Not a trickle: a wall of it, bursting through a bulkhead seam fifty feet down the hold, black and boiling with silt, and the air pocket began to die in front of us, the ceiling of it dropping, the reflections on the overhead shrinking toward us like a closing eye.

"Out!" Ivo roared. "Forward! Forward bulkhead — Delphine, the cross-passage — GO!"

What happened next, I can only tell you the way it felt, because it did not happen to three people. It happened to one body. Ivo shoved me toward the line; Delphine caught my harness and hauled; I got my gloves on the pipe run and pulled and someone's hand — Ivo's, Delphine's, both — pressed me onward whenever I slowed. The water rose past our waists, our chests. The air shrank to a wedge. Silt blinded us and we navigated by rope and by Delphine's voice, counting frames aloud like a prayer — *four, five, six, here, HERE* — until her glove found the cross-passage frame and we squeezed through into a narrower dark, and Ivo jammed the passage's old watertight door shut behind us.

It weighed nothing in the water and everything in the world. He spun the wheel with Delphine braced beside him, both of them hauling against decades of corrosion, and the door came around with a scream of rust and seated home with a boom I felt in my teeth.

Silence. Or near it. Just our four hands on the wheel, six lungs working, and beyond the steel, muffled to a heartbeat, the river doing its work.

"Is it holding?" I heard myself ask. My voice sounded like someone else's.

Ivo put his ear to the door. Delphine did the same, on the other side, and for a moment they looked — I almost laughed — like two people listening at a wall for whether the neighbors had gone to bed.

"Holding," Ivo said.

"For now," Delphine said. "This compartment's intact. There'll be air trapped high, forward. But Ada—" and she turned her lamp on me, and in its circle her face was streaked with silt and utterly calm, "—the ship is breaking up around us. That door buys hours, not days. When the fleet finishes their demolition and clears off, that's our window. Not before. Not after."

"So we breathe," Ivo said.

"So we breathe," she agreed.

We found the trapped air forward — a shallow lens under the upturned deck, barely enough to sit with our heads in. We wedged ourselves onto a fallen bunk frame, the three of us shoulder to shoulder, lines checked, lamps dimmed to save the batteries. Through the hull, faint and far, came the concussions of the remaining charges, each one smaller-feeling than the last, the fleet working its way aft, away from us, killing the ship section by section with bureaucratic thoroughness.

I sat between them in the drowning dark of a murdered ship, and I waited for terror to come back, and it didn't. What came instead was stranger. My father had described the *Vigilant*'s last night in his journal — the officers sealing compartments, men passing lanterns hand to hand, strangers becoming crew in the space of a single order. *In the end,* he had written, *they were magnificent, and none of them knew why the ship was dying.* I had read that line a dozen times and thought it sentiment. Sitting there, feeling Delphine's shoulder solid against mine and Ivo's hand briefly, wordlessly grip my arm every time a charge rolled through the hull — checking I was there, telling me he was — I understood it at last. Terror does something to strangers. It boils them down. What's left when the fear burns off is just people, holding on.

"We're going to surface with it," I said. It wasn't a hope. It was a decision, spoken aloud so the dark would have to hear it. "The manifest. All of it. Every name."

Neither of them answered right away. Then Ivo's hand found mine in the black and squeezed once, and Delphine said, "Every name," and outside, the last charge went off, and the great ship that had been dying for forty years finally, fully died — and the water around us began, very slowly, to move.

Not settle. Move. A current, pushing forward through the flooded passages, tugging our lines, dragging bubbles past our faces toward the bow.

Ivo's lamp snapped up. I saw him feel it the way a sailor feels weather change.

"That's not the river," he said. "The river doesn't flow through a sealed compartment." He was already unclipping his line, already moving toward the forward bulkhead, where — faint, impossible, unmistakable — daylight was beginning to seep through a seam in the steel.

The stern was opening. And something in the wreck was pulling us toward the light.
