## 19. What Rises

The water in the hold had a sound now, and the sound was wrong.

Ivo had spent eleven years listening to flooded steel — the tick of cooling metal, the groan of a hull settling into mud, the patient percussion of silt shifting against bulkheads. He knew the vocabulary of drowned ships the way other men knew weather. And what he heard through the black water of Number Three hold was not settling. It was screaming.

Above him, muffled by forty feet of river and two decks of riveted plating, the fleet's demolition charges were being placed. He could not see them. He did not need to. Delphine had counted the skiffs from the Magpie's wheelhouse before they'd gone to dark stations: six runs, six loads, cradles slung under each hull with the unmistakable silhouette of shaped charges. Not salvage gear. Not lifting bags. The navy wasn't raising the *Vigilant*. They were unmaking her.

And Ada, Delphine, and Ferro were sealed into the forward compartment behind him because there had been nowhere else to go — trapped between the fleet at their stern and the falls ahead, they'd run the Magpie into the torn-open flank of the very wreck the navy meant to erase, and Ivo had led them down through the cargo hatch into the one place on the river no one would look.

It had seemed like genius for about four hours.

Now the charges were armed, and the wreck that sheltered them had become a coffin with a fuse.

* * *

He found them in the old officers' mess, aft of the watertight door, where the air pocket still held — knee-deep in river water, lit by two hand lamps wrapped in oilcloth so their glow wouldn't leak through the portholes. Ada sat on an overturned mess table with her boots in the flood, holding her father's journal against her chest like a shield. Delphine stood at the door, ear pressed to the steel, reading the ship the way she'd once read the galley clock — by rhythm, by instinct, by the feel of something about to boil over.

"They've stopped running skiffs," she said when Ivo came through. "That means they're done placing. That means it's soon."

"How soon?"

Delphine looked at him with the flat patience of a woman who had fed three hundred sailors a day and learned not to flinch at bad news. "Ivo. You were the diver. You tell me."

He told her the truth, because they were past the hour for anything else. "When they fire a scuttling pattern on a hull this size, they want it to go down in one breath. First charge breaks the keel. The rest follow in sequence, half-second intervals, bow to stern." He put his palm flat against the bulkhead. Through the steel he felt the ship's pulse — quick, shallow, frightened. "The stern goes last. We're standing in the part of the ship that gets the longest warning."

"And after the warning?"

"After the warning," Ivo said, "the river comes in here faster than you can scream."

Ada's head came up. In the lamplight her face was all planes and resolve, Eben Marsh's jaw set against the dark. "Then we're not standing here when it happens."

"No," Ivo said. "We're not."

* * *

Here is what Ivo Okonkwo knew that the men laying those charges had forgotten, or never learned, or been paid not to learn: a scuttling charge does not only destroy. It moves water. Forty pounds of high explosive against a keel plate doesn't punch a hole — it displaces a column of river upward through every opening in the hull above it, a fist of pressure traveling at the speed of sound through water, seeking the path of least resistance the way any drowning man seeks air.

And Ivo knew where the path of least resistance was, because he had found it on his fourth dive into this wreck, back when he still worked for the people now trying to kill it. Aft of Number Three hold, where the cargo hatch had collapsed inward during the sinking, the deck plates had peeled back like the petals of a flower made of rust — a ragged funnel, three feet across at the throat, opening upward through two decks into the flooded well of the aft cargo trunk. The navy's surveys had never marked it. Their sonar read it as debris. But Ivo had swum into that throat with his lamp off and felt the current of the river breathing through it, slow and tidal, the wreck inhaling and exhaling around the wound in its own body.

A chimney, he had thought then. A dead ship's last breath.

Now it was going to be theirs.

"The hatch shaft," he said. "All of us. Now, before they fire. If we're in the water column when the charge goes, the surge carries us up the shaft instead of tearing us apart in the open hold."

Delphine stared at him. "You want to ride the explosion."

"I want to ride the *water* the explosion makes. There's a difference." He was already moving, pulling the dive gear they'd stashed against the bulkhead — the spare tanks, the single working regulator, the line spool. "The pressure wave will pass through us in a fraction of a second. Painful. Maybe bleeding ears. Then the displaced water comes up behind it like a piston, and if we're centered in the shaft, centered exactly, it lifts us. Two decks. Maybe sixty feet of rise. We come out the top of the cargo trunk or we don't come out."

Ada's voice cut through the dark, level as a surveyor's line. "Or?"

Ivo met her eyes. She deserved the arithmetic; she'd earned it in the lagoon, in the checkpoint, in every mile since she'd stepped aboard a rotting boat to finish her father's work. "Or the shaft is blocked higher up and the water has nowhere to go, and we're crushed between the piston and the plug. Or we surface inside the trunk and the trunk is sealed by wreckage and we drown in an air pocket going bad. Or the charges break the stern loose entirely and the whole section rolls over us on the way down." He buckled the tank harness across his chest. "Three ways to die. One way out. Same odds as everything else we've done since Kolo Bend."

For a moment nobody spoke. Then Ferro Lantz — smuggler, traitor-by-arithmetic, redeemed-by-ruin, who had burned his own ledgers that morning rather than sell them — laughed, short and hard, the laugh of a man who had finally run out of things left to lose and found it liberating.

"Three ways to die," he said. "On my boats we called that good odds." He picked up the second lamp. "Lead the way, diver."

* * *

They went down into the water together, four bodies slipping off the mess table platform into the flooded corridor, hand over hand along the guide line Ivo had rigged on their way in. The water was cold enough to stop breath, black enough to erase the world beyond arm's reach. Ivo went first, Ada's hand locked on his shoulder strap, Delphine behind her with Ferro at the end, his grip on the line white-knuckled and absolute. The regulator passed between them in a rotation Ivo had drilled into them in the lagoon, thirty seconds each, no argument, no panic — the discipline of people who had decided, somewhere along the drowned miles of this river, that they belonged to each other.

The corridor ended at the broken hatch. Ivo felt for the petals of peeled deck plate with his fingertips, found the throat of the shaft, and pulled himself into it. Above him, sixty feet of vertical black water, and at the top of it — he checked twice, by touch, by the faint gray ghost of light filtering down from the surface world — the open well of the cargo trunk. Clear. The chimney was breathing.

They stacked themselves in the shaft like coins in a roll: Ivo lowest, facing up, Ada above him with her knees drawn in, Delphine above her, Ferro at the top holding the lamp case against his chest. Four bodies in a throat of rusted steel, suspended in the dark, waiting for the navy to pull the trigger.

Through the water Ivo felt it before he heard it — felt it in his teeth, in the plates against his shoulders, in the river itself going tense the way a rope goes tense before it parts.

Then the world below them turned to thunder.

* * *

There is no describing the first half-second honestly. Language is built for surfaces, and Ivo was inside the drum.

The pressure wave came up through the keel and through their bodies in less time than a heartbeat — a hammer blow of compressed water that hit him in the chest and spine simultaneously, folded him double, cracked something in his left ear that sang a high white note nothing had ever sung before. Ada's grip on his strap became a death grip; he felt her small frame shudder through the steel like a struck bell. For one instant all four of them were simply shockwaves given flesh, and then —

— and then the piston came.

The displaced water rose beneath them like the hand of something enormous and impatient, and the shaft took them. Steel walls strobed past — rivet lines, cable runs, the fossil shadow of a ladder — and Ivo did the only thing there was to do, which was nothing: streamline, arms crossed, chin tucked, trusting the geometry he'd bet four lives on. The roar of the surge filled the shaft wall to wall. Rust flakes tore past like migrating birds. Somewhere below, the stern section was dying — he heard it as a deep structural moan rising through the water, the ship's skeleton letting go of itself, plates shearing, the whole aft body beginning its long slow roll toward the riverbed it had been cheated of for twenty years.

But the chimney held. The chimney carried them.

Sixty feet of rise in perhaps two seconds. At the top the shaft spat them into the cargo trunk well, and the water there was already boiling — the surge venting sideways, geysers climbing the trunk walls, the air space above compressing and decompressing like a lung in terror. Ivo broke the surface first, gasping, deaf in one ear, tasting blood and rust. Ada surfaced beside him, thrashing, and he caught her collar and steadied her against the trunk wall. Delphine came up roaring air, furious and alive. Ferro came up last, coughing, clutching the lamp case, and when Ivo got a hand on him he was laughing — laughing in the dark, in the boiling water, in the belly of a ship coming apart around them.

"Still good odds!" Ferro shouted, and no one could hear him, and it didn't matter.

Because now the wreck was killing itself in earnest, and they had maybe a minute to get out of its mouth.

* * *

The cargo trunk was open to the sky — that was the design, the reason the shaft had worked, the reason Ivo had gambled on it. Twenty years of salvage attempts had stripped the trunk covers away, and above them now was not steel but night: rain, spray, the distant sweep of the fleet's floodlights painting the low clouds orange, and the black silhouettes of the barges a quarter mile upstream, oblivious, finishing their work.

Between the trunk rim and the open river lay forty yards of shattered main deck, most of it awash, some of it already sliding. The stern section — the section they stood inside — was breaking free at the expansion joint, groaning on the hinge of its own keel, beginning the roll Ivo had named as the third way to die.

"Deck," Ivo said. "Now. Single file. Move like the ship is already gone, because it is."

They climbed. Hand over hand up the trunk ladder, out onto the deck plates, and the moment their weight crossed onto the open deck the ship reminded them whose territory this was: a plate shifted under Ferro, dropped six inches with a shriek, and Delphine hauled him off it by the collar without breaking stride. Rain hit the deck like gravel. Ahead, the bow section still stood true, and beyond it — Ivo saw it and his heart slammed once, hard, with a feeling he had no name for except *home* — the Magpie, wedged in the wreck's torn flank exactly where they'd driven her, dark, intact, her rail catching the orange flicker of the floodlights.

Ada reached the rail first. Below her, ropes still hung where they'd left them, and beyond the ropes the river waited, swollen with rain, running fast toward the falls.

"The manifest," she said suddenly, spinning on the wet deck, and Ivo saw the horror land in her face. "Ivo — the tube —"

"It's on me." He tapped the waterproof cylinder strapped flat against his back under the harness, where it had ridden through the piston, through the shaft, through the death of a warship. "It doesn't leave my body. That was the deal I made with myself at the bottom of Number Three hold, and I keep my deals."

Ada looked at him — really looked, rain streaming off her face, the journal still somehow dry inside her jacket — and whatever passed between them in that moment needed no words. Her father's map had started this. Her father's daughter would finish it. And the paper against Ivo's spine, the manifest that named the men who had sunk their own ship and shot the officer who tried to stop them, was going to rise out of this river tonight or not at all.

They went down the ropes into the water, four abreast, and swam for the Magpie while the *Vigilant*'s stern rolled over behind them like a leviathan turning in its sleep.

* * *

Getting the Magpie free was twelve minutes of pure violence, and Ivo remembered almost none of it in order. He remembered Delphine at the capstan with her sleeves torn off, hauling the stern line in hand over hand with a strength that seemed drawn from somewhere outside her slight frame. He remembered Ferro in the engine room coaxing the old diesel to life with a screwdriver and blasphemy, the engine catching on the fourth try with a bark that echoed off the wreck. He remembered Ada on the bow, pole in hand, fending the hull off the collapsing deck plates as the *Vigilant*'s superstructure came down in sheets of rusted steel close enough to shower the boat with scale.

And he remembered the moment the Magpie tore free — the exact instant the keel cleared the wreck's flank and the river took hold of them, and the stern section of the *Vigilant*, freed at last after twenty years of false buoyancy, slid beneath the surface with a sound like the world exhaling. The floodlights from the fleet swept across the spot where she'd been. Bubbles came up in columns. Decks, plates, the bones of a ship, the bones of a crime — all of it descending together into the dark, erased, or so the men on those barges believed.

Ivo stood at the wheel in the rain and watched the wreck die, and he did not feel grief. He felt something he had never once felt in eleven years of salvage diving, eleven years of counting his life in miles: he felt *witnessed*. The ship had died in front of witnesses who mattered. The dead officer in the flooded compartment, the shot man in the navy uniform whose remains Ivo had found and could not save and could not report — the manifest in its tube against Ivo's spine was that man's testimony, recovered at last, rising when he could not.

The river carried them downstream, away from the falls, away from the fleet, into the rain and the dark. Behind them the floodlights swept empty water. Ahead, fifty miles downriver, Port Meridian slept unaware that by morning its harbor office would be the most important building on the coast.

Delphine came up beside him at the wheel, soaked, bleeding from a split lip she hadn't mentioned, and put one hand on the spokes beside his.

"Well?" she said.

Ivo reached back and drew the tube from its straps. He held it up between them, rain beading on the brass caps, and for a long moment neither of them spoke. Inside that cylinder: a cargo manifest, waterproofed by a careful man twenty years ago, listing crates that had never been crates, weights that had never been cargo, signatures that would hang a fleet commander and crack open a cover-up from Port Meridian to the capital. Inside that cylinder: Eben Marsh's life's work, Ada's inheritance, the truth the navy had spent two decades and six tides' worth of explosives trying to drown.

"It survived," Ivo said. "We survived. Tomorrow it goes to the tribunal."

Delphine nodded slowly, watching the dark water slide past. "And if the tribunal's bought too?"

Ivo hadn't answered that question yet. He suspected he'd have to answer it soon, with more than words. But tonight — tonight the river had done the impossible thing, had taken four strangers sealed inside a dying ship and lifted them up its broken spine like a blessing, and set them down in a boat, alive, together, carrying the proof.

He steered into the current and rang the engine bell twice, and the Magpie ran for Port Meridian through the tail of the storm, carrying a dead prospector's promise, a murdered officer's testimony, and a family that had assembled itself out of the wreckage of four separate lives.

Behind them, in the grey hour before dawn, a navy launch came up the channel at full speed, closing fast, its searchlight sweeping the banks — someone aboard the fleet had finally understood what had risen from the wreck, and what it would do to them.

Ivo saw the light first. He said nothing. He just put the wheel over, and the Magpie leaned into the turn, running dark, running for the law, with the whole night between them and the men who wanted the night to stay closed forever.
