## 20. The Tribunal at Port Meridian

Delphine Okafor had not stood in a naval mess hall in eleven years, and she had sworn she never would again. But the tribunal had been convened in the customs house at Port Meridian, and the customs house had been built by the same colonial engineers who built every galley on the coast — the same long room, the same high windows, the same smell of varnish and salt and institutional dread. She recognized it the way you recognize a face from a bad dream. The room where they had once fed her was now the room where she would feed them something else entirely.

She had dressed for it. Not in her whites — those were gone, burned or buried or traded, she couldn't remember which, only that the night she'd been dismissed she'd stood on the pier of a navy yard and watched a supply officer carry her knife roll away in a canvas sack like it was contraband. No. Today she wore river clothes: a faded blue dress that Ada had pressed with an iron borrowed from the mission, her mother's beads, and boots still crusted with Fly River mud that no amount of scrubbing would surrender. Ivo had told her to clean them. She had told him the mud was testimony.

The Magpie sat at the public dock below the customs house, tied off among fishing boats and a coastal steamer that reeked of diesel and dried fish. Ferro Lantz's old berth was empty. Of course it was. Ferro was in custody two streets over, in a cell he had purchased with his own ruin, and Delphine had visited him the evening before with a tin of pepper soup and a promise she wasn't sure she could keep: *I'll speak your name kindly, if it can be done kindly.* He had eaten the soup sitting on his bunk like a man at a table, spoon held properly, elbows in, some last ember of the gentleman smuggler refusing to die. He'd said, "Tell them about the night. That's all anyone ever wants. The night."

So she would tell them about the night.

* * *

The tribunal chamber filled slowly, the way water fills a hull — quietly at first, then all at once. Three commissioners sat behind a raised bench: a heavyset woman with a magistrate's chain, a retired hydrographer with ink-stained fingers, and a young naval legal officer whose uniform fit him like a borrowed coat, as though he'd been assigned to this duty as punishment and knew it. Along one wall sat clerks with typewriters older than Delphine. Along the other, counsel for the Navy Directorate — three men in dark suits, pale as paper, arranged in descending order of seniority like organ pipes.

And in the gallery, packed shoulder to shoulder: fishermen, market women, dockhands, the whole loose population of Port Meridian that had heard the rumors running upriver faster than any boat. Someone had chalked a word on the customs house steps overnight and someone else had scrubbed it, but the ghost of the letters still showed in the damp: *MURDERERS.*

Delphine found her seat beside Ivo. He looked wrong out of the water — too tall in a pressed shirt Ada had bullied him into, his hands folded like a man at church who didn't believe in churches. His knuckles were still split from the dive. Around them sat the rest of their strange congregation: Ada in her nurse's whites with the sleeves rolled, jaw set; Tomás the engineer, who had never in his life been inside a courtroom and kept checking the exits; Nils, the one-armed rigger, who had brought his wooden hand and set it on the rail in front of him like a relic, daring anyone to look away from it.

At the back of the gallery, holding a battered notebook and chewing the end of a pencil, sat a thin man with sunburned ears whom everyone seemed to know and nobody trusted: Corrigan, the journalist. The muckraker. The man whose paper had been banned twice and resurrected three times under different names. Ivo had delivered the manifest to him and to the tribunal simultaneously — deliberately, so neither could bury it — and Corrigan had spent the last two days printing everything, verifying nothing yet, running headlines off a hand press in a shed behind the fish market. This morning's edition was already selling in the square outside: **THE SHIP THEY SANK TWICE**, above a facsimile of the manifest's stamp, blurred but legible.

Delphine watched the clerk carry the evidence tube into the chamber — the same tube Ivo had surfaced with, half-drowned, clutching like a drowning man clutches driftwood. The tube went onto the bench table and was opened under seal. Inside: the cargo manifest, waterproofed in waxed linen, its stamps crisp after however many fathoms and years; and the captain's logbook, its final entries written in a hand that shook.

The senior counsel for the Directorate rose first and made his argument before anything had even been said: that the document was unprovenanced, possibly forged, certainly inadmissible, recovered illegally from a restricted wreck zone by persons acting without authority, several of whom were themselves wanted on outstanding warrants. His voice was smooth. It was the voice of men reading inventory lists over bodies.

The magistrate let him have his minute. Then she looked down at Delphine's row and said, "We will hear witnesses. Beginning with the cook."

* * *

They swore her in with a phrase she half-remembered, and then it was just her and the room, and the room was listening harder than any dining hall full of officers had ever listened to her in eleven years of service.

"State your name and service," the hydrographer said.

"Delphine Okafor. Cook, second class, His Majesty's Naval Service, river flotilla. Eleven years. Dismissed without cause eight years ago, though the record says cause. The record says I poisoned a wardroom. The record is a lie, but we'll get there."

A rustle in the gallery. The young legal officer wrote something very fast.

"Tell us what you served aboard," the magistrate said. "And when."

"The *Vigilant*. River gunboat, stern-wheeler, converted to supply run two years before she went down. I cooked for forty crew and sometimes sixty when we carried marines. I know that ship the way you know your own kitchen — every bulkhead, every ladder, every place the deck plates squeaked. And I know her holds." She paused. "Especially the one that wasn't on my tour."

The hydrographer leaned forward. "Explain."

"When I took my familiarization walk aboard — every crew member does, first week — the engineering officer walked me stem to stern. Galley, mess, magazine, engine room, aft hold, forward hold, sealed compartment behind the forward hold marked on the plan as 'dry stores, reserve.' Except it wasn't dry stores. I asked why a reserve dry-stores compartment needed a separate ventilation line plumbed independent of the main system, and a lock keyed to officers only, and he shrugged and said, 'Ask the purser.' So I asked the purser, and the purser said, 'Ask the XO,' and the XO smiled at me the way men smile when they've decided you're furniture, and said, 'Cook, worry about the rice.'"

She let that sit. In the gallery, an old fisherman muttered something to his neighbor, and the mutter traveled.

"For six years I worried about the rice. And I watched. Because a galley sees everything. Officers forget you're there. You're steam and noise and a white apron; you're part of the machinery. I saw crates go into that compartment at night, crates heavier than flour has any right to be, carried by working parties who never spoke above a whisper and never met my eyes. I saw the purser keep a ledger nobody audited. I saw shore leave canceled the same week every month, always the same week, like a tide chart of somebody's convenience. And once — once — I saw the compartment opened in daylight, and inside, stacked on shelving, were cases stenciled with markings I did not recognize and could not read, wrapped in oilcloth, and beneath them, hidden under tarpaulin, crates stamped with a commercial shipping line's name. A civilian company. On a navy boat. In a locked compartment. Explain that to me, sirs. Because nobody aboard would explain it to me."

The senior counsel stirred. "You are aware, witness, that sealed compartments are standard for classified materiel—"

"I am aware," Delphine said, "that classified materiel does not arrive aboard in civilian freight crates, does not get inventoried off-book, and does not require the captain to dismiss the watch early so the dockside lights are out when it's moved. I am aware of what smuggling looks like. My father ran goods up the coast during the war years. He's dead, God rest him, but he taught me the difference between cargo and contraband, and that compartment was contraband wearing a navy stencil."

The young legal officer spoke up, hesitant: "Witness, did you report this?"

"I reported it to the XO. In writing. I kept a copy." She reached into her dress pocket and produced a fold of paper gone soft as cloth with age, and the chamber went quiet around it. "He returned it to me the next morning with a note. The note said: *Cook, your concern is noted. Your continued employment depends on your discretion.* I kept both. Eight years in a tin with my mother's recipes, because I didn't know what else to do with a thing like that. Now I do."

The clerk took the papers. The senior counsel's descending order of organ pipes shifted; the middle one began drafting something furiously.

"And the night she sank," the magistrate said. "Tell us about the night."

Delphine closed her eyes. She didn't need to; the night was still in there, whole, preserved like things preserve in brine.

"We were anchored off Kolo Bend. Storm coming — barometer falling all afternoon, and Captain Voss ordered double moorings, which was correct, which was careful, which was unlike him in small ways that made me uneasy later. Supper service was early because of weather. I remember exactly what I cooked, because cooks remember: river fish in tomato and okra, rice, fried plantain. Forty plates. I remember who ate and who didn't. The captain came through the galley at nineteen hundred, unusual, and asked me — asked *me*, the cook — whether the current felt stronger than the gauge said. I told him yes, it always lies a little at Kolo Bend, but tonight it lied a lot. He nodded and left, and I thought: good, he knows.

"At twenty-two hundred the storm hit. By midnight the anchor watch was reporting strain. And here is the thing I need this tribunal to understand, the thing I have carried for eight years like a stone in my chest: the engine order telegraph rang *ahead full* against the storm, and the engines answered — I felt it through the deck plates, I was in the galley securing the stores — and then, minutes later, the telegraph rang again, and the engines did *not* answer. And the engineering officer came aft past my galley at a run, and he was not running toward the engine room. He was running toward the sealed compartment. With keys.

"Fifteen minutes later the forward section began to flood. Not from the bow, gentlemen — from *inside*. From below the waterline, amidships, where no wave puts water. I stood in my galley and watched water come up through the deck drain in the passageway, clean water, cold water, river water rising where river water had no business rising, and I understood — I understood in my body before my mind caught up — that the ship was being opened from within. Scuttled. From the compartment. Through valves or charges or whatever they'd installed down there, I don't know the mechanism, I'm a cook, but I know flooding when I taste it in the air, and that air tasted of a ship swallowing herself on purpose.

"The order came to abandon. Some obeyed. Some were mustered aft to the boats and the boats were — I will say what I saw — the boats were insufficient, and the launching was chaos, and men I fed every day went into black water in a storm. Twenty-one souls lost. Twenty-one. I counted the names for years. I can still say them."

She stopped. Her voice had not broken, but the room had gone so quiet that the sound of the river outside the high windows was audible, slapping the pilings below.

"Captain Voss did not abandon. I learned later — much later, from the inquiry that never happened — that he was found in the forward compartment area. And I will tell this tribunal what the survivors whispered and the record denied: the captain was fighting it. He rang the telegraph, he tried to counter-flood, he tried to save his ship from his own officers, and the ship died anyway, and somewhere in that flooded dark, so did he. The logbook you have on your bench — the shaking hand at the end — that is a man writing while his ship is murdered around him. Read his last entry aloud in this chamber. Let everyone hear what a dying captain wrote about his own purser."

The magistrate did read it. Aloud. The words crossed the chamber like a slow tide:

*"Purser refuses to open manifest to me. Compartment secured against my order. Requesting board of investigation survive me. If this ship sinks at moorings in a gale with no damage to moorings, let no man call it weather."*

The gallery did not erupt. It did something worse. It went silent, and then, one by one, people began to stand — fishermen, market women, dockhands — standing without a word, the way congregations stand, until the whole gallery was on its feet and the magistrate had to call order twice before the hearing could continue.

Delphine sat down beside Ivo. He didn't look at her. He put his split-knuckled hand over hers, briefly, and took it away, and that was enough. Ada, on her other side, was crying the way nurses cry — efficiently, without noise, already back to work taking notes.

* * *

The afternoon belonged to the documents, and the documents were merciless.

The manifest, authenticated by the hydrographer against port records, listed six consignments over fourteen months aboard the *Vigilant*: arms and ammunition routed to a private concession company upriver, gold bullion inbound, and — the line that made the middle counsel stop drafting and start praying — "special cargo, sealed compartment, per XO instruction, no survey." Cross-referenced against the purser's personal ledger, which a former clerk had surrendered to Corrigan's paper that very morning in a brown envelope, the numbers matched to the shilling. The smuggling operation had a spine, and the spine ran from the sealed compartment up through the XO to two directors of the concession company, one of whom was currently — the gallery learned this from Corrigan's afternoon special, hawked in the square between sessions — a member of the Legislative Council.

The arrests began before sundown.

Delphine watched them from the customs house steps, because she refused to watch them from anywhere else. First the purser, taken quietly at a hotel, already packed, already shaved, already holding a ticket under a false name — a man who had known for days, maybe weeks, that the water was rising around him. Then the XO, taken at the naval yard with less quiet, two Marines and a provost officer, and the sight of it — the sight of the yard's own gate guards turning their own executive officer around by the shoulders — did something to the crowd that no speech could have done. They didn't cheer. They watched. Port Meridian had spent eight years being told the *Vigilant* was lost to weather, and today the weather was finally named, and the weather wore a uniform.

The two concession directors were harder. One had flown out on a chartered plane two nights ago — Corrigan's evening edition would name the destination and the airstrip and the fueling clerk who signed the chit, because Corrigan worked like Ivo dived: alone, deep, and without asking permission. The other was arrested trying to reach his launch at the yacht club, and the yacht club doorman, a small vindictive man with a magnificent memory, delayed him at the gate for four full minutes over a guest pass dispute, and those four minutes were the difference between exile and handcuffs. Justice, Delphine reflected, is mostly a matter of doors, and who is standing at them, and how long they choose to stand there.

By dark, the tribunal had adjourned for the day with three officers in custody, two fugitives named and flagged, and the Directorate's senior counsel requesting — in a voice that had lost its smoothness sometime around the captain's logbook — a fortnight's recess to "review the evidentiary basis." The magistrate granted him one week and told him, in front of everyone, that the evidentiary basis was a drowned ship, twenty-one dead men, and a cook's memorandum eight years old, and that if his office needed more than a week to review that, perhaps his office should review itself.

The gallery laughed. It was not a kind laugh. Delphine did not join it. She was thinking about Ferro, in his cell two streets over, eating pepper soup like a gentleman, waiting to learn whether his ruin had bought anything.

* * *

Corrigan found her at dusk on the Magpie's aft deck, where she was doing what she always did at dusk: cooking. The tribunal had shaken her more than she'd admitted, and the way she handled being shaken was to feed people. She had a pot of the same fish stew she'd cooked the night the *Vigilant* died — deliberately, ritually, the exact recipe, the exact okra, because some meals are memorials and you cook them the same way you'd want your own death remembered.

Corrigan came up the gangway smelling of ink and cheap tobacco and asked, politely, terribly, for thirty minutes.

"You testified well," he said.

"I testified true. Well is your department."

"Can I print the memorandum? The XO's note? '*Your continued employment depends on your discretion*'?"

"Print it whole. Print my name under it. Don't soften it, don't contextualize it, don't give the Directorate's rebuttal equal inches — they had eight years of inches and used every one of them to write *weather* over twenty-one names."

He wrote that down almost verbatim, which pleased her. Then he hesitated, pencil hovering, and asked the question he'd actually climbed aboard to ask.

"There's a rumor on the docks. About the sixth crate. The manifest lists six consignments; the purser's ledger reconciles five. There's a gap. A shipment that left the compartment but never appears inbound anywhere — not at the concession, not at any bonded warehouse on the coast. Forty cases of rifles, gone sideways. People are saying it went upriver past the falls, to somebody. Somebody with a name nobody will say out loud."

Delphine ladled him a bowl of stew and said nothing for a moment, letting the food do what food does, which is buy silence.

"I was a cook, Mr. Corrigan. I saw crates go in. Where they went out to is above my station, then and now."

"But you have a guess."

Everyone on this river had a guess. That was the trouble with rivers; they carried everything, including speculation, and deposited it downstream as fact. She gave him the only thing she actually owned: "I know the compartment was emptied twice that I saw. Once by working party, once by barge alongside at night, no lights, no paperwork, a barge flying no flag at all. A barge with no flag on this river isn't lost, Mr. Corrigan. It's owned."

He chewed that, literally and otherwise, and went off down the gangway into the dark with his notebook, and Delphine stood at the rail watching him go and feeling the shape of the next problem assemble itself in the fog ahead of her. Because tribunals convict the men you can catch. But a barge with no flag belongs to a man you cannot subpoena, and somewhere upriver past the falls, forty cases of rifles were sitting in whatever hands such barges serve, and those hands did not care about magistrates or muckrakers or cooks with good memories.

Ada came out and stood beside her, and they ate stew off the same pot lid, and Ada said, "You were different in there. In the chamber. You weren't performing."

"No. I was reporting for duty. Eleven years late."

"They believed you."

"They had no choice. That's the best kind of believing." Delphine scraped the lid. "Your father started this, you know. Eben Marsh, stealing a map off a navy clerk. A dying thief with a conscience. Everything today — the arrests, the headlines, the magistrate calling the Directorate's bluff — every thread of it runs back through that grease-stained paper to a man in a fever who made a stranger promise to find his daughter. Justice outside the law, vindicated inside it. That was the premise. We got the vindication. But the law only reaches as far as its writs, girl, and the writs stop at the falls."

Below them, the harbor lights came on one by one, and beyond the breakwater the river ran black toward the interior, carrying its unflagged cargoes to whoever paid for them.

Ivo appeared at the companionway, hair still wet from a bucket bath, and said the thing he'd been carrying since the afternoon session: "The tribunal wants us back tomorrow. All of us. They're calling the surviving *Vigilant* crew as a panel — and they've issued a summons for someone else." He paused, and his voice did something careful. "Ferro Lantz. As a material witness. The prosecution found his old ledgers — the ones he didn't burn. Turns out the ones he burned were the wrong ones. Or the right ones, depending on which side of the fire you stood."

Delphine looked at him. "Is he going to talk?"

"He's been talking since the cell. To his lawyer, to the prosecutor, to anyone with a pen. Ferro always pays his debts, Delphine. He's paying this one in testimony." Ivo's jaw tightened. "And the testimony goes past the *Vigilant*. Past the concession. Upriver. Past the falls."

Ada set down the pot lid. The clang of it hung in the salt air like a bell.

"Past the falls," she repeated softly. "That's where my father's camp was. That's where the journals came from. That's—" She stopped, and her eyes went wide and frightened and lit all at once. "That's where the sixth crate went. Isn't it. Whoever owns the barge with no flag — they're up there. In the country my father walked. In the country the map came from."

Delphine felt the deck shift under her feet, though the harbor was flat calm. Tomorrow the tribunal would sit again, and Ferro Lantz would take the stand in chains he'd forged himself, and whatever he said would point upriver like a compass needle — toward the falls, toward the unflagged barge, toward the one name nobody in Port Meridian would say out loud.

She picked up her knife roll — the new one, the one Ada had bought her at the market to replace the sack the supply officer had carried off eleven years ago — and began sharpening the long blade against the steel, slow strokes, the rhythm she used for thinking.

Because whatever came next, someone aboard the Magpie would need to eat, and someone upriver would need to be found, and Delphine Okafor had learned long ago that both jobs ran on the same edge.
