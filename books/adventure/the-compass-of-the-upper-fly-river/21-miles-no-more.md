## 21. Miles No More

The tribunal at Port Meridian sat in a converted customs house with a leaky roof and a ceiling fan that turned like it was apologizing for something. Ivo Okonkwo had spent three days in its public gallery watching men in white uniforms lie beautifully — the way they'd been trained to, the way he himself had once been trained to — and on the fourth day they stopped lying, because the manifest was read aloud into the record and the room changed temperature.

He didn't cheer when the arrests came. Neither did Ada, sitting beside him with her hands folded like a woman at church who had stopped believing in the sermon but not in the congregation. Delphine wept once, quietly, during her testimony about the cook's mess — eleven years of carrying that night alone, released in four minutes flat — and then dried her eyes and asked the stenographer whether the tribunal's kitchen needed help, because their coffee was criminal.

But it was the fifth day that Ivo dreaded, because on the fifth day they came for him.

Not with handcuffs. With an envelope.

* * *

The office belonged to Commodore Renn Vassek, or rather to what remained of his authority — a man two weeks from disgrace who still had enough paper left to sign one last thing. He looked smaller than Ivo remembered, though Ivo had only seen him twice before, both times across a table in a briefing room where the depth readings were already decided before the divers were briefed.

"Okonkwo." Vassek gestured at the chair. Ivo stayed standing. "You were dismissed under my authority. Incorrectly, as it turns out. The board has reviewed your refusal to falsify the survey data." He slid the envelope across the desk. "Your commission is restored. Full back pay. And there's a posting inside — hydrographic command, Port Meridian station. You'd be running your own dive teams within the year."

Ivo looked at the envelope without touching it. Through the window behind Vassek, he could see the harbor: the salvage fleet riding at anchor under new flags now, honest ones, their barges being inventoried by men with clipboards. And past them, tied up at the coal wharf where she always leaked a little, the *Magpie*.

"You know why I refused," Ivo said.

"I know you cost this service eleven years of truth by keeping your mouth shut about one survey."

"No." Ivo finally met his eyes. "I cost nothing. Your service did that all by itself. I just declined to hold the shovel."

Vassek's jaw worked. He was not, Ivo suspected, a corrupt man — merely a convenient one, which in the end amounted to the same wreckage with better manners. "You could do good work here," he said. "Real work. Clean water, honest charts. You'd never have to look at another classified hull again."

"That's exactly the problem, sir." Ivo picked up the envelope, weighed it once in his palm — the weight of a career, of a pension, of a life measured in miles traveled and depths logged and years accounted for in someone else's ledger — and set it back down on the desk between them. "Every mile I ever swam for the navy, someone else chose the destination. I'm done swimming other people's courses."

He turned for the door.

"Okonkwo." Vassek's voice caught him at the threshold. "The Marsh girl. The cook. The smuggler. You understand they'll hang everything on you now. Every failure, every scandal, every tide that goes wrong — a man without an institution behind him has no armor at all."

Ivo smiled for the first time in days. It felt strange on his face, like a knot untied after a long dive.

"With respect, Commodore — I've had armor my whole life. It never once kept me warm."

* * *

Ferro Lantz was waiting at the coal wharf, which meant Ferro Lantz wanted something, which meant Ferro Lantz was either broke or about to be generous, and with Ferro those were usually the same condition.

He sat on the *Magpie*'s gangplank like a vulture on a fence post, hat tipped forward, ledger — a new one, Ivo noticed; the old ones had gone into a stove upriver — balanced on his knee. Behind him the riverboat slouched against the pilings, her paddle wheel dripping, her paint peeling in long gray ribbons that made her look like something surfacing from a very long sleep.

"Heard you turned down a commission," Ferro said, without looking up. "Whole harbor's talking about it. Man walks away from a uniform and a pension in the same afternoon. They're calling you either a saint or a fool, and this town charges different rates for each."

"What do they charge for a smuggler?"

"Depreciated rates. Nobody expects me to appreciate anymore." Ferro closed the ledger and patted it. "Which brings us to business. You've been chartering my boat on a handshake and a promissory note for three weeks now. The tribunal's settled. The fleet's impounded. There's no more emergency, Okonkwo — which means there's no more reason for me to keep pretending I don't want my boat back."

Ivo studied him. Ferro Lantz had burned his own ledgers to buy them six hours' warning, had stood in front of Commander Hale's skiff and lied with the fluency of a man spending his last coin, and had then vanished upriver for two weeks like a man avoiding his own creditors — which, Ivo gathered, he had been, in every sense that mattered.

"How much?" Ivo said.

"For the boat? She's worth maybe four hundred in honest money, and we both know she isn't honest money." Ferro's eyes came up at last, sharp as fishhooks. "But you're not buying a boat. You're buying what she carried. Six tides, one manifest, one dead navy officer pulled out of a sealed compartment, and one family that shouldn't have worked and did. That's worth more than four hundred."

"So name it."

Ferro stood, slow, joints cracking like the gangplank timbers. He walked to the rail and spat into the brown water, watching the current take it.

"Three thousand," he said. "And one thing that isn't money."

"Ferro—"

"A berth." He said it fast, before pride could catch him. "Cook's mate. Galley hand. Whatever Okafor needs. I can navigate, I can forge anything short of a face, and I can smell a patrol boat at two miles in fog. I'm tired of owning things, Okonkwo. Owning things is how a man ends up burning them." He turned around, and for one unguarded second Ivo saw what lived under the smuggler's arithmetic: a man who had counted everything his whole life and arrived at zero. "I want to be somewhere the counting stops."

Ivo thought about Eben Marsh, dying on the planks of his shack, trading a grease-stained map for a promise. About how every important transaction in his life had been conducted in currency no bank would recognize.

"Two thousand five hundred," Ivo said, "and you sign articles like everyone else. No owner's cabin. No special terms. Partners, or nothing."

Ferro stared at him. Then he laughed — a real laugh, rusty, startled out of him like a bird from cover. "Partners. With a disgraced diver, a court-martialed cook, a nurse who talks her way past gunboats, and whatever else this floating madhouse collects." He stuck out his hand. "Done. God help us all."

* * *

That evening Ivo laid the articles out on the *Magpie*'s saloon table — real paper, bought from the tribunal's own stationer, because if a man was going to build something legal for once he might as well use good stock.

Delphine read hers twice, front and back, the way she read everything, then signed with the fountain pen she'd taken from the customs house as a souvenir of justice. Her line went down firm and slightly left-leaning, like a woman leaning into weather.

Ada read hers slowly, tracing each clause with one finger. When she reached the part about shares — equal shares, Ivo had insisted, over Ferro's spluttering objections — she looked up.

"You wrote 'family' in the margin," she said.

"It's not a legal term."

"No. It isn't." She signed beneath her father's name, which Ivo had entered first, in the space reserved for the man whose map had started everything. Eben Marsh, deceased. Partner, posthumous. His share held in trust for the finding of lost things. "He'd have hated this," Ada said quietly. "He'd have hated it and then he'd have signed it faster than any of us."

Ferro signed last, with a flourish that took up half the page, and then immediately tried to negotiate his own signature down to something less conspicuous, and failed, and sulked about it while Delphine brought out dinner — river fish in a sauce she refused to name, rice cooked properly for the first time in the *Magpie*'s rotten history, and a bottle of rum that was, for once, not cheap.

They ate in the saloon with the lamps lit and the river sliding black and patient past the windows. Nobody toasted. It wasn't that kind of crew. Instead Delphine raised her glass an inch off the table and said, "To the ones who didn't leave," and they drank to that, and it covered everybody: the brother at the bottom of the sea, the prospector in the grave upriver, the unnamed officer in the sealed compartment, the eleven years, the six tides, all of it.

Later, when the others had gone ashore or below, Ivo climbed to the wheelhouse and stood at the wheel he now owned outright, and did the accounting he had done his whole life.

Miles traveled: uncounted. Depths logged: forgotten. Years lived: thirty-eight, though some of them barely qualified.

Then he tried the new arithmetic. One cook who sharpened knives when she was thinking and fed strangers like it was a sacrament. One nurse who had come aboard hating her father's ghost and ended up carrying it gently, like ballast that finally made sense. One smuggler learning to sleep without counting exits. One dead man's promise, honored. One manifest, delivered. One boat, leaking, ugly, his.

Seven souls aboard, counting the dead, counting himself.

It was the first number in his life that had ever felt like enough.

Below him, the river ran north toward the upper reaches, toward the country past Kolo Bend where the charts went thin and the mission schools gave way to mist, and where — Delphine had mentioned it over dinner, casually, the way she mentioned everything that mattered — there were reports of boats going missing, and families asking anyone with a fast hull and a reputation for finding things whether they ran charters.

Ivo put his hands on the wheel.

"Magpie," he said to the dark boat beneath his feet, and felt her answer in the creak of her timbers, "let's go find somebody."

He rang the engine bell once, soft, and the river took them.
