## 22. The Compass of the Upper Fly River

The Magpie came upriver against the current the way she did everything now — stubbornly, noisily, and with more heart than sense.

Ada Marsh stood at the rail near the bow and watched the Upper Fly unspool ahead of them in the early light, brown-gold and patient, the same water that had nearly killed all of them a year ago and now carried them like it had forgotten the whole affair. The paddle wheel turned behind her with its familiar lopsided rhythm — *thump, splash, thump* — a heartbeat Delphine kept meaning to fix and Ivo kept refusing to let anyone touch. "That's her pulse," he'd said once, hand flat on the deckhouse wall as if taking a reading. "You don't correct a pulse."

She'd learned to read the river's moods from him. The glassy stretch below Kolo Bend meant rain two days out. The smell of crushed gingerweed on the wind meant the mission school was close, which meant children, which meant Delphine would be baking before they docked whether anyone asked or not. And the particular quality of light this morning — low, amber, catching the mist where the valley narrowed — meant they were an hour from Eben's grave.

One year. She still counted in tides sometimes. Old habits. But mostly now she counted in trips: forty-one charters since the tribunal, nine rescues, one birth aboard (a fisherman's wife, delivered by lamplight in the galley while Delphine boiled water she didn't need and told everyone stories to keep them calm), and one dog, retrieved off a drifting skiff, who had appointed himself captain and answered to nothing but the name Biscuit.

"Ada." Ivo's voice from the pilothouse, pitched just loud enough to carry over the wheel. He leaned out the starboard window, one hand on the brass rail, his face half-shadow under the canvas awning. "Current's running heavy past the bend. You want to make your stop at the point, we'll need to come around wide."

"I know." She smiled at him. "I've made this stop eleven times."

"And I've come around wide eleven times." He touched two fingers to his cap — the old navy cap, minus everything that had made it a navy cap except the shape — and disappeared back inside. Through the open window she heard him murmur to the wheel, to the engine telegraph, to the boat herself, the way he talked to the Magpie like a woman he was still learning. Maybe he was. Maybe they both were.

Delphine emerged from the galley hatch wiping her hands on a towel, flour to her elbows, smelling of cinnamon and woodsmoke. She planted herself beside Ada at the rail and followed her gaze upriver to where a stand of ironwood trees marked the point above the little cemetery the mission kept for people the river returned and people it didn't.

"You've got the letter," Delphine said. Not a question.

Ada touched her breast pocket. The paper there had gone soft as cloth from handling. "And the map."

"You're really leaving the map?"

"It was never mine. It was never even his, not truly — he stole it fair and square from a navy clerk, which makes it the most honest thing anyone ever stole." She watched the ironwoods slide closer. "It belongs in the ground with him. He drew his whole life toward those coordinates. Let him have them back."

Delphine was quiet a moment. Then: "Your father would have liked you, you know. If he'd ever gotten the chance to actually meet you instead of writing letters at you from a hundred miles away."

"He met me every time he wrote." Ada heard the defensiveness in her own voice and let it soften. "I'm only sorry it took me so long to read him properly."

"That's not sorry. That's inheritance." Delphine squeezed her shoulder, leaving a faint print of flour on her sleeve, and went below to rescue whatever she had in the oven.

The Magpie swung wide at the bend, her wheel biting, and came alongside the mission landing with a grace nobody would have predicted from her hull lines. Children were already running down the bank — word traveled faster than boats on this river — and Biscuit leapt the gap to the dock a full three seconds before the mooring line was thrown, barking his official welcome. Sister Agnes appeared in her grey habit, hands folded, wearing the expression of a woman who had been informed of their arrival by God or by the cook's boy, whichever came first.

"We'll take water and firewood," Ivo called down from the pilothouse wing. "And whatever Delphine trades for. She does the arithmetic."

"She always has," Ada murmured, and took the gangway plank at a walk, because running felt wrong today.

* * *

Eben Marsh lay under an ironwood tree on the ridge above the mission, in a plot Sister Agnes's girls kept swept clean, with a headstone the stonemason at Port Meridian had cut for free after the tribunal made the papers. The lettering was simple:

EBEN MARSH
PROSPECTOR. WITNESS.
HE TOLD THE TRUTH AND THE RIVER KEPT IT UNTIL IT WAS TIME.

Ada knelt in the grass with her skirt pooled around her knees and set her palm flat on the warm stone. Below, the Magpie sat at her moorings with smoke curling from her stack, and beyond her the river ran silver and endless, going about its business.

"I brought him home," she said quietly. It was how she always began. "He's steering us upriver this morning. He takes the bends too fast and argues with Ivo about it."

She took out the map first.

It had changed in a year of being carried — softer, more creased, the grease stains bloomed into continents of their own. But the lines were still sure. Eben had drawn those coordinates with a dying man's last steadiness, and they had held. They had led a fired diver, a disgraced cook, a nurse who wanted nothing to do with any of it, and a smuggler with a price on everything down into black water and back out again with the truth in a waterproof tube. She smoothed it on her knee one last time, tracing the little X he'd inked inside the wreck's outline — not treasure, never treasure, just a sealed compartment and a dead man in a navy uniform who deserved a name on a stone somewhere too.

"You were right," she told the stone. "About all of it. Even about me. You wrote that I'd know what to do when it mattered. I was so angry when I read that the first time — sitting in your camp with your journals, thinking, *you don't know me, you left.*" Her throat tightened. "But you did know me. That was the terrible thing about you, Papa. You knew all of us better than we let ourselves be known."

She folded the map along its old creases, careful, reverent, and worked it into the soft earth at the base of the stone, pressing it down with both hands until the ground held it. Then she covered it with a flat stone, the way prospectors marked a claim. *Claim staked,* she thought. *Paid in full.*

Then the letter.

She had read it aloud here eleven times. She would read it eleven hundred if the river allowed. She unfolded the thin paper, softened at the folds to the texture of moth wing, and began.

*"My Ada —*

*If you are reading this, then either I am dead or you have finally come looking for me, and knowing you it is both.*

*I have done things I am not proud of and one thing I am entirely proud of, and the one thing is twenty-six years old and stubborn and reads faster than I can lie. I stole a map because I saw men steal a ship — our ship, the country's ship — and sink it with a man locked inside, and everyone who could have said something chose instead to say nothing, loudly, in committee. I could not carry what I knew and stay respectable, so I chose to carry what I knew.*

*I memorized the manifest's location because paper can be taken from a man and memory cannot, though I am told by the doctor that mine is going, so I have drawn it besides, and if my drawing is bad it is because my hands shake and not because my mind is gone, whatever Ferro Lantz says.*

*Find the compartment. Find the manifest. Give it to someone who cannot be bought — I know that is a short list; start with the newspapers, they are cheap and hungry. And then, Ada, when it is done, when the men who did this are standing in the light where they belong — go home. Whatever home turns out to be. I spent my whole life moving because staying felt like dying, and I was wrong. Staying is the bravest thing. Staying is the whole trick.*

*Do not grieve me long. Grieve the man in the hold instead; he had less warning than I did.*

*I loved you across every mile I ever put between us. This is the compass I leave you: it points at people, not north. Follow it and you will never be lost, even on the river, even in the dark, even at the end.*

*Your father,
Eben"*

Her voice held until the last line. It always held until the last line, and then it didn't, and she let it not, kneeling in the grass with the wind moving through the ironwood leaves above her like slow applause.

When she looked up, Ivo was standing ten feet away at the edge of the plot, cap in his hands, having climbed the path quiet as a man approaching a sleeping animal. He didn't apologize for coming. A year ago he might have. Now he simply waited until she wiped her eyes and nodded, and then he came and crouched beside her and put his hand flat on the headstone the way he put his hand on the Magpie's deckhouse — taking a reading.

"He argued with me about the bends?" Ivo said.

"Constantly. You yield to him on the hairpin above the falls."

"I don't yield. I defer. There's a difference, and it's the difference between a crew and a family." He was quiet a moment, thumb moving over the stone's carved letters. "Eleven times up this path. You know what I was doing a year ago today?"

"Falsifying depth readings, or refusing to."

"Refusing to. Quietly. Which is the same as loudly, if you're the one getting dismissed." He looked out at the river. "I thought that was the end of my life. Standing in that office with my kit bag. Turns out it was the beginning, and I was too stupid to see it because I was counting miles."

"And now?"

"Now I count people." He said it plainly, without ceremony, the way he said true things. "There's four of us regulars, one dog with delusions of command, a rotating cast of Delphine's nieces and nephews, and a fisherman at Wabu Bend who owes us his boat and thinks that means he owes us his soul. It comes to more than I ever imagined owning."

Ada laughed, wet-eyed. "You own a boat, Ivo Okonkwo. Free and clear, papers signed."

"The boat is how the people get places. The boat is incidental."

"Don't let the Magpie hear you say that."

From below, faintly, the paddle wheel gave its lopsided *thump*, as if in agreement.

They stayed a while longer. Then Ivo rose, settled his cap, and offered her his hand, and she took it, and they walked down together through the ironwoods to where their family was waiting.

* * *

Delphine had laid out lunch on the aft deck — river fish in ginger and lime, fried plantain, the cinnamon rolls she baked whenever the mission was involved because Sister Agnes had once declared them "an argument for providence." Biscuit supervised from the pilothouse roof with the gravity of an admiral reviewing a fleet.

They ate slowly, unhurried, because the charter wasn't due at Wabu Bend until tomorrow and the river was theirs until then. Delphine told the story of the birth aboard again, improving it in the telling — there were now, apparently, three lanterns and a thunderstorm. Ivo corrected the details without looking up from his plate. Ada let them have it. Some stories belonged to the teller, and some truths were better served slightly cooked.

"So," Delphine said eventually, leaning back against the deckhouse with her plate balanced on her knees. "Articles renew next month. Partners' meeting. I move we vote ourselves a raise."

"You can't vote yourself a raise," Ivo said. "That's not how articles work."

"It's exactly how articles work when we're the entire company."

"I'll allow a discussion."

"A discussion," Delphine repeated, delighted. "Hear that, Ada? Democracy. We're rich in democracy."

Ada was laughing when she saw it — the way Ivo's gaze drifted past her shoulder to the north, where the valley opened, and stayed there. She turned.

Smoke. Not the grey smudge of a cooking fire but a column, straight and dark, rising from the tree line maybe six miles upriver, past the narrows where the current ran hardest. And beneath it, almost lost under the Magpie's own wheel-beat, a sound she'd learned to dread and never forgotten: a whistle. Three short blasts. Distress.

Biscuit was already barking.

Ivo was already moving — through the pilothouse door, hand on the telegraph, voice level and carrying the way it went level when the water got serious. "Delphine, secure the deck. Ada, glasses, tell me what you see. We're coming about."

The Magpie leaned into her turn, wheel biting, pulse quickening, and Ada braced at the rail with the binoculars and found the source: a trading canoe, swamped, three figures clinging to its hull in the current, and behind them on the bank — she adjusted focus, breath catching — a fourth figure, small, waving something white from the rocks at the mouth of the narrows.

"Three in the water off the port bow, one signaling from shore," she called. "They're drifting toward the falls channel."

"Then we don't drift with them." Ivo's hands moved on the wheel, sure and unhurried, and the compass on the binnacle before him swung and settled — not pointing north, never north, pointing at the people the way Eben had promised it would. "Hold on, all of you. Magpie's going to work."

The wheel beat quickened. The river opened ahead, gold and green and dangerous, and Ada stood at the rail with her father's letter warm against her heart and her family moving around her like one body, and the Magpie ran hard toward the smoke, toward the whistle, toward whatever the water was asking of them next.

Home, she thought, was not a place on any map. Home was this: a crooked boat, a steady hand on the wheel, and everyone aboard leaning the same direction when the river called.

She tied her hair back and went to ready the rescue lines.
