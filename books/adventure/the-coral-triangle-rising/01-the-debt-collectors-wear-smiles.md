## 1. The Debt Collectors Wear Smiles

The storm came in off the Coral Triangle like it had a grudge, and Ansel Montrose loved it the way only a broke man can love anything.

He stood on the deck of the *Salty Ledger* — a salvage rig held together by welds, prayer, and a loan he'd rather not think about — and watched the wall of black water build on the horizon. The barometer had been falling all morning, faster than any sane sailor would like. Perfect. Sane sailors didn't make money. Sane sailors went home to dry bunks and full accounts, and Ansel hadn't been either of those things in two years.

"Barometer's at twenty-eight and change," said Dov, his one crewman, a wiry ex-fisherman with a face like weathered rope. "That's not a storm, boss. That's an argument between gods."

"That's cargo," Ansel said. "That's a freighter somewhere out there losing containers over the rail, and every one of those boxes is worth more than my life insurance policy." He grinned, because grinning was free and everything else wasn't. "Trim the sails. We ride the edge."

"The edge of what?"

"Of disaster, obviously. It's where the money lives."

Dov muttered something that was probably a prayer to three different sea-gods, none of them friendly, and went to work.

Ansel turned back to the storm and let himself feel it — that electric lift in the chest, the old familiar hunger. Chasing wrecks was a young man's game and he was thirty-four, but desperation kept a man young the way nothing else did. He owed Veyra Solenne eleven thousand crowns. He owed her the *Ledger*, technically, though she'd never once mentioned taking it. That was the thing about Veyra: she never took. She just waited, smiling, while you discovered all the ways you could still lose.

Eleven thousand crowns. One good haul off this storm — a container of machine parts, a sealed crate of spirits, honestly at this point he'd take a floating pallet of tinned fish — and he could push her off another season.

The first rain hit like thrown gravel.

* * *

They found the freighter's wake at dusk, or what was left of it. A scatter of debris fanned across the swells: splintered decking, a life ring spinning lazily, and — Ansel's heart actually stuttered — a forty-foot container riding half-submerged, its orange paint barely visible under the churn.

"There," he breathed. "Dov. There."

"I see it." Dov didn't sound happy. Dov never sounded happy when there was money involved, which Ansel had long ago decided was a character flaw. "Boss, look at the swell direction. That box is drifting toward the shoals. We hook it now or we don't hook it at all."

"We hook it now." Ansel was already moving, hands sure on the winch controls, the rigging groaning as they came about. The *Ledger* leaned into the turn and the sea leaned back, and for about four minutes everything worked exactly the way it was supposed to. Grappling line out. Hook set. Winch engaged. The container swung toward them through the gray water like a fat orange fish on a line.

Ansel allowed himself, for exactly four minutes, to imagine Veyra Solenne's smile faltering. Just once. Just enough.

Then the wave came.

Not the big one — the big ones you saw coming, you braced, you cursed, you survived. This was the small one, the sneaky one, the one that lifted the stern at the wrong angle at the wrong moment while the winch pulled the wrong way, and the whole physics of his bad decisions resolved themselves in a single sickening lurch. The cable snapped with a sound like a rifle shot. The container sank. And the *Ledger*, freed of the drag, slewed broadside into the following sea and shipped about two tons of it over the rail.

"Engine!" Ansel screamed, and got no answer, because the engine had already drowned.

* * *

They washed up on the shingle beach below Cutter's Point at nearly midnight, half-drowned, the *Ledger* aground behind them with her hull opened like a gutted fish. Ansel sat in the wet sand and watched the surf pound her, and felt something in his chest go very quiet and very cold.

Dov stood beside him, dripping. "She's done," Dov said. Not cruelly. Just factually, the way Dov said everything.

"She's collateral," Ansel said.

"Yeah." Dov shifted. "About that. I'm going to say something, and you're not going to hit me."

"You're the only crew I can afford. I couldn't replace you."

"I know. I'm saying it anyway." Dov looked down at him. "You've been chasing storms for two years trying to outrun that woman's ledger, and every year the hole gets deeper. You don't have a debt problem, Ansel. You have a math problem. And math doesn't care how good your nerve is."

Ansel laughed. It came out wrong, cracked in the middle. "What would you have me do? Walk into her office and apologize?"

"No," Dov said. "I'd have you not walk into her office at all. But we both know you will."

* * *

Veyra Solenne's office sat above the chandlery in Port Meridian, and it was the cheeriest room Ansel had ever been humiliated in. Sun-yellow walls. Potted ferns. A jar of candied ginger on the desk, from which she offered him a piece before he'd even sat down, as if he were a favored nephew instead of a drowning man.

"Ansel!" She beamed at him. Veyra was perhaps fifty, round-faced, silver-haired, dressed in colors that suggested a woman who had never once worried about anything. Her reputation said otherwise. Her reputation said she'd smiled at three captains right before she owned their ships, their houses, and in one memorable case, their parrots. "Sit, sit. You look terrible. Storm-chasing agrees with you so badly, I sometimes wonder why you do it."

"Because it pays," Ansel said.

"And yet." She spread her hands, delighted, as if he'd made a wonderful joke. "Tell me about the storm."

So he told her. He gave her the whole account straight, because lying to Veyra was like lying to a mirror — she already knew what you looked like. The freighter debris. The container. The snapped cable. The *Ledger* on the rocks with her belly torn open.

When he finished, she nodded slowly, and her expression was pure sympathy, warm as sunlight through glass.

"Well," she said. "Isn't that something."

"Veyra—"

"Eleven thousand crowns," she said, tapping the ledger open on her desk. Her finger traced the column of numbers with genuine tenderness, the way another woman might touch a photograph of a grandchild. "Plus interest accrued, plus the handling fees we discussed, which brings us to — let me see — twelve thousand, six hundred, and forty. Give or take a crown. Isn't arithmetic marvelous? It never gets seasick. It never loses a container to the shoals. It just sits there, being true."

"I need time."

"Mm." She leaned back and studied him, and for a moment — just a moment — the smile thinned into something older and sharper underneath, something patient as stone. Then it bloomed back, fuller than ever. "Here's what I love about you, Ansel Montrose. You always ask for time like it's a thing I keep in a drawer. Like I could hand you a week the way I'd hand you a ginger candy." She pushed the jar toward him again. He didn't take one. "But you know what? I'm feeling generous. The season's turning. Business has been good. So here is my offer, and I want you to hear how reasonable it is."

He heard it. He heard how reasonable it was. That was the horror of Veyra — every term she offered was reasonable, every rate defensible, every deadline generous by the standards of people who broke fingers. She simply arranged matters so that reasonable terms and survival could not occupy the same room.

"One week," she said. "Twelve thousand six hundred and forty crowns, in my hand, seven days from today. Or the *Ledger* comes to me — hull, rig, and the mooring rights, which I'll sell within the month — and whatever balance remains after that, you'll owe me personally. Working it off, if necessary. I always have work for strong men with good nerve." She beamed. "Doesn't that sound fair?"

It sounded fair. That was the trap of it. It sounded fair the way a noose sounds fair if you've never seen anyone hang.

"One week," Ansel said.

"One week." She clapped her hands together softly, thrilled, and stood to walk him to the door, pressing a twist of paper — candied ginger, wrapped for the road — into his palm. "Oh, and Ansel? One more thing, since you'll be thinking clearly now." At the door she paused, and her voice dropped into something almost kind. "There's been talk along the coast. Old talk. About the ebb tide coming, the big one, the one that happens maybe once in a generation. Fishermen are saying the water's already pulling back strange near the Point. Saying there's a cave mouth showing that nobody names." Her eyes glittered. "Now, I don't put stock in stories. But a man in your position might. Desperate men find doors that sensible men walk past."

"Why are you telling me this?"

She smiled her widest smile yet, and it was genuinely, terrifyingly warm.

"Because I want you to succeed, Ansel. I believe in you. That's what everyone gets wrong about me — I'm not a threat. I'm an optimist." She patted his cheek with one soft hand. "One week. Don't disappoint me. Disappointment makes me creative, and neither of us wants that."

* * *

He walked the sea wall until midnight, turning the twist of ginger over in his hand without opening it. Twelve thousand six hundred and forty crowns. There wasn't a legal way to raise it in a week. There wasn't even an illegal way, not on short notice, not with his name already known in every port from here to the Spur.

And underneath the panic, quieter, Veyra's words kept circling: *the water's pulling back strange near the Point.*

He stopped at the rail and looked north, toward Cutter's Point, where the *Ledger* lay broken in the dark. The moon was doing something odd to the water — or rather, the water was doing something odd to the moon, the reflection stretched thin and broken, as if the sea itself were flinching away from the land.

Ansel had grown up on these coasts. He knew what an ebb tide looked like.

This wasn't one. This was the ocean holding its breath.

He thought about Dov's voice in the sand: *you have a math problem.* He thought about Veyra's finger tracing the column of numbers, tender as a lover. He thought about the cave mouth nobody would name, sitting exposed in the retreating water like a door someone had left open on purpose.

A sensible man would sleep on it. A sensible man would sell what he had, take Veyra's work terms, and spend ten years paying down a debt at a rate designed to never end.

Ansel Montrose had never once been accused of being sensible.

He turned north along the sea wall and started walking, and behind him the tide kept falling, and falling, and falling — farther than any tide he had ever seen, as if the whole Coral Triangle were drawing back to show him something.

As if it had been waiting for him specifically.
