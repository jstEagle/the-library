## 2. What the Tide Gave Back

The tide went out like a held breath finally released.

Ansel had chased storms for eleven years. He knew the sea's moods the way other men knew their mothers' faces — the green-black swell that meant a squall, the long glassy swells that lied, the way the water piled up against the sea wall before a typhoon came ashore. He had never seen the ocean leave.

But it was leaving. He stood on the harbor road at Saltmere Reach and watched it go, the shallows draining off the reef flats in silver sheets, fish flapping in pools that shrank even as he watched, boats in the anchorage settling into the mud with creaks of protest. The horizon of water pulled back and back, a kilometer, two, exposing a seabed no living fisherman had seen — black rock, drowned pilings from some settlement older than Saltmere, the ribs of a ship that had died so long ago its wood had turned to stone.

People came out of their houses to stare. He heard a woman begin to weep. He heard an old man say, quietly, "Not again. Not in my lifetime, I said."

Ansel didn't stop to ask what again meant. His boots were already on the wet sand.

The ebb had a direction. That was the wrong thing about it, the thing that should have stopped him — tides don't have directions, they have rhythms, in and out, the sea breathing on schedule. This was different. This was the water drawing back from one specific point on the coast like a crowd parting around a man with a knife. North. Past the fish-drying racks, past the last of the houses, to the cliffs where the mountains began their climb out of the sea.

Where the locals refused to point.

He'd asked about it his first week in Saltmere, back when he still thought charm was a form of currency that worked everywhere. *What's through the mountains? What's the fastest way over?* And the harbormaster, a woman with salt-cured hands and eyes that had seen too much weather, had gone carefully blank and said, "There's no way over. You sail around, or you stay."

"There's always a way over. Mountains are just geography."

"There's a cave," the harbormaster had said, and then had looked at him the way you'd look at a man about to put his hand in a fire, and had said nothing else, ever again, no matter how he asked.

Now the sea had peeled itself off that cliff face, and Ansel stood in front of the cave mouth, and he understood the silence.

It wasn't a hole. It was an architecture. The opening was tall as a cathedral door, its edges carved in spirals that no chisel had made — spirals that looked, he realized with a slow crawl up his spine, like tide-marks. Like the rings the sea leaves on stone, except these were perfect, concentric, deliberate, and they glowed. Faintly. A blue-green phosphorescence, the color of the water at night when the plankton bloomed, pulsing in a rhythm he was almost sure matched his own heartbeat.

The locals had followed him at a distance. He could feel them at his back, a hundred people standing on the sea wall, and not one of them calling his name. Not one of them saying *don't*. That was the loudest warning he'd ever been given.

He should have turned around.

He thought about Veyra Solenne instead. He thought about her smile, which was the worst thing about her — not the interest rate, not the men she kept on retainer, but the smile, wide and warm and utterly sincere, the smile of a woman who genuinely, deeply believed everything would work out for everyone, and who had never once in her life been the one it didn't work out for. *One week, Ansel. Seven little days. I have such faith in you.* She'd said it like a benediction. He'd walked out of her office feeling like a man reprieved and damned in the same breath.

Six days left. His rig was at the bottom of the strait. His salvage gear with it. His name was on a debt ledger that, he suspected, had a page all to itself.

And here, glowing in the cliff like an open door, was a way over the mountains. Ahead of schedule. Ahead of the snows. On the far side of the mountains were the deepwater ports, the salvage contracts, the kind of money you made in a season instead of a decade. He could be through and back with a crew before Veyra's week was half spent.

He knew what the harbormaster's silence meant. He knew what the weeping woman meant. He knew that no legend survives centuries by being kind.

He stepped forward anyway, and the tide-marks brightened as he approached, and that — that was the moment he should have run, because the cave was *noticing* him — but hope is a tide of its own, and it had gone out of him so long ago that when it came rushing back it took all his judgment with it.

The air inside was warm. That was the second wrong thing. Sea caves breathe cold; this one breathed like a living room, like somewhere expecting a guest. The floor was smooth stone, dry despite the ocean at its threshold, and the walls rose in that same spiral carving, ring over ring, and as his eyes adjusted he saw that the rings were moving. Slowly. Rearranging themselves. Tide-marks rewriting their own history.

"Hello?" His voice came back to him wrong — flattened, close, like the cave was holding it near.

*You are owed,* said a voice.

Not from the walls. From everywhere. From the stone itself, or from under it, or from the same place the glow came from — a voice like water finding its level, unhurried, enormous, and not unkind, which somehow made it worse.

"I'm owed?" Ansel laughed, and the laugh died fast in the warm air. "Lady, you've got the wrong man. I'm the one who owes."

*The sea keeps its own ledger,* the voice said. *It knows the difference between a debt and a debt. You owe gold. You are owed passage.*

"Passage." He said it carefully, the way you'd test ice. "Over the mountains."

*Over. Under. Through. The mountain is a formality.* The glow pulsed, and he saw it now for what it was — the tide-marks were words, or had been words once, or were words in a language that predated language, and they were rearranging themselves around him, spiraling inward, converging on the spot where he stood. *The snows come early this year. The passes close in nine days. Men have died waiting for spring. You would not wait. You cannot wait. We can carry you.*

"And the price."

The cave was quiet for a moment. He had the sense — absurd, undeniable — that it was pleased he'd asked.

*The tide gives,* it said. *The tide takes. That is the whole of the law.*

"Which means what, exactly? What do you take?"

*What you can spare least,* said the voice, gently. *That is how the tide works. It takes what the shore loves best.*

Ansel stood in the warm dark with the glowing rings closing around his boots, and every instinct he owned — the ones that had kept him alive through eleven years of storms, the ones that told him when a swell was lying, when a rig was overloaded, when a deal was too good — every one of them was screaming.

He thought about Veyra's smile. He thought about ten years of her work terms, her ledgers, her cheerful, bottomless optimism. He thought about the difference between owing a woman money and owing a cave memory — and the honest, ugly truth was that the cave scared him less, because at least the cave wasn't smiling.

"Nine days," he said. "You can get me through in nine days?"

*Three,* said the cave.

He put his hand on the wall. The stone was warm as skin. The nearest tide-mark pulsed under his palm, once, like a pulse.

"Deal," said Ansel Montrose, and the word had barely left his mouth before the rings of light surged up his arm like water finding a new shoreline, and somewhere far behind him, out in the ordinary world, the tide turned and began to come back in — and the entrance he'd walked through was no longer an entrance at all, but a wall of glowing stone, seamless, patient, already rewriting itself around the shape of a man who had just promised it everything he couldn't spare.

The cave began to move. Or he did. It was impossible to tell the difference, and getting harder to care.

The last thing he thought, before the warm dark took the shape of a road and started carrying him up into the mountain, was that he couldn't remember — suddenly, absurdly — whether his mother's eyes had been brown or gray.

He decided it didn't matter. He decided a lot of things didn't matter.

That was the first payment, though he wouldn't understand it until later, walking a trail he had no memory of entering, toward a woman sketching in the dirt who would look up at him and ask how he'd possibly gotten this far.

The tide, after all, had only just begun to take.
