## 3. The First Payment

The cave did not feel like walking into a place. It felt like walking into a decision he'd already made.

Ansel kept one hand on the wall because the dark was total and the floor was slick with something that wasn't quite water. The tide-marks glowed ahead of him in slow pulses — pale rings on the stone, like the growth lines inside a shell — and each time they brightened, they had moved. Not far. A hand's width, maybe less. But they were rearranging themselves around his footsteps the way water rearranges around a hull, and that was the moment the recklessness curdled into something colder.

*You can still turn back,* he told himself. *Veyra Solenne gives you a week.*

He laughed at that, alone, in the dark, and the laugh came out wrong — too high, too thin. Veyra Solenne didn't give anyone anything. She offered it, smiling, with both hands open like she was handing you fruit, and then you owed her, and owing her was a kind of drowning that happened on dry land.

The voice had come from everywhere when he first stepped inside. It hadn't been loud. It had been patient, which was worse.

*Passage over the mountains. Ahead of the snows. Ahead of your week.*

*What does it cost?*

*A fair price. Paid as you go.*

He should have asked what "fair" meant. He'd been in the Coral Triangle long enough to know that nobody who said *fair price* had ever meant it. But the storm season was coming early — every gull and fisherman and dockside drunk had said so — and the pass would close, and closed passes meant another month of Veyra's cheerful arithmetic compounding against him like rot in a hull.

So he'd walked in. Reckless hope. His oldest crewmate, and the one who always abandoned ship first.

The tunnel bent left, then right, then opened into a chamber where the ceiling vanished upward into blackness and the tide-marks ran across the floor in wide silver bands, dozens of them, layered like the rings of a felled tree. Ansel stopped at the edge of them. His boots were wet. He couldn't remember wading.

*One mile,* the voice said, gentle as a harbor pilot. *First payment due.*

"I haven't agreed to—"

*You walked in,* it said. *That is the agreement. It has always been the agreement.*

And then it took something.

He didn't see it go. That was the terrible part — there was no sensation of loss, no tearing, no cold spot where something used to be. There was just a moment when he thought about his mother, the way he did most mornings without deciding to, and found that the thought had a hole in the middle of it.

He could remember her hands. Flour on her knuckles, the burn scar shaped like a comma on her wrist. He could remember her voice saying his name, the particular way she made it two syllables when she was worried and one flat syllable when he was in trouble. He could remember her kitchen, the crooked shutter, the smell of salt and bread.

But her face —

He reached for it and it wasn't there. Not blurred. Not faded. *Absent*, the way a word sits on the tip of your tongue and won't come, except this was the whole architecture of her, cheekbones and eyes and the way her mouth went crooked when she lied about being fine, and it was simply gone, scooped out clean, and the edges of the absence were smooth as if it had never been filled.

"No," Ansel said. His voice bounced off the black ceiling. "No, no, no — give it back."

*Paid for one mile,* the voice said, unbothered, unhurried, the tone of a toll-keeper counting coins. *The road continues. So does the price.*

"That was my mother." He was shouting now, and the chamber ate the sound and gave nothing back. "You can't just — I want it back. Take the rig. Take my hands. Take—"

*It is taken,* the voice said. *Grief is permitted. Bargaining is not.*

And the tide-marks pulsed once, all together, like a slow blink, and the far wall of the chamber — which had certainly been wall — was now an opening onto gray daylight and wind, and Ansel understood with a lurch that he had no idea how much time had passed. Minutes. Hours. The light looked like late afternoon. He had entered the cave in the morning.

His legs carried him forward because standing still in a place that took pieces of you seemed strictly worse than moving.

* * *

The mountain trail hit him like a slap of ordinary reality — scree underfoot, wind smelling of pine and coming snow, sky the color of dishwater. Ansel stood at the mouth of a crack in the rock that, looking back, showed only stone. No cave. No glow. Just a seam in the mountainside, narrow enough that he might have dreamed it, if not for the smooth-edged hole where his mother's face had been.

He sat down hard on a boulder and tried to think.

Think. That was the thing he was good at. Storms rewarded clear thinking; panic got people killed, and Ansel Montrose had chased weather for eleven years without dying, which in his line of work counted as a pension plan. So: inventory. Name, age, trade. Debts — to whom? Veyra Solenne, yes, the name came with a smile attached to it, a smile like a hook. How much? He reached for the number and found it, but the number floated free of any reason. Why had he borrowed? What had he needed so badly that he'd signed himself over to the most dangerous optimist on the coast?

Nothing came. Not just no answer — no *shape* of an answer. It was like reaching into a drawer and finding the drawer had never existed.

And his mother. He knew he loved her. The love was intact, huge, sitting in his chest like ballast. He knew she had raised him, that she was — was she alive? He searched for her funeral and found nothing, searched for her last letter and found nothing, searched for her face and found a smooth white absence, and the grief that rose up in him had no object. It was mourning with the photograph turned face-down. He cried anyway, sitting on the boulder with the wind pulling at his jacket, sobbing for someone he could no longer picture, and the worst part — the part he'd carry the rest of his life — was that he couldn't even be sure what he'd lost. Only that its shape was exactly the size of everything.

A sound above him. Boots on scree, deliberate, unhurried.

Ansel scrubbed his face with both hands and stood up, because whatever was coming down that trail, he refused to meet it crying.

She was maybe forty, lean and sun-browned, with gray shot through her black hair and a canvas satchel slung across her chest that had clearly been repaired more times than it had been built. She carried a walking staff and a rolled leather case, and she stopped six paces away and looked at him with the flat professional assessment of someone reading terrain.

"You came out of the seam," she said.

Ansel glanced back. The crack in the rock was barely wide enough for a hand. "There's a cave in there."

"There is," she said. "Was the door open when you arrived?"

"The door — the tide was out. Freak low tide. Whole cove empty, fish flopping around wondering what happened."

"Freak low tide." She said it without mockery, but also without belief, the way a doctor repeats a symptom. "And you walked in. Alone. Without asking what it cost."

Something in his stomach dropped. "How do you know it costs?"

"Because I've spent nine years proving it does." She set the end of her staff on the ground and leaned on it. "Odile Ferran. Formerly of the Cartographers' Guild, currently of nowhere in particular. And you are standing on the route I was expelled for mapping, so I'd very much like to know how much you've paid so far."

Ansel's jaw worked. Pride said tell her nothing. But pride was a luxury item and he'd recently learned his accounts were frozen. "My mother's face," he said. It came out rougher than he intended. "I can't — I know who she is. I know I love her. I can't see her. It's not foggy, it's not faded, it's just *gone*, like it was cut out with a knife."

Odile Ferran went very still. Then she unslung the leather case, thumbed the clasps, and opened it on a flat rock with the care of someone handling scripture. Inside, nested in oilcloth, were maps. Dozens of them, hand-drawn, dense with notation in three colors of ink.

"One payment," she said quietly. "You're one payment in and you came out functional. Talking. Walking. Grieving, but grieving is survivable." She looked up at him, and there was something in her face that frightened him more than the voice in the cave had — a hunger, banked and disciplined, like a stove burning low. "Do you understand what that means? Nine years I've looked for someone who went in and came back *whole*. Everyone I ever found was either hollowed out or dead or wouldn't speak. You're the first one-mile case I've ever seen breathing."

"Lucky me."

"Don't do that. Don't make it small." She pulled out the top map and turned it toward him. It showed the coastline he knew, the cove, the mountains behind it — and threaded through the mountains, in red ink, a network of passages with annotations crawling over every inch. *Tide-marks recalculate per entrant. Price scales with distance. Storms open sealed galleries.* And along one margin, in a tighter, angrier hand: *Guild order 44-C: suppress. No commercial value in a road that takes the traveler's past — travelers cannot be billed for what they no longer miss.*

"They knew," Ansel said.

"The Guild has known for a century. They mapped enough to confirm the price and then buried it, because a safe crossing people don't remember paying for is worth more than an honest warning." Odile's voice stayed level, but her knuckles had gone white on the map's edge. "I published. They revoked my seal, burned my originals, and put my name on the exclusion rolls. This—" she tapped the case "—is what I rebuilt from memory. Every line of it."

Wind came down off the peaks and flattened the scrub grass. Somewhere above them, thunder muttered, though the sky held only cloud, no storm yet. Early snows, the fishermen had said. Weeks early.

"So what now?" Ansel asked. "I walk back down, sell what's left of my rig, and let Veyra Solenne—"

"You can't walk back down." Odile said it gently, which somehow made it worse. "The seam doesn't reopen on demand. It opens on tides measured in years. Whatever's behind you, the only way through is forward — over the pass, before the snow closes it. Which means more payments." She began rolling the map with precise, economical movements. "Which means you need to decide, right now, what parts of yourself you can afford to lose. Because the cave will decide for you otherwise."

Ansel looked back at the blank seam in the rock. He thought about his mother's hands, flour on the knuckles, and how those memories suddenly felt like items on a shelf that anything could take.

"We'll need a guide," he said. "Neither of us knows these passages."

"I know the passages better than anyone alive. On paper." Odile shouldered her satchel. "Paper doesn't climb."

"That's what I'm saying. We need—"

"There you are!"

The voice came from up-trail, young and furious, and a girl dropped down off a ledge with the ease of someone who had been born doing it — sixteen, maybe seventeen, wind-chapped, wrapped in a patched fisherman's coat three sizes too big, with a coil of rope over one shoulder and an expression that promised trouble with receipts.

She planted herself in front of them, breathing hard, and pointed at the seam in the rock.

"That's *my* cave," she said. "And you two owe it an explanation."
