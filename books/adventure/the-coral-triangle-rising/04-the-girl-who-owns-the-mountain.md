## 4. The Girl Who Owns the Mountain

Odile Ferran had been expelled from the Cartographers' Guild for drawing a map that told the truth, so she had developed a professional respect for people who lied with their whole bodies. The girl standing on the ledge above them was lying about being calm, and doing it beautifully.

"You're trespassing," the girl said. She couldn't have been more than fifteen, maybe sixteen, wrapped in a fisherman's oilskin two sizes too large, sleeves rolled back in thick cuffs. Her hair was braided tight against the wind, and she stood on a shelf of granite above the trail with the easy balance of someone who had never once thought about falling.

"Good morning to you too," Odile said.

"This is the Kessinvar approach. Clan land. You're on it without a guide, which makes you thieves of a kind." The girl's eyes went from Odile's satchel — stuffed with instruments, ink-stained at every seam — to Ansel, who sat on a rock ten feet away looking like a man who had been recently disassembled and reassembled by an amateur. He'd been that way for two days now. He kept touching his own jaw as if checking whether his face still fit.

"We're not thieves," Odile said. "We came out of the cave."

Something changed in the girl's posture. Not much. A settling, like ballast shifting in a hull. "Nobody comes out of the cave."

"Two of us did." Odile jerked her chin toward Ansel. "He came out wrong, though."

"I did not come out *wrong*," Ansel said, without much conviction. He said it the way a man says it when he has spent two days failing to remember what right felt like.

The girl descended the rock face in four movements that Odile's cartographer's eye automatically catalogued: handhold, foothold, drop, roll. She landed light and stood up already talking. "Show me your hands."

"Why?"

"Because cave-takers come out marked. If you're lying, I'll know."

Odile looked at her own palms. She hadn't shown anyone. She hadn't even looked properly herself, past the first horrified glance. Along the crease of each hand ran a thin line of luminescence, faint as moonlight through milk, tracing the lifeline the way a river traces its bed. It didn't hurt. That was somehow worse.

Ansel held up his hands without being asked. His marks were brighter than hers, and they moved — slowly, the way tide-marks moved inside the cave, rearranging themselves across his skin like something reading him.

The girl exhaled through her nose. "You took passage."

"We took passage," Odile agreed.

"And it took payment."

"It takes payment," Odile said. "Continuously. Which is why I'm mapping it, if you'll let me. Which is why I've been mapping it for six years, actually, which is why my own guild burned my charts and put me out on the ice."

The girl studied her for a long moment. Wind came off the glacier field above them and flattened the grass along the trail. Somewhere below, the sea they had climbed away from was invisible, but Odile could feel it the way you feel a wall behind your back.

"I'm Kess," the girl said finally. "Kessinvar. This mountain is mine. Well — mine to keep, not mine to own. There's a difference. My grandmother would explain it for an hour and I'd still get it wrong." She pointed at Ansel. "What did it take from you?"

Ansel opened his mouth. Closed it. Odile watched him reach for the answer and find the shelf empty. She had seen him do this eleven times since the cave. Twelve, counting now.

"I don't know yet," he said. "That's the problem. I know I lost something. I can feel the shape of it. Like a tooth gone — your tongue keeps going back."

"My mother," Odile said quietly. "It took my mother's voice. I remember that she sang. I don't remember what she sounded like." She had not meant to say it aloud. It came out anyway, flat and factual, because facts were how she survived things. "I'm telling you so you understand I'm not guessing about how this works."

Kess looked at the two of them — the disgraced mapmaker and the hollowed-out storm chaser — and Odile could see the arithmetic running behind her eyes. Weighing them. Weighing the mountain. Weighing whatever duty had put a sixteen-year-old alone on a glacier approach with an oilskin too big for her.

"You knew we were here," Odile said. "Before you saw us. How?"

"The tide-marks on the lower rocks rewrote themselves last night. New marks, pointing up-trail instead of down." Kess crouched and scratched in the gravel with a stick, quick and sure, reproducing a pattern Odile recognized with a lurch — the same glyph-geometry that glowed inside the cave, the same script that had crawled across her own palms. "The cave doesn't do that for weather. It does that for *traffic*. When it wants something to move along the mountain, it announces it." She glanced up. "It announced you two."

"That's not possible," Odile said, and hated how much she wanted it to be possible. Six years of exile rested on her certainty that the cave was a mechanism, comprehensible, chartable. Mechanisms didn't announce things.

"Everything on this mountain is impossible," Kess said. "That's what my family is *for*. Nine generations of Kessinvars keeping the accounts." She stood, brushed off her knees. "Here's what happens now. You're on clan land, you carry cave-taint, and the snows are coming three weeks early — don't argue, I read the lichen and the wind and the way the birds went quiet yesterday, and all three say the same thing. Alone, you die up here. Both of you. Confidently."

"And with you?" Ansel asked.

"With me, you probably die up here, but you'll die *correctly*, on the right route, having paid the right tolls, and there's a version of events where you make the pass before the storm closes it." Kess shouldered a ragged pack that had been sitting behind a boulder — she'd staged this meeting, Odile realized; the girl had chosen the ground. "Those are the terms. I come with you, or I go down to the clan winter-camp and report you, and the clan handles cave-takers the old way."

"Which is?" Ansel asked.

"They take your boots and your coat and wish you luck, and by luck I mean nothing." Kess smiled for the first time. It was a sharp, bright, entirely unapologetic smile. "I'm told I'm charming. This is my charm."

Odile caught Ansel's eye. Two days ago he'd been a stranger who stumbled out of the dark beside her, gripping a rope neither of them remembered tying. Since then their entire relationship consisted of sharing water, sharing silence, and sharing the specific companionship of the damaged. It was not trust. But it was something, and she found she could read him well enough now: he was going to say yes, and he was going to pretend it was his idea.

"What's in it for you?" Odile asked Kess. "Really. Beyond the old-way threats."

Kess's smile faded into something more honest. She looked up-trail, toward the white shoulder of the mountain, and when she spoke her voice had lost its performance entirely.

"Every Kessinvar for nine generations has kept the cave's accounts from the outside. Ledgers, tide-tables, toll-schedules. None of us has ever walked it end to end. It's the one map my family doesn't have." She tapped Odile's satchel. "And you're the first cartographer in history to come out the far side of the entrance with marks on her hands and her memory mostly intact. So either you help me finish the map my family was always afraid to start, or you're the most useful trespasser I've ever threatened."

"And if the mountain kills us?" Ansel asked.

"Then the account closes," Kess said simply. "Accounts always close. My grandmother says that like it's comforting."

Odile unslung her satchel and pulled out the one thing she had salvaged from her guild life: a half-finished chart of the upper approaches, drawn from rumor, banned testimony, and six years of obsession. She held it out. "If you're coming, you look at this and tell me everywhere I'm wrong. And there will be a lot of places."

Kess took the chart, and Odile watched her face do what faces did when they met a legend made flesh — a flicker of awe quickly buried under competence. The girl traced the routes with one finger, frowning, correcting angles from memory, muttering clan names for features no guild map had ever carried. Then she stopped. Her finger rested on a blank space near the high pass, a gap Odile had left because no source alive could fill it.

"There's a door here," Kess said. "A storm-door. The cave opens onto the pass when the weather's at its worst. My grandmother's ledger calls it the Ninth Mouth." She looked up, and the wind chose that moment to rise, carrying with it the first dry, mineral smell of coming snow. "And according to everything my family wrote down, that's exactly where we're going to need it — because the storm that's coming isn't going to wait for us to climb around it."

She folded the chart, handed it back, and started up-trail without waiting for an answer.

"Well," Ansel said to Odile, rubbing the glowing lines on his palm like a man warming himself at a fire he didn't trust. "I've followed worse guides. I just can't remember any of them."

Odile shouldered her satchel and went after the girl who owned the mountain, and behind them, far below where no one could see it, the tide-marked rocks at the cave mouth began, very softly, to glow.
