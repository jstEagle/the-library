## 5. Snowline

Kess knew the mountain was lying before anyone else did.

The clouds had come in wrong. Not from the sea, the way autumn storms always came, dragging their wet gray bellies up the coast — but from the north, thin and high and white-edged, the kind of sky that meant winter had decided to skip the pleasantries. She stood at the mouth of the cave's second gallery with her arms crossed and watched the light change on the peaks above the pass, and she felt it in her teeth the way her grandmother had taught her to feel it: *snow coming early means the mountain wants something.*

Behind her, the storm-chaser was laughing at something the cartographer said. Ansel Montrose laughed like a man who'd forgotten what he was paying for — which, Kess thought grimly, was because he literally had. Two payments into the passage and he still walked like someone with all his pieces. That was the cruelest trick of the tide-marks. They didn't take anything you could see missing.

"You're doing the staring thing," Odile Ferran said, coming up beside her. The cartographer's voice was dry as old vellum. "The one where you look at the sky like it owes you an apology."

"The sky doesn't owe anybody anything." Kess nodded north. "But that does. Look at the cloud line. That's snowline weather. Three weeks early, maybe four."

Odile went quiet in the way that made Kess respect her — no argument, just recalibration. She pulled out her field journal, the one with the hand-inked maps the guild had burned her for, and started sketching the ridge profile from memory. Her hands were steady. Kess had noticed that about her too: everything else about Odile was frayed and bitter and half-starved, but her hands never shook.

"Ansel," Odile called over her shoulder. "Come here. Your girl says we have a problem."

"I'm not her girl," Kess said automatically.

"She's not my girl," Ansel agreed, ambling over with that easy grin that Kess distrusted on principle. Storm chasers grinned like that right up until the storm ate them. "What problem? We're ahead of schedule. I feel great. I feel — honestly, I feel fantastic. Is that normal?"

"No," Kess said. "That's the cave smoothing you out. Like a stone in a current." She pointed at his chest. "You remember your name?"

"Ansel Montrose."

"Your trade?"

"Storm salvage. Chasing wrecks after the blows, pulling cargo off the reefs."

"Your mother's face?"

And there it was. The grin didn't fall so much as *thin*, like frost forming on glass. He blinked twice. "I know I had one," he said slowly. "A mother. I know the word. I know she — there was a song. I can almost—" His hand rose halfway to his temple and stopped, hovering, like a man reaching for a doorknob in a house he'd never entered. "It's gone, isn't it. It's really gone."

"It's really gone," Kess said. "First payment. You cried for an hour about it and then you forgot you'd cried. I watched you do it."

Ansel stared at her. Then at Odile. Then at the cave wall behind them, where the tide-marks glowed their slow phosphor green, rearranging themselves in patterns that Kess could read like her own name. A new mark had surfaced near the top of the archway — a spiral, tight and hungry.

"That's why you don't smile through this," Kess told him. "Every time you laugh, it takes something lighter next. It likes things that come easy."

* * *

They climbed.

The cave's main passage bent upward through the mountain's spine, and Kess led them along the tide-line — the glowing seam in the rock that marked where the ancient sea had once reached, back when the Coral Triangle was young and the mountains were islands. Her grandmother had walked this seam. Her grandmother's grandmother had mapped it in songs, because paper burned and ink faded but a song lived in the blood.

Except now there were songs in the blood that nobody living remembered singing, and passages that opened only under conditions the guild maps had never recorded, and Kess was beginning to understand that her birthright came with gaps too.

The snow caught them at midday, two days past the second gallery. Fat, silent flakes, falling straight down through windless air, which was somehow worse than a howling gale. Snow that fell straight down was snow that intended to keep falling.

"We can't be on the exposed traverse when this deepens," Odile said, squinting at the white curtain swallowing the ridgeline ahead. "There's a shelf maybe half a mile on. After that, cliff."

"There's something better than a shelf." Kess turned to face them both, and she saw them register her expression and go still. "There's a door."

"A door," Ansel repeated.

"In the mountain. My people call them the storm-mouths. Passages that only open when the barometer drops hard enough — the tide-marks wake up all the way, and sections of the cave that are solid rock in fair weather just... aren't. They're open. But only while the storm lasts." She hugged her arms against the cold. "My grandmother said the mountain breathes. In calm, it holds its breath. In storm, it exhales, and you can walk through its lungs."

"And if the storm ends while we're inside?" Odile asked. She already knew. Kess could see she already knew.

"You become part of the mountain. Permanently. There are places on the far slopes where the rock has shapes in it." Kess let that sit. "We climb higher instead. Above the snowline there's a storm-mouth I know of — the High Lungs, my grandmother called it. It opens widest in the worst weather. We wait out the worst of this on the approach, then go through during the peak."

"Climbing higher," Ansel said, "into worse weather. To enter a door that closes on us."

"When you say it like that—"

"It sounds exactly like every decision I've ever made," he finished, and grinned again, and Kess wanted to hit him. Didn't the fool understand that the grinning was how it got in? That the cave took the easy things first, and he kept offering them up like tribute?

* * *

The climb took everything.

By dusk the snow was horizontal and the temperature had fallen through cold into something older and meaner. They roped together — Odile's idea, her knots immaculate — and Kess led by feel more than sight, reading the tide-marks where they glimmered faintly through the ice-glazed rock, following the seam upward. Twice the mountain shrugged and they pressed themselves against the stone while avalanches hissed down gullies close enough to powder their faces.

They found shelter in a hollow beneath an overhang, and Kess built a windbreak from the rope and a folded sail-cloth Ansel had carried since the coast, and they sat shoulder to shoulder in the dark eating frozen ship's biscuit, and nobody spoke for a long time.

It was Ansel who finally broke the silence.

"I had a sister."

Kess went very still. Across the hollow, Odile's eyes lifted from her journal.

"I don't know how I know that," Ansel said. His voice was strange — flat, careful, a man walking on river ice. "I checked myself this morning, the way you taught me. Name, trade, mother — gone but known-gone. And then just now, watching the snow, I thought: she would have loved this. Someone would have loved this. And the someone had a shape, and the shape was — younger than me. A sister." He laughed, and it came out cracked. "I don't have her name. I don't have her face. I have the *fact* of her, like a receipt for something that was stolen before delivery."

"The cave hasn't taken her yet," Kess said quietly. "Or you wouldn't even have the fact."

"Then it's coming." He looked at her across the fireless dark. "Isn't it. That's what the spiral meant. That's what you read on the wall."

Kess didn't answer, which was an answer.

"Tell me about her," Ansel said. "Tell me whatever you saw. When I paid the first time — you said you watched me. Did I say anything? About her?"

"You talked in your sleep," Kess said. "The night after the first payment. You said a name over and over. *Maren.* You said, *Maren, get out of the water.*" She hesitated. "You sounded scared. Not for yourself."

Ansel closed his eyes. "Maren," he said, tasting it. "Get out of the water." He shook his head slowly. "Nothing. It's just words. Why would I say that? Was she drowned? Did I—" His voice climbed. "Did I fail her? Is that what I'm carrying? Because I can't even carry it properly, I can't even—"

"Stop." Odile's voice cut clean. "Listen to me, Montrose. Whatever it was, you paid it forward already. It's spent. Grieving it twice is paying interest on money you no longer owe." She held his gaze. "I've studied this cave longer than anyone alive who isn't dead or exiled. The memories it takes, it keeps. Somewhere in that rock is your mother's face and your sister's voice, whole and safe, better preserved than any grave. If there's ever a way back in — and I intend to find one — they're not destroyed. They're *held*."

"You believe that?" Ansel asked. "Or do you need to believe it?"

"Yes," Odile said.

* * *

The storm-mouth found them near midnight, or Kess found it — a seam in the cliff face above their hollow that hadn't been there at dusk, now breathing out a faint warmth and a green glow that pulsed in time with the wind's screaming. The High Lungs, opening early, wider than she'd ever seen it. Her grandmother's voice in her head: *when the doors open too easily, ask what the mountain is hungry for.*

They stood at the threshold. Inside, the passage glowed like the inside of a wave.

"It'll want a payment at the threshold," Kess said. "Big ones always do. The storm-mouths are the cave's deep veins. It charges tolls in proportion." She looked at Ansel. "I won't lie to you. This one will take something central. Something that makes you *you*."

"My sister," Ansel said.

It wasn't a question. He'd already done the arithmetic — Kess could see it land behind his eyes, the terrible clarity of a man counting what he had left. Mother: gone. The debt, the reason for all of it: still there, still burning, still his. The sister, Maren, the fact-of-her, the last warm thing: sitting in his open hand like a coin.

"If I keep her," he said, "we die on this cliff. If I pay her, I walk through knowing I loved someone and never knowing who." He smiled, and this time it wasn't the cave-baiting grin. It was smaller and realer and it frightened Kess more than the storm did. "You know what the worst part is? Some part of me is relieved. Some part of me wants to put her down. That's what it does, isn't it? It doesn't just take. It makes you *want to give*."

Kess had no answer for that. Her grandmother might have. Her grandmother wasn't here; the songs had gaps where she used to be.

Ansel stepped to the threshold. The tide-marks flared, and the spiral pattern from the second gallery bloomed across the stone in front of him, tightening, waiting.

"Maren," he said aloud, one last time, holding the name up to the light like a man toasting a ghost. "Get out of the water. Whoever you were. Wherever you are. Get out."

He walked through.

The glow swallowed him, swirled, dimmed. On the far side of the threshold he turned around, and his face was smooth and puzzled and empty in a way that made Odile make a small sound she'd probably deny later.

"Was I just somewhere?" he asked pleasantly. "Why are you both looking at me like that?"

Kess took his arm and led him into the warm dark of the High Lungs, and behind them the storm screamed against the door it had opened, and far below, in the valley they'd left, she saw — just for a moment, between gusts — a single lantern moving up the trail they'd climbed.

Moving fast. Moving deliberate. Carried by someone who knew exactly where they were going.

Nobody climbed toward the High Lungs in a blizzard by accident.

"Odile," Kess said softly. "Who else knows about the storm-mouths?"

The cartographer followed her gaze down to the climbing light, and Kess watched three years of exile and bitterness reorganize themselves into pure, focused fear.

"Someone from the guild," Odile said. "And if it's who I think it is — they're not following us, Kess. They're following *him*. The man who entered the cave and might actually come out the other side." She paused. "Alive. Whole enough to matter. There's never been one before. And certain people need very much for there never to be one."
