## 6. Odile's Map of Missing Things

They made camp in the lee of a granite shoulder that Kess called the Sleeping Man, because from the east it looked like a face turned up to the sky, waiting for something to wake it. Odile had checked it against her charts twice. The face wasn't on any of them. That was becoming the rule rather than the exception: the mountain kept producing things her guild's maps had simply declined to record, and each omission felt less like an error and more like a decision.

She built the fire small. Above the snowline you learned to burn little and often, to keep your heat close and your smoke closer. Kess had taught her that in a single sentence, two days ago, without looking up from the tide-marks she was scratching into the dirt with a stick — the cave's language, or the closest thing to it that could be drawn on land.

Ansel sat apart, as he always did now. He'd taken to watching his own hands like they belonged to a stranger. Odile knew the look. She'd catalogued it, in a way. Somewhere in her satchel was a folio of sketches she'd made of returning pilgrims, years ago, and every face wore that same expression — a man auditing himself and finding the books short.

She pulled the folio out now.

"Ansel," she said. "Come here a minute. Both of you."

Kess abandoned her marks mid-stroke and came, curious, always curious, dropping cross-legged by the fire with the boneless ease of someone who'd been sleeping on stone since before she could name the stone. Ansel came slower. He always came slower now.

Odile opened the folio across her knees.

"What is it?" Kess asked.

"Work," Odile said. "My old work. I want to show you why I'm really here, and then I'd like you to decide whether you still want to walk with me."

Ansel frowned. "You've been navigating us off guild charts you stole—"

"I didn't steal them. I made them. That's the problem." She turned the first page. A coastal chart, beautiful and precise, the kind of work that had won her her guild sash at twenty-six. "I was a surveyor. Meridian Guild, third rank. My specialty was anomalies — places where the recorded world and the observed world disagreed. And there was one anomaly that kept appearing in the reports, always dismissed, always filed wrong. Fishermen who came back from the Triangle coast with gaps in them. Sailors who'd sold their boats and couldn't say why. Pilgrims who walked into a cave that isn't on any chart and walked out of it lighter than they went in."

She turned the page. Sketches. Faces. Dozens of them, dated across nine years.

"These are the ones I could find. People who entered the cave and came out. I interviewed forty-one of them."

"How many came back whole?" Kess asked quietly. She was looking at the sketches with an expression Odile couldn't read — something older than her years.

"That's the thing." Odile's voice came out flatter than she intended. "None. Not one. They came back *functional*. They could walk, talk, work, remember their names. But I'd sit with them for hours, and I'd ask about their mothers, and they'd tell me their mothers' names and their mothers' trades and their mothers' burial places, and not one of them — not one — could tell me their mother's *face*. Not their wedding days. Not the sound of a voice they'd loved. The cave takes the load-bearing memories. The ones everything else is built on. And what's left stands, but it doesn't hold anything up."

Ansel had gone very still. The firelight moved on his face and he didn't blink. "You knew," he said. "Before the pass. Before the first payment. You knew what it was doing to me."

"I suspected. I hoped I was wrong." She met his eyes. "I'm not going to lie to you and say I didn't need you. I've spent nine years looking for someone who went in and came out whole, because if even one existed, then the price was negotiable. Then there was a way through that didn't cost a self. And I needed to know, Ansel, because—"

She stopped. Breathed. Turned to the last page of the folio.

It wasn't a sketch. It was a guild document, the seal broken, the expulsion order still crisp because she'd kept it like a wound keeps its knife.

"Read it," she said. "You'll want to."

Ansel took it. His lips moved as he read. Kess leaned in against his shoulder.

*For the crime of publishing unsanctioned charts depicting the Meridian Passage and its attendant tolls, thereby causing material harm to guild revenue and endangering sanctioned passage agreements—*

"Sanctioned passage agreements," Ansel said slowly.

"The guild has known for a century." Odile heard her own voice turn bitter and didn't fight it. Bitterness was the honest register, and she was done performing anything else. "The cave is on their master charts. The toll is on their master charts. Every year, sanctioned caravans pay it — memory, quietly collected, quietly never spoken of — and the guild charges a premium for the route because it's the only crossing that beats the snows. Do you know what a caravan master pays for a Meridian crossing? More than a fisherman earns in a decade. And do you know what the caravan masters pay *with*?"

"Their pasts," Kess said. "They pay with their pasts, and the guild takes its cut of the route fee, and nobody ever has to say the word *memory* out loud."

"The guild's official position," Odile said, "is that the tide-marks are a folk superstition. My charts showed the toll in plain ink. I cited forty-one witnesses. I cited the guild's own sealed ledgers, which I shouldn't have been able to read, which is the part they actually couldn't forgive. And they struck me from the rolls, voided my certifications, and put out a quiet word that Odile Ferran's maps were the work of a mind gone soft at the edges." She smiled, and knew it was an ugly smile. "Soft at the edges. That's what they called me. While they sold crossings that hollow people out."

Ansel set the document down. He was quiet for a long moment, and when he spoke, his voice was careful, the voice of a man walking on ice over deep water.

"The debt collectors," he said. "Veyra Solenne's people. The routes they run. The caravans they insure."

Odile nodded. "Solenne's house is one of the guild's largest clients. Her optimism has a balance sheet behind it. She doesn't just lend money, Ansel. She lends money to people desperate enough to take the Meridian crossing, and she takes collateral she can collect on the far side. I don't know the mechanics of it. But I've seen her name in the ledgers, next to the word *tolls*, more times than I've seen any other name."

The fire popped. Kess fed it a stick, her face unreadable.

"So that's my confession," Odile said. "I was exiled for telling the truth, and I've spent nine years walking toward this mountain trying to prove the truth mattered, and I used you. Both of you. I found a drowning man chasing a storm and I pointed him at a cave, because I needed a live subject more than I needed a clear conscience. I told myself you'd have gone in anyway. I told myself the charts would save you where they couldn't save the others. I don't know if either of those things is true."

Ansel looked at her. And Odile braced for the anger — wanted it, almost, the way you want a wound cleaned.

"You said forty-one," he said instead. "You interviewed forty-one."

"Yes."

"Did any of them remember why they'd gone in?"

She blinked. "What?"

"The debt. The reason. The thing that made them desperate enough to walk into a hole in the world." His hands were steady now, she noticed. Steadier than they'd been in days. "Did any of them remember *that*?"

"I... no. The debt-memories go first, usually. The cave seems to take the sharpest things first. Why—"

"Because mine's still there." He said it simply, like reporting the weather. "I lost my mother's face. I lost my sister — I know I had a sister, Odile, I can feel the shape of her absence, like a tooth gone from my mouth. But I still remember why I owed. I still remember the night I signed Veyra's papers. Every word of it." He looked into the fire. "Your forty-one came out hollow. Maybe the cave takes until there's nothing sharp left. Or maybe —" he looked up, and his eyes had something in them she hadn't seen since the first payment, something almost like an idea — "maybe the price isn't fixed. Maybe it's a negotiation, and nobody's ever negotiated."

Kess had stopped moving entirely. "The tide-marks," she said. "The ones that rewrite themselves. I told you they rearrange around whoever enters. My grandmother's stories say the cave doesn't take. It *trades*. It wants something. It's been wanting something since before my people had a name for it, and everyone who's ever gone in has just — handed it whatever it grabbed first."

"Then it's never been asked what it wants," Odile said slowly. The thought landed in her chest with a weight that was half terror and half the old surveyor's thrill, the anomaly-hunter's fever she'd been exiled for and never cured of. "Forty-one hollow men and a century of guild caravans, and not one of them ever asked."

"Then we'll be the first," Ansel said.

And that was when Kess went rigid, her hand snapping up for silence, her head cocked toward the dark slope above the camp.

Odile heard it a heartbeat later. Voices. More than one. And beneath them, carried on the wind off the glacier, a laugh she had heard across a hundred port town squares and hoped never to hear again on this mountain.

Veyra Solenne was climbing toward the pass. And she was not alone.
