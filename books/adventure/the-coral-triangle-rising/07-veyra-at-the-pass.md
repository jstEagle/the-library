## 7. Veyra at the Pass

The pass should not have had a fire in it.

That was the first thing Ansel understood, standing at the lip of the saddle with the wind trying to peel him off the mountain: fires needed fuel, and there was no fuel up here, only stone and sky and the blue-white glare of a glacier that had no business being this close to the equator. But there it was — a small brass stove, a kettle ticking gently, and beside it, on a folding chair that must have been carried up four thousand meters by someone with either tremendous devotion or tremendous contempt for physics, sat Veyra Solenne.

She was pouring tea.

"Ansel." She said his name the way she always did, like it was good news. "You look terrible. Sit down before you fall down."

Behind him, Odile made a sound low in her throat — not quite a word, not quite a warning. Kess stopped dead in the snow, one hand going to the cord of tide-bone beads at her wrist, and Ansel felt the girl go rigid the way an animal goes rigid when it scents something older than fear.

"You know her," he said. Not a question. He was learning, slowly, that most of what came out of his mouth these days wasn't questions so much as accusations aimed at his own ignorance.

"Everyone knows Veyra Solenne," Odile said quietly. "That's rather the point of her."

* * *

He had last seen her eleven days ago, though it felt like a season — which, he supposed, was roughly accurate, since he'd lost at least two seasons' worth of himself somewhere in the dark behind them. She'd been sitting in her office above the harbor in Port Meridian with her ankles crossed and her hands folded, smiling at him with the particular warmth of a woman who has already won and is simply waiting for the paperwork. One week, she'd said. Seven days. And she'd smiled wider when she said it, because Veyra Solenne never threatened anyone; she just made optimistic projections about your future and let you do the arithmetic yourself.

Now she was here. Above the snowline. Ahead of the snows. Pouring tea from a kettle that steamed against a sky that shouldn't have held her.

"How," Ansel said.

"The cave owes me," Veyra said simply, and gestured at the empty ground across the fire as if chairs grew there. "Sit, please. All of you. The tea's real — I had it flown in. Some comforts are worth the logistics."

Kess hadn't moved. "You can't be here," the girl said. Her voice cracked on the altitude but not on the conviction. "The pass isn't yours. Nothing past the tide-marks is yours."

"Oh, sweetheart." Veyra's smile didn't waver; it deepened, the way water deepens over something submerged. "I've been coming to this pass since before you were born. Who do you think paid for the survey that put your grandmother's name in the guild archives? Who do you think funded the expedition your friend Odile here was expelled for contradicting?" She turned the smile on Odile, gracious as a hostess. "No hard feelings, by the way. You were right about the maps. That's why they burned them."

Odile's jaw tightened. Ansel watched her hand drift toward the oilskin tube strapped across her back — the hand-drawn map, the one thing she'd salvaged from her old life — and then deliberately away from it. Smart, he thought. Don't show her what matters.

"What do you want?" he asked.

Veyra set down the teapot with a small ceramic click that sounded obscenely civilized up here at the roof of the world.

"The same thing I've always wanted, Ansel. To be repaid." She leaned forward, elbows on her knees, and for a moment the cheerfulness thinned and he could see the machinery underneath — the ledgers, the interest compounding, the patient arithmetic of a woman who had built an empire on other people's bad luck and called it faith in their potential. "You owe me forty-one thousand marks. Your rig is at the bottom of the Coral Triangle. Your salvage contracts are void. By any reasonable projection, you are insolvent." She spread her hands. "And yet. Here you are. Walking out over mountains that kill professional climbers, having paid a cave in pieces of your own past — and still alive. Do you know how rare that is?"

"No," Ansel said. "Tell me."

"There hasn't been one," Odile said flatly. Behind her, Kess nodded once, small and grim.

"Not one what?"

"A whole return," Odile said. "In three hundred years of records. Everyone who takes the passage comes out missing something. Most come out missing everything that mattered. The cave doesn't do refunds." She looked at Ansel, and there was something in her face he couldn't read — pity, maybe, or its exhausted cousin. "You've already paid twice."

"I know what I've paid," Ansel said, and hated how quickly the words came, how defensive, like a man guarding an empty room. Because that was the thing — he knew *that* he'd paid. He could feel the absences the way you feel missing teeth: two smooth gaps where his mother's face used to be, where his sister's laugh used to live. He knew the shape of the losses without the content. Grief without a grave.

"Do you?" Veyra asked softly. "Do you know why you owed me, Ansel? Forty-one thousand marks is a great deal of money. People don't borrow that kind of money for nothing. They borrow it for reasons. Reasons with weight." She picked up her cup, blew on the tea, didn't drink. "Here's my offer."

* * *

She laid it out the way she laid out all her deals — briskly, brightly, with the terms arranged like place settings.

"Betray the girl's claim," she said. "Formally. There's a registry in Port Meridian — a claim of ancestral right, filed under the Ferran-Kess line, contested. If you testify that the girl coerced you, that the cave-passage was taken under duress, that her birthright claim is fraudulent — the legal basis for the cave's protection dissolves. My people move in within the month. We run the passage commercially. Safe, fast, guaranteed." She sipped. "And your debt vanishes. Not deferred. Vanished. Written off as a cost of acquisition. You walk into Port Meridian a free man."

The wind moved through the pass, and Ansel heard it pass through the rocks behind them — heard, or imagined, a sound underneath the wind, like pages turning. Like tide-marks rewriting themselves.

"And if I don't?" he asked.

"Then the debt stands, the snows come early — and they will come early, Ansel, I've seen the pressure charts — and the three of you freeze on the far side of a mountain with nothing left to sell but each other." She said it kindly. That was the horror of Veyra Solenne; she said everything kindly. "I'm not threatening you. I'm projecting. There's a difference, and it's the difference between me and every lender you've ever feared."

Kess stepped forward, into the firelight. Up close her defiance looked younger and more expensive than it had from a distance. "It's not hers to buy," the girl said. "The cave doesn't belong to anyone. It belongs to whoever pays it honestly. That's the whole law. That's the *only* law."

"That's a lovely law," Veyra agreed. "Laws usually are. It's enforcement that costs." She turned back to Ansel, and her voice dropped into something almost intimate, almost gentle. "Think about what you're protecting, Ansel. Truly. You met this girl six days ago. You met Odile six days ago. Whatever you've lost in that cave — your mother, your sister, whatever else it took while I wasn't watching — those were *yours*. Twenty, thirty, forty years of being a person. And you're going to defend a stranger's inheritance for people you'll barely remember wanting to help?" She shook her head, sorrowful, reasonable. "You're not loyal, Ansel. You're just too hollowed-out to know the difference."

The words went in exactly where she wanted them. He felt them land — felt them find the gaps, the missing teeth, and rattle around in the space where a man's reasons used to live. Because she was right about the mechanics of it. He couldn't remember his mother's face. He couldn't remember his sister. He couldn't remember why he'd borrowed the money, what desperate beautiful stupid thing he'd done with forty-one thousand marks, and without that memory the debt was just a number nailed to him, arbitrary as weather.

A free man. He tried the phrase on. It fit like a coat cut for someone bigger.

"I need a minute," he said.

"Take two," Veyra said graciously. "The tea stays hot. Everything I own stays hot. That's rather my gift."

* * *

He walked twenty paces from the fire, to the edge of the pass where the glacier fell away into cloud, and stood there letting the wind hit him like a fact.

Odile followed him. Of course she did. She stopped beside him and said nothing for a long moment, which was its own kind of fluency.

"She's wrong about one thing," Odile said finally.

"Which thing? She seemed thorough."

"She says you're too hollowed-out to know loyalty from habit." Odile pulled her collar up against the wind. "But I've been keeping the map. Every camp, every payment, every mile. And I'll tell you what I've recorded, Ansel Montrose, cartographer's honor, for whatever that's worth now." She looked at him, and the wind pushed her gray hair across her face and she didn't bother with it. "At the second toll, when the cave took your sister, you screamed. You didn't know who you were screaming for. You couldn't have named her if I'd asked. But you fought the tide-mark with your bare hands until Kess dragged you back, and afterward you said—" Odile paused, and her voice did something complicated. "You said, *whoever she was, she deserved better than to be forgotten by me.*"

Ansel stared at the glacier. Something moved behind his ribs that had no name attached to it, and that was somehow worse than grief — grief at least knows what it's for.

"So no," Odile said. "I don't think you're hollow. I think you're load-bearing. There's a difference, and it's the difference between her and us."

From the fire, Veyra's voice floated up the pass, bright and patient: "Time's nearly up, Ansel! The snows won't negotiate, and neither, unfortunately, will the light!"

Kess hadn't moved from where she stood. She was watching him with those dark eyes that had seen the tide-marks rewrite themselves her whole life, and she wasn't begging. That was the thing he noticed. She wasn't begging. She'd folded her arms and planted her feet on her mountain like the answer was already written and she was merely waiting for him to finish reading it.

Ansel turned back toward the fire. Toward the brass stove and the folding chair and the woman pouring optimism like it was tea.

"Here's my counteroffer," he said.

Veyra raised her eyebrows, delighted. "Oh, I *love* a counteroffer."

"You keep your money," Ansel said. "Every mark of it. And I keep my silence — which is worth more, because I'm the only witness the registry would believe." He took a breath, and the cold went all the way down and lit something in the bottom of him that none of the cave's takings had reached. "But hear me clearly, Veyra. You said I'm too hollowed-out to know the difference between loyalty and habit. Maybe. Maybe I'm just a gap with legs. But I know this much, and I know it the way you know a storm is coming — not from memory, from *pressure*."

He stepped closer to the fire.

"If you touch her claim — if one clerk of yours files one page against that girl — I will walk back into that cave, find the heart of it, and tell it everything I know about you. And I have a feeling," he said, "that a cave which trades in debts would find you *fascinating*."

For the first time in the eleven days since Port Meridian — perhaps for the first time ever — Veyra Solenne's smile flickered.

It was small. It lasted less than a heartbeat. But Ansel saw it, and Odile saw it, and Kess saw it, and high above the pass the clouds began, very quietly, to turn.

"Well," Veyra said, setting down her cup with that same obscene ceramic click. "You should know that storms open doors in that cave of yours. Storm-only passages. And the great storm is coming early — I told you I'd seen the charts." She rose from the folding chair, unhurried, gathering her things, and her smile reassembled itself piece by piece like a ledger balancing. "So here's my final projection, free of charge: we'll all three of us meet again at the heart of that cave. You, me, and the girl's precious birthright. And next time, Ansel—"

The wind rose. The kettle shrieked. Somewhere beneath the glacier, deep in the mountain's throat, something vast began to open.

"—next time, you'll be the one making payments."

The first snowflake landed on the brass stove and died with a hiss, and Veyra Solenne walked into the whiteout as if it were a doorway, and was gone.
