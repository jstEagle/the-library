## 1. Six Tides

The squall came off the water like something with a grudge.

Ulyana Ashgrove saw it building twelve minutes out — a bruise-colored wall dragging its skirt of rain across the strait toward the causeway — and she did what she always did with weather: she made it a race. The chase truck groaned up through second gear, wipers flailing against the first fat drops, the whole chassis shuddering like an old dog shaking off the sea. Cape Reckoning's causeway was four miles of cracked asphalt and rusted guardrail stitched between black rock and gray water, and in twelve years of driving it she had never once let a storm beat her across.

"Come on, girl," she told the truck, patting the dash the way other people patted horses. "Not today."

The anemometer bolted to the roof rack screamed its numbers into the cab. Forty knots. Forty-five. She could feel the crosswind lean on the passenger side, trying to shove her into the rail, trying to introduce her personally to the Atlantic. Her hands stayed loose on the wheel. Loose hands read weather better than white ones; her father had taught her that before he taught her anything else worth keeping.

Fifty-one knots at the causeway's crown, where the wind funneled through the gap in the headland and tried to peel the roof off anything foolish enough to be crossing. The rain went horizontal. Somewhere behind her, she knew, the squall was swallowing the fishing sheds and the fuel dock and the crooked row of houses along Harbor Lane, knocking shingles loose, rattling windows in their frames. Let it. She'd already won.

She crossed the last hundred yards as the worst of it hit, and by the time she swung the truck into the gravel lot outside the Reckoning Point Inn, the storm had spent itself against the coast and gone sulking out to sea, trailing ragged veils of rain across the horizon like a curtain half-drawn on a bad play.

Ulyana sat for a moment with the engine ticking, watching the light come back — that strange, washed-silver light the Cape wore after weather, as if the whole peninsula had been rinsed and hung out to dry. Then she pulled the laptop off the passenger seat, thumbed through the data package one last time — barometric trace, wind profile, the squall's exact track and timing, all of it timestamped and clean — and sent it up through the inn's unreliable satellite link to a client three time zones away who paid her to know things about the sky before anyone else did.

The confirmation chime sounded. Payment pending. Pending was the operative word these days. Everything in her life was pending.

She looked at herself in the rearview mirror: thirty-four years old, salt-stiff hair escaping its braid, a face that had stopped bothering to look surprised around her twenty-ninth birthday. Gray eyes, her father's eyes, set in a face that had been sun-scoured and rain-lashed until it had settled into something like weathered teak. She looked, she thought, like exactly what she was — a woman who lived in a truck and measured her life in miles because miles, unlike years, never asked her to stay anywhere long enough to lose something.

The ledger said otherwise. The ledger said she owed eleven thousand, four hundred and six dollars to interests she preferred not to name, and that the interest compounded weekly, and that the men who collected were unfailingly polite about it, which was worse than if they'd been cruel.

She killed the engine. The inn's porch light flickered on through the thinning rain, and beneath it, leaning against the railing like they owned the building and possibly the coastline, stood the Voss brothers.

* * *

They were easy to tell apart if you knew what to look for, which Ulyana did. Aldous Voss was the tall one, narrow as a gaff hook, with a smile that arrived a half-second after everything else he said, as though it had been dispatched separately and was doing its best to catch up. Bram Voss was shorter and rounder and cheerier, a man built like a barrel of good rum, with small bright eyes that never moved quite the way a cheerful man's eyes should move. They dressed alike — oilskin jackets, knit caps, boots polished to a fisherman's shine they'd earned nowhere near a deck — and between them they held paper on half the working boats in the harbor and, as of fourteen months ago, on one battered storm-chase truck and the woman who drove it.

"Ashgrove!" Bram called, spreading his arms as if she were a daughter home from sea. "There she is. We watched you beat that squall across the causeway. Aldous, did you see it? Beat it by a length, I'd swear."

"I saw it." Aldous didn't smile yet. He saved his smiles. "You drive like a woman with nothing left to lose, Miss Ashgrove."

"I drive like a woman with a delivery deadline."

"And how is the delivery business?" Bram asked, pushing off the rail. "Lucrative? Steady? Does it cover, say—" he produced a folded paper from inside his jacket with the smoothness of a card trick, "—eleven thousand, four hundred and six dollars?"

"You're forty dollars off," Ulyana said. "Interest posted this morning. It's eleven four forty-six now."

Bram laughed, delighted, and even Aldous's mouth twitched toward the smile it kept caged. That was the thing about the Voss brothers that unsettled people who hadn't learned to read them: they genuinely enjoyed this. They enjoyed the arithmetic, the chase, the courteous tightening of the rope. Cruelty bored them. But a debtor with wit, a debtor who kept score right back at them — that was sport.

"Forty-six, then," Bram said, tucking the paper away. "You see, this is why we like you. Most of them round down. Hope is a kind of bad math, my father always said."

"Your father sounds like he'd have gotten along with mine."

Something flickered behind Bram's bright little eyes — there and gone, quick as a fish turning. "Maybe he did," he said pleasantly. "Small harbor, once upon a time."

Ulyana kept her face still. Twelve years since the *Meridian Ash* went down off the Cape with her father aboard, and still the mention of him landed somewhere under her ribs like a dropped anchor. She'd learned not to show it. Showing it to men like the Vosses was like showing blood to gulls.

"What do you want?" she asked.

Aldous pushed open the inn's door and gestured her through ahead of them — courtesy, always courtesy — into the dim taproom, where the fire was lit against the post-storm chill and the only other patron, a trawlerman sleeping off his morning pint, snored gently into his beard. Aldous chose the corner table with his back to the wall, the way he always did, and waited until Ulyana sat across from him before Bram dropped into the third chair, close enough that his shoulder nearly touched hers.

"We want," Aldous said, "to offer you a job."

"I have a job."

"You have a hustle. A job has terms." He laid a cream-colored envelope on the table between them, unsealed, heavy stock. "One job. One payment. Eleven five, rounded up, because Bram likes round numbers and I like you. Ledger closed. Truck yours free and clear. Every signature we hold on you, burned the same afternoon."

Ulyana didn't touch the envelope. "Nobody clears eleven thousand for one job unless the job is illegal, dangerous, or both."

"All three," Bram said happily.

"Bram."

"He'd have guessed anyway, Aldous. Look at her. She's already guessing."

She was. She'd been guessing since the envelope came out, running probabilities the way she ran weather — reading the pressure drop in Aldous's too-calm voice, the wind shift in Bram's cheer. The Vosses were collectors, lenders, men of paper. When they wanted muscle they hired muscle. When they wanted something retrieved quietly, from somewhere specific, without questions asked — that was when they came looking for a woman who could drive any road in any weather and find any point on this coast blindfolded.

"Salvage," she said.

Aldous's eyebrows rose a fraction. There it was — the smile finally arriving, late as ever, and colder for the wait.

"The government put a survey team ashore at Widow's Reach nine days ago," he said. "Six scientists, geologists and hydrographers, very hush, very funded. Four days ago they stopped answering their radio. Yesterday the Coast Guard declared them missing. Today the area is technically sealed while everyone decides what to do about it."

"And tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow," Bram said, "the salvage fleet arrives. Three ships, black hulls, no names painted on. They work for a man named Corvane, and Corvane believes — correctly, as it happens — that the survey team found something out there before they stopped being foundable. Something old. Something that isn't on any chart." He leaned in, and his breath smelled of coffee and peppermint, which somehow made it worse. "Corvane's fleet will take days to work the grid properly. Days. But you — you could walk out to Widow's Reach tonight, follow whatever instruments those clever scientists left logging, and be standing on top of what they found before the tide turns twice."

"And bring back what, exactly?"

"A case," Aldous said. "Sealed. Metal. Roughly the size of a ship's logbook. The survey team will have secured it — their protocols are thorough, we've seen their filings. You retrieve the case. You do not open the case. You deliver the case to us, here, within six tides." He slid the envelope another inch toward her. "And you ask nothing else. Not what's in it. Not why Corvane wants it. Not why we want it before he gets it. Nothing."

Ulyana looked at the envelope. Eleven thousand five hundred dollars. Enough to burn every signature the Vosses held. Enough to stop owing anybody anything — the state she hadn't been in since she was twenty-two years old, when the *Meridian Ash*'s insurance payout had evaporated into funeral costs and a loan she'd signed with steady hands and no idea what compound interest would do to a grieving woman over a decade.

Enough to be free. And freedom, she knew, was exactly the bait. Men like the Vosses didn't offer freedom; they offered trades, and the price was always hidden in the fine print of what you didn't ask.

"What happened to the last freelancer?" she asked.

Bram's grin widened, and for just a moment the cheer in it went all the way down to something with teeth. "Ah," he said softly. "You've been asking around. Good. Careful women live longer." He turned his mug — when had a mug appeared in front of him? — a slow quarter-turn on the table. "The last freelancer took a contract like this one and decided halfway through that he deserved a finder's fee. Opened the case. Kept the contents. Sold them to a buyer in Port Ellery." He looked up, and his small bright eyes were perfectly pleasant. "He's not in Port Ellery anymore. Or anywhere. The sea takes what's owed to it, Miss Ashgrove. My father taught me that too."

"So don't open the case," Aldous said. "Don't sell it. Don't photograph it. Don't tell your harbormaster friends, your radio contacts, your clients. Walk out, follow the instruments, take the case, walk back. Six tides. Then you're done with us forever, and we'll drink to it, and you'll never owe another soul on this coast a cent."

Outside, the last of the squall's rain ticked against the taproom window, and the light through the glass was going gold and low, evening coming early the way it did after storms. Six tides, Ulyana thought. Twelve hours a tide, more or less, if the weather held — and the weather, she could feel it in the set of the barometer she carried in her bones, was not going to hold. Something bigger was stacking up out past the shelf. She'd been reading that sky her whole life, and it was reading like trouble.

Trouble, and a window. Windows were her trade.

"And if I say no?" she asked.

Aldous spread his hands, all reasonableness. "Then nothing happens today. We're not monsters, Miss Ashgrove. We'll simply keep collecting. Weekly. Compounding. Until the truck goes, and then the gear, and then whatever else a woman can liquidate." He paused, delicately. "You have very little else."

It wasn't a threat, precisely. It was arithmetic. That was what made it a threat.

Ulyana looked at the envelope a long moment. Then she picked it up, weighed it in her palm — heavy stock, probably a cashier's draft, probably real — and tucked it inside her jacket without opening it.

"Six tides," she said. "I'll need fuel, the good batteries, and nobody watching me. If I see so much as one of your cousins' trucks on the coast road, the deal's dead and I start asking loud questions about your lending practices in front of the harbormaster's association."

"Done," Aldous said, and this time the smile came promptly, and it was almost warm. Almost.

Bram raised his mug to her, merry as Christmas. "To careful women," he said, "who live long."

Ulyana stood, dropped a coin on the table for the fire she hadn't asked for, and walked out into the washed-gold evening. Behind her she heard the brothers murmuring, low and pleased, two men watching a bet come home.

She climbed into the truck and sat with her hands on the wheel, looking east toward Widow's Reach, where the fog was already crawling back in off the water to swallow the headland whole. Nine days ago, six people had walked out there with their instruments. Four days ago, they'd stopped existing. And somewhere out in that fog was a case full of something a man like Corvane would send three black ships to collect — and two smiling brothers willing to pay eleven thousand dollars to get there first.

Her father used to say the Cape gave you exactly two choices in any weather: run for harbor, or run before the wind. He'd never much believed in harbor.

Ulyana started the engine and turned the truck toward the fog.
