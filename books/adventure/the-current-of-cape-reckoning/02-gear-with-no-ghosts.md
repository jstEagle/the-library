## 2. Gear With No Ghosts

The fog came off the water the way it always did at Cape Reckoning — not rolling in but *rising*, as if the sea itself were exhaling after holding its breath all morning. Ulyana Ashgrove drove through it with her headlights on at noon and the wipers slapping away a mist too fine to be called rain, and she told herself, for the eleventh mile in a row, that this was just another job.

The Voss brothers had given her coordinates written on the back of a fuel receipt: 44°17′ north, past the old coast guard station, down a track that hadn't been graded since before her father's time. *Government survey team went quiet,* the younger Voss had said, smiling like a man describing a canceled picnic. *Go look. Bring back what's worth bringing back.* He hadn't said what was worth bringing back. He hadn't needed to. When a man holds your ledger in his fist, you don't ask him to itemize.

The track deteriorated exactly as promised. Potholes filled with brown water; gorse clawed at the truck's flanks with a sound like fingernails. Her chase truck — a 1998 flatbed she'd bought with her first salvage money and kept alive ever since with prayer and parts cannibalized from three dead sisters — took the abuse without complaint, the way it always did, the way nothing else in her life did. The anemometer bolted to the roof rack spun lazily, reading eleven knots out of the southeast, which was nothing, which was the sea deciding whether or not to bother.

She parked where the track gave up against a wall of blackberry bramble and sat for a moment with the engine ticking, studying the last hundred yards of ground between her and the survey camp.

It should have been visible from here. A camp of six people leaves marks: antenna masts, tarps, the glint of solar panels angled at a sky that rarely cooperated. She could see none of it, and that was the first wrong thing, though she didn't name it as such yet. She filed it the way she filed everything — under *weather*, under *conditions*, under things that could be explained later by wind or light or the particular cruelty of coastal fog.

The second wrong thing announced itself when she got out and stood still long enough to listen.

There were no birds.

Not silence, exactly — the Cape never managed true silence; there was always the long gray respiration of the surf below the bluff, the creak of the anemometer above her head. But no gulls arguing over the tideline, no oystercatchers piping their idiot two-note alarm, no crows working the wrack line. She'd spent twelve years on this coast and she knew its soundtrack the way other people knew their mothers' voices. It was missing a layer, like a song with the vocals stripped out.

"Probably a hawk," she said aloud, because saying things aloud made them smaller. Her voice fell into the fog and did not echo. Fog ate echoes. That was normal. Everything was normal.

She ducked through the bramble, jacket sleeves snagging, and came out onto the shelf of rough grass where the survey team had pitched their operation three weeks ago according to the permit file Voss had shown her — one page, photocopied twice, the government seal smudged beyond legibility.

The tents were zipped.

All four of them. Zipped tight, guy lines taut, rain flies squared away with a neatness that looked almost ceremonial. She stood at the edge of the camp for a full minute before crossing into it, and the reason she stood there — though she wouldn't have admitted it under torture — was that camps like this had a smell when they were alive. Woodsmoke and instant coffee and wet nylon and the faint chemical tang of generator exhaust. This camp smelled of nothing but salt and cold canvas. It smelled already abandoned, though every physical sign said otherwise.

"Hello?" Her voice again, swallowed whole. "Survey team? Anyone?"

She unzipped the nearest tent with two fingers, ready — she realized only afterward — to flinch.

Inside: two sleeping bags rolled and strapped. A duffel packed, zipped, standing upright against the tent wall. A paperback novel facedown on an inflatable pillow, spine cracked at the halfway mark, a pair of reading glasses folded neatly on top of it. On the crate that served as a nightstand, a headlamp, a multitool, a wallet.

A wallet.

Ulyana stared at the wallet for a long time. People forgot wallets. People forgot wallets the way they forgot umbrellas — carelessly, in a hurry, certain they'd come back for them. But people did not forget wallets while also rolling their sleeping bags, packing their duffels, squaring their rain flies, and folding their reading glasses on page 187 of a novel they'd been enjoying enough to crack the spine. You didn't tidy up like this unless you expected to be gone a while. And if you expected to be gone a while, you took your wallet, your glasses, your book.

Unless you left in a hurry. But nothing here said hurry. Nothing here said anything at all. That was the problem. The camp said nothing, in the loudest possible way.

* * *

The second tent held the mess gear: a camp stove, dry and cold, its burner rings unstained — cleaned, or never used. Three kettles hung from a galvanized rail, and when she touched the nearest one it was dry inside, bone-dry, with a fine drift of salt crust at the bottom that told her water had been boiled in it once and then let evaporate rather than dumped. Days ago, maybe. A week. A crate of food sat against the wall, lid on, latch closed, and when she flipped the latch she found it more than half full: tins, vacuum packs, a brick of tea, a bag of rice swollen slightly with damp. Nobody eats half a food store and walks away. Nobody starves politely beside a full larder.

In the third tent, the instruments.

This was the part she'd actually come for, and she felt the old professional hunger rise in her despite everything — the collector's itch, the storm chaser's greed for data. Two ruggedized laptops on a folding table, both closed. A battery bank, charged, its indicator lights green. A weather station on a mast outside the tent flap, still turning, still transmitting to a receiver whose little screen glowed a patient amber.

Still logging.

She opened the receiver's log menu with a thumb worn smooth from twenty years of doing exactly this. The timestamps scrolled up the screen, one entry per minute, barometric pressure, wind speed and direction, humidity, temperature. Steady, boring, beautiful data — the kind she'd have killed for during the lean years, the kind that paid invoices when the storms didn't come. The entries ran back three weeks, unbroken.

They ran forward, too.

Ulyana's thumb stopped on the scroll wheel.

The survey team had been declared missing nine days ago. She knew that number the way she knew her own blood type; it had been in the briefing Voss gave her, delivered over eggs in a diner booth while his brother watched the door. Nine days missing. Coast guard searched four days, called it off, wrote it up as swept to sea — a plausible enough story on this coast, where the water took a dozen fishermen a decade and gave back boots.

But the log in front of her had entries timestamped forty minutes *after* the official declaration of missing. Forty minutes after the search was formally underway, someone — something — had recorded a barometric reading of 1013.2 millibars and a wind of eight knots from the east-southeast. And then another entry the next minute. And the next. Unbroken, minute on minute, all the way up to the present hour.

The instruments hadn't stopped logging because the instruments were still running. Fine. Instruments run until their batteries die; that was physics, not mystery. But these weren't running on their internal cells. They were running off the battery bank, and the battery bank was fed by solar panels, and solar panels don't clean themselves, and these panels — she walked outside and checked — were spotless. Wiped free of salt spray and gull droppings and the black dust the Cape's nor'easters carried off the peat bogs. Someone had been maintaining them.

Nine days after the team vanished, someone had wiped the solar panels.

Or —

She stood there with her hand flat on the cold glass of the panel and let the thought finish forming, because she was a scientist of sorts herself, in the self-taught, hard-knocks way of people who chase storms for money, and scientists don't get to flinch halfway through a hypothesis.

*Or the team never left.*

* * *

The fourth tent was the office. Maps pinned to a corkboard, annotated in four different hands — she could tell the handwriting apart, the careful engineer's block capitals, the looping scrawl of someone who thought faster than they wrote. Sonar printouts, tide tables, a laminated chart of the Cape waters with a section of it circled in red grease pencil so many times the wax had built up into a small red volcano. The circle sat over open water two miles northeast of the Reckoning light, over depths marked at thirty fathoms, and someone had written beside it, in pencil, in letters pressed so hard into the paper they'd nearly torn through:

*IT'S NOT ON ANY CHART. OLDER THAN THE CHARTS. HALE IS RIGHT.*

Hale. Dr. Imogen Hale, per the permit file — team lead, marine geophysics, the name signed at the bottom of the one-page contract Voss had waved at her. Ulyana read the penciled note twice, then a third time, and noticed what she'd missed on the first passes: the note wasn't dated, but the grease pencil circle had been drawn *over* a coffee ring, and the coffee ring had dried into the paper in a way that took days. The excitement in that handwriting was recent. Recent relative to the camp's abandonment. Someone had stood at this corkboard, recently, and been thrilled.

Her eyes went next to the desk beneath the corkboard, and there she found the thing that finally put cold water in her veins, properly, all the way down.

A field journal lay open on the desk, weighted at the corners with four stones brought in from the beach — beach stones, rounded, deliberately chosen, placed with care. The open page was dated nine days ago, the day of the disappearance, and the final entry read:

*0400 — All instruments nominal. Anomaly confirmed at 30 fm. Hale wants a dive window at slack. Weather says we have two days. Morale good. R— says he can hear something through the hull hydrophone at night. Probably the wreck shifting. Probably.*

And then, below it, in different ink — fresher ink, the pen still capped and lying in the gutter of the journal — a single line:

*They came ashore at dawn. We are going with them. Do not follow us. — I.H.*

Ulyana read it five times. The handwriting was the same in both entries. The same looping, fast, intelligent hand. The same pen, by the look of the strokes. *We are going with them.* Going with *whom*? Who comes ashore at dawn on an empty stretch of the Reckoning coast, and is persuasive enough — or forceful enough — that a team of six writes a farewell note instead of fighting, and then walks out of their own camp leaving their wallets, their glasses, their half-eaten food?

And who zips the tents behind them?

Because that was the detail her mind kept circling back to, the splinter it couldn't stop worrying. The tents were zipped. From the outside. Every one of them. You zip a tent from the outside when you're leaving it for the last time, or when you're closing it behind someone else. Six people walking out at dawn do not pause to zip four tents, square the rain flies, tension the guy lines, wipe the solar panels, cap the pen, weight the journal with beach stones, and fold a colleague's reading glasses on page 187.

Unless they wanted the camp to look lived-in. Unless they wanted whoever came looking — and they must have known someone would come looking — to find exactly this: gear with no ghosts. A camp so orderly it read like a message. *Nothing happened here. There is nothing to find. Go home.*

She stepped out of the office tent and turned a slow circle in the fog, and now she saw the rest of it, the thing she should have seen from the truck. Footprints everywhere inside the camp — boot prints, dozens of them, churned into the soft ground between the tents, ordinary as any worksite. But at the seaward edge of the camp, where the grass gave way to the shingle and the shingle to the tidal mud flats below the bluff, the prints simply stopped. Not faded. Not washed. Stopped, in dry mud, in a neat rank, all facing the water, as if six people had stood at the tideline in a row and then

and then

There were no tracks beyond the tideline. No prints coming back. The mud below was vast and smooth and undisturbed, a gray plain stretching out toward the hidden sea, holding its surface like poured concrete, and it would hold a footprint for days in this weather — she knew that, everyone on this coast knew that, the mud remembered everything — and it remembered nothing.

The tide had been out at dawn nine days ago. Low water, 4:47 a.m., she could look it up, would look it up tonight in the truck with the heater going. Which meant the flats had been walkable. Which meant anyone leaving on foot toward the water would have left prints in that remembering mud for half a mile or more.

No prints. No boat on the shingle. No wake record — and she was a chaser, she read water the way priests read scripture, and the mud showed no drag marks, no keel scars, nothing.

Six people had stood at the edge of the land and then become weather.

Ulyana became aware, gradually, that she was breathing too shallowly, and made herself stop at the camp's edge and breathe properly, four counts in, six counts out, the way she did in the truck when a mesocyclone went tornadic on her and the only sane response was technique. The fog pressed close. Somewhere below the bluff, invisible, the surf kept up its gray counting. Eleven knots from the southeast. Pressure steady. Everything nominal.

Everything nominal. The phrase from the journal. *All instruments nominal.* She thought about the word *nominal* — how it meant *in name only*, how engineers used it to mean *as expected*, and how it had always struck her, in twenty years of reading telemetry, as the most quietly sinister word in the technical vocabulary. Nominal meant the numbers were fine. It said nothing whatsoever about whether the world was.

She went back into the office tent one more time, because there was one more thing to check, and she'd been avoiding checking it, and avoidance was a luxury she'd stopped affording years ago.

The receiver log. The live feed. If someone was maintaining the panels, if the pen had been capped by a living hand, then maybe — the thought arrived with a lurch she felt in her sternum — maybe the log wasn't a fossil. Maybe it was a pulse.

She knelt at the receiver and watched the amber screen.

The minute turned over. A new entry printed itself: 1013.1 millibars. Wind seven knots, east-southeast. Humidity ninety-six percent. Temperature 11.4 degrees Celsius.

Nominal. Perfectly, precisely nominal. And that was the wrongness underneath all the other wrongness, the one she'd been circling for an hour without naming: the readings were *too* steady. Real weather flickered. Real anemometers stuttered and spiked; real barometers breathed with the gusts. This data was smooth as still water, minute after minute, day after day, nine days of it — a signal with no noise, a heartbeat with no arrhythmia, the kind of perfection that doesn't occur in nature and doesn't occur in broken equipment either. Only in something that was neither alive nor broken.

Forty minutes after the world declared them missing, the instruments had logged an entry. They had logged one every minute since. And the entries were flawless.

Ulyana straightened up slowly, knees cracking, and stood in the doorway of the tent with the fog drifting through it like breath through parted lips, and she understood that she had crossed some line of return without noticing the moment of crossing. Whatever the Voss brothers had sent her to fetch, whatever was "worth bringing back," it wasn't sitting in these tents among the packed duffels and the dry kettles. It was out there — two miles northeast, under thirty fathoms, inside a red grease-pencil circle older than the charts — and it had a gravity she could feel from here, pulling at the tideline, pulling at the smooth false heartbeat of the instruments, pulling, she realized with a cold that had nothing to do with the fog, at the one name in that journal she hadn't expected to see.

*HALE IS RIGHT,* someone had written. And below it, in the same looping hand: *I.H.*

Dr. Imogen Hale. Team lead. Author of the farewell note. *Do not follow us.*

Ulyana looked out across the undisturbed mud, at the place where six sets of footprints ended and the world's memory began, and she heard herself say, quietly, to nobody, to the fog:

"Hale."

The anemometer overhead ticked around through the calm. Down on the flats, far out where the gray deepened into nothing, the sea exhaled — and for half a second, no longer than it takes to doubt yourself afterward, Ulyana would have sworn the fog thinned, just there, just offshore, the way it does around a shape that has just decided, patiently, to move.
