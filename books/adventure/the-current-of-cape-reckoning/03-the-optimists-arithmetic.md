## 3. The Optimists' Arithmetic

Denny Voss believed in optimism the way other men believed in tide tables: absolutely, and with a private understanding that it would eventually drown someone.

He sat in the back booth of the Salt & Cinder, the only restaurant on the Cape that stayed open past October, and arranged the table the way he liked it. Two cups of coffee, both on his side. A folder, squared to the edge of the laminate. His brother's coat on the hook by the door, because Marta Voss — no relation, but the name carried weight in this county — had taught them both that a man who hung up his coat looked like he intended to stay, and a man who intended to stay got better answers.

Outside, the fog was doing its evening work, rolling off the water in slow gray ropes and swallowing the causeway whole. Denny could hear Ulyana Ashgrove's truck before he could see it — he'd have known that engine anywhere, twelve years of listening to it pass his window at all hours, that particular ragged idle like a dog with something caught in its throat. It came down the causeway road at a speed that suggested she hadn't decided yet whether she was coming or going.

She parked. She sat in the cab for four minutes. He timed it, because timing things was half his job.

Then she came in out of the fog with her collar up and her jaw set, and Denny felt the warm little lift in his chest that he always felt when arithmetic worked out. She needed money. He had money. The rest was conversation.

"Ashgrove." He stood halfway, offered his hand across the booth, let her decide whether to take it. She didn't. Fine. Some clients liked to keep their hands free for other purposes; he respected that. "Sit, sit. Coffee's fresh. Well — fresh-ish. This side of the Cape, everything's fresh-ish."

"I'm not here for coffee."

"Nobody's ever here for coffee." He pushed a cup toward her anyway. "That's what I love about this business. Everyone comes in angry about the price of something they already agreed to."

* * *

Her name was on a ledger in a safe in Corvane's harbor office, and Denny knew the number on it to the cent. Forty-one thousand, six hundred and change, compounding monthly at a rate that wasn't printed anywhere but lived in the gap between what the papers said and what the brothers actually collected. Twelve years of storm data sold piecemeal to fishermen and insurers and one very nervous lighthouse authority, and every dollar of it had gone into the same hungry mouth. She was good. She was the best reader of weather between here and the shipping lanes. And she was still losing, because that was how the ledger was built — not to collect her, but to hold her. Like a mooring line. Like a hook through the gill.

Denny found nothing cruel in this. That was the thing people never understood about him, the thing that made them call him the dangerous one even though Renny was the one who'd broken a man's arm over a card game. Cruelty was inefficient. What Denny felt when he looked at Ulyana Ashgrove was appreciation, the way a fisherman appreciates a fish that has learned exactly where the net ends and keeps living inside that knowledge, year after year, getting leaner and smarter and more valuable.

"You did good work tonight," he said. "The squall off the Reckoning shoals. My guy at the insurance co-op says your numbers beat the buoy array by forty minutes. Forty minutes is a lot of boats, Ashgrove."

"It's a lot of money," she said. "It's not my money."

"No. It isn't." He smiled and let the smile do some of the talking. "But here's my favorite part of my week, so bear with me. You know how much you owe?"

"Don't."

"Forty-one six. Which sounds like a mountain until you hear what I'm about to say, and then it's going to sound like a rounding error." He opened the folder. Inside was a single sheet, and on the sheet was a number with two more zeros than her debt, and beneath the number, three lines of terms written in his own careful hand, because he never trusted printers with the important parts. "One job. Six days. Your ledger goes to zero. Not reduced. Zero. We burn the page in front of you."

She looked at the paper without touching it. Good instinct. Everything on that sheet was clean, but he respected the caution the way he respected weather — as a force to be managed, not argued with.

"What's the job."

"Recovery," Denny said. "Government survey team went missing up the coast, past the old light station. Nine of them. Their gear's just sitting there in the fog doing its little electronic heartbeat, and somebody official wants it back before it rusts or gets stolen. You know the ground better than anyone. You can read that coast in the dark. You go up, you bring back their instruments and whatever else is lying around loose, you come home, we shake hands, and you're a free woman."

"That's not worth fifty thousand dollars."

"Everything's worth what somebody's scared enough to pay," Denny said pleasantly. "You'd be amazed what fear does to a budget."

* * *

Here was the trouble with charm, and Denny knew it, and used it anyway: charm was a loan too. Every smile you spent bought you a little less truth from the person across the table, and the interest came due later, all at once, usually at the worst possible moment. So he rationed it. With Ulyana he'd been spending carefully for years, and he judged he had maybe one honest-looking hour left in the account before she started hearing the machinery behind his voice.

So he gave her a piece of the truth. Not the whole truth — the whole truth was worth nothing, it was the one commodity that depreciated on contact — but a piece, sized and polished, set out where she could pick it up and turn it over.

"The survey team," he said, lowering his voice, leaning in like a man sharing a confidence rather than delivering a contract. "They weren't mapping kelp beds. They were following something. Old wreck, older than anything on the charts. Pre-colonial, maybe. Maybe older than that, if you believe the sonar techs, and I'll be honest, Ashgrove, those boys believe all kinds of things after their third beer." He tapped the folder. "My client wants whatever they found. There's a case involved. Sealed. You don't open it, you don't ask what's in it, you don't photograph it, you don't so much as lick the tape. You put it in a bag and you hand it to me and that's the end of your involvement and the beginning of your freedom."

"And the survey team itself?"

"Gone." He spread his hands, an open book. "Fog, rocks, bad luck. Coast's been eating people since before there were charts to lose them off of. You know that better than anybody." He watched her face do the thing it did — the small tightening around the eyes, the word *father* passing behind them like a bird across a window — and he did not press on it. Pressing was Renny's tool. Denny preferred to leave doors open and let people walk through them on their own.

"Six tides," he said. "That's your window. After that, a salvage fleet shows up — three black hulls, corporate money, lawyers with the tonnage of a warship. When they arrive, whatever's on that wreck belongs to them and everyone who touched it first becomes a legal problem instead of a contractor. Six tides, Ashgrove. In and out ahead of them, and you're paid, and I mean paid — cash, ledger burned, and a bonus I haven't even mentioned yet because I want to see your face when we get there."

"And if I say no?"

The question hung there, and Denny let it hang, because the answer was the whole architecture of his life and he'd learned to deliver it gently.

"Then nothing happens tonight," he said. "Nothing happens tomorrow. Nothing happens next month. The interest posts, the number grows, and one day — not a day I choose, not a day you choose, just a day — the math stops working, and then it's not me sitting across from you. It's the fleet's people, or the bank's people, or the county's people, and none of them like you. None of them have ever liked you. I like you, Ashgrove. I've liked you for twelve years. I am, genuinely, the best friend you have on this coast, and I know how that sounds, and I also know it's true."

* * *

Renny came in at nine, shaking fog off his shoulders like a wet dog, and slid into the booth without greeting anyone, which was his version of warmth. He picked up Denny's untouched coffee, drank it, grimaced, and ordered whiskey. The waitress brought it without being asked twice. Everybody on the Cape knew the brothers' order; that was the kind of town it was, the kind where knowing things kept you safe and saying them didn't.

"The freelancer," Renny said, by way of hello.

"Ah." Denny winced theatrically, a small performance, mostly sincere. "Yes. You'd want to hear about that."

Ulyana's eyes narrowed. "What freelancer."

"Before your time on this particular contract. Man named Okafor. Lovely fellow. Nigerian, came up from the Gulf rigs, did dive work, very professional." Denny turned his cup slowly on its saucer, one full rotation, watching her watch it. "Took a similar job a few years back. Similar terms. And Okafor — smart man, competent man — decided at the end that the case was worth more unopened than delivered. Or maybe he just got curious. Curiosity's not a crime, Ashgrove. It's a symptom."

"What happened to him?"

"He retired," Denny said.

Renny snorted into his whiskey.

"He retired," Denny repeated, with mild reproach aimed at his brother, "to a very quiet place where nobody bothers him, and the last confirmed sighting of him was a boat found drifting off the Sable line with the engine running and his boots on deck, paired up neat, toes pointing seaward. No body. No case. No note." He looked up, and let the smile arrive slowly, warmly, the way dawn arrives over cold water. "Now, me personally? I think he's alive somewhere. I choose to think that. Optimism, my mother called it. But the insurance company paid out, the client wrote off the loss, and everybody moved on, and that's the lesson I'd take from it if I were you: the job pays either way. The curiosity doesn't."

"You're threatening me."

"I'm *briefing* you." Genuine hurt, now, real enough to taste. "There's a difference and it matters. A threat is 'do this or else.' A briefing is 'here is the terrain, here are the hazards, here is where the last man stepped wrong.' I'm giving you the map, Ashgrove. Nobody else on this coast would. They'd just hand you the shovel and look sad at your funeral."

* * *

She almost walked. He saw it in her shoulders, in the way her hand flattened on the table — the pre-motion of a woman about to stand up and choose hunger over humiliation. Denny had watched a hundred people reach that exact moment, and he'd learned that the ones who walked were rarely saved by courage. They were saved by having somewhere to walk to. Ulyana Ashgrove had a truck and a debt and a coastline she couldn't stop driving, and Denny counted her exits the way he counted everything, and found that she had none that ended anywhere but back in this booth.

So he waited. Patience was the family trade, really. The ledgers, the smiles, the patient arithmetic of other people's desperation — it was all one long practice of waiting, and Denny Voss was the best waiter on the Cape.

"One condition," she said finally.

"Name it."

"I talk to whoever's left at that camp before I touch anything. If there's evidence of what happened to them — if they didn't just walk into the sea — I report it. To the actual authorities. Not to you."

Denny considered this with the seriousness it deserved, which was none, but which he performed at full value. "Report whatever you like," he said generously. "Ghosts make poor witnesses. And Ashgrove — the authorities have already been up there. County, state, the maritime board. They walked the camp, they took their pictures, they filed their paper, and they left the gear sitting in the mud because gear doesn't pay pensions. Whatever's up there that frightened nine trained professionals, the government looked at it and decided it was someone else's department." He leaned back. "My client disagrees with the government's arithmetic. My client thinks it's exactly his department. That's why the number on that sheet has two extra zeros."

She stared at him for a long moment, and outside the fog pressed against the windows like something listening, and down the coast, unseen, the survey camp sat zipped and dark and tidy, keeping its instruments warm for whoever came next.

"Six tides," she said at last. "And the ledger burns in front of me."

"In front of you," Denny agreed, "with a match I'll provide, and a witness of your choosing, and I'll throw in the bonus whether you ask about it or not, because I'm an optimist." He slid the sheet across the table and held out the pen, cap off, and watched her take it the way people take things they've already decided to regret.

While she read, he thought about the case, sealed and waiting somewhere in the fog north of the light station, and about what his client had told him was inside it, and about how many zeroes that was worth, and about how the last man who'd weighed it against his own life had chosen wrong in a way that left no body to bury.

Optimism, Denny thought, signing nothing himself, just watching her sign. It always pays. Somebody just has to be holding the ledger when it does.

She finished, capped the pen, and stood. At the door she paused, fog swirling in around her boots, and looked back at him with an expression he filed away for later study — not fear, not hate. Something more like a woman checking the weather before a long drive.

"Six tides starts when?" she asked.

"Sunrise," Denny said cheerfully. "Drive safe, Ashgrove. The coast's been hungry lately."
