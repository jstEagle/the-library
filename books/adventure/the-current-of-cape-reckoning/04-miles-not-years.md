## 4. Miles, Not Years

The coast road north of Cape Reckoning was a ribbon of cracked asphalt stitched to the edge of the world, and Ulyana Ashgrove drove it the way she did everything — fast enough that looking back became impossible.

Rain came in off the Pacific in long gray sheets, sweeping across the windshield like something alive, and the wipers beat their tired rhythm against it. The chase truck handled the curves with the loose-shouldered swagger of an animal that had learned its own body's limits years ago and had stopped respecting them since. Two hundred and forty thousand miles on the odometer. She knew because she'd stopped resetting the trip meter twelve years ago. Every mile went into the same long sum now, one unbroken column of numbers she never finished adding.

Behind her, in the cargo bed under a tarp lashed down with more faith than rope, sat the survey team's instruments — the ones she'd pulled from a camp where the kettles were dry and the tents were zipped and nobody was home. Ahead of her, forty miles up the coast, waited the Voss brothers and whatever it was they actually wanted from her. Between those two points stretched the only country she trusted: the road itself, going somewhere, always going somewhere.

She drove.

* * *

Twelve years ago, almost to the week, the *Meridian Grace* had gone down off the Cape with four hands aboard, and one of them had been Elias Ashgrove, who was six foot two and laughed from his belly and could read weather the way other men read newspapers. Ulyana had been nineteen. She'd stood on the bluffs above Widow's Reach in a wind that tried to take her hair and her sanity together, watching a gray sea do nothing dramatic at all — no wreckage, no flare, no body — just water, folding over itself, indifferent as arithmetic.

They'd given her a box with his spare glasses in it. That was all the sea ever surrendered. His spare glasses, folded neatly, salt-spotted, as if he'd set them down somewhere safe before walking into the deep.

People told her time would help. People said grief was a wound and wounds closed. But Ulyana had discovered early that grief wasn't a wound at all. It was a current. And you didn't heal a current; you either drowned in it or you moved faster than it did.

So she'd chosen speed.

She'd sold the house on Larkspur Lane within the year — couldn't stand the way the hallway held his height in the doorframe scuffs — and bought the truck with the insurance money and the first of what would become many loans. She'd gotten her storm certification because storms were the one thing on this coast that moved honestly, telegraphing every intention hours in advance, unlike the sea, which lied. She'd chased squalls for farmers who needed crop warnings, for the county, for anyone with a checkbook and a deadline, and when the work dried up she'd driven inland for fire seasons, then back for flood seasons, then north, then south, then anywhere the map had a line on it that ended somewhere other than Cape Reckoning.

Because that was the trick of it, the thing she'd figured out somewhere around mile eighty thousand: if you never stopped moving, you never had to arrive. Arrival meant unpacking. Unpacking meant walls. Walls meant silence, and silence meant the question she'd carried since the bluffs at Widow's Reach — *why him, why then, why without even a plank left to bury* — finally got room to speak out loud.

Miles were quieter than years. Miles asked nothing of you. You fed them diesel and they kept their mouths shut.

* * *

The rain thickened near Gullwatch Point, and she slowed without meaning to, some animal instinct pulling her toward the overlook where the road bent around the headland. Below, through a tear in the cloud, the ocean showed itself: vast, pewter-colored, working itself into whitecaps under the building southwest wind. Somewhere out there, under maybe ninety fathoms of cold green dark, lay whatever remained of the *Meridian Grace* — if anything remained. The Coast Guard had never found the wreck. Twelve years of sonar surveys, side-scan passes, volunteer searches funded by bake sales and stubbornness, and the sea had simply declined to produce it.

Her father had gone into that water and the water had kept him.

She pulled onto the gravel shoulder and let the truck idle, engine ticking, wipers slapping. Through the streaked glass she watched a fishing trawler work the swells far out, riding them with the patient arrogance of something built for exactly this. Her chest did the thing it always did around boats — a tightening, like a fist closing slowly around something soft.

She hadn't been on the water since. Twelve years, and she lived on a coast, chased weather along it, made her living reading its moods, and she had not put so much as a boot on a deck. Harbormasters knew her by name and knew better than to offer. There was a story she told people, when they asked, about seasickness, about a bad stomach, and everyone polite enough to accept it. The truth was simpler and uglier: the sea had taken the only person who'd ever made staying feel like safety, and she refused to give it a second chance at her.

Debt worked the same way, she'd learned. Debt was just another current. You didn't beat it; you stayed ahead of it, kept moving, kept the payments rolling forward like fence posts disappearing behind you at seventy miles an hour. The ledger in her name had started small — the truck loan, then equipment financing, then the bad winter when the contracts dried up and she'd signed papers from a man named Voss with a smile like a handshake. Then the other Voss. Then the refinancing that folded both into one, and the interest that grew in the dark like something damp.

Forty-one thousand dollars. She could say the number flat now, the way you could say a diagnosis after enough years. Forty-one thousand, and every month the number breathed and shifted and gained a little ground, and every month she drove a little farther to outrun it.

Her mother used to say — before the cancer took her too, three years after the sea took Elias, as if grief ran in families like eye color — "You can't outrun a tide, Ulyana. Tides come back. That's the whole point of them."

Ulyana had answered by leaving that night with the truck half-packed.

* * *

She got moving again when the light began to fail. Headlights carved cones out of the rain. The road dropped from the headland and ran along the flats, past shuttered cannery buildings with their windows boarded like closed eyes, past a church whose steeple leaned a degree or two seaward, as if even it were listening for something out on the water.

The survey instruments rattled softly in the bed behind her with every pothole. She kept thinking about them. Kept thinking about the timestamps. A camp full of gear still logging data, kettles dry on a cold stove, tents zipped neat as coffins — and not one footprint in the mud beyond the tideline. Forty minutes after the search teams had called them missing, someone or something had entered one final reading into the log. She'd checked it three times. It was there, precise and calm as a heartbeat: coordinates, depth readings, a notation string she didn't recognize, and then nothing. As if whoever wrote it had simply set down the pen mid-sentence.

You didn't leave a job like that. You didn't leave a job like that unless you couldn't walk out, or wouldn't, or unless walking out had stopped being one of the available options.

And yet the Voss brothers wanted her to go back out there. Wanted her to follow the data trail those instruments held, out past the last charted soundings, and bring something back. They hadn't said what. Voss the younger had smiled when he said *ask nothing*, and the smile had done more damage than any threat would have.

She should have said no. She knew exactly how the scene would have gone — the younger Voss tilting his head, the older one not smiling at all, the quiet recitation of her balance, her missed payments, the collateral clause she'd signed without reading on a night three years ago when the alternative had been losing the truck, which was to say losing the miles, which was to say losing the only life she had. They hadn't threatened her. They'd just shown her the ledger, the way you'd show a drowning man the shore.

Desperation dressed up as confidence. She'd worn the costume so long she sometimes forgot it was a costume.

* * *

Near Tenmile Creek the rain quit all at once, the way coastal rain does, like a curtain yanked back, and the sky opened into that bruised gold light that comes before evening. The wet road shone like poured metal. To her left the land rose in dark timber; to her right the ocean stretched out flat and enormous, catching the light and holding it.

She passed the turnoff to Widow's Reach without slowing. She always passed it without slowing. But tonight, for the first time in months, she caught herself calculating the distance anyway — eleven miles back, eleven minutes if she pushed it — and hated herself a little for the muscle memory of it. Some part of her had been making that calculation for twelve years, running it in the background like a process that refused to terminate. *How far to the bluff. How far to the water. How far from the last place my father was alive.*

That was the real odometer. She'd never once reset it either.

A sign rose out of the dusk: CAPE RECKONING — 40 MILES. Underneath, smaller, older, half-rusted: DRIVE SAFE. COME HOME SOON. Some chamber of commerce optimism from decades back, the kind of sign that assumed a town worth coming home to. She'd driven past versions of it in a dozen counties. Home was a word she'd filed under things the sea keeps, along with her father, the house on Larkspur Lane, and any version of herself that could sleep through a whole night in one bed.

But the sign stayed with her longer than signs usually did. Maybe because of the timestamp. Maybe because of the dry kettles. Maybe because forty miles up the coast, two cheerful creditors were waiting to hand her a job that smelled wrong in a way she couldn't yet name, and some old instinct — her father's instinct, the weather-reading one, the one that felt pressure drops in the back teeth — was telling her that this contract was a front coming ashore, and fronts like that didn't pass over clean. Fronts like that took roofs off.

She thought about what her father would have said, if he'd been in the passenger seat, boots on the dash, that belly laugh ready. He'd have said something infuriating and simple, the way he always did. He'd have said, *You've been driving away from that water for twelve years, sweetheart, and look how much good it's done. It's still right there.*

He'd have been right. That was the worst part. All those miles, two hundred and forty thousand of them, and the ocean was still right there, patient as interest accruing, and she had never once gotten ahead of it. You couldn't outrun a tide. Her mother had told her. Tides came back. That was the whole point of them.

Maybe the only way through a current was to stop fighting the direction of it. Maybe you turned and drove into it instead, straight at the thing you'd been circling for twelve years, and found out what was on the other side.

The first lights of the harbor town appeared around the bend ahead, strung along the black water like embers. Somewhere among them, the Voss brothers were waiting with a contract and a smile. Somewhere behind her, thirty miles back, a camp sat empty with its instruments still whispering numbers into the dark.

Ulyana Ashgrove eased off the accelerator, watched the speedometer needle fall, and for the first time in twelve years let the miles stop mattering.

The truck rolled down into the lights.
