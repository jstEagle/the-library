## 6. What the Instruments Recorded

The sea cave had a heartbeat.

Dr. Imogen Hale had noticed it on her first night, when the pain in her ankle had faded enough to let her listen: a slow, rhythmic surge somewhere deep in the rock, water moving through channels no map had ever bothered to draw, filling and emptying with a patience older than any of the instruments she'd spent her career calibrating. She had thought, in the fevered first hours, that it was her own blood she was hearing. Then she had understood it was the tide, breathing through the Cape like something asleep.

Now, on the third day — or what she believed was the third day; time had gone soft as kelp down here — she sat with the survey's field laptop balanced on her knees, its screen the only light in the chamber, and let the dead speak to her through their instruments.

The others were not dead. She had to keep correcting herself on that. *Not dead. Taken. There is a difference, and the difference is the whole point.*

* * *

She had been the one who insisted on the redundancy protocols. It was the sort of thing her colleagues had teased her about — Imogen and her backups, Imogen and her triplicate logs, Imogen who trusted no server she couldn't physically touch. She had copied the raw survey data to the laptop's encrypted partition every evening of the expedition, and on the last evening, the evening the men came, she had done it out of habit rather than forethought, kneeling in the mud outside the equipment tent with the transfer cable in her teeth while the wind worried at the guy-lines.

Forty minutes later, she had been running.

So now the laptop held everything: the sidescan mosaics, the magnetometer traces, the sub-bottom profiles, the multibeam soundings, six days of a government survey that had officially ended in an administrative footnote — *team unaccounted for, equipment presumed lost, search suspended* — and which had in fact ended in a rowboat with no lights and men who spoke only to give orders.

She worked by feel as much as sight. The screen's glow was a liability; she kept it dimmed to almost nothing, angled away from the cave mouth, her oilskin draped over the lid to strangle the spill. Her ankle had swollen to the size of a grapefruit, and every hour or so the tide's breath through the rock reminded her how narrow her margin was. But her hands were steady. Her hands had always been steady. It was the rest of her that the last three days had tried to shake loose.

She opened the magnetometer overlay first, because it was the anomaly that had started everything, six days and one lifetime ago.

The Cape's waters were a junkyard of iron. Three centuries of wrecks, moorings, dumped ballast, a war or two's worth of scattered debris — the survey's early days had been a blizzard of magnetic noise, and Hale had spent two nights sorting signal from scrap. The anomaly at grid reference K-14 had appeared on the second night as a smear, a long low ridge of ferrous mass lying in the scour channel half a mile off the northern headland, buried under nine meters of sand and silt.

Buried, and *large*. Larger than any wreck the Cape's records accounted for. She had pulled the hydrographic charts going back to the first Admiralty surveys, the old hydrographer's hand-inked soundings, even the Portuguese charts in the provincial archive's digital collection — the ones from before the Cape had an English name. Nothing. Every chart, every survey, every sounding ever taken of these waters showed open bottom at K-14. A hundred and eighty years of instruments, and not one of them had seen what lay under the sand.

That was the first thing she had understood, lying in the dark with her ankle screaming and the laptop's fan the only other living sound: whatever lay at K-14 had been *meant* not to be found. Ships didn't sink themselves into nine meters of silt in a scour channel. Channels scoured. They uncovered things. For this wreck to stay buried, something had to have kept it covered — or the channel itself had been changed, diverted, its whole hydrology altered, by human hands, at human cost, a very long time ago.

The second thing she understood on the third day, alone, with time enough to stop running from the knowledge and start reading it.

* * *

The multibeam data was the survey's pride. They had mapped the seabed off the Cape at two-meter resolution, a fabric of sound woven across every square meter, and Hale had been the one to process it, because she was the one who didn't trust the automated filters. She liked to see the raw returns. She liked to know what the water actually said.

At K-14, the seabed fabric showed the buried hull's outline as a subtle rise, a ghost of a shape under the sediment — and then, amidships, a gap.

Not a shadow. Not an acoustic shadow, which she knew well, which was what dense objects threw behind them. This was different. This was a region where the returns came back *wrong* — not absent, the water was too shallow for true acoustic blanking, but *incoherent*. Scattered. As if the sound had gone into the space under the sand and come back having forgotten where it had been.

She ran the processing three times. She ran it with the filters off, with the filters maxed, with the raw phase data pulled up in the spectral view at two in the morning of the expedition's fifth night, her tea gone cold, her colleagues asleep in their tents. The gap held. Roughly thirty meters long, eight wide, sitting in the middle of a hull that the magnetometer said was packed with mass — iron, dense, ordered, *stacked* — and in the middle of all that mass, a volume that returned nothing the instruments could make sense of.

A void.

She had written in the log that night: *Anomalous acoustic response amidships, consistent with large internal cavity. Recommend diver inspection at next slack. Possible structural collapse of cargo deck; possible —* and there her pen had stopped, because the next word her mind offered was *chamber*, and *chamber* was not a word a government survey was licensed to write.

Now, in the cave, she read her own stopped sentence and finished it in her head. *Chamber.* Thirty meters. Eight wide. In a hull old enough to predate the first chart that failed to mention it.

Hulls that old didn't have cargo holds that size. Hulls that old barely had *holds*. She had spent a decade in marine archaeology and she knew the proportions of historical shipping the way a musician knows scales, and the ghost-shape under the sand was wrong — too long, too fine at the bow, too deep-bellied, a silhouette that belonged to no catalogue, no typology, no century she could put a confident name to. The few timber samples the team's grab-sampler had brought up — she had fought for those too, one small permitted core — had come back from the lab with a radiocarbon date the lab had flagged twice as a probable contamination error.

It was not an error. She had known it was not an error when she'd read it, and she knew it now, and the number sat in her chest like a swallowed stone: the wood was older than any European vessel that had ever charted this coast. Older than the coast's *name*.

* * *

The tide turned while she worked. She felt it before she heard it — a change in the cave's breathing, the exhale lengthening, the inhale shortening — and she marked the time against her watch out of pure professional reflex, and then laughed, a small cracked sound that the rock took and gave back to her. Three days hunted, half-starved, ankle ruined, hiding in a hole in the earth, and she was still logging tidal states in her head. The habit would outlive her. Perhaps that was what a career was: the part of you that kept working after everything else had been taken.

The men had come on the sixth night.

She made herself replay it, because the data had a gap in it too, and gaps were where the truth lived. The survey had run a fixed hydrophone array off the headland, streaming ambient noise to a recorder in the equipment tent. She had the audio. She had not listened to all of it. She had listened to forty minutes of it, once, crouched in the cave with the earpiece pressed so hard to her skull it ached, and she had not been able to listen to more.

The evening was ordinary in the recording: swell, the groan of the mooring buoy, gulls. At 21:14, an engine. Not one engine — the harmonics said at least two vessels, running quiet, no lights, no radio traffic on any band the team's scanner monitored. At 21:31 the engines stopped. What followed was forty minutes of the sea, and under the sea, fainter than the sea, voices. She had counted six distinct speakers. None of them raised their voices. Orders, she thought. The flat, unhurried cadence of men doing a job they had done before.

At 22:03, her team's generator shut down. It was on a timer. It should not have shut down at 22:03.

At 22:11, footsteps in the recording — the hydrophone's cable ran ashore, and the array had always picked up the beach, a flaw she'd meant to file a report about. The footsteps were many and unhurried. And then her colleagues' voices, close to the shore mic, and the thing that she could not stop hearing, the thing she heard when she closed her eyes and would hear for the rest of her life: no shouting. No struggle. Okafor saying, calmly, *All right, all right, we're coming*, in the tone of a man reassured. Reyes asking a question and someone answering it in a voice like still water. Then gravel under boots, receding, all of them together, all of them walking *willingly* — or walking in the belief that willingness and survival were the same thing — and then the rowboat's oars, and then the sea, and then nothing.

The team's last data entry had timestamped at 22:47, thirty-six minutes after the search for them had officially begun. A temperature log. Automated. The instruments had kept working, faithful and blind, recording the temperature of an empty camp, and that automated line — 22:47, 11.2 degrees, system nominal — was the loneliest sentence she had ever read.

They had been taken ashore. That was the phrase her mind kept returning to, and it was wrong, she knew it was wrong — they had been taken *by* shore, taken *to* shore, taken *from* the shore — but her mind insisted on it, because *taken ashore* made it sound like weather, like a thing the sea did, and she needed, desperately, to believe it was a thing people did. People could be found. People could be bargained for. Weather could not.

Men flying no flag. She had seen them only in fragments — she had been at the headland calibrating the array when the boats came in, and she had done the only thing her body had decided to do, which was to go to ground in the sea cave the survey had used for equipment storage, with the laptop, a case she'd grabbed from Okafor's tent without fully knowing why, and the instinct of a small animal for the one opening its hunter hadn't checked. Through the cave's throat she had seen torchlight on the water, and men moving with the economy of professionals, and no markings on any hull, no registration numbers, no colors she could name. Vessels that were not vessels. Men who were not men but a function, an erasure, walking upright.

* * *

The sealed case sat beside her in the dark. Okafor's case — no, she corrected herself, not Okafor's; the case had been in Okafor's tent, but it belonged to the expedition, to the funding body, to whatever chain of custody had delivered it into a government survey with instructions that Okafor had never fully explained to her. *Chain of custody*, she thought, and almost laughed again. The chain had broken on the sixth night, and now custody was a woman with a ruined ankle in a hole in a cliff.

She had not opened it. That was not virtue; it was method. The case's lock was a cipher type she recognized, and she recognized it because it was not a government issue — it was private, expensive, the kind of lock that said *what's inside matters more than who's carrying it*. Opening it without understanding who was listening for its opening felt like striking a match in a room full of gas. The instruments had told her what was under the sand. The case would tell her *why men came in the night for a survey team that had found it*. She would need to understand both before she lit anything.

But she had read the survey's own final products, the deliverables the team had compiled on the fifth day, before the night, before the boats. And it was there, in the draft report Okafor had been too careful to send up the chain — *hold for review, something's off with the provenance, I want to check the archive before we file* — that she found the sentence that changed the shape of everything.

Okafor had written: *Site K-14 does not correspond to any recorded casualty in the regional record. Absence of documentation at this scale of anomaly is itself an anomaly of the highest order. Recommend immediate security review of survey data chain. Recommend the team not transmit findings until provenance of the commissioning interest is verified.*

*Provenance of the commissioning interest.* Okafor had suspected. Okafor had looked at the money behind the survey — the quiet money, the money routed through three foundations and a maritime institute that shared an address with a holding company — and he had smelled what she had smelled only later, too late: that they had not been sent to find the wreck. They had been sent to *confirm* it. Someone had known it was there. Someone had known for a very long time, and had spent decades keeping it uncharted, unsounded, unmentioned, and had finally needed a government instrument's stamp on it — legitimacy, a survey number, a file in an archive — and had hired six scientists to provide the stamp, never imagining that one of the six would read the data instead of just producing it.

And when the data read wrong, when the void amidships and the impossible date and the missing century came back from the instruments like a question no one was supposed to ask — the men had come.

* * *

Dawn, if the grey seeping through the cave's upper crack deserved the word, found her still awake, the laptop warm against her thighs, the battery at nineteen percent and falling. She had rationed it ruthlessly and was now spending it freely, because she had finally understood what the data was for.

Not the report. The report was dead; the report would never be filed. Not the commission; the commission had been a lie wearing a government's coat. The data was for *this*: for the moment when she found a person she could trust, and put the screen in front of them, and said *look*. The instruments had recorded everything. The instruments were the only witnesses the men in the unmarked boats had forgotten to drown. And a witness with a timestamp was a dangerous thing, more dangerous than gold, which was — she understood this now with a clarity that felt almost like calm — exactly why the case mattered, and exactly why she was still alive.

They had not killed her because they had not found her. But they would come back. Men like that always came back; erasure was a job, and jobs were finished. And when they came back, they would come for the cave, and the camp, and the data, and the woman in the data's shadow.

So she had three things to do. Find a way out of the cave that the hunters didn't know — the survey team had mapped these sea caves in the expedition's first week, she remembered suddenly, sitting upright so fast her ankle blazed, *Reyes had mapped them, the passage east, the one that ran under the headland to the shingle beach* — the map was in the data, the map had been in front of her for three days. And find the person the data pointed to.

Because there had been one more thing in the files, one more thing she had turned over and over through the long dark hours like a stone in the pocket. The wreck's manifest — no, not a manifest, they had no manifest, what she had was the magnetometer's mass distribution, the ordered stacking, the *cargo*, and cargo that old, packed that deliberately, was not cargo but *contents*, a ship sent down on purpose with its hold full — and among the grab-sample recoveries, one small concretion that the lab had cleaned to reveal a nameplate fragment, a ship's bell fragment, letters surviving in the corrosion: *—SHGR—*.

Ashgrove.

She had thought it a coincidence. She had thought, in the first shocked hour of reading it, that the name was common enough, that coastal counties were full of Ashgroves, that the mind reached for patterns the way drowning men reached for driftwood. But the name had stayed with her, had *weighted* her, the way true things weight a person, and when the men had come and she had run and hidden and read and reread, the name had kept surfacing, and with it the memory of the expedition's second evening, the harbormaster's assistant making small talk while they took on fuel, the old man's voice: *Ashgrove? There was Ashgroves working this coast back to before the charts. Boats out of this cove when this cove had a different name.*

Before the charts.

The same phrase the data kept whispering. The wreck predated every chart, and a family named Ashgrove had worked this coast before the charts existed, and somewhere out on that causeway, right now, was a storm chaser named Ulyana Ashgrove who owed money to men who might very well be the same men who had rowed her colleagues away into the dark.

The cave breathed. The tide was turning again; she could feel the inhale building in the rock, the slow pull of the sea gathering itself. Nineteen percent. One passage east, mapped by a dead — *not dead, taken, there is a difference* — a missing colleague. One name. One storm chaser who, if the harbormaster's gossip was anything, knew this coast the way the men in the unmarked boats never would.

Hale closed the laptop. In the dark, the case sat beside her like a held breath, and the wreck lay under its nine meters of sand half a mile out, keeping its century-old secret, and the tide turned, and turned, and would keep turning — six tides, the old fishermen said, before the big water came — whether anyone was ready for it or not.

She had been a scientist for twenty years. She had believed, with the whole of her reasonable heart, that the world was knowable, that data was light, that every anomaly had an explanation if you were careful enough. She still believed it. But she believed it now the way one believes in a rope bridge over a gorge: with both hands gripping, and no looking down.

They had taken her team. They had not taken the data. They had not taken her.

And somewhere up that coast road, an engine was running, and she was going to find it.
