## 7. First Tide Turns

The tide table in Ulyana's glovebox was a thing she trusted more than most people. She'd annotated it in pencil over the years — corrections, local quirks, the way the water off Cape Reckoning ran fifteen minutes late on a falling barometer and lied about it cheerfully. Now she sat on a wet rock inside the sea cave with that page flattened against her knee, a dying headlamp strapped to her brow, and did arithmetic that made her stomach fold in on itself.

Slack water: 6:41 p.m. Sunset: 7:03.

Twenty-two minutes of stillness between the tides. Twenty-two minutes to move a half-dead woman through a passage mapped by men who had then walked into the ocean and left no footprints behind them.

Behind her, deeper in the cave where the dark pooled like standing water, Dr. Imogen Hale lay propped against the survey team's own equipment cases, breathing in short, careful sips of air. The sealed case she would not release — not to shift her weight, not to sleep, not even when her hands shook so badly Ulyana could hear the aluminum rattling like teeth — rested across her lap under a fisherman's knit sweater gone gray with salt.

"You're doing the math out loud," Hale said. Her voice was scraped thin, but there was an academic's precision underneath it, the habit of a woman who corrected herself mid-sentence. "You're muttering numbers."

"I'm muttering tide tables."

"Then I should tell you the numbers are wrong." Hale shifted, and the sound she made was worse than any complaint. "The survey team's ephemeris. It's in the case. Page forty-one. They recalibrated for the Cape's tidal bore — the government tables run eleven minutes fast here. Your slack isn't 6:41. It's 6:52."

Ulyana turned her head slowly. In the weak spill of the headlamp, Hale looked like something the sea had tried to keep and failed — lips cracked, eyes fever-bright, cheekbones sharp enough to be dangerous. But her gaze was steady. It was the steadiest thing in the cave.

"Eleven minutes," Ulyana said.

"Eleven minutes is the difference between walking out and swimming out." A pause. "I watched three people swim out. They didn't make it to the swimming part."

* * *

They had found the map two hours earlier, folded into the lining of the survey tent's equipment locker — because of course the team had stowed their gear neatly before vanishing; of course they had. That neatness had been haunting Ulyana since the causeway. People who died messy left messes. People who were taken left order behind them, because someone wanted the camp to look abandoned rather than interrupted.

The map was drawn in pencil on waterproof stock, the surveyor's hand small and exact. It showed the cave system as a cross-section: the flooded chamber where Ulyana had found Hale, and above it, threading up through the basalt like a crack in a tooth, a dry passage rising toward a blowhole exit on the cliff shelf — the kind of vent the sea breathed through twice a day and forgot about in between. Someone had labeled it *Egress — passable at slack, low swell only.* Below that, in different ink, added later and pressed hard enough to tear the paper:

*If we don't come back, tell no one where this leads.*

Ulyana had read that line four times. Each time it meant something worse.

Now she crouched at the mouth of the passage and played the headlamp along it. The walls were black stone furred with pale lichen, slick as oiled glass. The floor rose steeply, broken by ledges you'd have to climb like stairs built by something that hated people. Forty feet up, the passage bent and vanished. Somewhere beyond the bend, if the map told the truth, daylight waited.

"How far can you walk?" she asked.

Hale considered the question the way she seemed to consider everything — honestly, and without flinching from the answer. "Fifteen feet," she said. "Then I'll need your shoulder. Then maybe another ten. After that you'll be dragging me, and I'm heavier than I look, because grief adds weight. Nobody tells you that."

Ulyana almost laughed. It came out as something else, a rough breath that fogged in the cold air. "I've dragged heavier."

"I imagine you have." Hale's eyes went to the compass hanging around Ulyana's neck — her father's brass compass, the one thing she'd pulled from the wreck of his boat's records, the one thing she never took off. Hale had looked at it that way since the moment she'd heard the surname Ashgrove, with an expression Ulyana couldn't file anywhere yet. "Your father," Hale had said, when they first spoke. "He didn't drown, Ulyana. He found this place first." And then she'd collapsed, and there had been no time for anything but keeping her alive.

There was time now. Time was exactly what they didn't have.

"We go at slack," Ulyana said. "6:52 by your correction. We're at the top before the water turns, or we're not going anywhere ever again." She checked the headlamp battery, then the backup, then the road flare she'd wedged in her jacket — twelve years of storm chasing had taught her that redundancy was just fear wearing a work shirt. "And Dr. Hale? When I say climb, you climb. When I say hold, you hold like the rock owes you money. No science. No hesitation. Agreed?"

Hale managed something that was nearly a smile. "Agreed. And Ulyana — call me Imogen. If we die in here, I'd rather it be as a person than a citation."

* * *

They started at 6:49, because Ulyana gave them three minutes' margin and knew even that was a gamble. The first fifteen feet went the way Hale promised — her boots scraping, her breath sawing, her hand white-knuckled on Ulyana's forearm. Then the shoulder. Then the ten feet after that, which took nine minutes, because the rock didn't care about tide tables.

At the first ledge, Ulyana boosted her from below, hands flat against Hale's ribs, feeling the birdcage fragility of her. At the second, Hale climbed alone while Ulyana stood below with her arms raised, ready to catch, watching the water in the chamber beneath them.

That was the thing about slack water. It didn't look like anything. The sea just stopped — a held breath, a held knife — and every instinct told you it was safe, that the pause meant peace. Ulyana had chased storms long enough to know what a pause really was. It was the atmosphere loading its arm.

The water below the ledge lay flat and black and utterly still, and it frightened her more than any squall she'd ever driven toward.

"Keep moving," she said, and heard how tight her voice had gone.

Halfway up the passage, the headlamp began to die.

It didn't fade politely. It stuttered — light, dark, light — each flicker printing the cave onto Ulyana's retinas in negative: the lichen, the wet gleam of the walls, Hale's face turned back toward her, gaunt and patient. Then dark. Full dark, the kind that has weight, that presses on the eyeballs like deep water.

"Talk to me," Ulyana said, swapping batteries blind, fingers working by memory. Twelve years of rigging instruments in hurricane rain at three in the morning. Her hands knew this work. Her heart didn't care. "Anything. Recite something."

A pause. Then Hale's voice, steady as a metronome in the blackness: "*The Current of Cape Reckoning* runs north-northwest past the old light, depth thirty fathoms on a clear day, forty on a lie—"

"That's my father's shanty."

"Yes," Hale said softly. "I know."

The lamp caught. Light bloomed, and Ulyana found Hale watching her with that same unfilable expression, and there was no time — there was never any time — but Ulyana heard herself say, "When we're out. You're going to tell me everything. All of it. Even the parts that hurt."

"Especially those," Hale said. "Those are the true ones."

* * *

The last stretch was the worst, because the passage narrowed and bent and then, without warning, opened — and there it was: the blowhole, a ragged oval of sky going bruise-purple with dusk, twenty feet above them down a chimney of stone. Beyond it, the cliff shelf, the truck, the coast road, the world.

And below, faint through the rock, a sound Ulyana felt in her molars before she heard it with her ears. The sea, drawing its breath back in. The turn beginning.

"6:58," she said. "We've lost our margin. It's now or it's never."

"Then it's now." Hale said it without drama, the way she'd said everything, and Ulyana thought — absurdly, in the middle of everything — that she understood why three governments' worth of paperwork had been filed about this woman. Some people you could follow into a fire because they'd already decided the fire was survivable.

She went first, jamming her back against one wall and her boots against the other, chimneying upward hand over hand, the compass swinging cold against her sternum. At the top she hooked an elbow over the rim of the blowhole and reached back down into the dark.

"Give me your hands."

Hale's hands came up out of the blackness and gripped hers — salt-cracked, trembling, iron underneath. Ulyana hauled. For one horrible second Hale's weight hung entirely on Ulyana's grip and the whole Atlantic seemed to pull the other way, a low groan rolling up the chimney as the flood tide found its voice again. Water slapped somewhere far below, once, twice, impatient.

Then Hale's shoulders cleared the rim, and they were both sprawled on the cliff shelf, gasping, the wind off the open water hitting them like a wall of clean cold glass.

Below and behind them, the cave mouth swallowed itself. The surge poured into the passage they'd climbed, filling it floor to ceiling, and Ulyana lay on her back and listened to the sea seal the door behind them, and laughed — a cracked, disbelieving sound that scared a gull off the rocks.

Hale rolled onto her side. Salt spray beaded in her hair. She was looking at Ulyana with an expression that had finally resolved into something Ulyana could name: recognition. The look of someone confirming a family resemblance across a hundred years and a drowned ship.

"You want to know how I know your father's shanty," Hale said.

The first tide closed behind them with a sound like a vault locking. Down the coast road, headlights swung onto the causeway — slow, deliberate, sweeping the cliff tops. Not searchlights. Watching lights.

Ulyana got to her feet and pulled Hale up with her.

"Tell me in the truck," she said. "Fast."
