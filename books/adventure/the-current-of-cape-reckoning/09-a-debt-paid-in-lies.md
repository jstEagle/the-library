## 9. A Debt Paid in Lies

The radio crackled at half past four in the morning, when the fog off Cape Reckoning lay so thick it seemed less like weather than like a decision the sea had made about the land.

Ulyana had been awake for two hours by then, sitting in the cab of the chase truck with her hands wrapped around a thermos of coffee gone bitter and cold, watching the cave mouth below the tideline where Dr. Imogen Hale slept propped against wet stone with the sealed case locked between her boots and her arms around her own knees. The tide was coming in. In an hour the cave's lower gallery would flood, and Ulyana would have to wake her and move her up to the shelf rock, and every hour after that would be another hour spent deciding what she already knew she was going to do.

She answered on the third crackle, because answering on the first would have told them she'd been waiting.

"Go for Ashgrove."

"Morning, sunshine." Tam Voss's voice came through warm as butter on a hot pan, that particular cheerfulness he wore the way other men wore brass knuckles. "You alive out there? Fog's thick enough to chew."

"Barely." She kept her voice flat, professional, the voice of a woman doing a job and nothing else. "Where are you?"

"Harborside. The *Maren K* is fueled and my brother's already aboard being disagreeable about the coffee. We got your message last night — the coordinates from the survey logs."

Here it was, then. The moment she'd been turning over all night like a stone in her pocket, feeling its weight, its edges, the way it fit exactly into the hollow of her palm.

"I've got them," she said. "But there's a complication."

"A complication," Tam repeated pleasantly. "I hate complications, Ashgrove. Complications cost money."

"This one saves it." She made herself lean back against the seat, made herself sound bored, a woman reading numbers off a page instead of a woman committing the first real betrayal of her life. "The survey team logged three sites before they went missing. Three sonar contacts in the same general grid. Two of them are geology — drowned moraine, glacial scarring, nothing but rock shaped like something interesting. The third is your wreck. But the logs don't flag which is which. They were still processing when they stopped logging."

A pause on the line. She could hear the fog dripping off the truck's antenna, each drop landing with a small fat sound, and beneath that, faintly, the breathing of the sea.

"So how do we know which one's ours?" Tam asked.

"We don't. Not yet. I need to cross-reference the magnetic anomaly readings against the depth soundings — the wreck's iron content will skew differently than bedrock. Give me until tomorrow's low water and I'll have the true position pinned inside fifty meters."

Another pause, longer this time. When Tam spoke again, the butter was still on his voice but there was a knife underneath it now, laid flat, not yet picked up. "You're telling me you want us to sit on our hands for a full day while the salvage fleet is already steaming down the coast."

"I'm telling you that if you run to the wrong coordinates and burn six hours dragging sonar over a pile of glacial till, Corvane's people will find the real site first and you'll have explained to him why forty thousand dollars of fuel and charter went into a hole in the seabed." She let that sit. Then, softer, almost kind: "You hired me because I read data better than anyone on this coast, Tam. Let me do the thing you hired me for."

The silence stretched. Somewhere behind her, down in the dark of the cave, she heard Hale shift, the scrape of a boot against stone, and Ulyana's heart slammed once, hard, against her ribs — irrational, ridiculous, since Hale was a hundred meters away and Tam was ten miles off across black water, but guilt does not respect distance. Guilt arrives ahead of consequence. It had arrived for her sometime around three in the morning, standing at the truck's tailgate with the survey logs spread under a headlamp, and it had not left.

"Tomorrow's low water," Tam said finally. "You get me a number by then or the deal changes, Ashgrove. And not in ways either of us will enjoy."

"You'll have your number."

"One more thing." His voice dropped, went casual in the deliberate way of a man reaching into his coat. "My brother says the survey camp looked strange when you went through it. Gear stowed neat. No tracks past the tideline. He says that's the kind of thing that makes a man wonder what else the logs might be hiding. What else *you* might be hiding."

Ulyana watched the fog move across the causeway, slow and total, erasing the world a few meters at a time.

"If I find anything worth hiding," she said, "you'll be the second to know."

Tam laughed, and the laugh was genuine, and that was somehow worse. "Attaboy. Tomorrow's low water." The radio clicked dead.

She sat there a long moment with the handset loose in her grip, listening to static hiss like sand through an hourglass, and then she set it down on the dash beside her father's old compass — the one thing from the boat they'd never recovered, the one thing she'd bought back from a pawnshop in Astoria eight years ago — and she put the truck in gear and drove nowhere in particular along the fog-bound road, because she could not bear to sit still with what she'd just done.

* * *

The lie itself had been simple. That was the part that kept catching in her throat, later — how simple it had been.

The survey logs contained three contact points, yes, but only one of them was ambiguous. The other two were clearly marked in Hale's own annotations: *moraine deposit, igneous, consistent with terminal glaciation.* Any competent reader could have ruled them out in ten minutes. Ulyana had ruled them out in ten minutes, sitting in the truck's dome light at three in the morning, and then she had done the arithmetic that mattered: the Voss brothers would take her word, because taking her word was cheaper than doubting it, and doubt cost fuel, and fuel cost money, and the Voss brothers worshipped money with the clean uncomplicated devotion of men who had never loved anything else.

So she had given them the moraine coordinates dressed up as uncertainty. She had invented a cross-referencing procedure that would take her a day, when in truth it had taken her eleven minutes. She had spoken in the flat competent voice that twelve years of storm chasing had taught her to use with clients who wanted to believe her, and they had believed her, because she was good, because she was reliable, because she was *theirs*, bought and paid for and listed on a ledger in some office above the harbor, and a purchased thing does not lie.

Except this one did.

She parked the truck on the bluff above the tideline and got out, and the fog closed around her like cold water. Below, the cave mouth showed as a darker darkness in the cliff base, and the tide was halfway up its climb toward drowning the lower gallery. She scrambled down the goat path with the sureness of someone who had been climbing this coast since she was tall enough to fall off it, and she found Hale awake, already moved up to the shelf rock, sitting with the case in her lap and her eyes fixed on Ulyana with that clinical, unblinking attention that seemed to be the woman's resting state.

"You talked to them," Hale said. Not a question.

"Half past four. They'll hold position until tomorrow's low water." Ulyana lowered herself onto the rock beside her, wincing where the cold seeped through her jacket. "They think I'm narrowing down three sites. By the time they figure out the other two are rock, we'll be gone."

"Gone where?"

"That's the part I'm still working on."

Hale studied her. In the gray pre-dawn light filtering down through the cave's throat, her face looked carved rather than lived-in, all planes and hollows, except for her eyes, which were alive in a way that made Ulyana uneasy — the aliveness of someone who had seen the bottom of something and come back up carrying it.

"You lied well," Hale said.

"Don't."

"I mean it as an observation, not an insult. Most people lie badly. They over-explain. You under-explained — gave them just enough rope to hang their own suspicion with." Hale tilted her head. "Your father would have been proud. Or horrified. From what I know of Elias Ashgrove, possibly both at once."

Ulyana's jaw tightened. "You keep saying his name like you knew him."

"I read his letters. All of them. There are forty-one pages of them in this case, written over eleven years, addressed to no one, hidden in a hull that hasn't seen daylight since before either of us was born." Hale's voice didn't rise; it never seemed to rise. It simply deepened, the way water deepens over a drop-off. "I know his handwriting better than you do, Ulyana. I've spent more hours with it."

The words landed somewhere beneath her sternum and sat there, heavy and cold as ballast. She looked away, out through the cave mouth at the fog swallowing the tideline, and for a moment she let herself feel the full strangeness of her situation: crouched on a wet rock in a drowned cave with a half-dead stranger who claimed to carry her father's ghost in a sealed case, having just sold a lie to the two men who owned the paper her whole life was written on.

"Why aren't you asking me why?" she said finally. "Why I lied for you. You don't know me. For all you know I'm going to hand you to the Vosses the moment it's convenient."

"Because you could have done it already." Hale said it simply, without drama, the way she seemed to say everything. "You've had me alone and helpless for fourteen hours. You had the case. You had the coordinates. Whatever deal the Voss brothers offered you for delivery, you could have collected it last night and been clear of your ledger by breakfast." She paused. "You didn't. So either you're playing a longer game than I can see, or something in those logs changed your mind about whose side lying serves. Either way, I'm not going to insult you by asking questions whose answers are standing right in front of me."

Ulyana laughed — a short, involuntary sound, startled out of her. "You always talk like a paper?"

"I used to talk like a person. The paper's more honest." Hale shifted the case on her knees. Her hands, Ulyana noticed, had a fine tremor that she was controlling the way a helmsman controls a wheel in heavy seas — constantly, invisibly, at cost. "How long until the tide closes the lower gallery?"

"Forty minutes. Maybe less with this swell coming in. Why?"

"Because there's a section of the log I haven't shown you. Timestamped forty minutes after the team was declared missing." Hale's eyes held hers. "It's the reason I ran instead of staying with the camp. And it's the reason your father's letters matter — because whatever took my team didn't come from the sea, Ulyana. It came from the shore. Men flying no flag, walking out of the fog on a night with no boats on any radar. And in the last entry, thirty seconds of audio before the recorder cut — a voice saying a name."

The fog outside had begun, very slowly, to lift, the way it did before wind changes, peeling back from the water in long gray rags. Through the widening gap Ulyana could see the horizon line sharpen, and far out on it — so far it might have been imagination, might have been a trick of the light on cold air — a shape that was too straight and too dark to be a headland.

"The name," Ulyana said. Her voice came out lower than she intended. "Whose name?"

Hale opened the case.

* * *

Later — much later, after the tide had closed the lower gallery with a sound like a door, after they had climbed out through the blowhole passage the survey team had mapped and stood gasping on the clifftop with the wind rising out of the southeast — Ulyana would try to remember exactly what Hale had said, and find that memory had blurred it, the way water blurs ink. But she would remember the shape of it. She would remember that the voice on the recording had been calm, unhurried, professional, the voice of a man giving instructions rather than threats, and that the name it said was *Ashgrove* — not hers, not her father's, but the older one, the family name, the one carved on the weathered stone in the churchyard above the harbor that she had walked past ten thousand times without reading.

And she would remember that even as Hale's words were still settling over her, even as the wind came up the cliff face carrying salt and the smell of a storm building somewhere beyond the horizon, the radio in the truck crackled to life again, and Tam Voss's cheerful voice filled the cab:

"Change of plans, Ashgrove. My brother found something in the harbor this morning you're going to want to hear about. Get yourself down here — and bring the doctor."

The line went dead before she could answer.

Ulyana stared at the handset. Beside her, Hale had gone very still, her pale eyes fixed on the southern horizon where the dark straight shape had resolved, unmistakably now, into the silhouette of a ship running before the wind — black-hulled, fast, and flying no flag at all.

"They know," Hale said quietly. "Somehow, they already know."
