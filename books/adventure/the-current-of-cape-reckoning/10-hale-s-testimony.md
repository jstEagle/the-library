## 10. Hale's Testimony

The safehouse was a fisherman's shack above the tideline at Graywater Cove, the kind of place that smelled of tar and old rain and had a stove that smoked when it sulked. Imogen Hale had been in worse shelters. She had been in worse shelters that week.

She sat at a table scarred by a century of gutting knives, the survey's portable drive connected to a battered laptop, and told Ulyana Ashgrove everything.

Not because she trusted her — though she was beginning to, which frightened her more than Corvane did. She told her because the story had grown too heavy to carry alone, and because the moment she had seen Ulyana's face in the cave, half-lit by a dying headlamp, she had understood that the story had never been hers alone to carry at all.

"Start at the beginning," Ulyana said. She had wedged herself against the wall where she could see both the door and the salt-fogged window, a habit of a woman who had been hunted before, or who had simply never stopped being hunted. "The camp. The night your team went into the water."

Hale wrapped her hands around a tin mug of tea that had gone cold an hour ago, for the warmth of holding something.

* * *

"We weren't looking for a wreck," she said. "That's the first thing you have to understand. We were mapping sediment drift along the outer shelf — boring work, grant work. The anomaly showed up on the third night. A mass under the seabed off the northern point, roughly two hundred feet down, sitting in what our geologist called 'geologically impossible' stratification. Too old for the sediment around it. Too intact for its age. He ran the sonar four times because he was sure the equipment was lying."

"And it wasn't."

"It wasn't." Hale looked at the laptop's glow, the waveform still frozen on the screen from her last decode. "The hull form didn't match anything in the registry. Not the shape, not the construction. The timber density readings suggested oak, but old oak — older than the colony charts, older than anything the Crown ever floated past this cape. Marcus — our geologist — started joking that we'd found a Viking longship. He stopped joking when the density numbers came back."

Ulyana said nothing. She had the stillness of a woman who had learned that silence pulled more truth out of people than questions did. Hale found she resented it and was grateful for it in equal measure.

"We petitioned for a dive extension. Denied — the denial came back within the hour, which should have been my first warning. Permits don't move that fast unless someone is watching the queue. So we dove anyway. Off the books. Four of us, on the night tide, using the survey sled to light the descent." She paused. The memory had a texture she could never quite sand down: the green-black water, the sled's lamps carving cones of light out of nothing, the way the wreck rose out of the dark like a cathedral that had decided to lie down.

"What did it look like?" Ulyana asked quietly.

"Intact." Hale's voice came out strange. "After — by our best estimate — a hundred and fifty years, maybe more. No rot. No teredo damage. The wood was black as bog oak and hard as iron. And the hull was breached, but not by storm, not by rock. The breach was cut. From the inside."

She let that sit in the shack's damp air.

* * *

"We went in through the cut," she said. "It was a deliberate scuttling channel — a section of hull designed to fail, plumbed with valves. Someone meant to sink that ship. Meant it, engineered it, and went down with it, or meant to and changed their minds, because the marks told us they didn't all drown."

"Marks?"

"Scratches. Scores in the inner planking, down near the hold's lowest deck. Tallies. Names, some of them — carved in hands that got shakier the lower they went. And dates." Hale closed her eyes. "The dates went on for weeks after the sinking. Someone lived in that hull for weeks, in the dark, in the cold, carving the days into the wood so they wouldn't lose count. So they wouldn't be forgotten without knowing how long they'd lasted."

The stove ticked. Outside, the fog pressed against the window like something listening.

"There were personal effects," Hale went on, and now her voice had flattened into the tone she used for lectures, because the other tone would have broken her. "A child's shoe. A rosary. A tin cup with initials — E.M. And in the purser's compartment, sealed in a lead-lined case — the case you're looking at right now, under the table — we found the manifest."

Ulyana glanced down, as if she could see through the wood. "Passenger manifest?"

"Passenger and crew. Two hundred and eleven names. Families — the manifest listed them as families, not individuals. Mothers with children in the same entry. It wasn't a naval vessel. It was a transport. And it was scuttled with its people aboard, or close enough to aboard that it makes no moral difference." Hale opened her eyes. "The manifest wasn't in any language we recognized at first. Old script, mixed registers — but the names transliterated. We spent two nights on it. Two hundred and eleven names, and the shipping line that owned the hull, and the insurance underwriters who paid out on the loss, and the salvage company that bought the wreck site sight unseen a month later."

She reached across the table and turned the laptop toward Ulyana. On the screen, a scanned page: columns of names in faded iron-gall ink, and beside them, in a modern hand — Hale's own — the transliterations.

"Find your name," Hale said.

Ulyana didn't move for a long moment. Then she leaned in, and Hale watched her eyes track down the columns, and watched them stop.

The silence that followed was worse than the storm they'd driven through two nights ago.

"Ashgrove," Ulyana said finally. Her voice was very even, which is how Hale knew it wasn't even at all. "There's an Ashgrove. Two of them. An Ashgrove and — a child. Listed as family." She looked up. "This is a coincidence. Ashgrove isn't a common name, but it's not — this cape is full of old families, half of them came off wrecks or came here to wreck things—"

"Read the entry," Hale said gently. "The one under the names."

Ulyana read. Her lips moved slightly, the way people's do when they're reading something they don't want to be true. "‘Cargo: one family, reclaimed. Passage paid by the estate of—'" She stopped. Started again. "‘Passage paid by the estate of Corvane.'"

* * *

Hale had rehearsed this part. She had rehearsed it in the cave, in the dark, with the tide coming in, because she'd known — from the moment she'd seen Ulyana's surname on the intake paperwork twelve days ago, before any of this, when Ashgrove was just a name on a subcontractor's form — that she would have to say it to someone eventually, and that the someone ought to be her.

"The manifest is a lie in its own ink," she said. "That's the thing I couldn't stop turning over. It calls them cargo. It calls a family with a child *cargo*. And the estate that paid for the passage is the same estate that underwrote the insurance loss, the same estate whose salvage company bought the site. Corvane. It's been Corvane from the beginning. They didn't lose a ship. They drowned one — a family that knew something, or owned something, or simply refused something — and they've been sitting on the site for a century and a half, and the moment our sonar touched it, they moved to bury it again."

"And my father," Ulyana said. Her hands were flat on the table, braced, as if the shack were pitching in a swell. "You said — in the cave, you said he found it first."

Hale nodded slowly. "The case had a false bottom. Under the manifest there were papers from a later hand. Modern paper. A journal, or the start of one. The handwriting matched a name we found in the survey office's historical registry when we pulled every record touching the northern shelf." She took a breath. "Elias Ashgrove. Your father didn't just find the wreck first, Ulyana. He found it, he dove it, he read the manifest, he understood what it meant — and he hid the evidence. He put the papers back in the case and he put the case back where no one would look, and he wrote down what he'd found, and then—"

"Then he drowned," Ulyana said. "Twelve years ago. His boat went down off the Cape. They never found the wreck. They never found him."

"Boats that go down off this cape in fair weather," Hale said quietly, "usually have help."

* * *

Ulyana stood up. Not abruptly — that was the terrible part. She stood the way a structure stands after something has knocked loose its foundations: slowly, testing the load, waiting to find out if she'd hold.

She walked to the window. The fog had thickened toward evening, and the cove below was a gray blankness with the sound of water somewhere inside it. Hale watched her back — the set of her shoulders, the weathered canvas jacket, the way one hand had drifted, without her seeming to notice, to the compass hanging on a cord around her neck. Her father's compass, Hale would learn later. The one thing salvaged from the boat that killed him. Or didn't kill him.

"Twelve years," Ulyana said to the window. "I've driven — I don't even know. Half a million miles? Chasing weather for people who pay late, sleeping in the truck, never signing a lease, never staying anywhere longer than a season. And you know what I told myself? That it was momentum. That I was a woman who moved because moving was how I breathed." She laughed, and it was the worst sound Hale had heard from her yet, worse than the crying in the cave. "I wasn't running toward anything. I was running a search pattern. Twelve years, and I've been flying a search pattern over my father's grave without knowing it, and the whole time the answer was sitting in a hole off the northern point with my name carved in a passenger manifest."

"Elias didn't drown," Hale said. "Or — he may have drowned. I won't pretend to know. But he didn't die the way the record says he died. He died because of what he found. And the journal — the pages we recovered — they end mid-entry. He was still writing when—"

"When they came for him." Ulyana turned from the window. Her eyes were dry and very bright, and Hale recognized the look, because she'd seen it in her own mirror for two weeks now: grief that had burned down past weeping into something harder and more useful. "Corvane. The same family. A hundred and fifty years apart, the same family, drowning the same ship twice."

"The first time with a hundred and eleven people aboard." Hale's voice cracked on the number; she let it. "The second time with one."

* * *

They sat with it a while. The stove smoked and sulked. Somewhere down the cove a gull complained about the fog.

It was Ulyana who spoke first, and when she did, her voice had changed — steadied, leveled, the way the barometer steadies just before a front arrives, which Hale would come to understand was the only kind of calm Ulyana Ashgrove truly trusted.

"The Voss brothers think I'm bringing them coordinates for this wreck," she said. "Coordinates I gave them are false — I pulled them from a decoy entry in your survey logs. That buys us days, maybe. Not weeks. And when the days run out, the salvage fleet comes, and Corvane comes with it, and he's spent a century and a half making sure that manifest stays at the bottom of the sea." She looked at the lead-lined case on the floor between them. "Two hundred and eleven names. My father's handwriting. And a fortune built on all of it."

"Yes," Hale said.

"So the question isn't whether it's true." Ulyana crouched and rested her hand flat on the case's cold lead lid, the way you'd rest a hand on the flank of a sleeping animal. "The question is who gets to still be standing the day it comes out."

Hale looked at her — this woman she'd known four days, this stranger whose name she'd carried like a coal in her pocket since the intake paperwork, this daughter of a drowned man who had maybe not drowned — and felt the last of her isolation break apart like fog under wind.

"There's something else," Hale said. "The journal. The last legible page. Elias wrote a line I've read a hundred times, trying to work out what he meant."

Ulyana looked up.

"He wrote: *‘If anything happens to me, the case goes to whoever the sea sends. It always sends someone. It sent me.'*" Hale held her gaze. "Ulyana. The sea sent you."

For a moment the shack was perfectly still, the fog holding its breath against the glass.

Then, out past the cove's mouth, low and long through the gray, came the sound of a ship's horn — one blast, deep as a struck bell, and then, after a pause, another. Not a fishing boat's signal. Not a Coast Guard pattern. Three black hulls, Hale would have bet her career on it, standing in off the Cape, patient as arithmetic.

The fleet had stopped believing the false coordinates.

Ulyana was already on her feet, pulling the case up by its handle, her father's compass swinging against her chest like a second heart.

"They're here," she said. "Get your boots on, Doctor. The tide won't wait and neither will he."
