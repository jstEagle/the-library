## 11. Storm Window

The barometer had been falling all morning, and by noon it fell the way a man falls when he stops pretending he's only sitting down.

Ulyana Ashgrove stood in the lee of the harbormaster's shed at Cape Reckoning with her jacket zipped to the chin and her eyes on the horizon, and she read the sky the way other people read letters from home — slowly, hungrily, line by line. The cloud deck out past the headland had organized itself into a long gray shelf, flat-bottomed and bruise-dark along its leading edge, and beneath that shelf the sea had gone a strange glassy green, the color of bottle bottoms, the color of held breath. The wind had backed around to the northeast in the last hour. It came off the water now with a wet iron smell that meant rain was not coming so much as already here, just waiting for permission.

She'd been chasing weather for twelve years. She knew its grammar.

And this storm, she thought, was going to be a sermon.

"You've got that look," said Tam Osgood, the harbormaster, from the doorway of his shed. He was seventy if he was a day, built like driftwood, and he'd been watching her watch the water for the better part of an hour without saying anything about it, which was its own kind of courtesy. "That look you get before you do something I'll have to write down in the log."

"Northeast gale by tonight," Ulyana said. "Maybe stronger. That shelf cloud isn't a squall line, Tam. It's the front edge of something with a low center sitting maybe two hundred miles offshore, deepening as it comes. Look at the swell period." She nodded at the harbor mouth, where the swells were rolling in long and slow and unnervingly tall for a windless hour, each one lifting the moored dinghies like a slow hand turning them over. "Fourteen seconds, maybe fifteen. Swell like that doesn't come from local wind. It comes from a big system a long way away, sending its mail ahead of itself."

Tam squinted at the horizon with the practiced indifference of a man who had been lied to by the sea often enough to trust nothing but his own knees, which ached before every blow. "Knees say tomorrow night," he said. "Early."

"Your knees are smarter than the buoy network." Ulyana pulled her notebook from her jacket — a fat, oil-stained thing held shut with a rubber band — and flipped it open against her thigh. She'd been sketching pressure readings since dawn, off the harbor barometer, off her truck's instruments, off the way the smoke from Osgood's chimney had gone flat and fast to the south. The numbers made a curve she didn't like and did respect: steep, steady, no stutters. A storm that fell into its own strength instead of fighting it. "This is going to come in hard and fast and then sit on us like a debt collector. Twelve hours of it, maybe fourteen. Gale force, gusting storm. Seas over the bar at twenty feet, twenty-five in the sets."

"And you're smiling about it."

"I'm not smiling." She realized she was smiling. She put it away. "I'm doing arithmetic."

* * *

The arithmetic went like this.

Corvane's fleet — three black-hulled vessels riding at anchor two miles off the Cape, lights burning through the fog like a private constellation — could work the wreck site only in weather that let their divers down and kept their crane barges stable. Ulyana had watched them for two days now, from the cliff road, from the dunes, from the high ground above the drowned cave where Imogen Hale lay hidden and healing, wrapped in every blanket Ulyana owned. The fleet worked with machine patience. Divers down at slack water, up before the turn. Survey launches running grid lines back and forth over the shoal where the old wreck slept under sand and tide. A pattern so disciplined it looked less like searching than like mowing.

They hadn't found the hold yet. Hale was sure of it — sure enough that her voice, hoarse and feverish as it still was, went hard whenever Ulyana raised the possibility. *The void reads wrong on sonar,* she'd said, propped against the cave wall with the sealed case between her boots like a dog that bit strangers. *It reads as rock unless you know what you're looking at. Corvane's people have good gear but they don't have my team's data, because my team is dead or scattered and my data is in this case.* Then she'd coughed for a long time, and Ulyana had held a canteen to her lips, and neither of them had said what they were both thinking: that the data wouldn't stay secret forever, and that the fleet's grid lines were tightening day by day like a net being hauled.

But the storm changed everything. Storms always changed everything. That was the first law of the coast, older than charts, older than wrecks.

Because a storm like this one would ground the fleet. Not inconvenience it — *ground* it. No dive master alive put divers in the water ahead of a northeast gale on an open coast. No crane barge held station in twenty-foot seas. The black ships would either run for shelter down the coast or ride it out at anchor dragging their hearts out, and either way, for the duration of the blow and the confused hours after it, the shoal would belong to nobody.

To nobody. Which meant it would belong to whoever was crazy enough to be there.

Ulyana turned the thought over, the way she'd turn a stone on the beach, looking at what lived underneath.

There was a window in every storm. Her father had taught her that, years ago, years before his boat went down — standing on the causeway with his hand on her shoulder and his compass open in his palm, pointing out how the wind veered as a low passed, how the seas ran highest from the northeast and then, as the center crossed, went briefly, treacherously calm. *That calm isn't mercy, Ulya,* he'd said. *It's the eye. It's the storm taking a breath. And a breath is exactly long enough to drown you if you mistake it for peace.* But a breath was also exactly long enough to cross a bar, if you knew your timing, if you respected the arithmetic, if you didn't ask the window to be wider than it was.

The wreck sat exposed — truly exposed, hull timbers rising from the sand like the ribs of something that had once breathed — only at the lowest spring tides, and only in the stretch of coast below the cliffs where the surge scoured the covering sand away. Hale's charts said the next proper low water came in thirty-six hours. The storm's center, by Ulyana's numbers, would pass overhead almost exactly then.

Which meant the lowest, most exposed tide of the week would fall inside the eye of a gale.

It was such an absurd piece of luck that she laughed out loud, alone on the harbor road, and a gull lifted off the pilings and gave her a look of professional offense.

* * *

She drove up to the cave in the late afternoon, taking the back track through the pines where the fleet's watchers on the coast road couldn't see the truck, and walked the last half mile with her pack bouncing against her spine and the first spit of rain arriving in fat, warm, wrong-feeling drops. The wind had begun to talk in the treetops — not the steady shove of a settled blow yet, but the restless muttering of something gathering its notes.

Hale was sitting up when Ulyana ducked into the cave mouth. That was new. Two days ago the surveyor had been gray-lipped and shivering, her broken ribs strapped, her hands too unsteady to hold a cup. Now she had her back against the stone with the sealed case open beside her and pages of decoded data spread on a flattened carton, weighted with pebbles, and she was reading them with the fierce concentration of a woman who had decided that if the world intended to kill her, it would have to do it while she was busy.

"You're wet," Hale said, without looking up.

"It's starting." Ulyana shrugged out of her pack and crouched by the little spirit stove, setting water on to boil more for the ritual of it than any need. "Real one, this time. Not the pretenders we've had all month. This is a proper Cape Reckoning gale, the kind they name after widows."

Hale looked up at that. Her face was still thin, still marked with the ordeal of whatever had happened to her team out on that tideline, but her eyes had come back from wherever they'd gone. "How long?"

"Twelve hours of the worst of it, starting tonight. Center passes over us or close to it tomorrow evening." Ulyana took out her notebook and flattened it on the stone between them, turning it so Hale could read her cramped columns of figures, the pressure curve, the swell timings, the tide table she'd copied out and annotated in red. "And here's the part I keep checking because I don't believe it. Lowest water tomorrow is at 6:40 in the evening. Spring low, the biggest drawdown of the cycle. The wreck will be out of the sand."

Hale went very still. Outside, the wind found its voice properly for the first time, a long rising note in the cliff grass, and neither of them moved until it settled into its rhythm.

"The eye," Hale said quietly.

"The eye, or near enough. The worst of the wind backs around as the center crosses. There'll be a lull — an hour, maybe ninety minutes, I won't promise more — and in that lull the tide will be at its bottom and the fleet will be hove to or run for cover, and—" Ulyana stopped herself. She'd learned long ago never to finish a sentence that sounded too much like hope. Hope on the water got people killed; you stated the conditions and let the conditions speak. "In that lull, the wreck will be accessible, and we will be the only people on this coast crazy enough to be standing on it."

"That's not a plan, Ashgrove. That's a suicide note with a tide table attached."

"It's both. Most good plans are." Ulyana fed the stove another twig and watched the flame take it. "I need you to understand the arithmetic before you agree to anything, because I'm not dragging a woman with cracked ribs down a cliff in a hurricane out of sentiment. Here's what we get: one tide. One exposure window. If we reach the hold and find what you think is there, we take it and we're back up the cliff path before the eye closes. If we miss the window — if the lull runs short, if the surge comes early, if anything goes wrong that we haven't imagined — the sea takes the decision out of our hands, permanently, and probably takes us with it."

"And if we don't go?"

Ulyana looked at her. Rain had started in earnest now, drumming on the rocks outside the cave mouth, and the light was failing into that particular green-gray dusk that preceded a big blow, the whole world dimming like a lamp being carried away down a corridor.

"If we don't go, the fleet finds the hold within the week. Their grid lines are tightening. You said it yourself. And whatever Corvane wants out of that ship badly enough to kill a survey team for it — he gets it, and everyone who ever had a name on that manifest stays drowned twice." She paused. "Including mine."

Hale was quiet for a long moment. Then she reached over and turned Ulyana's notebook toward herself, studying the pressure curve with a surveyor's eye, and Ulyana watched her check the arithmetic — really check it, digit by digit, the way she'd checked everything since the cave, as though rigor were the only rope left holding her to the world.

"The barometric gradient supports your timing," Hale said at last, grudgingly, like a scientist conceding a point to a truck driver. "Your swell-period estimate is crude but defensible. The tide prediction assumes the surge doesn't skew the actual water level, which it will — a northeast gale piles water onto this coast, so the real low might be shallower than predicted, the wreck less exposed—"

"So I'll plan for eighteen inches less drawdown. I've landed trucks on beaches with worse margins."

"—and the eye passage over a coastline this ragged can produce secondary wind maxima behind the lull, meaning your return climb might happen in worse conditions than your descent." Hale set the notebook down. She looked at Ulyana across the small flame of the stove, and something in her expression shifted — not softening exactly, but settling, the way sediment settles in a jar. "You've done this before. Read a storm and bet a life on the reading."

"My whole life is reading storms and betting lives on the reading. Mostly other people's, lately. Mine comes cheap." Ulyana managed half a smile. "My father taught me. He used to say the coast gives you one honest window in every blow, and the trick isn't finding it. The trick is being small enough and quick enough and honest enough with yourself to fit inside it."

"Was he a chaser? Like you?"

"He was a fisherman who liked knowing things. Same disease." Ulyana looked out at the rain-hammered dark beyond the cave mouth, where the sea was building itself into long gray walls. "He'd have loved this storm. He'd have hated what I'm planning to do in it. Both things at once — that was him entirely."

Hale gathered her scattered pages together with careful, deliberate movements, squared them, and slid them back into the case. When she spoke again her voice had the flat steadiness of a decision already made, the voice of a woman who had survived forty minutes that should have killed her and had stopped negotiating with fear somewhere in the middle of them.

"Then we go at the low water tomorrow. We go down the survey team's passage route — I mapped it, I know the ledges, and at low water the lower section drains enough to make the descent possible, barely. We take the eastern approach so the cliff shadows us from the fleet's anchorage. And we carry nothing we can't drop and run with." She held Ulyana's gaze. "One tide, Ashgrove. Your window or the sea's. Agreed?"

"Agreed." Ulyana put out the stove, and the dark came in close around them, friendly now, conspiratorial. Outside, the storm was arriving in earnest — she could hear it reorganizing itself offshore, feel the pressure dropping against her eardrums like a hand pressing gently on the world, and underneath the noise of it, faint and far out, the black ships' anchor lights would be swinging in long arcs against the building sea, their masters reading the same sky and reaching, for once, the same conclusion she had: *no one works this coast tomorrow.*

No one, she thought, except two women with a tide table and a grudge.

She lay back against her pack and listened to the gale come ashore, and for the first time in twelve years the miles ahead of her felt finite — one cliff, one lull, one breath of the storm, measured not in distance but in minutes, ticking down like the last grains in a glass.

At dawn, the fleet weighed anchor. Two of the black ships ran south for shelter, lights winking out one by one.

The third stayed on station, riding to the storm, and began lowering something small and dark into the water.
