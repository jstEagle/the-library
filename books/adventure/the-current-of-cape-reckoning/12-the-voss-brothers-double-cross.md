## 12. The Voss Brothers Double-Cross

The younger Voss brother's name was Denny, and he had learned long ago that arithmetic was the only honest language in the world. Everything else — love, loyalty, weather, the sea — was just noise with a good sales pitch. Numbers didn't lie. Numbers just waited for you to catch up to them.

He caught up to them on a Tuesday, in the back office of the Harborside Fuel & Bait, with a grease pencil, a nautical chart, and a pot of coffee that had gone bitter as regret.

"Look at it," he said.

His older brother, Marten, leaned over the chart with the unhurried patience of a man who had once held a struggling man's head under water just to see if he'd stop struggling. He had. The man had. That was the trouble with most people — they stopped. Ulyana Ashgrove, Denny was beginning to suspect, was not most people.

Marten's finger traced the coordinates she'd handed over two days earlier, the ones she'd sworn came straight out of the survey team's logs, the ones she'd delivered with that particular set to her jaw she got when she was telling the truth or something close enough to it that the difference didn't matter. The position sat twelve miles southwest of the Cape, out past the shelf, in water deep enough to drown a cathedral.

"Twelve miles," Marten said.

"Twelve miles," Denny agreed.

"Southwest."

"Southwest."

Marten straightened up and looked out the salt-hazed window at the gray water beyond the breakwater, where the salvage fleet's three black hulls rode at anchor like crows on a fence. "And the survey logs put their last instrument drop where?"

Denny didn't need to check. He'd checked eleven times. "Northeast. Four miles off the point. In sixty feet of water."

The silence that followed had a texture to it, the way fog has a texture. Denny poured coffee he didn't want, just to have something to do with his hands. The chart between them showed the Cape Reckoning coastline in all its treacherous detail — the causeway, the drowned cave country below the old light, the shelf dropping away into cold black nothing — and somewhere on that paper, in grease pencil, was the shape of the lie they'd been sold.

"She played us," Marten said. There was no anger in it. That was the frightening part. Marten's anger was a quick hot thing, over in a moment, like a match. This was the other thing, the quiet thing, the thing that made men sign papers they shouldn't and disappear boats they shouldn't.

"She played us," Denny said, because there was no point pretending otherwise. "Fed us coordinates off a dead drop. Somewhere she wanted us looking while she looked somewhere else."

"You admire her."

It wasn't a question, and Denny didn't insult his brother by denying it. "I do a little. Doesn't change the arithmetic."

"No," Marten said. "It never does."

* * *

The arithmetic went like this, and Denny laid it out for himself that night in the office with the lamp turned low, because he was a man who liked to see his problems written down where they couldn't shift shape in the dark.

Item one: they had contracted Ulyana Ashgrove to recover a sealed case from an uncharted wreck before Corvane's salvage fleet found it. She had taken the job because her ledger with the Voss interests was a hole with no bottom — the truck, the gear, the advances, the interest compounding like mold in a wet wall. She had taken the job because she had nothing else.

Item two: she had given them false coordinates. Which meant she'd found something — or someone — worth lying for. Which meant she was ahead of them, and ahead of the fleet, and had been for days.

Item three: if Corvane learned she'd played the Voss brothers, the Voss brothers became a liability. Corvane did not carry liabilities. Corvane amortized them. There was a fisherman out of Port Ellery who could explain the terminology, except nobody could explain anything about him anymore because he'd gone out on a clear night two winters back and the sea had kept him.

Item four — and this was the item Denny kept circling, the way a tongue keeps finding the chipped tooth — item four was that the Voss brothers' exposure ran deeper than one bad contract. There were the manifests they'd signed for Corvane's coastal shipments, cargo they'd never opened, bills of lading with weights that didn't match drafts. There were the names they'd passed along over the years, freelancers and fishermen and one surveyor's assistant, people who'd asked questions and needed to be introduced to the right silence. If Corvane went down — and there were federal people sniffing at the Cape now, Denny could smell them the way you smelled a coming front — the Voss brothers went down attached, like remoras on a shark when the shark is harpooned.

So the arithmetic wasn't really about Ulyana at all. Ulyana was just the number that made the equation balance.

"You're awake," Marten said from the doorway at some black hour, and Denny realized he'd been staring at the same line of the chart for forty minutes.

"Thinking."

"Don't hurt yourself." Marten came in, sat on the edge of the desk, and helped himself to the cold coffee without asking, the way he'd been doing since they were boys sharing a bunk in their uncle's trawler. "You've got the look."

"What look?"

"The look you get when you've already done the sum and you're just working up the nerve to say it out loud."

Denny turned the lamp toward his brother's face. Forty-one years old, forearms like dock line, a face the weather had been editing for decades. People thought Marten was the dangerous one because he was the big one, the quiet one, the one who'd held a man under water. But Denny knew better. Marten wasn't dangerous. Marten was *reliable*, which in their line of work was the same thing with better manners.

"She's got the case," Denny said. "Or she knows who does. And she's not running with it — she's sitting on it. There's a storm coming in off the shelf, a real one, and she's a weather woman to the bone. She's waiting for a window."

Marten drank his coffee. "Window for what?"

"That's the part I don't have yet. But it doesn't matter. What matters is what we do before Corvane does his own arithmetic."

"And what do we do?"

Denny looked at the chart. Twelve miles southwest, the grease-pencil cross she'd given them, sitting in empty water like a headstone for their credibility. Four miles northeast, the real ground. And anchored in between, three black hulls and the man who owned them.

"We sell her," Denny said. "But not the way you think."

* * *

Corvane received them on the flagship — the *Meridian Grace*, a hundred and forty feet of black steel that had been a research vessel in a previous life and was now something with no honest name — and he received them the way he received everyone: alone, in a cabin that smelled of old paper and machine oil, with a pot of tea he poured himself and never drank.

He was smaller than men expected. That was part of it, Denny understood. People came aboard braced for a monster and got a tidy gray-haired man in a cardigan with reading glasses pushed up on his forehead, and by the time they'd adjusted their expectations, he'd already adjusted everything else.

"The Ashgrove woman," Corvane said, before they'd finished sitting down. "Tell me about her."

Denny had rehearsed this. He'd rehearsed it in the truck on the way out, rehearsed it on the gangway, rehearsed it against the sound of the water slapping the hull. But rehearsal, he'd learned, only carried you up to the moment you looked into Corvane's eyes — pale, patient, unhurried as tide — and understood that whatever you said next was going to be weighed to the gram.

"She's good," Denny said. "Best tracker on this coast. She gave us false coordinates off the survey logs. She's found the wreck, or she's close. And she's waiting on the storm."

Corvane's face did something very small, very controlled. Interest, maybe. Or the memory of interest. "False coordinates," he repeated. "She deceived you."

"She did."

"And you came to tell me this." Corvane set down the teapot. "Why?"

Here it was. The moment the whole equation hung on. Denny felt Marten shift beside him — half an inch, no more — and knew his brother had arrived at the same instant he had: there was no version of this conversation where they kept their hands clean. There was only the version where they kept their hands.

"Because we'd rather be your instrument than your problem," Denny said. "You're going to find her either way. The fleet, the watchers on the coast road, the man you've got in the harbormaster's office — she can't hide on a coastline this small, not from you. The only question is whether you find her through us, or find out later that we knew and didn't say. One of those makes us useful. The other makes us ballast."

Corvane smiled. It was the worst thing Denny had seen all week, that smile — warm as a stove in a cold room, and about as safe to lean against.

"Ballast," Corvane said, tasting the word. "You've a poet's instincts, Denny Voss."

"I've a bookkeeper's instincts."

"Even better." Corvane took off his reading glasses and folded them with a click, and Denny understood that the negotiation had just ended, though nothing had been agreed to yet. "Tell me what you want."

"Clean paper," Marten said, speaking for the first time, his voice like a hatch closing. "Everything. The manifests, the shipments, the names. Whatever the federal people find when they come — and they're coming, Mr. Corvane, we can all smell it — none of it touches us. We were your harbor agents. We took fees. We didn't ask what was in the crates. That's the story, and we need it to be true when they check."

The cabin was quiet. Out on the water, a gull cried once and thought better of it.

"You're asking me to burn my own ledgers," Corvane said.

"We're asking you to burn the pages with our names," Denny said. "Keep the rest. You'll want the rest."

Another silence, longer. Corvane rose, went to the porthole, and looked out at his fleet the way other men looked at family photographs. When he turned back, his face had settled into the expression Denny would remember for the rest of his life, however long the rest of it turned out to be: the expression of a man who has just found the exact figure he needed at the bottom of a column.

"And the Ashgrove woman," Corvane said. "And the case. And the surveyor, if she's alive — and I believe she is, because dead women don't feed false coordinates to their creditors. You'll deliver them."

"The woman and the doctor, yes." Denny heard his own voice go flat and careful, a man walking a beam over a drop. "The case, we never saw. The case was always yours."

"Of course it was." Corvane smiled again, and poured three cups of tea this time, and Denny understood they'd just been paid — in the only currency Corvane dealt in, which was the promise of continued existence. "The storm gives us one window, as your weather woman well knows. She'll move on the wreck at slack water in the eye, or she'll not move at all. When she moves, you'll be watching. And you'll call me."

"We'll call you," Marten said.

"One more thing." Corvane came around the desk, and up close he smelled of nothing at all, which struck Denny as the most unnatural thing about him. "Her father's boat. The *Kindly Tide*. She'll go back to it, sooner or later. They always go back to the water that took them. When she does — be gentle with her, if you can. I have a use for sentiment. It makes people predictable."

* * *

They took the launch back to the harbor in a rising wind, the first long gray fingers of the storm already reaching over the shelf to the west, and neither brother spoke until the breakwater swallowed them and the harbor's dead water took the hull.

Then Marten killed the engine and sat with his hands on the wheel, and Denny watched the working of his brother's jaw and knew what was coming.

"You did the sum," Marten said. "Say the answer."

Denny looked out at the harbor. The chase truck was parked where they'd told her to leave it, down by the fuel dock, her whole life in a rusted cab — the antennas, the anemometers, the coffee cans of bolts, the photograph of a boat she kept taped to the dash that they'd both seen and never mentioned. Somewhere out in that weather, right now, she was moving, because the storm window was opening and she was a weather woman to the bone, and she had no idea that the two men she'd lied to had just sold the lie back to the man who owned them all.

"The answer's ugly," Denny said.

"It usually is."

"We wipe our names, and she pays for the ink." He said it the way he said all his sums: flat, exact, unarguable. "That's the answer. That's always been the answer. You want me to dress it up?"

Marten was quiet a long moment. The wind worried at the rigging of the boats around them, a low moan like the harbor clearing its throat.

"No," he said at last, and started the engine. "But I'll tell you this much, little brother. She gave us false coordinates because she's protecting something. People who lie to protect something are dangerous in a way we're not. We lie to protect ourselves. There's a difference, and it's going to matter before this is done."

"Arithmetic doesn't care about differences."

"No," Marten agreed, and nosed the launch toward the dock. "But storms do."

Denny climbed out onto the fuel dock and stood a moment in the strengthening wind, looking down the coast road toward the Cape, where the fog was beginning to move in off the water in long gray banners, and where — if his sums were right, and his sums were always right — Ulyana Ashgrove was already running.

He took out his phone. Corvane's number sat in it like a stone in a shoe.

The first drops of rain came down the wind, fat and cold, and Denny Voss, who had never in his life made a call he wasn't paid to make, stood in the weather and dialed.
