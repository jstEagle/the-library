## 13. Run Before the Wind

The knock came at four in the morning, three quick raps and then a pause, like a man who wanted to be heard but not remembered.

Ulyana was already awake. She'd been awake since two, lying in the cab of the chase truck with her boots on, listening to the wind change its mind out over the water. Hale slept badly in the passenger seat, her broken ribs strapped with tape from a first-aid kit Ulyana had bought with the last of her cash, and every hour or so she'd surface from sleep with a small sound like a bird being stepped on.

So when the knocking came, Ulyana slid the window down an inch and said, "Who's there," in a voice that meant *answer carefully*.

"Petra Nilsen." A woman's voice, low and rough as rope. "Harbormaster. We met in March, when you brought the Aldous in for survey work."

Ulyana hadn't brought any boat called the Aldous anywhere. But she remembered Petra Nilsen — a broad-shouldered woman with salt-gray hair who had once, without being asked, told her which dockmaster to avoid and which fuel pump ran honest. She opened the door.

Nilsen stood in the sodium light of the marina lot with her collar up and rain just beginning to spot her shoulders. She didn't come closer. That was its own kind of message.

"You've got company coming," she said. "Two trucks, maybe forty minutes out. Black Suburbans, government plates that aren't government. And Corvane's people have been buying diesel all night — not for the fleet, for vehicles. Somebody's planning to drive the coast road tonight."

Ulyana felt it land in her chest like a dropped anchor. "How do you know?"

"Because I sold them some of it." Nilsen's jaw worked. "And because twenty years on this harbor teaches you what fear looks like when it's organized." She glanced toward the truck, toward the shape of Hale curled under a blanket in the passenger seat. "That's Dr. Hale. The missing surveyor."

"She's not missing anymore."

"No," Nilsen said. "She's a liability now. So are you." She reached into her coat and pulled out a folded chart, creased into eighths, worn soft at the folds like cloth. She held it out. "Old coast road. The one they stopped maintaining after the flood year. It runs behind the dunes from here to Miller's Reach, and it doesn't show on anything newer than this. If you take the highway, they'll have it blocked by dawn. If you take the shore road, they'll see your lights against the water for ten miles."

"So I drive dark."

"I'd drive dark," Nilsen said. "And I'd drive now." She turned to go, then stopped, half-facing the rain. "Your father fueled here too, you know. Years back. He always paid cash and he always thanked me like I'd done him a favor." Her voice caught on something and she smoothed it flat. "Go on. Get her somewhere the storm can't find her easy."

Then she walked away into the wet dark, and Ulyana understood that whatever this cost Nilsen, the harbormaster had decided it was worth paying, and had decided not to discuss it.

Ulyana got behind the wheel. The engine turned over on the second try, coughed, caught. Beside her, Hale came up out of sleep like a diver surfacing — fast, silent, eyes wide and already cataloguing.

"What is it?" Hale asked.

"We're leaving."

Hale looked at the rain starting to stipple the windshield, then at the black line of the sea beyond the breakwater, and Ulyana watched her do the arithmetic — the same arithmetic Ulyana had been doing since the cave, since the false coordinates, since the moment she'd understood that the Voss brothers' silence had been purchased too cheaply to last.

"The fleet?" Hale said.

"Fueling vehicles. Two trucks on their way here." Ulyana dropped the truck into gear. "Somebody talked."

Hale was quiet for a moment. Then: "It wasn't me."

"I know it wasn't you."

"It could have been. In your position, I'd suspect—"

"Hale." Ulyana pulled out of the lot, headlights off, running on the faint gray suggestion of the harbor road. "I fed fake coordinates to men who hold my debt. You're the only person on this coast who hasn't tried to sell me yet. Buckle up."

*

The old coast road began where the new one gave up — a gap in the dune scrub barely wider than the truck, marked by nothing but a leaning post with no sign on it. Ulyana found it by feel and by memory of the chart, which she'd unfolded across the steering wheel at the last stoplight and memorized in ninety seconds, the way she'd learned to read weather maps in the years when reading them fast meant eating.

Sand hissed under the tires. The track climbed the spine of the foredune and then ran along behind it, and suddenly there was the whole ocean on her left, visible over the crest in flashes — white water, black water, the leading edge of the storm dragging itself ashore like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

The barometer in the cab had been falling all evening. It was falling faster now, and falling fast was a language, and the language said: *the real blow is still six hours out, but the front edge will reach you inside two.* Wind gusted from the southeast, shoving the truck sideways on the sand, and Ulyana corrected with both hands and thought about the window. One tide. Maybe. If the storm grounded the fleet the way it should, if the eye passed over the Cape at low water, if the wreck exposed itself the way it had in the survey sonar —

If. The whole plan was built on ifs stacked like cards.

Beside her, Hale had braced her taped ribs against the door and was watching the mirrors.

"Headlights," she said.

Ulyana checked. Nothing. Dune shadow, rain, the gray churn of surf.

"Where?"

"Behind us. High up. They're on the new road — we can see them when the track crests." Hale's voice was steady, clinical, the voice of a woman reporting instrument readings. "Two sets. Gaining when we're in the dips."

"They can't follow onto this track in anything heavy."

"They don't need to. They need to know where we exit." Hale turned. "There are three exits before Miller's Reach. If they parallel us the whole way, they'll be waiting at whichever one we choose."

Ulyana drove. Rain hit the windshield in fat, wind-flung slaps. The wipers couldn't keep pace; she ran them at full and still the world came at her in smeared strokes, dune grass whipping, the track dissolving ahead into a wet gleam that might be sand or might be soft enough to swallow a tire to the axle.

"Lights again," Hale said. "Closer."

"Then we stop being predictable." Ulyana killed the wipers. In the sudden blind smear she slowed, counted seconds, and took a cut she'd clocked on the chart — a fisherman's track angling down through the dunes toward a stilted shack and a rusted skiff upside down on blocks. The truck lurched, bottomed out, climbed. Sand sprayed. Behind them, on the higher road, the headlights swept past the gap without slowing, twin eyes fixed on the coast ahead, hunting a truck that was no longer there.

They sat in the lee of the shack for four minutes with the engine idling and the rain drumming the roof like applause from a very large, indifferent audience.

"That," Hale said finally, "was either brilliant or insane."

"On this coast those are usually the same thing." Ulyana eased back onto the track, but lower now, threading between the dune lines where the headlights of anyone on the shore road would pass above and beyond them. "We run the troughs. It's slower. It's also invisible."

For a while neither spoke. The storm grew around them the way weather does — not arriving so much as thickening, the wind finding new harmonics, the rain shifting from slaps to long driving sheets. Ulyana drove with her whole body, feeling the truck's weight shift through the wheel, correcting before the slides started, and part of her — the part that had spent twelve years outrunning things — noted with cold professional pleasure that this was what she was for. This exact thing. Bad road, worse light, everything chasing.

The other part kept seeing the Voss brothers' faces when she'd handed them the false coordinates. The younger one's smile, easy as a handshake. *Six tides, Ashgrove. Bring us the case and your ledger burns.* She'd lied to them, and the lie had bought her a day, and the day was almost spent, and lies to men like that didn't expire quietly.

"You're thinking about them," Hale said. Not a question.

"I'm thinking about how fast charm turns into arithmetic." Ulyana took a bend hard, tires skating, caught it. "The Vosses don't forgive. They itemize."

"And Corvane?"

"Corvane I haven't figured out yet. That's what scares me. The Vosses want money. Money has a shape. You can dodge a shape." She glanced at Hale — pale, bruised, hands folded carefully over her ribs like a woman holding her own cage shut. "Corvane wants something he's been patient about for longer than I've been alive. You don't dodge patience. You outrun it or you drown."

Ahead, the trough track ended at a washout — a creek swollen with rainwater cutting through the dunes in a brown torrent. Ulyana stopped, gauged it, and hated it. Too deep to ford slow, shallow enough to chance fast.

"Hold onto something that loves you," she said, and drove into it.

Water hit the truck like a hand. The engine screamed, the current shoved the tail sideways, and for one long second they were a boat, and then the wheels bit gravel on the far side and they were a truck again, climbing, streaming, alive. Hale let out a breath that sounded like it had been held since the cave.

"Your father," Hale said, abruptly. "Did he drive like this?"

"He drove boats like this." Something tightened in Ulyana's chest. "Everyone says he drowned careful. I never believed it. Careful men don't end up where he did."

"No," Hale said softly. "Careful men don't end up in wrecks that predate their own country's charts." She looked out at the dark. "He didn't drown, Ulyana. Whatever else is true, that isn't. I've seen the marks he left. Men who drown don't leave messages for people they hope will find them."

Ulyana gripped the wheel and said nothing, because if she opened her mouth she didn't know what would come out — grief twelve years old, or rage twelve minutes old, or something that was both and had no name. She drove.

*

Miller's Reach was a gas station, a shuttered diner, and a boat ramp, arranged along a curve of shore like afterthoughts. The chart showed the old road rejoining the highway a mile past it, and the highway was the risk: open, lit, watched. But the dune tracks were ending — the storm had already eaten sections, and the next headland funneled everyone, hunter and hunted alike, onto the same strip of asphalt.

Ulyana parked in the shadow of the diner's back wall and left the engine running.

"Five minutes," she said. "I need fuel and I need to look at the sky."

The gas station's pump light buzzed in the rain. An old man in a slicker watched her fill the tank without asking questions, which on this coast was its own dialect of kindness. She paid cash, bought coffee that tasted like hot salt, and stood under the awning reading the horizon.

The storm had organized itself while they drove. Out past the headland, lightning was working the cloud base in long silent pulses, like something signaling in a language she'd spent her life learning. The pressure was in freefall. The squall line would make landfall within the hour, and behind it — if the models in her head were honest — a lull. An eye. Low water in the eye, right at the Cape, right on schedule with the tide tables she'd memorized until they were scripture.

One window. One tide.

When she got back to the truck, Hale was sitting bolt upright, staring into the side mirror.

"Ulyana." Her voice had gone flat and precise, the way it went flat when she was frightened and refusing to perform it. "Highway. Northbound. Three vehicles, running dark, no headlights — I can see them when the lightning fires."

Ulyana looked. Lightning strobed. For a quarter-second the highway north of the Reach was a photograph: wet asphalt, dunes, and three black shapes moving in tight formation, closing.

"They knew the Reach," Ulyana said. "They didn't follow the dunes. They just went where we had to come out."

"Can we go back?"

"Back is four hours of washed-out track and they know that too." Ulyana dropped into the driver's seat and shut the door, and the sound of it was very loud and very final. "South, then. Past the headland. There's a coast guard access road on the far side — Nilsen's chart shows it — and from there it's back roads to the Cape."

"And if they're behind us the whole way?"

Ulyana started the truck. Pulled out. Turned south onto the last open mile of the old road before the headland swallowed it.

"Then we make sure," she said, "that the storm is chasing us harder than they are."

She put the hammer down. The truck surged, hydroplaning briefly, biting, surging again, and the headland rose ahead of them black against the lightning like the shoulder of something rising from the sea. Behind them, three pairs of headlights snapped on at once — done hiding, done being patient — and bloomed in the mirror like three small furious suns.

Hale braced her ribs and did not cry out when the truck took the headland curve at a speed that lifted the inside wheels. Ulyana drove into the wind, into the rain, into the narrowing dark between the cliff and the sea, and the storm's leading edge came down on all of them together like a curtain falling on the first act.

In the mirror, the nearest set of headlights was gaining.

And ahead, just visible in a lightning flash, a vehicle sat angled across the coast guard access road — engine running, one door open, someone standing beside it in the rain, waiting.

Waiting for them.
