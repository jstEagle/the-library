## 15. Her Father's Handwriting

The hold smelled of salt and old iron and something older still — the cold breath of a ship that had been waiting a hundred years to be found. Imogen Hale crouched beneath the low curve of the ceiling planking, her headlamp beam trembling across the darkness, and told herself the shaking in her hands was the storm's vibration coming up through the hull of the Cape itself.

It was not the storm.

It was the box.

It sat wedged in the shadow of a fallen rib of oak, a sea chest no larger than a bread crate, its brass corners green with age, its lock long since surrendered to rust. Around it, the hold of the wreck spread out like the ribcage of something enormous and patient. The records they had come for — Corvane's records, the manifest, the scuttling papers — lay stacked in oilcloth bundles where Ulyana had uncovered them an hour before, in the narrow window of the storm's eye when the sea had drawn back like a held breath.

But the chest was different. The chest had a name burned into its lid.

*E. ASHGROVE.*

Imogen had seen that handwriting before. She had spent three years of her life reading it — in margins, in field notes, in the cramped, slanting script of a man who wrote the way he walked, fast and certain, as if the page might be taken from him at any moment.

Edmund Ashgrove had been her doctoral supervisor. He had been the finest marine surveyor she had ever known. And he had been dead for twelve years, drowned off Cape Reckoning when his boat *Kestrel* went down in a squall that the coast guard had never fully explained.

She reached out and touched the lid.

"Imogen." Ulyana's voice came from the far side of the hold, low and strained. "The water's turning. We have minutes, not hours. Tell me you've got the manifest."

Imogen did not answer. Her fingers had found the seam of the chest, and it opened — not with the groan of a century of corrosion, but with the soft, oiled whisper of a hinge that had been maintained. Recently maintained. Within the last decade, her archaeologist's eye insisted. Within the last twelve years.

Inside, wrapped in waxed canvas that had been replaced at least twice, lay a journal.

She lifted it out with both hands, the way she had once lifted a Roman coin from a riverbed, and her headlamp steadied on the cover, and the world went very quiet and very far away.

* * *

She knew the hand before she knew the words. That was the terrible thing about handwriting — it arrived in the body before it arrived in the mind. The way the *E* leaned forward like a man starting to run. The way the *g* descended in a long impatient tail. The way he crossed his *t*s with a single slashing stroke, as though time itself were the enemy.

*Field Journal — E. Ashgrove.*

The first date stopped her heart.

Not 2013. Not the year the *Kestrel* went down.

The date was four years *after* his death.

"Imogen." Ulyana was closer now, her boots splashing through the shallow water that had begun to creep back across the hold floor. "What is it? What did you—"

Imogen turned the journal toward her and said nothing, because there was nothing to say. The light did the work. Ulyana Ashgrove stood in the rising water of a drowned hold and looked at her father's handwriting on a book that could not exist, and Imogen watched twelve years of grief rearrange itself on the younger woman's face — watched it crack, and shift, and show the raw strata underneath.

"That's—" Ulyana's voice failed and restarted. "That's his. That's *Dad's* writing. That's—"

"Read the first page," Imogen said quietly. "Read it aloud. I need to hear it, and I think you need to say it."

Ulyana took the journal as if it might burn, or as if it might be the only warm thing left in the world. Her gloves were soaked; she stripped them off with her teeth and turned the pages with bare fingers, and Imogen saw her flinch at the touch of the paper — dry paper, kept dry, kept *safe*.

Ulyana read.

*"'If anyone finds this, I am sorry for what I did to you. I am sorry for the funeral. I am sorry for the lie, but the lie is the only thing keeping you alive, and I need you to hate me for drowning rather than look for me, because the men who did this will be watching you, and they will be watching for exactly that — a daughter who doesn't believe.'"*

The words fell into the hold one at a time, and each one landed like a stone in still water.

Ulyana's voice made it through two more lines before it broke entirely.

*"'The wreck is real. It is older than any chart, and it did not sink — it was sunk, deliberately, with its people aboard, and the men whose fortune was built on that sinking are still in the world, still powerful, and they have been looking for this hold for a hundred years. I found it by accident, in my own survey, and I have been hiding it ever since, moving the evidence, burying the truth in pieces, because the moment it is found in full, the moment it can be read in one place, they will kill for it. They killed the last man who tried. I have seen his grave.'"*

Ulyana stopped. The water was at her ankles now. Somewhere above them, through forty feet of rock and storm, the sea was gathering itself for the surge, and neither of them moved.

"He wrote that," Ulyana said. It was not a question. It was a woman standing at the edge of a collapsed map, testing the ground. "He wrote that *four years after* — Imogen, the coast guard gave me a box. They gave me a *box*. I was nineteen. I buried an empty— I stood at a grave with an empty—"

"I know," Imogen said.

"You *knew*?" Ulyana's head came up, and for one raw second Imogen saw not grief but fury, white-hot and looking for somewhere to land. "You knew him. You were his student. Did you know he was alive? Did you—"

"I knew he was dead," Imogen said, and her voice cracked on the word, because she had carried that death for twelve years too, had chosen her whole field because of it, had come to Cape Reckoning in the first place because of the hole Edmund Ashgrove had left in the world. "I identified his effects. I wrote the memorial notice. I believed it, Ulyana, with everything in me — and then I read the last entry in his real field log, the one they never released, and I started asking questions, and that is why my team is dead. That is why I am in this cave. Whatever he found, it killed my people forty years after it killed him. It has been killing people the whole time."

* * *

The journal was thin — perhaps sixty pages, written over the span of three years, in the bursts of a man who could only write when it was safe, which was never often. Imogen read it by headlamp while Ulyana held the light, and the water climbed, and the storm above them built its pressure like a fist closing.

He had not faked his death, the early entries made clear. He had nearly died, and someone had made certain the world believed he had. The *Kestrel* had been found burned to the waterline, and the burn had not been the storm's work. He had been pulled from the sea by a fisherman who owed him a debt and who had the sense to say nothing, and Edmund Ashgrove, given a miracle, had understood immediately what it was for. A dead man could hide. A dead man could move evidence. A dead man's daughter would be watched, but pitied, not interrogated.

*"'I have moved the manifest to the third place,'"* Ulyana read, her voice steadying the way a hand steadies on a rail in rough water. *"'The chest goes back in the hold, under the rib, where the sonar shadow hides it. The void in the readings is the best lock in the world — nobody looks for a room that reads as nothing. Corvane's people have surveyed this coast four times in six years. They are close. They are always close. I have maybe a year. Maybe less. When they find it, I need it to look like nothing — a wreck, a tragedy, a hundred-year-old grief. Not evidence. Grief they can leave in the sea. Evidence they will burn the coast for.'"*

"Corvane," Ulyana said. "He names Corvane. In his own hand. Twelve years ago."

"He names the grandfather," Imogen said. "Aldous Corvane. The fortune — the shipping fortune, the one the salvage fleet is built on — it starts with a ship lost off this cape in 1919 with all hands. That's what the manifest proves. It wasn't lost. It was *scuttled* — for the insurance, and for the silence, because the passengers were witnesses to something worse, and the families, the *Ashgroves* —" She stopped. She had not meant to say it yet. There was no gentle order left for any of it.

Ulyana had gone very still. "Say it."

"The manifest lists the passengers' families who were paid off afterward. It's in the records we already pulled. Ashgrove is on it. Your family didn't just lose a boat off this cape, Ulyana. Your family was one of the families the Corvanes silenced. Your father didn't stumble onto this wreck. He was *coming home to it*."

The headlamp beam wavered. In the yellow circle of light, the hold seemed to breathe — water rising, shadows swelling and shrinking — and Ulyana Ashgrove stood in the middle of it with a dead man's journal in her bare hands and Imogen watched her whole self tear down the middle, cleanly, like a chart along an old fold.

"He let me bury him," she said. The words came out flat, stripped of everything. "He was alive. He was alive for *four more years*, and he let me — I was nineteen, Imogen, I stood at that grave and I promised him I'd — and he was *alive* and he *chose*—"

"He chose you," Imogen said. "Read the last page."

* * *

The last entry was short. The handwriting had changed by then — smaller, tighter, the slashes of the *t*s gone hesitant. A man writing with one ear turned toward the door.

Ulyana read it in a whisper, and the water rose around her knees, and Imogen memorized every word as it fell, because she understood, with the cold professional clarity that had kept her alive in the sea cave for six days, that paper could be destroyed. Paper was already, at this moment, doomed — they were standing in a rising tide inside a wreck that Ulyana intended, if Imogen knew her at all, to bring down in a surge of storm water before the night was out. The journal had perhaps an hour to live.

So Imogen memorized it.

*"'They found my third place today. I watched them from the ridge — Corvane's men, three boats, methodical as ants. They didn't find the chest. It's back in the hold, in the void, where it reads as nothing. But they know I'm alive now. The fisherman talked, or they made him talk. I have days, at most. So I am writing this for my daughter, who will find it someday, because she is the only person on this coast stubborn enough to keep looking after everyone tells her to stop.'"*

Ulyana's breath caught.

*"'If you're reading this: I was not a coward, and I did not choose the wreck over you. I chose you the only way left to me. The men who built their fortune on drowned families are still drowning families. Everything I hid, I hid so that it would survive them. If it ever comes into your hands, it will be because they are closing in again, and it will be the most dangerous thing you have ever held, and I am so sorry, little wren, because I know you, and I know you will not put it down.'"*

The headlamp beam shook. The water was at their shins. Somewhere far above, muffled through stone and sea, the storm's voice changed pitch — deepened, gathered — and Imogen felt it in her back teeth, the way she had learned to feel weather in the cave: the surge was coming, and it was coming soon, and it would be enormous.

"He called me *little wren*," Ulyana said. Her voice was wrecked and steady at once, the way a house is steady after an earthquake, everything standing and nothing trusted. "Nobody — I never told anyone that. Nobody alive knew that."

"He did," Imogen said. "It's really him. It was always really him."

Ulyana closed the journal. Held it against her chest with both arms, the way you hold a child, or a wound. And then she lifted her head, and through the salt-crusted dark of the hold, Imogen saw her eyes — red-rimmed, furious, and lit from behind with something that had not been there an hour ago. Something that looked terribly like purpose.

"Corvane's family drowned mine," Ulyana said. "A hundred years ago, and then again twelve years ago, and then again last week when they took your team. It's the same drowning. It's been the same drowning the whole time."

"Yes," Imogen said.

"And he hid the proof so it would survive them." Ulyana looked down at the journal, at the water lapping at the waxed canvas bundles of manifest, at the whole fragile archive of a century's crime. "And now we're standing in it, and the tide's coming back, and Corvane's fleet is anchored off the cape waiting for the storm to pass."

Imogen heard it before Ulyana said it. She heard it the way you hear a wave gathering behind you.

"He wanted it to survive them," Ulyana said slowly. "But there's one thing they want more than they want it destroyed, Imogen. There's one thing that hurts worse than losing it." Her jaw set. "Them *losing it forever*."

Above them, through the rock, the sea began to roar.

And with it, faint but unmistakable, carried down through the flooded passage they had come by — the sound of an engine. Then another. Boats, running the storm's edge, coming around the point toward the wreck that no longer read as nothing.

Corvane had found his void.
