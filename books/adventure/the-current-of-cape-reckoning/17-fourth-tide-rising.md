## 17. Fourth Tide, Rising

The tide had turned while Corvane was still talking.

Ulyana felt it before she saw it — a change in the air pressure inside the hold, a faint exhalation from the wreck itself, as if the old ship had drawn breath and was now letting it go. The water in the flooded companionway below them shivered. Somewhere out beyond the storm's eye, the sea was beginning to remember its hunger.

She stood with her back against the ballast channel, the journal in her father's handwriting pressed flat against her chest beneath her jacket, and listened to a man offer her everything she had spent twelve years wanting.

"Debts cleared," Corvane said again, patiently, as though repetition were a kindness. "Not just yours. The Voss brothers' exposure — gone. Your truck, your gear, your freedom to drive any road on this coast without looking in your mirrors." He gestured, an open-handed motion toward the iron box at his feet, the one that held the manifest, the survey records, the proof of what his family's money had been built on. "And this. His journal. You'd have it in your hands by nightfall. Every word he wrote after the day the world decided he was dead."

The storm's eye was passing over them. Through the broken skylight above the hold, where the wreck lay exposed at low water like the ribcage of something enormous, Ulyana could see a ring of pale sky — a lens of terrible calm — and around its edge, the black walls of the coming squall stacking up against the horizon like a held breath.

Two tides left. Maybe less.

"You've been quiet a long time, Miss Ashgrove," Corvane said.

"I'm doing arithmetic," she said.

* * *

It was astonishing how reasonable he made it sound. That was the thing she would remember afterward, if there was an afterward — not the menace, which was there, banked and patient under everything he said, but the reasonableness. He stood in the dripping dark of a century-old hold with salt water rising around his boots, and he talked like a man across a kitchen table, laying out figures.

"Consider it honestly," he said. "You take the records to the authorities. What happens? An inquiry. A hearing. Lawyers whose fees make the Voss brothers look like charity workers. My family's name is attached to foundations, hospitals, half the shipping registry on this coast. Do you know how long a case like that takes? Years. A decade. And at the end of it, maybe — *maybe* — a settlement, a fine, a footnote in a maritime history journal that eleven people read." He shook his head, almost sorrowful. "Your father waited too long to act, and it cost him everything. Don't inherit his mistake."

"Don't," Ulyana said quietly, "talk about my father."

"I'm the only person alive who can give him back to you," Corvane said. "That's not a threat. It's the truth. And you've spent your whole life chasing truths, haven't you? Storms. Data. Pressure gradients. You follow weather because it's honest — it tells you exactly what it's going to do before it does it." He smiled, thin and courteous. "I'm offering you the same courtesy."

Behind her, at the mouth of the hold where the ladder rose toward the exposed deck, Hale shifted. Ulyana heard the small sounds of her — the wet rasp of her breathing, still damaged from the cave, the click of the case's latches as her fingers found them and held on. Imogen Hale had not spoken since Corvane arrived. She didn't need to. Everything she believed was in the way she stood between the records and the water, a woman half-dead who had already given up everything once and was prepared, visibly, calmly, to do it again.

Corvane glanced at her, then dismissed her, the way a man dismisses furniture.

"The tide's turning," he said. "You can feel it. In ninety minutes this hold is under four meters of surge, and by tomorrow morning there will be divers here, and cranes, and men whose job descriptions don't include mercy. So. Three doors, Miss Ashgrove. Walk through one of them."

He raised one finger. "Take the deal. Everything I said. You, whole; your father's journal in your hands; the past returned to you intact. All it costs is silence, and silence is the cheapest thing on this coast."

A second finger. "Run with the evidence. Take the case, take the journal, disappear down the coast road the way you disappeared for twelve years. I'll give you a six-hour head start, because I'm generous, and because it changes nothing. I have three ships and a fortune, and you have a truck with two hundred thousand miles on it. You'll be caught, or you'll live the rest of your life as a fugitive holding paper nobody will ever be allowed to read. Either way, the truth dies in your hands instead of mine. At least mine would be quick."

The third finger came up slowly, and when he spoke again his voice dropped into something almost gentle.

"Or you use the storm. You've been looking at those ballast channels since I walked in — don't insult me, I watched your eyes track them. Old ships like this were scuttled once already; the channels are breached, the hull's soft as bread. Rig the surge against her and she comes down, and everything in her goes under forty tons of rock, and no one — not me, not the inquiry, not history — ever proves a word of it." He tilted his head. "But understand what that means. You'd be destroying the only proof your father ever lived past the day they buried him. His handwriting. His last years. The record that he *was*. You wouldn't be killing my family's secret, Miss Ashgrove. You'd be killing him all over again. And this time you'd be the one holding the knife."

The words went in like a splinter driven deep, and Ulyana felt it lodge somewhere behind her sternum, and she hated him for the accuracy of it, and she hated herself more, because he was right. That was the trap. That was always the trap. Every door cost something irreplaceable, and the man had built the arithmetic so carefully that whichever door she chose, she would walk through it knowing she had paid.

The water in the companionway had climbed another handspan. She could hear it now, a soft persistent sound, the sea taking its seat again.

* * *

She thought about miles.

Twelve years of them. Twelve years of measuring her life in the distance between places, because distance was safe — distance never asked her to stay, never asked her to be anyone's daughter, never asked her to stand still long enough for grief to catch up. Her father had died — had *seemed* to die — off this cape, and she had answered his death the only way she knew: by never arriving anywhere. Every client delivered, every squall outrun, every debt serviced and regrown like kelp, had been another mile put between herself and the day the coast guard boat came back empty.

And now a man was offering to sell her father back to her, and the price was the truth, and the horrible, unspeakable thing — the thing she would carry if she survived this night — was that some part of her wanted to pay it.

Not the part that read the journal by flashlight in the hold an hour ago, hands shaking, reading sentences in a hand she knew better than her own. That part had already chosen. But there was a smaller part, a cold practical animal part, the part that had kept her alive through twelve years of storms and creditors, and that part whispered: *take the deal, take the deal, you could have him back, you could stop running, you could sleep.*

She looked at Corvane and understood, finally, what he actually was. Not a villain out of a cheap novel. Just a man who had done this arithmetic so many times he no longer noticed it was arithmetic — who genuinely believed survival was the only honest virtue, because every other virtue had been priced out of his reach decades ago. He wasn't lying to her. That was the worst of it. He meant every word. And he was wrong all the way down to the keel.

"My father wrote something," she said. "In the journal. Did you read it?"

"I read enough."

"He wrote that he hid the records because some things belong to the families of the drowned, not to the men who drowned them." She let that sit. "He also wrote that if anyone ever found what he'd hidden, they should ask themselves one question before deciding what to do with it." Her voice stayed level. It cost her everything to keep it level. "He wrote: *what would the people in the manifest want?*"

Corvane's expression did not change. "Dead men want nothing."

"No," Ulyana said. "They don't. But their grandchildren are still alive. There's a schoolteacher in Harrow Point named Ashgrove. There's a fisherman in Tern Bay whose grandmother's name is on that manifest. They've spent a hundred years believing their people were lost to weather." She shook her head slowly. "You're right that an inquiry takes years. You're right that your money buys lawyers. But you're wrong about what I'd be destroying. Paper burns. Testimony doesn't. I've read every word in that case, Dr. Hale has read every word, and the Voss brothers — well. Men who sell you out once will sell anyone out. You should think about that."

Something moved behind Corvane's eyes then — the first true thing she'd seen in his face. Not fear. Recalculation. The sound, almost audible, of a ledger being re-balanced.

"You're bluffing," he said.

"Probably," Ulyana agreed. "I'm good at it. I learned from watching you."

* * *

The eye was closing. She could see it happening through the skylight — the ring of pale sky narrowing, the western wall of cloud leaning in like a door swinging shut. When it closed, the full storm would come down on the cape with everything it had, and the surge behind it would be the largest in a decade. She had read this storm the way other people read scripture, all afternoon, from the ridge above the causeway: its barometric signature, its forward speed, the way the wind field was asymmetric and mean. It would ground the fleet for exactly one tide. One.

Ninety minutes of slack, and then the sea would come back like a debt collector.

Which was, she supposed, fitting.

Hale spoke for the first time. "Ulyana."

Just her name. But the way she said it carried the whole weight of the sea cave, of the forty minutes of data logged after the team was declared missing, of six tides of trust built in the dark between two women who had every reason to betray each other and hadn't. Ulyana turned. Hale's face was gray with exhaustion, her lips cracked with salt, and her eyes were absolutely clear.

"The channels," Hale said. "I mapped them. The survey team mapped them, before—" She stopped, steadied herself. "The lower ballast runs are breached. The starboard side collapsed in '31, according to the harbor records I pulled. If the surge comes through the seaward breach under pressure, and the upper channels are opened—" She didn't finish. She didn't need to. Forty tons of capstone rock sat in the cliff shelf directly above the wreck, and both of them had seen it, and both of them had known, since the moment they reached the hold, that the third door was the real one.

Corvane laughed. It was a good laugh, warm even, and it chilled Ulyana more than anything else he'd done.

"You'd bury your own father's grave," he said.

"It's not his grave," Ulyana said. "His grave is wherever you people put him. This is just a ship." She looked at the iron case, at the manifest with its hundred names, at the void in the sonar that had swallowed a survey team and nearly swallowed her too. "And what's buried in it isn't treasure. It's a lie. And lies keep, Mr. Corvane. Underwater, in the dark, they keep forever. That's the problem with you people — you think the truth is fragile. It's the only durable thing on this coast."

The first drops came through the skylight. Fat, warm, preliminary. The eye was shut.

Corvane looked from her to Hale to the case, and Ulyana watched him arrive, in real time, at the understanding that neither of them was going to take the deal — watched the courtesy drain out of him like water through a breached hull, leaving something older and simpler underneath. His hand drifted, casually, toward his coat.

"Ninety minutes," he said, and all the gentleness was gone from it. "I'll be on the *Meridian*. When the weather lifts, I'm sending divers. Whatever you decide, decide fast — and know that if anything on this wreck is destroyed, the arithmetic changes for everyone involved. Including the doctor. Including the brothers who sold you to me."

He turned and went up the ladder, sure-footed in the dark, and was gone into the rising hiss of the rain.

* * *

For a long moment neither of them moved. Then Hale sagged against the bulkhead and let out a breath that seemed to empty her entirely.

"He'll radio the fleet," she said hoarsely. "He'll have men back here before slack water."

"He'll try." Ulyana was already moving, pulling the crowbar from her pack, crossing to the upper ballast channel where the old iron plate covered the run. "Storm'll jam their radios and tear up the shore road. We bought ourselves the window. Now we spend it."

"And if we fail?" Hale's voice cracked on it. "If the rigging doesn't hold, if the surge comes in wrong — we lose the records *and* our lives, and for nothing."

Ulyana set the crowbar's edge into the seam of the plate and paused there, both hands on the cold iron, feeling her own heartbeat slam against it. One breath. That was all the choice needed, in the end. Not three doors. Not an arithmetic. Just one breath, in and out, and whatever she was when she exhaled, that was who she'd be for the rest of her life.

Her father had hidden the truth and died for it. Corvane had buried the truth and lived rich on it. Both of them had treated the past as property.

She breathed in. The rain thickened overhead. Below her boots, the sea began, softly, patiently, to rise.

She breathed out — and wrenched the plate free.

The ballast channel opened with a groan like a waking thing, and above them, through the closing storm, the cliff shelf waited with its forty tons of stone, and Ulyana Ashgrove started to work.
