## 18. Loyalty Is a Kind of Truth

Ulyana

The storm's eye was a lie of mercy.

Ulyana had chased enough of them to know the stillness for what it was — a held breath, a fist drawing back. Above the Cape the clouds had torn open into a ragged ring of bruised violet, and through it the light fell strange and silver on the exposed wreck, on the black ribs of a ship that had no business existing in any century she could name. The sea had pulled back beyond the tideline like something recoiling from its own memory, and in that withdrawn hush, the hold lay open before them: crates of paper, iron-bound ledgers swollen with salt, and her father's handwriting staring up at her from a journal dated four years after the sea was supposed to have taken him.

She hadn't cried. She'd noticed that with a kind of clinical detachment, the way she noted barometric drops. There would be time for grief later, if there was a later, and right now there were exactly two tides left and Corvane's black hulls swinging at anchor beyond the headland, patient as debts.

Hale crouched over the manifest, her broken wrist splinted with survey tape and driftwood, reading names by the grey light. Ulyana watched her and did the arithmetic she hated herself for doing.

*Here is the thing you could do,* said the small cold voice that lived in the part of her brain that had kept her alive through twelve years of storms and twelve years of running. *You could take the journal. Just the journal. Leave Hale with the rest of it — leave her with the manifest, the ledgers, all of it — and walk out of this eye with the one thing that's yours. Corvane wants the records burned. He doesn't care about the woman. He might even let you keep the journal if you gave him everything else. If you gave him her.*

The voice was reasonable. That was the obscenity of it. It laid out the trade the way Voss had once laid out her ledger: clean columns, honest sums. Hale was half-dead already. Hale was slow. Hale knew the cave passages and the data and the truth about the team that had vanished, and every one of those facts made her valuable to Corvane and heavy to Ulyana. Two people could not outrun three ships. One person, traveling light, carrying only a father's journal — one person had a chance.

And beneath the chance, quieter, meaner: *She got your father killed. Or she knows who did. Either way, she owes you, and you've spent your whole life collecting what people owe.*

Ulyana stood in the wreck of a ship older than any chart, holding the proof that her whole life had been built on a false coordinate, and she understood suddenly why her father had hidden this place instead of shouting about it. Because the truth didn't set anyone free. It just changed what you were willing to carry.

"Hale." Her voice came out rougher than she intended.

The doctor looked up. Salt-crusted, fever-bright, four days half-drowned in a sea cave, and her eyes were still steady. "The Ashgrove entry," Hale said, before Ulyana could speak. "On the manifest. Passengers' families, listed for compensation after the scuttling. There are eleven Ashgroves."

"I know. I read it."

"Your father found this first." Hale's voice cracked on it. "Years ago. He came looking for his own name, same as — " She stopped. Swallowed. "I'm sorry. I should have told you on the beach instead of making you drag me here first."

"You were dying. It's fine."

"It isn't fine. Nothing about this is fine." Hale laughed, a dry broken sound. "My entire team walked into the water because I told them the instruments were malfunctioning. Eleven people, Ulyana. I did the math on their lives the way men like Corvane do the math on everything, and I chose wrong, and they're gone, and I'm sitting in their wreck reading their names out loud." She looked down at the manifest. "So whatever you're deciding about me — whatever deal you're weighing — just make it fast. We don't have time for either of us to be noble."

Ulyana went cold. "I wasn't — "

"You were. I'd have been." Hale met her eyes. "That's not an accusation. That's just what hunger does. I've watched it happen to better people than both of us."

The wind shifted outside the hold — a low moan through the ship's ribs, the eye beginning to close. Somewhere beyond the headland, an engine turned over. Not yet coming. Testing.

Ulyana looked at the journal in her hands. Her father's hand, cramped and urgent, describing ballast channels wide enough to flood, describing how he'd meant to bring the sea back over this place himself and never gotten the chance. *If you're reading this, finish it,* the last line said. *Some things shouldn't be salvageable.*

He had chosen the sea over the truth's survival. And he had done it alone, and alone was where being alone had gotten him — drowned twice, once in water and once in silence.

"No deals," Ulyana said. "Not with him. Not without you."

Hale blinked. "You don't owe me that."

"Nobody owes anybody anything out here. That's the whole point of the Cape." She tucked the journal inside her jacket, against her ribs, where the compass used to ride. "But my father wrote instructions. Ballast channels. He wanted this ship sunk properly, under rock, past anyone's reach — his, Corvane's, mine, everyone's. And I can't rig a century-old ballast system with a busted arm and a fever. So I need you ambulatory, doctor, and I need you angry, and I need you to stop giving me permission to sell you out, because it makes it harder not to."

Something moved across Hale's face — exhaustion cracking into something that might, in better weather, have been a smile. "That's the most romantic thing anyone's said to me on a dying ship."

"Don't make it weird. Can you walk?"

"I can limp with conviction."

"Good enough." Ulyana hauled her upright, took her weight, and felt the strange settling in her own chest — not relief exactly, but the moment when a storm system finally organizes itself into something with a name. "Then here's the plan. My father drew the channels. The surge comes up the old loading gap, floods the ballast runs, and takes the shingle bank down on top of the hold. We trigger it early, we get clear, and this whole thing goes under forty feet of collapsed rock."

"And the records?"

"The records go down with it." Ulyana heard herself say it and was surprised at how little it cost. "Corvane's fortune dies unnamed. My father's killer stays anonymous. Everything everyone bled for — gone." She looked at Hale. "Unless testimony counts."

Hale was quiet a moment. Wind screamed through the gap behind them; the eye was closing, the second act of the storm gathering itself offshore like a debt come due.

"Testimony counts," Hale said. "It's the only currency I've ever trusted." She gripped Ulyana's shoulder with her good hand. "I memorized the manifest. All of it. Every name, including yours. Whatever happens to the paper, the paper isn't the truth. We are."

It wasn't a vow, formally. But vows were for churches and courtrooms, and this was a wreck in the throat of a storm, and some promises are just two people choosing, out loud, not to do the arithmetic.

"Come on," Ulyana said. "We've got one tide and my father's homework."

* * *

Hale

Imogen Hale had spent thirty years believing that science was a form of honesty. You recorded what you saw. You reported what you recorded. The instruments didn't lie, and neither, therefore, did she — until the night she told eleven colleagues the sonar void was an equipment fault, and watched them zip their jackets and walk down to the boats, trusting her, and never come back.

Since then she had learned what honesty actually weighed. It weighed more than the sealed case. More than the ledgers. Right now, limping beside Ulyana Ashgrove along the wreck's tilted deck with the wind rising and the tide turning, it weighed approximately as much as a grown woman leaning on your shoulder.

Because she had done the other math too. In the cave, feverish, listening to the water breathe, she had lain awake and considered selling Ulyana out.

Not dramatically. That was the horror of it — how small the thought had been. *Tell Corvane's men where she keeps the truck. Trade yourself for transport off the Cape. She's stronger than you; she'll survive capture longer than you will.* Reasonable. Almost kind, if you squinted — framed as self-sacrifice, the weak handing over the strong. But underneath, the same engine that had sent her team into the water: the belief that her survival was the fixed point around which other people should orbit.

She had lain in the dark of the cave and realized that Corvane didn't need to buy her loyalty. He already owned the instinct. All he'd ever needed was for someone to be hungry enough to listen to it.

And then Ulyana had come down into the cave with a rope and a bad attitude and hauled her out anyway, knowing nothing, owing nothing, and Imogen had understood that there was exactly one way left to be an honest scientist: record what you saw. What she saw was a woman with every reason to abandon her, refusing to. That was data. She intended to report it accurately, with her life if necessary.

"Here." Ulyana stopped at a hatchway aft of the mainmast, where the deck planking gaped over a stone throat. "Father's diagram shows the channel running down under the keel line. Iron sluice gates — counterweighted. They were designed to flood the ballast runs deliberately. Someone built this ship to be sinkable."

"Ships of that era often were," Hale managed. "Insurance fraud was — " She caught herself. "Sorry. Habit."

"No. Say it. It matters."

"Deliberate scuttling for insurance was common enough," Hale said, easing down through the hatchway, one-handed, into the dark that smelled of iron and ancient salt. "A ship worth more dead than alive gets drowned on a quiet night, the owners collect, the passengers become a rounding error. Your manifest lists compensation payments. Someone paid out. Someone profited. Corvane's family name is in those ledgers on the paying end." Her voice echoed strangely in the channel. "This isn't a grave, Ulyana. It's a receipt."

Below, the ballast run opened like a cathedral nave — stone-lined, ribbed with iron, big enough to stand in. Ulyana played her lamp along the walls and found the sluice gate: a slab of green-crusted metal in its frame, counterweight chain vanishing up into shadow.

"That's it. That's the trigger." Ulyana ran her fingers along the chain links, testing. "Chain's sound. Gate moves — look, it shifts when I lean." She leaned; the gate groaned a half-inch, and somewhere below them the sea answered, a deep hollow knock, like a knuckle on a door. Both of them went still.

"It knows we're here," Hale said, and didn't entirely mean it as a joke.

"Everything on this coast knows we're here." Ulyana straightened. "Okay. Physics lesson. When the surge hits, water comes through the loading gap at pressure. If the gate's open, the run floods, the ship's buoyancy goes to zero, and the shingle bank above the hold — which is already unstable, I felt it shift when we landed — lets go. Forty feet of rock and gravel. Nothing salvages through that. Not Corvane, not a government survey, not God with a dredge."

"But the gate has to be open before the surge arrives, or the pressure slams it shut forever."

"So we pin it open now." Ulyana was already uncoiling rope from her shoulder. "Wedge it, lash it, and cut the counterweight free so nothing can pull it closed. Then we climb out, get to high ground past the treeline, and wait for the sea to do the accounting."

Hale looked up the length of the channel, at the ladder of rusted rungs leading back to the grey light, and did the last piece of arithmetic honestly, for once, with all the costs included. "One of us should stay to make sure the lashings hold when the water comes. A pinned gate that slips is a wasted death — theirs and ours."

"No."

"Ulyana — "

"No." Flat, final, the voice of a woman who had argued with hurricanes and won ties. "Nobody stays. We rig it so nobody has to. That's the whole design brief." She fed rope through the counterweight linkage, knotting with quick, furious competence. "My father stayed. Look where solo heroics got him — a fake drowning and four extra years of hiding. We do this together or not at all, and 'together' means both of us on that hillside when the rock comes down."

Hale held her gaze. In the lamplight, Ulyana Ashgrove looked like every storm chart she'd ever studied: chaos organized around a single unyielding center.

"All right," Hale said quietly. "Together."

They worked. It was strange how ordinary the work was — rope and chain and leverage, two women sweating in the belly of a murdered ship while the storm rebuilt itself offshore. Hale braced the gate with a spar of the old cargo crating while Ulyana lashed the counterweight, and between knots Ulyana talked, low, almost to herself: about her father teaching her to read clouds off the causeway rail, about the compass he'd left her, about twelve years of driving away from the Cape because staying meant admitting the sea had lied to her. And Hale listened, and understood that this — the talking, the witness, the testimony — was the real rigging. The gate would flood the ballast runs. The story would flood everything else.

When the last lashing held, Ulyana put her palm flat against the green-crusted gate, the way you'd touch a coffin lid or a door you weren't sure you wanted opened.

"For the eleven Ashgroves," she said. "And for Dad, who finished his sentence late." She looked back at Hale. "Ready?"

Above them, through the open hatchway, the light had changed — the violet ring of the eye sealing over, the far edge of the storm arriving like a wall falling toward them across the water. And with it, faint but unmistakable, carried down the wind: engines. More than one. Coming around the headland, no longer testing.

The tide had maybe twenty minutes left in it. So did they.

"Run first," Hale said, gripping the ladder rungs with her good hand. "Vow on the hillside."

Ulyana almost smiled. "Deal. After you, Doctor."

They climbed toward the light as the first rain of the returning storm struck the deck above them like applause — and out past the headland, black hulls began to move.
