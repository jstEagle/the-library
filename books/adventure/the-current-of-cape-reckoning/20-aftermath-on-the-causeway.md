## 20. Aftermath on the Causeway

Dawn came to Cape Reckoning the way it always did — not arriving so much as being permitted, the fog thinning by degrees until the world reassembled itself out of gray.

Imogen Hale sat on the causeway's seaward rail with her boots hooked in the cross-bracing and her hands wrapped around a thermos cup of coffee that had gone cold an hour ago. She hadn't drunk it. Holding it was enough. Holding something was enough. Her fingers still remembered the shape of the sealed case, the weight of it against her ribs through three days in the cave, and now that it was gone — gone with everything else, down under forty tons of collapsed headland and black storm water — her hands didn't seem to know what to do except hold whatever was nearest.

Below her, where the tide was running out over the shoals, there was nothing to see. That was the point of it. The wreck that had predated every chart, the hold that had read as a void on sonar, the ballast channels she and Ulyana had rigged with fuse cord and prayer — all of it lay beneath the new scar on the coastline, a raw amphitheater of fallen rock where the Cape's shoulder had slumped into the sea at 3:47 in the morning, exactly as calculated, exactly as hoped. The surge had done the rest. The sea had closed over it like a mouth.

She should have felt grief. She had spent eleven years of her professional life trying to reach that wreck, and six tides trying to keep its contents out of the wrong hands, and in the end she had helped bury it deeper than any dredge would ever go. But what she felt instead, sitting in the wet dawn cold with her ruined knee throbbing and her lungs still tasting salt and stone dust, was something she could only describe as *lightness* — the specific, almost shameful lightness of a woman who has set down a weight she'd forgotten she was carrying.

The records survived. That was the arithmetic she kept running, over and over, like a rosary. Corvane had his fortune built on drowned families; they had his proof. Not in paper — paper was at the bottom of the channel now, every manifest, every ledger page, every waterlogged journal including the one written in Thomas Ashgrove's careful hand — but in memory. In hers. Three days in a sea cave with nothing but survey printouts and a dying flashlight had made a copyist of her. She had read those pages until they were printed on the inside of her eyelids. She could recite the manifest from memory: forty-one names, the families of the *Meridian Star*, listed not as passengers but as cargo, valued line by line in a hand that had never once trembled. A Corvane hand. She could recite the insurance filings, the double-bottom hull modifications, the deliberate scuttle order dated nine days before the ship sailed into the Cape's teeth with all lights doused.

And she could recite the last entry in Thomas Ashgrove's journal, because she had read it four times before the water took it, and because it mattered to the woman sitting twenty feet away from her on the same rail, staring out at the same empty water.

Ulyana hadn't spoken since they'd climbed up off the rocks. Neither had much breath for it at the time — the climb out ahead of the collapsing surge had been a screaming, rope-burning scramble, and afterward there had been the Voss brothers' boats standing off in the rain, and then the long walk along the causeway as the storm died behind them like an animal spending its last strength. But even now, with breath available, Ulyana said nothing. She sat with her elbows on her knees and her eyes on the water and her father's compass open in her palm, and Imogen understood that silence was not emptiness. It was construction. Somewhere behind that weathered face, a whole interior map of herself was being redrawn, coast by coast, and it would take as long as it took.

Imogen let her have it. She drank her cold coffee instead, or pretended to, and watched the light come up.

* * *

The authorities arrived at half past seven, which by coastal standards was practically instantaneous, and by government standards was a miracle attributable entirely to the fact that three black-hulled salvage vessels were sitting in the roadstead with their work lights still burning and one of them was listing badly with a hole punched in its bow plating.

They came by sea first — two rigid inflatables out of the harbormaster's station, then a larger patrol cutter standing in past the shoal buoy with its radar turning. Then, forty minutes later, the causeway itself filled with vehicles: a police SUV, an ambulance that nobody had called for but everybody was quietly glad to see, and a mud-splattered four-by-four carrying a marine board investigator named Okafor who introduced herself twice, once officially and once humanely, and who looked at the two of them — a half-drowned scientist with a splinted knee and a storm chaser with salt-crusted hair and blood still dried in the lines of her knuckles — with the particular expression of a person recalculating everything she'd been told on the drive out.

"You're Dr. Hale," Okafor said. "Imogen Hale. Of the Reckoning Survey."

"I am."

"Your team was declared missing eleven days ago." Okafor's voice was level, but Imogen heard the question underneath it, the one the woman had clearly been chewing since the cutter: *eleven days, and here you are.* "There were search parties. Helicopters. Nothing."

"We weren't lost," Imogen said. "We were taken. And after that, we were hiding. Those aren't the same thing as missing, though I understand why the paperwork treats them identically."

Okafor looked at her for a long moment. Then she looked at the collapsed section of headland, at the fresh scar of rock and the disturbed water above the shoal, and Imogen watched her do the same arithmetic every thinking person on that causeway was doing: *something was under there worth destroying.*

"You want to tell me what happened to my coastline?"

"Yes," Imogen said. "That is, in fact, the only thing I want to do."

It wasn't entirely true. What she wanted, in the animal part of herself, was sleep — real sleep, horizontal, in a room without dripping stone — and food that hadn't come from a foil packet, and possibly to never see tidal water again. But underneath the exhaustion ran a harder current, one that had been building since the moment she'd decoded the sonar returns in the cave and understood what the void in the cargo hold meant. Truth was a perishable commodity. It kept only as long as the people carrying it stayed upright and coherent. She had watched men die for the contents of that hold — her whole team, gone ashore in a boat flying no flag, and she had never learned which ones had made it further than the beach. She was not going to let the truth die of administrative delay.

So she talked. Standing, because sitting felt too much like surrendering, with the paramedic hovering at her elbow trying to get a blood pressure cuff on her and being gently defeated. She gave Okafor the shape of it: the survey, the anomaly, the uncharted wreck, the instruments that had logged a hull older than any registry entry on the eastern seaboard. She described the men in the unmarked boat. She did not yet give names — not Corvane's, not the Voss brothers', not yet, because names were ammunition and she wanted to see how the wind lay first — but she gave everything else, in the flat precise cadence of a woman reading data aloud, because that was the only register in which her voice would stay steady.

Beside her, Ulyana listened. Imogen was aware of her the way you're aware of a load-bearing wall — silently essential, noticed only when you imagined the room without it. When Imogen's account reached the night the team went into the water, Ulyana spoke for the first time in hours.

"The survey boat was scuttled at anchor," she said. "I found the seacocks open when I dove the mooring. Somebody wanted it to look like gear failure." A pause. "It didn't look like gear failure."

Okafor wrote that down. Imogen caught the flicker of the investigator's eyes between them — assessing, weighing, already composing the report that would travel up the chain far beyond this county — and understood that whatever happened next, the two of them were now, formally and irreversibly, witnesses. There was a version of her life before this morning and a version after, and the hinge was a police notebook.

An ambulance medic finally won the argument about the blood pressure cuff. Imogen submitted to it with the weary grace of the defeated, and while the man fussed over her pulse — elevated, obviously, everything about her was elevated — she watched the harbor below fill with consequence. The black-hulled vessels were being boarded. She could see the inspection party's orange vests moving across the nearest deck, tiny at this distance, methodical. On the listing ship, pumps were running. Men stood at the rail with their hands visible, the universal posture of the detained.

One of the smaller boats was coming around the point toward the causeway landing. Two figures stood in it, and even at distance Imogen knew them by their posture alone — the identical easy slouch, the matched silhouettes, like parentheses around the word *trouble*. The Voss brothers, coming in voluntarily, hands in plain sight, wearing expressions of cheerful cooperation that Imogen had already learned to read the way sailors read cloud banks.

"They'll corroborate," Ulyana said quietly, beside her. It wasn't a prediction. It was a wager, and Imogen could hear her laying the stakes on the rail between them.

"You trust them?"

"No," Ulyana said. "But I've watched them do arithmetic for two years, and last night the numbers changed on them. Corvane's dead or drowning, the fleet's impounded, and the only currency left on this coast is testimony. They know which side of that trade pays out." She turned the compass over in her hand, closed it, opened it again. "People like that don't stay loyal. They stay solvent. Right now we're the solvent thing."

Imogen almost laughed — a short, cracked sound that surprised her more than anyone. "You make it sound almost comforting."

"It's the most comforting thing I know," Ulyana said. "Loyalty you can't depend on. Solvency you can chart."

* * *

By mid-morning the fog had burned off entirely, which felt like a personal affront — as though the sky hadn't earned the right to be clear, not after the night it had put them through. The causeway filled. A second investigator arrived with a forensic photographer who spent an hour shooting the collapsed headland from every angle, and a salvage-safety officer who stood at the rail staring down at the shoal with the expression of a man doing depth calculations and disliking the answer. Word moved through the small crowd the way oil moves through water: *there was a wreck under there. Older than the charts. They brought it down on purpose.* Nobody said the rest of it — the manifests, the names, the fortune — because Imogen hadn't given that part yet, and Ulyana hadn't given anything at all beyond the seacocks. But Imogen could feel the rest of it pressing outward through the official questions like water through a hull seam, patient, inevitable.

A young constable brought them blankets and terrible vending-machine coffee from the ambulance, and for a while they simply sat, side by side on the rail like a pair of old women watching a regatta, while the machinery of inquiry assembled itself around them.

"You should sleep," Ulyana said eventually. "They'll want formal statements. Recorded, transcribed, the whole liturgy. You'll need to be sharp."

"So will you."

"I'm always sharp. It's the only thing I own outright." But there was no edge in it. Ulyana was looking down at the compass in her lap — her father's compass, recovered from a dead man's hidden journal-cache aboard a ship that no longer existed, and Imogen saw her thumb move across the glass in a slow circle, an unconscious gesture, the way you'd touch a scar. "Eleven days ago I thought he drowned twelve years back and I hated him a little for it. For leaving me the debt and the truck and nothing else. This morning I know he was murdered for trying to do what we just finished doing, and I—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "I don't know what I feel. There isn't a word for it. It's not grief. Grief has a floor. This just keeps going down."

"Give it time to find bottom," Imogen said. "Everything here does, eventually. Even wrecks."

Ulyana glanced at her, and for a moment something passed between them that wasn't quite humor and wasn't quite tears — the shared, exhausted recognition of two people who had walked each other to the edge of the world and back and would never again be strangers. "You're going to write all of it down, aren't you. Every name. Every number. Even with the paper at the bottom of the channel."

"Every name," Imogen said. "Forty-one families. A ship's company sold for insurance money by a family that's spent a century buying lighthouses and harbormasters and, apparently, storm chasers in debt." She flexed her splinted knee, winced. "Paper burns. Rock crushes. Memory's awkward to sink. I'm the worst possible vessel for Corvane's kind — I float."

The corner of Ulyana's mouth moved. It wasn't quite a smile, but it was headed in that direction, and Imogen counted it as the day's first genuine victory.

Down at the landing, the Voss brothers' boat bumped against the pilings. The younger one — Callum, the charming one, the one whose smile had always arrived a half-second before his meaning — stepped onto the causeway stones with his hands raised in a gesture that managed to be both surrender and greeting. He looked tired. He looked, for the first time since Imogen had known of him, genuinely uncertain, a man standing in the wreckage of his own ledger. His brother followed, silent, watchful, carrying the folded flag of some arrangement they'd evidently struck among themselves on the water.

Callum Voss stopped a few paces away and looked at Ulyana, then at Imogen, then out at the scarred headland, and whistled softly — a low, appreciative note, almost reverent.

"Well," he said. "You actually did it. Sank the whole inheritance." He shook his head slowly, and something in his face shifted — not remorse, Imogen thought, he probably didn't have the equipment for remorse, but something adjacent, something like awe at the scale of what had been given up. "Corvane would've paid ten million for what you just buried. Ten million, and you dropped it in the drink to spite him." He looked at Ulyana with something dangerously close to respect. "I genuinely cannot decide if that's the stupidest thing I've ever seen or the only smart thing anybody's done on this coast in a hundred years."

"Let us know when you decide," Ulyana said. "I hear the investigators are taking statements."

The elder Voss huffed — almost a laugh. The younger one grinned, and this time the grin arrived honestly, tired and rueful and stripped of charm, and Imogen understood that Ulyana had been right. The arithmetic had changed. These men were going to stand up in front of Okafor's recorder and tell the truth, not because it was right but because it was the last asset on the table, and because aligning with the survivors now cost nothing and might someday buy forgiveness, or at least leniency.

Callum Voss turned toward the police SUV, then paused, half-turned back.

"For what it's worth," he said, "the old man's file on your father — the one Corvane kept in the cabin safe? It burned with the ship. Whatever's in your head, doctor, whatever's in hers—" he nodded at Ulyana— "that's all there is now. You two are the archive." He tapped his own temple. "Same as us. Funny place to keep a hundred years of history. No lock on it at all."

He walked off toward the waiting investigators, hands still loose and visible, his brother falling in beside him, and Imogen watched them go and felt the full strange weight of the morning settle onto her shoulders at last: the exhaustion, yes, bone-deep and total, but threaded through it — unmistakably, undeniably — peace. Not happiness. Peace. The peace of a sealed hold, of a finished sounding, of a chart with the last blank space filled in.

Okafor came back down the causeway with her notebook closed and her recorder in hand, and she looked at the two of them sitting on the rail in the strengthening sun, and she said, "Dr. Hale. Ms. Ashgrove. Whenever you're ready, I'd like to begin the formal statements. Separately, standard procedure. We'll start whenever you say."

Imogen looked at Ulyana. Ulyana looked at the compass, closed it, and slipped it into her jacket pocket over her heart, and when she raised her eyes they were clear and steady and aimed forward, at the recorder, at the day, at whatever came next.

"Start with me," Ulyana said. "I've got twelve years to account for."
