## 21. Testimony

The hearing room smelled of wet wool and old varnish, the way government rooms always did on the coast — as if the sea had been allowed to seep into the walls and no one had ever bothered to chase it out. Ulyana sat at a scarred oak table beneath a window that framed a slice of gray harbor, and she watched the water while the clerk arranged his papers with the ceremony of a man laying out surgical instruments.

Three days since the surge. Three days since the Cape had swallowed its own history whole.

Her hands were still scabbed along the knuckles from the ballast channels, from the iron and the old stone, from the moment when she'd pulled the lever and felt the wreck shudder beneath her like something finally allowed to die. She kept flexing them now, without thinking, testing whether they were still hers.

"Ms. Ashgrove." The clerk — a narrow man named Pell, with spectacles that magnified his eyes into something apologetic — cleared his throat. "Before we begin, I'm required to inform you of your rights in this proceeding. You are not under arrest. You are here voluntarily."

"I know," Ulyana said. "I asked to be here."

That was true, and it was the strangest thing about it. Her whole life she had run from rooms like this one — from harbormasters' offices, from loan officers, from anyone holding a document with her name at the top. She had spent twelve years keeping moving because movement meant no one could pin anything to her: not a debt, not an address, not a truth. And now she had driven herself here through the storm's ragged aftermath, parked the chase truck in a public lot where anyone could find it, and walked in with nothing but her account and her name.

It was the only currency she had left. So she was going to spend all of it.

* * *

They began, as such things always did, with the small questions. Name. Occupation. Residence — "nomadic," she said, and saw Pell write it down without judgment, which she appreciated. Then the recorder was switched on, a red light burning steady on the table between them like a small patient eye, and Pell folded his hands and said, "Please tell me, in your own words, what happened at Cape Reckoning beginning six tides ago."

Ulyana looked at the window again. The harbor was recovering already — fishing boats going out, gulls wheeling over the breakwater, the ordinary machinery of a coast that had decided to forget. Somewhere out past the point, under forty feet of new rock and churning sand, lay the hold that had held everything: the manifest, the survivors' marks scratched into the hull timber by people who had known they were being drowned, the proof that Corvane's fortune — Corvane's family, three generations of shipping money and civic philanthropy, their name on the hospital wing and the maritime museum — had been built on a murder committed slowly, deliberately, a century before anyone now living drew breath.

All of it gone. Buried on purpose, by her hand, with Hale's help, in the only grave deep enough to keep it safe.

Except it wasn't gone. Not entirely. Because memory was a kind of salvage, and testimony was a kind of cargo, and she was carrying both.

"It started with a job," she said. "The Voss brothers hired me to recover instruments from a missing survey team. I want that entered plainly: they hired me before anyone knew the team was dead or alive, and what they actually wanted — though they didn't say so at first — was a sealed case from an uncharted wreck, retrieved ahead of a salvage fleet backed by Aldous Corvane."

Pell's pen stopped. He looked up. "You understand what you're alleging."

"I understand exactly what I'm alleging." She heard her own voice come out level, and was quietly proud of it. "Corvane's fleet arrived off the Cape within two tides of my taking the contract. Three black-hulled vessels. They didn't search the coast — they watched it. Their master had a file on me. He knew my father's boat's name." She paused there, because that detail still had teeth. "He'd been waiting for someone to lead him to that wreck for a long time. My father found it first, years ago. He hid what he found. He was silenced for it. Dr. Imogen Hale can corroborate the survey evidence, and I'll give you her location when we're done here — she's been in protective hiding and she'll come forward herself, but she should come forward knowing this record exists first."

"Why?" Pell asked. It wasn't a challenge; it was genuine curiosity, and something in it made Ulyana trust him a little. "Why bury the evidence? Why destroy a historical site that could have vindicated you completely?"

Ulyana had expected the question. She had turned it over for three days, in the truck, in the cheap motel where she and Hale had slept in shifts, in the long gray hours before dawn. She had answers ready — the fleet would have taken it, the records would have been litigated into oblivion, Corvane's lawyers could drown paper faster than the sea drowned ships. All true. All secondary.

"Because some things shouldn't be owned," she said. "The moment those records surfaced, they became a commodity. Corvane would have bought them or burned them. The government would have sealed them. Someone would have made them a weapon or a wall. The families on that manifest — the Ashgroves among them — they've been dead a hundred years. The only thing left that anyone could do to them was sell them." She met Pell's magnified gaze. "I refused to let that happen. So I sank the proof and kept the story. That's what I'm giving you now. The story. Freely. On the record. Unsellable."

The red light burned. Pell wrote for a long time.

* * *

They broke at midday, and Ulyana ate a sandwich on the courthouse steps with her back against cold granite, watching the tide come in below the seawall. Hale found her there — summoned, as agreed, arriving in a borrowed coat two sizes too big, looking like a woman who had been underwater for a month and was only now remembering how air worked.

"They're treating you well in there?" Hale asked.

"They're treating me like a witness. Which is either respect or a trap; I haven't decided."

Hale lowered herself onto the step beside her. Up close, the surveyor's exhaustion had a geological quality, layer upon layer. "You know what happens after this," she said. "Even with Corvane dead. Even with the Vosses testifying. A fortune like that doesn't die with one man. There are cousins. Trustees. Holding companies with names like weather systems. Somewhere, someone is going to decide that the woman who gave the testimony is a problem that outlived its usefulness."

"I know."

"And you're doing it anyway."

Ulyana turned the sandwich wrapper over in her fingers. Out on the water, a trawler was running in ahead of the afternoon wind, riding the same swells she'd have been chasing a year ago for a client's data, for a few hundred dollars against the ledger. She thought about how she used to read the sea — as distance, as fuel, as miles between her and whatever was behind her.

"My father spent the last year of his life sitting on the truth," she said, "because he thought keeping it hidden was keeping himself and everyone with his name safe. And it got him killed anyway, and the truth stayed buried, and the men who killed him got hospitals named after them." She crumpled the wrapper. "I'm done paying rent on silence. If they come for me, they come for me in the open, and there'll be a record saying why. That's worth more than safety. I finally believe that."

Hale studied her for a moment. Then, unexpectedly, she smiled — tired, crooked, real. "You sound like your father's journal. The last entry. He wrote that he'd rather be a name in a story than a shadow in a ledger."

Ulyana's throat tightened. She hadn't told anyone she'd read that line aloud, standing in the flooded hold with the storm's eye passing overhead, her father's handwriting swimming in her headlamp beam. "Then I suppose it runs in the family."

"Afternoon session," Pell called from the doorway, polite as a tide table. "Ms. Hale — they're ready for you whenever you are."

* * *

The afternoon was longer, and harder, because the questions got smaller and sharper. Times. Distances. The exact wording of what the Voss brothers had promised her. Whether she had accepted payment. Whether she had intended, at any point, to deliver the case to them.

"No," she said. "And yes, and neither. I took the contract because I was drowning in debt and they knew it. I planned to give them false coordinates, which I did. And then I planned to disappear, which I didn't, because I found Dr. Hale and everything changed." She leaned forward. "But I want to be precise about something, Mr. Pell, because precision is all I have left to trade. The Voss brothers didn't know what Corvane was. They were middlemen who thought they were middlemen. When they figured out the shape of the real deal — when Corvane offered to buy me off them — they cut it. That's in your record somewhere, isn't it? Their arrangement?"

Pell's expression flickered. "There is... correspondence. Intercepted. You're confirming it?"

"I'm confirming it. And then I'm telling you the part that isn't in any file." She took a breath. Here it was — the part she had argued with herself about for three days, the part that could hang her or save her depending on how the wind sat. "At the surge, when the wreck came down, the Voss brothers were on the water. They had every reason to let us drown — Corvane was dead, the case was worthless, and two witnesses were loose ends. Instead they turned their boats toward us and pulled Dr. Hale out of the water themselves. I don't know why. Maybe the ledger changed for them. Maybe it was the first honest arithmetic they ever did. But they saved her life, and I want that on the record too, because testimony that only burns people isn't testimony. It's revenge. I'm not here for revenge."

The room was quiet. Outside, the gulls argued over the harbor.

"You understand," Pell said at last, gently, "that what you're describing — destroying a protected maritime site, however justified — carries consequences. There will be charges considered. Fines, at minimum. Possibly more."

"I know." And here was the strange thing, the thing she'd carry out of this room like a stone she'd chosen to pick up: it didn't frighten her. "Clear my debt with the law the same way I cleared it everywhere else — by paying. Whatever it costs, I'll pay it in the only coin I've got. Hours. Years. Work. I'm good at work. What I won't do is take it back."

* * *

It was near dusk when the door opened without a knock and the Voss brothers came in.

Ulyana had last seen them hauling Hale aboard their boat, black with seawater, their charm stripped down to something rawer underneath. Now they stood in the doorway of the hearing room in clean shirts, and for once neither of them smiled. The younger one — Callum, the talker, the one who'd explained the contract with that cheerful menace — looked almost embarrassed. The elder, Brogan, just looked tired in the way of men who had done sums all night and hated the total.

"We'll be brief," Brogan said to Pell. "We're here to corroborate."

Pell blinked. "You're aware that your own exposure—"

"We're aware." Callum stepped forward, and Ulyana watched him wrestle his smile down like a man restraining a dog. "Everything Ms. Ashgrove said this morning is accurate. The contract, the false coordinates, the side deal with Corvane — ours, not hers, and we'll sign to that. We were played, and worse, we tried to play along, and the only reason we're standing here instead of answering for a drowning is that we changed our minds at the last possible minute." He glanced at Ulyana, and something passed across his face that she'd never seen there before. It might have been shame. In the Voss family, it probably passed for it. "She fed us lies to protect a dying woman. We sold her out to protect ourselves. The arithmetic was never close."

Brogan set a folder on the table — contracts, correspondence, the whole architecture of the deal, laid open like a dissected fish. "We're settling our accounts," he said simply. "All of them."

After they'd gone, Ulyana sat for a while in the emptying room, listening to the building settle around her. Pell gathered his papers. The red light went off.

"That's everything," he said. "You understand what you've done today. The record exists now. It can't be buried, but it also can't be unsaid — and there are people who will not forgive you for saying it."

"I counted on that," Ulyana said. She rose, and her knees ached, and her knuckles throbbed, and she realized she was smiling. "A target means I'm standing still long enough to be aimed at. First time in twelve years." She shook Pell's hand. "Thank you for asking the small questions properly. Most people skip them."

Outside, the evening had come up clear and cold, the first clean sky since the storm. Hale was waiting by the chase truck, breath fogging, and beyond her the causeway ran out toward the Cape, dark and quiet, keeping what it kept.

"So," Hale said. "Paid in full?"

"Paid in full." Ulyana opened the driver's door, then paused, one hand on the frame, looking down the coast road — the road she had driven a thousand times running away, and was not driving tonight. "Imogen. How do you feel about staying in one place for a while? Long enough to write it all down. Properly. Every name on that manifest. Every mile."

Hale's answer was already forming when, far out past the breakwater, a single boat's running light appeared — holding steady, holding course, pointed straight at the Cape.
