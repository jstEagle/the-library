## 1. The Thief Who Wasn't

The hardpan east of Perdition Flats held footprints the way a grudge holds memory — stubbornly, and long after anyone sensible had stopped caring. Dominic Eastwick crouched over one of them, forefinger hovering a hair's breadth above the impression without touching it, and read it like a letter from an old friend.

Size ten boot, worn on the outer heel. A man who favored his right side, either from habit or from an old injury he'd never had looked at, because men like that never did. The stride was short — tired, or careful, or both — and the left prints dug deeper than the right, which told Dominic his quarry was carrying weight slung off the left shoulder. A pack. Not heavy. Thirty pounds, maybe thirty-five.

He'd been following this particular letter for two days now, across forty miles of country that would kill a man for forgetting his hat, and every mile of it had been a conversation conducted in silence between him and someone who didn't know they were having it.

*You slept here. You were proud of finding this overhang — good eye, it faces away from the morning wind. You ate beans out of a can and buried the can because you're not a total animal. You cut your hand two nights ago; you wrapped it with tape instead of stopping the bleeding properly, which means you're scared of something more than infection.*

Dominic straightened, rolled his shoulders until something in his back cracked like dry kindling, and allowed himself the small luxury of being pleased. Ten years in the service and the pleasure never dulled. There was a version of satisfaction in reading ground that nothing else had ever come close to matching — not the commendations, not the paychecks, not even the rare, startled gratitude of people whose husbands or daughters he'd carried out of places the maps drew as blank white space.

The thief himself was almost beside the point. A kid, probably. Somebody had lifted a cash box from the trading post at Perdition Flats — four hundred dollars and a tin of somebody's grandmother's jewelry — and the county had shrugged and called it a job for the rangers, and the rangers had called it a job for Dominic, because Dominic was the one they called when the job was supposed to be impossible.

That was the arrangement. That was the whole architecture of his life, built so carefully over ten years that he sometimes forgot he'd built it: be useful, be excellent, be *gone*. Show up, solve the unsolvable, file the report, drive three hundred miles to the next district before anyone could learn the shape of his face well enough to miss it. He'd gotten so good at leaving that leaving had become less an act than a condition, like altitude, like thirst. Something his body simply existed in.

It worked. He wanted to be clear about that, even in the privacy of his own skull where honesty was the only currency that couldn't be counterfeited: it worked. Nobody waited up for him. Nobody asked him questions he didn't want to answer. Nobody looked at him across a kitchen table and said *where were you* in a voice that already knew.

Nobody looked at him at all, really. And some mornings, standing at a trailhead with frost on his beard and the sun coming up over rock that had been old before there was anything alive to see it, Dominic found that the silence of his life fit him like a coat tailored by a master — and that he still, some mornings, caught himself checking the pockets for something that wasn't there.

He followed the boot prints north-northeast, into country where the hardpan gave way to broken shale and the shale gave way to the first gray shoulders of the Devil's Throat range, still sixty miles off but visible now, snow-capped even in late summer, wearing its glaciers like old scars. The kid was heading for the mountains. That was stupid. That was also, Dominic had learned over a decade of chasing stupid men through stupid country, completely normal. People ran toward the thing that frightened them because open ground felt worse. Open ground meant you could be seen all the way to the horizon, and somewhere behind you there was always a horizon.

The tracks climbed. They got fresher. By midafternoon Dominic was close enough that he'd started doing the thing he did at the end of every pursuit — slowing down, opening his senses wide, letting the country talk to him instead of the trail. A raven croaked twice off to the west and went silent, which meant something had moved through its territory recently enough to annoy it. A juniper's lower branches were scuffed at knee height. Fresh-cut sagebrush smell hung in a wind shadow, sharp as a struck match.

The kid was within a mile. Maybe less.

And then the trail ended.

Not faded. Not scattered by wind or erased by rock. *Ended.* At the mouth of a shallow drainage choked with boulders the size of trucks, the boot prints walked in — size ten, left-heavy, carrying their thirty pounds of stolen cash box — and then simply stopped, in the middle of a flat apron of sand, as though the man who made them had been lifted off the earth by the collar of his shirt.

Dominic stood at the edge of it for a long time.

He circled. He always circled; the ground always lied first and confessed later. He read the sand downwind of the last print, upwind, crosswind. He checked the boulders for handholds, for brushed lichen, for the faint polish a boot leaves on stone after a hundred placements. He lay flat on his belly in the dirt like a man praying to a god he didn't believe in, eyes level with the surface, letting the low light rake across it and throw shadows from disturbances too subtle to see from above.

Nothing. No exit. No climb. No crawl. No body.

There were only two explanations, and both of them were bad. Either the kid had possessed tracking skills that would embarrass Dominic — possible, but Dominic had spent ten years learning exactly how much he didn't know, and the margin was wider than most men's egos — or someone else had found the kid first, and had known precisely what they were doing. Someone who could walk across soft sand and leave no more trace than weather. Someone who understood that the art of vanishing isn't erasure. It's *arrangement*. Making the ground say what you want it to say.

Dominic sat on a boulder as the light went copper and then rose, and ate cold beans from a tin, and thought about the survey team.

It came unbidden, the way those thoughts always did lately — the ones about the seven people from the Geological Survey who'd walked into the Devil's Throat range eleven weeks ago and never walked out. Seven experienced field people with satellite phones, emergency beacons, resupply caches, and a flight plan filed with three separate offices. The search had lasted nine days and covered four hundred square miles. Helicopters, dog teams, infrared flights at dawn when the cold ground contrasted warm bodies. They'd found the team's base camp intact — food cooked, beds made, radios silent — and then they'd found nothing, and the search had been formally suspended, and the file had been closed with the word *presumed* in it, which was the service's way of burying people without the expense of a funeral.

Dominic hadn't been on that search. He'd been two districts over, pulling a drowned boy out of a reservoir, and by the time he was free the grid squares had all been walked and re-walked. He'd read the report afterward, cover to cover, three times, the way he read everything — hunting for the detail everyone else had stepped past. And there had been one. One line, buried in the terrain notes of a junior officer, describing a set of tracks near the base camp that "terminated without apparent cause."

Terminated without apparent cause. The same phrase he'd have written tonight, if he wrote reports anymore, which he mostly didn't, because reports required staying in one place long enough to write them.

The same signature. He was almost sure of it. Someone who could end a trail in open sand — cleanly, deliberately, with the confidence of a person who knew no one alive could pick it up again.

Dominic finished the beans and looked at the mountains going blue and then black against the last of the light, and felt something move in his chest that he hadn't let move in a long time. Not dread, exactly. Dread was a familiar tenant; he knew its footsteps. This was older. This was the feeling of a lock he'd thrown shut years ago beginning, very quietly, to turn.

Because here was the truth he kept in the bottom drawer under the commendations: he was good at finding things because he had spent ten years practicing on the one thing that mattered. Himself. He had erased his own trail so thoroughly — out of her life, out of that town, out of every life his duty had touched and damaged — that sometimes he wondered if there was anything left to find. And now the desert had put a vanished man in front of him whose disappearance looked exactly like his own handiwork, and some part of him that he'd thought was dead sat up and paid attention.

His radio crackled at dusk. Dispatch, relaying a message from District Supervisor Marsh. Dominic listened to the whole thing before answering, standing in the dark with the boulder field around him full of sounds that might have been wind and might not.

"Eastwick, acknowledge. Trail's cold, subject's presumed out of district, and you are reminded that the Throat range is outside your operational area pending the seasonal closure order. Return to Perdition Flats. That is an instruction, not a suggestion."

Marsh's voice had the flat administrative calm of a man reading words someone else had written. Dominic had known Ray Marsh for six years. Marsh had signed off on every one of Dominic's transfers, every one of his disappearances, and had never once asked why a ranger with Dominic's record requested the postings nobody wanted. Marsh protected him the way a wall protects something — without curiosity, without warmth, and without questions.

"Acknowledged," Dominic said, and paused, and the pause stretched a half-second longer than discipline allowed. "Tell me about the survey team's leader."

Static. Then, carefully: "That file's closed, Eastwick. Stand down."

"Who was running it?"

A longer static this time. Long enough that Dominic could hear Marsh deciding something on the other end of the state.

"Elena Vasquez," Marsh said finally. "You know that. Everyone knows that. Come home, Dominic."

Home. The word landed in Dominic's chest and found nowhere to set down.

He keyed off the radio without answering. He took out his map, unfolded it across a boulder, and studied the range by headlamp while moths threw themselves drunkenly against the light. The seasonal closure order was real — the high passes would start closing inside a month, and once the calving season opened on the glaciers, the Throat itself would be impassable except for a narrow window nobody could predict. Going in now was against orders. Going in now was, officially, career suicide for a man who had spent a decade making sure his career was the only thing he had.

He folded the map. He packed his camp in nine minutes, which was four minutes slower than his best time and he noted that with professional displeasure. He filled his water bottles at the seep he'd marked on the way in.

Then he shouldered his pack, turned his back on Perdition Flats and on the radio and on ten years of flawless, invisible obedience, and started walking toward the mountains.

The kid with the cash box was out there somewhere, alive or dead, and the kid mattered — Dominic didn't lie to himself about that either; four hundred dollars was four hundred dollars to whoever had earned it. But the kid was also a thread, and threads were how the desert talked when it wanted to tell you something true. Someone in those mountains could end a trail in open sand. Someone had taught seven surveyors to vanish. And eleven weeks ago, Elena Vasquez had walked into the Devil's Throat range with a filed flight plan and a satellite phone, and had apparently decided, with the same competence that had made her the youngest team lead in Survey history, never to be found.

Dominic climbed through the boulder field in the dark, alone, his breath hanging white in the cold, and for the first time in ten years he did not check over his shoulder to make sure no one was following.

He was done leaving. He just didn't know yet what he was walking toward.

* * *

Two days later, at the tree line, he found the second set of tracks.

They were smaller than the kid's. Lighter. A woman's boot, size eight, with a distinctive diagonal wear pattern on the left heel — the print of someone who favored that leg, who'd been favoring it a long time. The tracks came out of a hidden drainage that didn't appear on any map Dominic owned, crossed a talus slope in a line so efficient it looked drawn with a ruler, and ended at the edge of a snowfield.

Where they, too, terminated without apparent cause.

Dominic knelt in the snowmelt with his heart going hard and slow, the way it did when the country handed him something important, and touched the edge of the last print. It was days old. Weeks, maybe. But it was hers — he was sure of it the way he was sure of weather coming, the way he'd once been sure of other things about her, in another life, before duty had taught him the price of certainty.

Elena Vasquez was alive. She was in these mountains. And she was very, very good at not being found.

Dominic stood, and looked up at the white shoulders of the Throat, and began to climb.
