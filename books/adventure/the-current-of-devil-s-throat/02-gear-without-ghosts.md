## 2. Gear Without Ghosts

The survey camp sat in a bowl of granite below the tree line like a place that had been paused rather than abandoned.

Dominic saw it first as a wrongness of color — a rectangle of orange nylon where there should have been nothing but gray stone and the last stubborn pines — and then he did what a decade of this work had taught him to do: he stopped. He crouched at the edge of a moraine ridge two hundred meters out and let his eyes do the walking.

Wind moved through the bowl in long slow breaths, lifting spindrift off the talus in veils that caught the morning light and let it go. The tent stood square. The guy lines were taut. A tarp was strung between two boulders over what looked like a kitchen area, and beneath it he could make out the shapes of pots, a stove, a water jug set on a flat rock like an offering. Nothing had been torn down. Nothing had been scattered by animals or weather or panic.

Camps that end badly look ended. He'd seen them all — the shredded nylon, the gear strewn downslope, the dark stains you didn't need training to read. This camp looked like its people had stepped out for a walk and meant to come back before dinner.

That was the first thing about it that made the hair rise along his forearms.

He came down off the ridge in a wide arc, reading the ground as he went, and found the perimeter almost immediately because it had been *made* — not with wire or flags but with habit. Boot traffic worn into a loop between the tent and the kitchen tarp. A latrine site tucked downwind behind a boulder, properly dug, properly filled. A cache of food barrels lashed under a rock overhang, sealed against bears and marmots with the kind of care that spoke of someone who'd run field camps her whole life.

He stood at the edge of it all and felt the loneliness of the place come up through his boot soles like cold.

"Okay," he said quietly, to no one. "Show me."

* * *

Inside the main tent, four sleeping bags were rolled and stowed against the walls. Four foam pads lay stacked. Four duffels sat zipped, their compression straps cinched neat as church pews. Dominic knelt among them without touching anything at first, just looking, letting the details assemble themselves into a story.

The story they told was impossible.

Beds made. That was the phrase his mind kept returning to, circling it like a bird over carrion. People who abandon a camp in an emergency do not make their beds. People who leave in an orderly fashion pack their bags and take them. These bags were packed and *left*. The stove in the kitchen tarp still held a pot, and when he lifted the lid — using a stick, because some habits are older than others — he found oatmeal dried to a pale crust at the bottom. Cooked. Served from, judging by the four bowls hanging from a line, washed and inverted to drain. Breakfast eaten, dishes done, beds made, bags packed.

And then everyone had walked into the mountains and vanished.

He checked the radio next, because that was the detail that mattered most. The survey team's base station sat on a crate near the tent door, a ruggedized VHF unit with a solar panel wired outside. Its power light was dark. He opened the case. The battery was disconnected — not dead, not drained, but deliberately unclipped, the leads coiled and tucked beside it with the same tidy competence that had made the beds.

Someone had turned the camp off. Not shut it down — camps get shut down, struck, packed out. This one had been put to sleep. Left exactly as it would be if the occupants intended to return, except for every single thing a returning party would actually need: radios, packs, boots — he checked the duffels — spare boots, all present. Four sets of mountaineering gear hung on a line under the tarp, ropes coiled, crampons bagged. Whoever walked away from this camp had walked away in whatever they were wearing, carrying nothing, intending never to come back.

Or intending very much for whoever came looking to believe they hadn't gone far.

Dominic sat back on his heels inside the tent doorway and looked out at the bright, indifferent morning, and felt the dread arrive — not sharp, the way fear arrives, but heavy, the way certainty arrives. Because he knew this signature. He'd spent three days tracking a petty thief across hardpan whose trail had ended the same way: mid-stride, unbroken, as if the man had been lifted off the earth. And now here it was again, writ large across an entire government operation. Someone in these mountains knew how trackers thought. Knew that a tidy camp says *we'll be back*, and a dead radio says *emergency*, and a missing trail says *accident*, and all of it together says *stop looking, close the file, write the report.*

Someone wanted to be unfound by people who know how to find.

And there was only one person he'd ever known who could stage a disappearance with this much craft. One person whose hands he could feel in every knot and every swept patch of ground, the way you can feel a writer in a sentence.

"Elena," he said, and the name went out of him like breath into cold air, visible for a moment and then gone.

* * *

He worked the camp for two hours anyway, because obsession is just discipline pointed at the wrong thing, and he wasn't yet ready to admit which way his was pointing.

The kitchen told him numbers. Food stores calculated for six weeks; they'd been in the field eleven days when the record went silent. The latrine told him timing — use patterns tapering off over the final days, then nothing. The fire pit told him fuel discipline, ash buried, unburned scraps packed out. The cache told him something else entirely: three of the four core sample cases were still there, aluminum transit cases, government-stenciled, locked. But the fourth case's slot in the foam was empty, and around it the dust of the overhang floor had been disturbed in a way that had been *un*-disturbed — brushed, smoothed, faked back to innocence by someone who understood that trackers read absence more easily than presence.

Four cases in. Three cases out. One case of geological core samples, weighing perhaps fifteen pounds, had left this camp in human hands and had not come back.

Dominic stood in the cold shade of the overhang holding that fact the way you hold a live wire, and the dread in his chest sharpened another degree and changed flavor. Because samples don't walk. Samples don't get lost. Samples are either where they're supposed to be, or they are somewhere someone specifically wanted them to be — and everything else about this camp, every made bed and coiled rope and disconnected battery, was built to say *nothing happened here.* Except the one thing somebody had forgotten to fake, or hadn't thought mattered, or had wanted found by exactly the right pair of eyes.

His supervisor's voice came back to him across three hundred miles of desert: *Stand down, Eastwick. The Throat's a graveyard this time of year. Let the mountain keep what it took.*

The mountain hadn't taken anything. That was the whole point. The mountain was being used as a lock, and Elena Vasquez had swallowed the key.

* * *

He ate standing up, watching the bowl of the valley, because a man alone in country like this learns to eat with his eyes open. Then he began the search he'd been avoiding, working outward from the camp in expanding spirals, reading the ground the way other men read print.

They'd been careful. God, they'd been careful — rock-hopping drainages, walking in each other's prints for the first hundred meters, splitting up onto bare stone where boots leave nothing. But six people cannot move through living terrain without leaving *something*, and after an hour he found it: a thread of evidence strung northwest from the camp, faint as a whispered sentence. A scuffed lichen patch on a boulder where a boot edge had slipped. A single strand of blue polypropylene cord snagged in kinnikinnick, cut clean — trimmed, not torn, trimmed so it wouldn't flutter and catch a searcher's eye. A place where snowmelt had been crossed and the far bank showed the ghost of weight in the mud, already drying back to anonymity.

One set of prints. Not six. One person had broken from the group's shared trail and gone northwest alone, light-footed, deliberate, toward the black seam in the valley wall that Dominic had been noticing all morning without wanting to name it.

A slot canyon. The map called it Corrie Draw, which was a cartographer's joke, because a corrie is a gentle amphitheater and what cut into the flank of the mountain ahead of him was a wound — twenty meters deep in places, barely a body's width wide, walled in polished granite that funneled wind and meltwater and, in bad years, the full screaming violence of the spring release. It dead-ended against the headwall two kilometers in. Everyone local knew it went nowhere. Which was, of course, precisely why someone might go there.

Dominic stood at its mouth as afternoon light failed to reach the bottom, and the air that breathed out of it was ten degrees colder than the day around it, smelling of wet stone and old ice. His heart was going hard and slow again, that prospector's rhythm, and he made himself stand still and think instead of follow, because following was easy and thinking was the job.

She had staged the vanishing of five other people. She had cooked a breakfast nobody ate and made beds nobody slept in and disconnected radios she needed silent, and she had done it all so thoroughly that a search team would find a mystery with no solution and go home. She had taken one case of samples and left three, and she had walked into a canyon that goes nowhere, alone, carrying nothing but whatever she knew.

You don't hide from rescue that well unless you're hiding from something worse than the mountain.

He thought about the thief on the hardpan, whose trail had ended the same impossible way, and understood suddenly that he'd been reading the first page of this story for weeks without knowing it. Somebody had helped that man disappear too. Or the same hand had passed through both stories — a hand that knew how trackers think because it had been trained by the same service, shaped by the same doctrine, taught by the same instructors he'd sat under a decade ago.

His own methods, turned against his own agency. It was almost elegant. It was absolutely hers.

"Dominic," he said to himself, warningly, out loud, the way you'd say *careful* to a man leaning over a drop. Because there were two ways this went. He could log the camp, photograph the samples, radio it in — the professional act, the act that would put a hundred searchers and eventually a helicopter into this bowl within a day, and put Elena Vasquez's fate into the hands of the institution she was clearly hiding *from*. Or he could go into the canyon alone and unannounced, off the books, past the point where his badge meant anything, and find out why a woman that competent was willing to bury herself alive in a hole in the earth rather than be found by her own government.

The wind came down the slot and pushed against his chest like a hand, like the mountain offering an opinion.

He clipped his pack closed, checked the angle of the late sun against the headwall — two hours of light in the canyon bottom, maybe less — and stepped in out of the daylight.

The walls closed over him the way water closes over a dropped stone. And forty meters in, where the narrows pinched tight and the light went to a blue ribbon overhead, he stopped, because there on the sandy floor, plain as a signature, fresh enough that the wind hadn't yet softened the edges, ran a single line of boot prints heading deeper into the throat of the draw.

And beside them, placed on a stone at knee height where no natural eye would ever fall but a tracker's would — a small brass instrument case, government-stamped, unlocked, and open.

Empty.
