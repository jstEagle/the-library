## 3. What the Canyon Keeps

Elena Vasquez had buried men before — not bodies, but names. She had written them into reports that were then rewritten by people who had never stood on the ground they were describing. She had watched her own field notes come back to her sanitized, softened, stripped of everything inconvenient. So when the time came to make herself disappear, she did it the way she'd learned to do everything in this service: carefully, and with a certain bitter craftsmanship.

The slot canyon was called Serrano's Cut on the old maps and nothing at all on the new ones, which was precisely why she'd chosen it. It ran narrow and deep between walls of rust-colored limestone, so tight in places that she could press both palms against the rock at once and feel the mountain's cold pulse through the stone. At its head, where the canyon pinched shut against a wall of shattered talus, there was a hollow — a collapse chamber, maybe, or an old water-carved room the flash floods had hollowed out centuries ago. She had found it on the third day of the survey, when the others were still sleeping off their first night in the high country.

She had been carrying the core samples for eleven days now. Six aluminum tubes, each one wrapped in a wool sock and packed in a padded case that had once held seismic instruments. They rode against her spine inside her pack like a second skeleton, and she had begun to think of them that way — as bones, as the true structure of things, hidden under all this soft lying flesh of paperwork.

Because that was what the cores were. Bones. Proof.

She knelt at the seep where the canyon floor wept groundwater and filled her bottle, and while the water ran cold and mineral-bright over her hands, she let herself remember the moment it had all turned. Not the samples themselves — those had simply confirmed what she'd suspected from the first week, when the consortium's geological survey maps had shown ore bodies exactly where no honest geology would put them. No, the turning had come later, in the survey tent, going through the administrative packet they'd all signed off on without reading. Land-use waivers. Environmental impact exemptions. And at the bottom of the exemption filings, authorizing access to the protected basin under "emergency resource provisions," a signature.

*Marcus Hale.*

She had known the name before she knew why she knew it. Then she had remembered — a dinner seven years ago, a man in a ranger's uniform sitting too straight beside her, talking about the director who'd taken him in after his father died. *Hale. He's the reason I'm still standing.* Dominic Eastwick had said it the way other men talked about saints.

And Marcus Hale's signature sat at the bottom of documents that would hand forty thousand acres of protected basin to the Meridian Consortium, based on core data that her own drills had just proven fabricated.

She capped the bottle and pressed her forehead briefly against the cool stone. The canyon kept her secrets the way it kept water — grudgingly, and only because it had no choice.

* * *

Three miles south and eight hundred feet higher, Dominic Eastwick read the ground the way other men read letters from home.

He had picked the trail back up at dawn, where it crossed the shale bench above the dry wash — boot prints, size nine, a woman's stride by the spacing, loaded heavy. The prints were deliberate. Whoever made them had walked on stone wherever stone allowed, had stepped in the shadow side of every rise, had even backtracked twenty yards along her own line to confuse anyone reading from behind. It was good work. It was, in fact, the same work he'd seen yesterday at the place where the thief's trail had simply ended, as if the man had been lifted off the earth.

Except now he understood it wasn't a trick of the terrain. Someone out here had been trained to vanish by people who trained rangers. And the thief — small, quick, nervous, a poacher's build — had never made prints like these. He'd followed the wrong ghost into the range and gotten swallowed by it.

Dominic crouched over the print and let himself feel the thing he refused to name. Ten years of erasing himself, ten years of taking assignments nobody else wanted because empty country asked nothing of you, and here was a trail that spoke his own language fluently. Every evasion in it was a sentence he could have written. It felt less like tracking than like eavesdropping on a conversation he'd been having with himself for a decade.

His radio crackled. He'd muted it to a whisper after leaving the truck; now he thumbed the volume up just enough.

"Eastwick, confirm position." Reyes. His protégé's voice was careful, official, which meant someone was standing next to him. Which meant Hale.

"Copy, Reyes. Still working the theft case down-valley."

"Director wants an update on your availability. Search operation's mobilizing for the missing survey team."

"I heard." He paused, watching a hawk ride the thermal above the rim. "Tell the Director I'm where he sent me."

A silence on the line, long enough to mean something. Then: "Copy that." The channel went dead, and Dominic stood in the wind and thought about how many ways a man could lie without saying anything untrue.

Then he turned north, toward the slot canyons, and began to close.

* * *

Elena spent the afternoon doing what she'd done every day since the disappearance: staying alive, and hating how much of her energy it took.

The staging had cost her two full days. She'd cooked a final meal in the main camp — real food, pans left soaking, the domestic theater of people who meant to come back. She'd made four beds. She'd powered down the radios properly, logged a fake last transmission about moving up-survey to the eastern benches. Then she'd walked out alone, in boots that belonged to Tomás Okafor, half a size too large, because if they ever found the prints they should belong to a man. One set of tracks, leading into Serrano's Cut, ending at the choke point where any tracker would assume the worst: flood, fall, foul play. A dead end in every sense.

The rest of the team she'd sent out three days earlier with the supply runner, each of them carrying sealed orders to open only after reaching the highway — orders that told them nothing except to say nothing until she contacted them. They'd think she was being paranoid about the consortium's security detail. She hadn't corrected them. Paranoia was easier to sign off on than treason.

Because that was what it was, wasn't it? That was the word that would be used. She had falsified a federal survey's end date, misappropriated government equipment, and vanished with evidence belonging, technically, to the United States. If they caught her, none of the rest would matter. The cores would be confiscated, chain of custody would route them straight back through the agency, through the directorate, through Hale, and six months later some junior analyst would re-run the assays and find them *inconclusive*, and the basin would open for lease, and the words *protected* and *wilderness* would quietly stop applying to it.

That was the machine. She had fed it for twelve years. She had believed, right up until she saw that signature, that the machine was broken in places but sound at the frame — that bad results were anomalies, that the system caught its own errors eventually. Her whole career had been built on that faith. Her ambition had been built on it; she'd wanted the directorship herself someday, wanted to be the one who fixed the broken places.

Now she knew the frame itself was rotten, and the knowledge sat in her chest like the cold in the canyon — total, patient, coming from everywhere.

So she trusted the wilderness instead. The wilderness didn't file reports. The wilderness didn't have a director whose signature moved land from the public to the powerful with a stroke of a pen. The canyon kept her, and asked only that she keep herself.

At dusk she climbed to her overlook — a shelf of limestone halfway up the cut's western wall, screened by a fallen slab — and ate cold beans and looked south across the darkening basin. Somewhere out there, the search would be forming. Helicopters maybe, or horses, or men on foot with the kind of eyes she used to train recruits to develop. She should have been afraid of them. She was. But underneath the fear, braided through it so tightly she couldn't separate the strands anymore, was fury — white and clean and getting cleaner every day.

They would send someone good. They always did. And that person would walk into this range believing they were looking for survivors, and they would be hunting her instead, and they wouldn't even know it.

She finished the beans. Below her, the canyon breathed its evening breath, warm air rising off the stone in slow columns. She checked the sample case out of habit, counted six tubes through the fabric, zipped it again.

Eleven days. The pass through Devil's Throat opened with the calving season — everyone in the survey had talked about it, the way the glacier's retreat unlocked the high route for a few days each year before the ice re-formed. Three days of open throat, maybe less. On the far side was the state line, the university lab in Corvallis that owed her a favor, and a chain of custody no one in the directorate could touch.

Three days. She could do three days. She'd done harder things with worse odds.

She was wrong about one thing, though. She lay back against the stone and closed her eyes, and she assumed that whoever came for her would be a stranger.

* * *

Dominic reached the mouth of Serrano's Cut as the last light failed.

He almost missed it — that was the thing. Even knowing what to look for, even reading the approach slope twice, he found the entry trail only because a single displaced pebble had rolled downhill instead of uphill, a contradiction so small it might have been an accident of frost. But it wasn't. Nothing out here was accident. The pebble said: *someone passed here who did not want the mountain to know.*

He stood at the canyon mouth in the blue dusk, and the cold air flowing out of the cut touched his face like a held breath finally released. Slot country. Tight, deep, dangerous in weather, and — he tilted his head, listening — silent. No drip. No birds. The kind of silence a place kept when something inside it was listening back.

One set of prints led in. He followed them fifty yards by flashlight, careful, weight on the edges of his boots, and tracked them to the choke point where the walls pinched together over a jumble of collapsed talus. The prints ended there. Cleanly. Too cleanly — no scuff of struggle, no slide marks, no debris pattern consistent with a fall. Just a woman's stride walking into stone and stopping, as if the earth had accepted her.

Dominic played the light along the walls, upward, slowly. Twenty feet up, on the western side, a shelf behind a fallen slab — and on the lip of the shelf, catching the beam for half a second before he angled the light away, the faintest shine of disturbed dust.

Someone was up there. Someone alive, because dead people didn't adjust their hiding places, and that dust had been adjusted within the day.

He killed the light and stood in the absolute dark of the canyon, heart steady, mind anything but. Protocol said call it in. Protocol said Reyes, backup, extraction, a helicopter in the morning. Protocol said this person was likely the last survivor of a lost survey team, exhausted and frightened, and needed to be brought home.

But protocol had sent him down-valley chasing a thief who never existed, and protocol had a signature at the bottom of it that he hadn't seen yet — though some instinct older than evidence was already whispering *Hale* to him, whispering that the search forming behind him was not a search at all.

Above him, on the shelf, he heard it: the smallest sound in the world. Fabric shifting against stone. Breath, held and released.

Dominic Eastwick stood alone in the dark at the bottom of Serrano's Cut, and made the decision that would unmake his life.

He began, silently, to climb.
