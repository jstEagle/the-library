## 4. The Face Across the Fire

The cold came down off the high country an hour after sundown, and Dominic followed it the way he'd follow any animal — by what it disturbed.

He had spent two days reading the canyon's small dishonesties. A stone turned green-side up where nothing had walked. A thread of wool snagged on greasewood, gray-blue, government issue. The dead-end slot canyon that wasn't a dead end at all if you knew to look for the cairn rebuilt wrong, one rock too many, a signature as deliberate as handwriting. Someone in that canyon wanted to be found by no one — and had left a trail anyway, because seven days alone in November taught even the best of them that unfound and unrescued were different words.

Now he sat in the dark above her fire.

That was the thing that had told him before anything else did. The fire was small, screened, built in a hollow with the wind's cooperation — a ranger's fire, a professional's fire. But it was also burning at all. Seven days missing, and she was still lighting fires, still cooking warm food, still making her bed each morning in a camp she'd abandoned like a stage set. Whoever it was hadn't given up on being human. They'd only given up on being located.

He watched the light pulse against the slot walls and thought about how he would take her. Quietly, from downwind, before she could reach whatever she kept close — he'd seen the glint of a holstered sidearm when the flames flared. Standard procedure. He'd done it a hundred times to people who deserved it less.

Then the wind shifted, carried woodsmoke and something else beneath it — cedar shampoo, or the ghost of cedar shampoo, memory doing the work his nose couldn't — and every procedure he had ever learned went out of him like heat through a cracked window.

He knew who was down there.

He had known since the footprints, maybe. Since the cairn. Since the moment in the survey camp when he'd stood over a made bed with its corners tucked tight and felt something in his chest turn over like a stone in a streambed. Elena Vasquez made her corners tight. He had teased her about it once, in another life, in a kitchen with yellow walls, and she had said *a person should be able to find their edges*, and he had loved her so much it had frightened him into the arms of his job.

Dominic Eastwick sat on cold rock in the Devil's Throat range and understood that he had been walking toward this fire for seven years.

* * *

She heard him at the last possible second, which told him she was exhausted, which told him she was human, which broke something in him a little more.

He came down the talus with his hands visible and his weight deliberate, the way you approach a spooked horse, and she came up out of her bedroll with the pistol already clear of the holster and leveled at his chest, and the firelight caught her face and it was — God help him — it was her. Older. Sharper through the cheekbones, a white scar he didn't know cutting through one eyebrow, hair hacked short and practical. But her eyes were the same dark, furious, luminous brown they had been the last time he'd seen them across a hearing room, right before they testified to everything he'd signed his name to and everything she'd lost because of it.

"Stop there." Her voice was steady. Her hands weren't, quite. "Identify yourself."

"You know who I am."

"I know the silhouette." The muzzle didn't waver now; anger was steadying her, the way it always had. "Silhouettes lie."

"Dominic Eastwick." He said it plainly, the way you hand over a weapon. "Search and rescue, out of Kestrel Station. I'm the one they sent."

Something moved behind her eyes — not surprise. Confirmation. As if she'd been waiting seven years for the universe to be exactly this cruel.

"Of course they sent you," she said. "Of course."

"Elena—"

"Don't." The word cracked like ice under boot. "Don't say my name like we're people who say names. Put your hands higher. Now — kick the rifle off your shoulder. Gently."

He did it. The carbine landed in the scree with a sound too loud for the smallness of the canyon. He stood empty-handed in the firelight, ten feet from the muzzle of a woman he had once intended to marry, and found that his heart was not pounding from the gun at all.

"Seven days," he said. "Your team is gone. Your camp looked like you all stepped out for water. There's one set of prints going into a slot that ends in a wall. Do you know what I thought? I thought mass casualty. I thought animals. I spent two days preparing myself to find bodies."

"Did it prepare you to find me?"

"No," he said. "Nothing prepares you for finding her."

Her jaw tightened. The pistol dipped an inch — not lowered, never lowered, but the angle changed from *threat* to *argument*. "You tracked me. Through hardpan, through the slots, in the dark. You're still the best I ever saw."

"You're still the worst liar I ever met. You left a trail a first-year could read."

"A first-year wouldn't have known the cairn was wrong." And then, bitterly, almost admiring: "No. Only you."

* * *

They circled each other for an hour without either of them sitting down.

It went the way old wounds go — not clean, not linear. She asked him questions designed to prove he was still the agency's man: Who gave the order? What did Hale tell you about us? What exactly were you sent to retrieve? And he answered honestly, which was its own kind of wound, because honesty from him had never once saved her.

"Director Hale signed the tasking himself," Dominic said. "Missing survey team, presumed weather event. Recover personnel or remains. That's all I was told."

"That's all he told you." She laughed, and there was no humor in it, only a terrible economy. "Dominic. Say my team's name."

"Okafor. Lindqvist. Bright. And you."

"And where are they?"

"Safely down the mountain, I hope."

"They're in El Paso," she said. "They've been in El Paso for six days. I put them on a bus myself, and then I walked back into this range alone and let the world think we vanished, because the alternative was letting the world believe our data." She finally sat, abruptly, like her legs had filed their resignation. "You want to know why the camp looked staged? Because it was. By me. Every pot, every made bed. I wanted whoever came looking to waste time on ghosts."

"You faked a disappearance."

"I documented a crime and then disappeared next to it. There's a difference." She pulled a dry bag from behind her and set it between them like a card laid face-down. "Core samples. Forty-one of them, sealed, logged, chain-of-custody intact because I'm the only one left in that chain I trust. The consortium's geological claims for the Throat lease are fabricated. Fabricated beautifully — falsified assays, doctored stratigraphy, enough real-looking paper to fool a review board. And the mineral rights package they're pushing sits on top of land that's supposed to be protected until 2041. Except it won't be, because the signatures clearing it are already on the amendments. One of them belongs to a director of the agency that sent you to find me."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, the glacier ticked and settled, a sound like a vast door being tested.

"Hale," Dominic said. It wasn't a question.

Elena looked at him across the flames, and her voice went quiet in the way that was worse than shouting. "Say it again. Slowly. I want to watch your face while you decide whose side you're on."

And that was the moment he understood the shape of the trap he'd walked into, except it wasn't a trap, it was just the truth, and the truth had been waiting for him the way the canyon had been waiting — patient, inevitable, carved long before he arrived.

Because here was the thing Elena didn't know. Couldn't know. Hale had pulled sixteen-year-old Dominic Eastwick out of a burning truck outside Bitterfork, had held the line of his father's funeral, had signed the waiver that got a grief-dumb kid into the service at nineteen instead of twenty-one, had stood beside him at every promotion and said *you earn it, son, nobody gives you anything* — Hale, who was the closest thing to a father the desert had ever given him. Hale, whose name was on those amendments.

"He saved my life," Dominic said. It came out smaller than he meant it to.

"I know," Elena said. "I know he did. I was there, remember? I held your hand at the funeral while he made the speech." Her eyes were wet and furious and unbearably steady. "I also lost my career because you handed him my field notes when he asked. Seven years ago. You followed orders, and I stopped existing. So tell me, Dominic — does the man who saved your life get to keep taking things? Where does the ledger balance? Show me the page."

He had no answer. He sat with his empty hands and the cold coming off the rock and the woman he'd destroyed looking at him with the same love and hate braided together, and he realized neither of them had drawn a weapon in the truest sense, and both of them had wanted to, and the wanting had been the honest part.

"The samples stay with me," she said finally. "You can arrest me. You can radio it in. You can be the good soldier one more time and go home to a commendation and a director who pats your shoulder." She stood, slow, joints stiff with cold and fury. "Or you can look at forty-one core samples and tell me the mountain is lying."

* * *

He didn't sleep. Neither did she, though she pretended to, curled with her back to him and the pistol still within reach, because trust was a bridge that took longer than one night to build and they both knew it.

Dominic fed the fire and read her logs by its light, page after page, assay after assay, and the numbers did what numbers do — they refused to care about anyone's loyalties. The stratigraphy was beautiful and false. The amendments were dated three weeks before the survey team ever entered the range. Somebody had written the ending before the story started, and Elena had caught them at it, and now she was the only loose thread, hiding in a slot canyon in November, betting her life that the wilderness would keep her better than any institution ever had.

Near dawn, the glacier spoke. Not the small ticking of settling ice but a deep, rolling groan that came up through the rock and into his spine, and Elena's head came up off her pack instantly, eyes wide and fully awake.

"What was that?"

"The calving season's opening." He heard his own voice go flat and professional, because flat and professional was the only place he could stand. "Warm front off the Pacific hit the icefield two days ago. When the throat opens, the pass through it holds maybe seventy-two hours before the seracs start coming down in earnest. After that, nothing crosses until spring."

"Seventy-two hours," she repeated.

"To get over the top with your evidence. Or to sit here until the world decides what we are." He looked at her across the dying fire — across seven years, across a hearing room, across a child-shaped absence he didn't yet know the full weight of — and heard himself say the thing he had no right to say. "I'm not arresting you, Elena."

Her breath caught. "Dominic—"

"But someone else will come." He was already pulling his map out, spreading it against the rock, his hands steady and his heart anything but. "Hale doesn't send one man for a job like this. He'll send someone who knows how I think. We need to move before he gets here."

Elena stared at him, and slowly, slowly, the pistol went back into its holster — not surrendered, he noticed. Just holstered. Like her.

"Who?" she said. "Who would Hale send that knows how you think?"

Dominic looked at the map, at the thin blue line of the pass, and felt the cold come off the glacier like a warning.

"My protégé," he said. "Reyes."
