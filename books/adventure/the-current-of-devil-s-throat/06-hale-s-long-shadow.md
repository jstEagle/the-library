## 6. Hale's Long Shadow

The office at the Regional Directorate still smelled of cedar and old paper, the way it had for twenty-two years, and Marcus Hale had come to believe that a man's office was the truest portrait of him. Not his face — faces could lie, and his had been lying pleasantly for decades. But an office confessed everything. The framed photographs of search teams on ridgelines. The commendations under glass. The worn topographic map of the Devil's Throat range pinned above the credenza, its contour lines faded to ghost-gray where his thumb had traced them a thousand times, thinking about routes, about weather, about men.

About one man in particular.

He stood at the window now with a tumbler of rye he wasn't drinking and watched the last light drain off the parade ground below, where two of his rangers were walking the perimeter in the slow, bored gait of men whose real work had been taken from them. The survey team had been missing nine days. Officially, the Directorate was conducting a full-scale search: helicopters, dog teams, four field units sweeping the eastern approaches.

Officially.

Unofficially, Hale had pulled three of those four units back within seventy-two hours of the disappearance, quietly, through channels only he controlled, and redistributed them to the northern fire caches where they'd be visible but useless. A missing survey team was a tragedy he could survive. A found survey team was another matter entirely.

He turned back to his desk, to the folder lying open there like something dead.

The core samples were what bothered him most. He'd known about the falsified assays for fourteen months — had signed off on them himself, that was the poison of it, his own signature sitting at the bottom of the certification like a name carved into a headstone. Meridian Consolidated had paid for the survey, and Meridian's geologists had written the numbers before the drills ever touched stone, and the samples Elena Vasquez's team pulled out of the Throat would have told a different story. A story worth, by Hale's conservative arithmetic, somewhere north of forty million acres of protected high desert opened to extraction, and somewhere south of twenty years added to a federal sentence if it came out wrong.

He had told himself, in the beginning, that it was manageable. That the assay discrepancy would stay buried in a filing cabinet the way such things always stayed buried. He had not counted on Vasquez developing a conscience.

He should have. He'd read her file. Ambitious, principled, stubborn as frost-heaved rock — the kind of scientist who thought data was a form of morality. People like that didn't steal government equipment and vanish into the worst range in the district without a reason. They vanished because they were carrying something.

His desk phone buzzed. He ignored it. It buzzed again — the direct line, the one only three people had.

"Yes."

"It's Ostrander." The voice of Meridian's man in the district, smooth as drill mud. "We understand the search is... proceeding."

"The search is proceeding," Hale said.

"With appropriate thoroughness."

Hale looked at the map above the credenza. "Mr. Ostrander. When you hire a man to find something, you don't stand behind him telling him how to hold the lantern. I've managed this district for twenty-two years without losing so much as a radio. I'll manage this."

A pause, long enough to be insulting. "The board is concerned about timelines. The calving season opens soon. If the pass closes—"

"Then nothing comes out of that range until spring, including her evidence, including her body, including your problem. Which is exactly why I'm not panicking." He set down the glass. "Good night."

He hung up before the man could answer and stood there breathing, letting the anger settle into something more useful. Fear was a solvent; it dissolved judgment. Anger, properly contained, was a tool. He had built a career on knowing which was which.

* * *

The boy — no, not a boy anymore, though the word still rose unbidden — arrived at seven the next morning, and Hale watched him come up the walk from the window the way he'd watched him come up that same walk for fifteen years.

Reyes. Tomás Reyes. Twenty-nine years old, built like the wire fence posts he'd grown up stringing, with his father's Mayan cheekbones and his mother's habit of standing too straight when he was nervous. He carried his pack even indoors, out of habit, the way soldiers did, and his boots left red dust prints on Hale's floor that the cleaning staff knew better than to mop before he'd gone.

"You wanted me, sir."

"Sit down, Tomás."

Reyes sat on the edge of the chair, spine vertical, hands on knees. Hale poured him coffee without asking, black, the way he'd drunk it since he was nineteen and too proud to admit he wanted cream. Some things you learned about a person by feeding them for a decade and a half.

"Eastwick's gone dark," Hale said.

Something moved behind Reyes's eyes — quick, quickly hidden. "Gone dark, sir? His check-ins have been—"

"Intermittent. Deliberately intermittent. He found something in the eastern approaches and went quiet, which means he found *her*, or her trail, and decided he wanted to look at it alone before anyone else did." Hale sat across from him, elbows on the desk, and let the silence do some work. "You know how he is."

"I know how he is," Reyes said quietly.

Of course he did. Dominic Eastwick had trained Reyes himself, had taken a raw kid off a fire crew and made him the best tracker in the district inside five years, taught him to read scat and stride length and the way frost formed on south-facing stone, taught him patience the way a smith teaches iron — by putting him in the fire over and over until he held his shape. Hale had watched that apprenticeship with something close to envy. He had given Dominic a career. Reyes had gotten something rarer.

"Here's what concerns me," Hale said, and slid the folder halfway across the desk, stopping short of letting go. "Vasquez didn't disappear. She staged it. Camp intact, food cooked, beds made, radios silent, one set of prints into a slot canyon that dead-ends. That's not panic. That's craft. Somebody taught her how unfound people move, or she studied us." He tapped the folder once. "And she's carrying documents that pertain to an active federal matter. Sensitive ones. If she reaches a journalist, or worse, reaches the pass during calving season and crosses into the north country—"

"Then we need to find her first," Reyes said. "Before Eastwick does?"

The question landed precisely where Reyes had aimed it, and Hale felt the old warmth toward the boy flicker against something colder. Careful, he thought. Careful now.

"Eastwick has history with Vasquez," he said. "You didn't know that. Nobody did. Seven years ago, before your time on his team, there was an incident — a data integrity case out of the southern district. Eastwick followed orders. Vasquez lost her position because of it. If he finds her alone in that wilderness, with that history between them..." He spread his hands, regretful, reasonable. "I can't risk the integrity of this operation on sentiment. Can I?"

"No, sir," Reyes said, but slowly, and Hale watched the thought move through him like weather.

"I want you on her trail. You're the only one who reads sign the way he does — better, maybe, in broken country. Take the western flank, above the glacier line. She'll run for the Throat pass; calving opens in four days and she knows the window better than anyone. Cut her off before she reaches it." He finally pushed the folder across. "Everything we have is in there."

Reyes took it but didn't open it. He was looking at Hale with those level dark eyes, and for a moment — just a moment — Hale saw Dominic in the set of his jaw, that same maddening stillness, that same refusal to reach for the easy answer.

"Sir," Reyes said, "if I find her. And she resists. What are my authorities?"

"Detain and escort. Non-lethal unless lethal is met." Hale let his voice soften, and this was the part he was good at, the part twenty-two years had honed finer than any blade: making duty sound like trust. "Tomás. I'm going to tell you something I don't say often. This district raised me, and I've spent my whole life paying it back, and right now it's sick. There are people inside these walls who would let a woman die in the snow to protect a contract. I am not one of them. Everything I'm asking you to do, I'm asking because she deserves to be brought home alive, and because Eastwick —" he paused, and the pause cost him nothing and bought everything — "Eastwick is like a son to me. I can't send him after her. So I'm sending the next best thing."

Reyes's throat worked. "Yes, sir."

"And Tomás — if you find Eastwick first?" Hale held his gaze. "You bring him home too. Whatever state he's in. Whatever he says. Do you understand me?"

"I understand, sir."

The boy stood, shrugged the pack higher, and went out, and his boot prints crossed the office in red dust, and Hale watched through the window as he crossed the parade ground with that tireless tracker's stride, heading for the motor pool, heading west, heading into the mountains after the woman and the man who had been, between them, the two great loves of Marcus Hale's carefully constructed life.

* * *

Alone, he poured the rye properly this time and drank it standing at the map, thumb finding the contour lines around Devil's Throat as if by reflex.

People would call it corruption, if they ever saw it laid out plain — the pulled search units, the buried assays, the signature at the bottom of the certification. They wouldn't understand. They never understood that a man who builds something doesn't get to watch it burn for the sake of a clean conscience. He had held this district together through budget collapses and wildfire seasons and three congressional inquiries, had kept rangers employed and families fed and the wilderness itself, God help him, actually protected far better than the environmentalists ever had, and all of it rested on the goodwill of men like Ostrander, and goodwill like that had a price tag, and the price had come due.

It wasn't greed. He wanted them to know that, whoever wrote the history. It was stewardship. Corrupted, compromised, damnable stewardship — but a man did not abandon the thing he loved because loving it had become expensive.

Dominic would understand. That was the tragedy of it, the wound beneath the whole rotten arrangement: Dominic, of all people, knew what it was to carry a debt for someone who saved your life. Hale had pulled him out of the wreckage at seventeen, out of the truck his father died in, had signed the guardianship papers with ink-steady hands while the boy sat in a hospital corridor learning what silence was. Every year of the boy's career since had been built on that foundation. Dominic owed him the way bedrock owes the mountain.

Which was why it had to be Reyes. Why it could never be Dominic. Because Dominic would look at the choice — the institution versus the truth — and Hale had spent fifteen years watching that man's eyes and knew, with the certainty of a father and the dread of a guilty man, exactly which way they would turn.

He finished the rye and set the glass down on the map, directly over the Throat, covering the pass.

Four days until the calving season. Three, maybe, if the warm front pushed through early. Somewhere in that white labyrinth, Elena Vasquez was moving north with forty million dollars of other people's sins in her pack, and Dominic Eastwick was moving toward her with ten years of erased life at his back, and Tomás Reyes was already driving west with a folder he hadn't opened and questions he hadn't asked yet.

Three of them, converging on the ice.

Hale picked up the phone and dialed the airfield. "Get the helicopter fueled," he said. "I'll be going in myself."
