## 7. The Calving Season Opens

The mountain spoke first.

Dominic heard it before he was fully awake — a sound like a cathedral clearing its throat, deep and grinding, somewhere beneath the blue ice of the Throat. He lay still in the bivy sack, watching his breath feather upward into the pre-dawn dark, and listened to the glacier answer in a language he had spent ten years learning not to fear and had never once stopped respecting.

Beside him, Elena was already sitting up.

"You heard it," she said. Not a question.

"Whole range heard it." He worked the stiffness out of his shoulders, feeling the cold that had crept into the down during the small hours, the cold that never entirely left at this altitude no matter how good your gear was. "Calving season's open."

She nodded slowly, and in the gray light her face looked carved from something older than worry — older than fear, almost. Resolve, maybe. Or arithmetic. He had learned over the past three days of travel that Elena Vasquez did her grieving and her hoping in numbers: miles per hour, calories per day, hours of light. It was how she kept herself from drowning.

"The pass," she said. "How long?"

"Seventy-two hours. Maybe less if the warm front comes through from the south like the clouds are saying it will." He tipped his chin toward the horizon, where a band of bruised color was gathering above the eastern ridgeline. "After that, the seracs above the throat start letting go in earnest, and nobody crosses until spring. Nobody living."

"Then we don't stop."

"We don't stop."

They broke camp with the economy of people who had both spent their lives packing for departure — she from field seasons, he from a decade of assignments that ended with him vanishing before anyone could learn to expect him back. There was a strange comfort in it, Dominic thought, watching her coil rope with hands that were steady even now. Comfort, and an ache underneath it, because competence shared at four in the morning on a mountainside felt more intimate than anything he'd done with another human being in seven years.

The evidence rode against her sternum, wrapped in waxed cloth and sealed in a tube no bigger than a thermos. Core samples' worth of data — the falsified assays, the consortium's forged permits, the signatures of men who had sold protected land out from under the law while wearing the law's own uniform. One signature among them belonged to Marcus Hale.

Dominic hadn't said Hale's name aloud since the fire two nights ago. Neither had she. But he carried it the way he carried the extra weight in his pack — silently, constantly, in a place where it rubbed.

* * *

By midmorning they were on the glacier's shoulder, moving fast across ice that groaned and ticked beneath their crampons like a ship's hull under strain. The Throat itself opened ahead of them: a mile-wide corridor between walls of blue-black ice five hundred feet tall, its floor littered with the debris of centuries of calvings — boulders of glacial ice the size of houses, fields of shattered serac, meltwater channels running silver and loud.

It should have been beautiful. It was beautiful, in the way that made Dominic's mouth go dry.

"Seventy-two hours," Elena said again, as if saying it might make the number behave. She'd been counting all morning. He recognized the habit; it was his own. When you couldn't control a thing, you measured it.

"Sixty-eight now," he said. "We lost time on the moraine yesterday."

"I know how much time we lost, Dominic."

He let that sit. Three days together had taught him the shape of her anger — that it wasn't really aimed at him most of the time, that it was aimed at the years, at the child, at the institution that had taken turns devouring them both. But sometimes it found him anyway, and he had decided he deserved to stand in its path when it did.

They crossed a melt channel on a bridge of packed snow, and on the far side the glacier's voice changed — rose in pitch, went from groan to whistle. Wind coming down off the high col, funneling through the throat. It would be at their backs. Small mercy.

That was when Dominic saw the figure.

He stopped without meaning to, one boot planted, and his body did what ten years of training made automatic: he catalogued. Single person, half a mile east and below them, moving along the lateral moraine with a pace and rhythm he knew the way you know handwriting. Broad-shouldered. Carrying weight like someone who'd packed heavy for altitude. Dark jacket, service-issue cut.

Reyes.

Elena followed his gaze and went very still. "Is that—"

"Yeah."

"He found us."

"He was always going to find us. I taught him how." Dominic watched the distant figure pause at a cairn, crouch, study the ground. Even at this distance he could read the posture: not hunting. Reading. Asking the landscape questions instead of demanding answers. "He's not pushing. If he wanted to run us down he'd have cut the angle at the col and been waiting."

"Then what's he doing?"

"Asking questions," Dominic said again, and hated how much hope he heard in his own voice, and hated more that he couldn't kill it.

* * *

They didn't stop, but they didn't run either. Running told Reyes everything. They held their line up the glacier's spine, and Reyes paralleled them on the moraine, close enough now that details resolved: the gray threading his temples that hadn't been there two years ago, the careful way he favored his left knee on the scree. The kid Dominic had pulled out of a flood channel in the Sonoran borderlands, half-drowned and furious at the world, who had become the finest tracker the service had produced in a generation. Who had learned everything from a man who had taught him, deliberately, nothing true about himself.

At noon they stopped in the lee of a serac to eat, and Reyes stopped too, a quarter mile off, and sat down on a rock in full view. Deliberate. *I see you. I'm not hiding either.*

"He wants to talk," Elena said.

"He wants to understand. Talking comes after understanding, with him." Dominic chewed mechanically at a bar of pemmican that tasted like nothing. "There's a version of this where he's here to bring us in. There's a version where he's here because Hale sent him and he hasn't decided yet whose orders he follows. And there's a version I don't want to say out loud."

"Which is?"

"That he came looking for me, not for you. That whatever Hale told him didn't sit right, and he wanted to hear it from the horse's mouth before he did something he couldn't take back." Dominic looked at the distant figure, small against the immensity of the ice. "I trained him to do exactly that. Question the picture everyone else accepts. God help me, it worked."

Elena was quiet for a long moment. The glacier boomed somewhere behind them, a long rolling concussion like artillery, and both of them flinched and then pretended they hadn't.

"If he's Hale's," she said finally, "then every hour we let him shadow us is an hour he's reporting our position."

"And if he's ours, every hour we run from him is an hour we spend teaching him we're guilty." Dominic stood, brushing snow from his knees. "Either way, the clock doesn't care. Sixty-five hours. We move."

* * *

The afternoon went gold and then copper and then violet, and the Throat swallowed the light early the way it always did, the ice walls drinking the sun an hour before the peaks lost it. They made camp in a hollow the wind had scoured behind a fallen block the size of a chapel, and Dominic built the smallest fire he could — a handful of fuel tablets, barely enough to heat water — because heat mattered more than concealment now, and because Reyes already knew exactly where they were.

The shouting started at dusk.

Not shouting — carrying. Reyes's voice, pitched to travel across a quarter mile of ice, the way rangers talked to each other across canyons. Dominic cupped his hands and sent his own voice back, and for twenty minutes the two of them traded words across the glacier while Elena sat with her back against the ice block, listening, her face unreadable.

*"Uncle Dom."* The old nickname, from the first year, the year Reyes had still been half-wild. *"Hale says you're compromised. Says Vasquez is unstable, that she faked the whole disappearance, that the samples are fabricated."*

*"And what do you say?"*

A long silence. Somewhere deep in the glacier, something enormous shifted.

*"I say you taught me that a story that arrives too convenient is a story somebody wrote. And this one arrived very convenient, very fast."* A pause. *"I read the incident reports, Dom. I read what happened to her team's funding requests. I read the assay dates. Somebody cooked those numbers, and it wasn't her."*

Elena's breath caught — a small sound, quickly mastered, but Dominic heard it. Hope was a dangerous thing to carry at altitude. It weighed more than rope.

*"Then why are you following us?"* Dominic called.

*"Because I haven't decided what I am yet!"* Reyes's voice cracked on it, young suddenly, all the composure stripped away by distance and echo and the enormity of what he was admitting. *"Because if I come down there and you show me proof, I can't unsee it. And if I go back and tell Hale nothing, I can't unmake that either. So I'm going to follow you to the pass, and you're going to have seventy-two hours to decide whether you trust me. Same clock you're on."*

Dominic lowered his hands. His heart was hammering in a way that had nothing to do with the climb.

"Well?" Elena asked quietly.

"He's telling the truth. That's the worst part. He means every word."

"Can we afford him?"

Dominic looked up the throat, where the pass waited sixty hours and eleven hard miles away, under five hundred feet of ice that was beginning, season by season, hour by hour, to let go of the mountain. He thought about the boy in the flood channel. He thought about the man Hale had made of himself, and the man Reyes might become.

"We can't afford to find out what happens if we leave him on Hale's side of that question," he said. "We keep moving. He keeps pace. And when the ice starts coming down — because it will — we're all going to be standing in the same throat when it does."

Above them, as if in answer, the glacier spoke again — longer this time, deeper, a groan that rolled through the ice under their boots and up through their bones and did not stop for nearly a full minute.

Somewhere in the dark ahead, Devil's Throat cleared its throat once more.

Seventy-two hours had become sixty.
