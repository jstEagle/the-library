## 8. Icefall

**Reyes**

The mountain had been talking to him all morning, and Marisol Reyes had learned enough in six years under Dominic Eastwick to know that when a glacier started talking, you stopped and listened.

It wasn't a sound so much as a pressure — a deep, tectonic groan rolling up through the soles of her boots, the kind of noise that made her think of doors opening in rooms she'd never seen. Above her, the icefall spilled down from the shoulder of Devil's Throat like something frozen mid-fall, blue-green and fractured, hanging in seracs the size of chapel towers.

She crouched at the edge of the moraine and raised her binoculars. Two figures on the far flank of the valley, small as punctuation marks against the white, moving fast toward the pass mouth. One moved like Dominic — that low, economical stride she'd spent years trying to copy, the walk of a man who never wasted a step because he'd once had to count them all. The other kept pace with him, and Reyes felt something twist in her chest that wasn't professional.

*Elena Vasquez.* Alive. Walking out beside the man sent to bury her.

Hale's orders sat in her breast pocket like a stone: *Locate Vasquez. Secure any materials. Do not engage Eastwick unless necessary.* Hale hadn't said what *secure* meant, and Reyes had been in the service long enough to know that unsaid words were where bodies got buried. She'd told herself it was rescue. She'd told herself a lot of things since leaving base camp three days ago.

The glacier spoke again, longer this time, and somewhere high in the icefall something answered with a crack like a rifle shot.

"Come on," she whispered to the two figures below. "Move."

* * *

**Dominic**

They'd made better time than he'd dared hope. Elena climbed the way she did everything — fiercely, precisely, furious at the mountain for slowing her down — and by midday they stood below the throat itself, the pass yawning between walls of ice that calved and thundered with the warming air. Seventy-two hours, the survey's instruments had said before they died. They were using maybe forty of them.

"We'll make it," Elena said, reading his face. She always could. Seven years hadn't changed that; seven years hadn't changed anything that mattered, which was the cruelest discovery of his life.

Dominic didn't answer, because answering meant admitting how badly he wanted it to be true, and wanting things was a habit he'd spent a decade amputating. Instead he checked the sky, the slope, the sun-softened snow bridges — and found the third thing he'd been pretending not to look for.

A figure on their flank, half a mile east, descending through the rock bands. Small. Deliberate. Moving with a stride he knew better than his own heartbeat, because he'd taught it.

"Reyes," he said.

Elena went still. "Your protégé."

"My shadow." He watched the figure pause at a snow bridge, test it, cross. "Hale didn't send a search party. He sent her. Which means he knows exactly what you're carrying, and exactly what I'm doing."

"And you brought us straight toward her."

"I brought us toward the only way out." He turned to face her. "Elena. If she takes the samples—"

"She won't get the chance." There was iron in it, and grief too, and he recognized both because they were his own.

Then the mountain ended the argument.

The sound came first — not a crack this time but a roar, vast and wet and wrong, the sound of ten thousand tons of ice deciding its patience was over. High in the icefall, a serac the size of a cathedral leaned, shuddered, and let go.

"RUN!"

They ran. The slope tilted beneath them into a slide of powder and scree, and Dominic drove himself downhill on legs that burned like lit fuses, Elena's breath ragged behind him, the avalanche's shadow racing them across the glacier floor. It caught them at the edge — not the main mass but the tongue of it, a white fist that swatted them off their feet and buried the world in blinding cold.

When the air cleared, Dominic dug himself out gasping, hauled Elena free by the collar of her pack, and looked up.

Reyes stood thirty yards away, on the far side of a fresh wall of debris twenty feet high, staring at him across the ruin.

The avalanche had closed the valley behind them. And it had closed it in front of her.

They were all three of them trapped together in the throat of the mountain.

* * *

**Elena**

The woman who climbed down the debris wall moved like water finding its level, and Elena understood immediately why Hale had chosen her. This was not a soldier. This was a mirror — a younger version of the man standing at Elena's side, built by the same hands for the same purposes.

"Boss." Reyes stopped ten feet away, rifle slung, hands deliberately empty and visible. Her eyes went to Elena, catalogued her, dismissed nothing. "You're supposed to be dead."

"People keep saying that." Elena kept her voice level. "Your director seems disappointed I'm not."

"I don't work for a director. I work for the service." But there was a hairline fracture in the words, and Elena had spent two weeks alone in a canyon learning to hear fractures.

"You work for an institution," Elena said. "There's a difference. Ask your mentor. He learned it the hard way, seven years ago, when he followed orders and my career bled out on a conference room floor while I watched."

"Elena." Dominic's voice, low. A warning or a plea; she couldn't tell and didn't care.

"No. She came hunting us through an avalanche field. She gets the truth." Elena pulled the sample case from her pack — battered aluminum, dented, the most dangerous object in the state of Nevada — and held it where they could both see it. "Core samples. Falsified assays. Bribed officials. A land grab dressed up as a mineral survey, signed off by Director Marcus Hale. Your rescuer. Your benefactor. Whatever he is to you."

Reyes didn't move. Didn't speak. Her jaw worked once, twice.

"He saved your life," Elena pressed. "I know. He saves everyone's life. That's how men like him buy the loyalty they spend later."

"That's enough." Reyes's voice cracked like the ice above them. "You don't know anything about—"

The mountain interrupted again, and this time it did not warn them.

* * *

**Reyes**

Later, Marisol would try to reconstruct those ninety seconds and fail. What she would remember was this:

The sound — deeper than anything, a whale-song of breaking continents. Looking up. The whole eastern face of the icefall leaning outward like a door swinging open. Dominic shouting her name — *her* name, not Elena's — and moving, already moving, toward her instead of away.

And then the world coming down.

Ice the size of houses hit the glacier floor half a mile wide of them, but the blast wave of displaced air and shattered debris swept the slope like a hand clearing a table. She felt herself lifted, tumbling, the sky and the white trading places — and then a grip on her wrist, brutal, certain, and Dominic Eastwick hauling her out of the debris chute with strength that seemed borrowed from somewhere older than his body, dragging her behind a granite rib as the tail of the collapse roared past close enough to fill her hood with snow.

Silence after. The ringing kind.

Her pack was gone. So was most of the slope she'd descended. When she inventoried herself — fingers, toes, ribs, rifle — everything answered except the radio, which had been crushed to plastic lace in her chest pocket.

"Everyone breathing?" Dominic's face swam into focus above her, bleeding from a cut over one eye, utterly calm. He'd been like this her entire career. Calm in the fire, calm in the flood. She'd thought it was training. Now, looking at him, she wondered if it was penance.

Elena appeared at his shoulder, gray-faced, shaking, alive. Between them, sitting in the snow like an accusation, was the aluminum case — thrown clear, unbroken, absurdly intact.

"The supplies," Elena said quietly.

Gone. Both packs. Gone with the slope, buried under forty feet of fresh collapse. What remained was what they carried on their bodies: rope, a stove, two days of food split three ways, and seventy hours of cold.

And the case.

Reyes looked at it. At the man who had made her. At the woman he'd clearly chosen over every order he'd ever been given.

"You knew," she said. Her voice came out strange, small. "Before we ever left base camp. You knew what the survey really was, and you took the assignment anyway, and you've known since the hardpan trail that you weren't going to bring her back. You were going to take her side." She laughed, and it hurt her ribs. "Ten years I studied you. Ten years thinking you were the purest thing the service ever produced. You're just a man who finally picked someone."

"Yes," Dominic said.

No excuse. No explanation. Just *yes*, laid down between them like a card he couldn't take back, and Marisol felt something in her chest tear along a seam she hadn't known was there.

"Hale will deny everything," she said. "He'll bury the samples. He'll bury you. He buried people before you were born, boss. You know that better than anyone — he buried your father's file."

Dominic went very still. "What did you say?"

Reyes stared at him. "You know about your father's file. Everyone knows—" She stopped. Read his face. Read it the way she'd been trained to read terrain, for the places where the surface lied. "He never told you."

The wind moved through the throat, keening between the ice walls, and above them the broken face of the icefall settled and groaned and began, very slowly, to decide what it would do next.

"There's a relay station," Elena said suddenly, urgent, reading the geometry of the pass the way she read everything. "The survey's emergency relay — buried, hardened, independent of the agency network. If we can reach it before the pass closes, these samples go out regardless of what happens to us. Regardless of what Hale does. Regardless of what any of us choose."

Reyes looked at the case. At the pass. At the two people the institution had sent her to break, who had just pulled her out of an avalanche without a moment's hesitation, and who were now looking at her — not as a threat, not as an order, but as a person standing at a fork in the ice with the temperature dropping.

Seventy hours. Three days of food. One choice.

"Which way to the relay?" Marisol Reyes asked.

And high above them, the mountain began to answer.
