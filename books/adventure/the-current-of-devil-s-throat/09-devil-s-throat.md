## 9. Devil's Throat

The pass mouth opened before them like a wound in the world.

Dominic had crossed Devil's Throat twice in his career — once on a supply run, once carrying a man with a shattered femur down from the high country — and both times the glacier had been sleeping. It was not sleeping now. The ice groaned beneath a skin of fresh snow, a sound like some vast animal turning over in its den, and above them the seracs leaned against the sky at angles that made his hindbrain scream. Calving season. Seventy-two hours of grace, and they'd spent fifty-one getting here.

Elena stood beside him at the lip of the moraine, her breath tearing out in white rags. The sample case rode her back in a harness she'd built from survey webbing, and she hadn't let it out of arm's reach since Icefall. Since the second collapse that took their tent, their stove, half their food, and nearly took Reyes.

Reyes was thirty meters behind them, moving well for a man who'd spent two days on a broken ankle splinted with ski poles and duct tape. He hadn't spoken since the icefall. Hadn't needed to. Whatever he'd decided about his mentor and his institution, he was still walking in their tracks, and that was answer enough for now.

"We make the relay before we make the pass," Elena said. It wasn't the first time she'd said it. "The buried line is four hundred meters east. If we can transmit before—"

"I know where it is." His voice came out flatter than he intended. "I helped bury it."

That was true, and it was the thing he'd been circling for two days without landing on: seven years ago, a younger Dominic Eastwick had knelt in this same scree with a shovel and a spool of armored cable, following Director Marcus Hale's orders, believing he was building infrastructure. A weather relay. A safety net for anyone caught on the wrong side of the calving season. Hale had put his hand on Dominic's shoulder afterward and said, *This is the kind of work nobody thanks you for, son. That's how you know it matters.*

He wondered now whether Hale had known even then what else the line could carry. Whether the director had chosen him for the burial crew precisely because Dominic never asked questions he didn't want answered.

The wind shifted, and under it — thin, wrong, mechanical — came the sound of rotors.

Elena heard it too. Her head came up like a deer's. "No," she said. "No, no, no. We're inside the window. There's no rescue window yet."

"It's not a rescue."

The helicopter came around the southern spur low and fast, a service bird in agency gray, running lights strobing against the blue shadow of the ice. It swung wide over the throat, testing the air, then settled toward a shelf of wind-packed snow two hundred meters ahead of them — dead center in the path between Dominic and the pass.

Between Dominic and everything.

"Get behind the moraine," he said.

"No."

"Elena—"

"You don't get to die alone in front of me again." She said it without heat, the way you state a bearing or a barometric reading, and then she started walking toward the helicopter, and there was nothing on this earth he could do to stop her except walk beside her. So he did.

* * *

Hale stepped out before the skids stopped moving, ducking under the rotor wash with the easy economy of a man who'd been stepping out of helicopters for thirty years. He was smaller than Dominic remembered, or maybe the mountains had grown. Silver at the temples now. Same field jacket, same worn leather gloves, same voice that had read a fourteen-year-old orphan to sleep with stories of the service's founding generation.

"Dominic." Hale said his name like a hand on the shoulder. Then his eyes went past him to Elena, and the warmth shut off like a breaker thrown. "Dr. Vasquez. You've cost this agency a great deal of money."

"She's cost you the truth," Dominic said. "There's a difference."

"There always is." Hale gestured, casual, and two men climbed out of the chopper behind him — not rangers. Contractors. They spread out along the shelf with the loose competence of men paid by the hour. "Here's what happens now. You give me the samples. Both of you board. We fly out, the record shows I pulled three fools off a mountain in defiance of every protocol, and everyone keeps their pensions. Vasquez, you'll face an inquiry. It'll be fair. I'll see to it personally." He smiled, and it was almost the old smile. "Or you keep walking, and I leave you for the calving season. The throat doesn't negotiate, son. Neither do I."

The rotors idled. Snow blew off the shelf in flat white sheets. Somewhere deep under their feet, the glacier spoke — a long tectonic sigh, ice settling against ice.

"Hale." Dominic kept his voice level. "I read the core logs. The assay reports were falsified before they ever reached your desk. The consortium's claim maps don't match a single sample Elena pulled. And your signature is on the transfer authorizations — dated six weeks before the survey team ever entered the range." He watched the older man's face for the flicker, the tell, anything. There was nothing. Thirty years of composure held like poured concrete. "You knew the land was protected. You sold it anyway."

"What I did," Hale said quietly, "was keep this agency alive through three budget cycles that wanted it dead. Land is land, Dominic. It doesn't have children. It doesn't bleed. Institutions do." He stepped closer, and for one unguarded moment the mask slipped and Dominic saw the old grief underneath, real as bedrock. "I carried you out of the San Rafael fire when you were nineteen. I signed the letter that got you into the academy when no one else would look at a kid with your file. Everything you are, you are because I decided you were worth the trouble. Don't make me watch you throw it away on a woman and a box of rocks."

"That's exactly why I have to," Dominic said. "You taught me the job. The job is the land. Not the budget. Not the institution. The land."

Hale exhaled through his nose. "Last chance, son."

"I'm not your son."

And he turned his back on the director of the Desert Range Service, on the only father he'd had for twenty years, and walked toward the relay station with Elena falling into step beside him and Reyes limping behind them both.

He made it forty meters before the contractors started after them.

* * *

The relay housing was a slab of composite buried under a cairn Dominic himself had stacked, and his hands remembered the work even as they shook with cold and adrenaline. Cairn stones off. Access panel up. The cable junction was corroded but intact — twenty years of mountain winters and it had held, because he'd buried it right, because he'd always done the work right even when the work was a lie.

Elena crouched beside him, pulling the data drive from the sample case, her fingers too frozen for the coupling. He took it from her. Their hands met over the port, and for a moment neither moved.

"Once this goes," she said, "there's no version of your life that survives it."

"I know."

"They'll take your license. Your rank. Your—" She stopped. Wind screamed across the moraine. Behind them, boots crunched snow, closing. "Dominic. They'll erase you. It's what you're best at. It's what you've been doing for ten years. This just makes it official."

He looked at her — frost in her lashes, blood on her split lip from the icefall, eyes that had hated him and grieved him and, God help him, had started to warm again somewhere on the high trail — and he felt something in his chest come loose that had been cinched tight since the day he'd walked out of her life with his orders folded in his pocket.

"I spent a decade making sure no one could find me," he said. "You found me anyway. In a slot canyon at the end of the earth." He slotted the drive home. "Turns out belonging isn't a place they assign you. It's a person you refuse to leave. I'm done leaving."

He keyed the transmission.

The relay woke with a hum he felt through his knees, and the signal light blinked green — once, twice, three times — and somewhere beyond the mountains, beyond the range of any man's reach, the falsified assays and the forged authorizations and the consortium's whole rotten architecture began climbing out of the throat of the world on a carrier wave.

"Eastwick!" Hale's voice, raw now, the composure finally cracked. "Step away from that unit!"

Dominic stood. Turned. Put himself between Hale and Elena, arms slightly out, not a fighting stance — a wall stance. The contractors were close now, twenty meters, fifteen, hands moving inside their jackets.

"You're too late," Dominic said. "It's transmitting. Every second you spend threatening us, it's broadcasting again. Shoot the relay if you want — the packet's already hopping. Kill me, and the story gets better." He took one step forward, onto open snow, into the space between the guns and the woman. "Stand down, Marcus. It's finished."

"It's not finished until I say—"

The glacier answered.

Not a groan this time. A crack — a rifle shot the size of a cathedral, directly beneath the helicopter's shelf. The snow shelf shuddered. Everyone froze. Then, with a slowness that was somehow worse than speed, the entire shelf tilted, and the helicopter's landing skids sank six inches into softening pack as the ice beneath began to shear away toward the throat.

"The chopper!" One of the contractors broke first, sprinting back. The pilot was already gunning the engine, nose lifting, but the tail rotor clipped the shifting snow and the whole bird slewed sideways, sliding, metal shrieking against ice going liquid.

Hale didn't move. He stood in the sliding snow staring at Dominic with an expression Dominic had never seen on him before — not anger, not command. Loss. The face of a man watching the last thing he believed in walk away from him upright.

"Go!" Dominic shouted at him, waving toward the stable ground beyond the shelf. "Move, damn you!"

Hale moved. The contractors dragged him clear as the shelf gave way entirely — a hundred tons of wind-packed snow and fractured ice peeling off into the throat with a roar that swallowed the rotors, swallowed the shouting, swallowed everything. The helicopter yawed, clawed skyward missing a skid, and limped away over the spur trailing spray, leaving the four of them alone on the shaking moraine with a green light blinking steadily in the relay housing and the evidence already gone, irrevocably gone, out into the world.

Silence, after. Just wind, and the glacier's slow breathing, and Elena's hand finding his in the dark of his glove.

Reyes lowered himself onto a boulder, ankle out, and looked at the three of them — the mentor, the scientist, the director's empty shadow retreating downrange — and said, in a voice scraped raw:

"So which one of you is going to tell me what happens now?"

Dominic looked east, where the storm front was building over the far peaks, and squeezed Elena's hand, and realized he was smiling.

"Now," he said, "we walk out."
