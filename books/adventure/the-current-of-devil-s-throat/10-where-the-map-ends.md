## 10. Where the Map Ends

The morning they walked out of Devil's Throat, the light came down the eastern face of the range like something poured — slow, gold, unhurried — and Elena Vasquez stood at the tree line and watched Dominic Eastwick take off his badge for the last time.

He didn't make a ceremony of it. That was the thing about Dominic; she had forgotten that in seven years of remembering him wrong, and now it came back to her with the force of an old song: he did nothing ceremonially. He worked the leather strap loose from the buckle with cold-stiffened fingers, held the shield up so the sun caught it once — just once, a small flare of brass against all that white and blue — and then set it on a boulder at the edge of the service boundary, faceup, as if leaving it out for whoever came next.

"You're supposed to surrender that to the district office," she said. "There's a form."

"There's always a form." He rolled his shoulders, wincing where the harness had bitten during the icefall, and looked back at the mountains one more time. The Throat was closing behind them already; even from here they could hear it, a deep tectonic mutter, the glacier settling its accounts. "Reyes can file it."

"Reyes is going to be busy testifying."

"Then Reyes can file two forms."

She laughed — actually laughed, her breath smoking in the cold, and was surprised by the sound of it, rusty and unguarded, a voice she hadn't heard from herself since before the survey, since before the samples, maybe since before him. Below them the valley opened out in long brown sweeps of winter range, cut by the gray ribbon of the river, and somewhere down there was a road, and past the road a town with a sheriff who would want statements, and past the town a world that was about to learn what Meridian Consolidated had buried under four hundred acres of protected alpine ground.

But not yet. For one more hour, none of that had reached this high.

* * *

They had come down through the burn scar, the old fire ground where the lodgepoles stood black and bare as needles in a pincushion, and Elena had used the walk to do what she always did with unbearable things: she catalogued them.

The consortium was finished. She knew it the way you know weather coming over a ridge — not because any single sign says so but because everything leans the same direction. The core samples were in the hands of the state geologist's office as of yesterday's relay burst, duplicated twice, sealed with chain-of-custody tags she'd filled out in a snow shelter with numb fingers while Dominic held the light. The assay fraud was documented. The falsified reclamation bonds were documented. And the signature on the land-swap memorandum — Director Marcus Hale's own careful, upright hand, the handwriting of a man who signed things the way other men shook hands — was documented too.

Hale was under inquiry. She'd heard it on the radio traffic crackling across the relay band as they made their final descent: the word *inquiry* passed between dispatch points like a coin changing hands, flat and official and enormous. There would be hearings. There would be men in good suits explaining permafrost and mineral rights to senators who owned ranches. Hale would go down, and he would go down hard, because that was the only way men like him went down — they never stepped aside, they were stepped over.

And Reyes. She thought about Reyes more than she expected to. Twenty-six years old, service-issue parka, a tracker good enough that Hale had sent him into the range to hunt them and confident enough that he'd turned around at the icefall and chosen the harder right. He'd testified — no, he was going to testify; the deposition was scheduled for Thursday — and when Dominic had asked him, there at the pass mouth with the helicopter rotors still turning and the mountain still groaning, whether he understood what it would cost him, Reyes had looked at his mentor with those steady dark eyes and said:

"You taught me that the trail doesn't care whose it is. You follow it where it goes or you're not a tracker, you're just lost with equipment."

Dominic hadn't answered. He'd put his hand on the back of the boy's neck, briefly, the way you'd steady something you were afraid of breaking, and then he'd turned away so neither of them had to see his face.

That was Thursday. Today was Sunday, and the world was rearranging itself around the truth, and Elena was walking beside a man who no longer officially existed.

* * *

The license went the same day as the badge, though she hadn't witnessed that part. She'd learned about it secondhand, from the sheriff's deputy who met them at the trailhead with a thermos of terrible coffee and a look on his face like a man delivering both a rescue and a eulogy: the directorate had moved within hours, faster than the inquiry could move against them, revoking Dominic's commission and his wilderness permit and his radio call sign in a single administrative stroke. Stripped clean. A decade of service erased in an afternoon — which, Elena thought, watching Dominic listen to the news without expression, must have felt almost familiar to him. He'd spent ten years erasing himself anyway. The agency had simply done formally what he'd been doing privately all along.

"They think taking the credential takes the man," she said that night, in the bunkhouse the county had lent them, wood stove ticking as it cooled.

"It doesn't bother me."

"I know it doesn't bother you. That's what bothers me."

He looked at her across the small room, and in the stove's dying orange light his face did something complicated — the ghost of a smile, the residue of exhaustion, something underneath both of those that was older and quieter and had waited seven years to be said.

"Elena," he said. "I spent ten years being nobody on purpose. It turns out nobody is a place you can stand. But it isn't a place you belong."

* * *

On the far side of the mountains, three weeks later, the world kept its appointments.

The *Meridian Four* story ran everywhere at once — the name the papers had given the four sample sites, though Elena privately thought of them as the four lies — and the photographs of the core samples, wax-sealed and labeled in her own blocky field hand, became the images everyone used. The consortium's stock shed forty percent in nine days. Two state legislators resigned before anyone even asked them to. The protected acreage — her acreage, the ground she had loved enough to lose everything over — was placed under emergency federal stewardship pending permanent designation, and there was talk, real talk, of naming the drainage after the survey team that had found the truth about it.

Elena read all of it from a cabin on the west slope with no address worth printing, and mostly she let Dominic read it aloud, because hearing it in his low, even voice made it sound less like history happening and more like weather passing over.

Hale's inquiry opened in March. She watched the first session on a borrowed television, the picture shivering in the storm static, and saw the director she remembered — broad, silver-templed, a man built like a monument to himself — sitting alone at a witness table with a glass of water and no one beside him. He had saved Dominic's life once, pulled a half-frozen nineteen-year-old ranger recruit out of a crevasse on Mount Caldera and carried him down on his own back, and Elena knew — had known since the pass mouth, since the ultimatum, since the moment the ice took the chopper's landing skids and Hale's face finally showed what lived underneath the competence — that the crevasse and the corruption came from the same place in him. A man who saves people wants to own them. A man who owns people eventually sells the ground under them.

Hale looked directly into the camera once, during a recess, and Elena would have sworn he was looking at Dominic, wherever Dominic was. She couldn't decide if it was defiance or farewell. Probably both. Men like him never learned the difference.

* * *

Spring came late to the west slope that year, and when it came it came all at once, the way it does at altitude — a week of meltwater roaring in every gully, then green arriving overnight like a decision.

It was on one of those first warm evenings that Elena found Dominic outside the cabin, standing at the fence line, looking east toward the range. He'd taken work with the county, search-and-rescue contract labor, no rank, no shield, no call sign — just a man with a radio and a dog and an unreasonable talent for finding people who didn't want to be found. The town had been suspicious of him for exactly one season. Then he'd brought down a lost child off Saddleback in a whiteout, carrying her inside his coat, and suspicion had converted overnight into something the town wore awkwardly, like new boots: belonging.

"You're doing it again," Elena said, coming to stand beside him.

"Doing what?"

"Standing at edges. Measuring exits."

He considered that honestly, which was another thing she'd forgotten and recovered — how seriously he took even her smallest accusations, as if each one deserved a full expedition.

"I'm looking at the Throat," he said finally. "Calving season opens in five weeks. County wants a hazard assessment on the outlet drainage. They asked if I'd run it."

"And?"

"And I said I'd need a partner." He turned to look at her, and the evening light caught the lines around his eyes, deeper than they'd been in the fall, earned lines, and he wasn't hiding behind any of it. "Somebody who reads ice cores. Somebody who trusts the ground more than the paperwork. Somebody who—" He stopped, and started over, plainer. "Somebody I don't want to come back without."

The wind moved through the new grass. Somewhere downslope, the dog barked once at nothing and thought better of it.

"You know," Elena said, "seven years ago I made a list of every way I hated you. It was a very thorough list. Field-notes quality."

"I believe it."

"I lost it somewhere in the slot canyon. Along with the rest of the person who wrote it." She took his hand — his palm still ridged with rope callus, still cold at the fingertips the way it had always been, the way she'd have recognized blindfolded in a crowd of thousands — and laced her fingers through his. "Run your assessment. Take the contract. Take all of them. Just stop walking out of rooms before I get there."

He smiled then, fully, and it changed his whole face the way spring had changed the slope — all at once, like a decision.

"No more exits," he said.

Far to the east, the Throat caught the last of the sun and held it, white going rose going violet, the glacier keeping its own time as it always had, indifferent and magnificent. In five weeks it would begin to calve, shedding its old weight into the valley, making room for whatever grew next.

Elena watched it with her shoulder against his and thought: that's what we did. We crossed it, and it took everything we were carrying, and on the far side the map simply ended — and it turned out that was not the edge of anything.

It was the beginning of the country you draw yourself.
