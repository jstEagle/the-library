## 1. The Weight of Small Debts

The rain in Port Sallow didn't fall so much as settle, a gray insistence that had been going on since before Paloma arrived and would continue long after they left. It came down the harbor in sheets off the water, came down the gutters in brown ribbons, came through the roof of the boarding house in one reliable spot above the bed that Paloma had learned to avoid by sleeping diagonal. Everything in Port Sallow was wet except the money. The money stayed dry because it never came near you.

Paloma counted it again on the table in the boarding house room, though counting changed nothing. Counting was a ritual, like prayer, and about as effective. Three hundred and forty salins in crumpled notes and coins gone soft with handling. The payment due to Merren & Vole, Factors and Advances, was four hundred even, due by close of business, which in Port Sallow meant six o'clock, which meant Paloma had fifty-eight minutes and sixty salins' worth of a problem.

The arithmetic was simple. That was the insult of it. Anyone could do this math. A child could do this math. The math was not the hard part.

The hard part was the compass.

It sat on the corner of the table where Paloma had placed it an hour ago, deliberately, the way you set down something you intend to pick up again — or the way you set down something you're trying to prove you can live without. Brass casing gone dull at the edges from thirty years of a man's thumb. The lid hinged with a small stiff click that Paloma's hands knew better than their own heartbeat. Inside, under glass gone slightly cloudy with age, the needle swung and settled and pointed north with the serene indifference of a thing that has never owed anyone anything.

Aldous Dunmore's compass. Given to Paloma on their fourteenth birthday, at a dig site in the red hills above Tessel, handed over with both hands and a sentence Paloma could still recite word for word because they'd spent eleven years trying to forget it: *A person who knows where north is can't be lost, only delayed.*

He'd said it like it was a kindness. That was the thing about Aldous Dunmore — he distributed his love the way governments distributed aid: precisely, publicly, and always attached to conditions nobody had agreed to. The compass wasn't a gift. It was an inheritance of expectations. Every time Paloma opened the lid, they could hear the rest of the sentence, the part he hadn't said out loud: *so you'll have no excuse.*

Paloma picked it up. Weighed it. A little over two hundred grams of brass and grief.

The pawnshop was called Ondek's, which was funny in the way Port Sallow things were funny, because everyone knew old Ondek had been dead for years and the shop was run by his nephew, a wide soft-handed man named Cullo who had the particular talent of looking sympathetic while robbing you. His window displayed the town's slow hemorrhage: wedding rings, a violin with a split seam, someone's grandfather's medals, a child's silver cup engraved with a name worn nearly smooth. Port Sallow's whole history of bad luck, arranged on velvet under dusty glass.

The bell over the door gave its flat clank. Cullo looked up from a ledger, and his face did the thing it always did when Paloma came in — a flicker of recognition, then pity, then the careful blankness of a professional.

"Miss Dunmore." He used *miss* the way other people used *sir* to dogs. "Rain's set in."

"It has."

"Payment day," he said, not unkindly, which was worse.

"Payment day."

Paloma set the compass on the counter between them. Cullo looked at it the way a doctor looks at an X-ray. He picked it up with both hands, turned it over, opened the lid. The needle swung, settled, pointed north. He held it level, watching, and Paloma watched him watch it, and hated how much they wanted him to be impressed.

"Brass," he said. "Old. Good make — this is surveyor's grade, not the tourist rubbish." He tilted it toward the lamp. "Movement's clean. Somebody kept this serviced."

*Somebody did,* Paloma thought. *Every year, on the same week, oil in the hinge, fresh glass if it scratched. I watched him do it every year of my childhood and I hated it and now my hands know how to do it too.* They said nothing.

"Twenty-five," Cullo said.

The number landed like a slap. Not because it was low — it was exactly, insultingly fair, the price of brass in a town full of people selling their histories — but because it was so far from four hundred that the gap itself became visible, physical, a distance Paloma would have to cross some other way.

"I need three hundred."

Cullo set the compass down gently, as if it might bruise. "Then I'm not your buyer today."

"There's nothing else."

"There's always something else." He said it without malice. That was the horror of Cullo; he was honest the way the tide is honest. "You've got a coat there worth forty if the lining's intact. You've got boots. You've got—" he paused, delicately, "—whatever else a person carries."

Paloma thought of what else they carried. A bedroll. A knife. A tin of tea. A photograph, soft-cornered, of a woman who had died believing her children were fine. None of it was worth three hundred. All of it together might be worth ninety, and then Paloma would walk out into the rain owning nothing but debt and the clothes the debt allowed them.

"No," Paloma said.

"Then twenty-five for the compass, and you find the rest."

Paloma looked at the compass lying on the scarred wood of the counter, needle steady under its cloudy glass, pointing north toward a mountain range where a man was probably, at this exact moment, standing in some dry university office being respected. The distance between that man and this counter was the whole width of Paloma's life.

"Fine," Paloma said. "Twenty-five."

Cullo wrote the ticket. His pen scratched. He counted out the notes — old ones, soft ones, the whole transaction conducted with the tenderness of a man handling something alive. Paloma took the money, took the ticket, and stood there a moment longer than necessary, because the compass was still on the counter and walking away from it felt like a door closing somewhere inside them, a room going dark.

"You've got ninety days," Cullo said. "Redeem it anytime. Interest accrues—"

"I know how it works."

"Everybody knows how it works," Cullo agreed pleasantly. "Nobody believes it till it happens to them."

---

Outside, the rain had thickened. Paloma pulled up their collar and walked toward Merren & Vole with the twenty-five salins against their chest like a coal.

Merren & Vole occupied the only stone building on Cordwainer Street, because money insures itself. Inside smelled of ink and floor wax and the faint sweet rot of paper stored too long in damp air. Behind the counter sat a clerk named Doss, a narrow man with ink-stained cuffs and the patient eyes of someone who had watched a thousand people arrive carrying hope and leave carrying receipts.

"Dunmore," Paloma said. "Account of E. Dunmore. Payment."

Doss found the ledger without hurrying. Ran a finger down the column. "Four hundred due today."

"I have three hundred and twenty-five."

Doss looked up. He didn't sigh. He was too practiced for sighing. "Partial payment extends nothing, Miss Dunmore. It reduces principal. Interest continues. Next installment remains four hundred, due in thirty days, plus the shortfall carried forward."

"I understand the terms."

"Do you?" He said it mildly, almost curiously, and Paloma realized with a lurch that he wasn't being cruel. He was genuinely asking. Because most people didn't understand the terms — that was the entire architecture of the business. The terms were designed to be understood only after they had already closed around you.

"The estate," Doss continued, tapping the ledger, "remains in arrears following the default on the Tessel property. Your father's surety—"

"My father's surety is my father's problem."

"Your father's surety," Doss said, with the gentle finality of a man reading scripture, "is collateral. Collateral follows the debtor. The debtor is you."

Paloma put the money on the counter. Two hundred and seventy-five of it. Kept fifty back — bus fare out of town, food, the absolute floor of dignity. Doss counted it twice, stamped the ledger, and slid across a receipt so thin it was nearly transparent. Three hundred and twenty-five salins, and the debt had barely breathed. It had shifted its weight. That was all. Somewhere behind the wall, in a filing room, a folder grew one page thicker, and the folder did not care about the rain, or the compass, or the fourteen-year-old in the red hills holding a brass compass in both hands while a proud man said something that sounded like love.

Paloma stood outside Merren & Vole and did the arithmetic again, because the arithmetic was the only prayer that got answered. Fifty in pocket. Four hundred due in thirty days. Work at the docks paid nine a day when the boats came in, and the boats came in maybe half the weeks. Rent was eight a week. Food, if you ate like a person instead of a animal, was ten. The numbers didn't resolve. They never resolved. They just rotated, month after month, like the needle of a compass that points north no matter which way you turn it — and north, in Paloma's life, was always the same direction: *more owed than owned.*

Thirty days. Two hundred and seventy-five short, minimum, assuming nothing went wrong, and something always went wrong, because that was the interest the world charged on being poor.

Paloma started walking. No destination. Walking was free.

---

It was the rain that did it, or the light, or the hour — some alchemy of the gray afternoon that turned thought into memory without permission. Paloma passed the chandlery on Harrow Lane and saw, in the window, a boy of maybe twelve helping his father coil rope, and the father correcting the boy's grip with two fingers, gently, saying something Paloma couldn't hear, and the boy nodding like the correction was a gift — and that was it. That was the whole trigger. Twelve seconds of a stranger's decent childhood, and Paloma was fourteen again, kneeling in the red dirt above Tessel, holding a compass in both hands.

*A person who knows where north is can't be lost, only delayed.*

They had been so proud. That was the unbearable part, looking back — not the fight, not the leaving, not even the silence afterward, eleven years of letters unanswered and doors unopened. The unbearable part was that on that day, at fourteen, Paloma had been *proud*. Proud to be given the compass. Proud to be chosen as the inheritor of north. And then, three years later, standing in the doorway of the house with a bag packed, hearing their father explain — calmly, reasonably, in the voice he used for field reports — why Paloma's mother's medical debts were a lesson in imprudence rather than a wound, why the family land should be signed over to cover his expedition costs rather than mourned, why feeling otherwise was sentiment, and sentiment was a failure of discipline — after all that, the pride had nowhere left to live. So it had turned around and become its opposite, the way pride does. It became departure.

And now here was the joke, the beautiful rotten joke: Paloma Dunmore, who had walked out rather than belong to anyone's ambition, was owned outright by strangers. Had inherited nothing from their father but his debts, because surety follows the debtor, and the debtor is you.

Paloma stopped walking. Stood in the rain on Harrow Lane with water running off their chin, and became aware that their feet had made the decision already. Their feet were pointed back toward Cordwainer Street. Toward Ondek's.

No, they told themselves. That's madness. Twenty-five buys food. Twenty-five is a week of eating like a person. Twenty-five is the difference between next month's arithmetic resolving and not resolving, and the arithmetic never resolves anyway, and—

But the needle was pointing north under cloudy glass, and the needle had never once pointed at Aldous Dunmore's ambitions, and Paloma had sold it for less than a week's food to men who filed their mother's death under *imprudence*, and something in their chest had closed around the loss like a fist and would not open.

Spite, said the sensible voice. This is spite. Spite is expensive.

Yes, said the other voice, the older one, the one that had packed a bag at seventeen. Yes it is.

---

The bell clanked. Cullo looked up, saw Paloma, and his face performed its small sequence — recognition, pity, blankness — but faster this time, because he already knew. Pawnbrokers always know. Half his trade was people coming back within the hour, and he had a drawer for the tickets, organized by shame.

"Changed your mind," he said. Not a question.

"Twenty-five," Paloma said. "Plus whatever your fee is."

"One for the ticket cancellation." He was already writing. "Twenty-six."

Paloma counted out the last of the day's money onto the counter. Fifty minus twenty-six left twenty-four. Twenty-four salins and thirty days and four hundred owed, and the arithmetic was worse now, objectively, measurably worse, and Paloma felt — this was the shameful part, the part they would never say aloud — Paloma felt *better*. Lighter. As if the debt had been a coat and taking on more of it had somehow removed weight.

Cullo unlocked the case, took out the compass, and laid it on the counter between them. Then he did something he hadn't done that morning: he looked at Paloma directly, over the top of his ledger, with his soft professional eyes gone briefly human.

"Ninety days," he said. "Come sooner if you can."

Paloma picked up the compass. Opened the lid. The needle swung, wobbled, settled. North. Unbothered. Pointing at mountains, at rivers, at the high cold country where a certain city was supposed to sleep under stone, according to stories Paloma's father used to tell at campfires when he thought his children weren't listening — stories about terraces cut into the rock above the Kessler headwaters, older than any map, richer than any grant committee's imagination. Stories Paloma had swallowed whole at ten years old and spent a lifetime pretending to spit out.

Paloma closed the lid. The click was small and definite, a period at the end of a sentence.

"Thank you," they heard themselves say to Cullo, absurdly, and walked out into the rain owning twenty-four salins, four hundred salins of monthly obligation, and one brass compass that pointed, with serene indifference, toward everything they had sworn never to go back for.

That night, in the boarding house under the reliable leak, Paloma slept with the compass on the table by the bed, lid open, needle glowing faintly in the dark whenever lightning came. And sometime past midnight, half-dreaming, they heard the knock.

Not at the door. At the window. Third floor. Rain on glass, and under it, faint but unmistakable, the sound of knuckles.

* * *

Paloma sat up. The needle trembled. Down in the street below, a lantern moved through the rain toward the boarding house door, unhurried, the way men move when they are certain of their welcome — and Paloma, heart hammering, reached for the compass not to read it but to hold it, the way you hold the one true thing you own, as the knock came again, closer now, from the stairs.
