## 2. A Trade at Dusk

The rain had been falling for three days, and by the third day Paloma had stopped pretending it would stop.

They'd made camp on a shelf of gravel above the river road, where the Kessler ran brown and fast with the first meltwater coming down off the mountain. The tent was a cheap thing, bought used, its waterproofing gone to powder. Water came through the ceiling in slow beads that collected in the hollow of a tarp and dripped, at intervals too irregular to predict but too regular to ignore, onto the space between Paloma's sleeping bag and their boots.

It was the kind of camp that announced exactly what its owner was worth. Nothing, said the gear. Less than nothing, said the food — half a bag of rice, a tin of oil gone cloudy, salt in a twist of paper. The compass hung from the ridge line inside the tent, because Paloma still couldn't decide whether to sell it or keep it, and an object you can't decide about is an object you carry everywhere.

Paloma had spent the afternoon doing arithmetic. This was most of what they did now. The numbers were always the same numbers: four hundred and twelve owed to Ferro, two hundred to the woman at the fish market whose name they'd never learned, ninety-something to the ferryman who let it slide because he liked them, and the interest that grew on all of it while they slept. They wrote the figures in a notebook with a cover soft as cloth from handling, crossed out old totals, added new ones, and arrived every evening at the same conclusion, which was that the conclusion was worse than yesterday's.

There was no plan in the notebook. There hadn't been a plan in months. There was only the ledger, and the ledger only went one direction.

So when the light began to fail on the third evening, and the river's roar seemed to swell with the dark the way rivers do, Paloma was sitting under the tarp with the notebook open and a pencil stub worn round, doing sums they already knew the answer to. That was when they heard it.

Not a sound so much as a failure of sound — a dragging, halting footfall upstream along the gravel bar, then silence, then another, each one weaker than the last. Something was coming down the bank toward them. Something that could barely walk.

Paloma lifted the tarp flap and looked out.

A man stood twenty meters off, bent like a question mark, one hand pressed flat against his belly, the other reaching ahead of him toward the ground as if the ground might be persuaded to hold him up. He wore canvas that had once been white and was now the color of weak tea. His boots were ruined. Behind him he dragged a pack with a broken frame that caught and turned on every stone, and the pack was leaving a smear behind it on the wet gravel that Paloma would spend the rest of the night trying not to think about.

He took three more steps and fell.

He didn't fall dramatically. He fell the way exhausted things fall — sideways, slowly, his arm folding under him, his face turning down into the gravel as if he intended to keep going that way, downhill, toward the water, until the water finished whatever had started in him.

Paloma sat very still for a moment, doing the thing they always did when something happened that cost money: calculating whether they could afford it. A stranger dying at your campsite costs money. It costs fuel, it costs food, it costs the time you'd have spent working, and if he dies there it costs something else entirely, something without a number attached, though Paloma had learned that everything eventually got a number attached.

Then they went out anyway.

* * *

He was older than he'd looked standing up. On the ground, with the canvas soaked through and clinging to him, he looked like what he was: a man in his sixties who had spent forty years in mountains and had finally run out of mountain. His name, when he could speak, was Ede Voss. He said it twice, because the first time Paloma thought they'd misheard it over the rain.

"Voss," he said. "Like the bird."

"Paloma," they said back. "Also like a bird."

Something moved across his face at that — not quite a smile, more the ghost of one, a twitch of recognition between two creatures named after things with wings. Then his hand went back to his belly and stayed there.

The wound was bad. Paloma had seen wounds before — port towns teach you wounds the way inland towns teach you crops — but this one was different in a way they couldn't immediately name, and the difference frightened them more than blood usually did. It wasn't gushing. It wasn't even bleeding much anymore. It had passed through some threshold past bleeding, into the stage where the body simply begins closing accounts, organ by organ, and the blood stays inside where it does no good at all.

Someone had shot him, high in the gut, days ago by the look of it. Days. He had walked on it for days, downstream, away from wherever it happened, following the river the way wounded animals follow water, and Paloma understood with a clarity that felt almost like a gift that he had not been walking to find help. He had been walking to find someone. He just hadn't known who until he saw their fire smoke, or their tent, or their shape against the dusk.

"Who shot you?" Paloma asked, because it seemed important, and because asking questions was the only tool they had.

Voss considered the question. He lay on the gravel with the rain hitting his face and considered it, and then he shook his head, once, deliberately, the way a man refuses a thing he has already decided to refuse.

"No," he said. "Doesn't matter now." And then, after a pause, correcting himself in a way that suggested it mattered enormously but not in the direction Paloma expected: "Matters later. Not mine to say."

Paloma got him under the tarp. It took a while. He weighed more than a dying man should weigh, or maybe everything weighs more when you're lifting it alone in the rain, and by the time they had him on the sleeping bag with their own bedroll folded under his head, Paloma was soaked to the skin and shaking, and the arithmetic of the evening had acquired a new column of costs.

They heated water. They fed him sips of it, sweetened with the last of a packet of sugar they'd been saving for exactly no occasion, because there are never occasions, there is only saving. He drank, held it down, drank again. For perhaps an hour he seemed to come back from somewhere — color in his face, strength in his voice, enough to talk.

And then he talked. Not about the shooting. About the mountain.

* * *

Here is what Paloma learned in that hour, lying beside a dying stranger under a leaking tarp while the river roared below them like a freight train that never passes:

Ede Voss had spent most of his life looking for something, and in the last year he had found it, and finding it had killed him.

He spoke in fragments, the way dying men do, sentences that started strong and dissolved mid-word, so that Paloma spent the night assembling him afterward like a pot from shards. But the fragments were vivid. He had been up in the headwaters of the Kessler — the true headwaters, above the falls, above the gorge, in country so closed and so high that official maps simply stopped drawing it, the contour lines thinning out into blankness like a coastline fading off the edge of a chart. Prospectors called it the Quiet Country, because the government had sealed it after the landslide years ago, and because nothing that went up there ever seemed to come back down with anything except stories, and the stories got fewer every year.

Voss had gone up anyway. Old men and fools go up; Voss had been both.

He described it in pieces. A valley above the flood line, dry, sheltered, ringed with stone. Masonry — real masonry, cut blocks fitted without mortar, walls running up out of the grass like the ribs of something buried. A cavern mouth where the whole river came out of the mountain in one throat of white water, and the mouth of the cavern was faced with worked stone, dressed and fitted, older than anything anyone had a right to expect this far north and this far up. A city. Not a ruin — a city, intact, sealed, preserved by its own altitude, sitting above the flood line in the one place the water had never reached.

He said the word *city* the way you'd say a word you'd checked and rechecked for thirty years. He said it tiredly, without triumph, which was somehow worse than if he'd shouted it. Men who shout have found gold. Men who go quiet have found something they don't know how to hold.

"I mapped it," he said. "Drew it. All of it. Where I could stand, what I could see, the ways in and the ways that aren't ways, they only look like ways." He paused. "I drew the river too. Drew it honest. Some men draw maps that flatter the mountain. Mine doesn't flatter anybody."

Paloma asked the obvious question, the greedy one, the one any person with a ledger full of numbers would ask: how much was it worth?

Voss looked at them for a long time. Long enough that Paloma started to feel the question sitting in their own chest like a stone they'd swallowed.

"You're asking wrong," he said finally. "But I'll answer anyway, because you carried me in out of the rain and nobody made you."

He told them what it was worth, and the answer was: it depended. It depended on what was done with it. In certain hands, the map was a fortune — concessions, salvage rights, the kind of paper that turned stone into money overnight. In other hands it was dynamite and drowned villages and a second landslide with a different date on the report. He didn't say which hands were which. He said, instead, something Paloma would turn over in their mind for weeks afterward, something that sounded at the time like an old man's caution and would later reveal itself as the entire plot of the book they were living inside:

"The mountain keeps what it takes," Voss said. "That's the first rule up there. Second rule is: the mountain gives back what you promise it. Not what you want. What you promise."

Paloma didn't understand that yet. They wrote it in the notebook anyway, under the debts, in the margin, small, because it didn't fit anywhere else and wouldn't leave them alone.

* * *

Somewhere past midnight the sugar water stopped staying down, and the brief resurrection ended, and Voss began the long withdrawal that gut wounds deliver — the cooling, the graying, the retreat inward, hour by hour, like a tide going out over a man and not coming back.

He knew it was happening. Dying men know. He talked less and less, and what he said got simpler, and at some point in the smallest hours he stopped talking about the mountain altogether and started talking about the map itself — the physical object, the greasecloth, the folded square of waxed fabric he'd kept against his chest for a year, through weather and wariness and finally through the walk downstream that had opened his wound and emptied him.

"It's drawn on greasecloth," he said, "because paper dies up there. Paper rots, paper burns, paper blows away. Greasecloth survives being folded a thousand times. Survives rain. Survives you." A pause. "Survives almost anything except fire, and men who shouldn't have it."

Paloma asked who shouldn't have it.

He didn't answer directly. He answered the way he'd answered the question about who shot him — obliquely, with a refusal shaped like an answer. He said: "You'll know them when you see them. You'll know them by what they offer you." And then, after a silence long enough to be its own sentence: "What they offer will be generous. That's how you'll know."

Paloma lay awake beside him after that, listening to his breathing change register — the wet rattle rising, the intervals stretching — and thinking about generosity, and about how every generous thing that had ever entered their life recently had entered wearing a smile and carrying a bill.

Around four in the morning, Voss rallied one last time. It was a small rally, ten minutes maybe, the famous false dawn of the dying, and he spent it the way he seems to have planned to spend it since the moment he fell into their camp: on business.

He pulled himself partway upright — Paloma helped, hands under his shoulders, feeling the terrible lightness of him, the way a body starts shedding its own weight before it sheds everything else — and he reached into his shirt and brought out the map.

It was smaller than Paloma expected. Folded to the size of a thick book, bound with a cord, the greasecloth stiff and dark with age and handling, soft at the creases the way cloth gets soft, hard nowhere. It smelled of wax and cold and, faintly, of the man himself, of the year it had spent riding over his heart.

"This goes upstream," Voss said. His voice was nearly gone. He said it slowly, spending words like coins he couldn't earn back. "To the source. Before the monsoon. After the monsoon the high country seals — snow, water, slides — and nobody goes up, and nobody comes down, and whatever window there is closes till next year, and I won't be here for next year, and neither will you be able to afford next year."

He put the map in Paloma's hands. Both his hands over theirs, wrapping them around it, the way you'd wrap someone's hands around a rope you were about to let go of.

"I'm not giving it to you," he said. "Understand that. I'm lending it. To whoever carries it. Maps like this don't belong to people. They belong to the route." He coughed, and the cough cost him visibly, and he kept talking through it because he had very little left and he knew exactly what he wanted to buy with it. "Carry it up. Carry it to the source. When you get there — when you're standing where the river comes out of the stone — you'll understand what it's for. I can't tell you. If I tell you, you'll do the other thing. Everyone does the other thing."

"What's the other thing?" Paloma asked.

Voss smiled. It was the first real smile he'd produced all night, and it was tired and crooked and completely without warmth, and it frightened Paloma more than the wound had.

"Sell," he said.

* * *

He made them promise before dawn.

Not with a handshake — he was past handshakes — but with the grip. He took Paloma's hand in both of his, and his grip was still shockingly strong, the grip of a man who had hauled ropes up cliffs for forty years, and he held on, and he said the terms out loud, plainly, the way a man dictates a contract he intends to enforce from beyond the reach of courts:

"One promise. One. You carry this map to the source of the Kessler before the monsoon seals the high country. You don't sell it. You don't show it to anyone who offers you money for it — and people will offer, and the offers will be good, and that's how you'll know they're the wrong people. You carry it up. That's all. That's the whole contract. Everything else is yours to decide."

"And if I can't?" Paloma said. "If I don't make it?"

His grip tightened.

"Then give it to the river," he said. "Give it to the river and let it go. Better lost than sold. Say it."

Paloma said it. Out loud, into the gray pre-dawn, with a dying man's hands locked around theirs: *Better lost than sold.* It felt absurd. It felt like swearing an oath over a debt they hadn't agreed to, taking custody of a liability in greasecloth, accepting delivery of a package whose contents were unknown and whose sender was about to die. Every instinct they had — every instinct the ledger had trained into them — said refuse this, walk away, bury the man, burn the map, go back to town, sell the compass, start over smaller.

They promised anyway.

Later they would try to work out why, and the honest answer would be that they never fully could. Part of it was that refusing a dying man's last request felt like a theft worse than any they'd committed. Part of it was the word *city*, spoken quietly, thirty years' worth of quiet, into the ear of someone who had grown up on digs and had archaeology in their blood the way other people had salt in theirs. Part of it — the largest part, the part they'd admit to least — was that the promise was the first thing in years that pointed anywhere but down. Every force in Paloma's life pointed downhill: the debts, the interest, the tide of small obligations pulling them back to the port and the pawnshop and the fish market woman and the ferryman. The map pointed up. Upstream. Toward the blank place on the chart where the contour lines gave out.

Nobody had offered them uphill in a long time.

Voss died at dawn.

It was not peaceful, whatever the songs say. It was mechanical, and slow, and then suddenly not slow at all — the breathing spacing out, the intervals lengthening like a clock winding down, and then one breath with a long way to travel and no return trip in it, and then the grip on Paloma's hand easing not gradually but all at once, like a knot letting go.

He died holding their hand. Paloma stayed holding his afterward, for a while, because it seemed rude to let go first, and because they weren't sure yet what letting go would mean — whether releasing his hand released them from the promise or confirmed it, whether the contract died with the contractor or transferred, in full, to the carrier.

It transferred. They understood that eventually, sitting there in the first gray light with a dead man's grip cooling in theirs. Promises made aloud in the presence of death don't lapse. They convert. They become weight.

* * *

Dawn came up the color of dishwater, and the rain thinned to a drifting mist, and the river below the camp ran high and loud and indifferent, carrying meltwater down from a mountain that didn't care what had happened on its gravel bars in the night.

Paloma buried Ede Voss above the flood line, because it seemed correct — a man who had spent his life above flood lines should stay above one — and because the alternative was the river, and the river takes things, and Paloma had promised a dying man that some things were better lost than sold and had meant it about the map and found, shoveling the first spadeful of gravel, that they meant it about the man too. They marked the grave with a cairn, because Voss had mentioned cairns once, in one of the fragments, approvingly, as the honest architecture of the high country: stones stacked by hand, by travelers, for travelers, asking nothing and promising nothing except *someone passed here.*

They stacked the cairn well. It took an hour. Their hands were steady the whole time, which surprised them, and would continue to surprise them for days.

Then they sat down outside the tent with the map and the notebook and the pencil stub, and opened both, side by side, and looked at what they had actually inherited.

On the left: a ledger. Four hundred and twelve, two hundred, ninety-something, interest compounding nightly, a compass in hock-or-not-hock, a dead man's grave, a borrowed tarp, half a bag of rice. Solvent nowhere. Owned by everyone.

On the right: a folded square of greasecloth, cord-bound, warm from having ridden over a dying man's heart for a year, containing — when they finally, carefully, with clean dry hands, unfolded it — a hand-drawn map of the Kessler headwaters rendered in ink and grease pencil on waxed fabric, dense with notation, annotated in a small precise hand, the work of decades. Contour lines. Camp marks. Water sources marked with a symbol Paloma didn't recognize. Distances in days, not miles, because Voss had evidently understood that in the high country distance is time and time is everything. And at the top of the sheet, above the last drawn tributary, above the point where every official map went blank, the source itself: a cavern mouth, faced with stone, ringed with masonry, drawn in cross-section like an architect's plate, labeled in Voss's cramped hand with a single word.

Paloma read the word. Then read it again. Then sat for a long time without reading anything, just looking at the drawing, at the careful cross-hatching of the cavern, at the little figures Voss had sketched at the entrance for scale, stick-thin, awed, tiny against the stone.

The word was *CITY*. Just that. Capital letters, underlined twice, the underline pressed so hard into the greasecloth it had embossed the fabric.

And beneath it, smaller, in the same hand, a line that Paloma copied into the notebook because it refused to stay on the map alone:

*The river was here first. Remember whose city it is.*

Paloma looked at that line for a long time. The rain had stopped. Mist moved on the water. Somewhere downriver, faintly, a boat engine — the first sign of the world resuming, of the port waking up, of creditors and enforcers and men with offers beginning their day.

The arithmetic resumed in their head without being invited. Nine days, maybe ten, to the source, if the map's day-counts were honest and the weather held. The monsoon was due in eleven. The food would last four. The debts were due in five. Every number in the ledger said *stay*, *settle*, *sell*, and every line on the map said *up*, and for the first time in years the two sets of numbers were in open conflict, and Paloma Dunmore sat on a gravel bar at dawn holding a dead man's map and understood, with total clarity, that they could not afford to go —

— and that they were going anyway.

They folded the map along its old creases, softly, the way you handle a thing that has survived more than you have. They tied the cord. They put it inside their shirt, against their chest, where it had ridden for a year against another heartbeat, and felt it settle there, cool and stiff and heavier than its weight.

Then they picked up the notebook, tore out the page of sums, and wrote a single line at the top of a fresh page, in handwriting firmer than it had been in months:

*Day 1. Upstream.*

Downriver, faint through the mist, the boat engine grew louder.
