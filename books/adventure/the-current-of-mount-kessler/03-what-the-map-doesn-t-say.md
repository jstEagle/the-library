## 3. What the Map Doesn't Say

The café at Corren's Landing had a name once, painted on a board above the door, but the rain had eaten the letters down to a ghost and nobody had bothered to repaint it. Locals just called it the Wet. Paloma understood why within a minute of sitting down: the roof leaked in four places, and the owner had given up on buckets and simply positioned the tables where the drips wouldn't land.

The coffee was cheap the way medicine is cheap when it's the only medicine — it cost too much and worked anyway. Paloma wrapped both hands around the tin cup and let the steam climb into their face while the map lay flat on the table under a saucer at each corner.

Ede Voss had drawn it in grease pencil on waxed cloth, and he had drawn it the way a man draws a thing he has carried in his body for forty years. There were no survey marks, no grid references, no neat cartographer's flourishes. There was the river — a single unbroken line, thick and confident, running from the lower left corner to the upper right like a pulse across a chart. Everything else was annotation. Falls here, drawn as three small jagged strokes. A rope crossing, a loop with a gap in it. A place marked only *old burn, don't camp*. A bend where the line doubled back on itself and Voss had written, in a hand gone shaky with age or cold: *the river lies to you here. trust the stone.*

And at the very top, where the pulse began, a small circle. Not filled in. Outlined twice, as if he'd traced it over and over, the way you worry a loose tooth.

Beside it, one word: *the mouth.*

Paloma drank the coffee and read the map the way they'd been taught to read maps before they'd been taught much of anything else — by a man who believed terrain was a text and illiteracy was a moral failing. Aldous Dunmore, crouched over a trench in some sun-scalded country, tapping a trowel against the stratigraphy: *The ground remembers, Paloma. Your job is to not interrupt it while it talks.*

They'd been eleven. They'd believed him completely. That was the part that still stung — not the fights at the end, not the slammed door and the bus station and the eleven years of silence, but the beginning, when the belief had been whole.

Paloma bent over the map and started doing what their father had taught them to do with any document: interrogate the edges. Who drew this, and what did they assume the reader already knew?

The lower corner had a scale — Voss's own, in paces, which meant it was honest and useless at the same time. The upper corner had a date, forty-one years old. And along the right edge, running vertically, Voss had drawn a boundary line. Dotted. With a small square at the top and a small square at the bottom, the way you mark a gate.

Paloma stared at it. The coffee went cold in the cup.

They knew that line. Every kid who'd grown up in the Kessler watershed knew that line, the way you know the outline of a scar on your own body. It was the boundary of the Kessler Reserve — drawn on the schoolroom maps, printed in red on the warning signs at the trailheads, read out in the same flat voice every year on the anniversary: *closed by order of the Watershed Authority until further notice.*

Closed since the landslide. Closed since the dam above Serrat village let go on a night in the wet season and took eleven people down the valley in ninety seconds. Closed, officially, for "geological remediation." Closed, unofficially, for eleven years, because the government had discovered that the cheapest way to deal with a disaster was to stop talking about it and fence off the conversation.

Paloma set the cup down. Their hands were steady, which surprised them. Inside, something was moving like water finding a crack.

The source of the Kessler — the mouth, Voss's word, and Paloma was beginning to understand why he'd chosen it — was inside the reserve. Not near it. Not along the boundary. Up at the head of the gorge, above the falls, in the closed heart of the closed country.

They did the arithmetic without wanting to. The reserve wasn't patrolled — the Authority had cut the ranger budget years ago, there was maybe one ranger left for the whole watershed, an old man everyone said had gone strange. But the closure meant no maps sold, no guides hired, no rescue if things went wrong. It meant that if Paloma went in and something happened, they would not be a missing person. They would be a rumor.

It also meant that whatever Voss had found up there, nobody had found it first. Eleven years of fence line between the world and the mouth of the river.

And it meant the discovery — the city, the terraces, whatever the circle at the top of the map actually held — would be found by a person with no permit, no institutional backing, and no legal standing whatsoever, inside a site the government had buried for reasons that had never survived daylight.

Paloma sat back. The drip from the roof found a new seam and began tapping the floor three tables away, patient as a metronome.

*Deliver it to the source before monsoon season.* That was the promise. Voss had asked for delivery, not discovery. Paloma turned the phrase over and over, looking for the trap in it, the way you'd probe a floorboard in a house you didn't trust.

The rain thickened outside. The door opened.

Paloma knew before they looked up. Not by any sixth sense — by the quality of the silence. The two dockworkers at the far table stopped talking mid-sentence, the way people do when a large animal enters a room, and the owner behind the counter went very busy with a cloth and a glass that didn't need cleaning.

The man who came in was big without being heavy, the way a door is big. He wore a good coat that had been rained on enough times to stop pretending, and he moved like someone who had learned that moving slowly made other people move fast. He scanned the café once — a full sweep, unhurried, cataloguing exits and occupants in the time it took most people to find the counter — and then his eyes settled on Paloma and stayed there, and he smiled.

It was a good smile. That was the worst part. It reached his eyes. It looked like it had been used on friends.

"Dunmore," he said. "Paloma Dunmore."

Paloma's hand moved without deciding to, and the map was under their shirt before the man had crossed the room, the waxed cloth cold against their sternum where the cord had already warmed a groove.

"Who's asking?"

"Brannic." He pulled out the chair opposite and sat down in it like he'd bought the chair. "Corven Brannic. I work for Meridian Credit — you'll have met my colleagues. They're the ones who write you the letters. I'm the one who visits."

"I don't have anything worth visiting for."

"Sure you do." He raised two fingers toward the counter, and the owner, without being asked, brought a cup of the same cheap coffee and set it down and left. Brannic wrapped his hands around it the way Paloma had, and for a moment the mirror image of it was almost funny. "You've got a debt, a deadline, and as of last night, I'd guess, something valuable. Am I close?"

Paloma said nothing. Rain on the roof. Drip on the floor. Somewhere downriver, the boat engine they'd heard at dawn had faded to nothing.

Brannic drank his coffee like it was coffee and not a negotiation. "I'm going to tell you a story," he said, "and you can decide how much of it is true, because I couldn't possibly prove any of it. There was a prospector. Old, half-starved, been living up the coast on whatever he could pull out of the hills. Came into one of our offices two years ago trying to borrow against a claim. We turned him down. He got loud. Said he knew where something was that would buy and sell the whole bank." Brannic smiled again, smaller this time. "People say that. Ninety-nine times out of a hundred it's a vein of quartz and a head full of fumes."

"And the hundredth?"

"The hundredth time," Brannic said, "somebody finds him dead in a camp outside Corren's Landing with a hole in him and a map missing, and the map turns up in the hands of a debtor with a famous last name and eleven years of bad luck." He set the cup down. "I'm not accusing you of anything. I'm saying we've been watching for that map for two years, and last night a fisherman saw a stranger sitting up with a dying man, and this morning that stranger walked into the Wet and ordered coffee and sat alone with something flat wrapped in oilcloth, and here I am."

Paloma's heart was going hard, but they kept their face where it was. *The ground remembers. Your job is to not interrupt it.* They had learned stillness at the same trench, at eleven, from the same man. It was strange, the things you kept.

"So you want the map."

"I want to make you an offer." Brannic leaned forward, and his voice dropped into a register that was almost warm, almost confiding, the voice of a man letting you behind the counter. "You're going upstream. I can see it in you — you've already decided, you're just working out the how. And the how is a problem, because the source is inside the reserve, and the reserve is closed, and you've got no gear worth the name, no partner, no permit, and about forty days before the monsoon shuts the high country like a book."

"You've read the map."

"I've read *about* the map." He held up a hand. "I don't need to see it. I need you to understand your situation, and I'm the only person in this valley who understands it fully. So. The offer." He counted it off on thick fingers. "One: I outfit you. Proper gear, food for sixty days, rope, boots that aren't held together with hope. Two: I get you past the boundary clean — there are ways, and they're not forgeries, before you ask, I don't do forgeries, forgeries get found. Three: when you come out, whatever's up there — the claim, the site, the find, the name on the discovery — Meridian takes the paper interest. The debt. The whole ugly ledger, gone, signed off, stamped. You walk out owning the discovery and owing nothing."

"And you walk out owning—"

"The financing," Brannic said. "The boring part. The loans, the permits, the lawyers. You get the glory and the clean hands. I get the paperwork." He spread his hands, a man showing they were empty. "Paloma. Look at your life. Look at it honestly, the way I have to look at it, because it's my money on the line. You pawned a compass last month and bought it back the same day. That's not a man managing his affairs. That's a man drowning in inches of water. And now you're holding the one thing in this valley that could change all of it, and you're going to walk into a closed disaster zone alone with a dead man's pencil marks." He shook his head, gently, almost sadly. "I'm not your enemy. I'm the only person who's ever offered you a boat."

The rain came down harder. Paloma looked at the window, at the gray street running with water, at the hills upriver where the mist sat on the ridgelines like a held breath. Forty-one years ago Ede Voss had drawn a line up into those hills and circled the place where it began. Eleven years ago the government had drawn a different line across them and told everyone to forget what was inside. And now here sat a man with a friendly face and a ledger where his heart should be, offering to carry Paloma across both lines — for a price that wasn't written down anywhere.

*The river lies to you here,* Voss had written. *Trust the stone.*

Paloma looked back at Brannic. "Why do you care what's up there? Meridian lends money on boats and warehouses. Since when do they finance archaeology?"

Something moved behind Brannic's eyes — quick, controlled, gone. It was the first honest thing he'd shown.

"Since the numbers changed," he said.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer there is." He finished his coffee and stood, unhurried, and put coins on the table — too many, deliberately, a small performance of generosity for the room. "You don't have to answer today. You don't even have to answer this week. But the monsoon doesn't negotiate, and neither does the calendar, and I'm the only outfitter between here and the reserve who doesn't ask questions." He took a card from his coat — heavy stock, plain, a name and a number and nothing else — and laid it on the table next to the cold cup. "One thing I'll ask in return. Just one."

Paloma waited.

"Don't go in without me." Brannic smiled the good smile again, and this time Paloma watched it carefully, and saw what lived underneath it: not menace, exactly. Patience. The patience of a man who owned a great many deadlines and knew how to wait for one of them to come due. "The mountain's already taken eleven people off that valley. It doesn't need a twelfth, and I'd hate to have paid for your gear and lost the investment."

He went out into the rain without a coat over his head, as if weather were something that happened to other people, and the door swung shut behind him, and the two dockworkers started talking again all at once, too loudly, the way people do when a large animal has left the room.

Paloma sat for a long time. The coffee was cold. The card lay on the table, plain and patient.

They thought about the letters from Meridian — the polite ones first, then the less polite ones, then the ones that named dates and consequences. They thought about the pawnshop, and the compass coming home an hour later, and what that hour had cost in something that didn't show up on any ledger. They thought about their father, somewhere upriver already, with money that had almost certainly come through the same friendly hands, hunting the same city with the same institutional machinery Paloma had spent eleven years refusing to touch.

And they thought about Ede Voss, dying at dawn with his hand around theirs, and the one word at the top of the map, traced twice: *the mouth.*

*Deliver it to the source.* Voss hadn't said anything about partners. He hadn't said anything about financing. He had looked at a stranger in the dark and chosen them — not their name, not their credit, not their father — and he had made them promise one thing, and the promise had been about the river, and the river was the one party in all of this that had never lied to anybody.

Paloma picked up the card. Turned it over. Blank on the back, of course. Men like Brannic didn't put anything in writing they didn't have to.

They stood, left the coins where Brannic had overpaid — let the Wet keep them, a small debt discharged — and pushed out into the rain with the map against their chest and the cold working down their collar.

The outfitter's row was three streets over, and it opened at seven, and it was nearly seven now. Paloma didn't need Brannic's gear. Didn't need his rope, his permits, his clean ways past the boundary line. What they needed was to stand in front of a rack of secondhand equipment with almost no money and buy exactly what a dead man's map said they'd need, alone, on their own ledger, and walk out of town before the day was old — because if Brannic had found them in a leaky café in Corren's Landing, then he wasn't the only one looking, and every hour they sat still was an hour someone else spent closing the distance.

Behind them, up the gray street, a man in a rain slicker who had been reading the same notice board for ten minutes folded his paper and started to follow.
