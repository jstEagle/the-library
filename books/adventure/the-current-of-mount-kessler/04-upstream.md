## 4. Upstream

The gorge began where the road gave up.

Paloma stood at the break in the treeline with the map folded against their chest and listened to the river before they saw it — a cold roar rising out of the earth like something breathing. The Kessler didn't run here so much as fall and climb by turns, dropping through slots of green stone, gathering itself, falling again. The sound got into the ribs. It got into the teeth.

*Follow the water up,* Voss had said, in the last hour when his voice still worked. *The river knows the way home. You just have to be stubborn enough to go against it.*

Paloma was stubborn enough. It was possibly the only thing they had ever been in surplus.

They checked the map one more time at the mouth of the gorge, though there was nothing to check it against yet — just a hand-inked line of water between two ridgelines drawn in grease pencil, the paper soft as cloth from years of being carried somewhere in a pocket that sweated. Voss had drawn it from memory, or from having been there, or both; the difference mattered less than Paloma kept telling themselves it did. A cairn marked here. A deadfall there. A place where the canyon pinched to nothing and the trail left the water entirely, drawn as a small anxious zigzag above the river line, like the map itself had been afraid of that stretch.

Three days' walk to the falls. Four if the weather turned. Five and the monsoon would start making its own decisions about the route.

Paloma shouldered the pack and went in under the trees.

* * *

The first hour was all noise and wet light. Water everywhere — sheeting down mossy faces, hanging in threads off the cliffs, thundering white in the narrows below. The air tasted like iron and cold stone. Every few hundred meters the trail vanished into scree and Paloma had to read the river instead, working upstream along the banks the way you'd work a sentence backward, looking for where it started.

Their father had taught them that. Not the reading of rivers — he'd never cared much for water, called it the enemy of context — but the reading of ground. *The land is a document, Paloma. Most people only ever read the cover.* He'd said it kneeling in a trench outside some nameless hill fort, trowel in his fist like a pen, dirt to the elbows, happier than Paloma had ever seen him anywhere indoors. They'd been nine. Maybe ten. Old enough to hold the sieve and feel important doing it.

That had been the good years, or what passed for them. Seasons on the road in a truck that smelled of diesel and old paper. Learning to tell occupation layers apart by color the way other kids learned to tell candy apart. Falling asleep in tents while the adults argued stratigraphy by lantern light, their father's voice going low and certain, certain, certain, and Paloma drifting off believing that certainty was a thing a person could simply have, if they worked hard enough at the ground.

It took twenty years to learn that what he had wasn't certainty. It was appetite. And appetite doesn't care what it eats.

Paloma climbed over a fallen birch, boots skidding on bark slicked black with spray, and refused to think about the last fight. There would be time for that on the mountain. There was always time for it; it lived rent-free in their skull like a creditor.

* * *

By midday the gorge narrowed and the map began to earn its keep.

There was the cairn — smaller than drawn, half-collapsed, but unmistakably a human pile of stones in a place with no other human piles. And past it, the deadfall: three trees down across the slope in a fan, rotted to gray lace, exactly where the grease pencil said they'd be. Paloma stood in front of the deadfall longer than they needed to, feeling something dangerous loosen in their chest.

Because this was the part nobody warned you about — the part that made the whole profession a disease. When the map matched the world, when the drawing became the ground under your feet, something lit up in the deep wiring. Joy first. Then greed. Then the quiet conviction that you were meant to find this, chosen, special — and that was the trapdoor, right there, the one their father had gone through and never come back up out of. Every dig, every discovery, every artifact lifted shining out of the dirt: he'd taken each one as proof of himself. Paloma had watched him do it for years before understanding that the sites had never been the point. The point was Aldous Dunmore, vindicated, again, forever.

Voss hadn't wanted the city found. That was the strange thing, the thing Paloma kept turning over like a coin of uncertain metal. A dying man with the greatest map in the province, and what he'd asked for was delivery. Upstream. Before the water. As if the carrying mattered more than the arrival.

Paloma ate standing up, watching the river, and tried to remember Voss's face without the blood on it. It was already going. Memory was a sieve and they'd never learned to hold anything gently in it — not the fights, not the good seasons, not even the dying. Everything came through blurred.

They walked on.

* * *

The afternoon gave them the pinch.

The canyon drew in until the walls nearly kissed, the river compressed to a single furious channel, and the trail — such as it was — climbed out onto the rock shelf above, a ledge maybe a boot wide in places, running along the cliff face with the white water roaring thirty feet down and to the left. The map's anxious zigzag. Paloma stood at the base of the climb with their heart going hard and told themselves the truth about it: they were not afraid of heights. They were afraid of falling, which was different, and which was worse, because fear of falling was just honesty.

Halfway across the shelf, with the spray icing their sleeves and the drop breathing cold against their cheek, the memory arrived uninvited — the way it always did, in the worst places, where there was no room to set it down.

The last fight had happened in a hotel room in the capital, after the inquiry. After the landslide report came out and eleven names went into the ground twice — once under the mud, once under the official record. *Human error. Unauthorized settlement in a designated flood zone. No fault attributable to the survey team.* Their father had signed off on findings that buried a village twice, and Paloma had stood in that beige room with its government art and said the word *fraud* out loud for the first time in their life.

He hadn't shouted. That was the thing people never believed. Aldous Dunmore didn't shout; shouting was for people who could lose an argument. He'd looked at Paloma with that terrible patient expression, the one he used on junior staff and stubborn soil samples, and said, *You don't understand the data.*

And Paloma — twenty-six, broke, desperate for him to say almost anything else — had said, *No. I understand you.*

Then the door, then the stairs, then eleven years of small debts and smaller rooms, and the compass pawned and bought back and pawned again like a heartbeat on a monitor, flatlining and reviving, flatlining and reviving.

Paloma came off the shelf on the far side with shaking hands and no memory of crossing the worst section. The body had done it without them. Fine. Good. One of them could stay calm.

* * *

Camp that night was a hollow behind a leaning slab, out of the wind that funneled up the gorge after dark and made the pines talk to themselves. Paloma got a fire small enough to hide and heated water for the last of the coffee, which had gone stale and tasted faintly of the tin, and sat listening to the river do its endless work in the dark.

It was while feeding a stick to the flames that they saw it.

Not at first a thing. First a wrongness. The firelight caught the ground near the slab's edge and threw shadows the wrong direction, and one shadow had edges too clean. Paloma set down the cup and went over and crouched, and there it was: a boot print, pressed into the damp silt where the hollow drained toward the river. Recent — the edges still sharp, the silt at the bottom still dark, not dried. Too big for their own boots. Too fresh for yesterday.

And further along, half-hidden under the slab's overhang, a cigarette end. Filtered. Ground out with a heel, deliberate, the way someone grinds one out who doesn't care who finds it.

Paloma sat back on their heels and looked at the dark mouth of the gorge downstream, where everything they'd come from was, and then upstream, where everything they were going to was, and felt the exhilaration of the morning curdle into something colder and lonelier.

Someone else was on this trail. Someone ahead of them, moving slower or faster they couldn't tell, but ahead — because the print pointed up. And there was only one thing up this gorge worth walking toward, and only a handful of people alive who knew it existed, and Paloma had buried one of them at dawn two days ago.

Unless Voss had talked. Unless, in some bar, in some feverish hour before the end found him, he'd shown someone else the greasecloth. Or unless the map had a twin. Or unless none of it mattered and this was a hunter, a ranger, a smuggler, anyone, and Paloma was alone in a closed gorge at night inventing enemies out of boot prints.

They banked the fire low and sat up most of the night anyway, back to the stone, compass in one hand and the knife in the other, listening to the river and the pines and — once, around the coldest hour, unmistakable, half a mile up the dark —

a sound like a boot turning on gravel.

Then nothing. The gorge swallowed it. The river went on roaring, indifferent, carrying everything downstream except the truth, which apparently had to be carried up.

Paloma watched the sky go gray over the eastern ridge and thought about the arithmetic. Three days to the falls, minus whatever head start the print's owner had. Food for five days, now four and a half if sleep cost extra. And somewhere behind them, days back down the trail, a man named Brannic who smiled like a handshake and wrote contracts like traps, who knew roughly where they'd gone and exactly what they owed.

The compass needle steadied north-northwest, toward the source, toward whatever was waiting up there wearing somebody else's boots.

Paloma kicked dirt over the fire, shouldered the pack, and started climbing — faster now, and no longer quite alone, which on this mountain felt less like company than like being followed.
