## 5. The Ferryman's Toll

The rope crossing announced itself before I saw it — a groan of wet hemp somewhere in the fog ahead, rhythmic as a pulley, patient as a thing that had been waiting years for company.

I came down the last stretch of trail with my boots sucking at the mud and the river roaring somewhere below me, invisible, a sound like a freight train that never arrived. Three days in the gorge now. My shoulders had learned the weight of the pack. My feet had learned every way the mountain could lie to them — the root that looked like stone, the stone that looked like root. Somewhere behind me, hours back or maybe a full day, I'd found a boot print pressed into the clay of the trail, deep-heeled, recent, and I'd told myself it was nothing. A ranger. A poacher. Anyone but who my gut said it was.

The fog thinned and there it was: the gorge pinched to perhaps forty meters across, black water churning through the throat of it, and strung above the flood line a single rope spanning bank to bank, sagging in the middle like a tired smile. A wooden seat hung from a rusted trolley. On the far side, a small hut of corrugated tin leaned against a boulder, smoke curling from its stovepipe.

And standing at the near anchor, arms crossed, watching me come, was a woman.

She was maybe thirty, compact, dressed in the olive canvas of a reserve ranger with the insignia torn off — not faded, *torn*, deliberately, leaving pale rectangles on both sleeves. Her hair was cut short and practical. She held no weapon, which somehow made her more alarming, because she stood between me and the only crossing for fifty kilometers as if she were the weapon.

"You're not supposed to be here," she said. Not hostile. Just stating weather.

"Neither are you," I said, "if the reserve's closed."

"It is closed." She didn't blink. "That's why I'm here."

I stopped ten paces out and set the pack down, keeping my hands visible, an old habit from port towns where the arithmetic of debt made everyone a potential thief. Up close she looked weathered in a way that wasn't age — a particular kind of erosion around the eyes, like someone who'd watched something enormous happen and spent every year since measuring herself against it.

"Paloma Dunmore," I said.

"I know."

That landed wrong. "You know?"

"The university man's kid." She tilted her head toward the far bank. "There was a notice, years ago. Photos in the papers after the landslide. The archaeologist and his family at the memorial service." Her eyes did a slow inventory of me — the pawnshop jacket, the patched pack, the compass cord at my neck. "You look less expensive now."

Heat crawled up my neck. "You have me confused with—"

"I don't confuse things." She uncrossed her arms. "Sefa Imara. Reserve ranger, what's left of one. You want the crossing, you pay the toll."

"There's no toll posted."

"The sign washed away in 'nineteen." She said it flatly, and something underneath the flatness moved, quick as a fish under ice. "I'm the sign now."

* * *

We sat under the tin overhang while rain needled the roof and she boiled water for tea that tasted like bark and iron. The hut was one room: a cot, a shelf of canned goods arranged with military precision, a hand-crank radio with its antenna snapped, a wall covered in a chart of the river drawn in pencil so fine it looked embroidered. Depth soundings. Flow rates. Dates going back eleven years, unbroken, every single day.

Eleven years. The landslide had been twelve years ago. She'd started recording before the water even settled.

"You live out here alone?" I asked.

"I live out here *correctly*," she said, and poured the tea.

I drank. It was terrible and hot and I was grateful for it in a way that embarrassed me. Through the doorway the rope crossed the gorge into fog, and below us the river made its endless argument with the rocks.

"Half your food," Sefa said. "That's the toll."

"That's half my trip."

"That's why it's a toll and not a fee." She nodded at my pack. "Open it."

I hesitated. There are things you learn when you're broke that people with money never understand — how much of yourself lives inside a bag of rice, how dignity gets measured in kilos. But her hand wasn't shaking and mine was, slightly, from three days of short rations, and we both knew what that meant.

I opened the pack. She took inventory without touching anything: rice, lentils, a brick of tea, salt pork wrapped in wax paper, hardtack, a tin of condensed milk I'd been saving like jewelry. She took the salt pork, half the rice, half the lentils, and — after a pause that felt almost merciful — left the milk.

"Why?" I asked. "If you're enforcing a closure, arrest me. If you're not, why take from me?"

She looked at me for a long moment. Rain drummed the tin. When she spoke, her voice had changed register — lower, stripped of the ranger cadence.

"Because everything anyone carries up this mountain gets taken eventually. The river takes it, or the slope does, or the government does when they finally send someone. Better it goes to someone who'll use it." She turned her cup in her hands. "Better you learn now, with half a bag, than at the top with all of it."

"That's not an answer."

"No," she agreed. "It's a warning."

* * *

She let me stay the night — not kindness, she clarified, but sense; nobody crosses wet rope in the dark. I lay on the floor boards with my pack strap looped around my wrist like a child holding a mother's finger, listening to the river, and she sat up with the pencil chart, entering the day's readings by lamplight.

"Twelve years ago," I said into the dark, testing. "You were here? When it happened?"

The pencil stopped.

"My village was downstream," she said. "Kesselford Reach. Eleven meters of water in four minutes." She said the numbers the way other people say prayers — worn smooth from handling. "The dam upstream failed. Official report said illegal logging destabilized the slope. Said the villagers ignored warnings. Said it was, quote, a foreseeable consequence of local negligence."

"But?"

"But I was eleven years old, and I had helped my father carry those logs." Her voice didn't rise. That was the frightening part. "They blamed us for our own dead. Forty-one people. They blamed a village of farmers for the engineering failure of a dam built by the same ministry that wrote the report." She set the pencil down, aligned it parallel to the chart's edge. "So yes. I'm here. Someone should be where the truth starts, keeping records, since everyone downstream is paid to lose them."

I thought of Voss dying at dawn with my hand in his grip, making me promise things I hadn't understood yet. I thought of the map rolled against my spine.

"What do you know about the headwaters?" I asked. "About what's up there?"

"Nothing," she said, too fast.

"Sefa."

"Nothing," she repeated, "that I'd say to Aldous Dunmore's child."

The name hit like cold water. I sat up. "My father told you about me?"

"He talked. Men alone on a mountain talk." She banked the lamp low. "He came through here eight months ago with survey stakes and a permit he wouldn't show me. He asked about the upper gorge. Asked if anyone still went to the source." Her eyes found mine in the dark. "I told him the mountain keeps what it takes. He laughed. Said taking was the whole point of science."

I lay back down and stared at the tin roof, where rain shadows crawled like writing.

"He's ahead of me," I said. "On the trail. Those prints."

"Deep heel, right foot dragging a little?" She shrugged. "Could be. Could be his crew. He has money now — new money, money that smells wrong. Bought gear like a man buying forgiveness."

I said nothing. What was there to say — that the money might trace back through Brannic to my own creditors, that my father might be climbing my father's mountain on loans taken out against my name? That shame travels faster than mail?

Sleep came late and thin.

* * *

At gray dawn she walked me to the rope. The fog had lifted enough to show the far bank, a green wall rising into cloud, and the drop beneath the crossing was long enough that the river looked calm from up there, which is the cruelest trick water knows.

"Harness?" she asked.

"Don't have one."

"Of course you don't." She clipped her own carabiner to the trolley and showed me the brake hand position, brisk, impersonal, but her hands checked my grip twice anyway. "Weight forward. Don't look down until midspan — then look down once, all the way, so it stops being a mystery. Fear loves mystery."

"Is that from the report?"

"That's from living."

I stepped onto the trolley and pushed off, and the gorge opened beneath me, and the wind coming upriver smelled of snowmelt and old stone. Halfway across I looked down, all the way, like she'd said. The water churned white over drowned shapes — and for one heartbeat, one impossible clear-edged heartbeat, I saw geometry down there. Squares. Lines too straight for nature. Stonework under the flood, vast and patient, wearing the river like a blanket.

Then the spray took it and it was gone, and I was pulling myself hand over hand toward the far bank with my heart slamming and my mind refusing to file what it had seen.

Sefa waited until I'd anchored the trolley on her side — my side now — and shouted across the gap, her voice carrying clean over the water:

"Dunmore! Whatever you're looking for up there — decide *now* whether you want to find it!"

I wanted to ask what she meant. She was already turning back to her hut, already reaching for her pencil, already resuming the eleven-year vigil of a woman who believed the mountain kept accounts and intended, someday, to audit them.

I stood on the far bank with half a bag of food and a map against my spine and the ghost of drowned masonry burning behind my eyes.

Ahead, pressed fresh into the mud of the trail, a boot print with a deep heel and a slight drag on the right.

Not a day old.

I followed it.
