## 6. Footprints Not Their Own

Paloma smelled the camp before they saw it — woodsmoke and canvas and something chemical, the sharp solvent tang of conservation spray, of people who treated old things as inventory.

They had been climbing since first light, following the river's roar through a corridor of wet granite, and for three hours the only tracks on the trail had been their own. Then the gorge narrowed, the water dropped away below a shelf of stone, and the trail opened onto a flat bench above the current where someone had made themselves comfortable.

A fire ring. Neatly stacked kindling under an oilcloth. A tent pitched with the taut geometry of people who did this professionally, stakes driven at perfect angles, guy lines dressed like rigging. Two more tents beyond it. A latrine trench dug downwind and screened with cut boughs.

Nobody camps like that in a closed reserve by accident.

Paloma stood at the edge of the bench with the greasecloth map folded against their ribs and felt their stomach drop through the rock beneath them. They counted the tents twice. Three. They counted the fire rings. One, but large — built to serve a crew. The wood was split, not gathered. Someone had brought axes. Someone had brought a saw.

And then they saw the stakes.

They were driven into the earth in a line along the bench's edge, orange-capped survey markers spaced at even intervals, each one stamped with a number stenciled in black. Paloma crossed to the nearest one slowly, the way you approach a dog you don't know, and read the tag wired to its cap.

*Hartwell University. Department of Archaeology. Property of the Kessler Headwaters Survey.*

The letters swam. Paloma blinked and read them again, as if the second reading might produce different words.

Hartwell. Hartwell University. The crest beneath the lettering was a shield split by a river bend, and Paloma knew that crest the way you know a scar — from the letterhead on childhood homework, from the enamel pin their father wore at every dig, from the coffee mugs that had survived the divorce like refugees and sat in their mother's cupboard for years afterward, chipped and stubborn, refusing to leave.

Their father's seal. Their father's department. Their father's survey.

Paloma crouched beside the stake and put two fingers on the ground around it. The soil was turned, dark, still damp at the bottom of the print. Driven within the last day. Maybe less. The fire ring held ash that crumbled gray and fine when Paloma touched it — cold now, but not long cold. They pressed their palm flat against the stones and felt nothing, and somehow that was worse than warmth. Warmth would have meant they could still catch him. Cold meant he was ahead. Ahead, and moving, and pulling the whole machinery of Hartwell University up the mountain behind him like a sled.

"You're already here," Paloma said aloud, and the gorge took the words and threw them back off the far wall, thinner, smaller.

They stood up too fast and the world tilted. They hadn't eaten enough. Sefa had taken half their food at the rope crossing — *the mountain keeps what it takes*, she'd said, and Paloma had paid without argument because she'd earned it, because her eyes had that landslide look, the look of someone who had watched water take everything once and expected it to try again — and now the arithmetic of the trail was tightening around them the way it always tightened. Fewer days of food than days of climbing. The monsoon somewhere out over the coast, gathering itself like a debt coming due.

Paloma walked the length of the camp because they couldn't help it. They told themselves it was reconnaissance. It wasn't. It was archaeology of the worst kind — digging through the leavings of a life, looking for evidence of what the digger had felt.

In the fire ring: a tin of tea, half empty. Brand name cheap. Their father drank good tea, always had, bought it loose from the same shop in the university district for thirty years. So either he'd changed or this camp wasn't his. But the stakes said otherwise. The stakes said Hartwell.

Near the latrine screen, half-buried in the churned mud, a boot heel had printed deep and clean, and beside it a smaller print, lighter, the tread worn smooth on the outside edge — the exact wear pattern Paloma had spent their childhood staring at across dinner tables, across dig sites, across the last terrible kitchen of the house on Corben Street. Their father walked on the outside of his heels. Had always. Would always. Some part of Paloma's body recognized that print before their mind caught up, the way a hand flinches from a stove, and they had to stand very still and breathe until the recognition stopped feeling like a blow.

He was here. He had slept here. He had boiled his cheap tea on these stones and driven his university's stakes into this mountain and moved on upstream, toward the source, toward the city, toward everything Voss had drawn in grease pencil on a scrap of cloth that now weighed more against Paloma's ribs than any map should weigh.

Toward everything Paloma was carrying to him whether they wanted to or not.

Because that was the thought, wasn't it. That was the thought that had been circling since the rope crossing, since the falls, since the first night alone in the gorge with the river roaring its one note below: *the map points where he's going.* Voss had traded it for a promise — carry it upstream before the monsoon — and Paloma had said yes to a dying man because saying yes to a dying man is the easiest promise in the world to make and the hardest to carry. But every step upstream was also a step toward Aldous Dunmore. Every landmark on the greasecloth was a landmark on whatever survey chart was rolled up in his tent. They weren't racing each other to the source.

They were converging.

Paloma sat down on a survey crate left behind near the fire ring — abandoned, or forgotten, or simply not worth carrying — and put their head in their hands.

Turn back.

The thought arrived with real force, wearing reason's clothes. Turn back. You have half rations and a bad shoulder and a river rising behind you. He has funding, permits — forged ones, maybe, but paper all the same — a crew, equipment, a head start. What exactly is the plan? Walk into his camp and say what? *Hello, Father. I've brought you a dead man's map.* Hand over the one thing that makes you matter in this story? Deliver yourself, finally, into his expedition like a parcel?

And underneath that voice, quieter, older, the other one:

You left. You chose to leave. There was a door and you walked through it and you didn't look back because looking back was the one thing you couldn't afford. And now you're twenty years of walking downstream from that door and the trail has bent around and every step you take goes home.

Paloma looked at the stakes. Orange caps, black stencils, the little shield with the river bend. Eleven of them along the bench, and more, probably, up the trail. A line of small flags marking a claim on a mountain that had already killed eleven people once and kept them.

"The mountain keeps what it takes," Paloma said, to nobody, to Sefa, to the air. "So what does it want with us?"

No answer. Just the river, roaring its single syllable below the shelf.

They got up. They shouldered their pack. And then — because spite had carried them through worse than this, because spite had bought back a compass from a pawnshop an hour after pawning it, because spite was the only inheritance their family had ever passed down intact — they took out their knife and pulled the first stake from the ground.

It came free with a sucking sound. Paloma held it a moment, the orange cap bright and obscene against the gray stone, and then drove it back in crooked, shallow, wrong. A marker pointing nowhere. Then the next one. Then the next. By the time they finished, the survey line looked like something the mountain itself had shrugged off — tilted, scattered, stamped-over with boot prints that matched no record anyone would keep.

It was vandalism. It was petty. It accomplished nothing that a crew with spare stakes couldn't fix in ten minutes.

It felt wonderful.

Then Paloma climbed.

* * *

The dream came the way it always came — not gradually but all at once, the way floodwater comes around a bend.

The kitchen on Corben Street. Evening. The light going amber and then gone, so that by the end none of them could see each other's faces clearly, which was maybe why the fight lasted as long as it did. Words are easier to say to shapes.

Paloma was nineteen. They knew this in the dream the way you know things in dreams, absolutely and uselessly. Nineteen, with an acceptance letter in their bag — not to Hartwell, never to Hartwell, anywhere but Hartwell — and their father standing at the counter with his back turned, making tea he wasn't drinking, the kettle screaming and him not moving to take it off.

"Say it again," Aldous Dunmore said. "Say it to my face."

"You already know what I think."

"I want to hear it. I've earned hearing it."

"You've earned—" Paloma heard their own voice crack and hated it, hated being nineteen, hated the way fury and grief sounded identical at that age, identical at every age. "You dug up a burial ground and called it a rescue excavation. You put eleven workers on a terrace you'd been warned about and when it went you wrote the report yourself so the blame landed everywhere except your own name. Don't tell me what you've earned."

The kettle screamed. In the dream it never stopped screaming; it just became part of the air, part of the amber light, part of the shape of him at the counter with his shoulders set like a man braced against weather.

"That terrace failed because the slope was unstable," he said. "I testified to that. Under oath."

"You testified to what kept your funding."

"And what would you have done?" He turned, finally, and in the dream his face was the face from the photographs, younger, the face Paloma had loved without complication, and that was the cruelest thing the dream did. "Walked away? Left the site to the looters? I saved what could be saved. That's the work. That's always been the work. You don't get to stand in a dry room and judge the people in the water."

"There were warnings. There were *letters*, Dad. Sefa's village sent letters—"

"Don't." His voice changed. Something moved behind it, some door opening onto a room Paloma had never been allowed to see. "Don't say that name."

"Why not? Is it in the report? Is she in the report?"

"She was a child."

"She was a witness."

"She was a *child*—" and then the dream did what the dream always did, which was skip, the way damaged film skips, jumping past the middle of the fight to the end of it, to the front door open on the dark street, to their mother's hand on the frame, to their father saying the sentence that ended everything, the sentence Paloma had carried for fifteen years and could never repeat awake because repeating it would make it true in a way memory alone refused to confirm:

*"You were never mine. You were always hers. Go."*

Go. So Paloma had gone.

In the dream they stood on the porch of the house on Corben Street with their bag over their shoulder and the acceptance letter inside it and the streetlights coming on one by one down the block like a row of small cowardly suns, and behind them, in the kitchen, the kettle was still screaming, and their father was still standing at the counter, and neither of them ever turned around, and the dream held there, held, held—

Paloma woke with the compass chain cutting into their neck and rain starting to fall, fat slow drops through the canopy, the mountain's first cough before the real storm.

They lay in the dark under the tarp and listened to the river. Louder than yesterday. Noticeably louder. The water had come up overnight; they could hear it in the new depth of the roar, the way you can hear a crowd go quiet before bad news.

Fifteen years, and the fight was still running in them like groundwater, surfacing wherever the ground got thin. On the trail today it had surfaced as rage — pulling stakes, defacing property, spiting a ghost. Tonight it surfaced as the question they'd been outrunning since Voss died holding their hand:

*What if he was right?*

Not about the report. Not about the blame. About the work. *You don't get to stand in a dry room and judge the people in the water.* Paloma had spent fifteen years judging from dry rooms — apartments, hostels, the pawnshop, the ferry, every borrowed interior of a borrowed life — and now here they were in the water at last, actually in it, and the view from inside was not the view they'd promised themselves at nineteen. From inside, the work looked enormous. From inside, the temptation looked survivable. From inside, a person could understand how a man might save what could be saved and call the rest cost.

Paloma sat up under the tarp, dripping, and ate the last of the morning's ration in the dark because tomorrow-morning-them had no more claim to it than tonight-them did.

Ahead lay the upper gorge. Ahead lay whatever camp came next, closer, fresher, warmer. Ahead lay the moment — no longer hypothetical, now merely postponed — when the trail bent and there he was, real, aging, walking on the outside of his heels, and Paloma would have to decide in one breath what the map in their shirt was worth and what the truth about Voss was worth and what they themselves were worth, delivered or withheld.

The rain thickened. Somewhere above the falls, thunder rolled down the gorge like furniture moving in a room overhead.

Paloma checked the compass by feel, its needle finding north with the small faithful insistence that had outlasted everything else in the family. North. Upstream. Toward him.

They pulled the tarp tighter and did not sleep again, and in the gray hour before dawn they heard it — faint, carried down the current from somewhere above the falls:

Voices. More than one. And the flat report of a hammer driving stakes into stone.
