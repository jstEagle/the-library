## 7. Sefa's Story

The rain came in the afternoon, the way it always did in the shoulder season — not the monsoon yet, just a rehearsal of it, a gray curtain drawn down the gorge until the river below Sefa's station went from green to brown to something that sounded like a crowd.

She sat on the porch of the ranger hut with a tin mug of tea going cold in her hands and watched Paloma Dunmore pick their way up the switchback from the rope crossing. The stranger moved like someone who'd learned to walk carefully late in life. Weight low, eyes on the footing, but the head coming up every few seconds to check the treeline — not for danger, exactly. For company.

Sefa had let them pass at the toll that morning because there was nothing else to do with them. Turn them back and they'd only find another way in; the reserve was a colander of old trails and dead regulations. Take their food — she'd taken half, rice and dried fish and a block of sugar — and they'd either leave or keep climbing hungry. She'd watched them stand at the crossing with their pack half-open, doing the arithmetic, and she'd known which way it would go before they'd finished counting. People who came up here were past the point where hunger decided anything.

But now the rain had caught them, and the hut was the only roof on this side of the gorge, and so the question she'd been avoiding all day walked up her own stairs and stood dripping under her eave.

"Ranger." The stranger pushed back a hood gone soft and dark with water. Younger than Sefa had guessed at the crossing. Tired in a way that wasn't about the trail. "I can camp below the—"

"It's raining sideways and you're going to die of stupid on my mountain." Sefa stood. "Inside."

Paloma hesitated — pride, Sefa thought, recognizing it the way you recognize weather — then came in.

* * *

Sefa made them take the dry blanket and the spot near the stove without asking twice, because asking twice invited argument and she didn't have the patience. She put the kettle on. Outside, the rain worked at the tin roof like fingers working at a knot.

"You're going upstream," Paloma said. It wasn't a question either. They'd seen her checking the storm gauges, marking the river board. "Past the falls."

"I'm going where I'm posted."

"Nobody posts anyone above the falls. There's no station up there. There's nothing up there but—" Paloma stopped. Recalculated. "You know what's up there."

Sefa poured the tea. Two cups, because her mother had raised her to do that even for trespassers, and especially for them.

"I grew up downriver," she said. "Miren Hollow. You won't find it on maps anymore."

Paloma took the cup. Their hands were cracked at the knuckles, rope-burned at the palms. Hands that had been soft once, Sefa thought. A desk somewhere. Books.

"There was a dam," Sefa said. "Upstream of the village. Small thing, government-built, meant to hold back the spring melt so the lowland farms got an even flow instead of a flood every April. Eleven meters of concrete. My father helped pour it. He used to take me up there when I was six, seven, and we'd stand on the crest and he'd say, *this is what it looks like when people agree on something.*"

The stove ticked. Rain hammered.

"The night it failed, I was nine. April, early spring melt, more snowpack than anyone could remember. The sirens went off at eleven-something at night and my mother got me and my brother out with one bag between us. We climbed the east ridge — everyone did, there was a drill twice a year, we knew the route like our own teeth. We stood up there in the dark and listened to the valley fill."

Paloma had gone very still.

"Four hundred and six people," Sefa said. "That's the number they finally settled on. It moved around for years — two hundred, then three hundred, then they found the school. Four hundred and six. Miren Hollow, plus the farms along the bend, plus the road crew camped at the bridge. My father was on the dam crest when it went. He'd gone up with the maintenance crew because the gauges had been reading wrong for three days and nobody from the district would come look. He called it in four times. We found his last report later, in the inquiry files. *Seepage at the north abutment, discharge exceeding seasonal maximum, request immediate inspection.* Dated the morning of the day it failed."

"And the report blamed—"

"The victims." Sefa's voice didn't change. She'd said this so many times it had worn smooth, like river stone. "Unauthorized settlement downstream of a flood-control structure. Failure to evacuate on the first siren. Building codes. Land use. They wrote forty pages about how the people who died had chosen to live where they lived, and one appendix — one — noting that the dam's spillway had been undersized by thirty percent against the original engineering recommendation, and that the concrete core samples showed aggregate contamination consistent with cost-cutting during construction. One appendix. No names attached."

She drank her tea. It had gone bitter and she drank it anyway.

"I spent my twenties being angry at the right things in the wrong order. Then I applied for the reserve posting when they reopened the corridor for limited ranger service, and everyone thought I was crazy — the girl from Miren Hollow going back up the valley that ate her village. But it wasn't the valley that ate it. It was men who signed things. And I wanted to be the person standing on the ridge when the next siren goes off, except this time I'd be the one pulling it."

Paloma looked into their cup. When they spoke, their voice was careful. "You said the terraces. Earlier. At the crossing. You said the old city's terraces were sabotaged."

* * *

Sefa set her cup down. This was the part she told almost no one, because the part about the dam made people sad, and sadness was safe, and the part she was about to tell made people uncomfortable, and discomfort had consequences.

"When the reserve was closed — after the dam, when the whole upper valley was condemned and depopulated — there was a survey. Before your time, probably. A university team, licensed to assess whether the drowned terraces in the lower gorge had any heritage value worth salvaging before the flood zone was formalized. Pre-colonial stonework, agricultural terracing, maybe settlement. It was a big deal in the journals for a season. Then the team came down, filed a report saying the site was marginal, degraded, not worth protection status — and the flood line was drawn, and the water came up, and that was that."

She looked at Paloma directly.

"The team lead was a man named Aldous Dunmore."

Something moved through Paloma's face — fast, controlled, swallowed. Sefa watched it go and felt the floor tilt under her own certainty, because she hadn't been sure, until this moment, how the name would land. She'd half hoped the stranger wouldn't know it at all.

"He's my father," Paloma said quietly.

The rain filled the silence.

"Then you understand why I'm telling you," Sefa said. "I've read that survey report. I have a copy — the inquiry office released the annexes after the dam case reopened, everything touching the upper watershed. Dunmore's report describes the lower terraces as *slump-damaged, structurally compromised, of negligible archaeological integrity.* But I've been a ranger on this river for six years, and in drought years, when the water drops, I can see those terraces from the overlook. And they are not slump-damaged. They are cut. Clean faces, straight lines, blast scars in a fan pattern. That is not a hillside failing. That is a hillside being broken on purpose."

Paloma's cup had stopped halfway to their mouth.

"Why would a survey team dynamite what they were sent to assess?"

"That's the question, isn't it." Sefa fed a stick into the stove. "Here's what I think. I think they found something real down there. Something big enough that if they reported it honestly, the whole valley would have been locked as a heritage site — no flood zone, no dam expansion, none of the infrastructure money that was already promised. Or the opposite: I think they found it, realized they couldn't excavate it legally or affordably, and made sure no one else could either. Either way, the terraces had to be worthless. So they became worthless. One report, one signature, and a civilization got reclassified as rubble."

"You don't know that."

"No," Sefa agreed. "I don't know it. I know what the stone shows. I know the report doesn't match the stone. Everything else is a story I've built in six years of nights in this hut, and stories built out of anger are load-bearing in ways they shouldn't be." She met Paloma's eyes. "I'll tell you the doubt too, since you're sitting in my kitchen. Sometimes I think the blast pattern means exactly what it looks like and sometimes I think six years of wanting it to mean that has taught me to see faces in clouds. The inquiry never examined the terraces. Nobody has. The water covers the evidence most of the year. I am a woman with a theory and a pair of binoculars, and I know exactly how much that's worth."

"And you want me to—" Paloma started.

"I don't want anything from you." Too fast. She heard it land wrong and didn't correct it, because correcting it would be worse. "You asked why I let you pass. That's why. Not charity. Not toll-keeping. I let you pass because you're the first person to come up this river in eleven years whose name is written in the margin of my worst suspicion, and I needed to see your face when you heard it."

Paloma set the cup down. Their hands weren't steady.

"If he did that," they said slowly, "the report, the blasting — then everything downstream of it. The flood line. The closure. The—"

"The eleven people who died in the second incident," Sefa said. "Three years after the survey. Survey crew for the flood-zone markers, went in unauthorized, bank collapsed on them at the bend. Official cause: unstable terrain. Unofficially, everyone who lives along this river knows the banks have been unstable since the terraces above them were blown apart. Water does what broken stone tells it to." She shrugged, and hated how casual the shrug sounded. "The mountain keeps what it takes. I told you that at the crossing. I wasn't being poetic."

* * *

The rain eased near midnight, dropping from a roar to a long steady whisper. Sefa banked the stove and spread her bedroll on the far side of the room, and lay listening to Paloma turn over and over in the blankets, sleep refusing them like a door with a bad latch.

Near one in the morning, Paloma spoke into the dark.

"Sefa."

"Mm."

"The drought years. When you can see the terraces. When's the next one?"

She'd already checked, the way she checked everything, the way checking was the only prayer she trusted. "The drawdown schedule. Late summer, if the authority holds to it. Six weeks, maybe seven."

"And before that?"

"Before that, the monsoon comes up the gorge in—" she calculated, listening to the river, smelling the air, the animal arithmetic of twenty years on this water, "—nine days. Ten. The high country seals. Nobody goes up, nobody comes down, the river takes the middle of the map and keeps it."

Silence again. Then: "If someone wanted proof. Photographs, samples, whatever survives the waterline. They'd have to do it before the monsoon."

"Yes."

"And after the monsoon?"

"After the monsoon," Sefa said, "there's the drawdown. And if the drawdown happens, and the terraces are visible, and nobody has documented them first — the authority will post a hazard notice, shore up the slopes with riprap, and bury my theory under ten thousand tons of quarried rock before any archaeologist in the country can file a permit." She turned onto her side, facing the dim shape across the room. "So yes. Whatever's going to be done about it gets done in the next nine days. By someone."

The shape across the room didn't answer. But Sefa heard the breath change — the specific catch of a person arriving at a decision they'd already made and only now admitting it.

She should have felt satisfied. She'd spent six years wanting an ally, wanting anyone to look at the stone and see what she saw. Instead she lay awake feeling the doubt move in her chest like a second heartbeat: *what if you're wrong? What if the blast pattern is just a hillside falling the way hillsides fall, and you've just handed a stranger with your enemy's face a reason to climb higher?*

Because that was the other thing she hadn't said. The thing under the thing.

Voss's map — she'd seen it, that morning at the crossing, the corner of greasecloth and charcoal the stranger carried like a wound — pointed upstream. Past the falls. To the source. And the source, according to every story that survived the closure, according to the one thing the dying prospector had been famous for saying in the lowland bars, was the city itself. The heart of it. Dry, intact, above the flood line.

Which meant the terraces in the drowned gorge might be only the outskirts.

Which meant Aldous Dunmore's survey report — *negligible, degraded, not worth protection* — might have been written by a man who had stood at the mouth of the source cavern and decided the world shouldn't have it.

Or by a man who never got that far, and lied about something smaller, and Sefa had spent six years sharpening him into a monster because a monster was easier to carry than a mystery.

Toward dawn she slept, badly, and dreamed of the dam crest, and her father saying *this is what it looks like when people agree on something*, and the concrete singing under her feet the way it sang the last time she ever stood on it.

She woke to gray light and an empty blanket across the room, and the smell of someone who had left before first light taking the trail upstream — toward the falls, toward the source, toward whatever the water had been holding shut for twenty years.

On the table, under her spare tin mug, Paloma had left a note in pencil, pressed hard enough to tear the paper:

*If I find anything at the terraces, you'll have copies. If I find him up there — I'll ask him. That's a promise I can actually keep.*

Below it, smaller:

*What was the name of the maintenance chief who called in the seepage?*

Sefa sat down slowly at the table. Her hands, she noticed distantly, were shaking — not with fear. With the vertigo of a woman who had aimed six years of fury at a name and just watched her only ally walk toward that name carrying her questions for her.

She got out the reserve logbook, turned to the personnel records she'd memorized years ago, and ran her finger down to the entry she'd read a hundred times.

*Maintenance crew, spring rotation: R. Voss (chief), A. Okafor, T. Ilsen...*

Her finger stopped.

*R. Voss.*

Ede Voss. The prospector. The dying man who had given a stranger a map to the source and made them promise.

Sefa stood up so fast the chair went over behind her.

He hadn't been a prospector. Or he hadn't only been one. He had been on the dam crew — chief of maintenance, the man whose reports her father had countersigned, the man who had spent twenty years walking this river with a greasecloth map, and who had waited, dying, to hand it to the child of the man who had buried the terraces.

Not a treasure map, she thought, cold spreading through her chest despite the stove's warmth. A delivery. A correction. A debt being passed hand to hand up the river until it reached the only person alive with the standing to collect it.

And it was already climbing toward the falls, alone, ahead of the monsoon, ahead of Brannic's men — because Sefa had seen their sign too, two days ago, cut stakes and boot prints and the greasy shine of machine oil on stone — and it did not know what it was carrying.

She pulled her pack from its peg and started stuffing it, fast, no longer deciding anything.

Nine days. Maybe eight.

She was going up after them.
