## 8. The Drowned Terraces

The river changed its voice an hour past Sefa's crossing.

Below the rope bridge it had been a roar, all white water and argument. Here, in a gorge the walls pinched close enough that Paloma could have spat from one side to the other, it dropped to something lower and more patient — a sound like breathing, like something enormous asleep under stone. The water ran green-black and glassy over deeps that gave back no bottom.

Paloma had been following Voss's map for two days now, and the map kept doing something maps weren't supposed to do: it kept being right. The notch in the east wall where the trail climbed through a seam of white quartz — there. The leaning pine with the lightning scar that Voss had drawn like a crooked finger pointing upstream — there, older now, half-dead, but unmistakable. Even the little annotations in Voss's cramped hand held up. *Cold spring, drink left side.* It was. Paloma drank.

Which meant the rest of it was true too. The part circled twice in faded grease-pencil at the top of the sheet, where the lines of the gorge converged on a blank space Voss had labeled only *THE OLD HOUSES.*

Paloma didn't let themselves think about that yet. Thinking about it made their hands do things they couldn't afford — shake, or reach for the compass at their neck, or calculate how many days the monsoon had left before it came down the coast like a debt collector. Four, maybe five. Maybe less. The air had gone thick and sweet in the afternoons, the way it did before weather, and the clouds stacking up over Mount Kessler's southern shoulder had that bruised, organized look that meant they weren't drifting. They were assembling.

So instead Paloma thought about small things. The weight of the food bag, lighter by half since Sefa's toll. The blister on the heel that had stopped hurting, which was worse than it hurting. The way the light came down into the gorge in narrow gold blades for maybe two hours a day and then the walls took it back.

They were thinking about none of those things, actually, when they saw the first stone.

They'd stopped to refill the water bottle at a gravel shelf where the current slackened, kneeling with one boot braced against a slick rock, and it was the angle that did it — head low, eyes level with the waterline, the sun coming flat across the surface. From above, the shelf looked like any other shelf. From down here, three feet off the water, looking along it instead of at it—

There was a line under the water. A straight line.

Rivers don't make straight lines. That was the first thing Aldous Dunmore had ever taught them, crouched beside some creek on some dig when Paloma was eight years old, drawing in the mud with a stick. *Water is honest, little bird. It goes around what's hard and over what's soft. If you ever see water doing something it shouldn't — running straight, running uphill, pooling where it has no reason to pool — you've found something somebody built.*

Paloma went very still, knee in the wet gravel, and watched the line run downstream along the submerged shelf until the murk took it. Then they stood up too fast and the world tilted, and they stood there anyway, heart going like a bird in a box.

*Somebody built.*

They waded in without deciding to. The water came up over their boots, then their knees, cold enough to squeeze the breath out of them, and they didn't care. They followed the line. Ten meters downstream it turned a corner — a right angle, cut clean as a mason's chalk mark — and rose slightly, so that a hand's width of it broke the surface: a long gray slab furred with moss, with smaller stones fitted against it in a pattern that no glacier and no flood had ever laid down. courses. Dressed blocks. A wall.

A wall, underwater, running parallel to the current for as far as Paloma could see.

"Oh," they said out loud, to nobody, because there was nobody. "Oh, you're still here."

* * *

They spent the rest of the day in the river.

It wasn't a decision so much as a surrender. Paloma stripped the pack and left it high on the bank with the map wrapped in oilcloth inside their shirt, and then they just — worked. Followed the wall downstream until it stepped down in three broad terraces, each one drowned, each one faced with fitted stone, the joints so tight that in places Paloma could still catch the ghost of a chisel mark under two centuries of silt. Followed it upstream until it climbed the gorge wall itself, terraces stacked like the ribs of something enormous lying on its side, most of each course hidden below the waterline but the uppermost edges breaking the surface here and there like the backs of sleeping animals.

A city. Not a village, not a fort — the scale was wrong for anything smaller than a city. Terraces meant agriculture, meant population, meant roads leading somewhere, meant a center. And every terrace, every wall, every cut block ran *down* into the water and kept going.

That was the thing Paloma couldn't stop circling, the thought that kept snagging like a hook in cloth. The flood zone. The government had drawn the flood line on the reserve maps after the landslide, and everything below it was legally dead ground — sealed, unexcavatable, written off. And the city didn't respect the line. The city ignored it completely. Half of it stood dry above the waterline, yes — Voss's *OLD HOUSES*, somewhere up near the source, intact, waiting. But the rest of it, the greater part, the foundations and the lower quarters and whatever streets and plazas and graves lay between these drowned terraces —

All of it was under the water. Under the dammed and managed and politically poisoned water that the government would never, ever allow anyone to drain, because draining it meant admitting what the water covered.

Which meant there was exactly one way to excavate the lower city. One way anybody with money and permits — real or forged — could ever get at it.

You'd have to move the river.

Paloma sat down on the third terrace, water streaming off them, and hugged their knees, and let the implication arrive all the way. It came slowly, the way cold comes, and then all at once.

To dig here, you'd breach the gorge. Divert the flow, drop the waterline, dynamite a channel — the valley below would take the surge. The same valley Sefa had walked out of as a child with nothing. The same valley the last flood had already killed eleven people in, while the report called it an act of God and the survivors liars.

Any excavation means drowning the valley again. The wonder curdled even as they held it. This wasn't a treasure sitting innocent in the earth, waiting for trowels. It was a trap. The city itself was the argument for destroying everything around it.

"Voss," Paloma said quietly. "What did you know?"

The prospector had carried this map for years — decades, maybe. Had traded his dying breath for a promise to carry it upstream. Carry it, he'd said. He hadn't said *publish*. Hadn't said *sell*. Hadn't said *dig*. Just: carry it to the source before the monsoon seals the high country. As if the delivering mattered more than the finding. As if the map were a letter, not a claim.

Paloma had assumed it was the raving of a dying man. Now, sitting on a drowned wall with the river breathing under them, it sounded different. It sounded like a man who knew exactly what this place was worth, and exactly what it would cost, and who wanted it delivered into the hands of someone who might understand the difference.

*Or,* said a colder voice in their head, *he wanted it found by someone who'd owe him. Everyone wants something, little bird. Your father taught you that too.*

Paloma pulled the camera from the pack.

It was a cheap field camera, shockproof, bought secondhand with money they'd needed for rent — one of the many small stupid trades that made up their life. The battery light blinked amber. Enough. They waded back out onto the lowest exposed terrace and started shooting.

Hands shaking. That was the thing they noticed, the thing that embarrassed them even alone: the hands wouldn't hold steady. They braced elbows on knees, shot the wall line, the joint detail, the chisel marks, the terrace steps descending into the green water. Shot the far bank where a second wall face mirrored the first, which meant a street or a canal between them, which meant — their mind kept adding, helpfully, ruinously — *which meant population, which means records, which means your name in the journals, which means the debts paid, which means—*

Stop.

They lowered the camera. Stood in the river with it hanging from its strap, dripping, and made themselves look at the water instead of the pictures it promised.

Under the surface, the terraces went down into the dark in perfect order. Somewhere down there were doorways. Doorways meant thresholds, and thresholds meant people had crossed them carrying water, grain, children, grief. Eleven people had died in the modern flood; how many thousands had lived their whole lives on these stones? You didn't get to weigh that against a discovery. There was no exchange rate. Any ledger that tried was Brannic's kind of arithmetic — the friendly-face arithmetic, the *everything-you-find-is-mine* arithmetic, the arithmetic that had followed Paloma their whole adult life like a tide.

And yet. And yet the photographs in the camera were worth more than Paloma's freedom. Proof of the extension below the flood line was the single most valuable image in the country right now. To the inquiry, someday, if there was ever an inquiry. To the creditors, tomorrow, if Brannic caught up. To their father, tonight, if he was already on the mountain — and the survey stakes said he was.

Paloma climbed out, wrung their shirt, and built a fire on the gravel shelf with hands that finally, grudgingly, steadied. They ate half a ration and wrapped the camera in the oilcloth with the map, and sat watching the water go dark.

The river breathed. Upstream, toward the source, toward the old houses, thunder muttered once, far off, like something clearing its throat.

Then Paloma heard the other sound.

Downstream. Below them. A regular metallic knock, faint, carried up the gorge on the evening air — *clink... clink... clink* — the unmistakable rhythm of a piton hammer, or a survey stake driver, working its way upriver toward them through the dusk.

Someone was coming. And they were driving stakes.
