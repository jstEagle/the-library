## 9. Brannic Arrives

The rain started an hour before the reserve boundary, as if the mountain had sent a doorman ahead to announce it.

Brannic watched it come down the windshield in ropes and thought about ledgers. He always thought about ledgers when the weather turned, because weather was the one creditor nobody could buy off, and he respected that in a way he respected nothing else on this earth. Everything else had a price. He'd spent twenty-two years proving it, first for banks, then for men who preferred not to be called banks, and now — sitting in the passenger seat of a borrowed flatbed while a hired driver named Okonkwo ground through second gear on a road that hadn't been maintained since the closure — for a portfolio of debts so tangled that even he needed two days with the files to explain them to himself.

Behind him, in the truck bed under a tarp, rode six diggers and four hundred kilos of equipment that would not survive a customs inspection. Ahead of him, riding shotgun in the second vehicle, sat a professor.

Brannic checked the mirror. Aldous Dunmore was talking to the diggers again. Gesturing. Hands out of the window slit, sketching terraces in the air like a man conducting an orchestra only he could hear. The diggers were nodding along because diggers nodded along; half of them couldn't understand his accent and the other half didn't care what he was saying so long as the pay cleared.

*Let him talk,* Brannic thought. *Let him draw his invisible city all the way up the mountain.*

That was the whole architecture of it, really. Beautiful in its simplicity, the way the best arrangements always were. Aldous Dunmore had spent thirty years building a reputation and fifteen years dismantling it, and what remained was a man who would work for nothing but the chance to be right. The creditors owned his funding, his permits — forged, but functional, which was all permits ever needed to be — and now, by extension, his expedition. All they lacked was the site itself. And sites, unlike men, could not be leveraged. They had to be *found*.

So you let the professor find it. You gave him his diggers, his instruments, his audience. You let him stand on the stone and say the name of the city out loud, because a discovery announced by a famous archaeologist was worth ten times one announced by a survey crew, and because a man who has just realized his life's ambition is too happy to read the fine print of his own contract.

And then, when the find was confirmed and the paperwork was drafted and the story was already running in the capital papers — *that* was when you took it. Quietly. Cleanly. The permits would prove irregular. An inquiry would follow. Someone would have to carry the blame, and there was a long tradition, well documented, of blame rolling uphill until it found a man with a famous name and no friends left. Aldous Dunmore would end this expedition the way he'd ended his marriage and his career: alone, holding something valuable that belonged to other people, wondering how it had happened.

Brannic almost felt sorry for him. Almost. It was a feeling he kept in the same drawer as mercy and receipts — things he knew existed but rarely issued.

* * *

They stopped at the boundary post at dusk.

The gate itself had been welded shut years ago — good steel, government issue, rusted now at the hinges like everything else the state had abandoned here. A sign hung from the crossbar, faded to the color of weak tea: RESERVE CLOSED BY ORDER OF THE MINISTRY. Below it, someone had nailed a smaller board, hand-painted, the letters wobbling: *ELEVEN WENT IN. NONE CAME OUT.* Brannic read it twice. He appreciated signage that did his negotiating for him.

"Cut it," he said.

Okonkwo looked at him. "It's ministry property."

"So's the road we've been driving since noon." Brannic handed him the bolt cutters without getting out of the cab. "Ministry property that nobody's inspected in six years. Cut it."

The snap of steel carried further than Brannic expected. He saw two of the diggers flinch, and he made a note of which ones. Nervous men were useful later, when you needed someone to swear they'd been ordered to do a thing. Confident men were useful earlier, when you needed someone to do it.

Aldous came up the line while the gate was still swinging, rain streaming off his hat brim, and stood beside the fallen sign reading the small board with an expression Brannic catalogued automatically. Not guilt. Not fear. Something more like *recognition* — the look of a man greeting an old argument.

"You knew about the landslide," Aldous said.

"Everyone knows about the landslide. That's why the gate's shut."

"I mean before. When your people funded me. You knew I'd need access to a closed reserve, and you arranged it anyway." He wasn't accusing. That was the thing about Dunmore that made him so easy to manage — he never accused anyone, because accusation required believing that other people's behavior was the variable. "You knew."

"I knew the mountain is open to anyone determined enough," Brannic said. "I knew the closure is a filing cabinet, not a wall. And I know that in three weeks, when the monsoon seals the high country, whatever's up there belongs to whoever's standing on it." He smiled, the one that had closed a hundred deals and cost him two teeth. "We're not breaking in, Professor. We're early."

Aldous looked at the sign a moment longer. Then he turned back toward the trucks, and Brannic watched him go, and thought: *there*. That flicker. The conscience, surfacing for air, then diving again. Every man had one. The trick wasn't drowning it — drowning anything made noise. The trick was feeding it just enough to keep it quiet.

He'd been feeding Aldous's for eight months. Grants routed through three shells. A journal editor persuaded to un-retract a paper. A letter of introduction to a minister's cousin who owed the wrong people money. Small kindnesses, each one logged in a green notebook Brannic kept in an inside pocket, each one a hook set gently in living flesh.

By the time the professor understood the shape of the net, he'd have swum too far into it to turn around without tearing himself open.

* * *

Camp went up in the dark, badly, because diggers were diggers and not rangers, and Brannic lay in his tent listening to the river and doing arithmetic.

The Kessler ran loud below them — louder than the survey maps suggested, and the survey maps were six years old, which meant six years of water doing what water did. He'd read the old reports on the flight in. Landslide, dam failure, eleven dead, valley flooded above the gorge, official finding: unauthorized settlement on unstable ground, victims' negligence cited, case closed in nine pages. He'd read it the way he read everything, looking for the seam. And there was a seam, thin as wire: page four, a single sentence noting that the failed dam had been built on foundations "of unknown origin, possibly pre-colonial masonry."

Possibly pre-colonial masonry. In a report whose every other sentence was devoted to establishing that the valley contained nothing worth knowing.

Somebody had written that sentence and then been told to stop writing. Brannic had spent two weeks finding out who, and had found a junior hydrologist, retired now, living comfortably on a pension that had doubled the year after the inquiry. The man wouldn't talk. But he'd sold, for a sum Brannic considered reasonable, a box of field notes he'd kept out of spite.

In those notes, buried in a margin, in pencil, in a hand gone shaky: *terraces cut. not natural. someone cut them.*

Someone had sabotaged the old terraces above the valley. Someone with survey experience. Someone on the original team.

And the original team had been led by a rising young archaeologist named Aldous Dunmore.

Brannic lay in the dark and let himself enjoy it, briefly, the way a man enjoys a good hand of cards before playing it. Because this was the true keystone, better than the forged permits, better than the debt. If the sabotage could be traced to Dunmore — and it could, the notes were suggestive enough to ruin him and vague enough that Brannic controlled exactly how far the tracing went — then the professor was not merely an instrument. He was a hostage. His name, his legacy, his last shred of standing, all of it held in a green notebook in an inside pocket.

Aldous would find the city. Aldous would announce the city. And when the time came, Aldous would sign whatever he was told to sign, because the alternative was spending his final years known as the man who drowned a village to get at its graves.

Brannic wasn't sure the sabotage claim was even true. It didn't matter. True enough to threaten was identical to true, in every market he'd ever worked.

There was one loose thread. There was always one loose thread; he'd stopped expecting otherwise around his fortieth birthday. Voss. Ede Voss, prospector, occasional informant, occasional thief, a man who'd sold Brannic rumors for years and then, three months ago, gone silent — and then, two weeks ago, a port-town pawnbroker mentioned a stranger asking after Kessler headwaters, carrying a greasecloth map, paying with a dead man's compass.

Voss had drawn a map. Voss had given it away. Which meant Voss had died, or was dying, and had chosen — for reasons Brannic genuinely could not model, and this bothered him more than he admitted — to hand the mountain's front door to a stranger rather than sell it.

The stranger was presumably already on the trail. One person, alone, unpaid, unrecorded. A rounding error. But Brannic had built his career on rounding errors, on the small accounts everyone else wrote off, and he did not write this one off. He added it to the ledger: *one unknown, upstream, in possession of Voss's map.*

If the stranger reached the source first, it changed nothing material. Brannic had diggers, guns, documents, and time. But it changed the *story*, and stories were the only currency that appreciated. A discovery claimed by a lone heir of a dying prospector was a romance. A discovery claimed by a funded expedition was a transaction. He preferred romances to be strangled in their sleep.

Tomorrow he'd send Okonkwo and two of the steadier diggers up-trail to sweep for camps. Quietly. No stakes planted yet, no seal of the university displayed — let the professor believe this leg was reconnaissance. What the sweepers found, they'd bring back to Brannic before anyone else heard of it.

Especially before Aldous heard of it. If the stranger on the trail was who the pawnbroker's description suggested — young, dark-haired, answering to a name that had made the professor go very still when Brannic mentioned it once, casually, over brandy, watching — then that particular card was too valuable to play early.

Paloma Dunmore. He'd enjoyed saying the name. He'd enjoyed the half-second where Aldous's face did something complicated and then reassembled itself into scholarly indifference, badly.

Family reunions, Brannic believed, were best attended by surprise.

* * *

Rain again before dawn, hard, drumming the tarp lines. Brannic rose, dressed, and walked the camp perimeter with his hands in his pockets, checking his investments.

The diggers were digging already — test pits near the riverbank, Aldous directing them with a feverish energy that would burn out in a week if Brannic didn't ration it. Fine. Let him spend the first week on the wrong ground. Hope needed exercise.

At the camp's edge, Aldous stood alone facing the gorge, compass in hand — a battered brass thing, older than the expedition, older than the estrangement. Brannic had done his research. He knew the compass had once belonged to the child. He knew the professor carried it the way other men carried photographs.

"Good morning, Professor," Brannic said, coming up beside him. "Sleep well?"

"Well enough." Aldous didn't lower the compass. "The bearing's wrong."

"Wrong?"

"The river bends north below the falls. Every account agrees. But the water sound—" He tapped his ear. "It's coming from higher. Further east than it should. As if the channel's changed since the flood." He lowered the compass at last and looked at Brannic, and for a moment the fever in him banked into something colder and sharper, the mind underneath the obsession, the mind that had once been genuinely great. "Six years ago this valley drowned. Whatever the water covered, it also *moved*. My maps are out of date, Mr. Brannic. Everyone's maps are out of date."

"Then we'll make new ones," Brannic said pleasantly.

But he filed it. The channel had shifted. Which meant the source might sit differently than any map described — Voss's map included — and the only person guaranteed to know the new ground was whoever had walked it most recently.

Whoever was upstream right now, ahead of them, alone.

Brannic turned back to camp and raised two fingers. Okonkwo detached from the pit crews and came over, wiping red clay from his hands.

"Change of orders," Brannic said quietly. "Take Marek and Ilsa. Go up-trail past the rope crossing. You're looking for a camp — recent, one person. Food remains, boot prints, anything. If you find the person, you don't touch them. You watch, you count their supplies, and you come back and tell me where they're headed." He paused. "And Okonkwo. If you happen to see anything made of stone that looks older than the ministry — you say nothing to the professor. Nothing to anyone. Straight to me."

Okonkwo nodded the nod of a man being paid enough not to ask.

Brannic watched the three of them take the trail north, small figures swallowed quickly by wet green, and stood a moment longer in the rain with his ledger open in his mind, entering the morning's figures.

One professor, leveraged. Six diggers, expendable. One channel, moved. One stranger, upstream, holding a dead man's map and a head start.

The mountain kept what it took. Brannic had read that somewhere too, scrawled on some gate or grave marker, and he'd smiled at it, because it was the kind of thing people said when they hadn't yet met him.

He went to wake the professor. They had a city to find, and Brannic intended to be standing directly behind him when they did.
