## 10. Father and Child

The rain came in through the canopy in pieces, not sheets — a slow sifting, like the mountain was deciding how much of it Paloma deserved.

They had been climbing since first light, following the river's noise up through the gorge, and somewhere past the second switchback the trail narrowed to a ledge of wet stone with the water roaring thirty feet below. Paloma kept their eyes on their boots. The compass hung at their neck under the jacket, and every so often they touched it through the fabric, the way you'd touch a bruise to check it still hurt.

That was when they heard voices ahead. Not one voice — several, low and workmanlike, the sound of people who expected to be where they were.

Paloma stepped off the trail into the ferns and crouched, heart going hard, and watched four men come around the bend carrying survey equipment on their shoulders. Tripods. Stakes. The sealed aluminum cases that meant money, real money, the kind Paloma hadn't stood next to in years. They passed close enough that Paloma could smell wet canvas and cigarette smoke, and then they were gone down the trail, heading back toward the lower gorge, toward the camp with the stakes bearing the university seal.

And behind them, walking alone at his own pace, came Aldous Dunmore.

He was older. That was the first thing — stupidly, the first thing. His hair had gone from iron to ash, and he'd thickened through the shoulders the way men do when they stop walking anywhere and then start again all at once. He wore the same field coat, though. The same one, patched at the same elbow, and Paloma felt that detail land somewhere beneath the ribs like a dropped stone.

He hadn't seen them yet. He stopped at the ledge's edge, took out a pair of battered binoculars, and studied the far wall of the gorge with the particular patience of a man who has looked at stone his whole life and never once gotten bored of it.

Paloma should have stayed in the ferns. Every practical instinct said stay hidden, let him pass, keep the map dry against your skin and your face out of his sightline. But there is a specific gravity to a parent standing twenty feet away for the first time in six years, and Paloma stood up before they had decided to.

Aldous lowered the binoculars slowly, the way he did everything, and looked at them.

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The river filled the silence the way water fills anything — completely, without effort.

"You're off the marked trails," he said finally. His voice was exactly as Paloma remembered it: low, unhurried, the voice of a man reading an inscription. "This reserve is closed."

"So I've heard." Paloma's own voice came out steadier than they felt. "I hear the closure's official reason was a landslide. Eleven dead. I hear the real reason was embarrassment."

Something moved behind his eyes — not guilt, Paloma thought. Recognition. The look of a man hearing an argument he'd already had with himself and lost.

"Paloma." He said it like a measurement. Like he was confirming a reading. Then he put the binoculars away, carefully, in their case, and Paloma understood that whatever happened next would happen deliberately, because Aldous Dunmore did nothing otherwise. "You're thinner. Are you eating?"

"I'm eating."

"The coat's new."

"It isn't."

"No," he agreed quietly. "It isn't."

The rain sifted down between them. Paloma became aware of the map against their sternum, greasecloth and secret, and of how little separated this moment from a mugging. All he would have to do was ask what they were doing here. All they would have to do was lie well.

"What are you doing here?" he asked.

And there it was. Paloma had rehearsed answers for six years — in doorways, on buses, in the gray hour before dawn in rented rooms — and every one of them dissolved now, the way ink dissolves in the kind of rain this mountain makes. Because the true answer was a dying man's promise and a debt and a name they wanted back, and none of those words could survive being said aloud to this man, on this ledge, in this weather.

"Looking for something," Paloma said.

"Archaeology?" And there it was — the faintest lift at the corner of his mouth, not quite a smile, the expression he'd worn at every dig of Paloma's childhood when they found something before he did. Pride, wearing its disguise. "You always were. You used to find things I'd walked over three times. That sherd at Tember's Ford — you were nine."

"I remember."

"I know you do." He said it gently, and gently was worse. Gently was leverage. "You were good, Paloma. You are good. It's in the hands. You can't teach reading ground and you can't unteach it."

"Don't."

"I haven't said anything yet."

"You've said plenty." Paloma's hands had closed into fists inside their sleeves. "You don't get to do this. You don't get to stand here and be nostalgic at me. Six years, Dad. Six years, and you open with Tember's Ford."

His jaw tightened — the only crack. He looked out at the river for a moment, and when he turned back his face had been reassembled, smooth and reasonable, the face he'd worn across negotiation tables and lecture halls and, once, across a kitchen table while everything ended.

"All right," he said. "Straight, then. You want straight?"

"I want you to keep walking."

"I'm offering you a job."

The words landed wrong-footed; Paloma actually swayed half a degree, and hated it. "What?"

"A position on the expedition. Paid. Funded. Proper equipment, proper food — you look like you need both. Field lead, if you want it. There's no one whose read on terrain I trust more, and I'm not saying that to flatter you, I'm saying it because it's true and I've spent six years saying less true things to less important people." He took a breath, and for one unguarded instant he looked his age, and tired, and like a father. "Come with me. Work with me. Whatever happened—"

"Whatever happened."

"—can be worked through in the field. That's where things got worked through before. That's where we were best."

* * *

The worst part, later, was that Paloma could see the architecture of it. Not cruelty — Aldous didn't build in cruelty, he built in necessity. He'd seen them on the trail, alone, thin, off-permit in a closed reserve, and he had run the arithmetic the way he ran all arithmetic, and concluded that the correct move was to fold his estranged child back into his expedition before someone else's expedition found them and asked questions. Love, expressed entirely as leverage. He probably even believed it was love. That was the thing about Aldous Dunmore: he could make any calculation feel like a kindness from the inside.

And the second-worst part was how much of Paloma wanted to say yes.

Not for the money — or not only for the money, though the money sat in their chest like a warm coal, paid field lead, debts gone, Brannic's creditors' faces going slack. But for the tent light at night and the two cups of terrible coffee and the voice reading stratigraphy aloud like scripture. For being, once more, someone's good right hand instead of everyone's bad debtor. For the version of their life where the fight had never happened and the door had never closed and the compass around their neck was just an inheritance instead of a wound.

"Say something," Aldous said.

"Why now?" Paloma's voice cracked on it and they let it. "Six years of nothing. Not a letter. Not a wire when I — you knew I was drowning. Everyone knew. And now you're on a mountain with other people's money and suddenly I'm field lead?"

He flinched. Actually flinched, small, quickly controlled. "I wrote letters."

"They're still in the drawer. Unopened. I know, I checked last winter, drunk, feeling sentimental." A laugh came out of Paloma that wasn't one. "You wrote letters because writing costs nothing. Showing up costs something. You showed up today by accident."

"That isn't fair."

"No," Paloma said. "It isn't. Nothing about us is fair. That's sort of our whole thing."

The rain picked up. Down the trail, faint through the trees, came the clink of the survey crew staking something, the small bright sounds of a future being measured out.

"Who funded you?" Paloma asked, and watched him decide whether to answer.

"A consortium," he said at last. "Cultural recovery interests. Legitimate."

"Legitimate." Paloma tasted the word. "Dad. Look at me. Do I look like someone who wandered up a closed mountain by accident? Do I look like I have no idea who 'cultural recovery interests' are?"

Something passed across his face then that Paloma had never seen on it before, and it took a moment to identify because it had no business being there: fear. Quick, suppressed, gone. "What do you know?"

"Less than you. More than you'd like." They pulled the jacket tighter against the rain, buying seconds, hating that every second bought was a second closer to either lying or confessing. "Why does it matter? You're offering me a job. Jobs don't require disclosure."

"Paloma—"

"There's something under this valley." They said it before they could stop themselves, and immediately wanted it back, but it was out, hanging in the wet air between them, and they watched it hit him like current hits a wire. His whole body oriented toward it. Six years of estrangement and the man still couldn't help himself; the work was the work was the work. "There's something under this valley and you know it, and whoever's paying you knows it, and I think — I think if you find it the way they want it found, this whole gorge drowns again. And I think some part of you already knows that too."

The ledge seemed to narrow. Below them the river roared on, indifferent, patient, carrying everything downhill.

"That's a serious accusation," Aldous said quietly.

"I make serious accusations. You made me. Remember? Verify everything. Trust the record, not the man." Paloma's throat hurt. "You taught me that. It's the only thing you ever gave me that didn't come with conditions."

He stood very still. Rain ran off the brim of his hat in a thin unbroken line. When he finally spoke, his voice had gone somewhere Paloma didn't recognize — lower, stripped of the lecture-hall polish, almost young.

"If I told you I had terms," he said, "written terms, protective terms, written into my contract — that no charge goes near the flood line, that excavation is manual above the waterline or not at all — would you believe me?"

"No."

"Honest."

"I learned from the record, not the man."

He almost smiled at that. It was the saddest thing Paloma had ever seen him do. "Then there's nothing I can say on this ledge that changes anything."

"No."

"And you won't come down with me."

"No."

He nodded slowly, absorbing it, filing it — she could see him filing it, the great cataloguer, entering his child under some new heading. Then he reached into his coat, and Paloma's hand went to the knife before their brain caught up, but what he brought out was a tin. Old, dented, familiar enough to hurt: the ration tin he'd carried on every dig of their childhood, the one that had held biscuits and, once, a birthday candle because they'd been in the field on their twelfth birthday and he'd refused to let it pass unmarked.

He set it on a flat stone between them. Didn't offer it. Just left it, the way you leave food out for something wild you want to encourage.

"Biscuits," he said. "They're stale. They're always stale."

"Dad—"

"Field lead position stands. Indefinitely. If you change your mind, come down to the lower camp. Anyone can tell you where." He picked up his pack, settled it on his shoulders with the practiced roll of decades. "And Paloma. Whatever you're looking for up here—" he paused, and for a moment the mask slipped all the way, and underneath it was just a tired old man standing in the rain looking at the last thing he loved that he hadn't managed to lose, "—I hope it's worth more to you than what it costs."

Then he turned and went down the trail, unhurried, deliberate, and the green swallowed him the way it had swallowed the survey crew, and Paloma stood alone on the ledge with the river roaring below and the tin of stale biscuits sitting on the stone like a peace treaty nobody had signed.

* * *

They didn't touch the tin for a long time.

They stood there until the cold worked through the coat, until their fingers stopped obeying properly, doing the arithmetic they'd been refusing to do since he'd first lowered the binoculars. He was between them and the source now — no, ahead of them toward it, or possibly not, possibly his grid squares lay along some other arm of the valley, but it didn't matter, because the map against Paloma's chest pointed up and his money pointed everywhere, and sooner or later those two vectors were going to meet at the same stone.

And Brannic. The name surfaced now, unwelcome, inevitable. The friendly face from the port town, the generous offer, the ledger behind the eyes. If Brannic's money was Aldous's funding — and Paloma would bet the compass it was — then the enforcer already knew a Dunmore was on the mountain. Maybe two.

They opened the tin eventually. The biscuits were stale, exactly as promised, tasting of tin and years, and Paloma ate two of them sitting on the wet stone with their boots dangling over the roar, because throwing them away would have meant something, and eating them meant nothing, and they were so tired of everything meaning something.

Halfway through the third biscuit, they heard it.

Not the river. Not the rain. Under both — a sound from downstream, from the direction Aldous had gone, carried up the gorge in the gaps between gusts. Voices. Several. Moving fast, and not the workmanlike pace of the survey crew.

These men were climbing. Climbing hard, and gaining.

Paloma wrapped the rest of the biscuits in the tin, stowed it, and stood, and touched the compass at their neck once, checking the bruise.

Then they started upward, into the rain, taking the switchbacks two at a time, while below them — fainter with every turn, but never quite gone — the fast footsteps followed.
