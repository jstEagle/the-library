## 11. The Monsoon Warning

Sefa knew the storm was coming before the sky did.

It was in the river first. The Kessler ran milky at dawn, thick with silt that had no business being there yet — the meltwater from the high snowfields turning to rain instead of running clear. She crouched at the gauge station below the rope crossing and read the staff twice, because the first reading made her want to throw the notebook into the current. Forty centimeters above seasonal mean. Rising four centimeters an hour since midnight.

In her third year as a ranger she'd learned that the mountain spoke in two voices. The gentle one was birdsong and wind through the cedar terraces, the sound the tourism brochures sold to people who would never be allowed up here anyway. The other voice was water. And right now the water was saying *get out*.

She marked the reading, dated it, initialed it, and stood with her knees cracking in the cold. Downriver, maybe eleven kilometers by trail, the lower valley held three farmsteads, a footbridge, and the ruins of Old Sessamy — the village that wasn't there anymore, just foundations under the flood line and a memorial stone nobody visited. Eleven names on it. One of them was her mother's.

The government said no one lived in the reserve. That was the official position, stamped and filed: the Kessler Reserve was closed, uninhabited, unoccupied. Which meant, in the beautiful circular logic of the Ministry, that no evacuation order could ever be justified, because you cannot evacuate people who do not exist.

Sefa existed. She had spent nine years proving it, one patrol log at a time.

* * *

The radio station at the ranger hut was older than she was, a wall-mounted unit with a handset connected by a cord someone had spliced twice with electrical tape. She keyed the transmit switch and waited for the relay at Fort Merren to pick up, watching the needle drift across the dial like something dying slowly.

"Merren relay, this is Kessler Station Four, ranger Sefa Okonjo, priority weather traffic, over."

Static. Then a voice she didn't recognize, young and bored. "Kessler Four, say again your designation. There's no active station on Kessler."

"There's no active station on paper," Sefa said. "I'm reading a forty-centimeter anomaly on the main gauge with a four-per-hour rise rate. Barometer's dropped eighteen millibars since yesterday noon and the cloud deck over the summit is building east to west, which means it's coming off the sea, not off the range. That's monsoon-structure convection, early, and I want a formal advisory issued for the lower valley before nightfall. Over."

A pause long enough that she could hear the young man chewing something. "Kessler Four, the reserve is closed. There are no parties registered in the drainage. An advisory for an unoccupied zone isn't — hold on." Papers shuffling. "There's no protocol for it."

"The valley isn't unoccupied. There are three farmsteads below the closure line, and those families have radios too, but they'll listen to you before they'll listen to me, because you're the government and I'm just the woman who lives in the disaster."

"Ma'am—"

"Don't ma'am me. Log my traffic. Time-stamp it. If the valley floods and anyone drowns, the inquiry will pull these tapes, and I want your name on the record next to mine. Over."

Another pause. When he came back, his boredom had curdled into something more careful. "I'll forward your report to the district office."

"Forward it today. Not tomorrow. Today. Kessler Four out."

She hung the handset on its hook and stood looking at it, hating it, hating the whole chain of custody that turned a rising river into a memo into a folder into a drawer. She had done this dance before. Two years ago, a spring melt nearly as bad, and the district office had replied — she still had the letter — thanking her for her vigilance and noting that hydrological anomalies within the closed reserve fell outside the mandate of civil defense, as the reserve contained no civilian population.

No civilian population. Just her. Just the ghost of a village. Just, now, at least two sets of strangers walking around in the high country that the ministry insisted was empty.

* * *

She climbed to the lookout rock above the crossing to think, which was where she did most of her thinking, because the hut felt too small for decisions and the valley felt too big.

From up here she could see the gorge running north into the closed country — the walls narrowing, the river whitening, the terraced slopes of the old survey cut still visible after all these years, scars on the mountainside shaped like stairs. Her father had helped build those stairs. He'd been a laborer on the first survey team, the one that came before the dam, before the slide, before everything. He never talked about it. He'd died not talking about it, ten years back, and left her a box of papers she still hadn't finished reading because every page smelled like the day the mountain came down.

The cloud over the summit had organized itself while she watched. It had that green-black underside she'd only seen a handful of times, the color of a bruise deciding where to spread. Wind at the crossing had swung around to the northeast. All of it wrong, all of it early. Monsoon season was supposed to be three weeks out. The mountain was jumping the schedule, or the climate was, and either way the paperwork wouldn't care.

And somewhere upstream of her, past the falls, past the switchbacks, in the teeth of all of it, was the Dunmore kid.

* * *

She'd let Paloma cross three days ago. Took half their food for the toll, which was less mercy than ritual — the toll was hers to take because the mountain took things and someone had to keep its books. But she'd watched them go up the far bank with the particular walk of a person who has already decided how the story ends and is just filling in the middle. Stubborn. Underfed. Carrying grief like a packed bag they'd forgotten to set down.

Sefa knew that walk. She wore it herself most mornings.

The problem was arithmetic, and Sefa trusted arithmetic the way other people trusted prayer. Paloma had gone up with five days of food, maybe six. The source was two hard days' climb from the crossing in good weather. This was not good weather. Coming down would take longer than going up, because coming down meant fighting the river instead of following it, and if the front hit while they were in the narrows—

She stopped herself. Counted breaths. Made herself finish the calculation anyway, because futility was no excuse for imprecision.

If the front hit in thirty-six hours, and it would hit in thirty-six hours or fewer, then anyone above the falls needed to be below the washout line inside two days. Paloma didn't know the washout lines. Paloma didn't know that the second switchback lost its outer edge every spring, or that the slot canyon above the ferryman's pool filled to the walls in under twenty minutes when the headwaters spiked. Paloma knew a map drawn by a dying man and a promise, and neither of those would hold back water.

The radio couldn't reach them. There was no tower above the crossing; the terrain ate signals alive. She'd tried once, years ago, during a fire season, and gotten nothing but her own echo.

Which left her legs.

* * *

Back at the hut, she pulled the duty log and wrote the entry she'd been avoiding:

*1400 hours. Gauge anomaly continuing, 4 cm/hr sustained. Convective buildup NE quadrant, barometric fall accelerating. Advisory requested from Merren relay 0930; forwarded to district office per standard (nonstandard) channel. No acknowledgment received. Given closure status of reserve and confirmed presence of at least one unauthorized party upstream (see log, three days prior), initiating solo ascent toward upper gorge to warn and extract. Expected duration: two days up, conditions permitting. Station left secured.*

She read it twice. It sounded insane, written down. A single ranger climbing into a closed disaster zone ahead of a monsoon to fetch one stranger she'd known for ten minutes. Any review board would call it reckless. Any review board would also be sitting in an office forty kilometers from the water, so she signed it and put the pen down.

Then she packed.

The packing was its own kind of argument with herself. Rope — yes, sixty meters, though it doubled her weight. The good med kit, not the patrol kit. Food for four days, which meant eating light and lying to herself about it. The flare gun, three shells. Her father's old rain cape, oilcloth gone stiff as bark, because new gear was for people whose fathers hadn't died owing them explanations.

Last, from the locked drawer, the box of his papers. She didn't take it. She never took it. But she opened it, the way she always did before going up, and looked at the top sheet: a survey sketch of the upper gorge, drawn in a draftsman's careful hand, with a note in the margin she'd read a hundred times and understood maybe twice.

*The terraces will not hold what we're asking them to hold. Recommend full stop. — R.O., field day 41.*

R.O. Ravel Okonjo. Her father, telling the truth to men who didn't want it, six weeks before the dam failed and the report blamed the dead.

She closed the box. Locked the drawer. Stood in the doorway of the hut with her pack cutting grooves into her shoulders and looked downriver, where the valley lay green and oblivious under a sky that was still, for another few hours, pretending to be innocent.

Three farmsteads. She keyed the radio one more time and raised each of them in turn — the Adeyemis, the Cortas, old Marta at the footbridge — and told them the same thing in the same flat voice: river's rising wrong, storm coming early, move what you can't lose to the high shelf tonight, don't wait for an official word because none is coming. They thanked her. They always thanked her. Whether they moved was between them and the mountain.

That left the fourth party. The one that didn't exist on any list. The one walking around up there with a dead prospector's map and a stranger's stubbornness, heading straight for the throat of the drainage.

* * *

She started up at dusk, because the last light would show her the water level at the narrows and she wanted that number in her bones before dark.

The trail along the east bank was slick with the day's seep. She moved fast without hurrying — the difference mattered; hurry broke ankles, and a broken ankle up here was just a slower way of drowning. At the narrows she stopped and played her torch across the flood debris on the banks: seed pods and sticks caught in a ragged line a full meter above the current flow. Last year's high-water mark, or the year before. The river was climbing toward its own history, patient as a debt collector.

Somewhere above her, thunder rolled — not the sharp crack of local lightning but a long, structural grumble, the sound of a very large system organizing itself over the summit. The air tasted metallic. Her father used to say you could smell a big rain three valleys away, and she'd thought it was superstition until the day she smelled it and the big rain came.

She clipped her lamp to its highest setting and kept climbing.

An hour past the narrows, she found the first sign: a boot print in the clay of the trail, fresh, edges still sharp, pressed deep by someone carrying weight. Not Paloma's tread — she'd memorized that one at the crossing, the slight drag of the left heel. This print was broader, heavier, a working boot with a squared toe. Someone else was on the mountain. More than one someone, by the spacing of the prints, moving up in a loose file like people who'd walked together a long time.

Behind her, the river answered the thunder. She turned and saw it in the torchlight: the Kessler had come up another hand's width since the narrows, and it was no longer milky. It was brown. It was carrying soil from slopes that should have been holding snow.

Sefa stood in the wet dark with the prints pointing uphill and the storm pointing downhill and herself pointed both directions at once, and understood, with the clean clarity that only ever arrived when it was least useful, that whatever happened in the next two days, the official record would never contain it. The ministry would deny anyone was here. The inquiry, if there was an inquiry, would start from the fiction of an empty reserve. Everything she did from this step forward would happen off the books, unwitnessed, unwritten — unless she survived to write it herself.

Fine. She'd survived worse with less.

She turned uphill, set her boots in the stranger's prints because they were already broken ground, and started to climb.

Above her, the mountain swallowed the trail. Below her, the river began to sing the only song it knew, getting louder with every step, and the first fat drops of the early monsoon came down through the canopy and struck the clay like small dark fists knocking at a door.

Nobody home, the government said.

Sefa climbed.
