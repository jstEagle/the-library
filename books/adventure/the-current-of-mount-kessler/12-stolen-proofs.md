## 12. Stolen Proofs

Paloma woke to the wrong kind of silence.

The river was still there — it never stopped, a constant cold thunder through the gorge floor — but the small sounds were gone. The tick of cooling embers. The drip of condensation off the tarp line. The rustle of something small and bold working through the food cache. All of it absent, as if the night had been holding its breath.

They opened their eyes to a sky gone the color of wet slate and a fire pit scraped clean.

Not rained out. Not collapsed. *Cleaned.* Someone had taken the coals apart and scattered them, then kicked river gravel over the black. That wasn't an animal. Animals didn't tidy up after themselves.

Paloma sat up too fast and the world tilted, hunger and altitude trading places behind their eyes. The tarp was still strung. Their bedroll was still there. Their pack lay where they'd left it, propped against the root of a lightning-dead pine — but it sat wrong, leaning at an angle no careful person would have left it in, and the flap hung open two fingers wider than they'd cinched it before sleep.

They went through it with hands that wouldn't stay steady.

Stove: still there. Rope: still there. The tin cup, the spare socks, Sefa's dried fish wrapped in oilcloth — all present, all accounted for, all *arranged*, which was somehow worse than missing. Whoever had gone through the pack hadn't been searching for anything. They'd known exactly what they wanted.

The map case was gone.

The photographs were gone.

Paloma stood very still in the gray light and listened to their own pulse. Two days of work. Two days of wading the flooded margin below the drowned terraces with numb legs, bracing the camera against stones, waiting for gaps in the current to show the stonework beneath — the walls running down into the green water like the ribs of something enormous sleeping under a blanket. Proof that the city didn't end at the flood line. Proof that any excavation meant dynamiting a valley full of water above villages that had already been drowned once.

And Voss's map. Ede Voss's greasecloth, soft as skin from forty years of handling, drawn by a man who'd traded his dying breath for a promise.

Gone. Both. In one night, while Paloma slept twenty feet away.

*You're getting slow,* they thought. *Or you're getting tired, and tired looks exactly the same.*

They crouched at the edge of camp and found the tracks almost immediately, because whoever it was hadn't tried to hide them. Boot prints in the mud beside the stream — deep at the heel, shallow at the toe, the gait of someone carrying weight and moving fast anyway. A scuff where they'd cut across the gravel bar. Then nothing, because the ground turned to stone going upriver.

Toward the upper gorge. Toward the falls. Toward the source.

Paloma crouched over the prints for a long moment, doing arithmetic they didn't like. The boot was large — size thirteen at least, maybe fourteen — and the stride was long. A big man, strong, moving with purpose. One set of tracks. Alone, then. Or alone on this stretch.

One of Brannic's diggers. It had to be. Brannic's crew had come up the valley behind Aldous's survey stakes, and Brannic didn't send anyone anywhere without a reason. Which meant Brannic knew about the terraces. Knew about the photographs. Knew that Paloma had documented the one thing that could stop him — or, worse, the one thing he could use.

Because here was the thought that turned Paloma's stomach colder than the river spray: those photographs proved the city extended below the flood line. To a government inquiry, that was an argument against excavation. To a man like Brannic, it was a *survey*. He didn't need to find the site anymore. Paloma had mapped its edges for him, photographed the stonework, done the work of a dozen hired technicians — and left it all in an unguarded tent.

*I handed him the discovery,* Paloma thought. *I gift-wrapped it.*

* * *

They inventoried what was left with the flat efficiency of someone counting coins before a payment they already know will fall short. Food first, because food was time. Three days of fish if they ate thin. A handful of oats. Half a bar of the dense dark chocolate Sefa had pressed on them at the rope crossing with the words *the mountain keeps what it takes, so eat while you can.* Water was free; everything else cost.

Three days. And the monsoon was early this year — Sefa had said so, her face grim as she read the clouds stacking up over the western ridgeline two nights ago. *When it comes, it comes all at once. The gorge becomes a pipe. Nothing in it stays.*

Two options, and both of them bad.

Chase. Follow the tracks upriver toward the falls, catch the thief, take back the map and the film. But the thief had a head start measured in hours, a body built for carrying loads over broken ground, and probably a camp somewhere ahead with friends in it. And Paloma hadn't eaten properly in two days. Chasing meant gambling everything on catching a stronger man on his own terrain.

Or climb. Forget the thief, forget the map, go straight up the switchbacks toward the source and get there first. Find the site again on their own — Voss had described it well enough that Paloma could follow the drawing in their head even without the greasecloth in hand. Reach the cavern mouth, stand in the first chamber, and be there when Brannic arrived, with whatever proof they could make on-site.

But without the photographs, the drowned terraces existed only in Paloma's memory. Without the map, Voss's promise was just a story a dying man told a stranger. And if the thief reached Brannic first — if Brannic got the map and the film and moved on the site before anyone could stand between him and the charges —

Paloma sat on a wet stone and put their face in their hands.

This was the moment, they realized. Not the summit, not the confrontation, not whatever waited at the cavern mouth. This moment, sitting hungry on a rock with everything stolen, was where the trip would actually be decided. Every story their father ever loved had a hinge like this — the point where the protagonist chose, not between good and evil, but between the smart loss and the stupid fight. Aldous Dunmore had built a career on always choosing the smart loss. Always the calculated retreat, the strategic silence, the paper published before the truth could catch up to it.

Paloma looked at the boot prints heading upriver.

Then they ate. Half the remaining fish, forced down mechanically, and the last of the oats cooked into a paste they swallowed without tasting. If this was a gamble, they weren't going to lose it on an empty stomach. They broke camp in nine minutes flat — faster than they'd ever managed, faster than the army manuals their father used to quiz them on around the fire, *come on, Paloma, faster, the site won't wait* — and stood at the edge of the gravel bar with the compass out.

It swung, settled, pointed north-northeast, up the gorge. Toward the falls. Toward the thief. Toward the source, which happened to be the same direction, because of course it was. Everything in this valley funneled to the same place. That was the whole nature of a watershed. Everything flowed down except the people who insisted on going up.

"Okay," Paloma said aloud, to the compass, to Voss, to nobody. "Both."

Both, because there was a version of the climb that was also the chase. The switchback trail and the riverside track converged at the falls — Sefa had said so, marking it on the air with a finger: *all paths meet at the water.* If the thief was following the low route, Paloma could take the high trail, move parallel, and cut down to the falls ahead of him. Let him carry the load up the hard way while Paloma traveled light and steep. It was the kind of plan that worked in stories and failed in life about sixty percent of the time.

But the other forty percent had a name, and the name was *the only chance there is.*

* * *

The high trail was less a trail than a rumor of one — a shelf of broken limestone stitched to the gorge wall, threading up through scrub pine and fog. Paloma climbed it the way you climb anything desperate: hand over hand, three points of contact, thinking only about the next hold and never about the drop, because the drop was a hundred meters of white water and thinking about it was a way of falling.

The fog came and went. When it lifted, Paloma could see the river below, swollen now, brown-green and muscular, hauling whole trees along like a man carrying firewood who has forgotten he's tired. The rain hadn't started yet. But the air had a pressure to it, a held-breath quality, and the birds had gone quiet in a way that Sefa would have read instantly and that Paloma read only as wrongness, a wordless alarm bell ringing in some part of the brain older than language.

Twice they stopped to look down at the riverside track, and twice they saw what they needed to see: a figure, far below, small with distance, moving upriver with a heavy pack and a long, grinding stride. Still on the low route. Still slower than Paloma, now, because gravity was charging Paloma a different fee — vertigo and burning lungs instead of a fifty-kilo load — and Paloma was paying it gladly.

At midday the fog closed in completely and stayed, and Paloma climbed inside a gray room five meters wide, following the shelf by feel and by the cairns some earlier traveler — Voss? one of the old survey teams? — had stacked at the turns. At one of these cairns they stopped to breathe and noticed the top stone was dark. Wet. Not with fog.

Blood, maybe. Or mud. Impossible to tell, and no time to care.

It was on the third day's worth of climbing compressed into six hours that the washout appeared.

The shelf simply ended. Twenty meters of it had let go sometime in the last season — maybe the last week — shearing off the gorge wall and leaving a gap filled with loose rubble that ran at an angle of repose straight down into the river. Beyond the gap, the shelf continued, visible through the drifting fog like a promised land. Between here and there: a slope of stone shards balanced on nothing, ticking faintly in the wind as if counting down.

Paloma stood at the edge and felt the fear arrive whole, the way it does when the body understands before the mind consents. One slip here and the river would have them before they finished falling. No rope long enough. No second attempt. The mountain keeping what it takes.

They took out the compass.

Not for direction. For the needle. They held it flat against the slope of the rubble and watched the needle settle, and used it the way their father had taught them to use it thirty years ago on a hillside in another country, kneeling in the dirt with small warm hands over his large cold ones: *not to find north, little bird. To find level. North is where you're going. Level is whether you live.*

A bubble level in a brass housing. The old compass had one, and Paloma had never once used it, had mocked it silently for years as a relic of a man who trusted instruments more than people. Now they knelt at the lip of the washout and laid the compass on the largest flat stone they could reach and watched the bubble slide hard to the downhill edge, and began reading the rubble the way you read a sentence: this stone stable, that one floating, this line of shards a seam where the whole slope might let go at once.

They crossed it in twelve minutes that felt like a year. Weight on the big blocks, never the small ones. Three points of contact until the last four meters, where there was no fourth point to be had and they committed — a lunge, a scrabble, a heartbeat of pure free-floating terror — and then fingers closed on living root at the far edge and they hauled themselves onto the intact shelf and lay there gasping, cheek against wet stone, alive.

The compass was still in their hand. They hadn't dropped it. Some part of them had kept it clenched through all of it, the way a child holds a hand in a crowd.

"Fine," Paloma said to it, hoarse. "You win."

* * *

They came down to the falls in late afternoon, descending a side gully with the sound growing from thunder to a physical pressure, a drumming in the chest cavity. The fog thinned. And there it was: the falls, taller than they'd imagined, the whole river gathering itself and pouring over a lip of dark stone into a churning pool, spray rising in columns that caught the last gray light.

And on the near bank, hunched over a fire that smoked too much because the wood was wet, a big man with a heavy pack, feeding sticks to the flames.

The thief. Ahead of schedule, or Paloma behind theirs — either way, the forty percent had paid out. The man hadn't seen them yet. The spray and the roar covered everything.

Paloma sank behind a boulder and made themselves look, really look, past the anger. One man. Big. The pack at his feet — Paloma's pack, the oilcloth-wrapped shape unmistakable even at distance — sat beside him, and he wasn't guarding it. He was guarding himself, hunched, rubbing his shoulder, moving like a man whose body was failing him faster than his errand. And the fire was lit in the open, careless, the smoke standing up like a signal flag.

He wasn't trying to hide. He was trying to be *found.*

Which meant either he was bait, or he was waiting for someone, or — and this landed in Paloma's stomach like a dropped stone — he'd been told to wait here, at the falls, at the place where all paths met, because the people coming up from below would arrive here eventually, and the map and the photographs were meant to change hands at this exact spot.

Brannic didn't need the proofs delivered upriver. He needed them passed back down.

Paloma pressed against the cold boulder and did the last of the arithmetic. The man between them and the source. The map in his pack. Behind him, somewhere above the falls, the cavern mouth Voss had drawn, dry and untouched, with thunder rolling closer by the hour. Below, somewhere on the low trail, whoever the man was waiting for.

And cutting down the opposite bank, small with distance but unmistakable, moving with the tireless efficient stride of someone who had grown up in these mountains and intended to die in them on her own terms —

Sefa.

She'd seen the smoke too.

Paloma looked at the compass in their hand. It pointed north-northeast, up the gorge, past the fire, past the falls, toward the source. It had never once pointed at the easy thing.

They started down the gully.
