## 13. The Switchback Climb

The rain started an hour before dawn and did not stop.

It came down in sheets that turned the gorge into a single instrument, the river below playing its one note louder and louder until it wasn't a sound anymore but a pressure, a hand against the chest. Paloma climbed through it with their hood cinched so tight they could hear their own breath bouncing back at them, wet and ragged, like someone else panting just behind their shoulder.

The trail left the river here. That was the map's mercy — Voss had drawn the switchbacks in a tight zigzag up the eastern wall of the gorge, forty-one of them, he'd written in his cramped hand, *count them when you despair.* Paloma had counted eleven by first light. They were already despairing.

The stone underfoot was slick as soap. Not rock anymore, really — a skin of black algae over rock, grown fat on the spray that came off the falls somewhere above. Every step was a negotiation. Paloma had learned to walk like an old man, weight low, palms out, testing each hold before committing to it, and still they'd gone down hard twice in the last hour, once onto a knee that now bled through the trouser leg in a way they were trying very hard not to think about.

*Food short and time shorter.* That was the arithmetic now. One day of rations left, maybe a day and a half if they stretched it, and stretching meant weakness, and weakness on this wall meant falling. The thief — Brannic's man, almost certainly, though Paloma still couldn't make the tracks resolve into anything certain — had taken the photographs and the map and left the stove fuel, which told Paloma something about what he valued, and something about how little he understood what he'd stolen. The map was the only copy. Voss had drawn it once, in greasecloth ink that didn't run, because he'd known exactly what kind of country he was sending it into.

Paloma could have gone back. Down was easier than up; down was always easier than up. There was a rope crossing two days behind them, Sefa's toll, half a food bag lighter than they'd arrived. There was the port town, the creditors, the slow grinding shame of a life measured in payment schedules. Going back was not death. It just felt like it would be.

They kept climbing.

* * *

By midmorning the rain had found a rhythm and Paloma had found a hatred for it that felt almost holy. Not the dramatic hatred of a person in a storm story — no lightning, no wind worth naming — but the flat, patient hatred of someone being worn away grain by grain. Their fingers had gone white at the tips an hour ago and hadn't come back. Their boots squelched with every step. Somewhere around switchback twenty-three they stopped counting and started bargaining instead: *to the next bend, then rest. To the next bend.*

At the next bend there was no trail.

There was a gap.

Paloma stood at the edge of it with the rain running off their chin and felt their stomach drop through the floor of the world. The washout had taken thirty feet of the switchback entire — a slab of the mountainside, trail and all, sheared loose and gone into the gorge. Below the gap the cliff face dropped away in a series of ledges and slabs, green-black with algae, down and down to where the river boiled white around boulders the size of houses. The far side of the gap was right there. Ten feet across, maybe twelve. A jump, in dry weather, for someone with sure feet and nothing to lose.

In this rain, with these legs, carrying this pack? It was a joke. It was the kind of thing that got written up afterward in other people's reports, the way the eleven dead of the landslide had been written up — *failed to exercise appropriate caution,* or whatever the report had said. Sefa had told them. Blamed the victims. The mountain keeps what it takes, she'd said, and Paloma had thought it was a warning about greed.

It was a warning about everything.

They stood there a long time. Long enough for the cold to get into the standing still, which is how cold kills you — not dramatically, but by making you want to stop moving, and then making stopping feel reasonable. Paloma's hands shook. Some of it was fear. Most of it, honestly, was hunger and wet and the simple mechanical failure of a body running on too little for too long.

They took the pack off. Sat down on the wet stone with their back to the wall, legs dangling over the gap, and ate the last of the dried fish — half a ration, eaten early, a theft from tomorrow committed without ceremony. While they chewed, they looked at the problem the way their father had taught them to look at problems, back when looking at things together had been the whole shape of their love: *don't stare at the obstacle. Stare at the edges of it. Obstacles are never as solid as their edges.*

The edges. On the near side, the trail ended in a tangle of root — alder, by the look of it, thick as a wrist, anchored deep in the cut bank. On the far side, the trail resumed at a shelf of intact bedrock with a crack running along it, wide enough to see into. Between them: air, spray, and the drop.

Aldous Dunmore would have rigged an anchor. He'd have had four points of contact and a belay and a graduate student to test it. Paloma had a coil of cord, sixty feet of it, and a compass on a lanyard around their neck.

They laughed. It came out wrong, a single bark with no joy in it, startling them both.

The compass. His compass. Brass-cased, glass-cracked, the needle that had pointed at every dig site of their childhood while he held it and said *north is a promise, little bird, everything else is negotiation.* Paloma had pawned it and bought it back out of spite and carried it since as a wound you could hold — proof of him, proof of the leaving, proof of everything unsaid between them. An heirloom. An accusation.

But brass cases float. And a compass on a lanyard is a plumb line, and a plumb line is a pendulum, and a pendulum thrown across a gap will tell you things.

Paloma stood up. Uncoiled the cord. Tied a loop in one end — a bowline, fingers stupid with cold, tied three times before it held — and weighted the loop with a stone from the trail edge, snug in the hitch. Then they swung the compass off their neck and clipped the lanyard to the loop, so the whole assembly was stone, line, brass, line, stone. A messenger. A ferry for a thread.

*You're going to hate this,* they said aloud, to him, to the rain, to nobody. *I'm using your north to get across.*

They threw it underhand, a long lazy arc, the way you throw a line to a drowning swimmer. First cast fell short into the void and the drop swallowed it with a sound like a swallowed word. Paloma hauled it back, dripping, heart slamming. Second cast hit the far wall and slid off the algae with a squeal. Third cast —

Third cast the stone sailed clean over the lip of the far shelf and the line went taut across the gap and the compass dangled in space between them, swinging, needle steady, pointing north into the rain as if none of this were remarkable. As if it had done this sort of thing all its life instead of sitting in pawnshop drawers and museum cases and the pockets of men who used it to find cities.

Paloma fed the rest of the cord after it, shaking the coil out, watching the slack pile into the gap and then pull straight as the weighted end dropped down the far face. When the line hung vertical against the far wall, they pulled it gently sideways until the stone snagged in the crack — felt it catch, felt it hold, tested it with two careful pounds of force, then five.

Then they anchored their end around the alder roots, tied off, and stood at the edge of the gap with sixty feet of cord strung between them and the continuation of their own life.

Hand over hand. That was the plan. Hand over hand, leaning back, feet on nothing, over a drop that ended in white water. People died doing this on purpose with proper equipment. Paloma had a clothesline's worth of cord and a body that had already failed twice today.

They went anyway.

The first three pulls were fine. The fourth pull, the anchor stone shifted in the crack — a grating shudder transmitted down the line and into Paloma's arms like a sentence being read aloud — and for one full second the whole system was in question, stone and cord and grip and gut, and Paloma hung over the drop feeling the mountain consider them.

Then the stone settled deeper. Held.

Paloma moved. Pull, swing, pull, swing, the rhythm of it taking over from thought, arms burning, the river roaring up at them like an audience that wanted them to fail. Halfway across the cord sagged and their boots touched the wall and they walked themselves along it, sideways, tiny steps, the algae smearing under their soles, until the far lip rose to meet their hands and they hauled themselves over it and lay flat on the bedrock and breathed.

For a while they didn't move at all. Just lay there with the rain hitting their back and their heart going like a trapped bird, alive, absurdly, stupidly alive, on the far side of a gap that had every right to have kept them.

After a while they untied the line, coiled it, and retrieved the compass from where it hung against the stone. The needle pointed north. Of course it did. It always had. It had never once cared what Paloma thought about the man who gave it to them.

They hung it back around their neck. But it sat differently now. Lighter. Less like a debt and more like a tool — something that did what it was made for, without sentiment, without asking to be forgiven for anything.

*Count them when you despair,* Voss had written. Paloma started counting again from the gap. Switchback twenty-four.

* * *

The afternoon was worse, which Paloma would not have believed possible at noon.

The rain thickened toward true monsoon rain — not falling so much as arriving, a horizontal freight of water that turned the trail into a stream and the stream into a rumor and the gorge below into something that no longer sounded like a river at all. It sounded like a train. Like a hundred trains, in a tunnel, forever. Paloma caught glimpses of it through the vegetation at the switchback corners and each glimpse was worse than the last: brown now, not white, heavy with the mountain washing itself away, carrying whole trees past at a speed that made them look like matchsticks moving in fast-forward.

The high country was sealing. Voss had said it plainly: *before monsoon season seals the high country.* Paloma had imagined a door closing. This was not a door. This was the building coming down.

Somewhere above the thirtieth switchback the trail crossed a scree chute — a funnel of loose stone where the mountain shed its skin every winter — and the chute was running. Water sheeted across it ankle-deep, moving stones the size of fists, and beyond it the trail continued along a ledge barely wider than Paloma's boot.

They crossed the chute in the gaps between surges, reading the water the way Sefa had taught them to read the river without meaning to — *watch what it carries, it will tell you what it intends* — and made the far side soaked to the thigh and shaking so hard the ledge seemed to vibrate under them.

That was when the body filed its formal complaint.

It came on gradually and then all at once: the trembling turning from shiver to something structural, the legs developing a fine uncontrollable stutter, the vision narrowing at the edges into a soft grey vignette. Hunger, cold, exertion, fear — the four horsemen, riding together, and Paloma sitting on a wet ledge at thirty-five hundred feet with nothing left to give them.

They sat down. Deliberately, the way you do when sitting down might become not getting up. Put their back against the wall, pulled the hood tight, and let the rain hammer them while they took inventory with the cold honesty of a merchant facing bankruptcy.

One day of food: spent. Cord: fifty feet of it, shortened by the gap. Fuel: enough for one hot drink, maybe. Compass: functional. Body: failing. Will:

The will was the strange one. Everything else was running out and the will was somehow not. Paloma searched inside themselves for the exhaustion that should have been terminal by now and found instead something obstinate and wordless, a thing with no argument attached, no reason, no speech — just a direction. Upstream. The same direction the river came from. The same direction Voss had pointed with his dying hand.

*Carry it upstream.* He hadn't said *find the city.* He hadn't said *save yourself.* He'd said carry it, and Paloma had promised, and the promise was doing the work now that the muscles couldn't. It was the strangest fuel Paloma had ever burned and it did not run out. It just hurt.

They lit the stove under an overhang, cupped in their jacket against the wind, and made one cup of hot water with the last of the sugar scraped from the bottom of the bag. Drank it slow. Let it lie in their stomach like a coal. Then packed the stove away and stood up, and the standing up took three tries, and on the third try it worked, and that was enough.

Switchback thirty-eight. Thirty-nine. Forty.

At forty the rain changed again — softened, suddenly, into a fine drifting mist, as though the storm had paused to look at what it had done. Through the mist, above and to the left, Paloma heard water falling from a great height: the falls, the ones on the map, the landmark Voss had circled twice. And below the sound of the falls, fainter, threaded through it, another sound that stopped Paloma's breath.

Voices.

Distant, indistinct, carried up the gorge on the shifting wind — more than one voice, voices with the flat cadence of men working, calling numbers to each other. Somewhere ahead, between Paloma and the source, people were moving cargo. Rigging lines. Working in the rain the way hired men work, without love, on a schedule.

Brannic's crew. Or Aldous's expedition. Or both, braided together, upstream of everything, doing whatever it was that money did when it reached a mountain.

Paloma stood on the fortieth switchback with their body in ruins and their promise intact and listened to the voices come and go on the wind. The mist drifted. The river roared. The compass hung at their throat, pointing north, pointing nowhere, pointing wherever they chose to go.

Forty-one. The last switchback. The trail leveled out onto a shoulder of the mountain, and through the thinning mist Paloma saw it: the falls, white and immense, pouring off a shelf of dark stone. And beside the falls, half-glimpsed, a shape that didn't belong to any geology Paloma knew — a regularity, a line, a suggestion of cut blocks under centuries of moss.

And between Paloma and that shape, on the trail ahead, a campfire smoke thinning in the rain, and figures moving around it.

One of them turned. Looked up the trail. And began to walk toward Paloma through the mist, unhurried, as if he had all the time in the world — and Paloma knew the stride before they could see the face, knew it from a thousand childhood dig sites, knew it the way you know a scar.

The figure walking toward them out of the rain was their father.
