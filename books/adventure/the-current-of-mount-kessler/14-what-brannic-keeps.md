## 14. What Brannic Keeps

The rain had a rhythm to it that Brannic found almost restful. Not the port-town drizzle that got into your ledger and warped the paper — this was mountain rain, honest and heavy, drumming on the tarp he'd strung between two pines at the lower camp. It drowned out the diggers' complaints. It drowned out Aldous Dunmore's humming, which was worse.

Brannic sat under the tarp with the map spread flat on his knee and a copyist's pen in his hand, and he thought about how everything valuable in the world came down to the same three operations: acquire, copy, destroy.

He'd learned it young, working collections for a man in Corvane who bought dead people's estates before the families finished grieving. The man had a saying: *An original is just a liability with sentimental value.* Brannic had been twenty-two when he first understood what that meant in practice. He was forty-one now, and the greasecloth map of the Kessler headwaters lay under his fingers like a confession waiting to be edited.

It was good work, whoever had drawn it. Ede Voss, according to the scrawl in the corner — a name Brannic half-remembered from claim registries, a prospector who'd spent thirty years chasing ore bodies up the Kessler and finding nothing anyone would pay for. Nothing but this. A river traced in careful ink from its mouth to its source, with annotations in a cramped, patient hand: *flood line 1812. flood line 1904. stone below waterline here — old work, not colonial.* And above the last falls, a cavern mouth drawn in section, ringed with hatching that any surveyor would read as masonry.

Voss had spent a lifetime finding it and then given it away to a stranger for a promise. That was the part Brannic couldn't stop turning over. He understood men who sold things. He understood men who stole them. But a dying man trading the only thing he owned for a promise from someone whose name he'd learned an hour earlier — that was a kind of arithmetic Brannic had never made balance.

He didn't need to understand it. He just needed the map.

He worked by lamplight, copying onto waterproofed paper he'd brought for exactly this purpose. He was careful with the annotations, careful with the elevations, careful even with Voss's little marginal sketches — the notch in the ridge, the fallen pine used as a bridge marker. A copy was only useful if it was trusted. Later, if it ever needed to be distrusted, there were ways.

Behind him, across the camp, Aldous Dunmore stood at the edge of the tarp's shelter looking up into the rain as though the mountain might answer him. Sixty-one years old and he still stood like a man waiting for the ground to open and show him something. Brannic had watched him for eleven days now — watched him read the gorge the way other men read scripture, watched him touch cut stone with both hands like a priest handling relics — and Brannic had revised his estimate twice. First he'd taken Dunmore for a hired brain, decent credentials, no spine. Then, watching him work, he'd upgraded him: genuinely brilliant, genuinely obsessed, which made him more useful and more dangerous in equal measure.

Now, after today, Brannic had settled on the final assessment. Aldous Dunmore was a hostage who didn't yet know he'd been taken.

That was fine. Most people lived their whole lives that way.

* * *

The copy took four hours. When it was done, Brannic held the original up to the lamp one last time — the greasecloth soft as skin, the ink gone brown with age, Voss's name in the corner like a signature on a death warrant — and then he fed it into the camp stove.

Greasecloth doesn't burn clean. It burned dirty, blue-edged flames licking up through the wire grate, and the smell was thick and animal, like burning fat. Brannic watched every centimeter of it go. The annotations curled first, then the river itself, the blue line of the Kessler blackening and lifting off the cloth in flakes of ash that rose and died against the underside of the tarp.

Somewhere behind him, a boot scraped on wet ground.

"You're burning it." Aldous's voice. Not alarmed — puzzled, the tone of a man watching someone do something inefficient. "That's the primary document. Provenance matters. If this site is what you think it is, the discovery narrative will be examined line by line, and—"

"The discovery narrative," Brannic said, without turning around, "is whatever we say it is."

He let the last corner go and closed the stove door. When he turned, Aldous was standing three meters away with rain running off his hood, and his face had the particular stillness of a man recalculating something large.

"Sit down, Professor," Brannic said. "Have a drink. I want to show you some paperwork."

Aldous didn't sit. He never sat when Brannic asked; it was the one small rebellion available to him and he exercised it daily. He stood instead, arms folded, water dripping from his sleeves onto the groundboards, and waited. Brannic respected that, in the way he respected a lock that took longer to pick. It didn't change the outcome. It just billed him for the time.

He poured two measures of the rough grain spirit the diggers drank and set one on the crate beside Aldous anyway. Then he opened his document case and laid out three papers in a row, squaring their edges, taking his time.

"Permit of entry, Kessler Reserve, issued by the Ministry of Lands," he said. "Excavation license, provisional class. And a letter of indemnity covering liability for works within the flood zone." He tapped each one. "All bearing your name. All bearing your university's seal. All of them forged."

The rain filled the silence for a while.

"I signed those permits," Aldous said slowly.

"You signed documents that looked like these. Different text underneath. You have a habit of not reading past the letterhead — I counted on it." Brannic smiled, friendly, unhurried. "The seals are excellent. I paid a man in the capital who used to make the real ones. The ministry stamps are real, actually — bought from a clerk who sells blank stamped forms by the kilo. So if anyone checks, the paperwork holds up long enough to get the equipment in. What happens after that depends entirely on how cooperative everyone is."

"Cooperative." Aldous picked up the permit. His hands were steady — Brannic noted that, filed it away; steady hands meant the fear hadn't reached the surface yet, which meant it was still down deep where it could do more work. "These are criminal instruments. If this comes to light—"

"It won't come to light," Brannic said, "because you're going to make sure it can't."

He took the permit back out of Aldous's fingers, gently, the way you'd take scissors from a child, and squared it with the others.

"Here's your situation, Professor. Your expedition is funded — salaries, permits, equipment, the helicopter charter, all of it — by a lending syndicate. You know this. What you may not know is that the syndicate's exposure on you is considerable, and that they've assigned recovery rights to me. Which means every coin spent getting you up this mountain is a coin you owe, and the collateral is the find. Whatever's up there belongs to the debt. That was in the agreement you signed. Article nine. You did read article nine?"

"I read—" Aldous stopped. Brannic watched him actually go back through it, watched the memory turn over pages, and saw the exact moment he found the clause. It was beautifully done, that clause. Buried in a paragraph about salvage rights, worded so it read like standard maritime boilerplate. *All materials recovered in the course of debt-service operations vest absolutely in the recovering party.*

"There is no 'course of debt-service operations,'" Aldous said. "This is a scholarly expedition."

Brannic laughed — genuinely, warmly, letting it roll out over the sound of the rain. "Professor. There hasn't been a scholarly expedition since the Ottomans. Everything is debt-service operations. The question is only who holds the paper."

He stepped closer. Close enough that Aldous had to tilt his chin up slightly, which Brannic knew the older man hated.

"Now. Here's what happens next, and I'll say it once. In three days, maybe four, my crew reaches the upper gorge. We locate the site — your site, your life's work, I'm not disputing that, you'll get your name on it, first author, honors, the whole ceremony. But the survey you file has to describe the ruins as beginning *above* the flood line. Only above. No submerged terraces. No stonework below the waterline. Because if the city extends into the flood zone — and we both know it does, your own son photographed it, don't look surprised, I know about the photographs — then any real excavation means diverting the river. And diverting the river means the valley floods again. And the last time this valley flooded, Professor, eleven people died, and the government sealed the reserve, and there are people downstream — a ranger named Sefa, among others — who have been waiting fifteen years for someone to prove exactly what your team did to those terraces."

Aldous had gone very still. Not the stillness of denial. The stillness of a man hearing his own name read out in a courtroom.

"That report was falsified," he said quietly. "I said so at the time. I wrote a dissent. They buried it."

"I know. I've read your dissent. It's why you're here instead of some cheaper geologist." Brannic let that land. "You wanted redemption. Everyone on this mountain wants something. The prospector wanted a promise. Your child wants — well, who knows what Paloma wants, they barely seem to know themselves. And you, Aldous, you want the one thing that would make the dissent matter retroactively. Find the city intact, above the waterline, untouched, and suddenly the tragedy becomes a footnote instead of an indictment. *Yes, the valley flooded, but look what survived.* That's the story you've been telling yourself since the helicopter. Am I close?"

Aldous's jaw worked. Rain ticked on the tarp. Down the slope, one of the diggers cursed at a stuck winch, and the sound seemed very far away.

"So you file the survey my way," Brannic went on, softly now, almost kind. "Above the flood line. Clean, contained, excavatable with hand tools and a modest budget. The syndicate takes title, develops it carefully, everyone gets rich, nobody drowns. Or—" he gathered the three papers and held them up between two fingers, "—these go to the ministry, along with an anonymous note drawing attention to certain irregularities, and to the fact that the expedition's lead archaeologist has a documented history of disputes with the department over the Kessler landslide. Forged permits in the hands of a known dissenter. How do you think that reads? How long do you think you stay out of prison? And how much do you think your creditors recover from the estate?"

"They'd take everything," Aldous said. Flatly. "There's nothing left to take. You know that. That's why you're here."

"I know that," Brannic agreed pleasantly. "Which brings us to the beautiful part, Professor, the part I want you to appreciate, because it's rare. We are each other's hostage. Perfectly, symmetrically. If you expose my forgeries, you expose yourself, and the syndicate strips your life down to the studs. If I burn you, I lose the only man alive who can read that site properly, and the find dies in the ground, and my principals eat the loss, and men like me do not survive eating losses. Neither of us can move without destroying the thing we're both standing on." He raised his glass. "So we stand very still together. Like men on ice."

Aldous looked at the spirit for a long moment. Then he picked it up and drank it all, in one motion, like medicine.

"You talk about the flood," he said. "Eleven people. You say it like a line item."

"I say it like a risk factor."

"My daughter is on this mountain."

Something moved behind Brannic's eyes — not conscience, nothing so expensive, but calculation adjusting its footing. "Your daughter is a variable," he said. "Variables can be managed. She has the map — or had it; my people are retrieving it tonight. Once she's accounted for, she's either an asset or she goes home. I'd prefer asset. Family expeditions photograph well. Funders love them."

"She will never work with you."

"Nobody ever does, at first." Brannic refilled both glasses. This time Aldous let his be poured. "But here's what I've learned about people, Professor, and I offer it free of charge: everyone has a price, and it's never money. Yours is vindication. Hers is — I'd guess belonging, but we'll see. Mine, since you're wondering, is completion. I finish what I'm contracted to finish. It's the only virtue I have and I protect it jealously."

He clinked his glass against Aldous's, uninvited.

"So. The survey. Above the flood line. Do we have an understanding?"

Aldous stared into the fire through the stove's grate, where the last grey flecks of Ede Voss's map were still sifting down among the coals. Brannic watched him watch it and understood, with the clarity that sometimes came to him in the field, that the man was already writing the false survey in his head — already choosing which elevations to round, which stones to omit, already performing the small betrayals in advance so that when the moment came they wouldn't feel new. That was how it always went. Nobody decided to fall. They just arranged the falling ahead of time and called it prudence.

"Above the flood line," Aldous said at last. His voice was level. His hands, wrapped around the glass, had finally begun to shake. "On one condition. When the work is done — the real work, the excavation — my dissent goes into the record. The landslide case reopens. Whatever it costs your principals."

Brannic considered it. It cost him nothing he valued and bought him a partner who believed he'd won something. The best concessions were the ones the other side thought they'd extracted.

"Done," he said.

They shook on it. Aldous's grip was cold and strong, a digger's grip, and he held it a beat too long, and Brannic understood that too: the man was memorizing him. Filing him under debts of a different kind. Good. Hostages should keep ledgers. It kept them hopeful, and hope kept them compliant.

Aldous turned toward his tent, then paused, rain streaming off his hood.

"The photographs," he said, without turning around. "Paloma's photographs of the submerged terraces. When your people retrieve them — what happens to them?"

"They get copied," Brannic said. "And then they get destroyed. Same as everything else."

Aldous stood in the rain a moment longer. Then he walked on, and the tent flap fell shut behind him.

Brannic stayed under the tarp and finished his drink and listened to the mountain rain coming down on the gorge, on the river, on the drowned terraces far below the waterline where the old stones held the current apart. He felt good. Clean operations were rare, and this one was nearly complete: copy made, original burned, scholar collared, witnesses being handled. By tomorrow night he'd hold the map, the photographs, the site, and the man.

He was so busy admiring the symmetry of it that he almost missed the sound — a whistle from the lower trail, two notes, the signal his forward man used for *problem.*

Then again, urgent now, cutting through the rain.

Brannic set down the glass and reached for his coat.
