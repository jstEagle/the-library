## 15. Reunion at the Falls

The tracks told me everything I needed to know and nothing I wanted to hear.

They were fresh — hours old at most, pressed deep by a man carrying more than his body wanted to carry. The stride was long on the flats, shortened where the trail steepened. A digger's boots, the kind with the steel shank in the sole that left that little crescent mark in soft mud. I'd seen a hundred of them on my father's sites when I was small enough to sit in the shade and count them.

He was heading for the falls.

I didn't have time to think about what that meant. Thinking was a luxury I'd pawned along with everything else I owned. I had half a day of food, a compass, and a body that had been running on coffee and spite since the gorge mouth. The river roared somewhere below me through the trees, louder now than it had been two days ago, and every hour it climbed another hand's width up its banks. The monsoon wasn't coming. It was already here, gathering itself over the high country like a debt collector who knew exactly where you lived.

I ran.

Not well. Not the way I'd run at twenty, before the debts and the port towns and the slow erosion of everything I'd thought I was. But I ran the way people run when stopping is worse than falling — arms pumping, lungs burning, feet finding the wet stone because they had no other choice. The trail narrowed. The gorge walls closed in around me, black basalt streaked white with mineral stain, and the air went cold and wet and tasted like stone.

I caught sight of him once, maybe forty minutes in — a flash of yellow rain shell between the trees ahead, moving fast for a man carrying stolen goods. He heard me or sensed me, because he broke into a scramble too, and then it was just the two of us racing up the spine of the mountain while the river screamed below.

The falls announced themselves first as a change in the sound — the roar splitting into something higher, sharper, layered. Then the trees fell away and there it was: the upper gorge opening into a bowl of rock, and the Kessler River pouring out of the mountainside itself, dropping thirty meters down a face of wet black stone into a churning pool that threw spray fifty feet into the air. A permanent rainbow hung in the mist, pale and ghostly, like something the mountain was dreaming.

And standing at the lip of the pool, chest heaving, rain shell plastered to his back, was the thief.

He was younger than I expected. Late twenties, maybe. Square jaw, close-cropped hair, the build of someone who dug for a living. He had my pack open at his feet and the greasecloth map in one hand, and when he saw me come out of the treeline he did the arithmetic every cornered man does — me, him, the drop, the water — and came up with the wrong answer.

"Stop," he said. "Just — stop. I don't want trouble."

"You stole from me." My voice came out ragged. "That's trouble."

"I was told to." He held the map up like a shield. "Brannic wants it. That's all. Nobody said anything about — look, I'll give it back, okay? Just let me walk out of here."

Brannic. Of course. The friendly face from chapter three of my ruin, the man who'd offered to bankroll my trip in exchange for everything I found. I'd turned him down. Apparently he hadn't taken no for an answer; he'd simply sent his dogs to fetch instead.

"The photographs," I said. "Where are they?"

His eyes flicked to the pack. That was all I needed. I moved.

Later, I would try to reconstruct those ten seconds and fail. What I remember is spray and noise and the greased slickness of the rock under my boots, and him swinging the pack at me like a weapon, and the two of us grappling at the edge of the pool while the falls thundered loud enough to swallow shouting. He was stronger than me. It wasn't close. He got a forearm across my throat and walked me backward toward the water, and I remember thinking, with a strange detached clarity, *this is how it ends, drowned at the bottom of a mountain chasing a piece of cloth.*

Then my heel found the edge, and instead of fighting the fall I gave myself to it.

We went down together into the shallows at the pool's rim, and the cold hit me like a fist. The current grabbed my legs immediately — this close to the falls the water didn't pull you downstream so much as it pulled you *in*, back toward the base of the drop, toward the churning death-spiral where the whole river landed. I got a hand on a submerged root. The thief surfaced beside me, coughing, still clutching the pack strap, and for one absurd moment we hung there in the freezing water staring at each other like two men waking from the same nightmare.

"The map," I gasped. "Give me the map and swim."

He looked past me — at the falls, at the boil of white water six feet away, at the rainbow hanging over it all like a joke — and whatever he saw in my face convinced him. He shoved the greasecloth roll into my jacket and let go of the pack, and the river took it instantly, swallowed it whole. Then he grabbed for the root above mine and hauled himself toward the bank, and I followed, both of us crawling out onto the stones like drowned animals, lying flat on our backs in the spray, breathing.

The photographs were gone. I knew it before I checked. Every image of the drowned terraces, every shot of the stonework below the flood line — the proof that any excavation meant dynamiting the valley, drowning it again, killing whoever lived downriver — all of it, gone over the edge into the pool, into the gorge, into the sea of the river's long descent.

I lay there and felt something crack quietly inside my chest. Not dramatic. Just a seam giving way.

"I'm sorry," the thief said. He meant it, too. That was the worst part. "About the pictures. Brannic only asked for the map, but I figured — evidence, you know? I figured it was worth more."

"It was worth more," I said. "It was worth everything. It was the only reason anyone would believe what's under this valley."

He sat up. Water streamed off him. Up close he looked exhausted in a way that went deeper than the climb — the gray-faced exhaustion of a man who has started doing math about his own life and doesn't like the totals.

"My name's Teodor," he said. "Teodor Vasch. I've been with Brannic's crew eight months. Eight months, and I'm starting to understand what we're actually doing up here." He wiped his face. "You're Dunmore. The kid. Aldous Dunmore's kid."

"That's not what I go by anymore."

"Yeah, well." He almost smiled. "He talks about you. Did you know that? Out here, on the trail, when he thinks nobody's listening. Talks about you like you died."

Something moved behind my ribs. I ignored it. "Why are you telling me this?"

"Because an hour ago I watched a man rig a charge line at the cavern mouth above these falls." His voice dropped, though there was no one to hear it but the river. "Brannic's orders. They're going to blow the outlet and divert the flow to expose the lower ruins. You know what's below the falls? Everything. The terraces, the flood zone, the whole valley floor. If they divert the river during monsoon—"

"They drown it again."

"They drown it again." He nodded slowly. "And everyone downstream of it. And when the inquiry comes, if it comes, the report will say the mountain did it. Natural disaster. Act of God." He looked at me. "Brannic's very good at reports."

* * *

Sefa found us twenty minutes later, and she did not arrive gently.

I heard her before I saw her — a scrape of boot on stone above the bowl, and then she was coming down the last pitch with her rifle slung and her face set in an expression I recognized from the rope crossing: fury held at a simmer, just below boiling. She'd climbed after me. Alone. Through the same washouts, the same wet stone, with the storm building overhead and the river rising under her the whole way.

"You," she said, planting herself in front of me. Spray soaked us all equally. "I tell you the mountain keeps what it takes. I give you food. I let you pass. And you run straight up into the teeth of it like the rules are for other people."

"I was robbed."

"I know. I found your camp." Her eyes cut to Teodor, and her hand moved to the rifle sling, and for a moment I genuinely thought she might shoot him where he sat. "Him I know. Brannic's crew. They passed my station two days ago with forged permits and a surveyor who wouldn't meet my eye."

"Sefa." I put myself between them, which was either brave or stupid. "He gave the map back. He's telling me things Brannic won't want told."

She didn't lower the rifle. "Things."

"There's a charge line rigged at the source," Teodor said. His voice was steady, which cost him something; I could see it costing him. "At the cavern mouth. They mean to divert the river. During the monsoon."

For a long moment Sefa said nothing. The falls filled the silence, roaring, indifferent. Then I watched something happen in her face — not surprise. Confirmation. Eleven years of confirmation arriving all at once, the landslide and the drowned village and the official report blaming the victims, all of it clicking into place behind her eyes like tumblers in a lock.

"They did it before," she said quietly. "Your father's survey team. They weakened the old terraces to get at what was underneath, and when the dam failed they wrote it up as geology." She looked at Teodor. "And now they're going to do it again, bigger, with better paperwork."

"Yes," Teodor said.

"And you helped them."

"For eight months, yes." He didn't flinch away from it. "And now I'm done. Do what you want about that."

Sefa looked at him for a long time. Rain began to fall — real rain now, fat drops that struck the pool and vanished, the leading edge of the monsoon finally making landfall. She seemed to weigh him the way she'd weighed me at the crossing: not trusting, but calculating what trust might be worth against what the alternative cost.

Finally she unslung the rifle and leaned it against a boulder, which I understood was the closest thing to a handshake she offered.

"Here's what we know," she said, crouching, pulling a stub of pencil from her pocket and sketching on a flattened scrap of tarp. "The charge line runs from the cavern mouth down to a firing point on the east ledge. Teodor, how many charges?"

"Six along the outlet seam. One main line. Detonator's a plunger box on the ledge — Brannic keeps it himself."

"How long?"

"He fires at first light tomorrow. Said the river would be high enough by then to carry the diversion clean through the lower gorge."

Sefa drew a line with her pencil, hard enough to tear the tarp slightly. "So we have one night." She looked up at me, and the rain ran off her jaw, and beneath the fury in her face I saw something else — exhaustion, bone-deep, the kind I recognized because I was wearing it too. "One night, three people, and a mountain full of men with guns."

"One night," I said. "Two people and a deserter."

"Deserters know where the guns sleep," Sefa said. She almost smiled. It was a grim, rain-soaked almost-smile, but it was the first one I'd seen from her. "That makes him useful. Useful isn't the same as trusted. But tonight, useful is enough."

Teodor exhaled — a long, shaking breath, the breath of a man stepping off a ledge and discovering midair that he'd rather fall forward than back. "There's a supply cache," he said. "Halfway up the east wall. Ropes, flares, Brannic's spare radio. I can get us in. After that—" He shrugged. "After that I'm just a guy who used to dig holes for bad people."

"We've all dug holes," I said.

I meant it more ways than one. Sefa glanced at me, and something passed between us — not forgiveness, nothing so warm, but recognition. Two people shaped by the same loss, standing in the spray of the same falls, agreeing without saying it aloud that this time the hole would not be dug by someone else's ambition.

Thunder rolled down the gorge, long and low, and the rain thickened from fat drops to sheets. Above the falls, hidden somewhere in the storm, the source of the Kessler waited with its ring of ancient masonry and its rigged charge line, and somewhere between here and there, my father was camped with men who owned him, about to help destroy the thing he'd spent his life looking for.

Sefa stood and shouldered her rifle. "We move now," she said. "In this rain, their watch will be blind and ours will be desperate. Desperate beats blind."

We started up the east wall together — a ranger with a dead village in her past, a deserter with a live conscience, and me, with a greasecloth map rolled tight against my chest and nothing left to lose except the promise I'd made to a dying man.

The mountain kept what it took. Fine. Tonight we'd see what it could be made to give back.
