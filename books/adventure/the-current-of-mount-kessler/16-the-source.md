## 16. The Source

The climb above the falls took everything Paloma had left.

The gorge narrowed to a throat of wet granite, and the river — the same river they'd been following for six days, the river that had been a trickle in Voss's map and a rumor in every childhood story their father told — went from roar to thunder to something felt in the ribs rather than heard. Spray hung in the air like a second weather. Every handhold was slick, every ledge crumbled at the edges, and twice Paloma stopped with fingers hooked into stone and simply breathed, counting, because stopping was better than looking down.

Sefa climbed behind them without complaint. She moved like water finding its level, economical, patient, testing each hold before trusting it. The digger — Rell, he'd finally said his name was, though Paloma trusted it about as far as they could throw him — came last, roped to Sefa's waist because none of them were fool enough to let him climb unwatched.

"Left channel!" Sefa shouted over the noise, pointing up through the spray. "Voss's map — the notch!"

Paloma checked the greasecloth one-handed, pinning it against their chest where the wind couldn't take it. There it was: the little inked notch in the cliff line, annotated in Voss's cramped hand — *the door, when the water is low enough to forgive you.*

The water was not low. The water had never been lower in anyone's memory, probably, given the bruise-colored sky stacking itself over the peaks to the south. But there was a shelf, and then another shelf, and then a seam in the rock wide enough for a body if the body was willing.

Paloma went first, because that was what the promise required.

* * *

They'd left the falls an hour after dawn, after Rell confessed — or performed confessing; with him it was impossible to tell — that Brannic had sent three men ahead of him by two days, carrying the photographs and instructions to find the cavern mouth and mark it for charges. Two days' lead. On this terrain, in this weather, that meant maybe half a day of real distance, less if the storm slowed everyone equally.

"They'll wait for the main party," Sefa had said, crouched at the fire ring, feeding sticks into the flames with the deliberate calm of someone refusing to hurry. "Brannic doesn't spend men he can spend dynamite."

"And Aldous?" Paloma had asked, and hated how the name came out of them — half wound, half question.

Rell had shrugged, wringing rain from his sleeve. "The professor walks slow. Talks to the rocks. But he's coming."

Coming. Up the mountain, toward the source, toward the thing Paloma had promised a dying man they would reach first. Not to own it. Voss had been clear about that in his last hours, gripping Paloma's hand with a strength that shouldn't have been possible in a body that empty. *It isn't treasure, kid. It's a heart. You don't dig out a heart.*

But hearts could be cut out anyway. Paloma had seen the survey stakes. Had seen what money did when it met old stone.

So they climbed.

* * *

The seam opened onto a ledge no wider than a walkway, and the ledge curved around the gorge wall into a place the spray didn't reach — a pocket of stillness so sudden it rang in the ears. And there it was.

The masonry.

Not rubble. Not suggestion. Walls — fitted stone, dry-laid, each block dressed and seated against its neighbor with a precision that made the surrounding cliff look accidental. They rose from the living rock in courses, curving inward, forming a face: an archway taller than two men, flanked by pillars carved — Paloma's breath caught — carved with water. Rivers in relief, braiding down the stone in channels worn smooth by centuries of runoff, converging at the base where a basin still held rainwater, dark and mirror-still.

Above the arch, a lintel stone bore a single carved line, deep and deliberate:

A serpent, coiled around a mountain, its mouth open at the summit — and from the mouth, water.

"The source," Sefa said quietly. She'd gone very still. Her ranger's composure had cracked open into something older and rawer. "My grandmother used to sing a song about a snake that held the mountain's water in its mouth. We thought it was a story about the dam." Her voice caught on the word. "It wasn't. It was a memory."

Paloma stepped forward and pressed their palm flat against the pillar. The stone was cold and faintly damp, alive under the hand in the way old things are alive. Somewhere behind the arch, muffled by rock, the river ran — they could feel it, a vibration in the masonry, the pulse of the whole valley.

Exactly as Voss drew it. The notch, the ledge, the arch, even the basin — he'd rendered it all in grease and pencil with the fidelity of a man who had stood exactly here, in this exact silence, and understood what he was looking at.

And beyond the arch, a passage sloped down into the mountain, dry as bone, breathing cool air outward like something sleeping.

* * *

Inside, the darkness was total until Paloma risked the flashlight, cupping the beam low.

The first chamber opened around them like a held breath. The floor was paved. The walls rose in clean courses to a corbelled ceiling, black with age but sound — no collapse, no seepage, no roots. Along one wall ran a channel, cut into the stone, carrying a thin thread of glacial meltwater that emerged somewhere deeper in and slipped away through a slot in the floor toward the arch outside. An aqueduct. A living one. The city's plumbing, still working after however many centuries.

"It's dry," Paloma whispered, and their voice bounced back at them off faces carved into the far wall — processions of figures, hands raised, standing in rows beside more water-channels. "The whole city. It's above the flood line. Everything below the reservoir drowned, but this—" They turned a slow circle, the light trembling. "This survived. Intact. Nobody has stood here since—"

"Since before my people's oldest songs," Sefa finished. She had not entered far. She stood just inside the arch, one hand on the doorframe, as if asking permission. "Don't say 'discovered.' Please. Say *returned to.*"

"Returned to," Paloma repeated. Something in their chest unlocked and ached.

Rell hung back near the entrance too, and for once he wasn't performing anything. He looked at the carvings, then at his own boots on the ancient pavement, and shifted his weight like a man standing in a church he'd been paid to rob.

"There's a chamber past this one," he said finally. "Brannic's advance team will be looking for the big gallery. The one with the—"

He stopped. Paloma turned the light on him.

"With the what?"

"With the gold," Rell said reluctantly. "There are figures in there. Small ones. Cast. Brannic's got a buyer lined up already, some private collection, no questions. That's why the charges — the main gallery's sealed behind a rockfall, and blasting is faster than digging."

Thunder rolled overhead, long and low, rolling down the gorge like a dropped drum. Dust sifted from the corbelled ceiling. Both of them looked up.

"How long?" Sefa asked.

Rell wiped his mouth. "Storm's early. Brannic planned on monsoon covering the noise. If it breaks tonight—"

"Then we have until tonight," Paloma said.

The light found the far wall again, the procession of carved figures with their raised hands. Paloma looked at them a long moment. Not worshipping the water, they realized. *Guarding* it. Standing between the viewer and the channels, arms lifted, saying: this far and no farther.

Voss hadn't drawn a treasure map. He'd drawn a warning label, and Paloma had carried it up the mountain like a deed of ownership.

"We're not blasting anything," Paloma said. "We're not letting them blast anything. This stays whole."

"You're two people and a prisoner," Rell said, "against a crew with rifles and a man who owns your debts."

"I'm one person who made a promise," Paloma said, "and I intend to keep it while I still have hands."

Thunder again — closer now, hard enough to feel in the pavement. Outside, through the arch, the light had gone the color of old bronze. The river's pulse under their feet quickened, as if the mountain had heard the weather and begun, quietly, to prepare.

Sefa pushed off from the doorframe and stepped fully inside for the first time. "Then we block the passage," she said. "Tonight. Before they come. My grandmother's song says the snake holds the water in its mouth." She looked up at the ceiling, at the dust still drifting down. "I think it's time somebody taught the snake to bite."

Far downstream, faint beneath the thunder, came a sound neither of them wanted to hear: engines. Two of them. Climbing.
