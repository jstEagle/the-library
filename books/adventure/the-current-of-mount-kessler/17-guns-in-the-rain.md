## 17. Guns in the Rain

The rain did not arrive. It landed — all at once, like a verdict.

Paloma felt it before they heard it: a pressure change in the cavern mouth, a cold breath pushing out of the mountain as if the river itself had inhaled. Then the sky above the falls simply broke, and the world became noise.

They were still inside the first chamber when it happened, standing among the masonry with Sefa's lamp guttering, and for one impossible minute Paloma forgot everything else — the map rolled against their ribs, the digger's tracks, the photographs lost over the falls. The chamber was dry. That was the miracle of it. The river roared past the entrance on both sides, channeled by walls some long-dead engineer had cut so precisely that not a drop of spray reached the floor, and under their boots the stone was carved — spirals, fish, a procession of figures walking toward the water with offerings in their hands. A city's heart, beating dry behind a curtain of falling water, exactly as a dying man had drawn it in grease and charcoal.

"Paloma." Sefa's voice, tight. She was at the entrance, looking down-valley through the rain-haze. "Look."

Lights. Four, five, six of them, strung along the lower switchbacks like a lit fuse burning uphill. And beneath the thunder, faint but unmistakable, an engine — the flat two-stroke cough of a portable generator being carried up a trail no generator should ever climb.

"They came up in this?" Sefa said.

"No," Paloma said, and their mouth had gone dry despite the deluge. "They were already coming. The rain just made them hurry."

* * *

Brannic stood under a tarp lashed to the rock at the base of the falls and watched his men work, and he permitted himself, for the first time in eleven years, the luxury of satisfaction.

It had cost him. He knew what the ledgers would say later — the helicopter charter to the ridge, the gear porters, the bonuses paid to men willing to climb into a closed reserve ahead of a monsoon. But cost was a language he spoke fluently, and every credit spent tonight bought something the government would never be able to un-sell: possession. Physical, boots-on-stone possession of the source of the Kessler, taken before any official could draw a breath to object. By morning there would be survey stakes in pre-colonial masonry and a claim filed from a satellite phone, and whatever inquiry followed would find Brannic's company already holding the ground, already holding the permits — forged, yes, but stamped and laminated and indistinguishable from real to anyone who hadn't written them himself.

The old prospector's map lay open on the crate beside him, weighted with stones, already softening at the folds from the wet air. Beside it, the copy — clean, annotated, Brannic's own hand marking the chamber, the terraces, the flood line Voss had drawn in careful dashes.

The flood line. Brannic looked at it again, the way he'd looked at it three times since the falls, and each time the arithmetic came out the same.

The city didn't end at the waterline. The drowned terraces below proved that; the girl's photographs — recovered from the thief's jacket, grainy but damning — proved more. Which meant the visible site, the intact heart of it, was a fraction of the whole. The rest lay under forty years of impounded water, under the reservoir that had swallowed Sefa's village and eleven lives and every record of why.

To expose it, you drained the valley. To drain the valley quickly, before the monsoon filled it past arguing, you moved the river. And to move a river you used shaped charges at the natural choke points — four of them, marked on Voss's own map as if the old man had left instructions for his own desecration.

The water would go somewhere. Downstream, mostly. Through the gorge, past the rope crossing, into the low country where the resettlement villages sat — the ones built after the landslide, the ones full of people who had already lost everything once.

Brannic had done the math on that too. The warning window, if the charges fired at dawn, was six hours. Enough time. Probably enough time. And if it wasn't — well. Accidents happened in closed reserves during monsoons. The government had taught everyone that lesson themselves, years ago, when they buried the dam report and blamed the dead.

"Boss." Kellin, his site chief, ducking under the tarp, rain sheeting off his hood. "Charges are staged at points one and two. Three and four are up top — we'll need the winch line past the falls."

"And our academic?"

Brannic nodded up-trail, where a figure in a soaked field coat stood apart from the diggers, hands jammed in pockets, staring at the falls as though it might apologize to him.

"He's been up there an hour," Kellin said. "Talking to himself. Or praying. Hard to tell with that type."

"It's neither," Brannic said. "He's rehearsing. Get back to your points. I want the winch rigged before the light goes completely."

Kellin went. Brannic folded the map away from the wet, and allowed himself one more moment of satisfaction, because he understood something about men like Aldous Dunmore that Aldous Dunmore had not yet understood about him: they were both hostages, but only one of them knew who held the key.

* * *

Aldous Dunmore had waited thirty-one years to stand at the source of the Kessler River, and now that he stood there he could not stop shaking, and he could not tell whether it was the cold or the shame.

He told himself it was the cold.

He had rehearsed the speech on the climb — the speech he would give, eventually, to the inquiry or the press or whoever finally asked how a disgraced archaeologist with forged permits came to stand inside the greatest pre-colonial discovery of the century. *I was deceived. My funding was compromised. I intended only a preliminary survey.* All of it true. None of it sufficient. He had learned the difference between those two things a long time ago, on another mountain, in another lifetime, when a young engineer named Aldous Dunmore signed off on terrace drainage plans he had not fully reviewed, and a hillside came down, and eleven people died, and the report that saved his career buried them a second time.

And now here he was again, at the top of the same river, holding the same pen.

"You should see it from inside."

The voice came out of the rain and Aldous turned so fast he nearly slipped on the wet stone.

She stood ten meters off, half-lost in the downpour — mud to the knees, hair plastered flat, a compass on a cord at her throat. Thinner than the last time he'd seen her. Older in a way that had nothing to do with years. His child. Standing at the source of the Kessler as if she had walked out of one of his old slide lectures, out of the photographs of the two of them at the coastal digs, small dark heads bent over a screen together while the ocean made its slow argument with the shore.

"How did you—" He stopped. Recalibrated. Thirty years of habit supplied the next words automatically, the ones that managed people, that kept conversations on terrain he controlled. "You shouldn't be here. This reserve is closed. It's dangerous, the monsoon—"

"I know," Paloma said. "I've been here two days."

Two days. Something cold moved through Aldous that had nothing to do with the rain. Two days meant she had found the terraces. Two days meant she might have found the camp, the stakes, his seal on the survey markers. Two days meant she had climbed past all of it, alone, in the teeth of the season, to stand here — and she had not come to him.

"The photographs," he said quietly. "That was you. Below the flood line."

"That was me."

"You have to understand the significance—"

"I understand the significance." Her voice didn't rise. That was the worst of it; she had inherited his temper and mastered it, where he had only ever mastered the appearance of mastering it. "Do you? Do you know what they're rigging right now, Father? Down at the choke points. Do you know what happens to this valley when they fire?"

Aldous looked away, up at the falls, where the water fell through the lightning-lit dark like a column of hammered silver. He knew. Of course he knew. He had read the charge plan upside down across Brannic's crate two nights ago while pretending to study the map, and he had said nothing, because saying nothing kept him on the expedition, and being on the expedition kept him near the discovery, and the discovery —

The discovery was the only thing he had left that was worth anything. Not the money. Not the redemption, even. The work itself. The one love of his life that had never asked him to be anything other than what he was.

"There will be warnings," he heard himself say. "Downriver. There's time—"

"There's never time. You know that better than anyone." She stepped closer, rain streaming between them. "Eleven people, Dad. Eleven, and a report that called it their own fault, and you let it happen because the alternative was losing everything. And you're doing it again. Right now. You're standing here letting it happen again."

"That is not—" His voice cracked, and he hated it, hated the weakness in it, hated that she of all people could hear it. "You don't know what I've given up. What I keep giving up. This is my life's work, Paloma. Everything I am."

"I know," she said. And then, softer, almost gentle, which was somehow worse: "That's the problem."

Lightning whitened the gorge. In its glare Aldous saw the diggers moving along the upper trail with coils of detonation wire, saw Kellin shouting over the rain, saw the winch cable rising toward the lip of the falls — and saw, clearly, for the first time, the shape of the thing he had agreed to.

When he turned back, she was gone. Melted into the rain and the dark, the way she had melted out of his life eight years ago, and for the same reason, and he stood alone at the source of the river with the thunder rolling down the valley like a countdown.

* * *

Sefa counted the lights on the switchbacks and did the arithmetic she had been doing since she was nine years old, crouched on high ground while the water took her village.

Six hours, if the charges fired at dawn. Less, if the reservoir was already high from the early storms. The rope crossing would go under in ninety minutes. The crossing meant the low trail, and the low trail meant the resettlement villages — Marrow Bend, Little Ferry, the church with the bell they'd salvaged from the drowned town and rehung in a new tower as if a bell could mourn twice.

Her radio was dead. Had been dead since the falls — water in the housing, or the antenna snapped on the climb, it didn't matter which. The government repeater on Mount Kessler's shoulder had been "down for maintenance" for three weeks anyway, which was what the government said whenever it wanted the reserve to be officially silent. No one was supposed to be in here. No one official, therefore no one at all. Therefore no warnings required, no evacuations authorized, no helicopters that would have to explain in a hearing why they flew into a closed zone.

So the warnings would have to travel the way warnings traveled forty years ago. On foot. On a rope. In the rain.

Unless someone stopped the charges from firing at all.

Sefa crouched under the ledge at the cavern entrance and watched the lights crawl up the far switchbacks, and she thought about the toll she had taken from Paloma's pack — half their food, taken without apology, because the mountain keeps what it takes. She thought about how she had let the stranger pass, and why: not kindness. Recognition. You know your own kind by what they're running from.

Then she looked at the winch cable rising toward the falls, and the men silhouetted against the lightning, and she stopped thinking about running entirely.

"Paloma," she said into the dark of the chamber. "I need to know something, and I need you to answer fast."

Paloma emerged from the inner passage, lamp in hand, face wet. "What?"

"Those charges. If they fire, my people drown. Second time." Sefa's voice was flat and steady, the voice she used for rope crossings in bad weather, the voice that left no room for either of them to feel anything yet. "So I'm going down there to cut their lines. And I need to know if you're the person who promised a dying man you'd carry a map upstream — or the person who stands in a dry room looking at pretty rocks while the valley fills."

The thunder came down the gorge like a door slamming.

Paloma looked at the carved procession on the wall — the figures walking toward the water with their offerings — and then at Sefa, and something settled in their face, some last piece of hesitation laid down like a heavy pack finally shrugged off.

"Give me the knife," Paloma said.

* * *

At the base of the falls, Brannic checked his watch, checked the sky, and gave the order to begin rigging point three.

Above him, unseen in the rain, the winch cable swayed against the lightning, and the water fell, and fell, and fell — patient, enormous, keeping its own count of everything done in its name.

And upstream of everything, in the dry heart of a dead city, a lamp went out.
