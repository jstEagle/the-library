## 19. The Flood

The first charge went off like a knuckle cracking inside the mountain.

I felt it before I heard it — a shudder through the cavern floor, up through my boots, into my teeth. The sound arrived a half-second later, rolling out of the dark throat of the river's source and slapping against the masonry walls, and then everything that wasn't stone was screaming: Sefa shouting from the ledge where she'd been cutting lines, Aldous shouting at his own crew, Brannic's diggers shouting because shouting was all they knew how to do when a plan came apart.

The water in the pool below the cavern mouth dropped. Just a little. An inch, maybe two. The river pulled back from the stones it had touched for four hundred years, the way a man pulls back his hand from a stove, and I understood with a clarity I'd never once felt about anything else in my life: the mountain had flinched.

Then the second charge fired. Partially. A dull thump somewhere deep, more felt than heard, and a crack ran across the ceiling of the outer chamber — not dramatic, not cinematic, just a thin black line that hadn't existed ten seconds ago, weeping dust.

"Get out!" Sefa's voice cut through everything else. She was on her feet now, silhouetted against the grey light at the cavern mouth, rain already coming down behind her in sheets. "The whole face is going! OUT!"

Brannic didn't get out. Brannic went deeper.

I saw him do it. He was forty feet inside the chamber, past the first ring of columns, where his men had stacked the cases they'd carried up through the gorge — survey instruments, coils of det cord, and the artifact case, the one they'd pried open an hour ago, the one with the goldwork and the painted ceramics laid out on a tarp like evidence at a trial nobody had convened. Brannic crouched over that tarp in the strobing dark, cramming pieces into the case with both hands, fast and greedy and utterly certain that the mountain would wait for him.

"Brannic!" I screamed it. I don't know why. He was the reason any of this was happening. He was the reason Voss was dead in a ditch outside a port town, if you followed the chain of money far enough back, and here I was screaming his name like we were friends.

He looked up. For one second our eyes met across the chamber, across four hundred years of untouched air, and there was nothing in his face at all. No fear, no gratitude, no recognition that I was a person. He was doing arithmetic. That was all Brannic ever did — arithmetic wearing a man's face — and the sum said the case was worth more than the seconds.

The ceiling answered before he could finish loading.

It didn't collapse so much as decide. A section of the outer chamber roof, cracked loose by the partial firing, let go with a sound like the whole gorge clearing its throat, and the floor tilted under me as the pressure wave came through. Dust went black and total. Somewhere in it, Sefa was still shouting directions, and somewhere in it, closer, my father was not shouting at all.

That was the thing that got me moving. Not duty. Not heroism. My father had gone quiet, and in thirty-one years of knowing him, quiet was always the worst thing Aldous Dunmore could be.

* * *

I found him fifteen feet from the edge of the flooded gallery, on his hands and knees, one leg pinned under a slab that had come off the wall rather than the ceiling — a facing stone, old masonry, dislodged by the shock. The water was rising around him. Fast. The charges hadn't diverted the river; they'd done something worse, something the engineers Brannic never hired would have predicted in a heartbeat: they'd blocked the natural outflow channel partway, and now the source had nowhere to go but sideways and up. The pool was climbing the steps of the ancient basin like it was reclaiming them, one terrace per minute.

"Paloma." His voice was steady. Of course it was. His voice was always steady; it was everything underneath that had rotted. "The slab. If you lift from the low end, the leverage—"

"I know how levers work." I'd learned it on his digs, kneeling in trenches when I was nine years old, and the memory arrived without warmth, just as fact. "Give me your belt."

He shrugged it off and I looped it around a column base — intact, pre-colonial, carved with fish that were swimming upstream, which felt like a joke someone was making at my expense — and fed the other end to him. Then I got my shoulder under the slab's low edge and pushed with my legs, the way you push when the alternative is watching someone drown, and the stone moved maybe three inches, enough for him to drag his leg free, and then the water took the argument over.

It came up our thighs in the time it took him to stand. Cold enough to burn. The current in the gallery was no longer a pool filling; it was a river re-routing, and it wanted the gap in the chamber wall, and we were between it and the gap.

"Hold the line!" I wrapped the belt around his wrist and mine both, knotting it badly, knotting it fast. "Don't let go even if I go under!"

"Paloma—"

"Don't!" And the word came out with twenty years inside it, all the times I'd wanted to say it and hadn't, and he shut up, and we moved.

We moved the way drowning people move — not toward safety but toward the only direction the water permitted. The current shoved us along the gallery wall, past the carved fish, past a lintel I recognized from Voss's map, the greasecloth map that was, at this exact moment, in its oilskin roll strapped across my chest under my jacket, because I had refused to leave it in camp and now it weighed what a promise weighs, which is more than stone. The water lifted us, turned us, slammed us into the column I'd belayed to, and the belt held, and my shoulder screamed, and above us the crack in the ceiling widened with a sound like tearing cloth the size of a cathedral.

Sefa appeared at the edge of the floodlight spilling in from the cavern mouth. She had a rope. Of course she had a rope; she was a ranger, and rangers believe in rope the way priests believe in prayer. She threw it short on purpose — I saw her judge the current and aim where the water would carry us rather than where we were — and my father caught it on the second pass, and then it was Sefa hauling against the river with her boots braced on four-hundred-year-old stone, swearing steadily in two languages, and the three of us made a machine with one purpose, which was to not die in the place we'd all come to save.

We came out of the water at the cavern mouth like wreckage. I lay on the wet masonry with the rain hitting my face and coughed up half the Kessler River, and beside me my father lay on his back with his pinned leg bleeding through the torn trouser fabric, breathing in great whooping gulps, alive.

Alive. I checked the strap across my chest. The map was there. Soaked at the edges, but there.

Then I remembered the photographs weren't. Those had gone over the falls two days ago, torn from the thief's pack in our fight, fluttering down into the white water — every record I'd made of the drowned terraces, the proof that this city extended beneath the flood line, the proof that excavation meant dynamiting a valley full of villages downstream. Gone. And now the map was the last thing on earth that showed where any of this was.

I was still lying there doing that arithmetic when the third charge fired.

* * *

Not partially, this time. Fully. Someone below — one of Brannic's diggers, panicking or obedient or both — had triggered the remaining line manually, and the lower gallery took the blast square. The sound was different from the first two: deeper, wetter, a concussion through water, and the effect was immediate and obscene. The floor of the outer chamber bulged. Stone that had held the river's shape since before maps let go of it all at once.

The cavern began to eat itself.

Roof fall, then wall fall, then both, in a sequence I watched the way you watch a tower of blocks go — unable to look away, unable to intervene, understanding the ending long before it finished. The beautiful mouth of the source, the masonry ring Voss had drawn with such careful love, shed its facing stones into the torrent. The chamber where I'd stood yesterday morning, dry and reverent, thunder rolling outside like a held breath — that chamber ceased to exist. The river, given its head at last, came through the ruin with a roar that swallowed every other sound in the gorge, and the water level at our feet began to rise again, faster, hungry.

"The case!" It was one of Brannic's diggers, further up the slope, pointing back toward the collapsing mouth. "Brannic's still in there! He went back for the case!"

Nobody moved. That was the honest truth of the next thirty seconds, and I've thought about those thirty seconds every day since, and the truth doesn't get softer with repetition: none of us moved. Not the diggers, who knew exactly what their boss was and what he'd have done for any of them (nothing). Not my father, whose leg wouldn't have allowed it anyway. Not Sefa, who was busy anchoring a second rope for the rest of us and had spent her whole life learning that the mountain keeps what it takes.

And not me. I stood at the edge of the roar with the rain hammering down and I did not move, and I want to tell you it was strategy — that going in was suicide, that two dead instead of one helps no one — and all of that is true. But underneath the true thing was a simpler one. I looked at that collapsing mouth, at the brown water exploding through it, and some cold clear voice in me said: *he made this*. He bought the permits, forged or otherwise. He brought the det cord up the gorge on his own back. He gave the order while the monsoon clouds were already stacking black over the ridge, because waiting cost money and the case was right there.

The mountain keeps what it takes. Sefa had told me that at the rope crossing, days ago, taking half my food with an apologetic set to her jaw. I hadn't understood it then. Standing at the cavern mouth, I understood it completely, and I understood that it worked in both directions.

Brannic came out anyway. Partway.

The water spat him into the light like something it was done with. He surfaced in the churn below the ruined mouth, one arm hooked through the handle of the artifact case, and for a moment — one long, horrible, almost admirable moment — he swam. He actually swam, one-armed, boots kicking, dragging a case full of goldwork through a flood, heading for the near bank with his teeth bared and his eyes fixed on nothing but the arithmetic.

The river considered him. Then it took the case.

I watched it happen. A submerged slab, a seam of current, whatever it was — the water grabbed the case and wrenched it out of his grip, and Brannic, who had never in his life reached for anything he couldn't buy, reached. He let go of the rock he'd caught. He lunged after the case into the main channel, both hands out, fingers spread, and the river closed over him the way a ledger closes over a bad debt.

He came up twice more. The first time he was empty-handed and still swimming. The second time he was facing downstream, which is the last thing a swimmer does before the river wins, and then he wasn't visible at all, and the case wasn't either, and the gorge filled back up with rain and roar and the small human sounds of six people standing in the rain discovering what they were.

My father made a noise. I couldn't tell if it was grief or relief. I suspect, in the years since, that he couldn't either.

* * *

We climbed. There was nothing else. The lower trail was gone under the surge, the crossing Sefa guarded was a brown horror, and the only way out of the upper gorge was up and over the saddle, in the monsoon, in the dark that came down at four in the afternoon like a lid. We went in a string on Sefa's ropes, diggers and Dunmores and ranger, enemies an hour ago, now just bodies helping each other up wet stone because the alternative was being bodies at the bottom of it.

I went last. Behind us, the mountain kept settling — groans, slides, the river renegotiating its terms with the rubble — and each groan erased another piece of the approach, another landmark, another switchback I'd memorized from Voss's greasecloth lines. By the time we gained the saddle, the route behind us didn't exist. By morning, I knew, it wouldn't matter whether it existed on paper or not, because the paper was the problem.

At the saddle, in the lee of a granite spine, with the rain coming horizontal and everyone shaking too hard to speak, I took out the map.

Voss's map. Greasecloth, hand-drawn, the lines faded where his thumb had worn them, the headwaters rendered in a prospector's careful shorthand — the falls, the cavern mouth, the masonry ring, the basin steps. The promise I'd made to a dying man in a ditch: *carry it upstream before the monsoon seals the high country.* I'd kept it. I'd carried it up the gorge, past the ferryman's toll, past the footprints, past my own father, all the way to the source. Promise kept, complete, notarized by my own blistered hands.

And every word on it was now a weapon.

Anyone holding this map held the location of a site that could still be dug — illegally, violently, the way Brannic had tried, the way others would try once the inquiry started and rumors spread like the floodwater had. The photographs were gone over the falls. The field notes in my pack were soaked to pulp, coordinates dissolved into grey blooms. This greasecloth sheet was the last record on earth of where the city's heart had opened its mouth to the sky, and the mountain had just sealed that mouth with five thousand tons of its own body, and the river was running blind again, the way it had run for four hundred years before any of us came.

Sefa stood beside me, streaming rain, watching me look at it. She didn't say anything. She didn't have to. She'd lost a village to a dam and a report; she knew better than anyone alive what a piece of paper can do to a valley.

My father was watching too. From ten feet away, huddled under the diggers' shared tarp, leg bound with his own shirt, he watched me hold the last map of the greatest discovery of his career — the discovery that was supposed to redeem him, fund him, justify him, the whole rotten ledger of his ambition balanced at last — and he said nothing. He had watched Brannic die reaching for artifacts and he had watched his child stand at the edge of the roar and he knew, with the terrible precision of a man who has spent fifty years reading strata, exactly what layer came next.

I held the map a moment longer. Voss's thumb-worn lines. The fish swimming upstream on the column bases. The dying grip of a stranger's hand around mine at dawn, trusting me with the only thing he'd ever found that mattered.

Then I fed it into the storm.

The wind took it instantly — one flap, one glimpse of the headwaters' inked lines, and then it was gone over the saddle and into the rain, into the watershed, into the current of Mount Kessler, dissolving somewhere between the air and the sea. Every record of the site's location went with it. The city belonged to the mountain again, whole and hidden and safe, and no one — not the creditors, not the government, not the next Brannic waiting in some office with a friendly face — would ever find it unless the mountain chose to show it.

I stood there empty-handed in the horizontal rain and felt — not triumph. Don't let anyone tell you it feels like triumph. It felt like setting down a weight I'd forgotten I was carrying, and discovering that my arms still worked.

Sefa put her hand on my shoulder. Once, hard, and let go.

My father looked at me across the fireless, shelterless saddle, and opened his mouth, and for the first time in my life I watched Aldous Dunmore fail to find the words — and then close his mouth again, and simply nod, slow, like a man agreeing to terms he'd spent a lifetime contesting.

Below us, the river ran brown and swollen through the gorge, carrying everything away — the case, the charges, the map, the name Brannic, the whole drowned argument. Downriver, past the falls, past Sefa's rope crossing, past the villages that would wake tomorrow to a river higher than any living person remembered, the water was already telling the story we'd have to find a way to tell better.

There was an inquiry waiting down there. There were eleven names that had never been spoken aloud in any official room. And somewhere below the flood line, a ranger's radio, if it still lived, was about to become the most important object on the mountain.
