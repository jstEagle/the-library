## 20. Testimony

The radio lived.

Sefa found it on the third day after the flood, wedged in the roots of a fallen alang tree a kilometer below her rope crossing, its casing cracked, its battery dead, its antenna snapped off at the second joint. She carried it downriver the rest of the way like a wounded animal — cradled against her chest, wrapped in her spare shirt — because it was the only object on the mountain that had heard everything. The storm warnings she'd sent. The government's silence. Paloma's voice, ragged with static, saying *the charges are set, they're going to blow the cavern* while the rain erased every word between them.

It was also, she understood by then, the only proof that anyone had tried to stop what happened next.

She reached the lower valley on the sixth day, walking the river road through villages still drying their bedding on fences, past houses marked with brown lines showing how high the water had come. Higher than the flood of '09. Higher than the one before that. People stood in doorways and watched her pass, and some of them knew her — the ranger from the reserve, the girl who'd survived — and none of them asked where she'd been, because everyone already knew the mountain had been open again this season, quietly, unofficially, the way closed things opened when there was money behind them.

At the district office in Harrow Bend, a clerk with damp socks told her the inquiry wasn't taking public testimony yet.

"Then I'll wait," Sefa said, and sat down in the corridor with the radio in her lap.

She waited two days. She was good at waiting. She'd learned it as a child in an evacuation camp, watching officials count heads and find her village's number inconveniently small.

* * *

The hearing room smelled of wet carpet and old paper. Three commissioners sat behind a table too long for them, flanked by a recorder and a man from the Ministry whose badge said HYDROLOGICAL AFFAIRS and whose face said he wished he were anywhere else. Sefa had seen that face before, or faces like it — the ones that appeared after disasters to explain why nothing could have been done, why the numbers were regrettable but correct, why the dead had built where they shouldn't have, farmed where they shouldn't have, been where they shouldn't have.

Eleven names. That was the arithmetic last time. Eleven names reduced to a footnote about "unauthorized settlement within the affected zone."

She set the radio on the table first.

"This unit logged transmissions over eleven days during the closure period," she said. "Storm warnings I filed. Evacuation advisories. And a distress call from inside the reserve on the night of the breach — a call the Ministry's relay station received and did not forward." She slid a printed log across the table. She'd reconstructed it from memory, transmission by transmission, timestamp by timestamp, in the corridor while she waited. Her handwriting was steady. She'd made sure of that. "I'm Ranger Sefa Imel. I was stationed at the Kessler crossing for six years. My village is under forty meters of water behind the old dam, and the report you inherited called us squatters. I'm here so this report doesn't get to do that to anyone else."

The middle commissioner — a woman with reading glasses on a chain, who'd introduced herself as Commissioner Adeyemi — looked at the log for a long moment, then at Sefa.

"You understand the reserve was closed by order."

"I understand it was closed by order and then opened by exception," Sefa said. "Someone funded a survey team with forged permits. Someone brought blasting charges into a sealed gorge. Someone planned to divert the headwaters of the Kessler to expose ruins below the flood line, which would have put another wall of water down this valley during monsoon. None of that appears in your briefing materials, Commissioner, because the people who did it are either dead or hoping you'll stop asking questions before you reach their names."

The man from Hydrological Affairs shifted. "Ranger, these are serious allegations—"

"They're serious events," Sefa said. "I'm just the only person alive who watched all of them happen and can still walk here."

* * *

They took her testimony in sessions. Morning, afternoon, sometimes into the evening when the rain hammered the windows hard enough to make the stenographer flinch. Sefa gave them the landslide first — not the official version, hers. The dam that had been flagged unsafe three years before it failed. The survey team that had drilled test charges along the upper terraces against every geological recommendation, and the report that had blamed saturated soil and unauthorized settlement instead. The eleven names, which she read aloud slowly, from memory, because no document in the room contained them.

Commissioner Adeyemi stopped her once, near the end of the second day.

"The survey team you're describing. You've named its field director."

"Aldous Dunmore," Sefa said. "Yes."

"And you believe his work caused the failure."

Sefa had turned this question over for years, turning it like a stone in her pocket, feeling for the crack. On the mountain, in the weeks before the flood, she'd wanted it to be simple. A villain. A name to hate. But she'd stood at the drowned terraces herself; she'd seen the old drill cores, the careful notes in Dunmore's hand flagging the unstable seam, warning against exactly what the funding consortium had ordered done anyway. He hadn't been innocent. But he hadn't been alone, and the difference mattered, because the people who signed the orders were still signing them.

"I believe he knew," she said. "And I believe knowing wasn't enough, and that's its own crime. But the charges were ordered by the consortium that employed him, and the report was written by the ministry that employed the consortium's consultants. He was the hands. They were the will. This country has a long tradition of prosecuting hands."

Adeyemi wrote something down. The man from Hydrological Affairs drank water he didn't need.

* * *

On the fourth day, they brought in the survivor from the upper gorge.

Sefa saw him first in the corridor — a tall man, gray at the temples, moving with the careful stiffness of someone whose body had recently been used as a battering ram by a river. His right arm was splinted. There was a bruise along his jaw the color of a storm cloud. He looked older than his file photo, thinner, and when his eyes found Sefa in the corridor they didn't look away, and neither did hers.

"Dunmore," she said.

"Ranger Imel." His voice was rough. "You climbed up after us. In that weather."

"Someone had to bring down the truth. It seemed to keep getting heavier the longer it stayed up there."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile. "My child says the same thing. Different words. Same thing."

They put him in the hearing room an hour later, and Sefa sat in the gallery and watched him testify, and it was the strangest theater she had ever witnessed: a broken man methodically dismantling himself in front of three commissioners and a stenographer, naming names, dates, amounts. The forged permits — he produced copies Brannic had kept, recovered from a waterproof case that had washed downriver and been found by a fishing crew at Tenlow Bend. The funding structure, layer through layer, shell company through shell company, ending in the same creditors who had once held a lien on a pawned compass belonging to his own child. The order to place diversionary charges above the falls. The order to falsify the survey data if the site proved smaller than the investors required.

"Did you refuse any of it?" Commissioner Adeyemi asked.

Dunmore was quiet for a long time. Rain ticked against the high windows.

"In 2011," he said finally, "I wrote a memo recommending against the terrace drilling. It was accurate. It was ignored. And then I stayed, and I let them use my name to make it legitimate, because the alternative was leaving, and I told myself the work mattered more than the signature. That's the confession, Commissioner. Not that I didn't know. That I knew, and I stayed, and eleven people died downstream of my signature, and I spent twelve years telling myself the soil did it." He looked down at his splinted arm. "The river corrected my arithmetic last week. I'm not going to insult it by lying now."

"Why?" Adeyemi said. "Why talk now?"

He glanced toward the gallery — toward Sefa, though Sefa understood later that he'd been looking for someone who wasn't in the room, someone who'd pulled him out of the water at the cost of everything they'd carried down the mountain.

"Because someone saved my life who had every reason to let me drown," he said. "And because my child is going to read the transcript of this hearing someday, and I would rather they read the truth than the version I've been telling since they were nine years old." A pause. "That's the whole answer. I don't have a better one."

* * *

They reopened the case that evening. Not dramatically — no press conference, no cameras, just a single line added to the record: *Inquiry into the Kessler Valley failure of 20XX, reopened on new evidence.* The eleven names went into the record as victims of engineering negligence and institutional concealment, not squatters. It filled half a page. Sefa had imagined this moment for twelve years, and when it came it was so small she almost missed it — a clerk typing, a stamp, a folder moved from one shelf to another.

Justice arriving small and late. But real. She held onto *real* the way she'd held onto the radio.

Outside the district office, the river had dropped a meter and a half. It ran brown still, but slower now, thoughtful, carrying less. Down at the landing, crews were pulling debris — fence posts, roof tin, one of Brannic's aluminum equipment cases, empty, its lock sprung. They'd found the man himself two days earlier, ten kilometers down, and Sefa had identified the body without ceremony, because someone from the mountain should be the one to say his name into the record too. Even his. Especially his. The valley deserved an honest ledger.

Paloma was staying at the ferryman's house above the landing. Sefa walked up there in the evening light, boots squelching, and found them sitting on the porch steps looking at the water, a mug of tea untouched beside them, the old brass compass hanging outside their shirt on its cord.

"They reopened it," Sefa said, sitting down.

"I heard. The whole landing heard. The clerk shouted it out the window like a cricket score." Paloma turned the compass over in their fingers. "Eleven names."

"Eleven names."

Paloma nodded slowly. Then: "He testified. My father. I read the summary they posted."

"He gave them everything. Including himself."

"That's the part I can't—" Paloma stopped. Started again. "Twelve years. One memo and twelve years of silence, and now one morning of honesty and it counts? He gets to be the man who confessed instead of the man who stayed?"

"Yes," Sefa said. "Because that's how it works, and it's rotten, and it's still better than the alternative, which is men like him never confessing at all." She looked out at the river. "You pulled him out anyway."

"I pulled him out anyway," Paloma agreed, and there was no bitterness in it, just a kind of exhausted wonder, as if they were still discovering what they'd done. "Voss made me promise to carry a map upstream. Nobody made me promise anything about carrying him back down."

"Maybe that's the promise that counted."

They sat in silence. It was, Sefa realized, the first silence she'd shared with anyone since the flood that didn't feel like waiting for bad news. Below them the current ran steady toward the low country, toward the drowned terraces now permanently underwater, toward the city that would stay hidden — which was, Sefa understood now, the only preservation it had ever really been offered.

"There's a hearing Thursday," she said at last. "Closed session. Adeyemi wants to talk about the reserve itself. Whether it reopens, and on what terms, and who speaks for the valley." She stood, knees cracking. "She asked me to bring you."

Paloma looked up. "Me?"

"You're the only witness who saw the source and chose to leave it alone." Sefa shrugged. "Turns out that makes you the most qualified person in the province to say what it should become."

Paloma looked down at the compass in their palm — at the needle swinging, settling, pointing not north but somewhere along the river, upstream, toward everything that had happened and everything that hadn't yet. Then they stood up, and drained the cold tea in one swallow, and said:

"Thursday. All right. But I'm telling them the truth, Sefa. All of it. Even the parts that make me look like a fool."

"Those are usually the parts that convince people," Sefa said, and started down the steps toward the water, where the current — clear for the first time in days, running clean off the mountain — was already on its way to becoming whatever story came next.
