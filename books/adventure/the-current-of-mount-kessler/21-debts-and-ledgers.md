## 21. Debts and Ledgers

The inquiry tent smelled of wet canvas and burnt coffee, and it was the first roof Paloma had stood under in six weeks that wasn't trying to kill them.

They sat on a folding chair with their boots still dripping, watching a clerk transcribe Sefa's testimony in long, careful strokes. The clerk had a way of writing that made everything sound smaller than it had been — the flood became "a hydrological event," Brannic's charges became "unauthorized demolition activity," eleven dead villagers from nine years ago became "the Kessler incident (see prior file)." Paloma understood, distantly, that this was how governments digested grief. They'd seen it before, in the margins of their father's old reports, where whole seasons of someone's life got compressed into a footnote.

Sefa finished. She came out of the tent with her jaw set and her hands shaking only slightly, which for Sefa was practically serenity.

"They asked me twice if I was sure about the dam," she said. "Twice. Like I might have dreamed my own village."

"Are you sure?"

"I've been sure since I was nine years old." She looked at Paloma's boots. "You're next. They'll want your statement on Voss, on the map, on what you saw at the source. Tell them all of it. Even the parts that make you look bad. Especially those."

"Why especially those?"

"Because the parts that make you look good are the ones they'll use against everyone else."

* * *

The statement took four hours. Paloma gave them Ede Voss's name and the promise made at dusk in a camp that no longer existed. They gave them the map — or what remained of it, a greasecloth rag scorched at one corner, its lower third dissolved into pulp by river water. They described the masonry at the cavern mouth as precisely as memory allowed, knowing even as they spoke that precision was now the only form the city would ever take. No photographs survived. No survey stakes. The site's location died in the collapse along with Brannic, and when the inquiry officer asked, gently, whether Paloma could mark the headwaters on a contour map, Paloma found they genuinely couldn't. The monsoon had redrawn the gorge. Whatever door had opened above the falls had closed behind it, and the river kept its own counsel.

"You understand," the officer said, not unkindly, "that this makes verification difficult."

"It makes looting impossible," Paloma said. "I've decided to consider that a feature."

The officer almost smiled. Then he slid a ledger across the table, because the world did not stop being the world just because you'd nearly drowned in it.

"This is the claim filed by Meridian Credit on behalf of its client consortium. It asserts an interest in any archaeological materials recovered during your expedition, per the financing agreement brokered by one T. Brannic." He tapped the page. "However. The agreement requires the signature of Mr. Brannic as guarantor, and Mr. Brannic is currently—" he consulted his notes with bureaucratic delicacy— "unrecovered. Without a guarantor, the claim has no standing. Without standing, the underlying debt instruments revert to standard collection."

Paloma stared at the ledger. Numbers they knew by heart, numbers that had lived in their chest like a second heartbeat for three years: the pawn interest, the ferry fines, the medical debt from the winter their lungs went wrong, the small humiliations compounding weekly. All of it funneled through Meridian. All of it guaranteed by a man now somewhere beneath forty feet of brown water, reaching for a case of artifacts he'd never sell.

"He's dead," Paloma said.

"The inquiry records him as missing."

"He's dead. I watched the water take him."

"Then the water," the officer said, closing the ledger, "is your co-signatory. Congratulations."

It was meant as a joke. Paloma laughed anyway — a short, cracked sound that startled them both — because there was nothing else to do with a sentence like that. Somewhere downstream, a man who had spent his life turning other people's desperation into leverage had been converted, in a single instant, into the instrument of their release. The arithmetic of it was obscene. Paloma carried it out of the tent like a stone they couldn't put down.

Owing almost nothing. Owning less. The pawn ticket for the compass was still folded in their breast pocket, softened by sweat and river water until the ink had gone ghostly. They'd bought it back in that rain-soaked port town out of spite, and spite had carried it up a mountain, and now here it was, worthless paper for a worthless object, except for the part where neither of those things was true.

* * *

The relief camp sat on a bench of high ground above the lower valley, rows of tents and tarps and government-issue cots, full of people who had evacuated ahead of the flood on Sefa's warnings and were now waiting to learn what they'd lost. Paloma walked through it in dry borrowed clothes and felt like a ghost attending its own funeral. Every face was doing arithmetic. Every face was counting.

Aldous Dunmore sat alone on a supply crate near the riverbank, a blanket over his shoulders, looking older than Paloma had ever permitted him to look. His expedition gear was stacked behind him in sad, orderly piles — crates stenciled with the university seal, half of them burst open by water, their contents scattered or ruined. He'd corroborated the confession. He'd given the inquiry everything: the forged permits, the falsified survey schedule, the money that traced back through Brannic to the same creditors who owned Paloma. He'd done it in a flat voice, without once looking at his child, and then he'd come down here and sat by the river like a man waiting to be sentenced.

Paloma could have kept walking. There was a road out of the valley, a bus twice a week, a life waiting to be rebuilt from almost nothing. They stood at the edge of the camp for a long moment, weighing it.

Then they went and sat on the next crate over.

The river ran below them, swollen but honest, carrying branches and silt and the occasional drowned crate of somebody's ambition toward the lowlands. Neither of them spoke. That was the thing Paloma noticed first, the thing that made their throat tighten: the silence between them wasn't the armed silence of the last fight, or the polite silence of the estranged years, or the loaded silence Aldous had deployed like a tool across a hundred childhood dinners. It was just silence. Two tired people watching water. The first honest quiet they'd shared since Paloma was small enough to ride on their father's shoulders between trench walls, pointing at potsherds and asking questions he always answered properly, completely, as if a child's curiosity deserved the same rigor as a peer's.

When had that stopped? Paloma turned the question over in the silence and found, not an answer, but a softening. Maybe it had never been one fight. Maybe it had been a thousand small withdrawals, each one reasonable, each one defensible, until the account was empty and neither of them could remember making the last withdrawal or why it had mattered so much.

The light went gold, then gray. A cook tent somewhere started up its generators. Downriver, someone laughed — really laughed, the relieved kind, the kind people make when they've counted their children and all of them are present.

Aldous moved first. Not much. He reached into the blanket and produced a tin cup, filled it from a thermos, and held it out. Coffee, probably terrible, certainly cold by now. Paloma took it. Their fingers brushed and neither of them flinched, which felt like a treaty being signed somewhere far from any negotiating table.

"You gave them everything," Paloma said finally. Not a question. Not quite an accusation either.

"It was mine to give." He watched the river. "Most of it was true. That's the strange part. I built the lie around a true thing — the terraces, the survey, all of it real work, real discovery. And then I let them put their money under it like a foundation, and after that every true thing I said was serving the lie." He exhaled through his nose. "Your mother used to say I'd ruin anything by taking it seriously. She meant it as a compliment."

"She was right."

"She usually was." He turned the thermos cap in his hands. "The inquiry will take my credentials. Probably my position. There's a hearing in the spring."

"I know."

"You don't have to attend."

"I know." Paloma drank the cold coffee. It was terrible. "I'll be there anyway."

Something moved across the old man's face — too quick to name, gone before it landed. He nodded once, the way he used to nod over a trench section when a student got a stratigraphy right: acknowledgment, not praise. From him it was worth more.

They sat while the dark came up out of the valley floor. Paloma found themselves cataloguing, helplessly, everything they didn't say. *Why did you take their money.* *Did you know about the dam.* *Do you remember the dig at Carrow Fen, when I was twelve, when you told me the past doesn't belong to whoever finds it but to whoever tells it true.* *I hated you for eight years and I can't find the hate anymore and I don't know what to do with the space it left.* None of it fit in a sentence. Maybe none of it needed saying tonight. Sefa had told them once, on the rope crossing, that the mountain keeps what it takes. But sitting here, Paloma thought maybe the opposite was also true: that some things the mountain gives back, changed, smaller, usable.

Aldous stood at last, joints cracking, and gathered his ruined crates with the resigned efficiency of a man who had packed and unpacked his whole life.

"There's a truck going down-valley tomorrow," he said. To the river, not to Paloma. "The driver owes me a favor from a dig in '09. Room for two, if two were going."

Paloma looked at him. He didn't look back. He stood there holding a waterlogged crate of a lifetime's work, an old man offering a ride home without once using the word home, or forgiveness, or any word at all that would require an answer harder than yes.

"I'm not going to your house," Paloma said.

"No."

"And I'm not staying past the spring hearing."

"No."

"And if you ever take money like that again, I'll testify against you myself."

That, finally, made him turn. He studied his child's face in the last gray light, and whatever he saw there seemed to satisfy some old, exacting standard, because the corner of his mouth moved — not a smile, nothing so generous, but the place where smiles used to live.

"Seven o'clock," he said. "Don't be late. He won't wait."

He walked off toward the tents, slow, upright, carrying his ruins. Paloma stayed on the crate a while longer, finishing the terrible coffee, listening to the river run. The compass hung at their neck under their shirt, warm from their skin, needle swinging loose and private in its housing, pointing wherever they chose to go.

Tomorrow, down-valley. After that, the spring, the hearing, the telling of it all again — Voss's name entered into the record at last, Sefa's village given back its truth, the drowned terraces spoken aloud so they existed somewhere besides memory. There'd be no city to excavate, no treasure, no redemption arc anyone could print. Just testimony. Just the story, told true.

Paloma stood, pocketed the ghost-inked pawn ticket they no longer needed and kept anyway, and went to find a cot.

Behind them the current of Mount Kessler ran on in the dark, clear at the top of the world and muddy at the bottom, carrying everything toward the sea — and for the first time in years, Paloma Dunmore owed it nothing, and slept.
