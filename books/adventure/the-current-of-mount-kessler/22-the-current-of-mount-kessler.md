## 22. The Current of Mount Kessler

The morning groups are the worst. They come off the ferry at Sefa's landing rubbing sleep out of their eyes, clutching paper cups of coffee, asking before they've even stepped onto the trail: *So where's the gold?*

Paloma has learned to love the question. It's a door, and every door is an invitation to walk someone through it the wrong way first.

"There's no gold," they say, turning to face the group — six today, two retired teachers, a young couple, a botanist who keeps drifting toward the ferns, and a teenage boy whose mother signed him up because he needed "structure." The river roars below the switchbacks, high with early snowmelt, loud enough that Paloma has learned to pitch their voice like a guide on a ship's deck. "There was never gold. There was a city that figured out how to live with a river instead of against it, and there were eleven people the government decided to blame for a dam they didn't build. That's what we're here to see."

The boy perks up. They always perk up at *eleven people*. Paloma hates that it works, and uses it anyway.

They start up the trail.

* * *

The reserve reopened fourteen months after the flood, which everyone agrees was fast and no one says was because the inquiry made silence more expensive than honesty. The official report now runs to four hundred pages. Sefa has read all of them. She can recite the paragraph where her village stops being a footnote and becomes a finding. She keeps a copy in the ranger station, dog-eared, and when visitors ask her — some of them ask, the ones who've heard — she doesn't perform grief or triumph. She just hands them the page number.

Paloma sees her most mornings. She checks their permits with the same dry suspicion she showed the first day at the rope crossing, as if fourteen months of clean paperwork might still be a trick.

"Six today," Paloma says, handing over the manifest.

"Six." She stamps it without looking up. "The boy's going to ask about bodies."

"The boy always asks about bodies."

"Tell him the mountain keeps what it takes." She finally looks up, and there it is — the almost-smile, rationed like everything else she owns. "You're taking the upper route?"

"Lower gorge first. The terraces. Then up past the falls if the weather holds."

"Storms Thursday."

"Then I'll be down Wednesday."

She stamps the last page. "You always say that."

"And I'm always right."

"One time you were wrong," she says, "and I had to climb a cliff in the rain after you. So forgive me if I keep the weather reports coming."

That was three months ago, the squall that came in a day early and pinned Paloma's group under an overhang for six hours while Sefa climbed up with rope and thermoses and the particular fury of a woman who has already buried people this mountain took from her. Paloma had apologized. Sefa had said, "Don't apologize. Just be boring. Boring guides die of old age."

Paloma intends, this morning, to be extremely boring.

* * *

The lower terraces are visible now from the overlook — not excavated, never to be excavated, that was the whole point — but mapped by sonar and marked by brass plaques the size of a hand. The water sits two meters below its old line; the diversion works were dismantled, the dam records unsealed, and the valley breathes again at something like its natural depth. You can see the tops of the drowned walls through the clear water on calm days, green-slicked stone laid in courses so fine the masons' fingerprints would have fit between the joints.

Paloma lets the group look for a full minute before speaking. This is the discipline: silence first, story second. Let the place make its own argument.

"Everything below that waterline," they say, "was going to be dynamited. Not looted — dynamited. To get at the dry chambers above the source, the plan was to drop the river into the lower valley and take the walls apart with charges." They nod at the water. "There are villages downriver. There were eleven graves in the ones upstream, from the first flood, and the plan would have made new ones."

The retired teacher raises her hand like she's still in a classroom. "And your father was part of that?"

The question lands the way it always lands, in the same soft spot, and Paloma has stopped flinching only because they've practiced.

"My father drew the survey that made the plan possible," Paloma says. "He didn't draw the charges. That distinction mattered to him enormously and matters to me very little." A pause. "He testified anyway. Fourteen months ago he stood up in front of the inquiry and said the words that ended his career, and I have spent a long time deciding what that was worth, and I still don't have a final answer, and I've decided that's allowed."

The boy asks about bodies. Paloma tells him the mountain keeps what it takes, and he writes it down in a notebook, and something about seeing it in a child's handwriting makes the sentence heavier than it's been in months.

* * *

They lunch at the falls.

This is the part of the route Paloma both loves and dreads, because the falls are where everything happened — the thief, the map, the photographs going over the edge in their waterproof case, spinning down into white water like a bird with a broken wing. The photographs survived, technically; one turned up downstream in a fisherman's net six weeks later, warped but legible, and it hangs now in the inquiry archive behind glass. But Paloma's memory of losing them is stored in this exact square of rock, and every visit they feel the case leave their fingers again.

They tell the group the true version. Not the heroic one. How they chased a man twice their size across wet stone because a dying prospector had made them promise, and how the promise was never really about delivering a map — Voss could have given the map to anyone. He gave it to Paloma because he wanted someone to understand, before the end, that the thing worth carrying upstream wasn't treasure. It was the river itself, whole.

"He died at dawn holding my hand," Paloma says, "and I spent the entire expedition trying to figure out what he'd actually asked me for. It took me until the water was rising around my ankles to get it right."

The botanist asks what was in the chamber at the source. Paloma describes it honestly: masonry ringed around a cavern mouth, dry, intact, the river emerging from the dark like it was being handed forward generation to generation. No gold. No idols. Carvings of water — channels, weirs, a civilization's whole theology cut in stone, a people who worshipped by engineering.

"And then?" the boy says.

"Then the monsoon hit, and men with guns arrived, and the charges fired anyway, half of them, and the cavern started to come down." Paloma turns a stone over in their fingers. "I lost the map in the flood. Every record of how to find that chamber went into the river. Which means the city's heart is sealed again, exactly as it was for five hundred years, and the only way back in is the way the original builders intended: by understanding the water better than we currently do."

"So nobody can ever see it?"

"Somebody will," Paloma says. "Slowly. Correctly. With permits and sonar and twenty years of patience. And when they do, it'll belong to the descendants of the people who built it, not to whoever got there fastest." They look at the boy. "Fastest is how valleys drown."

* * *

Sefa meets them at the upper crossing on the way down, walking the rope line with her usual insult toward gravity. She falls in beside Paloma at the rear of the group and lets the others drift ahead along the ledge.

"You told them about the chamber," she says.

"I always tell them about the chamber."

"You told them where it isn't." Her mouth twitches. "Half those people are going home and drawing maps tonight."

"Let them. The maps will be wrong. Everyone's maps are wrong until the water tells the truth." Paloma glances at her. "How's the village case?"

"Compensation hearings start next month. Eleven families, eleven settlements, and a government signature that took fourteen months and your father's testimony to get." She says it flatly, but her hand brushes the railing — the small involuntary touch she gives anything solid when the numbers get large. "My mother cried when the letter came. First time since the flood. Then she said the money should buy a school, and now I'm on the committee, so apparently grief has administrative duties."

"You'll be good at it."

"I'll be terrible at it and honest about being terrible, which is the best anyone offers these days." She stops at the junction where the trail splits — her route down to the landing, Paloma's to the overnight shelter. "Thursday's storm moved up. Tomorrow night."

"Then I'm down tomorrow afternoon."

"You always say that."

"And I'm always—"

"Boring," she says. "Be boring." She hesitates, then does the thing she's done maybe four times total: squeezes Paloma's shoulder, once, hard, like testing a rope anchor. "Dinner Sunday? My mother wants to meet the person who made the government apologize. She has opinions about your father. She wants to share them."

"She can join the queue."

"Sunday," Sefa says, and drops down the trail, calling back over her shoulder, "and bring nothing. My mother feeds people whether they're hungry or not. Resistance is noted and ignored."

* * *

The overnight shelter is a converted survey hut — Aldous's old stakes pulled out, the university seal scraped off the door, a wood stove and four bunks and a window that faces the gorge. Paloma stays here between trips, alone more often than not, and has come to think of it as the first room that has ever belonged to them rather than to a debt.

Debts, these days, are almost funny. With Brannic gone — swept away reaching for a case of artifacts, his body never recovered, his creditors' claims dissolving into a legal swamp that has swallowed three law firms so far — the arithmetic collapsed. Paloma walked out of the final hearing owing four hundred and twelve dollars, which they paid in cash, in twenties, savoring every one. The pawnshop broker in the port town sent a letter asking if they'd like to donate the compass's history to a museum. Paloma wrote back: *It's still in use.*

Because it is. It hangs at their neck now on a leather cord, dented, glass scratched, needle swinging lazy and sure whenever they check it. They used to think it pointed north. It doesn't, particularly — it points wherever they choose to go, and lately that choice has been the same direction every morning: upstream, toward the sound of the water, toward whatever small true thing can be told about it today.

Aldous writes. Letters, actual letters, on university letterhead he no longer possesses — he must buy his own paper now, and there's something in the careful handwriting that suggests he finds the blankness of unbranded pages humiliating and does it anyway. He doesn't ask for forgiveness. He asks questions. About the trails, the birds, whether the terraces show through the water in autumn. Last month he asked whether Paloma would co-author a paper with him — a corrective history of the first survey, naming the dead properly. Paloma hasn't answered yet. They've read the letter eleven times. They're waiting to want to say yes instead of merely running out of reasons to say no.

Tonight, after the group descends to the landing and the shelter goes quiet, Paloma sits on the step with coffee gone cold and watches the light fail over the gorge. Somewhere below, the river runs over the drowned terraces, and somewhere above the falls, behind fallen stone, the dry chamber waits with its carvings of water, patient as it has been for five hundred years, in no hurry at all to be found by anyone in particular.

Their debts are paid. Their name is repaired — or maybe just renamed, *the guide who kept the river whole*, which is a stranger name than the old one but fits better. They are broke in the ordinary way and rich in the way that took twenty-two chapters of ruin to learn: belonging to no one's ambition but their own.

The compass swings at their throat. North, south, upstream, home — it doesn't care. It just points, and Paloma chooses.

Tomorrow there's a storm coming and a dinner on Sunday and a letter on the table that deserves an answer. Tonight there is the river, running clear from its hidden source, saying the only thing it has ever said:

*Keep going.*
