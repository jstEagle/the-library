## 1. Rain Shadow

The wind came off the Andes at dawn the way it always did, stripped of its moisture and hungry, and Casper Underhill stood in the yard of his rented shack with a coffee can of instrument parts and let it sandblast his face. Forty kilometers east, over the cordillera, storms were probably stacking up in towers ten miles high. Here on the dry side, all that arrived was the wind—air that had already given everything away on the other side of the mountains and crossed over with nothing left but appetite.

He'd chosen this place for exactly that reason. A man who used to chase weather does not stop reading it just because he's stopped chasing it. The wind told him things. It told him the front was coming through tomorrow, weak and dry, nothing to worry about. It told him the dust off the Lago Buenos Aires flats would be bad by afternoon. It told him, most days, that the world was still turning according to rules, and that he could stand outside those rules like a man watching a river from a bridge.

The shack sat alone on a gravel spur off Route 40, south of Perito Moreno, surrounded by a quarter mile of nothing: coiron grass gone gray, thorn scrub, a fence line held up more by habit than wire. He'd hung his instruments along the north wall where they'd catch the wind clean—an anemometer scavenged from a dead weather station, a tipping-bucket rain gauge he'd modified himself, a barometer whose needle he'd replaced with one cut from a windshield wiper arm. On a plywood board screwed to the wall above them, he kept his logbook under a rock. Every morning, same numbers, same handwriting getting smaller as the years went on.

*Wind WNW 14 kt, gusting 22. Humidity 11%. Pressure steady 1013.2. Precip last 24h: 0.0 mm.*

Zero point zero. That was the whole religion out here. The rain shadow wasn't a place so much as an argument the mountains had won.

He went inside, put the kettle on the two-burner propane stove, and opened the visor of his truck.

The ledger was taped there, where it had been taped for three years—a sheet of legal-pad paper gone soft at the folds, covered in columns of figures in three different inks because he'd run out of pens at various points and never cared enough to be consistent. He didn't need to read it. He knew it the way other men knew their own pulse. But he read it anyway, every morning, the way you touch a bruise to check if it still hurts.

It still hurt.

**Total outstanding: $214,600 USD equivalent. Interest accruing.**

Below that, itemized with an accountant's cruelty:

*Ferro Capital Partners — principal + compounded interest — $189,000*
*Miscellaneous creditors (sold down) — $25,600*

One hundred eighty-nine thousand dollars owed to a company he'd never visited, in a country he'd never seen, run by people who had bought his debt the way you'd buy a distressed property—at a discount, with intent. He'd signed the original notes back when he was someone else, back when the name on his passport was different and the money had seemed like a tool instead of a leash. Storm chasing equipment. Radar units. A truck. Fuel for a season that was going to make him famous or at least solvent. He'd been so sure the sky would pay him back.

The sky hadn't paid him back. The sky had taken everything else and left him the bill.

The kettle screamed. He poured the water over instant coffee, sat on the edge of his cot, and drank it looking at the ledger until the numbers blurred. Then he flipped the visor shut, because that was the ritual—the closing of the visor meant the day could begin—and went outside to log the morning readings.

* * *

Route 40 south of town was a straight line drawn by someone who hated drivers. Two hundred kilometers of gravel and washboard with the steppe unrolling on both sides, flat as a table, brown as cardboard, broken only by the occasional guanaco standing in the middle of the road with the serene stupidity of an animal that has never met a predator capable of doing sixty. Casper drove his truck—a 1998 Hilux with a homemade rack on the roof carrying a disassembled mobile weather station he no longer had any professional reason to operate—into Perito Moreno once a week for diesel, flour, yerba mate, and the particular species of loneliness you can only buy at a small-town fuel stop.

The YPF station sat on the edge of town where the pavement gave up. It had two pumps, a kiosk that sold everything from spark plugs to birthday candles, and a counter where four men played cards most afternoons with the concentration of men guarding the last warm thing in the world. Casper had been coming here long enough that nobody asked him questions anymore. That was the currency of places like this: not friendship exactly, but the mutual agreement not to be curious. He paid cash. He said little. He fixed the station owner's generator once in exchange for credit, and now the owner, a heavy silent man named Beto, nodded at him the way you nod at weather—acknowledging its presence without expecting conversation.

He was filling the tank when the sedan pulled in.

He noticed it before he consciously looked at it, the way you notice a change in pressure. The car was wrong for the road—a dark gray Audi, spotless, the kind of vehicle that exists only to travel between airports and offices, appearing at the end of the world the way a shark appears in a swimming pool. It parked with precise economy beside the pumps. The driver stayed behind the wheel. The passenger door opened.

The woman who got out was maybe forty-five, dressed in a way that cost more than Casper's truck, with dark hair pulled back and the unhurried movements of someone who had never once needed to hurry. She walked toward him across the gravel in low heels that somehow didn't sink, and stopped at a distance of two meters, which was close enough to talk and far enough to suggest she'd calculated even that.

"Casper Underhill," she said. Not a question.

"That depends who's asking."

"Marisol Ferro." She pronounced it the way you'd lay a card on a table. "Ferro Capital Partners."

The name went through him like cold water through a wetsuit. He'd known, abstractly, that the debt had been bought by someone. He'd imagined a bank, a fund, a filing cabinet in a city he'd never visit. He had not imagined her standing on the gravel in the wind with dust sticking to the hem of her coat, looking at him with an expression of mild, patient interest, like a scientist observing something she'd ordered grown in a lab.

"You're a long way from anywhere," he said.

"I'm exactly where I need to be." She glanced at the truck, at the instruments on the roof rack, with what appeared to be genuine curiosity. "You know, when we acquired your paper, I read the file. Former meteorologist. Storm intercept specialist. Published research on mesoscale convective systems. Then twelve years ago—" she made a small gesture, delicate, like brushing away a fly, "—you became a ghost. You've been hiding very well, Mr. Underhill. This is a good place for it. Nothing out here but wind and honest arithmetic."

"What do you want?"

"To talk about arithmetic." She reached into her coat and produced a slim folder. "Your balance is one hundred eighty-nine thousand dollars. You've been paying... irregularly. Small amounts. Enough to keep us from formal action, not enough to matter. We appreciate the gesture, but gestures have a shelf life." She opened the folder and turned it so he could see a single page. "Our investors want resolution. So here is what I've come to offer you, personally, because I believe in second acts."

She laid a pen on the folder's edge.

"Two weeks. In fourteen days, either you present a credible plan to begin meaningful repayment—or you sign over the truck, the equipment, and whatever claim you have to your future earnings, and we take everything and pursue the remainder through channels you will find considerably less pleasant than this conversation." Her voice never rose, never sharpened. It was the tone of a woman discussing delivery schedules. "Or," and here something moved behind her eyes, something quick and bright and almost kind, "if you hear of work—specialized work, the kind a man with your training might do—we pay finder's fees for referrals. We have interests in this region. Resource interests. A man who knows the terrain and knows how to keep quiet could retire his entire balance in a single engagement."

"I don't want to work for you."

"Of course not. Nobody ever wants to work for me. They want the debt to disappear, and then they discover that wanting and working are the same thing viewed from different angles." She picked up the pen and held it out to him. "Sign acknowledging receipt of terms. It changes nothing legally. It just tells me you heard me."

He took the pen because refusing felt like giving her something better than a signature. He signed. His hand was steady, which surprised him; some part of him had expected it to shake.

"Ferro," he said, handing it back. "That's an unusual name for a finance company."

"It means iron." She smiled, and it was the first expression she'd worn that reached her eyes, and it was worse than all the others. "My grandfather was a blacksmith. He used to say iron remembers every shape it's ever been forced into. Debts are the same, Mr. Underhill. They remember." She slid the folder into her coat. "Fourteen days. I'll be in touch."

She walked back to the Audi. The driver already had the engine running. As the door closed, she paused and looked back at him through the glass, and mouthed something he couldn't hear, and he understood that it didn't matter what she'd said—it mattered that she wanted him to wonder.

The sedan pulled onto the road and shrank to a dot and was gone, and the wind came across the flats and pushed against him like a hand, and Beto appeared in the doorway of the kiosk and looked at Casper and then at the empty road and then went back inside without asking anything, because that was the arrangement.

Casper stood by his truck for a long time.

Then he flipped open the visor, took down the ledger, and beneath the total—$214,600—he wrote, in the smallest handwriting yet: *14 days.*

* * *

That night the front came through, exactly as the wind had promised—dry, loud, useless. He sat inside the shack with the lamp on and listened to it hammer the tin roof with air that carried not one drop of water, and he thought about the word *interests*. Resource interests. Ferro Capital had bought his debt at a discount, which meant they believed it was worth collecting, which meant they believed he was worth collecting, and nobody spends money sending a woman in good shoes to the end of the earth for a deadbeat unless the deadbeat has something they want.

Skills. She'd said skills. A man who knows the terrain and knows how to keep quiet.

He got out his logbook instead of drinking, because drinking was a door he'd nailed shut years ago, and he read back through the months of zeros. *Precip: 0.0. Precip: 0.0. Precip: trace.* Twelve years of data from a place where nothing happened, recorded by a man pretending nothing had happened to him. There was a strange comfort in it. The instruments didn't lie. Whatever else he'd done, whatever he owed and to whom, the anemometer spun honestly, and the rain gauge stood empty and true, and somewhere in that honesty was the last clean thing he owned.

Around midnight the wind dropped, the way it did before certain kinds of days, and in the sudden silence he heard his satellite phone buzz on the shelf.

Nobody called that phone. He'd bought it for emergencies and given the number to exactly two people: a supply contact in Comodoro, and no one else. He stared at it through three buzzes, telling himself it was a wrong number, a telemarketer, a surveyor needing directions.

On the fourth buzz he answered.

"Casper Underhill?" The voice was male, clipped, government-cadence, the accent of Buenos Aires laid over impatience. "This is Delgado, Ministry of Infrastructure. I'll be brief. Six days ago a provincial survey team missed its scheduled check-in. Team Kestrel. Six personnel, last known position in the badlands west of the mesas. Their families are asking questions the Ministry can't answer, and the police are busy. We need someone who can drive that country and read it." A pause, the sound of pages turning. "Your name came up. Oddly, it came up twice—from a former employer of yours, and from a private party. Both recommended you highly. That doesn't usually happen."

Casper closed his eyes. *A former employer.* He hadn't had one in twelve years. *A private party.* He could see the good shoes, the smile that reached the eyes.

"The rate," Delgado said, "is triple your last documented daily rate, plus expenses. We need an answer tonight."

Outside, the wind had died completely, and the silence was the loudest thing he'd heard in years—the silence right before a storm decides which way it's going to break.

"When do I leave?" Casper said.
