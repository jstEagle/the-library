## 2. The Vanishing

The wind came off the mesas at four in the morning the way it always did, and Casper Underhill lay in his truck bed listening to it read him his own instruments.

He'd built the anemometer himself from a bicycle dynamo and a soup ladle, and it ticked against its mast in a rhythm he knew better than his mother's voice—thirty-one kilometers an hour, gusting to forty when the cold air spilled down off the plateau and went looking for the valley floor like water looking for a drain. The barometer in the cab had fallen three millibars overnight. Nothing was coming. On this side of the Andes nothing ever came; the mountains wrung the clouds out on the wet side and let the dry side keep its dust. Eleven years of that arithmetic had made him fluent in a language of almost-nothing: the whisper of humidity at two percent, the temperature swing of twenty degrees between midnight and noon, the way the sky could be so clean it felt less like weather than like the absence of an argument.

He liked it here for exactly that reason. A place with no weather was a place with no forecasts, and a man who no longer made forecasts could almost pretend he'd never made one badly enough to kill people.

The ledger rustled above his head where it was taped inside the visor, stirring in the draft through the cab's broken window seal. He didn't need to look at it. He knew every line the way other men knew psalms.

*Ferro Capital — 41,300 USD, compounding monthly.*
*Mendoza Freight — 6,800.*
*Dr. Amaro, dental — 1,150.*
*Assorted, Esquel and south — 3,900.*

Fifty-three thousand dollars, give or take what the peso had done this week, which was fall. He'd stopped adding interest by hand years ago because the number only ever did one thing. It grew like salt crust on a windshield. You couldn't wipe it off. You could only drive until you couldn't see it anymore, and then it was still there.

At dawn he boiled water on a camp stove balanced on the tailgate and ate oatmeal with powdered milk, watching the light come up over badlands the color of old brick and dried blood. The town of Puerto Deseado was ninety minutes north if he needed diesel or shame; mostly he didn't. His clients were few and strange—a sheep estancia wanting to know when to shear, a road crew wanting to know when not to pour concrete, a widow who paid him in lamb and conversation to tell her whether the coming winter would be kind to her husband's grave. He charged little. Charging little kept him invisible, and invisibility was the one asset Ferro's collectors hadn't figured out how to lien.

His radio crackled at seven-forty. Not the ham band he monitored out of habit, but the satellite phone, which rang maybe twice a year and never with good news.

"Underhill." He said it flat, the way you answer a door you haven't decided to open.

"Casper Underhill. Meteorological consultant, licensed in Río Negro province, currently operating without—" A pause, papers shuffling. "Forgive me. I've read your file."

Everyone who called him had read his file. That was the trouble with files.

"Who's asking?"

"My name is Delgado. I work for the provincial ministry of infrastructure. There is a matter requiring someone with your particular—background." The word *particular* did a lot of quiet work. "Are you able to receive a visitor today?"

"I'm able to receive a visitor any day. Whether I do is another question."

"We'll pay triple your standard rate." Delgado said it without inflection, the way a man sets a heavy object down on a table. "Per diem plus expenses. And there would be a completion bonus."

Casper looked at the ledger taped inside his visor, visible through the windshield even from the tailgate, fifty-three thousand reasons arranged in his own handwriting. Then he looked east, where the land ran out into scrub and stone and the sort of emptiness that swallowed helicopters as easily as it swallowed men.

"When?" he said.

* * *

Delgado arrived at noon in a white Toyota Hilux with government plates and a driver who stayed behind the wheel with the engine running, which told Casper everything about how long the man expected to stay and how welcome he expected to be.

He was younger than his voice—mid-thirties, city-pressed shirt already wilting in the heat, a leather folder held against his chest like a shield. He stood at the tailgate and looked at the instruments with the expression of a man trying to remember why he'd been told they mattered.

"You live out here," he said. "Alone."

"It saves on arguments."

Delgado opened the folder. "Six days ago I would have offered this contract to someone else. But the situation has developed qualities that make my superiors nervous, and nervous ministries want specialists." He drew out a photograph and laid it on the tailgate, weighting one corner with a rock before the wind could take it. "Survey Team Kestrel. Six members. Hydrology, geology, seismology, logistics, two field technicians. Contracted by the ministry under permit 4471-C to conduct a groundwater assessment in the meseta country southwest of here. Four hundred kilometers of nothing, give or take."

Casper picked up the photo. Six people squinting into sun outside a government office in Esquel, holding a banner none of them looked happy about. He noted faces the way he noted cloud formations—automatically, cataloguing. A tall Black woman front and center with the stillness of someone used to being obeyed. A wiry blonde woman grinning like the banner was a joke she was in on. Four others, forgettable in the way group photos make everyone forgettable.

"Eleven days overdue," Delgado said. "Their scheduled check-in from the last waypoint never came. Aerial reconnaissance located their camp intact. Tents pitched, vehicles present, generator and equipment undisturbed. No sign of violence. No sign of fire. No tracks leading away from camp in any direction."

"No tracks."

"None. The ground there is gravel and hardpan. If six people walked out, they left no evidence of it. If they drove out, the vehicles are still there." Delgado's jaw worked. "The air force flew the grid twice. Thermal imaging found nothing. No bodies, no movement, no smoke. It is as though the team walked off the face of the earth and took the earth's permission with them."

Casper set the photo down. Wind worried the corner of it against the rock.

"What exactly do you think a storm chaser does with a missing survey team?"

"I think you're not actually a storm chaser." Delgado said it mildly, watching him. "I think you're a field tracker with an unusual education who found it convenient to be a storm chaser, and I think whoever reads your file at whatever agency still keeps it agrees with me, because your name came up twice before anyone thought to ask why." He turned a page. "I also think you know this country better than our search aircraft pilots do, and I think you have no family, no obligations, and no reputation left to damage, which makes you ideal."

"That's a hell of a sales pitch."

"I'm not selling. I'm describing." Delgado produced a second sheet—a contract, dense with clauses—and laid it beside the photo. "Triple rate. Expenses covered. Completion bonus sufficient to, ah—" his eyes flicked, briefly, deliberately, to the visor inside the truck cab, and Casper felt the skin go cold on the back of his neck— "*restructure certain outstanding obligations.*"

The wind ticked the anemometer. Thirty-four kilometers an hour. Somewhere far off, a hawk rode the thermal off the mesa and didn't flap once.

"How do you know about my debts?" Casper asked quietly.

"The ministry knows many things. Some of them are even relevant." Delgado straightened the contract's corner. "Underhill, I will be direct, because I've read that you respond to directness and because I don't enjoy this any more than you do. Team Kestrel is eleven days overdue in terrain where eleven days overdue means dead. My superiors are preparing to declare them lost and seal the file—not from callousness, but because a sealed file is cheaper than a search, and because permit 4471-C has attracted attention from parties we would rather not name in writing. If the team is alive, they are alive somewhere our aircraft cannot see. Which means someone has to go on the ground. Someone who doesn't mind being unfindable themselves."

"And if I say no?"

"Then we hire someone worse, and in three weeks I write a letter to your creditors confirming that you were contacted regarding lucrative employment and declined." Delgado shrugged, apologetic and absolute. "You know how collectors interpret that."

Casper looked at the contract. Triple rate. Bonus. *Restructure certain outstanding obligations.* They'd done their homework down to the visor. That was the part that frightened him more than the missing team did—that somewhere in a ministry office sat a folder with his real history in it, and someone had cross-referenced it against a debt ledger, and the sum of those two documents was a man who could be bought cheap and aimed precisely.

But underneath the fear, in the place he kept locked and visited rarely, something else stirred. No tracks. Gear intact. A seismology team that vanished from hardpan without leaving footprints. He'd spent eleven years telling himself the earth held no surprises left for him, and here was a government man standing at his tailgate saying the earth had swallowed six professionals whole and left the dishes washed.

It was a terrible feeling, that stirring. It felt like hope, and hope was the thing that had wrecked him the first time.

"Conditions," Casper said. "One. I work alone. No ministry handlers within a hundred kilometers. Two. Whatever I find, I report to you first and to no one else—not your superiors, not the parties you'd rather not name. Three." He tapped the photo, the tall woman with the stillness. "This one. Okafor, according to your paperwork. If she's alive, she's in charge, and whatever she says about the site goes in my report word for word. You don't edit her."

Delgado considered. "Accepted. And mine: you leave tomorrow. And Underhill—if you find evidence of foul play, you do not investigate it. You mark coordinates, you withdraw, you report. You are a locator, not an avenger."

"I'm neither," Casper said. "I'm a man paying off a ledger. Everything else is weather."

They shook on it. Delgado left a copy of the contract weighted under the same rock, along with a memory card containing Kestrel's route plan, last known GPS fixes, and personnel files, and then the white Hilux pulled away south trailing dust, and Casper stood alone in the enormous afternoon with the wind reading him his instruments and the contract flapping gently against the tailgate like something alive.

That night he spread the route plan across the hood under a headlamp and traced Kestrel's line with a finger: north from Esquel, west past the gas fields, then south into the meseta—the deep interior, the true rain shadow, where the maps gave up naming things and settled for contour lines. Their last fix put them in a basin between two mesa chains, forty klicks from the nearest track, in country he happened to know better than the ministry realized. Country he'd driven through once, twelve years ago, chasing something else, in a life under a different name.

He tried not to think about that. He failed.

Around midnight the barometer ticked upward half a millibar—high pressure building, stable air, five days of clear flying weather at least. Good conditions for a search. Good conditions for anything.

Casper folded the map, checked his fuel, his water, his spare parts, and slept badly in the truck bed with the ledger rustling overhead, and dreamed, for the first time in years, of water moving underground in the dark.

* * *

He fueled at the co-op station outside Puerto Deseado at nine the next morning, buying diesel in cash and packing it in jerry cans because the meseta had no stations and no forgiveness. The attendant, a boy of nineteen who topped up Casper's tires without being asked because Casper had once told him which week the spring winds would die down, chatted about football and asked nothing else, which was why Casper liked him.

He was screwing the last jerry can into the truck bed when the black Audi pulled in.

It came up the road too fast for the town, all gloss and torque in a place of dust and patience, and it parked across the lane from the pumps with the precision of a chess move. Two men got out. One stayed leaning against the passenger door with his arms crossed, doing nothing visibly except existing, which was enough. The other was small and neat and wore a linen jacket in a color that had never seen work, and he crossed the forecourt smiling like they were old friends.

"Señor Underhill." His accent was Buenos Aires polished smooth. "Casper Underhill. It's good to finally meet you in person. I'm Ferro's man in the south—you can call me Bruno. Everyone does."

Casper kept his hands on the jerry can. "I pay quarterly. Next payment's on the fifteenth."

"Oh, I know. I have the schedule memorized. It's one of the things I admire about you—reliability." Bruno glanced around at the empty plain with genuine appreciation. "Such space. Such silence. You could hear a debt collector coming from ten kilometers." He smiled again. "We're not here about the fifteenth, Señor Underhill. We're here about the future. Señora Ferro asks me to convey her continued confidence in you. She notes that you are a man who honors his obligations, and she hopes that whatever opportunities come your way in the coming weeks, you'll remain—how shall I say—*liquid*. Available. A man whose circumstances improve is a man whose payments improve, yes? She wants very much for your circumstances to improve."

The boy at the pump had gone very still, pretending to check a gauge.

"Tell her," Casper said, "that my circumstances are my own business."

"Of course they are." Bruno stepped back, hands raised pleasantly, and for a moment the pleasantness dropped and something older and colder looked out of his face, the way a river shows stones when the water shifts. "Everything is everyone's business eventually, señor. That's just hydrology." He tipped an imaginary hat. "Safe travels. The interior can be unforgiving this time of year. So many ways to disappear."

The Audi pulled away north. Casper stood in the diesel smell with his heart going slow and hard, doing arithmetic he didn't like.

They knew he was taking a job. They knew before he'd told anyone, which meant either Delgado's ministry leaked like a sieve or Ferro's fingers were already inside it—and either way, the words *so many ways to disappear* had not been a threat aimed at him. They'd been a promise aimed at someone else. Or an offer.

He finished securing the cans, climbed into the cab, and sat for a moment with the engine idling. Inside the visor the ledger hung in its tape, patient as gravity. Fifty-three thousand dollars. Triple rate. A bonus big enough to restructure everything, from a ministry that might belong to Ferro, hunting a team that had financed itself through shell companies nobody wanted named.

Every direction pointed somewhere bad. That was the thing about the dry side: there was no weather to blame, nowhere to hide, nothing between you and the consequences of your own heading.

Casper dropped the truck into gear and turned south, toward the mesas, toward the last known coordinates of Survey Team Kestrel, toward whatever the earth had taken and might, if he was lucky and unlucky in the right proportions, give back.

Behind him the dust hung in the still air for a long time, marking his passage—six hundred kilometers of it, a line drawn across country that kept no other records.

He hoped it would still be there to follow home.
