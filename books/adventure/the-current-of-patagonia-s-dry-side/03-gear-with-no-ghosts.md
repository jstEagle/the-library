## 3. Gear With No Ghosts

The camp announced itself a half-mile before I saw it, the way all human things do on the dry side: by what they interrupt. The wind had been running clean off the Andes for three days, combed flat and featureless across the steppe, and then it stuttered. Swirled. Picked up a thread of something that wasn't dust—nylon, maybe, or the ghost of coffee—and carried it to me like a rumor.

I topped the rise at noon and there it was in the shallow below: six dome tents in two neat rows, guy-lines taut, rainflies staked with the crisp geometry of people who expected to come back to them. A cook shelter strung between two trucks. A solar panel array angled south, panels dusty but unbroken. A flag on a fiberglass pole, the government crest faded to a pale bruise by eleven days of sun.

No smoke. No movement. No sound except the wind worrying the guy-lines into a low, tuneless hum, like the camp was trying to sing and had forgotten how.

I sat on the ridge with the engine off for a long time before I went down. That's a habit from the old life—reading a scene before you enter it, because the first thirty seconds are the only ones you'll ever get clean. In storm country you learn that a green sky doesn't mean a tornado is coming; it means one has already decided. You learn to look for what's missing instead of what's there.

What was missing here was everything a camp looks like after a disaster. There was no scatter. No torn fabric, no blood-darkened ground, no gear dragged in panic. The trucks were parked nose-out, which meant whoever left had left deliberately, in order, expecting roads. The tents were still zipped shut from the inside.

Eleven days overdue, the liaison had said. Gear intact at their last camp. No tracks leading out.

He'd undersold it. There were tracks leading out. That was the problem.

* * *

I walked the perimeter first, boots deliberate, hands loose. Old training. You don't approach an unknown site head-on; you circle it the way water circles a stone, learning its shape without committing to it. The wind came off my left shoulder and carried the camp's smell to me: canvas, diesel, sun-baked plastic, and underneath it something fainter and wrong—a mineral sourness, like water that had been sitting in rock too long.

The first tent was Dr. Okafor's. I knew this because the name was written on a strip of tape above the door flap in careful block letters, the way field scientists label things when they expect other people to need to know. Inside: a sleeping bag rolled tight, a foam pad squared to the floor, a folding table bearing a laptop closed over a dead battery, a paperback novel face-down on the pillow with a pencil marking her place. A mug on the table, rinsed clean, upside down to dry.

Nobody packs like that if they're leaving in a hurry. Nobody leaves a book mid-chapter if they think they're coming back tomorrow, either. It was the specific tidiness of a person who believed she'd return within hours, and it made the back of my neck prickle worse than any mess would have.

The second tent held two cots, both made. The third: more of the same, plus a hanging organizer of toiletries arranged by size. The fourth had collapsed at one corner where a stake had worked loose, and inside, a pair of reading glasses sat folded on a sleeping bag with their arms open, waiting for a face.

Five tents accounted for. The sixth stood slightly apart, upwind of the latrine trench, and its tape read REYES, I. Inside, the sleeping bag was unrolled but empty, and on the cot lay a short recurve bow, unstrung, and a quiver of arrows fletched in turkey feather and orange vinyl vane. Field archery kit. Small-game hunting, maybe, or just a scout who liked to keep her hands honest. I didn't touch it. Some part of my brain had already filed the bow under *information I might want later*, and another part noted that a team's scout is the last person who leaves her weapon behind.

The cook shelter told the same story in food. Under a tarp: crates of ration packs, still sealed. Jars of dulce de leche, a bag of yerba mate with the scoop standing upright in it, bread gone stale as pumice under a cloth. Water drums, four of them, two full enough to slosh when I rocked them. Eleven days and nobody had come back to eat. Either they'd found food elsewhere—which on the dry side means found *water* elsewhere—or they hadn't needed food where they went.

Or they couldn't come back at all.

I stood in the middle of the camp with the wind humming through the guy-lines and let myself say the thing I'd been not-saying since Esquel. Six people don't walk away from a camp like this. Six people don't leave water behind on the Patagonian steppe, where water is the only currency that matters. Whatever had pulled Team Kestrel out of their tents had pulled them out completely, confidently, and fast—

—and it had happened in an orderly fashion, because the tents were zipped, the gear was stowed, and nobody had even knocked over the damn mug.

Disasters are messy. This place was immaculate. Somehow that was worse.

* * *

The seismograph was in the second truck.

I found it because the truck's rear canopy was latched but unlocked, and when I swung the doors open, the smell of electronics hit me—warm circuit boards and lithium and the faint ozone tang of something working hard. In the shade of the canopy, strapped to a frame bolted through the bed, sat a portable broadband unit: sensor canister buried in a sandbag cradle, digitizer box blinking a steady green, and a ruggedized tablet tethered to it, screen alive, logging.

Eleven days on internal battery. Somebody had sized the power budget generously, or somebody had planned to be back soon, or both.

I climbed into the bed and looked at the display. The trace scrolled in real time along the bottom third of the screen—a flat, nervous line, the earth's small talk. Wind noise, mostly, and the micro-tremor of thermal expansion as the afternoon heated the ground. Normal. Boring. The kind of record that puts grad students to sleep.

Then I scrolled back.

The archive went back nineteen days—the whole deployment. For the first week, the trace was exactly what you'd expect from the dry side: quiet, flat, occasionally scribbled by wind gusts, one tidy event on day four that I recognized as a distant quarry blast, probably out of Esquel. Textbook background. I've spent enough of my life around instruments to read them like EKGs, and this was a healthy heart.

On day eight, at 0347 local time, the heart skipped.

It wasn't an earthquake. I've ridden out real ones, and they announce themselves with a sharp P-wave arrival, a slap, then the long rolling S-wave tail. This was different. This started low and *stayed* low—a slow, swelling displacement, the needle leaning like a man pushing against a door, building over ninety seconds to an amplitude that should have been impossible for a surface instrument in stable crust, then easing back over ten minutes to something like normal. Not a jolt. A *shift*. As if the ground had taken a breath and let it out.

And then it did it again. Day nine, 0412. Day ten, 0358. Day eleven, 0405. Once a night, always in the hour before dawn, always the same signature: lean, hold, release. By day fourteen the events were stronger, and the baseline between them had changed character—the flat line now carried a continuous low-frequency tremor, so faint you'd miss it unless you were looking, a hum beneath the hum, like a note sustained under the floorboards of a house.

I zoomed into the most recent event, two nights ago. The lean was bigger. The tremor between beats was louder.

Whatever was down there, it was getting closer to the surface. Or the surface was getting closer to it.

I sat in the truck bed with the tablet glowing in my hands and felt the old machinery start turning in my chest—that part of me I'd spent twelve years feeding gravel and keeping starved. The scientist. The idiot who used to lie awake loving data. Because this trace was beautiful, in the way that a wave on a radar screen is beautiful right up until you understand what it means for the ship, and every instinct I owned was screaming the same word:

*Karst.*

Not karst the way schoolchildren mean it—limestone caves, sinkholes in Florida lawns. This was older and stranger. The dry side sits on layered volcanics and sediments a hundred million years deep, and somewhere down in that stack there could be anything: a dissolved cavity system, a fault-breached aquifer, a void the size of a cathedral that had been holding its roof up out of pure geological stubbornness until something—recharge, pressure, time—changed the arithmetic. The breathing pattern, the pre-dawn timing, the slow amplification: that was a system responding to a gradient. Pressure finding a path. Water going where water goes.

Six hydrologists and geophysicists would have seen this trace on day eight and done exactly one thing.

They'd have gone down to look.

* * *

The tracks were on the northeast side of camp, and once I found them I couldn't believe I'd missed them coming in—I'd approached from the southwest, and the wind had been doing its work, but still. They led from the edge of the tents toward the mesas in a loose column: boot prints, at least six sets overlapping, some doubled back, a scuffed drag-line where someone had hauled something heavy. A sledge or a litter. My mouth went dry looking at it.

The prints headed for the mesa country—the broken rampart of rust-colored rock that stood up off the steppe maybe four kilometers out, all shadowed slots and tilted slabs, the kind of terrain that swallows light and people with equal indifference.

I followed them. The column held together for the first kilometer, prints crisp in the alkali flat, spaced like walking, not running. Then the ground rose into gravel, and the prints thinned, and the drag-line ended beside a flat boulder where someone had clearly rested—two boot prints planted side by side, facing the mesas, motionless long enough to press deep.

After that, one set continued alone.

One person, walking ahead of the others? Or the others' prints simply lost to the gravel while this one set survived because its owner was heavier, or walking slower, or last? I crouched over them and read what I could: a heavy man or a loaded man, right foot rolling outward, a boot with a lug pattern I recognized as standard government issue. The stride lengthened, then shortened, then lengthened again—someone tired, pushing, stopping, pushing.

Four hundred meters farther on, the gravel gave way to a sheet of exposed caliche, smooth as poured concrete, and the boot print crossed it and simply stopped.

Not faded. Not scattered by wind. *Stopped.* Mid-stride, heel dug deep, toe-off absent—as if the walker had lifted his foot for the next step and the next step had ceased to exist. Beyond the print, the caliche ran clean and unbroken to the base of the nearest mesa, two hundred meters of blank ground, and I walked every meter of it twice, bent double, reading stone like scripture, and there was nothing. No print. No scuff. No dropped gear, no blood, no sign of turning aside.

The caliche just took him. Or something else did.

I stood at the end of the trail with the sun dropping toward the mesas and their shadows stretching toward me like fingers, and I understood—with the cold, complete certainty that I used to feel in the last minutes before a tornado dropped, that animal clarity I'd traded my soul to chase—that I was looking at the place where six trained field scientists had walked into the earth.

Because that was the only explanation left. The seismograph said there was a way down. The tracks said they'd found it. The stopped print said the ground had opened and closed, patient as a trapdoor spider, and kept their secret.

And eleven days later, no one had come back up.

* * *

I got back to the camp as the light failed. I did the things you do: checked the trucks—one had fuel, one was dry; drained the water drums into my own containers because thirst kills faster than mystery; ate half a ration pack without tasting it. I strung my own small tent apart from theirs, because I'm not sentimental and I sleep better knowing whose fabric I'm inside.

Then I sat outside in the blue dark with the seismograph tablet balanced on my knees, watching the live trace scroll, and I waited for 0400.

The steppe at night does something to your sense of scale. The stars come down close enough to lean on, and the horizon dissolves, and you become very small and very loud—every breath, every shift of gravel sounds broadcast. Around midnight I heard the camp's guy-lines change pitch as the wind died entirely, and the silence that followed was so total I could hear my own pulse in my ears, and under it, faintly, absurdly, I could have sworn I heard the ground doing the same.

At 0351, forty minutes early, the needle leaned.

I watched it happen on the screen and felt it happen through my body at the same time—a slow tilt in the world, subtle as vertigo, the kind of motion your inner ear argues about. Lean. Hold. Somewhere out under the mesas, something vast exhaled. And then, riding on the release, so faint I had to press the tablet against my sternum to be sure I wasn't imagining it, the tremor came again—that sustained subterranean note, deeper than hearing, more felt than known.

Except this time it wasn't quite alone.

Underneath the hum, at the very floor of the spectrum, there was a new component. Rhythmic. Irregular. Almost organic. I switched the display to the raw waveform and stared at the tiny oscillations marching across the screen, and my mind, being a mind, insisted on translating them, insisting on the one interpretation I didn't want:

Footsteps. Many of them. Far below.

I killed the screen and sat in the dark with my heart going like a trapped bird, telling myself it was settling sediment, thermal contraction, wind loading on the mesa walls, any of the twenty boring explanations that a competent scientist would have listed first. Twelve years ago I would have listed them and believed them. Tonight I listed them and knew I was lying, because I'd spent my whole life listening to the atmosphere tell me what it was about to do, and I knew the difference between weather and intention.

Something down there was moving. Something down there had six visitors already.

I looked east, where the mesas stood black against the lesser black of the sky, and thought about the boot print that stopped, and the bow lying unstrung in Ilsa Reyes's empty tent, and the words the liaison had used in his airless office in Esquel: *no tracks leading out.*

He was wrong. There were tracks leading out. They just didn't lead anywhere a rescue convoy could follow—and if Ferro's people reached this camp before I understood what Kestrel had found, they wouldn't bother following them at all. They'd bring drills.

I packed the seismograph's tablet and its data cards into my jacket, against my chest, where a man carries the things he's decided to steal, and I turned my eyes toward the mesas, and the dawn came up gray and cold over the breathing ground.
