## 4. Ferro's Optimists

The office had no name on the door because the building had no doors with names—only numbers, and even those were assigned by a numbering system designed to be forgettable. Suite 1104 of the Torre del Sur in Puerto Madero looked out over the Río de la Plata, brown and patient as a ledger, and Marisol Ferro liked to begin her mornings watching it. Water that went nowhere. Water nobody owned. It calmed her before the day began asking who owned what.

She was forty-six years old and had the kind of face that made strangers confess things: soft at the jaw, unremarkable at first glance, built for being underestimated. She wore gray. She drank her coffee black from a paper cup she refilled three times until the cardboard went soft, a habit her analyst would have said was about scarcity and her mother would have said was about stubbornness, and both would have been wrong. It was about not leaving fingerprints on nice things.

At 7:40, Tomás Aldunate came in without knocking, which he could do because he'd earned it, and set a folder on her desk the way a man sets down something dead.

"Kestrel," he said. "Eleven days."

"Eleven days overdue?"

"Eleven days since anyone has heard anything. The ministry is calling it a communications failure. Satellite uplink down, solar interference, whatever story keeps them from admitting they've lost six people in the worst terrain in the country." He sat across from her, uninvited, and opened the folder. "Their last scheduled transmission was routine. Barometric readings, GPS ping, a note about equipment. Then nothing."

Marisol turned pages without reading them. She already knew what was in them; she'd written half of them herself, or rather, companies she controlled had written them, through lawyers who wrote other lawyers who wrote the permit applications that had put six government surveyors into the badlands east of the mesas with instruments capable of mapping what lay beneath them.

"And Casper Underhill?" she asked.

"Ferro—" Tomás began, in the tone he used when he wanted to slow her down, which was often, and which never worked.

"I asked about Underhill."

"He took the job. The liaison found him at a fuel stop outside Gobernador Costa. He negotiated for exactly one hour and then agreed, which tells you his finances are worse than we thought. He left this morning."

She nodded once and carried her coffee to the window. Below, joggers ran along the Costanera in the early light, small and earnest, burning energy for no reason anyone could profit from.

"You're worried," she said.

"I'm always worried. That's my function. But I'm specifically worried that we've hired a man to find a team whose discovery we financed, while our own acquisition vehicle is two days behind him, and none of these people know about each other except us."

"That's called structure, Tomás."

"It's called a house of cards standing sideways."

Marisol smiled. People found her smile warm, which was useful, because it wasn't. It was arithmetic. "Sit down properly. I want to walk through it, because you keep acting like we're doing something delicate, and delicate is the wrong word. What we're doing is simple. We just have to be precise."

He sat. He was a good man in the way that accountants are good men—honest within the frame he'd been given—and she valued him more than she valued most of the people who loved her.

* * *

Here was the shape of it, laid out over the next hour in her dry, unhurried voice, the voice she used for board meetings and confessions:

Three years ago, a junior minister in the provincial water authority had needed money—not much, by Buenos Aires standards, but enough—and a consultancy called HidroAustral Evaluaciones S.A. had offered him a consulting retainer for work that consisted of signing things. HidroAustral was owned by a holding company in Montevideo, which was owned by a trust in Luxembourg, which answered, in the end, to a portfolio managed out of an office in Zurich where the man who signed the letters had never heard of Patagonia and did not need to. The chain existed so that no one along its length knew what they were really buying, and everyone along its length got paid.

What they were really buying was a permit.

Not a drilling permit—that would come later, and would need a different minister—but a survey permit. Permission for a scientific team to spend fourteen months mapping subsurface water across four hundred kilometers of the rain-shadow badlands, using public funds, publishing eventually through official channels, and producing, as a byproduct of honest science, the single most valuable document in Argentina.

"The optimists," Marisol said, "believed there might be something under the mesas. I am not an optimist. I read the same geological surveys everyone else did—the old oil company cores from the fifties, the gravity anomaly data, the folk knowledge of the estancieros whose wells never went dry in drought years. The evidence suggested a fossil aquifer. Not a possibility. A likelihood. Optimism is for people who haven't done the reading."

Tomás made a note he didn't need. It was a ritual between them, giving her monologues the dignity of minutes.

"So we financed the permit so that the survey would happen, and we waited for the survey to tell us how big it was. That's all. We didn't corrupt science. We funded it. There's a difference, and the difference is that the science is real. Whatever Kestrel found down there, they found honestly. I've never had any use for fake data—it's worthless precisely because it's fake. Real data is the only thing worth stealing."

"And now they've gone silent."

"Now they've gone silent, which means one of three things. One: they're dead, in which case the data is in their camp or buried with them, and we recover it and mourn publicly and generously. Two: their equipment failed, in which case they'll surface eventually and we simply need to be the first friendly face they see. Three—" she paused, and the pause was the only theatrical gesture she permitted herself, "—they found something so large that Dr. Okafor decided the world wasn't ready to know about it yet. In which case they are sitting on it, guarding it, and every day they wait makes them harder to move and easier for someone else to find."

"Someone else being?"

"The ministry itself, if they stop believing the satellite story. A rival fund—I can name three that have been asking quiet questions about southern water rights since January. Or a journalist." She said the last word the way other people said *lawyer*. "Elena Brandt has published twice on water concessions in the south. She's careful, she's credible, and she has sources I haven't been able to buy, which annoys me professionally."

Tomás leaned forward. "And Underhill? Where does he fit? Because I've read his file, Marisol, and the file reads like a cautionary tale. Falsified flood forecasts in the north, eleven years ago. The town. The inquiry. He was never convicted—the evidence chain conveniently degraded—but he was finished, and he knew it. He's been living out there under a name that isn't quite his name, chasing storms for ranchers and insurance assessors, drinking too much at fuel stops, owing money to—"

"To us," Marisol said. "Owing money to us. Through three intermediaries, but ultimately to us. Forty-one thousand dollars, accruing interest he cannot possibly service, which is not a debt, Tomás. It's a leash. And today the leash did its job. When the liaison offered him triple rate, he couldn't say no. A free man might have said no. A free man might have smelled the setup—a government job appearing exactly when a syndicate needs a finder. Underhill can't afford to smell anything."

"You think he suspects?"

"I think he's smart, tired, and cornered, and cornered men notice everything except the trap they're already inside." She turned back to the window. The river had brightened; a container ship was crawling toward the port, riding low with cargo she would never ask about. "I don't need him to suspect or not suspect. I need him to find them. Everything after that is negotiation."

* * *

The negotiation, as she framed it to Tomás, had three possible endings, and she ranked them by preference the way she ranked everything: cleanly, without sentiment.

The best ending: Kestrel was alive, intact, and frightened. Frightened scientists were the easiest people in the world to deal with, because they understood risk. You arrived with doctors, helicopters, warm food, and a contract. You explained that the data belonged, legally and procedurally, to the consortium that had financed the permit—this was even true, in the sense that everything was true if you read the documents carefully enough. You gave them careers, protection, co-authorship on terms they'd sign without reading. Scientists told themselves stories about integrity, but integrity was expensive, and Marisol had never met anyone who could pay its price indefinitely.

The acceptable ending: Kestrel was dead. Then the data was inert matter—hard drives, notebooks, film—in a camp that belonged to no one, and recovery became logistics rather than persuasion. She would fund a memorial. She was genuinely good at memorials. There would be scholarships. Okafor's name on a research station, someday, if the body was ever found.

The ending she did not allow herself to think about, except that she thought about it constantly, in the way a pilot thinks about engine failure: Kestrel alive, defiant, and already talking. Already transmitting. Already writing to Elena Brandt or some ministry official with a conscience, in which case the value of the discovery began decaying the moment it entered another person's mind, because information shared is information priced, and priced information is information fought over, and fights over water in a country with water anxiety were not business disputes. They were politics. And politics, unlike business, could not be bought—only survived.

"So the convoy," Tomás said. "Two days behind Underhill. Armed contractors, medical staff, drilling permits signed by our friend the minister. Walk me through the legal theory again, because when you say it fast it sounds insane."

"The legal theory is that there is no legal theory. The convoy is a rescue operation. That's what the paperwork says—private response to a missing government expedition, coordinated with provincial authorities who will stamp whatever we put in front of them because our friend signs their budgets. If we find survivors, we rescue them. If we find data, we secure it as evidence, pending review. Every word defensible. Every word a lie. That's not insanity, Tomás. That's how the country actually works. I'm not inventing anything. I'm just faster than the people who'd do it slower."

"And if Underhill gets in the way?"

She considered the question honestly, which was her private discipline: to consider the ugly questions honestly, alone, before anyone forced her to consider them publicly.

"Casper Underhill is a man who falsified a flood forecast because someone above him leaned on him, and then spent twelve years paying for it in a place where it doesn't rain. Do you know what that tells me? It tells me he's not corrupt. Corrupt men sleep fine. He's weak—he was weak, under pressure, once, catastrophically—and weakness like that doesn't repeat. Men like him overcorrect. He will follow the rules of whatever game he thinks he's playing with a rigidity that would embarrass a judge." She tapped the folder. "Which is why he'll bring me the data. Because bringing me the data is what the game says to do. His debts vanish. Everyone lives. The file closes. He gets to go back to his instruments and his silence."

"And if the game changes?"

"Then I'll change it back." She said it lightly, and meant it completely, and watched Tomás decide not to write that down.

* * *

At noon she took the call she'd been waiting for, from a man named Reyes—no relation, as far as anyone could prove, to any Reyes in Patagonia—who handled the syndicate's interests in the south and reported in the flat tone of someone paid to have no opinions.

"The advance team crossed the Chubut line at dawn," he said. "Six vehicles. They'll stage at the last fuel stop and go off-grid from there. The minister's office confirmed the rescue authorization this morning. Radio silence until contact."

"Any movement from the north?"

"The rivals you mentioned. Two of them have chartered aircraft into Esquel. Sightseeing, officially."

"Sightseeing," Marisol repeated. Outside her window, the river kept moving its brown water toward the sea, carrying silt from places she would never see, past cities that drank it without asking where it came from. Everyone downstream of everyone. That was the whole world, if you looked at it correctly. "Keep me informed at six-hour intervals. And Reyes—if the survey team makes contact with anyone, anyone at all, before our people reach them, I want to know within the hour. Not the content. Just the fact. The fact is what I price."

She ended the call and stood for a long time at the glass.

Tomás, gathering his papers, watched her and made his one mistake of the day, which was kindness. "You could still unwind it," he said. "Let the government find them. Let it become public the slow way. We'd take losses, but we'd take them clean. There are other assets. There's always other—"

"Do you know what I was doing eleven years ago, Tomás?" She didn't turn around. "I was in Santa Fe. The floods. My family lost the farm—my father drowned in his own field, thirty meters from the house, because the forecast said the levees would hold and the forecast was wrong. Wrong because a man upstream falsified it. I read the inquiry transcripts. All nine hundred pages. I know the name on the signature line, and I know the name he uses now." She turned, and her face was doing something Tomás had never seen it do, which was nothing at all. "So understand me. I am not in this for the aquifer. The aquifer is money, and money is a tool. I am in this because somewhere under those mesas is the largest reserve of fresh water on the continent, and I intend to decide—with my own hands, in broad daylight, with my name on the documents—who it saves and who it drowns. No more forecasts. No more men upstream. Never again."

Tomás stood very still. Somewhere below, the city went on making noise.

"And Underhill?" he asked quietly. "When this is over. What happens to him?"

Marisol picked up her soft paper cup, found it empty, and set it down with care, the way you set down a thing you intend to use again.

"That depends entirely," she said, "on what he finds first."

* * *

That evening, in a rented room above a fuel stop in Gobernador Costa, Casper Underhill taped a new page inside his visor—a page with a number on it three times larger than any number he'd ever owed—and drove north into the dark with his weather instruments humming on the passenger seat, toward a silence in the badlands that Marisol Ferro, four hundred kilometers away, was already pricing.

She would learn, later, that he had checked the wind before leaving. That he had stood in the lot with his anemometer raised like a man taking a blessing, and had turned his truck not north toward the mesas, as the liaison's map instructed, but east, first, for eleven minutes, for reasons he wrote down in a notebook no one paid him to keep.

By then, of course, it would be too late to ask him why.
