## 5. Into the Badlands

The road ended forty kilometers past the fuel stop, and after that the land stopped pretending to be anything but what it was.

Casper had driven the dry side for three years and had a private taxonomy for its roads. There were roads, which were graded gravel and could be trusted in daylight. There were tracks, which were two ruts and an argument. And then there was whatever this was—a suggestion of passage across the bajada, marked by cairns some shepherd had stacked before either of them was born, half-swallowed by the moving skin of the ground. The truck's front wheels dropped into a hollow he hadn't seen until they were in it, and the chassis slammed down hard enough to rattle the anemometer mast lashed to the bed.

"Easy," he told the truck, because there was no one else to tell.

He'd left at first light with water for ten days, diesel in jerry cans, spare belts, two spares plus the tire that had already been patched twice, and the liaison's folder riding shotgun like a passenger who wouldn't stop staring at him. Six people. Eleven days overdue. Gear intact, no tracks out. He'd read the file four times on the drive south from Gobernador Costa and each reading had produced the same arithmetic: teams don't vanish from intact camps unless something made leaving impossible or staying necessary, and neither option appeared anywhere in the government's tidy incident report.

The wind data did appear in it, though—buried in an appendix nobody had annotated, because the liaison clearly hadn't understood it and Casper suspected the survey team hadn't fully understood it either.

That was why he'd come this way instead of following the obvious line toward the mesas where the boot print had been reported stopping. He was chasing weather that shouldn't exist.

* * *

The first anomaly showed up at noon on the first day, and it was so small he almost drove past it.

He'd stopped to check the mast—the corrugations were punishing every weld—and while he stood there with a wrench in his hand, the wind died. Not eased. Died. One second the zonda was pushing grit against his back at fifteen knots, steady as a machine, and the next the air went still and warm and close, like stepping into a mouth. He'd lived around storms long enough to know what a dead calm meant: it meant air going somewhere else, fast, and taking its pressure with it.

Then the stillness turned cool. Not much—two degrees, maybe three—but on the dry side, where the afternoon heat came off the stones like a held breath released, two degrees felt like a hand laid flat on your forehead. It lasted ninety seconds. Then the zonda snapped back and the world resumed cooking him.

Casper stood in the grit with the wrench forgotten in his hand and felt something turn over in his chest that he hadn't felt in twelve years.

He got the laptop out of the cab, pulled the last hour of telemetry off the logger, and looked at the trace. There it was: a temperature dip of 2.4 degrees Celsius, a humidity bump of six percent, a pressure wobble too small to be a front. Duration ninety-one seconds. And underneath all of it, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, the barometer had ticked upward during the cool spell.

Air rising cools. Everyone knows that. But rising air has to come from somewhere, and on the dry side of Patagonia there was nowhere for it to come from except the ground—and the ground out here was stone, dust, and thorn scrub that hadn't seen real rain since before Casper started lying for a living. Cool, humid air rising out of rock that should have been baking dry.

He sat in the cab for a long time with his hands on the wheel, not driving.

*Don't*, said the voice that had kept him alive for twelve years. *It's nothing. A seep. A cave venting. Drive to the camp, find the answer in a footprint, get paid, pay Ferro, keep breathing.*

But another voice, older, rusted, buried under the ledger taped inside his visor, said: *Ninety-one seconds. That's a column of air. Columns need a source.*

He drove on. He caught himself doing math at the wheel—buoyancy estimates, volume flux, the kind of back-of-envelope work he used to do for fun, before fun became a liability—and had to make himself stop.

* * *

The second day the terrain began eating vehicles in earnest.

It started with the track itself dissolving. What had been ruts became braided channels of loose scree where the truck floated sideways when he gave it throttle, tires spinning up dust that hung in the dead air like smoke in a sealed room. Then a dry wash took the track entirely, and he spent an hour walking it on foot, reading the banks the way he used to read cloud decks, before committing to a crossing that bottomed out the suspension with a sound like a bone cracking.

In the early afternoon the patched tire let go. He changed it standing in heat that came up through his boot soles, sweat evaporating off his forearms before it could bead, and while he worked he noticed the light doing something wrong.

Shimmer. Heat shimmer over the badlands was normal—it made the mesas swim and waver all day. This was different. Off to the northwest, maybe eight kilometers out along a line of low broken hills, the air above a particular fold in the terrain wasn't shimmering. It was *moving*. A slow vertical drift, visible only because the dust motes in it rose instead of fell, a column perhaps two hundred meters wide, climbing into the blue without any thermal source he could account for. Every other patch of ground was throwing heat straight up; this one was lifting air that had no business being lift-able.

He got the binoculars out. Through the glass the column resolved into fine dust rising in a slow spiral, patient as something breathing in its sleep.

Casper lowered the binoculars and looked at his own hands on the glass. They were shaking, and not from the heat.

He knew this feeling. He'd spent twelve years making sure he never felt it again. It was the feeling from before—the night drives across Oklahoma wheat, the green glow on the western horizon, the anemometer screaming, the sense that the atmosphere was about to show him something no human being had ever stood close enough to see. Wonder, the textbooks called it. His ex-wife had called it the sickness. His creditors called it an asset. Casper had called it the thing that killed a town in Kansas and then kept living comfortably inside him ever since.

He put the binoculars away and got back under the truck, where the only wonder available was whether the spare would hold.

* * *

That night he camped in the lee of a basalt dike and ran the numbers properly for the first time in years.

He set the logger to sample every ten seconds, rigged the mast upright on the truck bed, and sat outside with the laptop balanced on a fuel can, watching live traces scroll. The zonda blew all evening, hot and gritty, scouring everything. At 21:40 the wind died again. Ninety seconds of stillness. Cool air, humid air, pressure tick. At 23:15 it happened again. At 01:50. At 04:30. Roughly every two and a half hours, with metronome patience, the desert exhaled.

Not random convection. Random doesn't keep time.

Casper stared at the screen until the battery warning blinked, and the scientist he'd buried climbed out of the grave and started talking, and this time Casper didn't have the strength to shovel dirt back over him.

Tidal. That was the word forming in the back of his skull. Barometric tides—global atmospheric oscillations driven by solar heating, arriving in semi-diurnal waves. They modulated surface pressure everywhere on Earth, twice a day, small and predictable. But this wasn't twice a day. This was every hundred and fifty minutes, which was—

Which was nothing atmospheric. Which was closer to the period of water sloshing in a very large basin.

He shut the laptop. Sat in the dark. Somewhere north, a coyote ran a long note down the scale and quit.

*There is water under you,* said the rusted voice. *A lot of it. And it moves.*

He thought about the appendix in the liaison's folder—the seismic trace Kestrel's team had been running, the one still ticking on battery at their abandoned camp, pointing at something below. He thought about a boot print that walked toward the mesas and simply stopped, as if the ground had opened a door.

He thought about Ferro's two weeks, nine days gone now, and about what a man does with knowledge that someone is willing to kill for before he even understands it himself.

Casper banked the fire, checked the tie-downs on the mast, and lay in the cab with the folder open on his chest, reading Kestrel's last positional fixes by headlamp. Their route bent northwest on the final day. Toward the broken hills. Toward the breathing column of dust he'd watched through the glass.

They hadn't wandered off their survey line. They'd followed it exactly where it led.

* * *

The third day the truck nearly died for good, and Casper stopped fighting the land and started listening to it.

The transmission began whining on the climbs around mid-morning, a thin metallic complaint that meant fluid running hot and low, and by noon he was picking his lines like a man crossing ice—momentum over brute force, always rolling, never stopping on a grade, because if he stopped here the truck might not start again and then the desert would simply add him to its inventory. He passed the ribcage of some earlier traveler's vehicle, decades dead, stripped to its frame and half-buried, and saluted it with two fingers off the wheel.

The broken hills grew. From eight kilometers they became four, then two, and the closer he came the stranger the country looked. Scrub thickened in the drainages—greener than it had any right to be, tufts of coarse grass where grass didn't grow, a stunted ñire tree leaning out of a crack in the rock with actual leaves on it. In a land where everything survived by hugging the ground, things here stood up.

And the air changed. Not in gusts, not in fronts, but as a gradient. Every kilometer brought another degree of coolness, another breath of moisture, until by late afternoon he was driving through air that smelled faintly of wet stone, in the middle of the driest landscape on earth, and the hair on his arms stood up because his body recognized the smell before his mind consented to.

Spring air. Cave air.

He topped a rise and the column was right in front of him.

Up close it wasn't a column at all but a broad, slow circulation over a shallow basin between two folds of dark basalt, perhaps half a kilometer across, dust rising through it in lazy spirals and cool air sinking down the flanks to feed it. A microclimate the size of a town square, running on nothing visible, humming along in defiance of every synoptic chart he'd ever drawn. He parked on the rim, engine ticking, and just watched it work for twenty minutes while the sun went orange and long.

Then he noticed the ground inside the basin.

The dust there moved differently—not with the wind, which was light, but in small radial shivers, converging inward toward the center of the basin like iron filings around a magnet. As if the whole floor of the place were gently inhaling.

Casper got out. Walked down into the bowl with the handheld anemometer, watching the numbers do impossible things—outflow at the rim, inflow at the center, a circulation cell anchored to a point on the ground. He followed the inflow on foot, quartering the basin the way he'd once quartered storm cells, until he found it: a depression thirty meters across, floored in fine pale silt, ringed by stones that were dark with damp at their bases.

Damp. In the rain shadow. Stones wearing moisture like jewelry.

At the center of the depression the silt was disturbed. Not by animals—he knew their sign, and this wasn't it. The disturbance was geometric. Boot prints, several sets, days old, hardened by wind into sharp-edged reliefs. Rope burns on a rock lip at the near edge. And cut into the silt beside the largest print, deliberate and deep, a mark he recognized from the liaison's folder: the survey team's station stamp, a stylized kestrel in flight.

Below the rock lip, the ground simply wasn't there. A fissure opened into darkness, breathing. He could feel it on his face—cool, moist air flowing steadily *upward* out of the earth, carrying the mineral smell of deep water, enough of a current to stir the dust at his boots and send it spiraling away into the basin's great slow lung.

Casper knelt at the edge of the fissure with the anemometer in his hand and watched the needle hold steady at a number that meant a cave the size of a cathedral was exhaling into his face, and twelve years of carefully maintained numbness came apart all at once, quietly, like ice going out of a river.

Something down there was alive with water. Something down there had swallowed six surveyors—or sheltered them.

He leaned over the dark, cupped his hands, and shouted down into the breathing ground:

"Hello! Anyone down there? I'm looking for Kestrel!"

The fissure took his voice and gave nothing back but that cool, patient breath against his cheeks.

Then, from far below—so far it might have been imagination, might have been water moving through stone, but that landed in his chest like a struck bell—came a sound.

A voice. Answering.
