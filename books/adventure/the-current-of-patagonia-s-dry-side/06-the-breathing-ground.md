## 6. The Breathing Ground

The wind changed at three in the morning, and Casper woke in the cab of his truck with his heart already hammering before his mind caught up to why.

He'd spent twelve years training himself to sleep through wind. Patagonia's dry side never stopped talking—wind was the permanent tenant of this country, rattling the truck, hissing through the coirón grass, sanding down the mountains a grain at a time. A man who couldn't sleep in wind couldn't sleep here at all. So he'd learned to file it away, the way a dockworker learns to sleep beside a rail yard.

But this was different. The wind had *stopped*—and in its absence, something else was moving. A slow exhalation, rhythmic, patient, coming from the northeast where the mesas stood in their black ranks against the stars. Cool air. Air that smelled, faintly and impossibly, of wet stone.

Casper sat up in the dark, fumbled for his anemometer on the passenger seat, and watched the readout: 0.4 meters per second. Then 0.1. Then a dead flat zero that the instrument hadn't shown in the four years he'd owned it. The needle wasn't just calm. It was being *overridden*—pulled, gently, toward the mesas, like a tide going out.

He'd spent three days driving into terrain that ate vehicles, following exactly this kind of anomaly. A cold current rising out of nowhere. Barometric pressure that hiccuped on a schedule. Humidity readings that belonged to a coastal rainforest and not to the driest ground in the hemisphere. He'd told himself it was instrument error, then thermal inversion, then some trick of the basalt canyons funneling air from the Cordillera two hundred kilometers west. He'd told himself a lot of things, because the alternative was a feeling he hadn't let himself have in twelve years—the feeling of standing at the edge of a map and finding out the map was wrong.

Now the land was breathing, and there was no explaining it away.

* * *

He drove the last eight kilometers with the lights off, by starlight, feeling the track more than seeing it. The truck's tires whispered over caliche hardpan. To his left the ground dropped away into a shallow valley, and as he crested the rise he saw it: a stain of darkness in the valley floor, denser than the night around it, and above it a pale shimmer that moved.

Fog. Actual fog, in a country where the annual rainfall could be measured in the tears of the occasional passing cloud. It rose out of the ground in a slow column, unfurled at head height, and drifted apart in the starlight like breath on a winter window. Then it gathered again. Rose. Drifted. Gathered.

The sinkhole was breathing.

Casper parked behind a low rise, killed the engine, and sat listening to the silence that wasn't silence. Under the wind's absence there was a sound he couldn't name at first—low, diffuse, almost geological. Then it resolved, and the hair on his arms stood up.

Voices.

Not one voice. Several. Muffled by stone and distance, rising through the ground the way the fog did, in the same slow rhythm. Words he couldn't make out. A woman's voice, clipped and precise, giving instructions. A man answering. The murmur of more people than two.

Eleven days overdue. Gear intact, no tracks out. A boot print walking toward the mesas and stopping as if the earth had opened and swallowed the walker whole.

Casper sat in the cab for a long time, one hand on the keys, and had the honest conversation with himself he'd been avoiding since Esquel. He could turn around. He could radio the liaison and report a sinkhole and a search area, let the government send its own people down into the dark, collect his fee, and go back to being a man who measured wind for ranchers and owed money to people named Ferro. That was the sane play. That was the play of a man who had learned, at terrible cost, that the middle of things was where people died.

But the voices were alive. And he had spent twelve years listening to instruments tell him about water that wasn't there, rain that wouldn't come, rivers that would stay inside their banks. He had built his whole second life on the principle that the land lies, that hope lies harder, and that the only safety is in expecting nothing.

The ground was breathing. The ground was *full of water*. He could feel it now, the way you feel a storm before it shows on radar—a pressure in the ears, a coolness on the skin, a wrongness that was also, unmistakably, a wonder.

He got out of the truck.

* * *

The descent into the valley was a study in trust. He went on foot with a headlamp turned down to its lowest red setting, picking his way through broken basalt, and the fog thickened around him until the stars went out and the world contracted to a sphere of gray three meters across. The air was cold and clean and tasted of minerals. His instruments, clipped to his pack strap, were going quietly insane: humidity climbing past ninety percent, temperature falling six degrees in fifty meters, a barometric pulse so regular he timed it against his own heartbeat and found the ground breathing slower than he was. Once every forty seconds. In, out. In, out.

The sinkhole itself was thirty meters across, a perfect bowl in the basalt, rimmed with a rind of white mineral crust that his lamp turned the color of old bone. The fog poured upward out of it in a smooth column, and the walls dropped away below into darkness that his light couldn't reach.

He lay on his stomach at the rim and looked down.

Forty meters down, maybe fifty, a faint glow moved along a ledge—a lantern, or a headlamp, small as a firefly. And beneath the glow, echoing up the stone throat of the sinkhole with the fog, the voices resolved into words.

"—second pump's drawing sediment, we can't keep filtering it—"

"Then we don't drink it. We have the condensers. We have *time*."

"Adaeze, we have four days of condenser capacity and two people who can't walk—"

The voice that cut through then was the woman's, the clipped one, and it carried up the shaft with the authority of someone who had been giving orders for a long time and intended to keep doing it. "Then we ration, we hold, and we wait. Nobody goes to the surface alone. That was the decision and it stands."

Casper exhaled slowly. Survey Team Kestrel. Alive, organized, rationed, and hiding—hiding, he thought, from exactly the question he'd driven three days to ask.

He was still processing it when the cold point touched the back of his neck.

* * *

"Don't move."

The voice was a woman's, young, and very steady. The point at his neck was narrow and hard—not a knife. An arrowhead. He knew the weight of it somehow, the way it sat against the skin without the flat menace of a blade: a thing designed to go through him, held by someone who hadn't yet decided.

"Slowly," the voice said. "Roll onto your back. Hands where I can see them. Do it like you're tired, because if you do it fast, I get tired too, and then this goes through your throat."

Casper rolled over, hands out. Above him, silhouetted against the fog-glow, stood a slight figure with a drawn bow, crouched in the stance of someone who had been holding that stance for a long time and could hold it longer. Her face was a shadow with two bright eyes in it.

"You're the first one who came quiet," she said. "That's the only reason you're not dead. The last truck that came up this valley had four men in it and a winch on the front, and they spent two hours taking core samples off my sinkhole rim like they owned the deed." The bow didn't waver. "Ferro's people. Drilling permits, satellite uplink, the whole circus. So before you say one word, you're going to tell me who you are, and you're going to say it slow."

"My name's Casper Underhill." His voice came out rougher than he intended. He hadn't said the name aloud to a stranger in— he did the math and stopped doing the math. "I'm a private locator. The government liaison in Esquel hired me to find your team. Six people, eleven days overdue. That's the whole job."

"Underhill." She said it like she was tasting it for tampering. "The liaison sent one man. Alone. In an unmarked truck."

"The liaison sent a man who works cheap and doesn't ask questions," Casper said. "I'm both."

"Convenient."

"It's the most honest thing about me." He kept his hands wide and still, and made himself breathe the way you breathe when a storm is deciding whether to kill you—slow, deliberate, giving the sky nothing to react to. "Look at my truck. Look at my gear. Rain gauges, anemometers, a barograph older than I am. I chase weather for ranchers and insurance adjusters. I'm not Ferro's. I didn't know who Ferro was until this month, and everything I've learned about her since has made me want to know her less."

The eyes above him narrowed. "Everyone says they're not Ferro's."

"Then don't take my word." He moved, slowly, two fingers toward his chest pocket, watching the arrowhead track him. "Wallet. Left breast. Take it yourself if you want. There's a government work order in there, signed, with the search authorization. There's also a fuel receipt from Gobernador Costa and a pawn ticket from Esquel, which I'd rather you not read, but life's unfair."

She didn't take the wallet. She crouched lower, keeping the draw, and studied him with the particular attention of a scout who had learned that everything on the surface of the earth was a possible lie. "You came at night. Alone. On foot, last two hundred meters, no radio call. Either you're very good or you're very careful, and Ferro's men were neither."

"Ferro's men came in daylight with a winch," Casper said. "I came in the dark because I've spent three days reading wind data that says there's a cold air source out here that shouldn't exist, and I wanted to see it before I told anyone it was real. That's the truth. It's not a good truth, but it's the one I've got."

For a long moment the only sound was the sinkhole breathing, the fog rising and falling, the murmur of the voices far below—Okafor's voice still going, patient, laying down the law of the underground. The woman with the bow watched him through all of it.

"You heard them," she said at last. It wasn't a question.

"I heard them."

"And you didn't radio it in."

Casper hesitated. It was the first hesitation she'd caught in him, and he saw her catch it, saw the arrowhead tilt a degree closer.

"Answer that one carefully," she said.

He thought about lying. He was, by his own private accounting, good at it—the way a man who has burned down one life gets good at building the scaffolding of another. But lying to a woman with an arrow at his throat, forty meters above her people, on the first night, seemed like a poor investment.

"Because I don't know yet who I'd be radioing it *to*," he said. "Because the liaison who hired me is too clean to be dirty and too quiet to be clean, and because a week ago a woman named Marisol Ferro told me I had two weeks to pay what I owe her, and yesterday I found out her company financed your team's original permit. So the chain of custody on the words 'I found them' has a hole in it, and I don't put my name on things with holes in them. Not anymore." He heard his own voice harden on the last words and didn't soften it. "I'm not your enemy, Ilsa Reyes."

Her breath caught—small, but he heard it. "How do you know my name?"

"Your boot print," Casper said. "At the abandoned camp. Size seven, outside edge worn, left foot heavier than the right—you favor it. The print walked toward the mesas and stopped. I've been following a ghost for three days, and ghosts don't favor one leg." He let his hands lower an inch, testing. "You're the scout. You went out ahead, the ground opened, and you've been the door between your team and the surface ever since. Which means you're the one who decides who comes in. So I'm asking. Not telling. Asking."

The bow held. The fog breathed. Somewhere below, the lantern light moved along the ledge, and a man's voice drifted up, tired and joking, and was gently hushed.

Then, slowly, Ilsa Reyes lowered the bow—not all the way, the arrow still nocked, the string still half-drawn—but lowered, and crouched down to his eye level, and he saw her face for the first time: young, sun-cracked, with a healing cut across one cheekbone and eyes that had done too much deciding for one person.

"Here's how it is, Underhill," she said. "You're going to sit at the rim, in the open, where I can see you, until the sun's up. Then you're going to show me every piece of paper you own, and then—*maybe*—you're going to meet Dr. Okafor. And if at any point I think you've lied to me, or if I see so much as a satellite phone pointed at the sky, the ground that swallowed my team will swallow you too, and nobody will ever find the part of you that matters."

"Fair terms," Casper said.

"One more thing." She came closer, and her voice dropped to almost nothing, and what was in it wasn't threat anymore. It was something rawer. "The men with the winch. The drilling permits. You said Ferro's people were here *already*?"

"Two days ago, by your account. A full convoy left the north two days before that—armed, funded, carrying permits signed by a minister." Casper watched the words land, watched the scout's face do the arithmetic he'd already done alone in his truck at midnight. "They're coming back, Ilsa. Whatever your people found down there, the people who paid for the shovel are on their way to collect."

Ilsa Reyes looked down into the breathing dark, at the lantern moving on the ledge fifty meters below, at the small stubborn lights of the people she'd kept alive for eleven days. When she spoke again, her voice had gone flat and fast, the voice of someone converting fear into plan.

"Then you'd better come down," she said. "There's something you need to see. And there's something Dr. Okafor needs to decide—before the sun comes up, and before anyone else finds out what water is doing under this ground."

She stood, slung the bow across her back, and began fixing a rope to the mineral-crusted rim of the sinkhole, into the breathing fog, down toward the voices.
