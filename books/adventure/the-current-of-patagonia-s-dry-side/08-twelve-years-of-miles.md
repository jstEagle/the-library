## 8. Twelve Years of Miles

The fire was small by design. Ilsa had built it in a hollow of the sinkhole's rim where the rock itself seemed to swallow light, and even so Casper kept glancing at the sky above the cavern mouth, cataloguing cloud cover the way other men checked their wallets. Overcast. No moon. A fire this size would read as nothing from the air, nothing from the mesas, nothing from anywhere but the circle of stones where six exhausted people sat passing a pot of guanaco stew.

Below them, down the rope ladders and through the slot canyon and into the dark, the aquifer breathed. He could feel it even here—a faint cool exhalation rising from the earth, carrying the mineral smell of water that had never seen the sun. It was the same breath he'd followed for three days across the badlands, the anomaly in his wind data that had made no sense until it made all the sense in the world. Cool air rising from nowhere. A buried sea exhaling through the cracks of the world.

He ate slowly. The stew was good, better than it had any right to be, seasoned with some dried herb Ilsa had foraged from a wash. Around the fire, the wounded slept or pretended to sleep. Dr. Okafor sat apart, writing in a waterproof notebook by headlamp, her pen moving with the patience of a woman cataloguing the end of her career. The two men with the seep-water sickness—Voss and little Amaru—dozed propped against their packs, their skin still carrying the gray-yellow tinge of contamination.

Ilsa settled onto the rock beside him. She did it the way she did everything, without announcement, as though the space had always been hers and he was merely borrowing it. She held her tin cup in both hands and watched the fire with the flat attention she'd aimed at him when she'd first held him at arrow-point.

"You navigate like a local," she said.

"I don't."

"You do. Three days ago, in the sandstorm, when the GPS was useless and the compass was spinning lies off the basalt—you turned left. You turned left before the wind told you to. You turned left like you'd turned left there before." She sipped. "Nobody turns left like that unless they've been somewhere."

"Storm chasers read terrain. It's the job."

"Storm chasers read *weather*." She said it without heat, without accusation, just the clean precision of a scout who had spent eleven days overdue in a hole in the earth and had learned that imprecision killed. "You read weather like a man reading scripture. But terrain—you read it like memory. So." She turned her head and looked at him, firelight catching the scar through her eyebrow. "Why does a man who can smell a storm three hundred kilometers out hide in the driest place on earth?"

Casper looked at the fire. He had answers for this question. He kept them like the ledger taped inside his truck visor, organized, rehearsed, each one a small locked door. *I like the quiet. The dry air is good for the instruments. The land's honest.* He had given these answers at fuel stops and border crossings and once, memorably, to a woman in Esquel who had lasted five weeks before his silence wore her down.

But Ilsa had held an arrow on him and then fed him. She had carried Voss on her back for six kilometers. And she had asked the question the way she did everything—directly, and expecting a direct answer.

And maybe it was the fire, and the breathing dark below, and the strange liberty of being eleven days from anyone who knew his name. Maybe it was that he owed these people something no invoice could cover. Confession as currency. He'd heard Okafor use the phrase that afternoon, arguing with herself in the cavern, and it had lodged in him like a splinter.

"Have you ever been wrong," he said, "in a way that killed someone?"

Ilsa didn't flinch. She considered it, the way she considered everything, seriously. "I called a route once. Through a river canyon. Flash flood season. I read the sky wrong." She turned the cup. "Four of us on that route. The flood took the canyon an hour behind us. Nobody died." A pause. "But I know the sound a canyon makes when it's too quiet. I hear it sometimes when I'm falling asleep. So—yes. I've been wrong in a way that could have."

"Then you know the difference," Casper said, "between being wrong and being *made* wrong."

The fire popped. Down in the dark, the earth breathed.

"Twelve years ago I had a different name," he said. "And a different job. I was a hydrologist. Flood forecasting, mostly—river systems, catchment models. I was good. Not gifted, not a genius, just *good*, the kind of good that comes from caring about decimal places nobody else cares about. I could tell you what a river was going to do three days before it did it. There's a pride in that. There's also a weight, because when you're right, people live, and when you're wrong, people don't, and the model doesn't care which. It just runs."

He turned his cup in his hands. The metal was warm.

"There was a town. I won't name it. It doesn't matter—it's on maps, it rebuilt, there's a memorial with names on it that I've never gone to read because I already know them. Upstream of the town there was a dam. Old, but not old enough to be innocent. And upstream of the dam there was a mining consortium, and upstream of *them*—in the way that money is always upstream of everything—there were investors who needed the mine's output to keep moving, which meant they needed the dam's spillway schedule to stay generous, which meant they needed the flood forecasts to stay quiet."

Ilsa had gone very still. Not the stillness of the arrow—something else. The stillness of a woman who had followed a story into a hole in the ground.

"The season was wet. I mean *wet*—a rain year that came along maybe once in thirty years, the kind you dream about if you're a hydrologist and have nightmares about if you're honest. My models were screaming. Not ambiguous, not borderline. Screaming. A hundred-year event stacking on a saturated catchment with a dam that had a documented seepage problem." He laughed, and it came out wrong, dry as the land around them. "I wrote the report. I wrote it three times, and each time it got shorter, and each time the numbers got softer, and each time I told myself the same thing every coward tells himself: *someone else will catch it.* The regional authority. The dam inspectors. God."

"What did they promise you?" Ilsa asked. Not *did they threaten you*—she'd gone straight past that, to the thing underneath.

"Continuation," Casper said. "That's the currency they pay in. Not money, not exactly. Continuation. The next contract, the next season, the next version of the life you've built. They don't buy your soul in one transaction. They rent it, month to month, and every month the rent gets easier to pay because you've already paid it once and survived. You tell yourself you're the same man. You are not the same man. But the difference is small enough, day to day, that you can carry it." He looked into the fire. "Until the river comes."

The fire had burned down to a core of orange. Somewhere below, water moved through stone with a sound too deep to be a sound.

"The rain came on a Tuesday. The models I'd softened said peak flow Thursday, manageable, within spillway capacity. The river crested Wednesday night. The dam held—mostly. The seepage became a breach, the breach became a channel, and the channel went through the low quarter of town at two in the morning, when everyone was asleep, because that's what rivers do when cowards schedule their disasters." His voice stayed level. He'd practiced this levelness for twelve years; it was the one skill the desert had perfected in him. "Twenty-three people. Two of them were kids. The inquiry found the dam operators negligent, found the consortium's paperwork clean, and found that the forecast had been—here's the phrase they used—'*within acceptable modeling uncertainty.*'"

"And you?"

"I testified. That's the joke. I testified to the softened version, under my real name, with my real credentials, and I was very good at it, because I'd always been good at it. Nobody caught it. The inquiry closed. The consortium sent a bottle of something expensive and a note that said *difficult season, glad we weathered it together.*" He turned the cup over in his hands, watched the firelight die on the metal. "And I sat in my apartment with the note and the bottle and the newspaper, and I understood that I had gotten away with it, and that getting away with it was the sentence. Not prison. *This.* Walking around with it. Every forecast after that, every decimal place, knowing exactly what I was."

"What did you do?"

"I ran," he said. "Not dramatically. I didn't burn my papers or vanish into the mountains. I just—moved. Changed my name through channels that exist for people who need channels. Went somewhere no river could find me." He gestured, a small motion that took in the badlands, the mesas, the whole rain-shadowed emptiness pressing in around their fire. "The dry side. Twelve years of miles. I chased storms because storms don't drown towns—they just scare them, and scared is survivable. I kept instruments because the instruments don't lie, they just report, and reporting is the one thing left I trust myself to do. And I stayed poor on purpose, for a long time, because debt is a leash and I deserved the leash."

Ilsa was quiet for a long moment. The fire had sunk to coals. Around the camp, Okafor's pen had stopped; Casper couldn't tell if she was listening or if the silence was simply the silence of a tired woman. He found he didn't care. That was the strange thing about confession—once it left you, it stopped belonging to you, and you couldn't control who picked it up.

"Half a story," Ilsa said finally.

"What?"

"You said you'd tell me why you hide. That's half the story. The running. The falsified forecast. The town." She set her cup down on the stone with a small, deliberate click. "But there's a second half. Because a man who runs doesn't stop reading terrain like memory. A man who runs doesn't navigate the badlands in a sandstorm like he's coming *home*. You've been here before, Casper. Not this exact ground, maybe. But this country. Before the name change, before the debt, before all of it. Something in this desert knows you, and you know it, and you haven't said that part yet."

He looked at her. The coals lit the bottom of her face and left her eyes in shadow, and he thought: *she's a scout, this is what scouts do, they find the route you haven't shown them.*

"Another night," he said.

"Another night," she agreed, and there was no threat in it, only patience, and somehow that was worse. "We've got nothing but nights."

She rose, gathered the pot, and went to check on Voss and Amaru, her footsteps soundless on the stone. Casper sat alone with the dying fire and the breathing dark.

The truth was that she was right, and the truth was worse than the half he'd told. Because it hadn't been only the town that drove him south. It had been the year *after*—the year he'd spent, under his first name, with a survey crew in this same rain shadow, mapping dry washes that his instruments insisted had once run with water, standing on these same mesas and feeling the land hold its breath around something buried and vast. He had filed those findings and forgotten them, the way he'd been taught to file things—neatly, softly, within acceptable uncertainty.

And now the earth had exhaled, and a survey team had fallen into the crack, and a syndicate was coming with drilling permits, and every road out of this story ran through the one thing he had spent twelve years refusing to do: tell the truth, under his own name, and let the consequences come.

He was still staring into the coals when Okafor's headlamp clicked off, and in the sudden dark he heard her boots cross the stone toward him, unhurried, deliberate, stopping just at the edge of the firelight.

"Mr. Underhill," she said quietly. "We need to talk about what happens when your creditors find out where we are."

* * *

Casper looked up. Okafor stood with her notebook against her chest, a silhouette against the faint gray glow of the cavern mouth, and behind her voice he could hear it already—the far-off sound of engines on a horizon two days out, or three, or one. The earth breathed below them. The clock, which had been running the whole time he talked, did not care that he had finally told the truth.

"They won't find out," he said. "Not from me."

"Everyone tells me that," Dr. Okafor said. "And then the trucks arrive."
