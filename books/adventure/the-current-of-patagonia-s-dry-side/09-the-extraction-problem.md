## 9. The Extraction Problem

Dr. Adaeze Okafor had kept a journal for thirty-one years, through four continents and two marriages and the slow erosion of every certainty she'd started with, and in all that time she had never once written a lie into it. She thought about that now, sitting on a supply crate at the bottom of the world with a pen uncapped and nothing honest to say.

The light down here was wrong for writing anyway—a greenish, submarine glow that came from nowhere she could identify, filtering up through fissures in the limestone ceiling forty meters above. It made everyone look drowned. It made her look, when she caught her reflection in the still black water at her feet, like a woman she didn't recognize: sixty-one years old, gray at the temples, cheeks hollowed by eleven days of short rations and bad decisions.

She wrote instead the only true sentence available to her.

*Day twelve below. The water is older than we are. It does not care what we decide.*

She capped the pen. Around her, the cavern went about its business of being impossible.

The aquifer stretched beyond the reach of their lamps—she was sure of it now, sure in the way she'd been sure of only three or four things in her career. The seismographs Ilsa had rigged along the eastern gallery returned profiles that looked less like groundwater and more like an inland sea, a fossil ocean pinned beneath a hundred meters of Patagonian stone, untouched since before the Andes finished shrugging themselves skyward. Freshwater. Cold, clean, pressurized, ancient. Enough to green a desert the size of a country, or to buy one.

Enough, she thought, to get us all killed.

"Doctor." Tomás Vidal's voice came echoing up the gallery, hoarse with the sickness that had been working through half the team since day six. "Okafor. You should come see Nkem."

She was already moving. The sick bay—if you could call a ring of sleeping bags around a propane stove a sick bay—occupied a dry shelf near the sinkhole mouth, where the air moved and the fog came through at dawn smelling faintly of minerals and cold. Six of them lay there now. Nkem Obi, her own countryman, her field assistant of nine years, had drawn the worst of it: the contaminated seep water they'd been forced to drink in the first desperate days after the fall, before Ilsa found the clean vein deeper in. His fever climbed all morning and broke all night, and each cycle left him a little thinner, a little further from her.

She knelt beside him. His skin was hot and dry as paper. His lips had cracked and he'd stopped complaining about it, which frightened her more than the complaints ever had.

"Nkem." She touched his wrist. The pulse ran quick and shallow, a bird's heartbeat. "I'm going to need you to drink for me."

He opened his eyes. They were clear, which was worse than delirium—clear eyes meant he knew exactly how bad this was. "Adaeze." He almost never used her first name. "You have to stop deciding."

"I'm not deciding anything right now. I'm getting you water."

"You're deciding every hour you don't decide." A ghost of his old smile. "I know you. You're building a cathedral of options in your head and living in it. But the walls are closing, Doctor. You can feel it too."

She held the canteen to his mouth and let him drink, and said nothing, because he was right, and because admitting it out loud in front of the others would be the same as surrendering.

* * *

Casper Underhill arrived at the council fire looking like a man who'd been assembled from spare parts and bad weather.

Okafor watched him come down the scree slope with Ilsa a pace behind, and she took inventory the way she took inventory of everything: the man was maybe forty-five, lean in the way of someone who'd been hungry on purpose for a long time, sun-scoured, hands nicked and scarred. He carried himself like the terrain owed him money. When he reached the fire he didn't sit so much as lower himself onto a rock, and he looked at her with eyes that had done arithmetic on their situation before his boots had even stopped moving.

He'd been down here eighteen hours. In that time he'd mapped their exits, counted their rounds—Ilsa had told her, apologetically, that he'd asked—and drunk three cups of her tea without comment. Now he said, without preamble:

"We need to talk about moving them."

"No," Okafor said.

"They're dying down here."

"Some of them might die on the surface too. That's not the argument, Mr. Underhill, and you know it isn't. Sit down properly and let's do this honestly."

Something flickered across his face—surprise, maybe, or respect. He shifted on the rock. Ilsa settled cross-legged near him, her bow within reach out of habit rather than suspicion; that particular war had ended sometime in the night, Okafor judged, when Casper had spent two hours hauling water for the sick without being asked.

"Then let's be honest," Casper said. "Six of your people are down with seep poisoning. Two of them—Obi and the Dutch woman—are past what you can treat with a first-aid kit and willpower. You've got maybe ten days of food if you stretch it, less if the fever breaks appetites open again. Your radio is dead, your satellite phone is dead, and your location is a hole in the ground that took me three days of driving and a lifetime of luck to find. And there is a convoy coming."

"You told Ilsa. Ferro's people."

"Ferro's *rescue* team. Armed, funded, carrying drilling permits signed by somebody in Buenos Aires who's already been paid. They'll be here in two days, maybe three. They won't come down the sinkhole with blankets and soup, Doctor. They'll come down with survey equipment and lawyers' instructions, and everyone in this cave becomes either an employee or a problem."

"And your solution is to carry six critically ill people across fifty kilometers of badlands on improvised litters, exposed, slow, leaving a trail a blind man could follow." Okafor leaned forward. The firelight did its best with her face and gave up. "Mr. Underhill, I have run field operations on four continents. I have evacuated teams through civil wars, through cholera camps, through a flood season in the Delta that killed more people than the war did. I am telling you, with that experience behind me: a litter convoy over that terrain moves at perhaps two kilometers an hour. We would be on the surface for three days. Visible for three days. If Ferro's advance men reach the trucks before we do—and they will, because they have satellite imagery and we have a broken antenna—we are caught in the open with wounded people and no cover, and everything you're trying to protect gets taken anyway, along with six lives thrown away for nothing."

The words came out harder than she'd intended, and she saw them land. Good. Let them land.

"So what's your alternative?" Casper asked quietly. "Wait here?"

"Negotiate. From strength. They want the data. Fine—the data exists, and it exists in my head and in Ilsa's head and in encrypted files that will go public if any harm comes to this team. That is leverage. Men with permits still prefer deals to funerals. We stay hidden, we keep the site sealed, we make contact on our terms—"

"On whose terms? You'd be negotiating with the people who financed your permit through shell companies. You knew that, didn't you?" He wasn't gloating. That was the unsettling thing—he said it flatly, a fact among facts. "Ferro's syndicate built Kestrel. They didn't fund science, Doctor. They funded an option. And options get exercised."

"I'm aware of how my funding was structured." Her voice cooled several degrees. "I've been aware for some time."

Ilsa's head came up sharply. "You *knew*?"

And there it was. Okafor closed her eyes briefly. Twelve days underground, six people sick, an ocean under her feet, and now this—the thing she'd been carrying alone since Esquel, surfacing at the worst possible hour because honesty, once invited into the room, refused to wait politely by the door.

"I suspected," she corrected. "Then I confirmed. Eight months ago, before we deployed. I traced the permit chain back through two holding companies to a financial group registered in Puerto Madero. I couldn't prove it led to Ferro specifically—not then—but I could read the shape of it. Money that hides its face wants something."

"And you went anyway." Ilsa's voice was very quiet, which was how Okafor knew it was very angry.

"I went because the science was real and the window was open, and because I believed—God help me, I still believe—that whoever owned the paper, the findings belonged to the world. I told myself I'd handle whatever came. That I could outrun the structure." She spread her hands. "That was vanity. I'm owning it now, in front of both of you, because you deserve to hear it before you stake your lives on my judgment. My silence helped build this trap. Whether I stay or go, whether we fight or bargain—that debt is mine, and I'll pay it however it comes due."

The fire cracked. Somewhere deep in the eastern gallery, water dripped into water, a sound like a slow clock.

Casper surprised her. He didn't pounce. He sat with it for a long moment, turning something over, and when he spoke his voice had changed—lower, rougher, as though the words were being hauled up from somewhere below his usual depth.

"I falsified a flood forecast once," he said. "Twelve years ago. Under pressure from people who sign things. A town drowned. I've been hiding in the driest place on earth ever since, telling myself the same story you just told—next time I'll handle it, next time I'll be faster than the structure." He looked at her across the fire. "There is no next time, Doctor. There's only this time. And in this time, staying hidden isn't neutrality. It's just dying slower while they set up the drill rigs."

"That's not—" She stopped. Started again, because he deserved rigor, not reflex. "Moving them kills people, Mr. Underhill. That's not abstraction. Obi cannot survive a litter trek. I cannot sign off on an operation I believe will kill my own team to protect data that—"

"—that will protect everyone else's water for the next century." He shrugged. "Yeah. I know the math. I hate the math too."

"Do you?" For the first time, heat came into her voice, and she let it. "Do you actually understand what's under our feet? I don't think you do. None of us fully do. That water has been sitting there since before there were cattle in this province, before there were fences, before there were deeds. It is the largest unrecorded freshwater reserve in the Southern Hemisphere, and the only reason it still exists is that nobody knew. The moment it's known—truly known, publicly, legally—it stops being geology and starts being politics. Every government ministry, every agribusiness, every fund in three hemispheres will descend on it. Some will want to save it. Most will want to sell it. And the difference between those outcomes will be decided by who holds the evidence first, and whether the first public word spoken about this place is 'wonder' or 'asset.'" She jabbed a finger at the stone floor. "If we surface now, half-dead, chased, with Ferro's convoy on our heels, the first word will be theirs. They will frame the story before we can draw breath to tell it. Rescue turned rescuee. Surveyors who needed saving. A dangerous site, unstable, contaminated—did you know they'd love that angle? Contaminated seep water. We drank it ourselves; it's in our medical logs. They'll wave our own injury reports as proof the aquifer is compromised, worthless except to them, and no commission on earth will have the stomach to fight a syndicate's lawyers over poisoned brine."

Silence. Even the drip in the far gallery seemed to pause.

"She's right about the framing," Ilsa said slowly. "I hadn't... the medical logs. God. They could use Nkem's fever as evidence."

"They could use anything," Casper said. "But dead men use nothing." He rubbed his jaw, and Okafor watched him wrestle it down—the argument, the anger, whatever engine drove a man to drive three days into terra that eats vehicles for strangers he'd never met. "Okay. New proposal. Not your plan, not mine. Both."

"Go on."

"The sick can't walk and can't be risked in the open. Agreed. So we don't move the sick yet. We move the *problem*. I take the data out—real data, copies, encrypted—and I take one healthy person, and we move fast and light tonight, ahead of the convoy, and we put the evidence somewhere it can't be buried no matter what happens to the rest of you. Meanwhile you seal this site. Collapse the sinkhole approach if you can rig it. When Ferro's people arrive, they find a camp of survivors guarding a hole full of nothing they can prove. No instruments running, no maps lying around, nothing but sick people and rock. Their permits are for extraction, not excavation-by-hand. Without the data, they're standing on a rumor."

"And you?" Okafor asked. "Where does the data go?"

He was quiet a moment. "There's a journalist. Elena Brandt. Esquel. I've been feeding her small truths for years—never enough to burn me, always enough to keep her sharp. She's the only person I know who can't be bought, mostly because nobody's figured out what price she'd even understand." A dry, humorless almost-smile. "The data goes to her. Public record. First word spoken about this place is 'wonder.'"

"You'd trust a reporter with an ocean?"

"I'd trust her with it before I'd trust a minister, a syndicate, or myself." He met her eyes. "And Doctor—I'd trust *you* with the decision. That's why I'm asking instead of just doing it. But I need your answer tonight. The clock doesn't care about our process."

Okafor looked into the fire and felt the cathedral of options, her life's great architecture, groaning at last under its own weight.

Every instinct she'd honed in thirty-one years of fieldwork said hold, consolidate, protect the vulnerable, negotiate from position. Every instinct, and underneath it, she could finally admit, a colder older thing: the leader's terror of the operation that fails on her signature. She had buried team members before. She knew the exact weight of the report she'd have to write, the exact tone of the inquiry, the exact way a career ends when the math goes wrong.

But Casper was right about one thing, and it was the thing she'd been circling for twelve days: there was no version of this where waiting kept her hands clean. Staying hidden was a choice. Silence was a choice. She had made it once already, eight months ago in Esquel, reading the permit chain by lamplight, and the bill for that silence was walking toward her across the badlands at this very moment with drilling permits in its glovebox.

"Nkem was right," she said aloud. "I've been deciding not to decide."

"Doctor—"

"Here is my answer." She stood, and her knees cracked, and she let them. "We split the difference, but not the way you proposed. The data goes out tonight—you, Ilsa, the fast route, Brandt in Esquel. That part stands; I authorize it. But I'm adding a condition. Before you hand anything to anyone, you bring me proof this journalist is what you say. A recording, a meeting, something I can weigh. Because if I'm spending my team's safety on a stranger's integrity, I want collateral."

"Fair."

"And the site stays sealed, the sick stay put, and when Ferro's people arrive, I do the talking. I've negotiated with armed men before. They expect a hysterical scientist; they'll get a bureaucrat with a better memory for regulations than their lawyer has. While they're untangling me, you're north with the truth." She looked down at him, and for the first time in twelve days she felt something adjacent to steady. "One more condition, Mr. Underhill. Whatever happens—whatever they offer you, whatever they threaten—you come back for us. All six. I don't trade people for principles. I've seen what that costs, and I refuse to learn the exchange rate personally."

For a moment something moved behind his eyes—some old room, some old flood—and then it was gone.

"Yeah," he said. "I come back."

"Then we have a plan." She turned toward the sick bay, toward Nkem's bird-quick pulse and the long night of monitoring ahead, and paused with her hand on the crate. "Underhill. The man who falsified the forecast. The town that drowned."

His whole body went still.

"When this is over—when the data is public and the commissions start calling—there will be inquiries. About Kestrel's financing. About my silence. Possibly about you." She didn't turn around. "When they ask you who you are, I suggest you practice saying your name. It's heavier than it looks. Best to start lifting it now."

Behind her, the fire popped. Down in the black water, the oldest sea in the world went on breathing, patient as stone, indifferent to all of them—and somewhere far to the north, invisible against the stars, the headlights of Ferro's convoy were already rolling south.
