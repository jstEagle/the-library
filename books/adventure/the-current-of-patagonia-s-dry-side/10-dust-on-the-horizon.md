## 10. Dust on the Horizon

The satellite phone came alive at 4:17 in the morning, and Casper knew before he touched it that everything had just gotten worse.

It wasn't supposed to be on. He'd kept it powered down since the sinkhole—had wrapped it in a sock and buried it in his duffel like a snake egg, because a phone was a beacon and a beacon was a confession. But Ilsa had woken him an hour before dawn with her hand over his mouth and the handset pressed into his palm, and her eyes in the firelight said everything her whisper didn't.

*It's been receiving. Listen.*

So he lay in the cold dark at the mouth of the cavern, the wounded breathing slow and wet behind him, and listened.

The voice on the other end was a woman's, clipped and professional, the kind of voice that read numbers off manifests. It was speaking to someone named Delgado, and it was giving him coordinates.

"—convoy departs Puerto Madryn at oh-six-hundred. Three vehicles, heavy. Delgado has the drilling permits under the ministry seal, countersigned. You'll meet him at the junction of the old sheep trail, forty-one degrees twelve minutes south. He'll have the water analysis equipment, so keep the site sealed until he confirms. Two days, Delgado. Two days and this is a different country."

A pause. Static, the long hiss of a signal bouncing off satellites and falling back to earth across a thousand miles of nothing.

Then a second voice, male, bored the way dangerous men are bored: "And the surveyor? The one who went in after them?"

"Underhill is an asset or a corpse. Either way he's accounted for. Ferro says don't waste bullets on him unless he makes you."

The line went dead.

Casper lay there with the phone against his ear long after the silence filled it up, watching his breath hang in the air like something he'd exhaled and couldn't take back.

*Ferro says don't waste bullets on him unless he makes you.*

There it was, then. The arithmetic of his life, done in someone else's hand. He was a line item. He was a rounding error with a name. And Marisol Ferro—patient, reasonable, terrifying Marisol Ferro, who had sat across from him at a fuel stop table and explained his debt to him the way a banker explains a mortgage—had decided that the cleanest way to close his account was to close the whole ledger at once. The team, the site, the data. A rescue convoy. Drilling permits under a ministry seal.

He'd heard the word *countersigned* and understood it the way you understand a bone breaking—not the mechanics of it, just the fact of it, sudden and total. Somewhere in Buenos Aires a minister had put his name to a document that turned a national secret into private property, and the ink wasn't even dry, and three heavy vehicles were already rolling north through the dark with armed men in them and water analysis equipment in the back, because you don't send analysis equipment to a corpse site. You send it to a claim.

They weren't coming to rescue anyone. They were coming to take possession.

Casper sat up. The cavern mouth breathed its cold fog across him, the aquifer's slow exhalation, and somewhere below—forty meters down a shaft they'd rigged with rope and truck winch cable—Dr. Okafor's lantern was already lit. She slept four hours a night. She had been sleeping four hours a night for eleven days, and it showed in her hands, which had begun to tremble when she thought no one was watching.

He climbed down.

* * *

The shaft was a wound in the earth that Kestrel had fallen into by accident and then, in the way of scientists, had learned to love. The first three meters were a scramble; after that the team had fixed anchors and a rope ladder, and below that the rock opened like the throat of something enormous and patient. Casper went down it in the dark with the phone in his jacket pocket, and by the time his boots found the cavern floor his eyes had adjusted enough to see the shape of it: a cathedral of limestone, ribs of pale stone rising into blackness, and the floor of it—not floor, he corrected himself, *surface*—a pool of water so still it reflected the lantern light like polished obsidian.

The fossil aquifer. A freshwater sea older than the last uplift of the Andes, sealed under a hundred kilometers of basalt and badlands, untouched by anything except the slow patience of geology. Ilsa had shown him the test results on the second night. The numbers had made him sit down. Purity beyond anything in the municipal systems of three countries. Volume that the seismic readings could only bracket, not bound—*at least* the size of Lake Erie, Okafor had written in the margin, and then crossed it out and written *probably much larger*.

Water, in the driest place on earth. Enough water to make or break nations. He'd spent two days trying to feel awe about it and mostly what he felt was dread, because he knew exactly what human beings did with things like this, and one of them was named Ferro.

Okafor was at the water's edge with her notebook, because of course she was. Sixty-one years old, a frame like wire, a voice that had spent thirty years cutting through committee meetings and could cut through limestone. She didn't look up when his boots crunched the calcite gravel.

"You're up early, Mr. Underhill."

"Phone intercepted a transmission." He crouched beside her. The water lay between them like a held breath. "Ferro's sent a convoy. Armed. Three vehicles, heavy. They've got drilling permits with a minister's signature on them. Two days out."

Now she looked up. In the lantern light her face did something complicated and brief—grief, calculation, a flicker of something that might have been relief, the relief of a woman who had been waiting for the axe and was glad to finally see it fall—and then it was still again.

"Two days," she said.

"Two days."

She closed the notebook. Around them, the cavern held its sounds: the drip of seep water into the pool, the murmur of the sick in the sheltered alcove where they'd rigged cots from pack frames and tarps, the soft irregular breathing of six people whose bodies were fighting the contaminated water they'd drunk in the first desperate days before they'd realized the seeps near the entrance were poisoned with ancient salts. Two of them were bad. Mendez had a fever that climbed every evening like a tide. Chen hadn't kept food down in four days.

"They can't travel," Okafor said. It wasn't an argument. It was a statement of physics, the way she said most things. "Mendez can't walk to the rope ladder. Chen can't walk at all. If we move them up the shaft and across open ground—"

"They're dead if we don't." Casper heard his own voice come out flat and hard, the voice of a man who had learned, twelve years ago and at terrible cost, the exact difference between a forecast and a wish. "You said it yourself the first night. This site stays secret only as long as no one else finds it. Ferro's found it. Or she will, in two days, with armed men and a minister's signature and a legal document saying the water under our feet belongs to her syndicate. You want to be down here when they come? Six sick people, four rifles between the seven of you, and one rope ladder?"

"I want my team alive," Okafor said, and her voice cracked on *alive*, just once, just enough, and then was iron again. "Which is why they stay. Underground is defensible. Underground is *hidden*. You pull them up onto that plateau and put them in trucks and drive them across sixty kilometers of open country, and if Ferro's people intercept—"

"They won't."

"You don't know that."

"I know dust," Casper said. "I know wind. I've spent twelve years reading the sky over this kind of ground, and in two days there's a system coming down off the cordillera that will put a sand wall across the entire eastern plain for six hours, maybe eight. I can time it. I can put us inside it. Ferro's convoy will be driving blind in conditions where their own vehicles can't see two hundred meters, and so will we, but we'll know exactly where we are because I'll have been navigating this ground by dead reckoning since before they left Puerto Madryn."

Okafor studied him. The lantern hissed. Below them, impossibly, the water moved—not waves, nothing so crude, but a slow shifting of the reflections, the way a sleeping animal shifts its weight.

"You falsified a forecast once," she said.

The words went into him like a splinter, deep and clean, and he made himself not flinch. Ilsa had told her, then. Of course she had. He'd told Ilsa himself, around the fire, trading his worst night for a place at her fire, confession as currency, and currency circulates.

"I did."

"And a town drowned."

"Yes."

"And now you want me to bet six lives on your reading of the weather."

Casper took the phone out of his pocket and set it on the calcite gravel between them, the screen dark, the antenna still warm from the transmission it had stolen out of the sky.

"I'm not asking you to bet on me," he said. "I'm asking you to count. Ferro's convoy arrives in two days with armed men and a piece of paper that makes everything down here legally hers. If we're underground when they arrive, we're witnesses. You know what happens to witnesses on private property claimed under a ministry seal. If we move now, tonight, tomorrow, ahead of the storm—we're refugees with evidence, and evidence is the one thing Ferro can't shoot. Her whole play depends on the data never reaching anyone who matters. That's why she sent analysis equipment instead of a coroner. She doesn't want the team dead, Doctor. She wants the team *unfound*, and the data hers, and a hole in the ground with her name on it. The only way we beat that is to be somewhere else, loudly, publicly, with the numbers in our hands."

Okafor was quiet for a long time. He watched her do the thing he had watched scientists do his whole life, the thing he'd once done himself before he'd broken it: weigh the model against the cost of being wrong.

"Mendez dies if we move him wrong," she said finally. "Chen might. I've kept them stable down here because the water's cold and the air is clean and nobody has to carry them. Up there—" She gestured upward, at the hundred meters of rock between them and a plateau that would be an oven by midmorning. "Up there is exposure, dehydration, vibration. Every kilometer over that ground is a wager with their lives."

"Every hour down here is the same wager," Casper said. "You're just used to its odds."

She looked at him then with an expression he'd remember for a long time—not anger, not agreement, but the cold attention of a woman recalibrating. Recalibrating *him*.

"You've thought about this," she said. "The route, the timing, the storm. You've thought about it since before the convoy existed."

"I've thought about it since the sinkhole," Casper said. "The day Ilsa put an arrow six inches from my chest, I started planning how to get all of you out. I just didn't know the clock yet." He picked the phone up off the gravel. "Now I do."

* * *

He climbed back up into the grey beginning of morning and found Ilsa waiting at the rim, crouched behind a wind-carved fin of basalt with her bow across her knees, watching the eastern horizon the way she watched everything—like it owed her money.

She was the best of them, Casper had decided. Not the smartest—Okafor was the smartest—or the toughest—that was probably the geologist, Voss, who'd set his own dislocated shoulder on day three with a tent pole and a belt—but the best. Ilsa Reyes, scout, twenty-nine years old, who had held a stranger at arrow-point in the fog and then, forty minutes later, shared her water with him, because her suspicion and her decency lived side by side and neither ever won permanently.

"They heard it too," she said. It wasn't a question. The whole camp had heard the phone; sound carried down the shaft like water.

"Two days. Three vehicles. Armed."

"Delgado," Ilsa said. "I know the name. He did security for one of the shell companies that financed our original permit. He shook my hand at a ministry reception in Neuquén and asked me very polite questions about our seismic equipment." She spat, with feeling. "I liked him. That's the part that gets me. He seemed like a decent man."

"They always do," Casper said. "That's the job."

She turned and looked at him fully, and he saw her doing the arithmetic she'd been doing since the checkpoint of his arrival—since the moment a stranger walked out of the fog into the one place on earth she was supposed to be guarding. *Why is he here. Who sent him. What does a man like that want.*

"Okafor's going to say yes," Ilsa said. "Aren't you going to tell me why you want her to say yes? The real reason. Not the storm, not the tactics. The reason underneath."

Casper looked east. The horizon was doing its morning trick, the one that had fooled him the first week he'd arrived in the rain shadow and still fooled him sometimes: the light came up gold and soft over the mesas and for about ninety seconds the badlands looked like a place where things grew, and then the sun cleared the rock and the gold burned off and it was just stone and dust and wind again, the way it had been for ten thousand years.

"Because I've spent twelve years running from a number," he said. "A dead number. Fourteen people, in a valley in the north, because I wrote a forecast that said the river would hold and I knew it might not and I signed it anyway because a man with a briefcase told me my career would hold instead. I ran here because nothing grows here. Because a place with no water can't flood." He laughed, and it came out wrong, dry as the ground. "And now I'm standing on top of the largest freshwater reserve on the continent, and the woman who owns my debt wants to own it too, and I finally understand that you can't run from water. It finds you. It finds everyone. The only question—the only question there's ever been—is who's standing between it and the people downstream when it arrives."

Ilsa said nothing for a while. Below them, faintly, came the sound of Okafor's voice, giving orders in the clipped cadence of a woman who had made her decision and was already three steps into executing it.

"Two days," Ilsa said at last.

"Less, if the storm comes early." Casper was already moving toward the trucks, his mind doing what it did, what it had always done, the machinery he couldn't shut off any more than he could shut off his heart: wind speeds, load weights, litter rigging, fuel, the junction at the old sheep trail where Delgado would be waiting with his permits and his analysis equipment, the sixty kilometers of ground between here and Esquel, every arroyo and escarpment and soft patch of caliche he'd catalogued in three years of aimless, hiding driving. "I need the camp moving by tonight. Litters built, water loaded, the wounded stabilized for transport. I need Voss on the winch and I need you on point."

"And you?"

Casper stopped. The wind came up the plateau, dry and constant, carrying dust from a hundred kilometers away, carrying the smell of nothing, carrying—already, maybe, if you knew how to listen, and he knew how to listen—the faint pressure-change of a system building over the cordillera. Two days. Maybe less. A sand wall six hours wide, and a convoy of men who thought they were coming to claim a sea, and on the far side of it, if every calculation held, the road north to Esquel and a journalist he'd never named himself to.

"Me," he said, "I'm going to go stand on the highest ground I can find and watch the horizon until I see the dust. Because the storm is our cover, but the convoy is our clock—and I want to know, to the minute, when it starts hammering."

Ilsa shouldered her bow and fell in beside him, and together they walked down toward the cavern mouth, where the fog was rising off the breathing ground like the first breath of something about to wake.
