## 11. The Bargain

Marisol Ferro had learned long ago that the most dangerous hour of any operation was not the beginning or the end but the middle—the dead stretch when nothing could be verified and everything had to be assumed. She sat in her office on the fourteenth floor of a building in Buenos Aires that she technically did not own, watching the lights of Puerto Madero smear across the river glass, and understood that she was now precisely in the middle.

The convoy had left two days ago. Eleven men, two drilling rigs disguised as water-treatment equipment, permits signed by a minister whose signature she had bought for less than the cost of the pens he'd used. Satellite check-ins every six hours, each one a single word transmitted to a number that existed nowhere in any registry: *Adelante.* The last transmission had come at 04:12. *Adelante.* Since then, silence—not alarming silence, not yet, but the patient kind, the kind the badlands imposed on everyone who entered them.

She poured herself two fingers of whisky she wouldn't drink and stood at the window.

Her father had taught her the business from the wrong end. He'd been a hydrologist, a man of instruments and integrity, who spent thirty years mapping aquifers for provincial governments and died with a pension worth less than his funeral. Marisol had been nineteen at the funeral, already three years into learning what water actually was. Not a resource. Not a right. A *lever*. Every civilization in history had been built within reach of it, and every one of them could be moved by whoever controlled the reach.

The syndicate called itself—when it called itself anything—the Consorcio Austral. Twelve partners, if you counted the ones who counted. Oil money, agribusiness money, two sovereign wealth funds that preferred not to appear on paper. They had financed Survey Team Kestrel's original permit through three shell companies and a foundation dedicated, ironically, to watershed conservation. The plan had been elegant in its simplicity: let the government pay to find the water, let Kestrel do the dangerous work of confirming it, then acquire the data through channels no one would ever trace back to the source.

Except Kestrel had vanished. And vanished teams did not transmit data.

Ferro turned from the window and looked at the wall screen where a map of Chubut Province glowed in topographic relief. The dry side. Eleven days overdue, gear intact, no tracks out. Her people had intercepted the government's own search coordination by Tuesday; they knew Casper Underhill had been hired before the official channels even finished their paperwork. That had surprised her, briefly. Then it hadn't. Of course they'd hire him. He was the best tracker working the Comahue badlands, and more importantly, he was *hers*—or nearly hers, or as close to hers as a man like that ever came to belonging to anyone. Two hundred and forty thousand dollars in obligations, spread across lenders who reported to lenders who reported to her. She had spent four years building that leash one link at a time. Small loans first. Reasonable rates. Then the restructuring, the consolidation, the quiet purchase of every note until the man's entire financial existence funneled through a single pipe she controlled.

Not because she'd planned this exact moment. Because men like Casper Underhill were only useful if you owned the ground they stood on, and you never knew which piece of ground would matter.

She picked up the satellite handset. It was heavy in the way old tools were heavy, and she liked that about it.

* * *

Casper answered on the fourth ring, and she could hear the wind behind him—that flat, endless Patagonian wind that sounded like the earth exhaling through its teeth.

"Ferro." His voice was exactly as she remembered from their one face-to-face meeting, eighteen months ago at a fuel stop outside Gobernador Costa: unhurried, slightly hoarse, giving away nothing. "I wondered how long you'd wait."

"Two days," she said. "Long enough to seem patient. Short enough to seem serious."

"And here I thought you were just letting me work."

"You are working. I'm calling to discuss what happens after." She walked to the map, though the map was for her benefit alone; he couldn't see it, and she didn't need it. She had the coordinates memorized the way other people remembered phone numbers. "You found something."

It wasn't a question, and she watched herself make the classic error even as she made it—she'd meant it as an opening, a soft probe, but the words came out with the weight of certainty, and certainty told him what she knew and didn't know. She heard him register it. There was a half-second pause, barely perceptible over the compression of the satellite link, and in that half-second Marisol Ferro, who had negotiated against oil ministers and once talked a cartel accountant out of a warehouse full of evidence, felt the particular chill of talking to someone smarter than the conversation required.

"I found a camp," Casper said. "Tents, food, a seismograph still running. No bodies. No tracks leading out except one set that stops."

"A seismograph still running." She let the words sit. "Running because someone wound it recently. Or because whatever it's measuring hasn't stopped."

"Your optimism is showing, Ms. Ferro."

"My optimism is why you're not in prison, Mr. Underhill, and why your truck still has wheels." She sat down at her desk, opened the folder she'd prepared before making the call, and did not look at it. Looking at notes during a negotiation was weakness; knowing them cold was armor. "Here is what happens next. You're going to find Team Kestrel. I have no doubt you will—you're the best there is at finding things that don't want to be found, and I say that with genuine professional respect. When you find them, they will have data. Seismic surveys, flow measurements, chemical analyses, depth readings. Everything a team like Okafor's would collect before reporting home. You are going to bring me that data. All of it. Original files, unaltered, with checksums intact so my people can verify you haven't touched them."

"And in exchange?"

"In exchange, every obligation attached to your name ceases to exist tonight. Not paid—*erased*. There's a difference, and you'll appreciate it. Paid debts leave records. Erased debts leave nothing. No collector will ever find you again because there will be nothing to find. Two hundred forty thousand dollars becomes a rounding error in an account that never existed."

The wind filled the pause. She pictured him standing beside his truck, the handset pressed to his ear, his eyes doing whatever his eyes did when he was calculating—which, she suspected, was nothing at all. That was the unsettling thing about Casper Underhill. Most men's faces leaked. His didn't. She had learned to read micro-expressions the way her father had learned to read strata, and eighteen months ago, across the scarred hood of his vehicle, she had gotten absolutely nothing off him except courtesy and a faint smell of dust and solder.

"There's a second part," she said, "and we both know I don't need to say it, but I'm going to say it anyway, because I believe in clarity between professionals."

"Clarity," he repeated. Flat.

"If you bring me nothing—if you come out of those badlands empty-handed, or with excuses, or with a story about how the data was destroyed, or lost, or never existed—I will bury you with the team. Do you understand what I mean by buried? Not killed. Killed is fast and creates questions. Buried is slower. Your name goes into systems. Your fingerprints go onto documents you've never seen. The government finds evidence that you sold Kestrel's location to parties unknown, that you were seen in Esquel meeting with people who later turned up dead, that the money trail from your creditors leads directly to accounts in your name that you cannot explain. By the time anyone finishes untangling it, you'll have been tried in the press twice and convicted in absentia once, and the actual truth will be one voice among forty shouting voices, and no one listens to the fortieth voice." She paused, and allowed herself one degree of warmth, because warmth was more frightening than cold from a woman like her. "I don't want to do that. I want to erase two hundred forty thousand dollars and shake your hand. But I have partners, Casper, and partners have expectations, and expectations are the one debt I can't restructure."

Another pause. Longer this time. She let it run. Silence was pressure, and pressure was honest—it showed her the shape of a man's resistance by how long it took to deform.

"The data belongs to the Republic of Argentina," Casper said finally. "Kestrel is a government survey. Whatever they found down there is state property."

"Everything is state property until someone holds it," Ferro said. "That's not cynicism, that's physics. Possession is the only law with enforcement mechanisms. And before you start composing a speech about conscience, consider: what do you imagine happens to that water if my consortium doesn't take it? You think the ministry will protect it? The same ministry that signed our drilling permits? The same provincial government that auctioned grazing rights on land it didn't own? If we don't move on that aquifer, someone else will—someone worse than us, someone without my patience, and there are very few someones with my patience. I am, genuinely, the best-case scenario for that water. Ask yourself who else knows about it. Ask yourself who else has been asking questions in Esquel this month."

She was lying, mostly. She had no idea who else knew. But the lie was constructed on a foundation of truth, and truth was what made lies load-bearing.

"You said erased," Casper said. "All of it. Every note, every lender, every record."

"Every record. Tonight. Before you even deliver—I'll send the release orders while you're still standing in that hole in the ground, so you can watch them execute. Trust flows both directions or it doesn't flow at all."

"And if the team won't hand over their data?"

"Then you'll have to be persuasive." She smiled at the darkened window, at her own reflection ghosted over the lights of the city. "You're a persuasive man, Casper. You persuaded six lenders to extend credit to a man with no fixed address and no verifiable history. That takes talent."

"That took desperation. Yours and mine."

"Yes," she agreed pleasantly. "Desperation is the great lubricant of commerce. So. Do we have an agreement?"

And here it was—the hinge of the whole call, the moment she had rehearsed in three variations, and Marisol Ferro listened the way a seismograph listens, not to the answer but to the tremor beneath it.

"Yeah," Casper said. "We have an agreement. Data for erasure. I'll need seventy-two hours minimum. These things aren't sitting in a briefcase; they're distributed across the team's equipment, and some of it may be analog. Paper charts. Film. I'll need time to consolidate."

Seventy-two hours. A request for time was either honesty or stalling, and she could not yet tell which, and that inability irritated her like grit in a bearing. "Seventy-two hours. My convoy reaches your position in roughly the same window. Consider them an escort."

"I don't need an escort."

"No one needs an escort. Escorts are how we say hello in the country you're standing in." She closed the folder. "One more thing, and then I'll let you get back to your wind. When you talk to Dr. Okafor—and you will talk to her—remember that she signed her funding documents. She knew whose money built her expedition. Whatever speech she gives you about scientific integrity, understand that she was silent for four years while we positioned ourselves, and silence is a choice, and choices have owners. Don't let her make you feel like the corrupt one in the conversation. You're just the man carrying the box. Carriers walk free. That's the oldest rule there is."

"Goodnight, Ms. Ferro."

"Goodnight, Casper. Seventy-two hours. Bring me the sea under the stone, and walk away rich in the only currency that matters to a man like you: a clean slate."

She ended the call and sat for a long moment in the blue dark of her office, the handset cooling in her hand.

* * *

Ríos came in at eleven, as he always did for the night briefing—her operations chief, ex-army signals, a man whose loyalty she trusted not because he loved her but because she had made herself the only stable structure in his life, which was far more durable than love. He read her face the way her analysts read satellite imagery.

"He took the deal," she said.

"Of course he took the deal." Ríos set his tablet on her desk. "Men with that much debt always take the deal. The question is whether he intends to honor it."

"He asked for seventy-two hours. Said the data might be analog. Paper charts, film."

Ríos considered that. "Could be true. Okafor runs an old-school operation. Half her instruments predate USB."

"Could also be a man buying time to think." Ferro rose and walked back to the window. Down below, the city went on glittering, eight million people drinking water piped from reservoirs that were at thirty-one percent capacity and falling, none of whom would ever know how close the arithmetic of their survival came to passing through rooms like this one. "Here's what bothers me, Ríos. He agreed too easily. Not suspiciously easily—he haggled the timeline, he raised the sovereignty objection, he performed reluctance exactly correctly. That's what bothers me. The performance was correct. Men who intend to keep a bargain negotiate badly, emotionally, because they're negotiating with their own consciences. Men who intend to break a bargain negotiate beautifully, because the bargain costs them nothing. It's a prop."

"So you think he's going to run."

"I think Casper Underhill is a man who falsified a flood forecast once and has been paying for it ever since, which means he understands better than anyone alive what a lie costs, which means he will not tell one casually." She turned. "Which means if he lies to me, it will be a lie he's decided is worth the price. And I need to know what price he thinks he's paying, and to whom, before my convoy arrives and I no longer control the tempo."

Ríos tapped the tablet. "Second team is staged in Esquel. Eight men, air support on standby at Trelew. We can have eyes over that position within six hours of your word."

"Not yet. Overhead imagery might spook him, and if he's telling the truth about analog data, spooking him loses us the originals." She studied the map again, the mesas and dry canyons rendered in shades of amber, the blank space at the center where the terrain models simply gave up. "But I want the seismic archives pulled. Every regional survey recorded in the last sixty years, including the military gravity anomalies from the seventies that everyone forgot. If there's a body of water under that plateau large enough to matter, it will have bent something somewhere—a gravity reading, a deep reflection line, an old well log that came up sweet when it should have come up salt. Find me the corroboration. Because if Casper hands my advance team a drive full of numbers, I want to be able to check those numbers against something independent before the convoy drills a single meter."

"And if the numbers don't match?"

Ferro picked up the whisky finally, and drank it, and the peat smoke of it settled over the colder taste already in her mouth.

"Then the carrier is carrying something else," she said, "and I'll want to know what it weighs."

Outside, far to the south, under a sky with no city in it, a man was standing beside a truck in the wind, holding a dead handset, deciding what she would never see coming. She could feel it the way her father claimed he could feel rain three days out—in the scar tissue of an old life, an ache with weather behind it.

Marisol Ferro had bought and sold ministers, moved rivers on paper, and broken men gentler and stronger than Casper Underhill. But she had built her fortune on one assumption she had never examined: that everyone, eventually, wanted what she was selling.

She went home at two in the morning and slept badly, and did not ask herself why.
