## 12. Carrying the Wounded

We built the first litter out of a broken tent pole, two sleeping pads, and the rope Ilsa had been using to haul water buckets up from the cavern's lower shelf. It took us an hour to get it right, and by the end of it my hands were shaking—not from fear, from the specific exhaustion of doing something delicate while someone watches you do it wrong.

"Again," Ilsa said.

"It'll hold."

"You said that about the last one."

"The last one held."

"For forty meters." She crouched and pressed her palm flat against the rope where it crossed the pole, testing the tension the way you'd test a pulse. "Then it stretched and dropped Dr. Adeyemi's shoulder into the scree. He didn't say anything. That's how I know it hurt."

I took the rope back down and re-tied the hitch with a trucker's knot my father taught me when I was nine years old, standing in a driveway in Nebraska, learning that a load is only as honest as the line holding it. The knot seated clean. Ilsa watched me tie it and said nothing, which from her was nearly praise.

Six of them could not walk. Okafor counted them off for me the night before in the blue light of her headlamp: Adeyemi with the infected shoulder, two of the geologists with dysentery bad enough that they'd stopped pretending they could stand, a young tech named Bruno whose ankle had gone septic around the break, and the Vidal twins, both dehydrated past the point of reason because they'd been giving their ration to the others. The other three—Okafor, Ilsa, and a quiet man called Sosa—could move, but slowly, burdened, watchful. Eleven days underground on contaminated seep water had written its arithmetic into all of them.

Two days to the trucks. Maybe thirty kilometers as the condor flew, but nothing out here flew straight, least of all us.

* * *

We left at first light, or what passes for first light on the dry side—a gray bruise along the eastern mesas, no color in it, just the dark lifting like a sheet pulled off furniture in an empty house. I'd spent the night before studying the wind the way I used to study radar loops: lying on my back with my eyes closed, reading the pressure against my eardrums, tasting the air for grit. There was a storm coming. Not rain—the dry side doesn't give rain, it gives *dust*, whole countries of it lifted off the flats and thrown east. Two days out, maybe less. A wall of it moving down from the northwest at walking pace, patient as debt.

I'd told Okafor: we travel inside it. She'd looked at me for a long moment with the expression of a woman deciding whether a tool is worth the risk of holding.

"You want to hide eleven sick people inside a sandstorm," she said.

"I want to hide them from satellites and aircraft and anyone standing on a mesa with binoculars. Dust does that better than night does. Night has infrared. Dust has nothing—it eats every spectrum equally. It's the only weather that's fair."

"And if it eats us too?"

"Then we were dead anyway," I said, "just slower."

She hadn't laughed. But she'd started counting litters.

Now we moved. I walked point, twenty meters ahead, and behind me came the slow procession: Sosa pulling the first litter over the gravel on improvised runners—strips of tent floor we'd waxed with candle stubs—Ilsa on the second, Okafor alternating between the third and carrying Bruno when the terrain turned against wheels entirely. The able ones rotated through the pull-lines every hour. Nobody complained. There's a kind of silence that comes over people who have already decided they might die together, and it isn't grim. It's almost gentle. They'd had eleven days underground to make their peace; all I'd given them was a direction.

The land did what the land always does out here, which is pretend to be featureless and then punish you for believing it. Gravel gave way to bentonite clay that grabbed boot soles like wet teeth. Then a field of wind-carved stones sharp enough to open a litter runner's underside, and we learned to lift instead of drag, six people hoisting a seventh over a hundred meters of knife ground, grunting in a rhythm nobody organized and everybody kept.

Adeyemi never made a sound. That frightened me more than screaming would have.

* * *

At midday we stopped in the lee of a basalt dike, a black seam in the earth running north-south like a scar somebody had tried to stitch. I got the water going—iodine tablets, then a boil, because after what Kestrel had drunk underground nobody trusted anything that hadn't been killed twice—and I climbed the dike to look back the way we'd come.

Nothing. That was the point of nothing. But I looked anyway, the way you touch a bruise.

From up there I could see the whole shape of the basin: the mesas stepping down in terraces toward the sinkhole country, the pale alkali flats beyond them shining like spilled milk, and far to the northwest, at the very edge of sight, a smudge on the horizon that wasn't cloud. The storm, arriving early, right on schedule. Weather always keeps its appointments. It's people who don't.

"Casper."

Ilsa had climbed halfway up the dike behind me. She stood with her bow slung across her back and her weight on her downhill leg, and she was looking not at the horizon but at me.

"You knew this storm was coming," she said. "Yesterday. Before any of us saw a thing. You told Okafor 'two days' and you were right to the half-day. How?"

"Experience."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the truest kind I've got."

She came up beside me. Close, she smelled of woodsmoke and cave dust and iodine, and there was a raw patch on her cheekbone where the harness strap had rubbed it for two days straight. She didn't blink much. Out here, I supposed, blinking was a luxury the wind taxed.

"Ferro's people will have forecasters," she said. "Expensive ones. Satellite models. And they'll still be guessing, because this basin doesn't behave. The winds turn against the models. I've watched it. So explain to me how a man who chases storms in trucks reads this place like he grew up in it."

"I didn't grow up in it."

"No," she agreed. "But you've been here before."

The wind moved along the top of the dike and I let it talk for a while. Below us, Okafor was feeding Adeyemi water in small sips, tilting the cup with the patience of a woman who had done triage in worse places than this and carried the receipts.

"There was a flood," I said. "Twelve years ago. Not here. North—same dry side, different province. A river valley that wasn't supposed to flood. I was working for a company that sold certainty to developers, and I gave them the forecast they paid for." I heard myself say it flatly, the way you recite a sentence you've rehearsed in the dark so many times it's worn smooth. "The town drowned. I ran. I've been running south ever since, and somewhere along the way running became driving, and driving became a job."

Ilsa was quiet. Then: "That's why you hide in the driest place on earth. Water took everything from you, so you went where it can't follow."

"It follows anyway," I said. "It's under our feet right now. A sea of it, older than the mountains. You found it. I'm just trying to keep it from drowning anybody else."

She studied me with those unblinking eyes, and whatever she was weighing, she finished weighing it. "You navigate like a man coming home to a house he burned down," she said. "I've seen it since the sinkhole. You read the wind off your own skin. You picked the route through the clay pans without a map. Yesterday you told Sosa to skirt the east side of the second mesa before he'd even seen what was on the west side of it. How did you know?"

Because I had run this country once, at night, at speed, in a truck full of instruments I no longer trusted, chasing a storm cell that the models swore couldn't exist in a rain shadow—and finding it anyway, a black rotating fist over the flats, and understanding in one white flash of lightning that the dry side had weather nobody had ever bothered to measure, because nobody believed it was worth measuring. Because I had driven these tracks until they wore into me like a signature into stone, and then buried the years under other years.

But some truths are ballast. You don't drop them all at once; you sink.

"I chased something through here once," I said. "Something I shouldn't have been chasing. I learned the ground the hard way, and the ground doesn't forget being learned."

Ilsa nodded slowly, and I understood that she had accepted the answer the way you accept a bridge—you test it with your weight, and when it holds, you cross, and you don't ask the bridge to explain itself.

"All right," she said. "Then get us home the hard way."

* * *

The storm caught us at dusk on the second day, exactly where I'd promised it would, and it was beautiful in the way that only large indifferent things are beautiful.

We heard it before we saw it—a sound like a freight train that never arrives, rolling across the flats from the northwest, growing until it filled the skull. Then the horizon stood up. That's the only way I can describe it: the horizon *stood up*, a brown wall two thousand meters high, moving toward us at the pace of a tired man, and above it the sky stayed obscenely clear, blue and calm, as though the violence below were none of its business.

"Litters down! Goggles on! Scarves over the mouth—wet them if you've got water to spare!" I was shouting and didn't remember starting. We lashed the wounded to the litters with the last of the rope, tucked tarp around them, weighted the edges with stones. Okafor worked with a surgeon's economy, checking each restraint twice. Bruno, feverish, smiled at the sky and said something in Portuguese that I chose to believe was a prayer.

Then the wall arrived and the world ended politely, all at once, into orange.

Daylight went to rust. The sun became a coin behind smoked glass. The air thickened until breathing was a decision you made deliberately, again and again, and the grit found every seam—teeth, eyelids, the creases of knuckles. Sound collapsed to a single note, the roar of a continent being rearranged. We roped ourselves into a chain, me at the front, and I walked by feel and memory and the animal compass in my inner ear, counting paces, holding a bearing I'd fixed on the last visible landmark before the world erased itself.

This was the part I hadn't told them: navigating inside a dust storm is not navigation. It's negotiation. You offer the storm your patience and it offers you a corridor, briefly, where the wind eddies around some buried feature of the land—an old river terrace, a rise of harder rock—and in that corridor you can hold a line. Storm chasers call it reading the grain. Farmers call it luck. I called it the only skill I owned that had never once been sold.

Twice the chain stopped because someone behind had fallen. Twice I went back down the rope hand over hand, blind, and hauled them up, and the grit was so thick I recognized people by their hands—Okafor's square practical grip, Sosa's long fingers, Ilsa's callused bowstring hand closing around my wrist hard enough to leave a mark I'd keep.

Hours passed, or years. At some point the light changed from rust to blood to a deep bruised violet, and I understood the storm's leading edge had passed over us and we were inside its belly now, in the long calm eye of dust that would last most of the night. We camped there, in the orange silence, huddled around a stove whose flame burned a strange clean blue in the particulate air. Nobody spoke much. Adeyemi slept, which the medic in Okafor said was either very good or very bad, and she sat with her fingers on his wrist for a long time, counting.

Ilsa sat next to me with her back against a litter wheel, sharing a scarf's worth of filtered air near the stove.

"You're smiling," she said.

"I'm not."

"Your eyes are. In all this—" she gestured at the wall of moving earth that had swallowed creation— "you look like a man at a reunion."

I watched the stove flame bend and steady, bend and steady. "When I was a kid in Nebraska," I said, "a storm came through that the radio swore was nothing. My father put me in the truck and drove at it. Everyone else was driving away. He said, 'Everybody's scared of the same sky, Casper. The difference is what you do facing it.'" I turned the cup in my hands. "First time in twelve years I've used what he taught me for something that matters."

Ilsa was quiet a moment. "The data," she said. "The real survey. Okafor says you have a plan for it that you haven't told us."

"I have a plan."

"One that involves lying to Ferro."

"One that involves telling Ferro exactly what she wants to hear." I met her eyes through the grit-haze. "And telling everyone else the truth. Those aren't the same audience. That's the whole trick."

"And if the trick fails?"

"Then I'll be the one holding the bag. Not you. Not Okafor. Me." I said it lightly, and hated how lightly it came out, the old salesman's polish still living in my voice like a scar tissue. "I've got practice losing things."

Ilsa looked at me for a long time. Somewhere out in the orange dark, the storm dragged its train across the flats, unhurried, enormous, keeping its appointment.

"When we reach the trucks," she said finally, "you should know that I checked your story. About the flood. The town north. I asked Sosa—he worked provincial water ministry before Kestrel. He remembers the case. He remembers the forecaster who vanished." Her voice didn't change at all. "He said the man's name was different. But the description fit."

The stove flame bent. I let it.

"So I know who you are," Ilsa said. "Or close enough. And I'm telling you I know, so you understand that everything from here on, you don't carry alone." She stood, brushed grit from her knees, and offered me her hand—the bowstring callus, the grip that leaves marks. "Get us to the trucks, Casper. Whatever name you're using this week."

I took the hand. Above us, invisible now, the first stars were being strangled by dust, and somewhere north, in the clean air beyond the storm's edge, Ferro's convoy was rolling south with drilling permits and rifles, believing the desert was empty.

By morning, they'd believe something worse: that it was full.

* * *

We reached the trucks an hour past dawn, staggering out of the storm's ragged tail like survivors of a shipwreck washing onto a beach. The vehicles sat where I'd cached them, half-buried, windows frosted with fine silt, tires softened by cold nights into lazy ovals. I dug out the intake vents with my hands, said a small selfish prayer to the fuel gods, and turned the first engine over. It caught on the fourth try with a rattle like applause.

We loaded the wounded in shifts. Adeyemi went flat in the truck bed, Okafor riding with him, her fingers never leaving his pulse. Bruno got the cab seat reclined all the way back and apologized for bleeding on the upholstery, and Ilsa told him the upholstery was already ruined, and he laughed, and the laugh cost him something and was worth it.

I was cinching the last strap when Ilsa touched my arm and pointed north.

Not at the storm—that was behind us now, a fading brown memory on the southern horizon. North, across the flats, where the air was clean and the light was hard and honest, there was a thread of dust rising off the distant road. Thin. Moving. Too fast for wind, too straight for weather.

Vehicle traffic. Coming this way.

I got the binoculars up and found them: two points, maybe three, crawling along the trace that led toward the sinkhole country, kicking up their own small weather. Ferro's advance men, hunting the camp we'd already emptied—or hunting us.

I lowered the glasses. Ilsa was watching me, and behind her, Okafor had risen from Adeyemi's side and was looking north too, her face unreadable, her hand still resting on her dying colleague's wrist.

"They're early," I said. "They weren't due for two days."

"Can we outrun them?"

"With the wounded? No." I swung into the driver's seat and felt the old cold clarity settle over me, the one that used to arrive before every intercept, every chase, every gamble with the sky. "So we won't try. We'll give them exactly what they're expecting to find."

I reached under my seat for the decoy drive, wrapped in a plastic bag where I'd hidden it weeks ago, and my hand came out empty.

The bag was gone.
