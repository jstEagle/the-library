## 13. The Checkpoint

The convoy came down off the high pampa in the last hour of light, six vehicles strung out along a track that wasn't a road so much as a scar the land had agreed to tolerate. Casper drove the lead truck with the window cracked, tasting the air the way he always did before weather—except this time what he tasted was diesel and dust and the faint alkaline bite of a playa forty kilometers east. Behind him: the second truck carrying Okafor and two of her walking wounded, then the flatbed with the litters lashed down, Ilsa's people breathing shallow under tarps against the grit. Sixteen souls, three working vehicles, one lie.

The decoy drive sat in his shirt pocket like a coal.

He'd built it over two nights by headlamp, kneeling on the cold ground outside the tents while Kestrel slept or pretended to. He'd taken the real survey files—the ones Ilsa had carried out sewn into the lining of her jacket—and copied their structure exactly, every header, every timestamp, every instrument signature, so that anyone who opened them would see the fingerprints of the same machines that had made the originals. Then he'd gone through the numbers the way a forger goes through a face. Conductivity readings inflated past the threshold where freshwater becomes poison. Salinity gradients rewritten to show brine intruding from the deep formations. The porosity models quietly gutted, the aquifer's vast dark body redrawn as a thin, contaminated lens not worth the cost of casing a well.

It was good work. It was the best forgery he'd ever made, and he'd spent twelve years forging smaller things—a name, a history, a forecast that had killed eleven people in a river valley in another country under another sky. He knew how lies were built. You didn't hide the truth; you dressed it in true clothing. Every fake number was anchored to a real one. The instruments had really been there. The dates were honest. Only the earth itself had been made to lie, and the earth couldn't testify.

"You keep touching your pocket," Ilsa said from the passenger seat. She hadn't slept more than an hour at a stretch since the ambush planning began, and it showed in the fine tremor at the corner of her jaw, the way her eyes kept sweeping the mirror even when she talked about nothing.

"It's a habit."

"It's a tell." She turned in the seat to face him. In the slanting light her face was all planes and dust. "When you're nervous you touch the left side of your chest, like you're checking a wound. Ferro's men will have watched enough scared people to know what fear looks like. So don't be nervous."

"That's your advice. Don't be nervous."

"My advice is be something else." She looked back at the mirror. "Be bored. Bored men don't get searched twice."

* * *

They saw the checkpoint before the checkpoint saw them—or rather, Casper saw it, because he'd been reading the land the way he read sky, and the land had told him twenty minutes early. A low rise where the track pinched between two outcrops of rust-colored basalt. A place where a vehicle would have to slow to walking pace to thread the gap. If you wanted to stop someone in this country, that was where you'd do it, and the fact that no dust plume rose behind the rocks yet meant they were parked in shadow, engines off, waiting.

"Contact," he said, and keyed the radio. "All units, single file, my spacing. Nobody stops until I stop. Nobody speaks unless I ask you a question directly. We are a rescue convoy bringing out sick people, we are tired, we are cooperative, and we are expected. Say nothing else."

From the flatbed, Okafor's voice came back level and dry: "Understood. Casper—the wounded first. Whatever happens, whatever they demand, the wounded come out of this alive. That's the only instruction I'll give you, and I'm giving it to you as the person whose expedition this is."

"The wounded first," he confirmed. "That was always the plan."

It wasn't entirely the plan. Plans were things you wrote down. What he had was a shape in his mind, a sequence of moves he'd rehearsed the way he'd once rehearsed storm intercepts—knowing full well that the atmosphere never honored the briefing. But he said it like it was a plan, because the sixteen people behind him needed it to be one.

The basalt walls rose on either side. The track narrowed. And then the rocks themselves seemed to stand up—a pickup with a mounted light bar rolling out of the shadow of the eastern outcrop, a second vehicle angling across the gap, four men in civilian clothes with the particular posture of men who were civilians the way a wolf is a dog. One of them raised a hand, palm out, unhurried. The gesture of a man who had stopped many vehicles and had never once been in a hurry about it.

Casper braked. Killed the engine. Let the silence settle, because silence was a tool too—he'd learned that from collectors, from men like Ferro, who understood that the person who speaks first in a standoff has already given something away.

The lead man walked up on the driver's side. Forty-ish, sun-cured, a rifle slung casually backward across his shoulder in the manner of someone who wanted it visible but not threatening. His eyes did the truck first—plates, cargo bed, tarps—then Casper, then Ilsa, then the column behind.

"Papers," he said. Not unkindly. That was the worst part. Men who were cruel were predictable; men who were pleasant and armed were a different arithmetic entirely.

Casper handed over the convoy authorization—the genuine article, signed by the same ministry that had signed Ferro's drilling permits, which was its own kind of joke—and the man read it without hurry while his partner drifted toward the flatbed and lifted a corner of the tarp. One of the wounded men moaned. The sound did more work than any argument could have; the partner let the tarp fall and stepped back with the careful neutrality of a man who didn't want to know details.

"Survey Team Kestrel," the lead man said. "Eleven days overdue. And here you are." He smiled without warmth. "The minister will be pleased. There's been a lot of worry in Esquel."

"A lot of worry," Casper agreed. Bored. He let his shoulders slump, let his voice come out sanded flat with fatigue. "Three days hauling litters across the badlands. Two of these people need a hospital tonight or they won't need one at all. If you've got a satphone with better range than ours, call ahead for ambulances. Otherwise get out of our light."

Something flickered in the man's eyes—not suspicion, exactly. Recalculation. Fear looks like fear. Exhaustion looks like exhaustion. But irritation, genuine bone-deep irritation, looked like a man with nothing left to hide, and Casper poured it on like fuel.

"You're Underhill?" the man said. "The contractor?"

"I'm whoever the invoice says I am."

"And the survey data? The minister wants the team's findings secured. Standard procedure. Chain of custody." He held out his hand, palm up now instead of palm out, and the shift in that small gesture was the whole conversation: from stopping them to receiving them. "You understand. Six people missing, a government contract, shell companies and permits—there are parties north who've invested considerably, and they'll want assurance their money bought something."

There it was. Not even disguised. Casper felt Ilsa go very still beside him, felt the entire convoy holding its breath behind him, sixteen people pretending to be tired instead of terrified.

He took the decoy drive from his shirt pocket.

For one half-second—one full rotation of the earth beneath him, as far as he could feel it—he held it between finger and thumb in the open air and understood that whatever happened next, there was no version of this day he would ever come back from. Hand it over and the die was cast: Ferro's syndicate would read poisoned water where there was a buried sea, would radio north, would begin to stand down, and if any one of them ever learned the truth—if Ilsa talked, if Okafor talked, if some technician north ran a sample and got clean water instead of brine—then Casper Underhill became a dead man with a very long list of people owed an explanation. Keep it in his pocket and they'd search the trucks, find the real data sewn into a jacket lining, and sixteen people would die in this canyon for a spreadsheet.

He set the drive in the man's palm.

"Everything's on there," Casper said. "Raw logs, conductivity series, the salinity model, drill-core projections. My advice to whoever reads it: don't quit your day job. It's salt. All of it, deep formation brine, worse than seawater. Your investors bought a hole in the ground that will poison cattle at fifty meters." He shrugged, and the shrug was the masterpiece, the small human collapse of a man delivering bad news he'd personally suffered through. "I chased storms for eleven years before this. I know a dry hole when I drive one out of a canyon."

The man weighed the drive in his hand like a coin he suspected of being clipped. Then he nodded to his partner, who produced a ruggedized laptop from the cab of the pickup, and for ten minutes that lasted approximately one geological age, they sat in the shade of the basalt and read Casper's forgery.

He watched them the way he used to watch a supercell: peripheral vision, total stillness, reading micro-movements. The partner scrolled. Frowned. Scrolled back. Called something to the lead man, who leaned in, and Casper tracked their hands—the pointing at screens, the pulling up of a graph, the long pause. Then the lead man laughed. Short, ugly, genuine.

"Brine," he said, shaking his head. "Eleven days missing and six nearly dead, over salt water." He looked up at Casper with something almost like sympathy, which was the most dangerous expression Casper had seen all day, because sympathy believed what it was told. "The minister's friends put seven figures into this permit."

"Then they should hire better geologists next time."

"They hired us to make sure." The man closed the laptop. "And you're telling me the data confirms it."

"I'm telling you the earth doesn't care who paid for the shovel."

Another pause. The wind moved grit across the track in long hissing sheets. Somewhere behind Casper, a wounded man coughed, wet and deep, and the sound went through the checkpoint like a knife through canvas—because it was true, and truth was the one thing Casper hadn't forged, and it was doing all the heavy lifting now.

The lead man pulled a satellite phone from his chest rig and dialed. Turned slightly away, but not far enough, and Casper caught every word because he'd spent years listening to weather and weather taught you to hear through noise.

"Checkpoint two, convoy intercepted, all accounted for... yes, the contractor, Underhill... No, sir, I've read it myself. Conductivity off the scale, sodium-chloride dominant, the model shows the whole formation contaminated from the deep intrusion. There's nothing here. It's a salt grave." A pause, listening. "No, sir. I wouldn't drill a fence post in it." Another pause, longer. "Understood. Standing down the second team. Convoy proceeds to Esquel. Yes. Underhill signs the custody transfer and we photograph him with the drive—that covers us."

He lowered the phone and looked at Casper almost apologetically. "Photograph. Protocol. Nothing personal."

"Nothing in this country is personal," Casper said. "That's what I like about it."

They photographed him standing between the trucks, holding the decoy drive up like a trophy fish, wearing the exhausted grimace of a man who'd found nothing worth finding. The flash popped twice. Somewhere north, in an office Casper would never see, a file began building with his face in it and a lie underneath, and somewhere south, in a canyon behind them, a sea of water older than the mountains kept its own counsel under a hundred meters of stone.

The lead man shook his hand. His grip was dry and strong. "You did your job, contractor. Go home."

"That's the plan," Casper said.

They waved the convoy through. The engines started one by one, and Casper pulled out through the gap in the basalt with the setting sun full in his eyes, turning the whole western sky the color of the forge he'd just walked through.

Nobody spoke for five kilometers. Then Ilsa exhaled, long and slow, like a diver surfacing.

"'Salt grave,'" she repeated. "You sounded like you meant it."

"I did mean it. That's the trick. You can't act a lie. You have to believe it for ninety seconds, all the way down, and then walk out of it." He kept his eyes on the track. "I've had practice."

She was quiet for a moment. When she spoke again her voice had changed—flatter, more careful, the voice of a woman re-measuring a room she thought she knew. "They're standing down the second team. You heard it. It worked."

"It worked today."

"And tomorrow?"

Ahead of them the track ran north toward Esquel, toward Elena Brandt, toward testimony and ruin and maybe, if the weather held, toward the end of twelve years of running. Casper touched his left chest, the old wound-check, and this time he let his hand stay there.

"Tomorrow," he said, "we find out whether the second team really stood down—or whether somebody north is smart enough to check the chemistry twice."

Behind them, in the mirror, the checkpoint lights came on against the dusk, and one of Ferro's pickups peeled away from the basalt gap and headed not north after them, but east—toward the coast, toward a satellite uplink, toward whatever Ferro did when the numbers finally reached her desk.

Casper watched it shrink to a red taillight in the gathering dark, and drove faster.
