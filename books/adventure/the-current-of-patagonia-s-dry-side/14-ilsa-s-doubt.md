## 14. Ilsa's Doubt

The convoy stopped for the night in the lee of a basalt ridge that looked, in the last of the light, like the spine of something enormous buried on its side. Ilsa Reyes helped drag the tarps off the truck beds and stake them against the wind, and she did it with her hands and not her mind, because her mind kept returning to the same image: Casper Underhill standing in the dust at the checkpoint, holding out a drive full of lies with the easy posture of a man handing over a receipt.

*Brine-poisoned. Worthless.* She had watched the data scroll across Dr. Okafor's laptop in the cavern, three hundred meters under the earth, while the aquifer breathed its cold fog around them. She had seen the salinity maps, the flow models, the volume estimates that made Okafor go quiet for a full minute before she said, *We need to sit down for this.* Ilsa had seen what was real.

And then she had watched Casper hand armed men a fiction, smile at them, and drive away with all their lives in the bed of his truck.

She worked until the work ran out. Then there was only the fire, small because fuel was scarce, and the wounded arranged on pads around it — Mateo with his seeping leg wound, Anselmo shivering through whatever the contaminated water had put in his gut, the others gray-faced and sleeping badly. The wind came down off the ridge and combed through the coals, throwing sparks eastward like a signal no one should answer.

Casper sat apart, as he always did, at the edge of the light with his back against a wheel, cleaning instruments that were already clean. A barometer, a sling psychrometer, a wind meter the size of a cigarette case. He handled them the way other men handled rosaries.

Ilsa crossed the camp and stood over him until he looked up.

"We need to talk," she said. "Not here."

He read her face the way he read his instruments — quickly, without comment — and got up. They walked out past the tarps, past the picket line where the trucks stood nose-to-tail like animals huddled against weather, out onto the flat where the wind could take their words apart before they reached anyone else. The moon was a day from full and threw their shadows long and blue across the gravel.

"Say it," Casper said.

"You lied to them." Her voice came out flatter than she intended, stripped by the wind. "You stood in front of men with rifles and you lied, and you did it *for us*, which means we're in it now. All of us. Do you understand what you did back there?"

"I understand exactly what I did."

"Do you?" She stepped closer. "Because I don't think you do. I think you think you handed over a decoy drive and made a clever move. But that's not what happened. What happened is that six people who have told the truth about this aquifer since the day we found it are now attached to your lie. If that drive gets checked — if anyone runs those numbers against the seismic record — every one of us becomes a conspirator. Okafor. Mateo. Me. You didn't just lie to Ferro's men, Casper. You lied *as* us."

He didn't flinch. That was the thing about him that unnerved her most — not the silence, not the way he seemed to be listening to some frequency beneath the conversation, but the stillness. Most men, accused, would shift their weight. He just stood in the wind and let it hit him.

"The seismic record is why it'll hold," he said. "Ferro's second team will compare the decoy against the raw data eventually. But the decoy wasn't built to survive forensics. It was built to buy forty-eight hours. By the time anyone pulls the thread, we're in Esquel and the data's out of our hands."

"And if we don't make Esquel? If an axle goes, or a pass is blocked, or they catch us — then what are we? We're the survey team that falsified its own findings. We're the ones who poisoned the well, so to speak." She heard her own voice rise and forced it down. "You've taken the one thing we had left. The truth. You've spent it."

"Yes," he said.

Just that. No defense, no softening. *Yes,* the way a man admits to a debt.

Ilsa turned away and walked a few paces, because looking at him was making it worse. The moonlit plain stretched to the horizon, silver-gray, featureless except for the wind-carved hollows that held shadow like water. Somewhere out there, behind them, the ground breathed fog into the dawn. Somewhere ahead of them, Esquel, and a journalist whose name Casper wouldn't say aloud, and a story that would either save the aquifer or bury it under a minister's signature.

"When you told us," she said, "around the fire. About the flood. The forecast you changed."

"I didn't change it. I signed it. There's a difference, and the difference matters, but either way the number on the page was wrong and ninety-one people died believing it." His voice didn't waver. It also didn't warm. It was a report, delivered to the correct authority. "Go on."

"Why should I believe anything you tell me now?" She turned back to face him. "You want me to trust you. You want Okafor to trust you. You're asking us to bet the aquifer — the biggest freshwater find in a century, maybe ever — on the judgment of a man who has already sold the truth once. Not once. Twice, counting today. So convince me. Tell me why this time is different, and don't tell me it's because you feel bad. Feeling bad is cheap. Everyone feels bad afterward."

For a long moment he said nothing. The wind moved between them, carrying grit that ticked against the truck panels like slow rain. Out at the edge of the camp, one of the wounded coughed, a wet sound that went on too long.

"You're right," Casper said finally. "There's nothing I can tell you that proves anything. Trust isn't provable. If it were, it wouldn't be trust." He crouched, slowly, and picked up a flat stone, turned it in his fingers. "But I can tell you what actually happened. Not the version I gave you at the fire. The whole one."

"Then do that."

He set the stone down again, deliberately, as if putting something back where it belonged.

"Twelve years ago I worked flood forecasting for a river basin authority in the north. Good job. Clean numbers, real stakes, and I was good at it — I could read a basin the way I can read this sky. And there was a storm coming that the models said was going to crest higher than the levees. My models. My numbers said evacuate the lower town." He paused. "My director said the cost of a false evacuation — lawsuits, panic, the governor's office calling twice a day — was worse than the risk. He told me to adjust the confidence interval. And I want to be honest with you, Ilsa, because I think you'll know whether I'm lying: I didn't need much pushing. I wanted to be pushed. I wanted someone to share the weight of the decision, and he offered, and I took it. I widened the error bars until the forecast said *probably fine.*"

The wind gusted. He waited it out.

"It wasn't fine. The town drowned in four hours. Ninety-one people. And afterward there was an inquiry, and my director testified that the adjusted forecast reflected his direction and his alone — which was true, technically, and it destroyed him, and it should have destroyed me too, but I'd already run. New name, new trade, south, dry country. I came here because nothing here floods. Because in a place with no water, nobody needs a man who reads water." He looked at her, and in the moonlight his face was older than it had been at the fire, or maybe just less guarded. "That's the whole story. Not that I was pressured. That I *wanted* to be. That's the part I left out, and it's the part that matters."

Ilsa stood very still. She had expected a defense. She had prepared, on the walk out from the fire, a whole architecture of counterarguments, and now they stood in her chest with nowhere to go.

"That's not a reason to trust you," she said, but her voice had lost its edge and she knew he heard it.

"No. It's a reason to know exactly what you're trusting. There's a difference." He straightened. "Here's what I can offer that isn't a promise. Every decision from here to Esquel, I make in the open. You watch me make them. If you see me start to shade something — a route, a risk, a number — you call it, in front of everyone, and we decide together. I'm not asking you to believe I'm cured. I'm asking you to keep me where you can see me."

"And if you shade something anyway?"

"Then you'll have learned what I am twelve years later than you should have, and you'll still have time to stop me. Probably." Something moved at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile. "I'm a better liar than I am a forecaster. That's not a boast. It's a warning label."

Despite herself — despite everything — Ilsa felt something loosen in her shoulders. Not trust. Nothing so clean as trust. But the specific relief of hearing a man describe his own rot accurately, without anesthesia. She had served under officers who couldn't do it. She had watched Dr. Okafor struggle toward it for eleven days in the dark before managing a piece of it.

"My father kept sheep out here," she said, before she decided to say it. "On the dry side. He used to say the worst thing about a drought wasn't the thirst. It was that after enough years, you stopped trusting rain. You'd see clouds and think, *that won't come to anything.* You'd stop preparing for it. And then when the rain finally came, it caught everyone wrong-footed, because nobody believed it anymore." She looked at him. "I think you're the rain, Casper. I think somewhere under all that lying there's something true trying to fall, and nobody believes it anymore, including you."

He didn't answer right away. When he did, his voice was rougher. "Your father sounds smarter than me."

"He drowned in the flood of '99," Ilsa said. "So no. He was exactly as smart as everyone else, which is to say not smart enough, which is why I'm standing here asking you these questions instead of trusting you like a fool." She hugged her arms against the cold. "Here's where I am. I watched you lie to armed men today, and it might be the only reason I'm alive tonight. That's a fact. And I watched you build that lie out of our truth, and that's also a fact, and both facts are going to stay true no matter how this ends. I don't forgive it. I don't condemn it yet, either. I'm going to watch you, like you said. Every decision. In the open."

"Fair terms."

"One more term." She stepped closer, close enough that the wind couldn't fully take her voice. "When we get to Esquel — when the data goes to this journalist, whoever she is — you give your real name. To her, and to whatever comes after. No more running. If we're spending the truth, we spend all of it, starting with yours. Otherwise this whole thing is just you laundering your conscience through our aquifer."

Something flickered across his face — fear, actual fear, the first she'd ever seen on him — and then settled into something steadier underneath it.

"All right," he said quietly. "All of it. My name too."

She studied him for a long moment, looking for the seam in it, the place where the lie would show if there was one. She found nothing, which proved nothing, which was the whole problem with trust. But her father had also taught her that at some point you had to drink the water, because dying of caution was still dying.

They walked back toward the fire together. As they came into the ring of tarps, Mateo lifted his head from his pad, fever-bright, and croaked, "Everything all right?"

"Peachy," Ilsa said. "Go back to sleep."

But Casper had stopped. He was standing at the edge of the firelight with his head tilted, the way he tilted it when he was listening to something below the range of ordinary hearing, and Ilsa followed his gaze up to the ridgeline — where the wind, which had blown steadily from the west all evening, had gone suddenly, completely still.

Then it reversed. A breath of air came down off the basalt, warm and smelling of dust and diesel, from the east. From the direction of the pass.

Casper pulled the wind meter from his jacket, thumbed it alive, and watched the reading. Whatever it said, his face went the color of the moonlight.

"They know," he said. "The decoy didn't hold. Someone's already driving hard to catch us — and they're coming downwind, so they'll smell our exhaust before we hear their engines."

Ilsa looked east, at the empty dark, and felt the night tilt beneath her.

"How long do we have?"

"If they push through the night?" Casper was already moving toward the trucks, waking the camp with a low, urgent voice. "Dawn."
