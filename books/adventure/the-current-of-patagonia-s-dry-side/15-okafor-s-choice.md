## 15. Okafor's Choice

Dr. Adaeze Okafor had spent thirty years learning to read rock the way other people read faces, and for thirty years the rock had never lied to her. It was people who lied—beautifully, efficiently, with grant applications and progress reports—and she had told herself for eleven days in the dark that her silence was a kind of armor, a shield raised over her team. Now, sitting on an ammunition crate in a cavern that could feed a nation, she understood it had been something else entirely.

It had been a down payment.

The camp below was quieter than it had been in weeks. Casper Underhill's arrival had changed the acoustics of their exile—the scrape of his boots on scree, the way Ilsa shadowed him now with a wariness that was becoming something else, the low argumentative hum of men who had been given a plan after too long without one. Two wounded were stable. Tomás's fever had broken at dawn. The contaminated seep water had done its worst and no more, and Okafor had logged every symptom in her field book with the same detached precision she used for stratigraphy, because if she let herself feel what she'd allowed to happen to them, she would not be able to do what needed doing next.

She climbed the switchback trail alone, past the rope anchors Kestrel had driven into limestone, up to the ledge where the sinkhole opened its gray eye to the sky. Dawn was coming to the dry side—that pale, apologetic light that made even badlands look briefly forgiven. She sat where the fog rose around her, cool and sweet-smelling, the breath of a sea that had been sleeping under Patagonia since before the Andes finished shrugging themselves upright.

Fossil water. She still said the words to herself like a prayer she didn't fully believe she'd earned.

* * *

She had known about Ferro from the beginning. That was the sentence she had been avoiding for six years, and it fit itself into the cavern air now with the ease of a key finding its lock.

Not Marisol Ferro by name—not at first. In 2019 there had been a consortium: three shell companies nested inside each other like geological strata, funding the Kestrel permit through a ministry program designed to look like public science. Okafor had run the checks herself. She'd been younger then, hungrier, standing in a ministry corridor in Buenos Aires with a proposal that four review boards had called *speculative* and one had called *a fishing expedition with a government letterhead*. The money arrived anyway, clean-looking, well-documented, attached to a liaison named Duarte who smiled too readily and asked, once, very casually, whether the survey would include groundwater mapping.

She should have walked away then. She told herself she had considered it. But the truth was simpler and uglier: the consortium's money was the only money, and the aquifer—if it existed—was the discovery of a lifetime, and Dr. Adaeze Okafor had decided, in the private parliament of her own ambition, that she could take the money and control the outcome. That she was smart enough, careful enough, principled enough to accept poisoned funding and stay clean.

She thought now of her mother's proverb, the one about the woman who carried fire in her hands and swore she wouldn't get burned.

For two years the arrangement held. She filed reports that said less than she knew. When the consortium's requests for interim data grew insistent, she delayed. When Duarte asked directly for the seismic profiles, she told him the equipment had failed—a lie so smooth she'd surprised herself with it. And when the ministry began asking why Kestrel's timeline kept slipping, she absorbed the pressure the way bedrock absorbs stress: silently, structurally, storing it against some future fracture.

Then the funding shifted hands. The shells consolidated. A new name appeared on the correspondence—M. Ferro, Director, Agua Austral Holdings—and the tone of the letters changed. No longer requests. Timetables. Deliverables. A clause she hadn't noticed in the original contract surfaced like a fault line under load: *all survey findings remain the intellectual property of the financing party until jointly released.*

Jointly released. Meaning: released when they said. Meaning: a freshwater sea beneath three provinces, discovered with public roads and public universities and a public minister's signature, owned privately until a woman in a Buenos Aires office decided the world was ready.

Okafor had gone to a lawyer. The lawyer read the contract, looked at her over his glasses, and said, "Doctor, did you read this before you signed it?"

She had. Of course she had. She'd read all forty pages, and she'd signed anyway, because the alternative was losing the expedition, and she had told herself that possession was nine-tenths of the law and that science, once published, could not be un-published. She had a plan: reach the aquifer first, verify it, publish fast, make the discovery so loud and so public that ownership became irrelevant. Facts as fortification. Truth as a wall built faster than the money could climb it.

And then the ground had opened beneath Team Kestrel, literally, and swallowed the plan whole.

* * *

"Dr. Okafor."

Ilsa Reyes came up the trail with the silence that seemed bred into her, bow slung across her back though there was nothing left to hunt down here but time. She settled onto the ledge a meter away, close enough for conversation, far enough for respect. Below them the sinkhole breathed its morning fog into the light.

"You're not sleeping," Ilsa said.

"I'm thinking."

"That's worse." A ghost of a smile. Then it faded, and Ilsa looked out at the horizon, where the land ran flat and rust-colored to the edge of seeing. "Underhill says two days. Maybe less. He says the convoy will have drilling permits. Signed ones."

"I know. I heard."

"He also says you have a decision to make." Ilsa turned to face her, and in the growing light Okafor saw how much the last month had carved out of her—the scout who had once moved through these badlands like weather now sat like stone. "He won't say it to your face. He thinks it's not his place. But everyone down there is waiting for you to decide what we are. Refugees or witnesses."

Okafor was quiet for a long moment. The fog curled past them both, rising from the dark, carrying the cold mineral breath of deep water.

"Do you know what I did, Ilsa? Before any of this?"

"You found the aquifer."

"I sold it first." She heard her own voice come out flat, clinical, the voice she used for describing things too large to feel. "Six years ago. I signed a contract with the same people who sent those trucks. I took their money because it was the only money, and I told myself I could outrun the terms. Publish first, publish loud, make ownership meaningless." She shook her head slowly. "Every disaster I've ever studied started with someone saying *I can control this.*"

Ilsa said nothing. Down in the camp, faintly, someone laughed—Tomás, probably, well enough to joke again—and the sound drifted up through the fog like something from another country.

"The injured," Okafor went on. "Vidal's leg. Priya's kidneys. That water they drank in the lower gallery—I tested it myself, and I knew within a day it was seep contamination, brine pocket, my responsibility to map and I hadn't mapped it yet because we were still celebrating." Her hands closed in her lap. "They are lying on cots in a cave drinking filtered water through a straw because I wanted one more week of glory before I went public. One more week to confirm the extent, date the recharge, make the paper unassailable. One more week of being the woman who found the water."

"You were protecting the science," Ilsa said, and it wasn't quite forgiveness, wasn't quite accusation. It was the question underneath both.

"I was protecting myself wearing the science as a coat." Okafor stood. Her knees cracked—fifty-four years old, three weeks underground, and the body keeping its own honest ledger. "Underhill is right. If Ferro's convoy reaches this site with signed permits, everything becomes legal. That's the genius of it. They don't need to kill us. They just need paperwork. A fence, a drill rig, a court order, and the greatest freshwater reserve on the continent is a holding company's asset, extracted province by province until some future generation reads about it in a history of the drought wars."

"So we run. We carry the wounded out, we go north, we give the data to Underhill's journalist—"

"And then what?" Okafor turned to face her fully. "A story. Page one for a week. A scandal, an inquiry, a commission that meets for eighteen months while the permits sit in a ministry drawer and lawyers argue about clause fourteen. I've watched inquiries, Ilsa. They move at the speed of sedimentation." She looked back down into the breathing dark. "No. If this ends me, it ends me properly."

"What does that mean?"

"It means the data doesn't go to one journalist in one city where it can be buried, bought, or litigated. It means everything goes out at once. Full dataset, full contract, my name on the cover letter explaining exactly how the financing worked and exactly what I signed and exactly why I stayed silent. Coordinates, chemistry, flow rates, the seep contamination that hurt my own team—all of it, public, simultaneous, to every ministry, every university, every press office that has a fax machine or a satellite uplink. Not a leak. A flood." She almost smiled at that. "You don't fight a flood with a fence. You open every gate at once and let it go where it goes."

Ilsa stared at her. "That ends your career. The contract breach alone—"

"The contract breach is the least of it. I falsified timelines. I concealed findings from a public ministry. I took money whose provenance I suspected and chose not to confirm, because confirming it would have required me to refuse it." She said each item like reading core samples. "There will be hearings. There may be charges. My name will be in the same paragraphs as hers, and for the rest of my life, whenever anyone cites the Patagonia aquifer, some footnote will say *discovered under disputed circumstances by a compromised principal investigator.*" She breathed out. "I have thought about that footnote every night for eleven days. And you know what I've concluded?"

"What?"

"A footnote is survivable. A stolen sea isn't." She met Ilsa's eyes. "I spent thirty years learning to read rock because rock doesn't negotiate. Water doesn't negotiate either. It only moves, and it takes the shape of whatever contains it. Right now the container is Ferro's contract. My job—maybe the last job I'll ever have—is to break the container."

* * *

She went down into the camp while the light was still new.

They gathered the way teams gather when the leader's posture changes—Priya sitting up despite the IV line, Vidal propped on his elbow, Tomás watching from his cot with fever-bright but clear eyes, Mendoza and Chen by the radio, and Casper Underhill at the edge of the fire ring, hat in his hands, a man visibly uncomfortable attending anything resembling a speech.

Okafor stood among them and did not perform. She had decided against performing somewhere on the climb down; these people had earned the unaudited version.

"Six years ago I signed a contract I should have burned," she said. "The money that put us here came from the people whose trucks are two days out. I knew. Not everything—but enough that knowing more would only have cost me the expedition, and I didn't want to pay that price. So I paid yours instead. The seep water. The cots. The eleven days." She let the silence hold. "I'm not asking forgiveness. I'm telling you what I should have told you in week one, because you can't choose honestly if you don't know what you're choosing."

Chen spoke first, quietly. "What's changed?"

"Nothing changed. I finally stopped lying to myself, which is slower work than it sounds." She took the waterproof case from her pack—the real drive, the one Underhill had nearly died crossing the badlands to reach, the one that had already survived one checkpoint disguised as worthless brine data. She held it up. "This goes out today. All of it. Not to one reporter in one city—to everyone. Ministries, universities, wire services, the commission in Buenos Aires, Elena Brandt, and forty other inboxes I memorized last night. Simultaneously. With a cover letter in my name that describes the financing, the contract, and my own conduct in detail. If Ferro wants to bury this, she'll need to bury it in a hundred places at once, and every burial is a headline."

Mendoza whistled low. "The upload window from here is garbage. Underhill said the relay only clears for twenty minutes at dusk."

"Then we use the twenty minutes." She looked at Underhill. "You've been quiet."

He turned the hat in his hands. "I was going to say you don't have to torch yourself to save the water. There are versions of this where you come out the other side."

"There are versions of this where I come out the other side and spend ten years testifying in rooms where her lawyers outnumber the truth," Okafor said. "I'm fifty-four, Mr. Underhill. I'd rather be a cautionary tale attached to a protected aquifer than a respected director attached to a company town." Something eased in her chest as she said it—the specific relief of a fracture finally propagating after years of stored stress. "Besides. You of all people should understand. Some debts can't be settled with money."

Something crossed his face then—quick, involuntary, like a bird startled off a fence line—and he put his hat back on and nodded once.

"Dusk, then," he said. "Twenty minutes. I'll get the antenna rigged."

* * *

They spent the day preparing, and Okafor spent it writing.

The cover letter ran nine pages. She wrote it longhand first, in the field book, because typing made her edit and editing made her flinch. She described the shell companies. She described Duarte's smile. She described clause fourteen and the lawyer's question and her own answer. She described the week she'd stolen for glory and the water her team had drunk because of it. Near the end she wrote a paragraph she suspected no lawyer would advise:

*I am the principal investigator of Survey Team Kestrel, and I am the reason our discovery was nearly taken from the public before the public knew it existed. Whatever this document costs me, it costs less than silence. The aquifer beneath these coordinates belongs to no consortium, no ministry, and no market. It belongs to the century after this one, which cannot attend this hearing, cannot sign this contract, and cannot speak except through us.*

At 6:40 in the evening, with the relay window opening, Underhill keyed the uplink and read her the signal strength like a man calling out surf heights. The files went in batches—chemistry, seismicity, hydrology, the contract scans, the letter. At 6:58 the relay dropped, as it always did, mid-transmission of the final batch.

"Eighty percent," Underhill said, checking the log. "The letter went. The contract scans went. Chemistry and coordinates went. Flow-rate tables truncated." He looked up. "Tomorrow's window gets the rest."

Okafor exhaled and found her hands were shaking—not fear, she realized, but the tremor of a structure releasing load it had carried too long. Eighty percent was enough. Eighty percent, in a hundred inboxes, was a fact the world now possessed, and facts, once published, could not be un-published. She had bet her life on that axiom once, cynically. Tonight she bet it honestly.

Ilsa appeared at her shoulder, looking at the dead transmitter. "When she finds out—"

"She already knows," Okafor said. "Or she will by morning. And then this stops being a rescue mission and becomes something else entirely." She folded the field book closed and looked south, toward the darkening horizon and the dust that was coming with it. "Get some sleep, Ilsa. Tomorrow we find out what a woman like Marisol Ferro does when the thing she came to buy announces itself to the world without her permission."

Far off, past the mesas, thunder rolled across a sky that hadn't rained in months—and Okafor, who had spent her life listening to the earth, could not tell whether it was weather, or trucks, or both.
