## 16. The Long Drive North

We made ninety kilometers before the radio told us we were dead men.

It was Ilsa who heard it first—she'd been monitoring the scanner since the checkpoint, headphones clamped over one ear, the other ear left open for the road. She pulled them off slow, like a woman taking off jewelry at a funeral.

"They've stopped believing us," she said.

Casper kept his eyes on the washboard track ahead. The convoy was three vehicles now: his truck in front with Okafor and two of the walking wounded, then the flatbed carrying the litters, then the pickup with Ilsa and the fuel drums. Behind them, four hundred kilometers of badlands they'd crossed in eleven days going the other way, and somewhere back there, Ferro's second team rolling south with better trucks and worse intentions.

"Say it plain," Casper said.

"The decoy drive showed brine contamination. That's what your doctored numbers said. But their seismic records—the real ones, from Kestrel's own instruments—they don't match brine. Brine kills the signal differently. Their geologist would have seen it within an hour of running the comparison." Ilsa turned in her seat to face him. "You bought us a day. Maybe less."

"Then we spend it well."

He said it like it was simple, and for a moment he almost believed it himself. That was the trick of driving, he'd learned long ago—you could lie to yourself at eighty kilometers an hour more easily than standing still. The engine noise filled up the places where doubt lived.

The land had changed since dawn. They'd left the true desert behind and entered country that only pretended to be habitable: scrub steppe stitched with thorn, the occasional wind-bent ñire tree leaning east like everything here leaned east, pushed by forty thousand years of the same wind. The Andes sat on the western horizon as a blue-gray wall of cloud, holding all the rain the sky owned, letting none of it through. Casper had spent twelve years in this rain shadow and still found it obscene—the mountains right there, fat with weather, while the ground below cracked like old paint.

Okafor stirred beside him. She'd been sleeping sitting up, which was a skill the last week had taught her. Her hands were stained yellow from the antiseptic they'd used on the wounded, and she smelled of diesel and iodine, and when she spoke her voice was gravel from breathing dust.

"How far to the paved road?"

"Two hundred ten kilometers. Then Esquel is another hundred and forty."

"And our friends behind us?"

"Four hours back. Closing."

She looked at him for a long moment. He could feel her doing arithmetic—the same arithmetic he'd been doing since the checkpoint, since he'd handed that decoy drive across the hood of a car to men with rifles and watched them drive away believing him. Every kilometer they gained, Ferro's people lost patience. Patience was the one resource she couldn't buy more of.

"There's a shortcut," Okafor said. "Kestrel mapped it coming down. An old ranch track through the Sierra de los Baguales—it cuts maybe sixty kilometers off."

"Sixty kilometers of what?"

"Ranch track."

"That's not an answer, Doctor."

"It's the only answer I have." She rubbed her eyes with the heel of her hand. "I won't pretend I know its condition. Nobody's driven it in years, probably. But sixty kilometers is two hours, and two hours might be the difference between Esquel and a ditch."

Casper weighed it. In storm chasing you learned a rule early: never take the road you can't see the end of when the weather's turning. But the weather here wasn't clouds. It was men in trucks, and men in trucks stayed on main tracks because their maps were printed, not lived.

"Show me," he said.

* * *

They turned west at a cairn of stones that Okafor swore marked the junction, onto a track that was less a road than a rumor—one set of wheel ruts grown over with grass, cattle skull half-buried at the entrance like a warning nobody had bothered to write down. The trucks bucked and yawed. In the mirrors, the flatbed's litters swayed, and Casper heard one of the wounded men moan with each jolt, a sound that got into his teeth and stayed there.

The man's name was Tomás. Twenty-six years old, the team's hydrologist, and the seep water had gotten into him worst of all—kidneys inflamed, fever that came and went like weather. He'd been lucid enough at the checkpoint to grip Casper's arm and say, *Don't let them have it,* and Casper had promised, and promises were a currency he'd devalued so thoroughly in his life that this one felt strange and heavy in his pocket.

An hour in, the track climbed into low hills and the ruts became stone. Casper dropped to second gear and picked his line the way he'd once picked lines through hail cores—reading the ground ahead, committing, correcting. Behind him he heard Ilsa's pickup laboring, and ahead the flatbed's driver—a quiet man named Brandt, no relation to the journalist, though the coincidence had made everyone laugh once, days ago, when laughing had still been available—was handling it well.

Then the sound came.

It wasn't loud. That was the terrible thing about mechanical failure in the field; it rarely announced itself. It was a change in pitch, a faint grinding under the engine note, and Casper's hands knew before his mind did. He eased off the throttle and the grinding stopped, and he thought, *maybe*, and then the steering went loose in his palms like the truck had developed a joint where no joint should be.

"Axle," he said. "Front left."

He brought it to a stop in a shallow draw, engine ticking, dust settling around them like a curtain falling. In the mirror he watched the other two trucks halt in a ragged line. Doors opened. People got out and stood in the wind looking at the front end of his truck the way people look at a body.

The axle hadn't broken clean. It had cracked and then bent, and the wheel canted inward at an angle that made even the unmechanical wince. Casper lay on his back in the dirt beneath the chassis for ten minutes with a wrench he didn't need, confirming what he already knew, because lying under a broken truck buys time to think and thinking is easier when your hands are busy.

When he slid out, they were all gathered. Sixteen people, counting the wounded. Two hundred kilometers of nothing in every direction. Four hours of lead time, shrinking.

"Well?" Okafor said.

"We're not fixing this. Not out here. Not today."

"Options."

"One. We strip this truck for parts and weight, transfer everything to the other two, and run. Two. We wait here and someone walks back to the paved road, thirty hours round trip if the wind's kind, and we pray Ferro's people are kind too." He stood, brushing dirt off his shoulders. "Three. We split up."

Nobody liked number three. He could see it in their faces—the way a family looks at each other when someone suggests leaving someone behind.

"I'm not splitting," Ilsa said flatly.

"You're not listening. We don't split the people. We split the load." He pointed at the flatbed. "That truck carries the litters and the worst wounded and Dr. Okafor and the data—every copy, physical and digital—and it runs straight through, no stops, straight to Esquel. My truck's dead but my truck's also the fastest thing we've got once it's fixed, and it isn't fixed, so—" he turned to Ilsa's pickup, "—you and me and whoever else fits take the light truck and the fast route, and we arrive either first or not at all, and either way the story gets out."

"And if Ferro's second team finds the flatbed?" Okafor asked.

"Brandt doesn't stop for anyone. You tell him that. Anyone waves him down, anyone stands in the road, anyone fires—he drives through. I know how that sounds. I'm asking anyway."

Okafor looked at the litters. At Tomás, whose fever had come back and who was shivering under a wool blanket in air that must have been twenty degrees. She was quiet for a long time, and Casper watched her carry the whole weight of command again, visibly, like a woman picking up furniture alone.

"He'll do it," she said finally. "Brandt will drive through. He's that kind." She looked at Casper. "And you? What's your cargo?"

"Me and Ilsa and the backup drive. And the truth about what happened at that checkpoint, in case the decoy fails and we need to explain why the world thinks this aquifer is poisoned salt water."

"You'd confess to it?"

"I'd testify to it." He heard his own voice say it and something in his chest shifted, some plate tectonics of the self. "There's a difference. Confession is for God. Testimony is for the record."

* * *

They worked for fifty minutes in the wind, and the work was good, because work is the only prayer that always answers.

They stripped the dead truck to its bones—spare fuel, water, tools, the winch cable, every liter and kilo that the living trucks could carry. They redistributed the wounded with the gentleness the clock allowed, which was not much gentleness. Tomás cried quietly while they moved him, not from pain, he insisted, but from shame, and Ilsa held his head against her shoulder and told him about the cavern, about the freshwater sea under the stone, about how he was the one who'd measured its temperature gradient, how his numbers were going to be in newspapers, in courtrooms, in laws. *Your numbers, Tomás. You found the water.* Whether it helped him or not, Casper couldn't say, but the crying stopped.

At the moment of departure, Okafor came to Casper's window. Through the glass she pressed something into his hand: a folded page from her notebook, handwritten, dense.

"If we're separated and anything happens to the flatbed, this is the summary. Coordinates, depth, flow estimates, contamination readings from the seep, and my statement about the financing. Signed." She tapped the glass. "You wanted testimony. There's mine. Don't lose it."

"I won't."

"Casper." She paused, and for the first time since he'd met her, Dr. Adaeze Okafor looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "What you did at the checkpoint. Lying to those men. I want you to know I understand exactly what it cost you to do it, given—" she didn't finish, didn't need to, "—and I want you to know that when this reaches a courtroom, I'll say so. On the record."

He nodded, because his throat had closed, and drove.

* * *

The light truck flew—or flew the way things fly in Patagonia, which is to say it bounced, slewed, and occasionally achieved genuine airborne moments over crests that Ilsa took without lifting off the throttle, because slowing down meant being caught and being caught meant everything they'd carried out of the ground had died for nothing.

They were nine kilometers past the split when the radio crackled.

"—flatbed, this is lead, come in—" Casper's own voice, recorded, playing from Ferro's frequency. Someone was spoofing them. Using his checkpoint handshake to call the flatbed, trying to raise Brandt, trying to get him to stop.

Ilsa's eyes found his across the cab. "They're hunting."

"Brandt won't answer."

"Brandt won't answer," she agreed, and keyed the radio anyway—not to transmit, just to listen, sweeping frequencies with the practiced boredom of someone who'd grown up on a station where the radio was the only company. Static. Static. Then, faint, in Spanish, a woman's voice, clipped and professional:

"—negative on visual, but the tire tracks leave the main route at the stone pile. Confirming divergence. We're turning west."

West. Toward the shortcut. Toward the flatbed, heavy and slow, full of litters.

Casper did the math and hated every digit. The second team had reached the cairn. The flatbed was perhaps forty minutes ahead of them on a worse road. And he and Ilsa were on the fast route with a choice: run for Esquel and safety, or turn back into the hills and become the decoy themselves—two people in one light truck, drawing the hunters off the wounded.

He didn't decide. His hands decided, swinging the wheel north onto a stock trail that paralleled the hills, and Ilsa watched him do it and said nothing, and after a moment she reached over and turned the radio volume up.

"Lead them," she said. "Make noise. Be interesting."

"I'm always interesting."

"Not to armed men. To armed men you want to be *visible*." She grinned, and it was a wild, exhausted, magnificent grin, the grin of a woman who had held him at arrow-point six days ago and was now choosing to die in his truck if it came to that. "Get on their frequency. Tell them where we are."

So he did. He keyed the mic and gave their position in clear, careful Spanish—the stock trail, heading north, two persons, one vehicle, carrying documents—and then he put the truck sideways into a turn and ran.

For an hour they played the game, and it was the strangest hour of his life: fleeing and broadcasting simultaneously, a hare ringing a bell. Twice he saw dust plumes swing toward them in the mirrors and corrected course to keep the plumes interested. Once a rifle shot cracked across the scrub, distant, a warning or a miss, and Ilsa laughed out loud, actually laughed, high and disbelieving.

And then the plume behind them stopped following.

It turned west. Toward the flatbed.

"No," Ilsa breathed. "No, no—we were right here—"

"They got new orders." Casper's mouth had gone dry. "Someone smarter looked at a map. Or someone counted trucks. We came out of the badlands with three vehicles. They've only ever seen one."

The flatbed was forty minutes ahead of four armed men on a road it couldn't leave. Casper floored the accelerator and the little truck screamed north, and in the mirror the dust of the hunters shrank, and ahead the hills rolled on, indifferent, ancient, keeping their water.

"Hold on," he said. "Hold on, Tomás. Hold on, all of you. I'm coming around."

Ilsa looked at him. "Around through where?"

He pointed east, where the land fell away toward the dry lake beds he'd crossed years ago, in another life, chasing another kind of storm—the hard pan that went on forever, featureless and fast, the kind of ground where a man who knew the old lake shores could drive at full throttle while pursuers crawled the edges.

"Through the salinas," he said. "Trust me. I've driven them before."

"When?"

"Twelve years ago." He didn't look at her. "Under a different name."
