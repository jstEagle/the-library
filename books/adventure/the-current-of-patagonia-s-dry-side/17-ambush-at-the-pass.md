## 17. Ambush at the Pass

Ilsa Reyes had grown up in country that lied to you. The sierras of her childhood looked close enough to touch and took a full day's walk to reach; the streams ran clear over stones that were not stones but bones of old floods. So she had learned early to distrust the way land presented itself, and she trusted the canyon ahead of them the way she would trust a stranger with kind eyes and no name.

"Stop," she said.

Casper braked. The convoy—two trucks and the ambulance van they'd rigged from Kestrel's supply vehicle—shuddered to a halt in a curtain of its own dust. Ahead, the track narrowed where two walls of rust-colored rock leaned toward each other like conspirators, leaving a gap maybe forty meters wide. Beyond it the pass opened onto the long gravel descent toward Esquel, ninety kilometers of bad road and then asphalt and then witnesses.

"What is it?" Casper asked. He was driving with his forearms bare, sleeves rolled, and she noticed again how calm his hands were. A man who had spent twelve years running should have had nervous hands. His were still as ballast.

"The walls are too clean."

He looked at her. "Clean?"

"No talus. No slump. Every other cut in this range has rockfall piled against it like snow against a fence." She nodded at the gap. "That one doesn't. Somebody cleared it recently enough that the wind hasn't dressed it back up."

Casper was quiet for a moment. Then he did something she hadn't seen him do before: he turned off the engine entirely and rolled down his window, and just listened. The idling trucks behind them coughed and settled. Wind moved through the canyon with a low, hollow note, the sound of breath across the mouth of a bottle.

"Southwest wind," he said finally. "Fifteen, maybe eighteen knots. Steady all morning." He pointed through the windshield at the western wall, where the sun struck the rock full on. "And look at the dust coming off that face. It's blowing *into* the canyon. If anyone's up there, we're downwind of them. They can hear us. We can't hear them."

"You can tell all that from dust?"

"I can tell all that from dust," he said, "because I've spent twelve years learning what dust does when nobody's watching it." He picked up the radio. "Okafor. Hold position. Nobody comes forward."

Through the windshield Ilsa studied the gap in the walls, and the more she studied it the less she liked it. There was a truck-sized boulder sitting oddly at the southern lip, as if placed rather than fallen. There was a fold of shadow near the base of the northern wall that didn't match the geometry of the rock above it. And there was the silence—not the honest silence of empty country, which always had something in it, some bird or insect or settling stone, but the manufactured silence of held breath.

"They're here," she said. "Aren't they."

"Ferro's second team." Casper's voice was flat, almost bored, and she understood suddenly that the boredom was armor. "The ones who figured out my decoy drive didn't match the seismic record. She wouldn't send amateurs to fix an arithmetic problem."

"How many?"

"If I were them? Eight men, two vehicles, one blocking the far mouth of the pass and one high on the north wall for observation and interdiction. You don't need more than eight to stop a convoy in a slot like that. You just need to be patient and own the geometry."

"And if you're wrong?"

"Then I've insulted eight innocent rocks." He put the truck in reverse. "We go around."

"There is no around. You told me that yourself. This is the only cut through the ridge for sixty kilometers. South of here it's dune field; north it's lava country that eats axles for breakfast."

"I know what I told you." He reversed twenty meters, thirty, until the convoy behind him had room to turn, and then he stopped again and got out, and stood in the open with his head tipped back like a man reading scripture written on the sky.

Ilsa climbed out beside him. The heat came up off the ground in visible shimmer. Behind them, in the ambulance van, Dr. Okafor leaned out the window, waiting, saying nothing, trusting him or pretending to. In the second truck, Mateo cradled his splinted arm and watched them both with the expression of a man who had already decided the day would end badly and was merely confirming the details.

Casper raised one hand, fingers spread, and held it motionless. Then he turned it slowly, like a weathervane finding itself.

"She's changed the wind," he said.

"What?"

"Since this morning. It was southwest, steady. Now—" he turned his palm again, frowning—"it's coming out of the northwest, gusting, and it's warmer. That's not weather. Weather doesn't pivot forty degrees in an hour in a rain shadow. Something's feeding it." He looked south, along the line of the ridge, and Ilsa followed his gaze and saw it: a haze on the horizon, brown-gray, low, like smoke without fire. Like the land itself beginning to stand up.

"Dust storm?"

"Hausto del viento—the wind's harvest. When a front drags across the plateau it strips the dry pans and throws the whole topsoil into the air. I chased these for years before—" he stopped himself, the old reflex, editing his own biography mid-sentence. "They move fast. Thirty, forty kilometers an hour once they're organized. Visibility drops to nothing. And the wind ahead of the wall—" he smiled, and it was not a comforting smile, "—the wind ahead of the wall turns. Everything ahead of it gets pulled backward into it. Into the storm."

Ilsa felt the idea assemble itself in her mind the way a trap assembles itself under leaves. "You want to let the storm push us through."

"I want to let the storm make their positions worthless. Right now they own the geometry because they can see and we can't. In forty minutes nobody will see anything. Their rifleman on the wall will be shooting blind into a dust wall that's moving faster than he can adjust. Their vehicles at the mouth of the pass will be blinded from the same direction we are." He was walking back toward the lead truck as he talked, pulling his goggles from the dash, tossing her a pair. "But there's a window. The gust front arrives before the main body—five minutes, maybe ten, when the wind has turned and the dust hasn't yet. During the window, everyone in that canyon hears everything. Sound carries on the front like a drumhead. So we wait until the front hits, we let it announce us, and then we run inside the wall itself, where they can't follow and can't aim."

"And if the timing's wrong by five minutes," Ilsa said, "we're in a slot canyon between armed men and a moving wall of sand, in vehicles, with wounded."

"Yes," Casper said simply. "That's the bet."

* * *

They waited forty minutes in the shadow of the trucks, engines off, water rationed, and Ilsa used every minute of it hating how much she had come to trust him.

It wasn't the navigation—that uncanny instinct he had for country he claimed never to have crossed, the way he'd read the dunes during the trek out like a man returning to a house he'd lived in. It wasn't even the decoy drive, the cold arithmetic with which he'd handed Ferro's men a lie worth millions. It was smaller things. The way he checked on the wounded without being asked, kneeling by Marta's fever with a patience that seemed borrowed from someone gentler than himself. The way he'd given Okafor the radio during the worst hours and let her command her own people, understanding that leadership was a thing you could starve. The way he'd sat up all night outside Ilsa's bedroll after their fight about the decoy—not guarding her, exactly, just being present, the way you stand watch over a fire you're afraid will die.

She had confronted him about the falsified forecast, twelve years ago, the drowned town, the name he'd shed. He hadn't defended himself. He'd said: *I know what I am. The question is whether knowing it makes me useful or just dangerous.* And she had not been able to answer him then, and could not answer him now, and suspected the answer would arrive whether she wanted it or not, wearing whatever clothes the moment provided.

Okafor crouched beside her at the tailgate, watching Casper stand his strange vigil at the hood, hand raised to the wind like a man taking a pulse.

"You trust him?" the doctor asked quietly.

"I trust his craft," Ilsa said. "I'm still deciding about the rest of him."

"That may be the correct order of operations." Okafor's eyes went to the southern haze, which had thickened and risen, a bruise-colored rampart now, eating the horizon. "In medicine we say: first, do no harm. But there's a corollary nobody teaches. Sometimes the only available choice is between harms, and then the question isn't whether your hands stay clean. It's whose hands you put them in."

The wind shifted.

Ilsa felt it before she heard it—a pressure change, a warmth rolling over them like the breath of an opened oven, carrying the smell of hot stone and ancient dust. Casper's raised hand trembled once, steadied.

"Gust front," he said. "Ninety seconds. Everyone in vehicles. Windows up, goggles on, headlights on—we'll be inside the wall in daylight and it'll be midnight. Mateo, you drive second truck. Okafor, keep the wounded low in the van. Ilsa—" he turned to her, and behind the goggles his eyes were very steady, "—you ride with me. I need someone who can shoot straight when I can't see."

"Why can't you see?"

"Because I'll be driving." He said it lightly, but she heard the weight underneath: he was putting himself in the seat where he couldn't act, couldn't shoot, couldn't do anything but hold a line through blindness, and giving her the gun because he trusted her hands more than his own.

The front hit them like a door slamming.

The temperature dropped six degrees in a breath. The wind swung hard from the northwest and began to scream down the canyon, and with it came the sound—everything at once, the whole plateau talking, rock and sand and the groan of the trucks rocking on their springs. Dust came first in threads, then sheets, then a total brown obliteration, and through it Ilsa saw the far mouth of the pass flicker and vanish like a candle pinched out.

"Now," Casper said, and started the engine.

They went into the storm at speed.

* * *

Inside the dust wall the world ceased to exist except as instruments and instinct. The headlights made two feeble gold coins that illuminated nothing. Sand hissed against the windshield like static. Casper drove with half his attention on the wheel and half somewhere else entirely—Ilsa could see it, the way his eyes moved, sampling, integrating—and the truck swayed and bucked over ground she couldn't see and he never lifted, never hesitated, holding some invisible thread of grade and surface that existed only in his head.

"Two hundred meters to the narrows," he shouted over the roar. "When we hit the gap, look high right! Their overwatch position!"

"You want me to shoot at a position I can't confirm?"

"I want you to *cover* it. Put three rounds into the base of that shadow fold as we pass. Not to kill—to make whoever's there keep his head down while we're in the kill zone. Ten seconds of courtesy, that's all I'm asking."

The canyon walls appeared out of the brown like the sides of a ship. The wind funneled, doubled, became a physical hand shoving the truck sideways, and Casper corrected with small, precise movements, a surgeon working inside a hurricane. Then the narrows swallowed them, and the sound changed—amplified, reverberant, the storm now playing the canyon like a pipe organ—and Ilsa saw it: the shadow fold on the north wall, and within it, a flicker of movement. Metal. A scope catching her headlight.

She rolled her window down two inches, braced the rifle against the door frame, and fired three times into the base of the fold. The muzzle flash lit the canyon like lightning. She saw a figure flinch back, saw a rifle barrel swing toward them and stall, the shooter blind and deaf and pinned by a woman who could not possibly have seen him either and had shot anyway, on faith, on Casper's arithmetic.

The second truck flashed past behind them. The van. The gap opened, the walls fell away, and they were through—through, into the open descent, the storm roaring at their backs—

Headlights bloomed out of the dust ahead.

Two vehicles, parked nose-out across the track, blocking the lower mouth of the pass. Men beside them, rifles up, firing at the sound of engines, wild, panicked, the storm stripping their aim apart. A round starred the windshield. Another punched through the door panel an inch below Ilsa's elbow.

"Casper—"

"I see them!" He wrenched the wheel. The truck left the track, slewed across the gravel shoulder, and for one endless second Ilsa was certain they would roll—but the ground caught them, firm, a hidden shelf of caliche he must have known was there, must have *remembered*, and they skimmed past the roadblock's flank through a gap no wider than a coffin lid. Rifle fire chased them and lost them. The second truck followed their tracks. The van followed the second truck.

Behind them, in the mirror, the roadblock dissolved into the brown. Ahead, the dust thinned in ragged curtains, and gray light leaked through, and the long gravel run to Esquel unrolled like a sentence finally finished.

Ilsa started to laugh—started to—and then the rear window exploded.

Not the storm. Not sand. A single aimed round, fired from somewhere behind and above, from the overwatch position she had pinned for ten seconds and no more. The glass came inward in a white bloom and something struck her between the shoulder blades with the force of a falling tree, and the world tilted, and Casper was shouting her name, and the last thing she registered clearly was his hands—those still, calm, ballast-steady hands—leaving the wheel to catch her, which meant the truck was now guided by nothing but memory and faith, which meant he had chosen her over the road, which meant—

The dust closed over everything, and she let it.

* * *

She surfaced in pieces. Engine noise. The smell of blood, hers, copper-bright. Bandage pressure high on her back, expert, urgent—Okafor's voice counting something, pulse probably, counting her pulse. The truck moving fast, too fast for the road, suspension hammering.

"She's awake," Okafor said. "Ilsa. Stay down. The round went through the meat of your shoulder, missed the chest cavity by the width of a prayer. You were lucky."

"Lucky," Ilsa managed. Her mouth tasted like pennies and sand.

"In this business," Casper said from the front, his voice scraped raw, "that's the technical term."

She turned her head. Through the shattered rear window the dust wall was falling behind them, unraveling into ordinary afternoon, and beyond it, faint, two points of light: vehicles, following. Patient. Unhurried. The second team had regrouped, and they knew now exactly what they were chasing, and they had all the time in the world.

"Elena Brandt," Ilsa said. Her voice came out cracked, barely hers. "How far?"

Casper looked at her in the mirror, and she saw in his face the cost of the third path laid bare—the arithmetic he'd done in the narrows, ten seconds of covering fire traded for a bullet, the debt accruing in bodies now, hers added to the ledger.

"Ninety kilometers," he said. "Hold on to the truth, Ilsa. We're going to need both hands to carry it."
