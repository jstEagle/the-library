## 18. What Water Is Worth

The boardroom of Meridian Holdings occupied the forty-first floor of a tower in Puerto Madero, and Marisol Ferro had always thought it looked like the inside of a lie—glass and pale wood and a horizon of river that glittered like something clean. She stood at the window while the others argued, watching a container ship crawl toward the delta, and let them wear themselves out before she turned around.

They had been at it for two hours.

"The numbers don't care about your conscience," said Voss, who represented the Dutch money and wore his conscience like an accessory he'd misplaced years ago. "Brackish contamination across sixty percent of the surveyed field. That's what Underhill's drive says. That's what our own hydrologist confirmed from the seismic overlays. It's a dead aquifer. We write it off, we recover what we can through the insurance structures, and we move on."

"It's not a dead aquifer," said Renata Quiroga.

Renata was new to the table—eighteen months, brought in when her family's agricultural holding absorbed a debt position—and she said things like that in a flat voice that made the room go quiet the way a dropped glass goes quiet. She had a tablet open in front of her and she didn't look at it. She looked at Voss the way you look at a man across a card table when you've already seen his hand.

"Say that again," Voss said.

"You heard me." Renata turned the tablet around. On it was a page of spectral analysis, dense with figures Ferro could read at a glance because Ferro had paid for the instruments that produced them. "The brine signature is concentrated in the upper strata. Below two hundred meters, the conductivity readings drop to drinking standard. The decoy data is layered wrong. Whoever built it understood the geology well enough to make it convincing to a minister and not convincing enough to fool anyone who's ever drilled a well."

Ferro felt the room tilt slightly, the way a ship tilts when the ballast shifts. She kept her face still. She had learned stillness in rooms like this one, twenty years ago, when she was the only woman among men who pronounced her name wrong on purpose.

"Decoy," Voss repeated. "What do you mean, decoy."

"I mean," Renata said, "that we were handed a forgery, and someone signed drilling permits against it, and if this reaches a court before we unwind those permits, every one of us is exposed."

* * *

Ferro let them fight for another hour. It was useful to watch. A syndicate was a machine made of people, and machines showed you their load-bearing parts when they strained. Voss wanted out—he always wanted out, that was his function, the exit liquidity. The brothers from Salta wanted escalation; they'd spent real money on the convoy, on the bribed minister, on the armed men now sitting in Esquel with nothing to show for it, and men who spend on violence want violence to have been worth it. Renata wanted—Ferro wasn't sure yet what Renata wanted, which made her the most dangerous person at the table.

When the shouting burned down to embers, Ferro spoke.

"Underhill gave us a drive," she said. "One drive. He told my advance team the survey instruments were damaged by sand ingress, that most of the raw logs were corrupted, that this was what Kestrel managed to salvage. He cried a little. He was very good."

"You believed him," Voss said.

"I authorized accepting his account under field conditions, yes, because the alternative was shooting six government employees in a canyon and explaining that to a parliamentary commission." She let that sit. "I don't believe him anymore. I believe he looked me in the eye over a satellite phone, agreed to bring me the data, and then handed my men a fabrication polished enough to pass a customs inspection. Which means one of two things. Either he decided somewhere between the sinkhole and the checkpoint that he valued the survey team's lives over his own debts—" she paused, "—or he never intended to deliver anything true, and the whole extraction was theater staged for my benefit."

"Which is it?" asked one of the Salta brothers.

"That's what I intend to find out," Ferro said. "And then I intend to close the account."

She dismissed them. Voss left first, fast, already composing his denials. The brothers left together, murmuring. Renata stayed.

"You're going to put a price on him," Renata said. Not a question.

"He defrauded us. He's the only living link between this table and Survey Team Kestrel, and Kestrel is the only proof the aquifer exists in a form anyone will believe. Yes. I'm going to put a price on him."

"And the aquifer itself?"

Ferro went back to the window. Down below, the river carried its freight of silt and history toward the sea, indifferent as arithmetic.

"The aquifer," she said, "is worth more than any of you understand. Voss sees a write-off. The brothers see a hostage. You see—" she glanced at Renata's tablet, "—apparently you see the truth, which is refreshing and inconvenient. But none of you see the actual asset. Freshwater under the driest rangeland in the country, fossil water, older than the mountains, replenishing at a rate so slow it might as well be zero. That's not a commodity. That's a century of leverage over every farm, every town, every election south of the Colorado. Whoever controls it doesn't sell water. They decide who gets to exist."

Renata was quiet for a moment. "And you think you can still control it. After this. After a parliamentary commission starts asking why a survey team vanished for three weeks and came back with a story about armed convoys."

"The commission won't convene unless someone makes noise," Ferro said. "So we make sure no one makes noise. Starting with the man who has been making noise his whole life without anyone noticing."

She picked up her coat. At the door she stopped.

"One more thing, Renata. Your family's debt position—the one that bought you that chair. You might ask yourself who structured it, and what happens to it if I decide you're a liability too."

Renata held her gaze without blinking. "I'll ask myself," she said. "Right after I ask why your advance team's radio log shows them confirming the decoy data north eleven minutes before Underhill handed it over."

Ferro kept her face level, but behind her ribs something cold turned over. Eleven minutes. She hadn't read the radio logs herself; she'd had them summarized. Someone inside her own operation had moved first, had confirmed the worthless data before verification, had either been lazy or bought—or had wanted the failure locked in.

Corruption, she thought, walking out into the marble corridor, is the only business that eats its own inventory.

* * *

Her office was in the same tower but a different world: no glass, heavy curtains, a desk that had belonged to her father and before him to a man he'd ruined. She poured herself two fingers of whisky she wouldn't drink and sat down with the file.

Casper Underhill. The name was false—that much she'd known going in, had known since the first background check came back with seams showing. A storm chaser with twelve years of clean paper and no past. No tax history before a certain year. No photographs. A man who had shed himself somewhere and grown a new skin in the rain shadow, where nothing rots because nothing stays wet.

She'd liked him for it, if liking was the word. She recognized the architecture. She had built her own over a father's ruins.

She opened the folder of intercepts. Her people had pulled his satellite traffic for the past week—legal gray, entirely routine. Most of it was navigation chatter, weather downloads, position pings. But there was a pattern: short bursts, encrypted, routed through a relay in Esquel, always within an hour of local dawn. Twelve of them over five months. Too regular for a debtor hiding from collectors. Too disciplined for a man living hand to mouth.

She had her analyst run the timing against known press activity in Patagonia.

The answer came back in ninety minutes, and when it did, Ferro laughed—one dry note, alone in the curtained office, the laugh of a woman finding a receipt for something she'd suspected but never priced.

Elena Brandt. Investigative correspondent, byline history going back fifteen years, a reputation for being unbuyable and unbriable and therefore, in Ferro's taxonomy, insane. Brandt had published three pieces in the last two years about water rights in the south—careful, sourced, infuriatingly incomplete pieces, the kind written by a reporter receiving fragments from a source who would not give a name.

A source who fed small truths for years without giving his name.

Ferro set down the printout and looked at it for a long time. So that was the shape of it. Underhill hadn't stumbled into Kestrel's disappearance. He'd been building toward something for years—a slow accumulation of leverage, fragment by fragment, waiting for a story big enough to trade it all for. And she had handed him the story. She had financed the permit, dispatched the convoy, cornered him at a fuel stop with two weeks on the clock. She had assembled, at her own expense, the exact pressure required to turn a frightened little informant into a man with nothing left to lose.

He'd played her the way he read weather. Patiently. From a long way off.

She should have been furious. She was, in a low banked way, the way a fire is furious in a furnace. But underneath the fury there was something worse: respect, and the recognition that respect was more dangerous than anger, because it meant she could predict him. Men you despise are opaque. Men you understand are maps.

* * *

She worked until midnight.

First, containment. The minister who'd signed the drilling permits received a call from a lawyer he'd never met, conveying terms he understood immediately: resign for health reasons within the month, and the recordings of certain meetings would remain in a vault. The Salta brothers' convoy in Esquel was ordered to stand down and disperse—officially as cost control, actually because armed men near a hospital where a wounded survey scout lay recovering were a liability with a body count attached.

Second, the fracture. She called Voss personally and offered him his exit at par, contingent on silence, knowing he'd take it and hate it, knowing his hatred would be quiet because hatred with money attached always is. To the Salta brothers she said nothing yet. Let them plan their escalation. Men planning violence tell you everything, and everything they tell you can be sold later to whoever needs to hear it.

Third—and here she paused, glass untouched, city lights bleeding through the curtain gap—third, the price.

She thought about how to price a man like Casper Underhill. Not in currency; currency was the least interesting denomination. You priced a man in what he couldn't survive losing. For most men: family, freedom, skin. For a man who had already lost his name once and rebuilt it out of dust and instruments—

For that man, you priced him in the thing he was building.

If Brandt published, the commission convened, the aquifer went into statutory protection, and Underhill's long game paid off—but only if he stayed hidden behind his anonymity. An unnamed source could feed a reporter forever. The moment his name entered the record, he became a witness, and witnesses could be discredited, delayed, disappeared into proceedings. And if the name never entered the record—if he stayed a ghost—then the story would always be Brandt's story, and ghosts don't get to come home.

Either way, she took him from himself.

Unless he testified. Unless he walked into the light on purpose, which no rational man did, which no man who had spent twelve years shedding his old life would ever—

Ferro stopped. She set down the pen.

She had watched him across a fuel stop counter, watched him agree to her terms with fear sweating through his competence, and she had filed him as a survivor. Survivors ran. Survivors took the deal, took the money, took the tunnel out. Everything in her experience said he would run.

But the radio log. Eleven minutes. Someone inside her own house had confirmed the decoy early, had locked the failure in place before she could verify—which meant either incompetence or a second player at her own table, and Ferro had survived twenty years by assuming, in the absence of proof, the second.

She opened a secure line and gave instructions in the flat cadence she used for orders she never wanted quoted back.

"Find Underhill. Esquel, or wherever Brandt keeps him. Don't touch him—watch him. I want to know who he meets, what he carries, and whether he looks like a man running." A pause. "And pull the personnel files on my advance team. Everyone who touched the checkpoint transmission. I want to know who confirmed that data before it existed."

She hung up and stood at the window a final time. Somewhere south of here, under a thousand meters of dry stone, a sea of ancient water waited without opinion, without loyalty, caring nothing for any of them. It would still be there when they were all gone. That was the joke of it, the joke she'd built her life on refusing to find funny: they were fighting over the right to spend something that had been saving itself since before there was anyone to spend it.

On her desk, the intercept printout lay face up. Elena Brandt's byline stared back at her like a door.

Ferro picked up the phone again.

"And one more thing," she said quietly. "Get me everything on the journalist. Everything. If Underhill won't come to the light, maybe we introduce the light to him."

Outside, far to the south, over a rain-shadow badlands that had not seen weather worth chasing in weeks, the first clouds of a front neither of them had named were gathering in the dark.
