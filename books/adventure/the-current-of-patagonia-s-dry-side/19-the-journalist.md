## 19. The Journalist

Esquel arrived out of the dust like a town assembled from spare parts—sawtooth roofs, a water tower leaning against the wind, poplars planted in defiant rows by someone who had believed trees could hold. After nine days of badlands it looked like civilization, and civilization looked fragile.

Casper drove the lead truck with one hand and watched the mirror with his eyes. Behind them: two vehicles, four walking wounded, one unconscious man strapped into a litter frame welded from fence posts, and Ilsa Reyes gray-faced in the back seat with a bullet through her shoulder and her jaw set like she intended to argue with the bullet until it apologized.

"You're bleeding through again," he said.

"I'm fine."

"That's the third time you've said that. In medicine we call that a pattern."

"In storm chasing we call it persistence," she said, and then laughed, which turned into a hiss, which turned into silence. The clinic was three blocks past the fuel depot. He took the potholes slow, an apology in every gear change.

The doctor on duty was young and tired and asked no questions about the shrapnel scars on the other survivors or why none of them had identification beyond a government satellite phone wrapped in a shirt. She stitched Ilsa's shoulder, hung a bag of saline, and told Casper in the corridor that his friend would keep the arm if infection stayed away. "Keep her here," she said. "No matter what anyone tells you."

He meant to keep her there. He meant to keep all of them there, behind walls, under lights, where Ferro's people couldn't reach without witnesses. But plans were weather. You drew them clean and they came apart in your hands.

The real problem was riding in a waterproof case inside his jacket, against his ribs, warm as a second heartbeat: eleven terabytes of Kestrel's survey data. The fossil aquifer's true extent. The maps that made a nation's future legible. The thing Marisol Ferro had sent armed men across four hundred kilometers of desert to take from him.

And he knew exactly who it belonged to now. Not him. Not Okafor, not yet. A woman at the *El Sur* offices who had spent six years printing his anonymous tips and never once learning his name.

* * *

Elena Brandt worked out of a room above a print shop that smelled of ink and cold coffee, because the newspaper's actual building had been sold to a bank two years ago and the paper survived the way weeds survive pavement—stubbornly, and in the cracks. Casper climbed the outside stairs at dusk, when the streetlights were still arguing with the last light off the Andes, and knocked the way you knock on a door you've never knocked on but have imagined a thousand times.

"Closed," said a voice through the wood. Then, sharper: "If this is about the mining piece, run it past legal or don't run it."

"It's not about the mining piece."

Silence. A chair creaking. He heard her cross the floor, unhurried, and he understood suddenly what six years of dead drops and burner emails had built: not trust, exactly. A habit. She was deciding whether to break it.

The door opened on a chain. One eye, brown, unimpressed, taking him in—the road dust, the split lip healing crooked, the exhaustion worn like a coat too heavy to take off.

"You're the weather man," she said.

"I'm the weather man."

She unchained the door. "I figured you for older."

"Everyone does. It's the guilt."

Her office was a desk drowning in paper, two monitors, a wall map of Chubut Province bristling with pins in colors only she understood. She was maybe fifty, built like a question mark bent over keyboards for decades, and she moved with the economy of someone who had learned that speed attracts attention. She poured coffee from a carafe that had clearly been through wars and handed him a cup without asking.

"So," she said. "Six years of tips. Water tables falsified in the Río Senguer reports. The gravel concession that ate a wetland. The minister's brother-in-law and the drilling permits." She ticked them off on ink-stained fingers. "Every one verifiable. Every one sourced well enough that I could burn my sources' anonymity and still stand it up. And never a name. Never a voice on the phone. Until tonight you were a style of writing to me." She sat on the edge of her desk. "So either you're dead, or something finally got big enough to make you stupid. Which is it?"

"Both, probably." He set the case on the desk between them. "Do you know what Survey Team Kestrel was doing out west?"

"Everyone knows what they were *supposed* to be doing. Hydrological baseline survey. Eleven days overdue, official line is equipment failure and bad terrain, families are being told to be patient." Her eyes narrowed. "You know where they are."

"They're in a hospital four blocks from here. Six of them. Two won't walk right for months. Their leader is downstairs making statements to nobody, because nobody's listening yet." He opened the case. Inside, the drive sat in foam like a relic. "This is what they found. And before you touch it, you need to understand what it costs, because I'm not handing you a story. I'm handing you a war."

Elena didn't reach for it. That was the first thing he loved about her, in the way you love a colleague you've never met: discipline before appetite.

"Talk," she said.

* * *

He told her all of it, standing, because sitting felt like surrender. The sinkhole breathing fog at dawn. The cavern system under the mesas, cathedral-vast, carved by water older than the mountains above it. Dr. Okafor's voice cracking over the phrase *fossil aquifer* as she explained that beneath the driest ground in Patagonia lay a freshwater sea the size of a small country, sealed off from contamination for tens of thousands of years, replenished so slowly that every liter drawn was a liter spent forever—and every liter mismanaged a theft from people not born yet.

"The dry side has been dying of thirst for a century," Casper said. "Towns abandoned. Ranches sold. And underneath it all, this. Whoever controls the data controls whether that water becomes a public reserve or a private asset. Ferro's syndicate financed Kestrel's permit through shell companies. They have drilling permits signed by a bribed minister. They sent a convoy with guns. When that failed, they sent another one, and we outran it by—" he touched his split lip, "—some cost."

"And the official data? What did the government receive?"

"A decoy. Doctored. Brine-poisoned, commercially worthless. Ferro's advance team verified it and radioed it north. As far as any ministry knows, Kestrel found salt." He met her eyes. "I lied to armed men to get these people home. I'm telling you that up front, because if this goes wrong, that lie becomes the story, and I'd rather it come from me."

Elena was quiet for a long moment. Outside, a truck downshifted on the avenue; the sound passed through the room like weather.

"You understand what you're giving me," she said finally. "Not the drive. The decision. Once I verify this and start calls—to the commission, to the federal judge in Rawson, to the wire services—it stops being yours. It stops being Okafor's. It becomes mine, and I will spend it however serves the truth, even if that means burning your decoy lie wide open on page one. Even if that puts your name next to words like *falsified* and *deceived*. Can you live with how I use it?"

"That's why it's going to you and not to a ministry," Casper said. "Ministers can be bought. I've seen the receipts. You can't."

"I can be sued."

"Not successfully."

That almost got a smile out of her. She reached for the drive at last, held it in both hands like something that might still be alive, and plugged it into an air-gapped laptop she pulled from a safe under the floorboards—an old reporter's paranoia that Casper found deeply reassuring.

Data bloomed across her screens. Elevation models. Isotope dating. Flow simulations rendered in colors that meant nothing to him and everything to anyone who could read them. Elena scrolled in silence, and he watched her face do what faces do when a rumor turns into a fact turns into a responsibility: nothing at all, while everything behind it reorganizes.

"The aquifer underlies four provinces," she said quietly. "At conservative extraction rates, it could end the water emergency in the south for two hundred years. At *commercial* rates—" she tapped a simulation, watched a blue basin drain to red in eleven animated years, "—eleven years. Eleven." She looked up. "Who else has copies?"

"Casper?" The voice came from the stairwell door, thin and furious and unmistakable.

Ilsa Reyes stood in the doorway holding her stitched shoulder with one hand and a canvas satchel in the other, hospital gown under an unbuttoned coat, having walked out of a clinic four blocks away against direct medical instruction. Her face was white as paper and her eyes were on fire.

"You gave her *a copy*," Ilsa said. "You gave her the drive. Without Okafor. Without the team. Without—" she stopped, swayed, caught the doorframe. "That data belongs to the people who bled for it."

Casper was moving before she finished, getting a chair under her, but she shoved his hands away and stood on her own, which cost her visibly.

"Ilsa—"

"Don't." She set the satchel on the desk with a thump. "Okafor sent me. She figured out within an hour of reaching the clinic that you'd already gone to Brandt—because of course you did, you've been feeding her crumbs for years, Ilsa-the-scout-notices-things. So she sent the original field notebooks. Handwritten. Sealed. Chain of custody, signed by every surviving member of Team Kestrel, witnessed by our medic." She unzipped the satchel and put five bound notebooks beside the drive, each stamped with the government crest, each signed on the flyleaf. "She said to tell you: *the data was never his to give, but she forgives him, because the notebooks make it everyone's.*"

Elena had gone very still. Casper recognized the look. It was the look of a woman watching a story acquire the one thing stories need most and reporters get least: documentation no lawyer on earth could challenge.

"Dr. Okafor," Elena said slowly, "is authorizing publication."

"Dr. Okafor is *demanding* it," Ilsa said. "She said the silence was hers to order and hers to end, and she's done letting men decide when the truth comes out. No offense."

"Some taken," Casper said. "Deservedly."

Ilsa lowered herself into the chair at last, gray and shaking, and looked up at him with an expression that wasn't anger anymore—or wasn't only anger. "You should have waited."

"There wasn't time. There's still not." He turned back to Elena. "Ferro knows the decoy doesn't match the seismic record. Her second team chased us half the way here. When she confirms we reached Esquel—and she will, tonight—she has two moves left. Bury the story legally, or bury us literally. We just made the second option cheaper than the first."

Elena was already working, fingers flying, pulling up contacts, drafting encrypted messages. "Then we move faster than she does. I can have verification done by midnight. Commission convenes Thursday. If I hand the chairman authenticated originals before the session opens, with Kestrel's own signatures and a named source willing to testify—" she glanced up at Casper, and there it was, the question six years of anonymity had been building toward, arriving at last with its hand out, "—then I need a name. On the record. From your mouth, in that room, under oath."

The room went quiet. Rain-shadow wind worried the window frame. Ilsa was watching him with something that might have been pity, or hope, or both wearing the same face.

Twelve years ago he had buried a name under miles of desert, under false forecasts and borrowed trucks and a life measured in fuel stops. Twelve years of being the weather man, the ghost, the style of writing. He thought about a town underwater and a ledger taped inside a visor and a woman named Ferro who bought futures in other people's thirst.

"Elena," he said. "My name is Casper Underhill."

She wrote it down by hand, in pen, in a notebook, the old way, and the scratch of the nib sounded like a lock turning.

"Underhill," she repeated. "There was a hydrologist by that name. Comahue district. Twelve years ago. The Villa Ríos flood."

"The same."

"He disappeared after the inquiry."

"He ran." Casper looked at the window, at the dark coming down over Esquel, at the north road where headlights would soon be crawling toward them carrying men with a price list. "He ran for twelve years. He's done."

Elena closed the notebook. "Then you'd better get some sleep, Mr. Underhill. Thursday you testify." She paused, and the lamplight caught something hard and bright in her face. "Because I checked the commission schedule an hour ago, before you climbed those stairs. They've added a surprise witness tomorrow morning. Closed session." She slid a printed agenda across the desk, and Casper read the name at the bottom of the witness list and felt the floor tilt.

**M. Ferro — appearing voluntarily, matter of provincial water concessions.**

"She's not waiting for Thursday," Elena said. "Neither are we."
