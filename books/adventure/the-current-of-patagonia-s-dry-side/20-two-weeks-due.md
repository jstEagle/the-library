## 20. Two Weeks Due

The fuel stop looked smaller than I remembered.

That was the trick of places like this—out here on the dry side, where nothing grew tall enough to cast a shadow worth hiding in, a building didn't shrink so much as it refused to change. The same four pumps under the same corrugated awning, two of them still out of order with the same plastic bags knotted over the nozzles. The same wind-scoured concrete apron, cracked in the same spiderweb pattern. The same dog, or a descendant of the same dog, asleep against the warm wall of the kiosk with its nose tucked under its tail, dreaming whatever dogs dream in a country where rain is a rumor.

I had left this place eleven weeks ago with a ledger taped inside my visor and two weeks to live. I was coming back with no ledger at all, and I wasn't sure yet whether that made me richer or poorer.

The attendant recognized me. Of course she did. There were maybe forty people in a hundred kilometers who bought diesel at this pump, and a stranger's truck was an event the way a comet is an event. She glanced up from her crossword, took in my face, the dust plume behind my rear axle, the way I parked nose-out toward the north road—the old habit of a man who never fully trusted a parking spot—and went back to her puzzle without a word. But I saw her register me. In a place like this, registering things was survival. Everyone out here was a little bit of a weather station.

I filled the tank. Twenty minutes to Esquel if I pushed it. Elena Brandt would be at her desk by nine; I'd called from a ridge outside Gan Gan where the satellite signal came and went like a shy animal, and she'd said only, "Bring it before noon or don't bring it." Elena did not waste syllables. It was one of the hundred reasons I trusted her.

Except I wasn't going to make it to Esquel. I'd known that since the pass.

* * *

They were good, I'll give them that much. Ferro's people always were. No black SUVs idling conspicuously at the edge of the lot, no men leaning against fenders with their arms crossed like extras in a film. Instead: a white pickup with mining-company decals parked at the air compressor, two men checking tire pressure they clearly didn't care about. A sedan with a cracked windshield pulled around the back of the kiosk, engine off, positioned so that anyone leaving south had to pass within six meters of it. And a third man inside the kiosk itself, studying the shelf of motor oil with the focused intensity of someone who had never changed oil in his life.

I counted them the way you count lightning strikes—not because counting stops the storm, but because knowing the interval tells you how long you have.

Three visible. Probably a fourth on the road as a cutoff. Ferro didn't send less than four for anything she cared about, and she cared about me now in the way you care about a splinter working toward your heart.

I could have run. Let's be honest about what that would have meant. Nose out, tank full, three hundred kilometers of gravel ahead of me and a sedan with a cracked windshield between me and it. Maybe I beat them. I was, after all, very good at driving away from things—it was practically the only skill I'd maintained without interruption across twelve years and four names. I could be in Esquel by ten, hand Elena the drive, and vanish into the coastal fog until this all resolved itself one way or another.

And then what? Then Ferro's people found Ilsa in a hospital bed in Esquel with a bullet in her shoulder and the real data in a lockbox under it. Then they found Okafor, who had already decided—God help her, God help all of us—that silence was finished as a strategy. Then they found the wounded men scattered through clinics down the length of Chubut, six ghosts of Survey Team Kestrel, each one a loose thread Ferro could pull until the whole weave came apart.

Running would save the data. It would not save the people who'd carried it.

There was a version of me—the version from twelve years ago, the one whose name I'd buried somewhere east of the mountains—who would have run and called it tactics. That man had been very skilled at confusing escape with strategy. He'd falsified a flood forecast because staying and fighting the bureaucracy felt impossible, and a town had drowned, and he'd driven west until the map ran out of west.

I stood at the pump with the nozzle ticking in the filler neck and understood, with the strange calm of a barometer falling, that I had been driving toward this fuel stop for twelve years. Every road out here forked and forked again, but they all eventually led back to the same handful of places, and this was one of them, and Ferro knew it, because Ferro understood something simple about men like me: we are creatures of habit, and habit is a leash.

I hung up the nozzle. Paid the attendant in cash. Bought a bottle of water I didn't want, because it gave my hands something to do while my heart made its arguments.

Then I walked over to the white pickup with the mining decals.

* * *

The taller of the two men saw me coming and didn't reach for anything, which told me he'd been told not to. Ferro wanted me breathing. That was either very good news or very bad news, and I wouldn't know which until I was sitting across from her, and possibly not even then.

"Casper Underhill," he said. Not a question. My name—the new one, the real one now, the one I'd given to a parliamentary commission's subpoena server three days ago in a hotel lobby in Esquel while Elena Brandt watched from a rented car with the engine running. Saying it out loud still felt like trying on a coat that fit better than I remembered.

"That's me," I said. "You're blocking the air hose."

He almost smiled. "Señor Ferro would like a word."

"Señora."

"Excuse me?"

"Ferro is a woman," I said. "Marisol Ferro. You work for her. You should probably know that." I said it mildly, the way you'd correct a man about the time of day. Behind him, his partner had come around the truck bed, and the man in the kiosk had drifted to the doorway, and the geometry of the parking lot had quietly closed around me like a hand.

The tall one didn't rise to it. "We're asked to bring you in. Gently, if possible."

"And if not gently?"

"Gently is cheaper," he said. "For everyone."

I looked past him, south down the empty road, at the heat shimmer standing on the asphalt like water that wasn't there. A mirage. Out here the land lied to you constantly, generously, about water most of all. You could spend your whole life chasing those shining pools and die of thirst a hundred meters from a well. I had spent twelve years doing approximately that.

I thought about Ilsa in the hospital, her shoulder packed in ice, refusing the good painkillers because she wanted her head clear for the questions she knew were coming. I thought about Okafor's voice on the phone last night, steady as a seismograph needle: *The truth doesn't need us to survive it, Underhill. It just needs us to deliver it.* I thought about the aquifer—that impossible freshwater sea lying under the badlands like a held breath, older than the mountains above it—and about the drilling permits with a bribed minister's signature, and about the word *worthless* I had fed to Ferro's advance men along with a doctored drive, and how long a lie like that could hold before the seismic records contradicted it.

The lie was failing. Ferro would know by now, or nearly. Which meant the window was closing, which meant every hour I spent being clever was an hour her lawyers spent getting cleverer.

"Okay," I said.

The tall one blinked. Whatever script he'd rehearsed, it hadn't included the word *okay* arriving this early. "Okay?"

"I'll come. But I'm driving my own truck, and you're going to follow me, and if anyone puts a bag over my head I will crash this vehicle into the first culvert we pass, and you can explain to Señora Ferro why her conversation has to happen with a coroner instead." I popped the door. "Also, I get the window seat."

"There's no window seat in—" He stopped himself. Recalibrated. These were professionals; they could absorb a joke the way a good suspension absorbs a washboard road. "Fine. Follow us. Don't be clever."

"No chance of that," I said. "I've retired from clever. It didn't pay."

* * *

We drove north for an hour, my truck trailing their sedan at a distance calibrated to look voluntary, the white pickup bringing up the rear. The land unspooled around us in its long brown sentences—scrub steppe, basalt flows gone black and lichen-crusted, the occasional guanaco lifting its head to watch us pass with the indifference of creatures who had survived everything this country could do and expected to survive us too.

I used the hour the way I used everything anymore: as evidence. I noted the wind out of the southwest, steady at thirty knots, strong enough to raise dust devils along the road cuts. I noted the cloud deck building over the cordillera far to the west, high and fibrous, the kind of sky that promised nothing today and possibly something tomorrow. Old habits. Or maybe not habits. Maybe the only honest relationship I'd ever had was with weather, because weather never pretended to be anything other than what it was coming to do.

My phone buzzed once in the cupholder. I risked a glance at a straight section. Elena Brandt: **Where are you?**

I typed with my thumb, steering with my knee, feeling like a teenager and not caring: **Going to see Ferro. She found me first.**

The reply came fast enough that she must have been holding the phone. **Are you insane?**

**Probably. Keep the drive safe. If I'm not at the commission Thursday, publish anyway. Everything. My name too.**

A long pause, long enough that we covered eight kilometers of nothing. Then: **What name?**

I smiled at the windshield. Twelve years, and she'd never once printed the question she must have carried the whole time—a journalist sitting on the biggest story of the decade, and the missing piece she wanted most wasn't coordinates or documents. It was the man.

**Underhill,** I typed. **Casper Underhill. It's mine. I earned it.**

She sent back nothing for a while, and then just: **Thursday.**

* * *

Ferro's compound sat in a fold of hills that didn't appear on any map I owned, which was exactly how she liked it. What had been, years ago, a sheep estancia's shearing shed and manager's house was now something else: solar arrays, a generator building, a communications mast bristling with dishes, and two helicopters tied down on a gravel pad with their blades lashed. Money, out here, didn't look like money. It looked like redundancy.

They took my keys at the gate, which I let them do, and walked me across the yard, which I also let them do, and I noticed—I made myself notice—that nobody touched me. That was Ferro's signature. Violence was a cost center. Everything she did was designed to make force unnecessary, and when force became necessary it meant someone downstream had failed her, and she was currently in a position where too many someones had failed her at once. The decoy drive. The seismic mismatch. The minister under investigation. Her whole elegant machine grinding against its own tolerances.

She was waiting on the veranda of the old manager's house, at a table set for tea, because Marisol Ferro believed—correctly, mostly—that civilization was a weapon. She was a compact woman in her fifties with gray shot through her dark hair and reading glasses pushed up on her head, and she looked less like a syndicate boss than like a university dean about to discuss a budget shortfall. Two cups poured. Steam rising straight up in the dead air of the lee side.

"Mr. Underhill." She gestured at the chair opposite. "Sit. You look terrible."

"I've been carrying wounded men through a sandstorm," I said. "It's not a spa treatment."

"So I hear." She lifted her cup, blew across it, didn't drink. "Do you know what impresses me about you? It isn't the decoy. Anyone can forge data. It's that you forged it *well*. Brine contamination at plausible concentrations, with plausible sampling gaps, and a salt profile consistent with the regional geology. My own hydrologist believed it for six hours. Six hours, Mr. Underhill. Do you understand what a compliment that is?"

"It wasn't meant as one."

"Everything is meant as something." She set the cup down and looked at me, and for the first time I saw the thing underneath the composure—not rage, nothing so cheap. Arithmetic. She was calculating me the way her analysts calculated reserves: flow rates, extraction costs, break-even points. "Here is where we are. My colleagues are frightened. Frightened men do loud, stupid things, and I have spent thirty years keeping them quiet and smart. Your reporter has documents. Your Dr. Okafor has a conscience she's discovered late in life. And on Thursday there is a commission room in Buenos Aires with microphones in it." She folded her hands. "I can't stop all of that anymore. I know it, you know it, and the interesting question—the only interesting question—is what happens to the people holding the matches when the fire starts."

"You're offering me a deal."

"I'm offering you a *shape*," she said. "There are versions of Thursday where you testify and walk out a hero and I spend a decade in courtrooms and lose some holdings and keep others. There are versions where you testify and certain associates of mine decide that a man who lied to them once will lie again, and act on that conclusion in a country with long roads and short memories. And there are versions—" she turned her cup slowly on its saucer, "—where you don't testify at all. Where the survey data stays ambiguous, the aquifer stays unproven, and everyone keeps their options open. Including you. I'd settle your debts this afternoon. All of them. Every name on that little ledger you kept in your visor. You'd owe nothing to anyone, ever again. Think about what that means for a man like you. Freedom, Mr. Underhill. Actual freedom. You've forgotten what it weighs."

I looked at the tea. At the steam rising straight up, because there was no wind on this side of the house, because she had built her life in lee sides, in the calm pockets behind larger forces.

"The town I falsified the forecast for," I said quietly. "Villa Roca. Forty-one people died. I got a new name and drove until the roads pointed at the ocean and turned south. And for twelve years the debt I couldn't pay wasn't money. It was that I knew what I was, and I arranged my whole life so I'd never have to say it out loud." I met her eyes. "You're offering to buy the one thing I finally figured out how to give away for free."

Something moved behind her face—regret, or its efficient corporate cousin. "So it's Thursday, then."

"It's Thursday."

She nodded slowly, and picked up her cup, and this time she drank. "Then finish your tea," she said, "because I'm driving you to the airport myself, and my pilot gets nervous when I'm late."

I stared at her. "Why?"

Marisol Ferro set down the cup and looked out past the solar arrays, at the brown hills, at the dry country that had swallowed Kestrel and given it back changed.

"Because," she said, "thirty years ago I sat in a room like that commission room, and a man testified, and I swore I'd never be on either side of a microphone again. And I'd like to watch you do it right, since I apparently never learned how." She stood, buttoned her jacket, and became once more entirely the woman everyone feared. "Come along, Mr. Underhill. We have a hearing to get you to—and you should know that my former colleagues have just realized exactly where you're going."
