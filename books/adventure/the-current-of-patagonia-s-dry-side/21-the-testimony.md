## 21. The Testimony

They gave him a room the night before, in the annex of the National Congress building, with a bed he didn't sleep in and a window that looked out on the Avenida del Libertador, where the jacarandas were dropping their purple flowers onto the hoods of parked cars like a slow violet snowfall that nobody had asked for and nobody would clean up.

Casper sat on the edge of the bed with his boots off—someone had told him to look rested—and read his own name.

*Casper Elias Underhill.* It was printed on the summons, on the witness schedule, on the laminated card they'd left on the desk with a lanyard through it. Twelve years since anyone had put those three words together in official ink. He'd been other names since. He'd been a line in a fuel ledger in Río Gallegos, a signature on a truck registration in Esquel, a voice on Elena Brandt's answering machine that she never once tried to trace because she understood what anonymity was worth to a man who was paying it off one small truth at a time.

He said the name out loud once, quietly, testing it the way you test ice before you commit your weight.

It held.

The real data was already in the building. He knew this because Elena Brandt had told him at the hospital, standing over Ilsa's bed with her recorder running openly now, no more games: *Sealed testimony, notarized copies, three sets. One went to the commission clerk tonight. One goes to the press if anything happens to me. One is yours, if you ever want to hold your own life in your hands.* Ilsa had been awake for that part, propped up against pillows with her shoulder strapped and her color coming back, and she'd looked at Casper across the hospital room and given him a single nod, the same nod she'd given him at arrow-point on the mesa when she decided he was a man instead of a problem.

Tomorrow he would say the coordinates out loud. Into a microphone. Into the record.

He lay back on the made bed with his boots off and did not sleep, and the jacarandas kept falling.

* * *

The hearing room was smaller than television had taught him to expect. Wood-paneled, horseshoe-shaped bench, seven commissioners behind microphones, a stenographer's fingers moving like a pianist's, maybe forty people in the gallery including two of Kestrel's survivors—Okafor in a blazer that fit her badly, as though she'd borrowed it from a man, which she probably had—and Elena Brandt in the third row with a notebook she barely touched. She'd already written her story. Everyone in that room was living inside it.

Ferro was not there. Ferro had been arrested eleven days earlier at a estancia outside Puerto Madryn, mid-handshake, mid-envelope, mid-sentence—the minister's aide turning state's evidence had worn a wire, and the transcript of what Marisol Ferro said when the police came out of the treeline had already leaked, all four seconds of it, because some words are too good to keep. She was in pretrial detention in Buenos Aires. Her syndicate was eating itself alive in public, subsidiary turning on subsidiary, the optimists finally pessimistic about each other. Casper had read every word of it and felt nothing he could name. Not victory. Something more like the stillness after a storm passes and you walk outside and check the sky and find it ordinary.

"State your name for the record," said the commission chair, a gray woman named Deputy Ávila whose face had been carved by thirty years of hearings into something patient and unhurried.

Casper leaned toward the microphone. The room was very quiet. He could hear the ventilation, the stenographer's keys, his own pulse in his ears like water moving underground.

"Casper Elias Underhill."

Saying it was easier than reading it. Saying it was almost easy. The sound came out of him and did not come back changed, and twelve years of alias fell away from him so smoothly he wondered why he'd carried them so long, the way you wonder about a limp only after it stops hurting.

"You are aware," Ávila said, "that under the terms of the immunity agreement executed on the fourteenth, statements made today regarding the events surrounding Survey Team Kestrel cannot be used against you in criminal proceedings, but may be entered into public record without redaction."

"I'm aware."

"And you've elected to waive anonymity regardless."

"Yes." He looked down the horseshoe at her. "I'd like to start earlier than the fourteenth, though. If the commission will allow it. There's something you should hear first, or none of the rest of it makes sense."

Ávila glanced left and right along the bench. Nobody objected. "Proceed, Mr. Underhill."

So he started with the flood.

* * *

He told it plainly, because he had learned from Elena Brandt that plainness was the only register truth could survive in. Twelve years ago he had been a hydrologist and storm modeler working the floodplains north of Santa Fe, good at his work, ambitious, twenty-nine years old and certain that talent was a kind of armor. A developer's lobby had leaned on his agency. His supervisor had leaned on him. The model said the levee would fail at a river stage the town had never seen; the pressure said publish the comfortable number; and he had split the difference, which is the most expensive thing a scientist can do, because the river does not split differences.

Forty-one days later the town of Arroyo Claro drowned. Nine people died. The inquiry found modeling error, insufficient data, systemic underfunding—a verdict of weather and bureaucracy—and Casper Underhill packed a truck, shed his name, and drove south until the land stopped promising water.

"I falsified a forecast," he said into the microphone, and heard his own voice do something it hadn't done in twelve years, which was shake. "Not by much. By exactly enough. And then I spent twelve years in the driest place in Argentina telling myself that hiding was penance. It wasn't. Hiding was just the second lie. Today I'm trying to stop lying."

Nobody in the gallery moved. Okafor had closed her eyes. Elena Brandt was writing now, after all.

Then he gave them Kestrel. All of it, in order: the liaison's offer, the camp with its seismograph still whispering to itself, the boot print that stopped, the sinkhole breathing fog at dawn, Ilsa's arrow drawn to full anchor six meters away and her voice steady as a plumb line asking him who sent him. He described the caverns the way Ilsa had described them to him around the fire, and then the way he'd seen them himself on the descent—because the commission needed to understand scale, and scale was the whole story. A fossil aquifer beneath the mesas, recharged when the Andes were young, holding freshwater older than any nation that might claim it. Enough water to green a province. Enough water to buy a government. Enough water, in the wrong hands, to hold an entire country's agriculture hostage one growing season at a time.

He named the shell companies. He named the permit numbers. He named the bribed minister—not the arrested one, the second one, the one who thought he was safe—and watched a man in the gallery stand up very fast and leave, and made a note to tell Elena Brandt to find out who that was.

And then Ávila said, "Mr. Underhill, we have the survey data under seal. What the commission requires from you specifically are the coordinates of the primary aquifer body, for entry into the record supporting the protective statute."

That was the moment. He had known it was coming for weeks, rehearsed it on the long drive north, rehearsed it in the hospital corridor, and it still arrived like weather.

Because coordinates were the thing Ferro had wanted. Coordinates were the thing worth killing for, worth a convoy and drilling permits and a price on a debt-ridden man's head. Once he spoke them, they were in the world forever, in Hansard and archives and search engines, available to every future optimist with capital and patience. The statute would protect the aquifer—but statutes were younger than greed, and greed was older than the water.

He looked at the gallery. Okafor opened her eyes and met his gaze, and nodded once, slowly, the way a person releases another person from something.

"The commission should understand," Casper said, "that publishing these coordinates creates risk. Permanent risk. I want that in the record too. I'm not giving you a secret. I'm giving you a responsibility, and I'm doing it because a secret kept by six exhausted people in a hole in the ground was never going to hold anyway. Sunlight is the only aquifer that refills itself."

He gave the latitude. He gave the longitude. He gave the depth interval and the strike of the fault system, and the stenographer's keys ran like rain on a roof, and it was done. The numbers existed outside his head for the first time, and he felt lighter and more exposed than he had since Arroyo Claro, since before that, maybe since childhood, the specific lightness of a man who has set down something heavy and can't pick it back up.

Deputy Ávila consulted her papers. "One final matter. During the events under review, you presented falsified survey data to agents of the Ferro syndicate, representing the aquifer as brine-contaminated. Do you wish to address that?"

"Yes." He turned the microphone slightly toward himself, an old habit from conference rooms, from the life where people had believed his numbers. "I lied to armed men to save six lives. I'd do it again. But I want the record to show I know the difference between that lie and the one I told at Arroyo Claro. One lie protected the truth. The other buried it. I spent twelve years pretending those were the same act because they both came from the same cowardice, and they don't. A man who lies to protect power is a criminal. A man who lies to protect people is still a liar—he just gets to choose what kind. I've been both. I'm finished being the first."

The room stayed quiet a long moment. Then Ávila said, "Thank you, Mr. Underhill. The commission has no further questions."

He sat there while the gavel came down, while the horseshoe emptied, while Okafor came down the aisle and shook his hand with a grip like a drill core sample—brief, firm, taking a measurement—and said, "You know the statute won't pass without your continued cooperation. Hearings in September. They'll want you back."

"They know where to find me," Casper said. "I'm in the book now. Under my own name."

* * *

Outside, the afternoon had gone gold, and the jacaranda petals were ankle-deep along the balustrade, and Elena Brandt was waiting on the steps with her notebook finally closed and her hands in her pockets, looking younger than her bylines.

"Twelve years," she said. "You could have given me that flood story anytime. You let me chase half-truths for years."

"You'd have printed it," Casper said. "And I'd have run. That was the arrangement. Small truths, no names."

"And now?"

He looked out across the plaza, at the traffic and the pigeons and the ordinary enormous city going about its ordinary enormous day, none of it knowing that beneath a mesa fifteen hundred kilometers south lay enough water to rewrite the map, and that its location was now public property, defended by nothing but law and a woman named Ávila and a journalist with three sealed copies.

"Now I need a ride to the bus station," he said. "There's a truck in Esquel with my instruments in it, and a reserve down the road that's going to need a warden who knows how to read wind. And—" he paused, because the last part still surprised him when he said it, "—there's a scout in the hospital who's promised to teach me how she shoots, once the shoulder heals. Says a warden who can't hit anything is just a tourist."

Elena smiled and unfolded herself from the steps. "For the record, Mr. Underhill—what happens now?"

"For the record?" He tested the phrase, found he liked carrying it. "Now it rains, eventually. It always does. The trick is being there when it does."

They walked down together into the falling purple light, and somewhere south, under stone older than nations, the water waited, patient as testimony, rising.
