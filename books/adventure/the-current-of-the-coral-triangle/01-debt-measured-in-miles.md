## 1. Debt Measured in Miles

The monsoon came up out of the Sulu Sea like something with a grudge.

Astrid Reyes felt it before she saw it — a pressure change in her back teeth, a taste of iron on the wind, the sea going that flat, bruised green she had learned to read the way other people read weather reports. She stood at the wheel of the *Coriolis* with her boots braced against the deck plates and watched the sky stack itself into towers over Mindanao's coast, black at the base, bruise-purple above, and she thought, as she always did: *there you are.*

"Barometer's falling like a drunk down a stairwell," called Pak from the engine hatch, his voice half-swallowed by the wind. "You sure about this, boss?"

She wasn't sure about anything. That was the point. Certainty was for people who didn't owe the Vela syndicate two hundred and forty thousand credits.

"We're sure," she said, and turned the bow into it.

Storm chasing was honest work, in its way — the only kind left to her. The Vela syndicate ran cargo insurance across the archipelago the way a spider runs silk, and they had discovered fifteen years ago that a fast boat with a fool at the helm could ride ahead of a typhoon and pull their barges out of harm's way for a fraction of what a lost hull cost them. They paid per storm survived. They docked pay for every hour of hesitation. It was work that ate boats and crews, and Astrid had been doing it long enough to know that Vela didn't hire storm chasers so much as they rented people who couldn't afford to die.

The first rain hit like gravel. Then the world went white.

She flew the *Coriolis* into the heart of it by feel — the wheel shuddering under her palms, spray coming over the bow in sheets heavy enough to bruise, lightning walking down the water in stuttering columns. Somewhere behind her, Pak sang to the engines, an old Bisaya hymn with the words worn off it; somewhere forward, Suri lashed down the last of the deck cargo with hands that had been doing this since before Astrid was born. The boat groaned and answered. The storm screamed and got no answer it liked.

This was the part she loved, and she hated herself for loving it. Out here there were no ledgers, no enforcers waiting at the dock with their tablets full of numbers, no interest compounding hourly like mold in a wet hold. Out here the only arithmetic was simple: the wave, the wind, the next ten seconds. A person could be clean inside a storm. A person could be *weightless.*

Fifteen years ago, another river had taught her the opposite lesson.

She didn't think about it. She never thought about it, not on purpose — but the monsoon dragged it up anyway, the way storms always did, because the interior rivers of the Coral Triangle ran on the same rain that filled this sky, and somewhere north and east of here, past the drowned lowlands and the gorges no chart bothered to map, a river was running through country that had killed Elena Vasquez and swallowed every promise Astrid had ever made about never going back.

Elena, who had taught her to read water the way she was reading this sky now. Elena, who had said *the source is worth seeing once, mija, and dying for never.* Elena, who had died anyway, upriver, in the white noise below the falls, while Astrid held the rope and Marcus Thane held the other end and neither of them held enough.

The rope had come back up empty. Astrid had come back up hollow. Marcus had come back up minus his left hand and plus a silence between them that had lasted fifteen years and counting.

The storm peaked at 0300 and broke apart at dawn, and the *Coriolis* limped into Zamboanga's outer anchorage trailing weed and glory, having pulled two Vela barges clear of the worst of it. Two barges meant a payout. A payout meant maybe three weeks' breathing room before the interest ate it.

Astrid stood at the rail watching the sun come up dirty behind the harbor smog and let herself have exactly one minute of feeling weightless. Then the pilot boat put out from the quay, and she saw who was standing in it, and the minute was over.

* * *

They were waiting for her at Berth Nine, which told her everything. Dockside enforcers didn't wait at berths for people who were current on their accounts.

There were four of them, in the gray coveralls that weren't uniforms except everyone knew they were, and one of them — the tall one with the burn-scarred jaw, Rask, she thought his name was — held a tablet the way a priest holds a missal. Behind them, the morning market roared on oblivious: fish ice smoking in the heat, a boy hauling a cooler bigger than himself, someone frying something with garlic and chilies until the whole quay smelled like hunger.

"Astrid Reyes." Rask didn't smile. Enforcers who smiled wanted something worse than money. "Good run last night. Vela Salvage Mutual appreciates your service."

"Just tell me the number."

He told her the number. It was worse than she'd calculated, because of course it was — the storm surcharge on the insurance rider, the deferred maintenance penalty on the *Coriolis*'s lien, the compounding that ran daily and had been running for six years, since the season the bottom fell out of the salvage market and she'd signed Vela's paper because the alternative was watching Pak's daughter go without her heart surgery. Six years of chasing weather for the spider, and the web had only grown.

"The syndicate," Rask went on, "is prepared to discuss restructuring."

"There's nothing to restructure. I don't have collateral."

"You have a boat. You have a crew. And—" he paused, delicately, a man laying down a card he'd been saving, "—you have a skill set the syndicate finds intermittently useful."

Behind him, at the edge of the berth where the shadows of the fish sheds pooled, someone else was standing. Astrid hadn't seen him arrive, which was impossible, because she would have known that silhouette anywhere — the breadth of him gone narrower with age, the left sleeve of his jacket ending in a matte-black prosthetic hand he'd stopped bothering to disguise years ago.

Marcus Thane stepped into the light, and fifteen years collapsed like a bad scaffold.

He looked older. Of course he did. They both did. But the eyes were the same — pale river-gray, patient, the eyes of a man who read currents for a living and had learned to read people just as well. He wore his charm like she wore her cynicism: as armor, as tool, as apology for everything underneath.

"Hello, Astrid."

"No." She said it before he could say anything else, before the dockside enforcers could exchange their small knowing looks. "Whatever this is, no. Rask, if this is some Vela game—"

"It isn't a game." Marcus held up his right hand, palm out, the old conciliatory gesture. "It's a contract. Legitimate salvage. A research vessel, the *Meridian Star*, lost upriver eleven days ago. Insurance underwriters want recovery of hull and instruments before the monsoon season floods the site. Standard fee. Enough to clear your account twice over."

"Upriver." The word came out of her flat and cold. "Which river?"

He hesitated. It was a small hesitation, a professional's hesitation, but she had spent fifteen years learning to hear the things Marcus Thane tried not to say, and she heard it now.

"That's not relevant to—"

"*Which river.*"

Rask cleared his throat and looked at his tablet, suddenly fascinated by the numbers. Even the market noise seemed to lean away.

"Sungai Larat," Marcus said quietly. "The upper gorge."

The quay tilted. Or she did. Fifteen years of running, and the river had simply waited, patient as water, until she walked back to its mouth on her own two legs.

The Sungai Larat. Where the charts went blank and the gorges narrowed to throats and the ground itself breathed steam through cracks in the rock. Where Elena had stood on the bank with her hair full of mist and said *listen to it, Astrid, the earth has a heartbeat here* — and then the river had taken her, and taken the expedition, and taken whatever soft thing Astrid had still been carrying at twenty-six years old.

"We swore," Astrid said. Her voice sounded far away, like it belonged to someone standing further down the dock. "We swore on her grave we'd never—"

"I know what we swore." For a moment something moved behind the gray eyes — grief, or guilt, or the memory of a rope gone slack in both their hands. "But the *Meridian Star* is real, the fee is real, and your debt is real. I've done the arithmetic you haven't. This job clears you, Astrid. All of it. Every credit."

"And what does it get you?"

That hesitation again. Longer this time. Down at the end of the berth, the fish-market boy dropped his cooler and the crash of it scattered gulls into the smudged sunrise.

"A clean slate too," Marcus said. "That's all any of us are owed."

It was a lie. She knew it was a lie the way she knew a barometer falling — by the pressure in her teeth, by the iron taste of it. But Rask was holding out the tablet with the contract glowing on it, and behind her Pak and Suri had come down the gangway and were reading the number over her shoulder, and she could feel Suri's breath catch, and Pak's hand close briefly on her arm — not steering, just steadying, the way he'd steadied her through six years of storms.

Two hundred and forty thousand credits. Twice over. Freedom, priced at one trip up a river she had promised a dead woman she'd never see again.

Desperation, she thought, always dressed itself as pragmatism. It wore the same clothes. It spoke in the same reasonable voice. *This is just arithmetic,* it said. *Nothing personal.*

Astrid took the tablet. The signature field glowed at the bottom, patient as water.

She signed.

Marcus smiled — the old charm, the old armor — and turned toward the quay, and over his shoulder he said, almost lightly, almost kindly:

"Provision your boat, Captain. We sail with the tide tomorrow. And Astrid — bring everyone you trust. We're going to need all of it."

Then he was gone into the market crowd, and the river, far to the north and east, went on running toward the place where the earth kept its heartbeat, patient as water, certain as debt.
