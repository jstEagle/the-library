## 2. The Rival's Offer

Marcus Thane had learned long ago that the secret to a good lie was to build it out of true things.

The bar at the Sable Anchor was true: rain-streaked windows, a ceiling fan turning with the lazy reluctance of something that had given up on its job years ago, and a smell of diesel and salt that no amount of bleach would ever kill. The chart he unrolled across the scarred table was true — every sounding, every contour, lifted from a survey he'd paid three men to die producing. Even the money was true. That was the beauty of it. He counted it out in front of her, banded stacks of pesos and dollars, and watched Astrid Reyes's eyes do the arithmetic she couldn't help doing.

Fifteen years since he'd sat across a table from her. She hadn't softened. If anything the river had carved her down to something harder and leaner, like driftwood stripped of everything that rots. Her hair was shorter now, shot through with grey she didn't bother hiding, and there was a new scar along her jaw that he itched to ask about and knew better than to.

"You look well," he said, which was the first lie.

"Bullshit," she said, which was why he'd come.

He smiled and let the smile do its work. Charm was a tool like any other — a winch, a pry bar — and Marcus had learned to use his left hand for gestures now, keeping the right one still beneath the table where the prosthetic wouldn't catch the light. The surgeons had done remarkable work, all things considered. A lattice of carbon fiber and silicone, fingers that answered to the ghost-signals of nerves that no longer existed. It could hold a coffee cup. It could not feel rain. Some nights he dreamed the hand was still there, whole and tanned and gripping a rail on the *Cormorant*, and he woke with phantom fingers curled around nothing.

The river had taken the hand. He intended to take the river back.

"Salvage contract," he said, tapping the chart. "Research vessel, the *Meridian Star*, lost upriver eleven months ago. Hydrographic survey outfit out of Davao. Insurance company wants the hull located, sensitive instruments recovered, salvage rights transferred. Standard terms." He slid the first stack across the table. "Retainer. The rest on delivery."

Astrid didn't touch the money. She looked at the chart instead, and Marcus watched her face with the patience of a man who had spent fifteen years learning to wait.

Her eyes went to the coordinates. He saw the exact moment they landed — a small contraction, like a hook setting.

*There you are.*

"The Panganiban Gorge," she said quietly. "Third bend past the confluence."

"That's what the paperwork says."

"That's not what I asked."

Marcus kept his voice level, pleasant, bored even. "It's a river, Astrid. Rivers have bends."

She leaned back. Outside, the monsoon was winding down into a drizzle that silvered the window glass, and somewhere down the dock a winch groaned through its rusted gears. She looked at him the way she used to look at weather building on the horizon — measuring, weighing, deciding whether to run before it or stand and take it.

"We swore," she said.

"We were young," he said. "Young people swear stupid oaths."

"We buried Tomas in that river. What we could find of him." Her voice didn't rise. That was the thing about Astrid — her anger never rose, it *descended*, going colder and quieter the deeper it went. "You lost a hand. I lost—" She stopped. Whatever she'd been about to say, she swallowed it, and Marcus felt the old frustration flicker in his chest. Fifteen years, and she still kept the ledger of that expedition locked where he couldn't read it.

"I lost more than a hand," he said softly. "But we're not talking about us. We're talking about your crew. Your boat. Your debt."

That landed. He saw it land, saw her jaw tighten, because everyone in this part of the world knew about Astrid Reyes's debt, and the Vela syndicate made sure debts were public knowledge the way other institutions made sure marriages were. Fear was cheaper than collateral.

"Vela doesn't care about your pride," Marcus went on. "They care about principal and interest, and both are growing faster than storm work pays. You can keep chasing typhoons until the sea takes you or they do. Or you can take one honest salvage job and buy your people clear." He turned the chart toward her fully now, an offering, a dare. "I'm not asking you to forgive me. I'm asking you to get paid."

For a long moment she said nothing. Then she pulled the chart toward herself and studied it, and Marcus let his gaze drop to the table and allowed himself, briefly, the luxury of certainty.

She would take it. She had to take it. And whatever she believed about the *Meridian Star*, about insurance companies and standard terms, by the time she learned the truth they would already be past the point of turning around — literally, geographically, irrevocably. The gorge did not permit retreat. That was one of the things he loved about it.

* * *

The crew vote happened two nights later aboard the *Tamarind*, Astrid's boat — a converted patrol craft with a crane arm and too much history in her hull. Marcus came as a guest, which meant he came armed with charm, charts, and a second installment of retainer, and he stood at the galley table while seven faces considered him.

He'd done his homework on each of them. The engineer, Deshawn, soft-spoken, with a daughter in a Manila hospital whose bills were a number Marcus had taken the trouble to learn. The navigator, a wiry kid named Paolo who'd never seen real whitewater but talked like he had. The cook, the diver, the rigger. Desperation, desperation, loyalty, desperation. Good crews were built from exactly these materials; Marcus had commanded enough expeditions to know that idealism sank ships faster than reefs ever did.

And then there was Suri.

The old deckhand sat at the end of the table with her arms folded, and she was the only one who didn't look at the money. She looked at Marcus. She had looked at him the same way fifteen years ago, on the *Cormorant*, when he'd stood at the bow pointing at the gorge and promising glory, and her eyes then had held the same flat, patient judgment they held now — the look of a woman watching a man walk toward a cliff edge and deciding whether shouting would help.

"Suri," Marcus said warmly. "You're still with her. I'm glad. Loyalty like yours is worth more than sonar."

"Loyalty's why I'm going to say what I'm going to say," Suri replied. Her voice was gravel over deep water. "This is the Panganiban. This is where Tomas died. And this man—" she jerked her chin at Marcus without ceremony— "stood on that deck and told us the gorge was passable in flood season, and we believed him because he was charming and because we wanted the money. Nothing about him has changed except the hand."

The galley went very quiet. Marcus felt the smile on his face hold, perfectly, the way a dam holds — and understood, as he always did, that the pressure behind it was the only interesting part.

"Suri," he began.

"No. Let me finish, boy. I'm old, I've earned it." She unfolded her arms and laid her hands flat on the table, knuckles like river stones. "I've seen what's upstream. Not the postcard version. The real one. The river up there doesn't want anything from us. It just is. It's us that brings the wanting." Her eyes moved around the table, touching each of the younger crew members. "You vote yes tonight, some of you won't come back. That's not superstition. That's arithmetic. I've done this sum before."

Deshawn cleared his throat. "How much is the balance, Captain? After this job?"

Astrid told him. The number hung in the humid air of the galley like smoke, and Marcus watched it do its work — watched the engineer think of hospital corridors, the diver think of her mother's house, the kid Paolo think of proving himself against water that ate boats.

The vote was five to one. Suri raised her hand against it anyway, alone, and then folded her arms again and stared out the porthole at the black harbor water as if she could see all the way upriver, all the way to the source, all the way to whatever waited there wearing the future's face.

Astrid walked Marcus to the gangplank herself. The rain had stopped; the air smelled of bilge and frangipani, and the harbor lights lay doubled on the slick water.

"One condition," she said. "We go for the wreck. Just the wreck. We find it, we pull the instruments, we come home. Nobody goes past the third bend. Nobody chases rumors."

"Agreed," Marcus said, and shook her hand with his left.

She held the grip a beat longer than necessary. "You've changed, Marcus. I can't tell how much."

"Neither can I," he said, and it was the most honest sentence he'd spoken all week.

He walked away along the wet dock, and behind him he heard the *Tamarind*'s generator kick over, heard the small sounds of a crew settling into the sleep before a voyage — sounds he remembered from another life, another boat, another promise. His right hand ached inside its silicone skin, the way it always ached when he thought about the gorge. Phantom pain, the doctors called it. Nerves remembering a limb that wasn't there anymore.

Marcus thought it was something else. He thought it was the hand remembering the river — remembering the cold turquoise water closing over his wrist, the white roar, the moment the current had decided, with perfect indifference, what it would take and what it would leave.

Soon, he promised it silently. Soon we'll go back, and this time the wanting will be bigger than the river.

In his cabin that night, he opened a steel case and looked at what lay inside: survey maps older than the *Meridian Star*'s supposed sinking, marked in his own handwriting, dated six years back. The vent field glowed on them in colored ink like a wound in the earth's crust.

He closed the case. In two days they would provision, and in four they would sail, and the river would open its throat for all of them.

He only hoped Astrid would forgive him afterward. Or, failing that, that she would live long enough to hate him properly — which, if he knew her at all, amounted to nearly the same thing.
