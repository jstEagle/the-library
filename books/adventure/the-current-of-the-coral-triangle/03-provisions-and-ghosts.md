## 3. Provisions and Ghosts

Port Salangan did not appear on any chart worth trusting, which was precisely why it thrived. It clung to the coast like a barnacle on a hull nobody wanted to scrape — a crescent of rusted tin roofs, fish-gut stink, and diesel haze where the jungle came down to the sea and pretended, badly, to be civilized. The tide brought in smugglers' dhows at dawn and took the law back out with them.

Astrid Reyes walked its single main street with a list in her head and a knife at her hip, and she counted exits out of habit. The noodle stall. The fuel depot with its leaning palm and chained drums. The alley behind the pawnshop where a man had once tried to take her satchel and learned, briefly and painfully, why that was a mistake.

The *Kestrel* rode at anchor in the roadstead, patched and patient, and every credit Astrid spent today would go into her belly like blood into a starving body.

"Diesel first," she murmured, ticking through the arithmetic she'd already done four times since dawn. "Then water. Then food. Then whatever's left."

There would not be much left. There never was.

The fuel depot was run by a woman named Osang who weighed bribes the way other people weighed produce, and Astrid paid her in Vela scrip because Vela scrip was what she had. Osang turned the notes over twice, sniffed at the watermark, and jerked her chin toward the drums stacked under the tarpaulin.

"You're going upriver," Osang said. Not a question. Everyone in Port Salangan knew the shape of a river expedition by what it bought: extra fuel, spare props, the long-shaft outboards, the anti-fouling paint. "Nobody goes upriver for salvage."

"Everybody goes upriver for money."

"Mm." Osang rolled a drum down the ramp with one hip, an economy of motion earned over decades. "Last crew that bought this much diesel from me went up the Sulu three years ago. Boat came back." She tapped ash off a hand-rolled cigarette. "Crew didn't."

Astrid signed the chit and didn't answer. There was nothing to say that wasn't true, and the truth was a currency she couldn't afford to spend this morning.

* * *

She found Ilya Voss in the back room of a bar called the Drowned Man, which was either a joke or a prophecy depending on how you looked at it. He sat alone at a table scarred by cigarette burns, drinking something clear and local and terrible, and he was reading — actually reading — a battered hydrographic survey of the upper gorges, annotated in three colors of ink in handwriting so small it looked like insect tracks.

He'd been pointed out to her twice before she ever saw him: *the Russian who used to work for the coastal authority, the one they stripped his license from.* Disgrace wore different clothes on different men. On Ilya Voss it wore a threadbare linen shirt and an expression of permanent, exhausted curiosity, as if the world were a problem set he'd been assigned and could not put down.

"I need someone who knows the gorges above the second cataract," Astrid said, standing over him without sitting. She'd learned long ago that sitting was a negotiation and standing was a statement.

Ilya didn't look up. He finished his paragraph, ran a finger down the survey map as if checking a pulse, and then raised eyes the color of weak tea. "Everyone needs someone who knows the gorges. That is why everyone who goes there dies. The knowledge does not help. It only tells you exactly when."

"When what?"

"When the river decides to kill you." He closed the book with a soft, final sound. "You are the storm chaser. Reyes. The one who reads weather like scripture."

"And you're the hydrologist who falsified flood data."

"I corrected it," Ilya said, with the mild precision of a man who had made this correction many times and been punished for it every time. "The government's numbers were wrong. The villages downstream were going to drown. I changed the figures so the warnings would be issued. They issued prison instead." He shrugged, a small, tired motion. "So now I drink in a bar named after me."

Astrid pulled out the chair and sat after all. Some negotiations required it.

"The job is salvage. A research vessel lost upriver. We need passage through the gorge system — whirlpools, logjams, the narrows. Marcus Thane says—"

"Thane." Something flickered across Ilya's face, too quick to name. "The man with the beautiful boat and the beautiful contract and the metal hand."

"You've met him."

"He bought my maps last year. All of them. Paid too much, asked too little." Ilya turned his glass slowly on the table, watching the ring of moisture it left. "A man who pays too much for maps is not buying maps, Señora Reyes. He is buying certainty about something he already knows."

The words settled into Astrid's chest like cold water into a boot. She thought of Marcus's practiced charm, the way his prosthetic hand had stayed hidden inside his jacket through the entire pitch, the coordinates he'd recited from memory — *from memory*, fifteen years later, as if he'd been carrying them in his mouth all that time.

"He offered me more than the job's worth too," she said quietly.

"Yes. And you took it. Because your crew eats debt for breakfast." Ilya said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "We are all owned by something. Yours has better tailoring than mine." He drained the glass and stood, joints cracking like rigging in a squall. "I will come. Not for the money. For the river." He paused at the door, silhouetted against the bar's amber gloom. "And because when the moment comes that you must choose between the contract and the truth, I would very much like to watch you make it."

* * *

They loaded through the afternoon, and the work was good — good the way only honest labor can be, each drum and crate a small argument against despair. Suri directed the loading with the tyrannical grace of forty years on deck water, Jun and the twins hauled and joked and sweated, and the *Kestrel* sank lower in the water by degrees, taking on weight and purpose together.

It was Jun who spotted the tail.

"Don't look," he said, conversationally, passing her a coil of line. "Gray shirt. Third time past the pawnshop. Been there since we started loading."

Astrid tied off a cleat hitch and let her gaze drift, lazy as the tide. Gray shirt. Young. Clean hands for Port Salangan, and shoes that had never seen mud. He held a phone he wasn't looking at and watched the *Kestrel* the way a creditor watches collateral.

"Vela," she said.

"Vela," Jun agreed. "Since the dockside? Or since Thane?"

"That's the question, isn't it." She coiled the line over her arm, slow, thinking. If the syndicate was watching the outfitting, fine — they held her paper, they watched their investments, that was almost domestic. But if they'd been watching since Marcus made his offer, then the syndicate knew about the contract, knew the destination, and was content — *content* — to let them provision and sail.

Creditors didn't stay content. Creditors circled. Unless—

Unless they weren't worried about the debt at all. Unless the debt was just the hook, and the salvage just the bait, and somewhere upstream something waited that the Velas wanted badly enough to let a whole boatload of desperate people go fetch it for them.

"We're not recovering a wreck," Astrid said, mostly to herself. "We're retrieving something. And they know what it is."

Jun said nothing. He was a quiet man; his silences were load-bearing. But his jaw tightened, and he glanced once toward the gray shirt, and Astrid understood that he'd arrived at the same shore she had.

She should walk over. Confront the man. Make a scene, force the issue, find out now rather than later what the syndicate expected.

Instead she finished coiling the line, because the *Kestrel* sailed on tomorrow's tide whether she liked it or not, and a captain who stopped to fight every shadow on the dock never left the dock at all.

But that night, the shadows followed her anyway.

* * *

She dreamed of green water.

Not the brown-green of the lowland river, heavy with silt, but a deep, luminous, impossible green, the green of light filtered through fathoms, and in the dream she was swimming down into it though every instinct screamed that down was death. Her mentor's voice came through the water the way voices do in dreams — not heard but remembered, arriving whole. *Count your air, Astrid. Always count your air.*

She was counting. She was always counting. Fifteen years of counting, and still the numbers ran out.

In the dream the wreck hung below her like a cathedral fallen on its side, and the dead moved through it gently, going about their drowned business, and one of them turned. She knew the face. Of course she knew the face. She had memorized it in the worst way a face can be memorized — slack, blue-lipped, carried up through rapids on a plank door while the rain fell sideways and Marcus screamed at the sky with half his hand gone.

*Astrid,* the drowned man said kindly. *You came back.*

She woke with the deck lights burning through the porthole and salt sweat drying cold between her shoulder blades, and lay listening to the harbor slap against the hull. Somewhere aft, Suri snored like distant artillery. Somewhere beyond the breakwater, the sea breathed in and out.

Fifteen years, and the river had waited. Rivers were patient that way. They knew that everything eventually came back to them — the rain, the boats, the debts, the dead.

Astrid got up, dressed in the dark, and climbed to the wheelhouse, where she kept the charts. She unrolled the one Marcus had given her and weighted its corners with coffee mugs, and there it was: the coordinate block, the lost vessel's last position, sitting upriver in the forbidden interior like a splinter working its way toward the heart.

She stared at it until the sky went gray, then rose-pink, then gold, and told herself the tightness in her chest was just the debt, just the job, just the ordinary dread of a hard passage ahead.

But when the tide turned and the *Kestrel* weighed anchor, Astrid Reyes stood at the wheel and did not look back at Port Salangan — because looking back meant admitting forward, and forward meant the gorges, and the gorges meant the green water, and the green water meant the dream was right.

*You came back.*

The river bit at the hull, patient as hunger, and carried them up.
