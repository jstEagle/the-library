## 4. The River That Bites Back

The river began to fight them on the third morning, and Ilya Petrov loved it immediately.

He stood at the bow of the *Kestrel's Debt* with the spray hitting his face like thrown gravel and felt something in his chest unclench for the first time in six years — since the university had taken his credentials, his office, his name off the faculty rolls, and left him a man who read hydrology journals in internet cafés just to keep from forgetting the shapes of things. This water was not a shape in a journal. This water was alive, and it wanted them dead.

"Whirlpool, port side!" he shouted, and watched Astrid Reyes lean into the wheel without hesitation, her whole body becoming part of the boat the way only river people do. The vortex opened beside them like a slow black eye, wide enough to swallow a truck, ringed with foam that looked almost gentle. They slid past it by less than a meter. Ilya could feel its pull through the hull, a low dragging whisper, as if the river were testing their weight and finding it interesting.

"You weren't lying about these gorges," Astrid called back.

"I never lie about water," Ilya said. "It's the one thing that always tells you the truth eventually."

The gorge walls rose on either side of them now, limestone cliffs streaked green with moss and hung with vines that trailed in the current like drowned hair. Somewhere above, birds wheeled through mist. The air smelled of wet stone and rotting fruit and something mineral underneath, sulfur-adjacent, a smell Ilya catalogued without meaning to. Geothermal signature. He'd noticed it two days ago, faint but persistent, growing stronger as they climbed toward the coordinates Marcus Thane had given them. He hadn't mentioned it yet. He wasn't sure why. Perhaps because everyone else on this boat was carrying secrets too, and it seemed rude to be the first to open his.

* * *

They lost half their fuel at the logjam.

Ilya saw it coming — he'd been reading the water all morning, watching for the telltale flattening of the current where debris collected below a bend — but seeing it and avoiding it were different animals entirely. The jam stretched across the narrows like a barricade built by angry giants: whole trees, root balls the size of houses, trunks bleached white and slicked black, all of it grinding slowly against itself with a sound like furniture being broken in another room.

"We can't go around," Suri said. She'd been quiet most of the voyage, the old deckhand keeping to herself at the stern, but she came forward now with her arms crossed and her jaw set. "There's no channel. I can feel it."

"There's a gap," Ilya said, pointing. "Starboard quarter, where the current piles up against the far bank. It's narrow. Maybe four meters."

"Maybe," Suri repeated, in the tone of a woman who had survived forty years on rivers by refusing every maybe.

Astrid studied the gap for a long moment. "Throttle up, hit it straight, pray we don't catch a root ball on the transom." She glanced at Marcus, who stood near the cabin in his immaculate field jacket, his gloved right hand resting on the rail. "Your boat, technically."

"My boat is your command," Marcus said smoothly. "That was our arrangement."

Something passed across Astrid's face — Ilya couldn't name it, some old weather moving behind new clouds — and then she turned to the wheel and the moment was gone.

The approach took ninety seconds. It felt like ninety years. The current grabbed them fifty meters out and started shoving them sideways toward the jam, and Ilya heard the timbers groan against each other ahead, heard the deep wet snap of a trunk shifting under tons of pressure. Astrid corrected, overcorrected, corrected again, her hands moving in small fast increments, and then they were in the gap and the world became noise — wood shrieking along the hull on both sides, close enough to touch, water exploding over the bow, Suri shouting something in a language Ilya didn't know that was probably a prayer or probably a curse and probably both.

A root ball caught the stern.

The boat slewed. For one full second they were broadside to the current in a channel barely wider than themselves, and Ilya understood with total clarity that they were going to capsize, that he was going to die in a tangle of logs in a gorge no map named, and the strange thing was how calm the understanding made him. Then Astrid did something with the throttle and the rudder simultaneously — something wrong, something that should have spun them into the jam — and instead the current took the boat's nose and swung it through like a door closing, and suddenly they were through, wallowing in slack water beyond, taking on perhaps twenty centimeters of river in the bilge and half their remaining diesel, which had ruptured somewhere aft and now rainbow-slicked the surface behind them.

Nobody spoke for a full minute. Then Dez, the youngest of the crew, started laughing — high, hysterical, uncontrollable — and one by one the others joined him until even Suri cracked a smile, and Ilya found himself laughing too, leaning on the rail, soaked to the skin, more alive than he had any right to be.

"That maneuver," he said to Astrid when he could breathe again. "That was not in any textbook."

"No," she agreed. Her hands were shaking on the wheel now that it was over; he could see it, the delayed tremor. "Textbooks are written by people who survived by accident."

* * *

That evening they camped on a gravel bar in the lee of the gorge wall, and the crisis did what crises do: it stitched them together.

Dez and the engineer, Kofi, patched the fuel line by lantern light, arguing cheerfully about whose fault the rupture was while passing a bottle of cheap rum between them. Suri cooked rice and dried fish over a driftwood fire and refused, as always, to let anyone help, but she served Ilya first, which he understood to be an honor of some kind. Marcus sat slightly apart, as he always did, polishing the chrome fingers of his prosthetic hand with a cloth, and told stories — good ones, charming ones, stories about ports and storms and famous people he'd allegedly dined with — and the crew listened the way tired people listen to anything warm.

But Ilya watched. Watching was his profession, really, more than hydrology ever was. And he noticed that when Marcus thought no one was looking, the charm drained out of his face like water out of a cracked bowl, leaving something older and hungrier underneath. He noticed that Marcus's prosthetic hand was not standard issue. He noticed the way Marcus touched it, absently, the way a man touches a scar.

And he noticed the sonar.

It was near midnight. Ilya had gone down to the boat to check the bilge pump — old habit, old anxiety — and found the cabin dark except for the green glow of the sonar display. Marcus sat before it, alone, headphones on, logging data into a waterproof tablet with his left hand. On the screen, the riverbed rendered in ghostly contour lines, and Marcus was annotating them. Not searching for wreckage — there was no wreckage protocol here, no grid pattern, no systematic sweep of the sort a genuine salvage operation required. He was mapping. Methodically. The way a man maps a resource.

Ilya withdrew before Marcus saw him, and stood outside in the dark listening to the river grind past, feeling the cold come down off the gorge walls.

He should have said something to Astrid. He told himself he would say something in the morning. But there was a problem, and the problem was this: Ilya knew what geothermal vent fields looked like on a sonar survey, and he knew what the sulfur smell meant, and he knew — had known since the second day — that the coordinates Marcus had given them did not sit near any plausible wreck site. They sat near something else. Something worth mapping.

And Ilya had signed on because Marcus Thane paid in advance, cash, no questions, to a man whose own government had questions enough to end his career. His silence had been purchased before the boat ever left the dock. He simply hadn't let himself count the cost until now, standing in the dark, listening to the river tell the truth the way it always did, eventually.

* * *

In the morning, Astrid caught him at the rail with coffee, and for a moment he thought — hoped — she was going to ask him something difficult. Instead she asked about the gorges ahead.

"The worst is tomorrow," Ilya said. "The Serpent's Teeth. Three cataracts in succession, no portage possible on the north bank." He hesitated. "There's a ledge on the south side. Narrow. If we winch the boat up and line it through—"

"Winching takes a day. Maybe two."

"Yes."

She looked upstream, chewing on it. The morning mist was burning off the water in long silver ribbons, and the cliffs ahead turned gold at their crowns. Behind them, Marcus emerged from the cabin with his tablet tucked under his arm, and Ilya watched Astrid's eyes track it — just for a heartbeat, just a flicker — and something in her face changed. Not suspicion exactly. Recognition. The look of a woman noticing a current she'd been feeling for days without being able to name it.

"Ilya," she said quietly, not turning her head. "What does he have on that tablet?"

The question landed like a stone in still water. Ilya held his coffee and considered the geometry of his life: the money in his account, the career in ruins behind him, the river ahead, and this woman beside him who had once, according to Suri's drunken telling on the second night, carried her mentor's body out of a gorge very much like this one.

"Survey data," he said. "Of the riverbed. He logs it every night. It isn't salvage work, Astrid. It's mapping."

She was silent for a long time. The river ran beneath them, brown and patient, carrying everything downstream except the truth, which it kept for itself.

"Mapping what?" she asked.

And Ilya, who had decided overnight that his silence was bought and finished, heard himself answer anyway — because some currents, once you've spent your life reading water, you cannot pretend not to see.

"Sulfur," he said. "Heat signatures in the bedrock. Captain — I think he's not looking for a wreck at all. I think he's looking for the source."
