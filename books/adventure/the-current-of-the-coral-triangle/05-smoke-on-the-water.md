## 5. Smoke on the Water

Suri smelled it before she saw it — a sweetness under the river's green breath, like palm sugar left too long on the fire. She was mending nets on the aft deck, fingers working the old patterns her mother taught her, when the wind shifted and brought the smell upstream. She stopped. Her hands knew what her mind refused: that smell did not come from cooking or from jungle rot. It came from houses.

"Turn us around," she said to no one, and then, louder, "Astrid. Turn us around."

The *Kestrel's Wake* rounded the bend at half throttle, and the village opened before them like a wound.

It sat where every river village sits — on stilts above the brown water, walkways strung between homes, drying racks hung with fish that would never be eaten now. Or it had sat there. The stilts were blackened to charcoal below the waterline. The roofs had gone in first, Suri could see; the fire had climbed them and then leapt house to house along the walkways, greedy as a monkey. What remained were skeletons of bamboo and ironwood, still standing because the water held their feet, their upper halves collapsed inward like burnt offerings. Smoke still rose from three of them in thin gray threads, patient as prayer.

On the mudbank, a dog stood ankle-deep in ash and watched them pass. It did not bark. That was the worst of it, Suri thought later. Not the burned things, not even the small shapes she made herself look away from near the largest house. It was the dog, standing its post over nothing, waiting for people who were not coming back.

Nobody spoke on deck. Ilya turned his face away toward the far bank. Marcus stood at the bow rail with his prosthetic hand folded over the living one, and if he felt anything he kept it behind his teeth, which was where he kept everything. Astrid stood at the wheel and did not blink, and Suri watched her not blinking and understood that it cost her something enormous to keep her eyes open.

They passed a canoe, swamped, its prow carved into the shape of a hornbill. A child's paddle floated past the hull, turning slowly, and Suri reached for it without deciding to, pulled it aboard, and held it across her knees like something rescued from a drowning.

"They went through here ahead of us," Ilya said quietly. "Gunboats. Two days, maybe less. You can still taste the fuel oil."

"Vela," Suri said. It wasn't a question.

Astrid's jaw worked once. "Vela," she said.

* * *

They anchored that night in a side channel, screened by pandanus, engines cold. Suri found Astrid alone at the stern, sitting on the fuel drums with a mug of coffee she hadn't touched, watching the dark water slide past. Suri lowered herself onto the drum beside her with the groan of an old woman, which she was entitled to, being fifty-eight and having earned every year the hard way.

She set the child's paddle between them.

"You saw it," Suri said.

"I saw it."

"And you're still steering north."

Astrid looked at the paddle for a long time. In the lantern light her face was all planes and shadows, older than her years, the way the river makes people old without asking permission. "We turn back, we lose everything. The boat. The crew's wages. My mother's house is already collateral — you know that. Vela doesn't forgive, Suri. They amortize."

"I've heard that word from your mouth before," Suri said. "Fifteen years ago. On the beach after we buried Halim. You swore on his grave you'd never take another contract upriver. You said some rivers are owed more than they can collect." She tapped the paddle. "This is one of those rivers. And now there's a village burning behind us and gunboats ahead of us, and you want to talk about amortization?"

"It isn't that simple."

"It never is. That's how they get you."

Astrid set down the untouched coffee and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. When she spoke again her voice was low and rough, stripped of the captain's polish. "You think I don't know? You think I slept last night, or the night before? I know exactly what this looks like. A syndicate burns a corridor clean so their salvage boat can pass without witnesses, and I'm the salvage boat. I'm the mop they sent in after the fire." She dropped her hands. Her eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "But if I turn this boat around, Suri, Vela takes the *Wake*, takes my crew's pay, takes my mother's house, and puts me in a room with two men and a pair of pliers until I agree to pilot for them anyway. The only difference is I'll be doing it broken, and my people will be starving. At least this way—"

"At least this way what? You get paid to be complicit?"

"Yes!" It came out cracked, almost a sob, and Astrid strangled it down to a whisper. "Yes. That's exactly what it is. I'm choosing to be complicit because the alternative is worse, and I hate myself for the arithmetic, but I'm not going to pretend the arithmetic isn't real. You've never owed anyone your whole life, Suri. You own your hands and your nets and nobody can take them. Don't stand there and tell me survival is a sin until you've had to price it."

Suri was quiet for a while. The river talked to itself in the dark, the way it always did, patient and indifferent. Somewhere downstream a night bird called three times and got no answer.

"Halim used to say," she said finally, "that the river forgives everyone except the ones who know better." She picked up the paddle and turned it over, feeling the smooth worn wood, the small hand-shape ground into it by use. "I'm not telling you to turn back, girl. I'm too old to swim home and too stubborn to drown. I'm telling you to open your eyes all the way. This job isn't salvage. Whatever Marcus is paying for, whatever Vela is clearing the road for — it's conquest. And when conquest finishes eating the people it came for, it eats its tools next. I've seen it. You've seen it. We buried the proof of it fifteen years ago."

Astrid said nothing. But Suri saw her hand drift, unconsciously, to the scar on her forearm — the old rope burn from the drowned expedition, the one she never explained and never hid. Fifteen years of carrying that mark, and still reaching for it whenever the truth got close.

"I need to know one thing," Suri said. "When the moment comes — and it will come, up there at the source, it always comes — when you have to choose between the money and what's right, which way does the current run?"

Astrid looked out at the black water for a long moment. Then she looked down at the child's paddle lying between them, and something in her face closed and hardened, like a fist forming around a decision.

"The money's already spent," she said quietly. "In my head, it's been spent since the dock. All that's left to decide is what I do with what's actually mine."

"And what's that?"

"My hands. My word. Who I am when the river asks." She stood, took the cold coffee, and poured it over the side — an offering, an ending, Suri couldn't tell which. "Get some sleep, old woman. Tomorrow we make the gorge shallows, and I need your eyes on the water."

Suri stayed on the drums a while longer after she'd gone, holding the paddle, listening to the river. She had asked her question and gotten half an answer, and half an answer from Astrid Reyes was worth more than a whole one from most captains. But half was only half.

And far off to the north, past the sleeping jungle, the sky glowed faintly — not stars, not moonrise, but a dull orange stain on the clouds, like a second sunset that refused to end. Another village, Suri thought. Another corridor being cleared. The gunboats were moving faster than they were.

She tucked the paddle under her arm and went below, and dreamed, as she had not dreamed in fifteen years, of Halim standing waist-deep in green water, saying her name.
