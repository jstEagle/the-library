## 6. What Marcus Buried

The rain came at dusk, the way it always did in the interior — not falling so much as arriving, a warm gray wall that turned the river to static and the world to sound. Astrid stood under the *Kestrel's* aft canopy and watched Marcus's cabin door across the deck, watching it the way you watch a dog you don't trust near a child.

He'd been down there for three hours. The generator hummed. Dinner cooled under a tarp on the galley counter, rice going gummy, Ilya's fish stew losing its heat one degree at a time. Nobody had called him up. Nobody would.

It was Suri who finally said it, without looking up from the net she was mending, her needle moving with the patience of fifty years of knots: "You're going to go look."

"I'm not."

"You've been staring at that door since the light went." She pulled a stitch tight. "In my village we had a saying. A locked room is either empty or full. You never find out which by standing outside it."

"Suri—"

"He logs sonar at night," she said simply. "When he thinks we're sleeping. I hear the pings through the hull. We are not looking for wreckage, Astrid. Wreckage doesn't need mapping."

Astrid looked at the old woman's hands, brown and knuckled, steady as mooring cleats. Then at the door. The rain drummed the canopy like fingers.

"Keep everyone fed," Astrid said. "If he comes up, tell him I'm checking the anchor line."

* * *

Marcus Thane's cabin smelled of cedar oil and machine grease, the two smells of everything he'd ever been — the polished surface and the machinery underneath. She'd been in this room once before, fifteen years ago, on a different boat, when they were still young enough to share charts over cheap rum and argue about current patterns until dawn. That memory had a patina on it now, soft-edged, almost kind. She made herself look past it.

The cabin was neat in the way of a man who had learned to live with half his body replaced. Everything stowed. Everything squared. His prosthetic hand lay on the desk beside a charging cradle, fingers curled slightly open, and Astrid's stomach turned at how much it looked like a sleeping thing, some pale crustacean washed ashore. He was above deck, then, or in the head. Either way: minutes, not hours.

She told herself she was looking for the manifest. Fuel ledgers. Anything that explained why their fuel margin had shrunk twice as fast as the distance justified — because she'd done the math three nights running and the math kept telling her someone was skimming, or lying, or both.

She found the chart tube behind the life vest locker, wedged where a man who trusted no one wedges things he can't afford to lose.

It was heavier than paper should be. Waterproofed canvas, military-grade, the kind of tube survey companies used, not salvage outfits. Her hands knew what that meant before her mind agreed to say it. She popped the cap and slid the contents onto the desk, and the lamp threw its light across them, and for a long moment Astrid Reyes did not breathe.

Maps. Not charts — maps. Bathymetric surveys of the upper gorge, rendered in contour lines fine as woodgrain, the river rendered as a blue vein threading through shaded relief. And there, upstream of every landmark she remembered, upstream of the narrows, upstream of the falls they'd portaged around with her mentor's body lashed to a stretcher — a vent field. Drawn in red. Dozens of thermal anomalies along a fault line beneath the riverbed, clustered around a source basin, annotated in Marcus's handwriting — that handwriting she'd know anywhere, the same slanted capitals he used on birthday cards he never sent:

**THERMAL GRADIENT 3X PROJECTION. COMMERCIAL SCALE.**

She checked the date stamp because some part of her still hoped. Some part of her, stupid and loyal after all these years, wanted the map to be new. Wanted it to be something he'd discovered on this trip, something she could forgive.

*Survey completed: eleven years ago.*

Eleven years. Four years before the *Meridian* went missing. Before the distress beacon that had drawn them all upriver like iron filings. Before the wreck that didn't exist yet.

Astrid sat down slowly on Marcus's bunk, because her legs had decided the matter without consulting her.

He hadn't lost a research vessel in the forbidden interior. He had built one. Built the story of one — a hull, a beacon, a contract with a salvage clause generous enough to make desperate people say yes. Bait. Fifteen years of grief and debt and nightmares, and the whole time the wreck was a lure on a hook, and the hook was baited with the only water in the world Astrid Reyes had sworn never to enter again.

And Vela. God. Vela wasn't chasing a salvage fee. Vela gunboats burning villages ahead of them — clearing a corridor — for a deposit Marcus had mapped before Astrid's mentor died. Which meant the syndicate had known. Which meant the contract she'd signed in desperation was written years ago, in ink she'd never seen, by a man who needed her exactly as much as she needed money: someone with a reputation for finding wrecks, and a reason never to ask questions about this one.

Her mentor's face came to her then, unbidden — the way it always did at night, wet-haired, patient, saying *the river gives nothing back, Astrid, it only lends.* He had died lending. And Marcus had spent eleven years turning that loan into a business plan.

She was still holding the map when the deck above her boots changed its sound.

Rain has a rhythm. Anyone who works water learns it in their spine — the even drumming of weather, and then the wrongness that means footsteps. The footsteps crossed overhead, stopped, and came back. Then the engine note shifted, dropped half a tone, and Ilya's voice carried down through the planking, tight and flat the way it got when he was frightened and refusing to show it:

"Contact. Two contacts, astern, running dark. They're not fishing boats."

Astrid rolled the maps with hands that shook only a little, which surprised her, and slid them back into the tube, and took the tube with her, because evidence was the only weapon she had left that fired straight.

* * *

The narrows chose themselves. That was the cruelty of the upper gorge — a hundred kilometers of wide, braided, forgiving water, and then a single place where the banks pinched together and the current funneled through like a fist closing. No room to run. No room to hide. Every pilot who worked the interior knew the narrows, and every pilot who worked the interior avoided being inside them when strangers came calling.

"Cut the lights," Astrid said. "Kill the running lamps. Ilya, take us to the north bank, slow, let the current carry us — we drift through, we don't drive."

"They'll have thermal," Marcus said beside her. He'd come up from below with his prosthetic hand already seated, already flexing, and his face wore an expression Astrid now understood completely: not surprise. Preparation. "They'll see our engine heat against the cold water regardless. Drifting buys us minutes, not safety."

"Then those minutes are yours to explain." She turned and held the chart tube between them like a bar. "Eleven years, Marcus. You surveyed the vent field four years before your ship sank. Except it didn't sink, did it. There is no *Meridian*. There never was."

Around them the crew had gone very still. Rain ticked off the canopy. Somewhere downstream, close now, an engine note that did not belong to them rose and fell, patient as a shark.

Marcus looked at the tube. Then at her. And Astrid watched him decide — watched the calculation move behind his eyes like a fish under ice — and when he spoke, his voice was gentle, which was worse than shouting.

"You were always the best of us, Astrid. That was never in question. It's why you're here." He nodded toward the dark ahead, where the gorge walls rose black against blacker sky. "Everything I did, I did to get back to that place. Do you understand? The river took my hand. It took your teacher. It took fifteen years of both our lives. And sitting under all that grief, the entire time, was the largest untapped energy deposit in the Coral Triangle. Grief with a price tag. I just had to be the one patient enough to collect."

"And the villages?" Suri's voice, from the shadows by the wheel. "The smoke on the water. Was that in your projection too?"

"The syndicate's methods aren't mine." But he said it too fast, and everyone heard it.

"Contacts closing," Ilya called. "Half a kilometer. They're boxing us — one ahead in the narrows, one behind."

Astrid felt the moment tilt, the way a boat tilts when it takes water on one side. Ahead: the narrows, and whatever waited in them. Behind: the gunboats, and Vela's version of the future. Between: a man she'd once loved like a brother, holding a map of a lie.

"Give me the tube," Marcus said quietly, extending his flesh hand. "We can still do this cleanly. You get paid. Your crew walks free of Vela. Everyone eats. Isn't that what you wanted — everyone intact?"

"That's what I wanted," Astrid said. "You wanted everyone *owned*."

The first flare went up then, off the water behind them, and the night turned white — hard, chemical white, throwing the gorge walls into relief like a photograph, throwing the gunboat's silhouette sharp against the rain, throwing all their faces into sudden terrible clarity. In the light, Astrid saw the forward gun mount begin to traverse toward them, and heard the loudhailer crackle to life with a voice flattened by static and authority:

"*Vessel Kestrel. Cut engines. Prepare to be boarded. This corridor is closed by order of the Vela Consolidated.*"

Marcus moved first — of course he did, he'd rehearsed this — reaching for the tube, for the helm, for the plan. And Astrid, with the river roaring ahead and the flarelight dying and her crew's faces turning to her out of the dark, did the only thing the last fifteen years had taught her to do.

She threw the maps overboard.

The tube hit the black water and was gone between one heartbeat and the next, swallowed whole, and Marcus made a sound she'd never heard from him before — raw, brief, human — and then the first shot cracked across the water, and the narrows took them all.
