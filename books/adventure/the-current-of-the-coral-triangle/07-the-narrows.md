## 7. The Narrows

The narrows announced themselves before we saw them — a change in the river's voice, from the broad mutter of the middle channel to something tighter, angrier, the sound of water being forced through a throat. I stood at the bow of the *Kestrel's Wake* with my prosthetic hand resting on the rail, feeling the vibration of the current climb through the metal and into the bones they'd left me, and I thought: *this is where it happens. This is where I stop lying.*

Fifteen years of lies, and it came down to a stretch of water half a mile long.

Behind me, Astrid was at the helm, her hands light on the wheel, her eyes doing that thing they'd always done — reading the surface like scripture, finding the safe line through chaos. She hadn't spoken to me since the night before, since she'd come out of my cabin with my survey maps folded against her chest like a wound dressing, and her face had been the color of the river in flood season. She knew now. Of course she knew. The maps were dated four years before the *Meridian* ever went missing; there was no honest way to explain that, and I had stopped trying to find one somewhere around the third burned village.

So when the first shell hit the cliff face above us and the rock came down like the sky was shedding its skin, part of me felt — relief. No more performance. No more charm worn thin as hull paint. Just the truth, delivered at two hundred meters per second.

"Gunboats!" Suri's voice cracked across the deck from the stern watch. "Two of them, coming out of the eastern cut — *hard* behind us!"

I turned. They were beautiful, in the way that everything Vela owned was beautiful — low black hulls, matte superstructures, autocannons mounted fore like the snouts of hunting dogs. The syndicate had cleared this corridor for us, village by village, and now they'd arrived to collect on the corridor. Or perhaps to make sure that what waited upstream never reached anyone's hands but theirs. With Vela, the distinction was academic. Everything they touched became an invoice.

Astrid didn't scream orders. That was the thing about her that fifteen years hadn't changed and nothing ever would — under fire, she went quiet, her voice dropping into a register you felt in your sternum rather than heard. "Hard to port. Everyone below the gunwale. Marcus — get off my bow."

"Captain—"

"*Get down.*"

I got down. There was no point arguing with her about whose boat it was; the contract said mine, the fuel and the blood said hers, and in the arithmetic of rivers, blood always carried the greater weight.

* * *

The second shell took out our stern fuel drum, and the explosion wrote itself across my back in heat even before the sound arrived. I lay flat against the deck plates and watched the fire crawl up the aft rail, and I did the math I'd been doing for fifteen years, except now it was happening in real time, in fire, and the numbers were people.

Two gunboats. One wounded riverboat carrying six souls and half its fuel. A gorge narrowing ahead to perhaps thirty meters, walls of wet black stone rising two hundred, the current through it running nine knots if it ran one. No maneuvering room. No escape downstream — the gunboats had seen to that. Only forward, into the narrows, toward the source, toward the thing I had spent a decade and a fortune and most of my left hand learning how to hold.

I could hear Astrid shouting to Ilya — *the eddy behind the horn rock, can we make it, can we make it* — and Ilya shouting back that the eddy was a rumor, that no boat our size had ever made it, that the last man who tried was still in the river as far as anyone knew. And underneath all of it, the autocannons stitching the water astern of us, closer, closer, each burst throwing up white feathers of spray that rained down hot on the deck.

So I stood up. Into the fire, into the gunfire, into the full sight of both gunboats, because there was one card left in my hand and it was not a card you could play lying down.

"Astrid!" I shouted over the din. "They're not here for salvage!"

She looked at me from the helm, and her eyes were terrible and clear, and I understood in that moment that she had known — had known since the maps, probably since the dockside, maybe since fifteen years ago, since the day we buried Elena Vasquez in the shallows and swore on her body never to come back — and that knowing had not saved her from coming anyway, because debt is a current stronger than grief. I had counted on that. God forgive me, I had built the entire voyage on that.

"They're here for the source!" I called. "And so am I! The vent field is real, Astrid — the readings are real — enough geothermal power to run a province, to run three! Vela wants it, and I'm the only man alive who knows how to tap it without killing everyone within fifty kilometers!"

"And the wreck?" Her voice carried across the burning deck like a blade laid flat. "The *Meridian*? The salvage contract? Was any of it true?"

"The wreck was real once." I spread my hands — flesh and steel. "I made it real again. I sank her myself, Astrid. In the delta, in deep water, where no one would look. The coordinates I gave you were always the source."

Something moved through her crew then — I saw it pass along the rail like a shockwave through water. Reyes at the winch, going gray. The twins by the cargo net, gripping each other's arms. Old Suri, who had objected from the first night, who had read the whole voyage like weather, turning toward me with an expression I will see when I close my eyes for the rest of my life, however long that turns out to be.

"You used her," Suri said. Not loudly. The guns almost swallowed it. "You used Elena's grave as bait."

"I used the only thing strong enough to bring Astrid Reyes back to this river," I said. "Yes."

Because it was true, and because the truth was all I had left to offer, I gave them the rest of it, standing in the open while the shells walked closer across the water.

"I don't want to sell the source to Vela. I want to take it *from* Vela. Every peso they bled out of my district after the flood, every boat they burned, every family they moved off land their grandfathers died on — the deposit pays all of it back. It buys the coast back. It buys—" and here my voice did something I hadn't authorized, cracked like young ice, "—it buys back what the river took. My hand. My crew. My name. And yours, Astrid. Yours too. We go in together, we hold the source, and nobody ever owns us again. Not Vela. Not the banks. Not the river. That was always the plan. That was always—"

"That was always just you," Astrid said. "Dressed up as all of us."

The next shell hit the water ten meters off the port beam, and the world turned to wall.

* * *

I remember the wave in fragments. The boat lifting as though the river had decided to inspect its cargo personally. Cargo netting parting. The twins going into the water together, which was the right call — paired swimmers survive these rivers — and Reyes diving after them with a line around his waist like a man leaping into a furnace because his children were in it. Ilya at the engine hatch, keeping us alive with both hands and profanity.

And Suri.

Suri, sixty-eight years old, bad knee, good eye, hauling the stern line to swing the *Wake*'s tail out of the autocannon's arc — putting her own body between the gun platform and the rest of us, working the cleat with hands that had been splicing rope since before I was born. A burst caught the rail above her. Splinters and worse. She went down against the deckhouse and stayed down, and Astrid's cry when she saw it was not a captain's cry. It was a daughter's.

"Eddy!" Ilya roared. "Horn rock — NOW — hard aport, all of it, ALL OF IT—"

Astrid put the boat into the turn of her life. I have watched pilots thread typhoons and I have never seen anything like it: the *Kestrel's Wake* heeling until the river looked up at us sideways, sliding across the current's shoulder into the slot behind the horn rock where the water pooled and breathed, a pocket of stillness the size of a chapel, and the gunboats — the gunboats overshot. Their momentum was built for open water. They swept past the mouth of the narrows, cannons blazing at cliffs, and the current took them exactly the way the current takes everything, sideways and down, and for ninety glorious seconds we were alone with our fire and our dead weight and our truths.

Astrid knelt over Suri. Pressed both hands to the woman's side, below the ribs, and her hands came away red, and I watched Astrid Reyes — the hardest bargainer on the coast, the woman who had chased storms for money until money was the only language she spoke — look at her oldest friend bleeding out on a deck I had led her to, and make a calculation.

I knew the calculation. I'd written it. Salvage fee: enough to clear every debt, every lien, every hungry month. Cost: pushing upstream another six hours with a dying woman, into the teeth of whatever Vela sent next, toward a source I would control and she would not.

"Marcus," she said, without looking up. "The contract. Read me the abandonment clause."

"There isn't—" I started, and stopped, because there was, because I had written that clause too, in a moment of arrogance so complete it bordered on religious: *the charter may be abandoned at any point prior to recovery, forfeiting all consideration.* I had put it in thinking she'd never dare. Thinking she needed the money too much.

"Forfeit," she said. "All of it. Log it. Witness it." She tore her shirt hem for bandages. "We're done, Marcus. Not the voyage — the contract. You can keep your fee, your source, your war with Vela. I'm taking my crew and getting Suri to a hospital if I have to carry her to the sea in my teeth."

"You'll die in the narrows," I said. "Both gunboats are regrouping. You've got no fuel, no fee, no—"

"I've got her," Astrid said, and pressed the bandage down, and Suri groaned and cursed her with magnificent fluency, which meant she was alive, which meant Astrid smiled — a small, ruined, absolutely unbought smile — "and I've got them, and that was always more than you thought it was worth. That's the difference between us, Marcus. You price things. I keep them."

The gunboats' engines came back up the gorge then, deeper-throated, patient. Behind them, far upstream, past the last bend, the air above the water had begun to shimmer — a heat-haze rising off the river itself, and beneath it a sound like the mountain clearing its throat.

The source, breathing. Waiting for all of us.

Astrid lifted Suri gently as the first shot sparked off the horn rock above our heads, and shouted for Ilya to give her everything the engine had left, and drove us upstream — away from the money, away from the exit, away from every sane heading on the chart — straight into the steam.
