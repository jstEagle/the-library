## 8. The Forbidden Source

They carried Suri up the last of the gorge on a litter made from the shattered hull planking and two paddles, and every step of it was a step Astrid had taken before, fifteen years younger and fifteen years more certain that she would never come back.

The river had narrowed to something barely wider than her boat. It ran clear here — impossibly, obscenely clear — over stone the color of old coins, and where it tumbled through the rock teeth below them it made no sound like any river she knew. It hissed. The whole gorge breathed. Steam rose from fissures in the banks in slow white columns, and the air tasted of sulfur and wet iron, hot against the skin one moment and cold as a cellar the next, as though the mountain could not decide whether it was dying or being born.

"Almost there," Ilya said, for the fourth time in an hour. He said it the way men say *almost* when they are trying to convince themselves the map still loves them. His hydrologist's eyes kept going to the water, to the temperature shimmer on its surface, to the faint blue-green glow rising from the deep channel where the current slowed. "The discharge is wrong," he murmured. "It's been wrong since the narrows. Something down there is feeding heat into the whole system."

Astrid adjusted Suri's weight on her shoulder. The old deckhand had gone gray-lipped during the climb, the bullet wound in her side bound with strips of sailcloth and a prayer, but her eyes were open and tracking, and every so often she would say, dry as driftwood, "Put me down, girl, I can limp." And every time, Astrid pretended not to hear.

Behind them, three days downstream, the narrows were still smoking. Behind them lay the *Kestrel's* scattered wreckage, half their fuel burning on the water, and the sound of Vela gunboats pulling away from the fight to regroup — because Marcus Thane had stood on his burning deck with his prosthetic hand raised and told them, calmly, that there was nothing left to salvage but the thing itself.

Ahead, around one last bend of black basalt, was the source.

* * *

She smelled it before she saw it: a sweetness under the sulfur, mineral and green, like rain on hot slate. Then the gorge opened.

The basin was perhaps four hundred meters across, a bowl walled in by cliffs streaked white with centuries of mineral bleed. At its center, the river simply *ended* — rose out of the earth in a vast, slow boil of turquoise water, luminous from beneath, lit by some furnace far below the floor of the world. Steam hung over it in curtains and columns, drifting, parting, reforming, so that the light came through in shafts and the whole place felt less like a landscape than a cathedral mid-sermon. Terraces of travertine stepped down from the vent pool in shelves of cream and rust, each one rimmed with water so clear it seemed carved from glass.

Fifteen years ago, Astrid had stood on the lowest terrace with Tomás Okafor's blood drying on her hands and watched this same light come through the steam, and she had sworn on his body that no one — not Vela, not Thane, not God — would ever put a pipe into it.

"You're doing the face," Suri rasped from the litter. "The one you do before you do something stupid."

"I don't have a face."

"You have a whole *catalogue*." But the old woman's hand found hers and squeezed, once, hard. "He'd have liked it here. Tomás. He always said the river was a church and we were just tourists."

Astrid set the litter down on a terrace above the waterline and looked at what remained of her crew. Ilya, soaked to the ribs, already crouched at the terrace edge with a thermometer he'd salvaged from the wreck, muttering numbers to himself like a man counting rosary beads. Dez, the youngest, standing guard with a rifle he barely knew how to hold, watching the gorge mouth they'd climbed out of. And Suri, gray and stubborn and alive, which after the narrows felt like a miracle Astrid hadn't earned.

"We rest until dark," she said. "Then we go back down the gorge on foot. No boat, no cargo, no pay. We walk out, we disappear, and Vela can invoice the jungle."

Ilya looked up. "And the deposit?"

"There is no deposit. There never was. There's a wound in the earth and men who want to pour money into it until it kills them." She heard her own voice go flat and hard. "We leave it buried."

Dez shifted. "Marcus—"

"Marcus sold us." She said it without heat, because the heat had burned off somewhere in the narrows, between the first shell and Suri falling. What was left was colder and more useful. "He built the wreck, he baited the contract, and he walked Vela's gunboats upriver behind us like sheep with a bell. Whatever he thinks he owns at the end of this, we're not going to be standing near it."

But even as she said it, her eyes went to the vent pool — to the turquoise heart of it, breathing steam, patient as it had been patient for ten thousand years — and she thought about fail-safes. Tomás had drilled them into all of them on that first expedition, years before anyone dreamed of drilling anything else: the vent field's natural plumbing, the choke points in the rock, the places where a few kilos of shaped charge or a well-placed collapse of the overhanging cliff would drop a thousand tons of basalt into the throat of the system and seal it forever. He'd called it the river's self-respect. *If it ever comes to this,* he'd said, laughing, *promise me you'll let the mountain win.*

She had promised. She had spent fifteen years hoping she'd never have to keep it.

* * *

They came at dusk, and they came loudly, because Marcus wanted her to hear them coming.

First the gunboats — three of them, running dark up the final reach of navigable water, engines throttled to a growl that echoed off the cliffs like distant thunder. Floodlights bloomed on the terraces. Men in Vela gray fanned out along the basin rim, rifles slung with the casual menace of professionals who had been promised easy work. And in the middle of them, walking up the travertine steps like a man arriving at his own coronation, came Marcus Thane.

The prosthetic hand gleamed under the floodlights. He'd stopped hiding it. That was the first thing Astrid noticed — fifteen years of gloves and long sleeves and careful angles, and now the chrome was bare, catching the light, almost a boast. The second thing she noticed was that he wasn't looking at her at all. He was looking past her, at the vent pool, and his face had gone young and naked with want.

"Astrid." He spread his arms. "You beat us here. I told them you would. I told them you always were the better riverman."

"You told them whatever got them to carry your bags," she said. Her voice carried flat across the basin. "You sank your own ship, Marcus. You drowned your own crew — those men on the *Kestrel* who thought they were running salvage. For *this*."

"For this." He turned at last, and the floodlight caught the scar tissue climbing his neck, the ruin the river had made of him, and for one unguarded second there was grief in his face, real grief, old as the wound itself. "Do you know what they gave me, Astrid? After? A pension and a handshake. Do you know what Vela gave my family when I couldn't work? Nothing. Do you know what this is worth?" He gestured at the boiling turquoise heart, and his voice dropped to something almost reverent. "Enough to buy back everything the river took. From both of us. Your debt. My hand. Tomás—" he caught himself, and she watched him decide to say it anyway, "—Tomás would have understood. He understood what power was worth."

"He understood what it *cost*." Astrid didn't move. Dez had drifted to her shoulder; Ilya had pulled Suri's litter back into the shadow of a steam column, and she prayed the old woman had the sense to stay there. "You think you own this because you led them here. Look at them, Marcus. Look at Vela's guns. They don't need you anymore. You brought them to the door, and the moment it opens, you're furniture."

Something flickered across his face — the truth of it, landing — and then the Vela commander stepped forward, a compact woman with a rain-dark uniform and a voice like a filed blade.

"The deposit survey," she said. "Your engineer will mark the drill sites tonight. Extraction begins at dawn." Her eyes moved to Astrid, unhurried, cataloguing. "Reyes. The syndicate remembers you. The debt survives you, if nothing else does."

"The debt," Astrid said, "is about to become very difficult to collect."

Because while they talked — while Marcus believed he was using Vela and Vela believed they were using Marcus, and each of them smiled at the other across the steam — Astrid had been backing, one slow step at a time, toward the base of the western cliff. Toward the overhang of fractured basalt that Tomás had shown her fifteen years ago, that hung above the vent's throat like a held breath. Toward the satchel of demolition charges Dez had hauled out of the *Kestrel*'s wreck because salvage crews never travel light, and which now sat against her hip, heavier than any debt she had ever owed.

The commander saw it a heartbeat too late. So did Marcus.

"Astrid." His voice cracked, all the charm gone out of it. "Don't. *Please.*"

Above the pool, the steam parted, and the turquoise light came through in a single vast shaft, and the mountain breathed in, and held.
