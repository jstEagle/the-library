## 9. Collapse

**Astrid**

The source was a cathedral, and they had come to rob it.

Steam rose in columns from the turquoise terraces, thick as incense, veiling the cliffs in a shifting white that made the whole basin feel like the inside of a lung. The water glowed from below — a blue so saturated it seemed lit by its own conviction — and where it overflowed the mineral shelves it fell in curtains of scalding lace. Fifteen years ago Astrid had stood on this same shelf and watched her mentor walk into that glow and not walk out. She had sworn the place a grave. Now she was standing in it again with Vela gunboats idling at the mouth of the gorge and Marcus Thane's voice carrying across the terraces like a man giving a tour of his own kingdom.

"Look at it," he called, arms wide, his prosthetic hand catching the steam-light. "Enough energy under this floor to run a nation for a century. Enough to buy back every debt ever written against us. You could be free, Astrid. All of you."

She heard Suri breathing behind her — shallow, wet, the bullet still lodged somewhere the field dressing couldn't reach — and Ilya's hand steady on her shoulder, and she understood that everything came down to what she did in the next thirty seconds.

The fail-safes were real. Her mentor had designed them: emergency vent plugs, steel and concrete, sunk into the bedrock flanking the main fumarole, meant to seal the system if it ever threatened to blow. He had built them to protect the river. Marcus thought they were relics. The Vela engineers thought they were plumbing.

Astrid knew better. She had helped pour them when she was twenty-three, her hands gray with cement, her mentor laughing at how badly she'd mixed the first batch.

The gunboats were pushing into the basin now, their wakes wrinkling the turquoise shallows. Marcus stood on the lead boat's deck, and behind him Vela men with rifles, and above them all the steam rose like prayers no one was listening to.

"You'll never get another chance," Marcus said. "Neither will I."

He was right about himself. He was wrong about her.

Astrid turned to the nearest plug housing. The release wheel was crusted with fifteen years of mineral scale, but beneath the crust the steel was sound, because her mentor had never built anything cheap. She set both hands on it. The metal burned cold through her palms even in the steam-heat, as if it remembered what it was for.

"Don't," Marcus said, and for one moment there was no charm left in his voice at all — only the boy he'd been before the river took his hand, only the man who had loved the same water she had, and hated it, and needed it to owe him something.

"I'm sorry," Astrid said, and she wasn't sure anymore whether she was saying it to him or to the dead.

She threw her weight against the wheel.

**Marcus**

The sound the mountain made was not an explosion. It was a decision.

Deep below the terraces, in chambers no survey had ever fully mapped, the plugged vents began to close, and the pressure that had spent millennia learning patience found itself with nowhere left to go. Marcus felt it through the deck plates before he heard it — a shudder rising through the hull like the river clearing its throat.

"No," he said. "No, no—"

He had planned for betrayal. He had planned for Vela's greed, for Astrid's sentimentality, for every current except the one thing he had never been able to account for in fifteen years of scheming: that Astrid Reyes would rather destroy a fortune than let anyone hold it. He had built his entire future on the assumption that she wanted what he wanted. Freedom, wealth, the past undone. He had never once considered that she might want the river more.

The first terrace blew.

It went up in a column of boiling water and shattered travertine, a white geyser two hundred feet tall, and the shockwave flattened the steam clouds like breath blown out. The lead gunboat heaved sideways. Men who had been aiming rifles a moment ago were suddenly crawling, screaming, grabbing railings as the turquoise shallows drained away between the terraces — sucked down into the collapsing vent field like the basin exhaling its last breath.

Then the surge came back.

It came down the gorge as a wall, brown and white and roaring, carrying trees and rock and the shredded remains of the upper terraces. The second gunboat simply ceased to exist — lifted, folded, swallowed. The third spun broadside and went under with its engines still turning.

Marcus stood on the listing deck of the lead boat and watched his fortune bury itself. A thousand tons of rock coming down where the deposit had been, sealing it under stone and scald, and the energy he had chased across half his life now locked away where no syndicate, no engineer, no amount of money would ever reach it.

Astrid's voice reached him over the roar — thin, almost lost, but he knew it anywhere.

*"There's room! Get off the boat!"*

She was on the high shelf near the old survey station, arm outstretched toward a rope Ilya had anchored, offering him the one thing he'd spent years telling himself he'd earn: a way out. A way back. A future with his remaining hand gripping something other than a ledger.

He looked at her. He looked at the flood taking everything else.

His prosthetic hand flexed once against the railing, the servos whining, and Marcus Thane understood that the river had finally given him exactly what he'd asked for. He had told her once, drunk, in another life: *I'll have it all back or I'll have nothing.* He had never imagined those were the same offer.

"I'm staying," he said, though the water was already too loud for words. "Tell them—" and then the surge took the sentence, and the boat, and him.

Astrid screamed his name into the flood. It sounded, to him, almost like forgiveness.

* * *

**Ilya**

Ilya had spent three years being told he was finished — stripped of his credentials, his name dragged through hydrology journals, his life reduced to drinking in port towns and knowing things nobody paid for. He had joined this voyage because Astrid Reyes was the first person in years to ask what he knew instead of what he'd done.

So when he saw her go down.

The secondary collapse caught the lower shelf as she ran for higher ground, and the ground itself moved — a whole section of travertine calving away into the churning basin, and Astrid with it, sliding on wet mineral glass toward the brown water that had just eaten two gunboats. Ilya saw her catch the edge of a fissure, saw her boots find nothing, saw the flood reach up for her the way it had reached for her mentor fifteen years ago, and every calculation he had ever loved abandoned him at once.

He went over the anchor rope.

The rope burned through his gloves, through his palms, down to the meat of his hands, but he had tied it well because Astrid had taught him to tie it well, shouting at him across a whirlpool in chapter four of this disaster, and it held. He got an arm around her wrist as the water tore at them both. For a moment they were just two bodies on a rope above a drowning world, and the flood pulled, and the mountain groaned, and Ilya said her name into the steam because he didn't know what else to say.

"Hold on. *Hold on.*"

Her grip was failing. He could feel it — the fingers going slack, the exhaustion of a woman who had thrown a fail-safe wheel, outrun a surge, and offered her enemy salvation in the space of four minutes. So Ilya did the only thing left. He wrapped the rope twice around his forearm, braced his boots against a boulder older than any nation, and hauled.

She came up out of the water gasping, coughing turquoise spray, alive.

They lay together on the high shelf while the mountain finished dying below them — vent after vent sealing with deep percussive booms, the great geyser column sagging, whitening, sinking back into rock. The steam thinned. The turquoise dimmed to a pale, exhausted green, and then to ordinary water, and then the basin went quiet in a way it had not been quiet in ten thousand years.

Suri was alive. That was the first coherent fact. Ilya found her propped against the survey station with Juno pressing a fresh bandage to her side, her face gray but her eyes open, and when she saw Astrid crawl onto the shelf beside him she said, hoarse:

"The boats?"

"Gone," Ilya said.

"Thane?"

Astrid didn't answer. She was looking at the water.

* * *

**Astrid**

Grief is a strange salvage. You come up from it holding less than you dove with, and somehow heavier.

Astrid sat on the shelf as the light changed, as the steam thinned to wisps, as the river below the collapsed field ran clear for the first time in days — running cold again, running free, running over a grave that held a fortune and a man and, somewhere deeper, a promise she'd finally kept. Her hands were torn open from the wheel. Her ears rang with a silence that used to be a cathedral of steam. Somewhere downstream, the Vela syndicate's corridor of burned villages led to a treasure that no longer existed, and the men who had lit those fires would learn it soon, and she could not decide whether that was justice or just another kind of ash.

She thought about Marcus's face in the last second. Not fear. Recognition. He had finally seen the shape of the thing he'd been fighting his whole life, and it wasn't her, and it wasn't Vela. It was the simple, ancient fact that some things are not for sale, and the river had always known it, and he had died refusing to learn it in time.

"We should move," Juno said gently. "Water's still unstable. And Vela won't be done looking."

Astrid nodded. She looked at her crew — Ilya with his ruined hands, Suri gray-faced and stubbornly alive, Juno already breaking down the shelter — and felt the strangest thing settle over her, lighter than relief, warmer than victory.

Nothing. They had nothing. No fee, no proof, no debt forgiven, no wreck salvaged, no fortune. Just each other, and a battered boat, and a long way downstream.

"Help me carry her," Astrid said, sliding her arms under Suri. "We're going home."

The river, quiet now, agreed.
