## 10. Downstream

The river carried them the way it had never carried them before — gently, as if it understood what they'd lost and were too tired to fight about it.

Astrid sat at the stern with one hand on the tiller and the other resting on Suri's blanket, feeling the old woman's breath come slow and even beneath her palm. The *Mariana's Ghost* — Juno had repainted the name overnight, in dripping letters, over the scorched hull of what had been the *Vela's Ledger* — rode low in the water, listing to port, her engine coughing every fourth stroke like a smoker climbing stairs. Half her plating was new, scavenged from a gunboat wreck they'd passed on the second day. The rest was scar tissue.

They all were, now.

The gorge opened around them in stages: first the black basalt walls where the steam had scoured everything green, then the fern-hung terraces where the water ran clear again, cold enough to make your teeth ache when you drank it. Ilya stood at the bow for hours at a time, his bandaged hands tucked into his armpits, reading the current the way other men read scripture. He didn't say much. When he did, it was things like *"Fifty meters off the left bank, there's a shelf rock"* or *"The water's telling us something. It's happy."* Astrid had stopped questioning it. A disgraced hydrologist who could hear a river's mood was worth ten sonar rigs, and they had neither.

On the third morning, Juno found her checking the fuel gauge for the hundredth time.

"Still empty," Juno said.

"Still looking anyway."

"That's either hope or habit."

Astrid snorted. "Habit. Hope costs money."

Juno leaned against the rail beside her. She was twenty-six and had signed onto this voyage because Vela collectors had burned her brother's ferry license and Astrid had paid the bribe that got it back. She'd been promised a cut. Everyone had been promised a cut. The cut had died under a thousand tons of collapsing limestone along with Marcus Thane and the syndicate's entire claim to the richest geothermal deposit in the Coral Triangle.

"We're going to get back to Port Salang," Juno said, "and they're going to laugh at us."

"They're going to try."

"The debt—"

"Is uncollectible." Astrid turned the phrase over like a stone she kept finding in her pocket. "You can't collect on paper when the collateral is a vent field that doesn't exist anymore. Vela's surveyors will take core samples for a year and find warm mud. Their lawyers will argue. Meanwhile the debt sits in a ledger nobody wants to touch, because touching it means admitting their asset evaporated." She paused. "Also, we sank two of their gunboats and buried their guide. They may decide we're more trouble than we're owed."

"You believe that?"

"No," Astrid admitted. "But I've spent fifteen years believing worse things, and they came true, so my track record's compromised."

Juno laughed — a short, surprised sound, the first one since the narrows. Downriver, a kingfisher flashed blue off an overhanging branch, and for a moment nobody said anything, and the river said everything.

* * *

They passed the burned village on the fifth day.

It had been burned from the upstream side, so they met it gradually: first the smell — wet ash and charred palm, sweetish and wrong — then the canoe hulls pulled up on the bank, split open by machete or bullet, then the houses themselves, their bamboo skeletons standing black against the green like ribs. A dog watched them from the ruins of a fish-drying rack. It did not bark. There was nothing left in it that remembered how.

Suri made them stop.

She was still too weak to stand without help, but she stood, propped between Ilya and the rail, and looked at the ash line running down to the waterline, and said nothing for a long time. Then she said, "We should have turned back at the narrows."

"Yes," Astrid said.

"You knew what the corridor was for."

"I knew."

"And you took the contract anyway."

Astrid looked at the village. Somewhere in the treeline a bird was singing, bright and oblivious, the way birds are. "I told myself it was salvage. I told myself the debt left me no choice. Both of those things were true and neither of them was an excuse, and I've had five days of drifting to work out the difference."

Suri studied her with eyes that had seen forty years of rivers and had stopped being impressed by any of them. "And what did you work out?"

"That 'no choice' is a story people tell after the choosing. That I chose. And that the only thing I did right was at the end, when I finally chose against the money." She swallowed. "It doesn't balance the ledger. I know that. But it's the only currency I have left, so I'm going to spend it carefully."

Suri was quiet a while. The current nudged the hull against the bank; Ilya fended them off with a boat hook, gentle as a man waking a child.

"When we get to Salang," Suri said finally, "I'm going to tell everyone you're a fool."

Astrid felt something loosen in her chest. "That's fair."

"But I'll tell them you're *our* fool. There's a difference, and it matters." She let Ilya guide her back to her bunk, and at the hatch she paused. "Your mentor would have done the same thing. At the source. You know that."

"I know," Astrid said, and found that she could say it without the word *drowned* following it down.

* * *

Port Salang announced itself the way it always did: by smell. Diesel and durian and drying fish and the particular sourness of a town that had never once been law-abiding. They came in on the evening tide with no lights, because lights cost battery, and tied up at the derelict pier on the north side where the harbor fees were collected by whoever happened to be standing there, and tonight that happened to be a boy of maybe fourteen with a machete and a clipboard.

Astrid paid him three cigarettes and he wrote them down as a fishing boat.

Word traveled fast in Salang, faster than tide. By full dark there were people on the pier — not many, and not the right ones. A woman from the chandlery who owed Suri money and now pretended not to see her. Two men in Vela-gray jackets who stood at the far end of the pier drinking coconut beer and not approaching, which was its own kind of message. And old Mateo from the engine shop, who looked at the *Mariana's Ghost*, looked at the bandages on Ilya's hands, looked at Suri being carried down the gangplank on a stretcher cobbled from a hatch cover, and said only:

"I'll look at your engine tomorrow. No charge."

"There's no money, Mateo."

"I heard." He spat into the black water. "There's no money anywhere anymore. Might as well fix engines for people I don't hate."

The Vela men finished their beer and left. Astrid watched them go and understood: the syndicate had run the numbers. Two gunboats, a guide, a deposit, and fifteen years of patience, all written off against a crew of four with no assets and no future earnings. Chasing them would cost more than the debt was worth. The ledger entry wouldn't be forgiven. It would simply be forgotten — which, in Salang, amounted to the same mercy.

Juno appeared at her elbow with two bowls of noodle soup from the night market, bought on credit, and handed her one.

"So," Juno said. "This is it. This is what having nothing looks like."

Astrid blew on the soup. Steam rose off it and tangled with the harbor mist, and somewhere across the water a bar was playing music too loud, and Suri was asleep below decks with her heartbeat steady as a bilge pump, and Ilya was sketching the mouth of the river by lantern light, muttering about silt loads, and Mateo was already gone to find parts.

"This is what it looks like," Astrid said.

"It's terrible."

"It's the best thing I've ever owned."

* * *

She woke before dawn, the way she always did, and for a moment lay in her bunk listening to the sounds she'd spent fifteen years forgetting how to hear: the hull ticking against the pier, the winch cable creaking, Juno snoring like a landslide in the next compartment. Not the river's voice. The boat's voice. Her boat, such as it was.

She climbed out and walked to the end of the pier.

The mouth of the river spread wide and brown-green into the sea, and the sky above it was doing the thing it did here every morning — building itself out of charcoal and ember, layer by layer, while the fishing boats went out past the bar with their lamps still lit, small and stubborn as votive candles.

Her whole life, Astrid had measured distance in miles. Miles to the next charter. Miles of debt, converted at some brutal exchange rate into nautical terms. Miles upriver to the place that killed her mentor, miles back down with less than she'd started with. Even as a girl she'd stood on piers like this one and calculated: how far, how fast, how much. Distance was just debt wearing a different uniform.

She stood at the end of the pier and did not calculate anything.

The sun came up over the bar in a long red smear, and the water caught fire from the horizon inward, and the fishing boats turned briefly to silhouette and then to gold, and Astrid Reyes — broke, unpaid, unwitnessed, the only person alive who knew what had actually happened at the source of the river — watched all of it without once wondering how many miles it was to anything.

Footsteps behind her. Ilya, hands still bandaged, carrying two cups of coffee that was mostly chicory.

"You're up early," he said.

"I live here now," she said, and heard it land true. "I mean — I'm not going anywhere."

Ilya handed her a cup and followed her gaze out to the bar, where the first boat of the morning was punching through the swell, spray flying, headed for open water.

"Beautiful," he said. "Terrible currents, though. Rips all across the mouth. You'd want someone who knows them."

Astrid smiled into her coffee. "I might know a guy."

Behind them, the *Mariana's Ghost* rocked gently at her moorings, holding everything they had left — which was, for the first time in fifteen years, enough.
