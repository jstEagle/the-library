## 1. Salt and Ledger

The wreck breathed when the tide came in.

Tamsin Sterling had learned that on her first dive, three years ago, before she knew the *Corvane* had a name or a number or a reason to be lying broken on the shelf off Gullwatch Point with no record of ever having existed. The hull flexed against the current like a lung, and the water pushed through the torn plates in slow exhalations, carrying silt and rust and the faint mineral sweetness of things dissolving. She felt it through her gloves now, hanging at fifteen meters in the black, waiting for the patrol boat's running lights to swing past the point.

They did. White to red, moving seaward, half a kilometer out. She counted to sixty anyway.

Then she dropped.

The descent was muscle memory by now—fifth dive on this wreck, each one paid for in borrowed time she didn't have. Her torch cut a cone through water the color of steeped tea, and the *Corvane*'s superstructure rose to meet it: a coastal freighter, two hundred feet of her, settled on her port side with her starboard rail pointing at the surface like a hand reaching up and giving up halfway. No registry. No casualty report. No name on any harbor board from here to the capital. Tamsin had checked. She'd spent four months checking, back when checking still felt like it might lead somewhere.

Now she just worked.

She swam the familiar line along the starboard rail, past the collapsed lifeboat davits, past the cargo hatch she'd already cleared twice, to the gap in the deck plating where a stairwell had folded down into the lower hold. The wreck's interior was a cathedral of bad angles. She moved through it the way you move through a house you grew up in—shoulder turned, fins tucked, one hand trailing the guide line she'd rigged herself. Her breathing stayed long and even. That was the whole discipline of salvage, her father used to say. Not strength. Not nerve. Just breathing slower than the situation deserves.

*Slower than the situation deserves.* She could hear his voice saying it, hear the river-water rasp of it, and she let the thought pass through her and out the exhaust valve with the rest of her air.

The strongbox was where she'd left it, wedged under a fallen cable drum in the aft hold, exactly as stubborn as she remembered. Forty centimeters of riveted steel, salt-welded shut, its lock eaten to a brown bloom of oxide. It had taken her two dives to find it—following the way the silt had scoured in patterns that didn't match the current, following the hollow space behind the purser's bulkhead where something heavy had been dragged and hidden rather than stored. People hid things on ships for two reasons, her father said. Taxes, or trouble. On an unregistered wreck, she'd bet on trouble every time.

She clipped the lift bag to the box's handle and fed air into it in short pulses, watching the fabric swell, watching the drum shift, watching for the groan of stressed metal that would tell her the pile above was thinking about moving. Nothing groaned. The bag took the weight, the box slid free with a grinding shrug of sediment, and the cloud that bloomed around her turned her torch beam into a wall of milk.

That was when she heard the engine.

Not the patrol boat—that one had gone seaward. This was different: a shallow-draft work engine, the kind the harbor authority ran, idling somewhere above and ahead, close enough that the sound came through the hull before it came through the water. Tamsin went still inside the cloud, one hand on the cable drum, and killed her torch.

Darkness underwater isn't empty. It has texture. She hung in it and listened the way her father taught her—not with her ears, which were useless down here, but with her chest, feeling the pressure stutter of prop wash when the engine above changed pitch. It circled once. It stopped. Somewhere far overhead, a searchlight clicked on; she saw it as a pale smear diffusing down through forty feet of dirty water, sweeping, patient.

They were looking for bubbles.

Tamsin looked at her gauges. Twelve minutes of bottom time burned. Ninety bar. Enough to make the surface if she skipped the safety stop, and not enough to care about what skipping it might cost her someday. The lift bag with the strongbox hung beside her, tugging gently upward, a liability shaped like a payday.

She thought about the ledger in her cabin aboard the *Magpie*. The neat columns in Odile's handwriting. The total at the bottom, circled twice. Fourteen thousand, six hundred and change, owed to a man named Corben Hale who wore linen suits and never raised his voice because he'd learned, sometime early, that he didn't need to. Interest compounded weekly. The number had been eleven thousand when she started diving the *Corvane*, and every dive bought her another month and cost her another month, and the arithmetic between those two facts was the closest thing she had to a religion.

Hale hadn't threatened her. That was the elegant part. He'd simply explained, over tea in his office above the chandlery, that the *Magpie* was collateral, and that collateral was a word with a very flexible meaning, and that he admired her father, actually—he said this warmly, as though offering a gift—and that men who died owing money left their debts to their children like heirlooms. He'd smiled when he said it. She'd signed the renewal anyway. That was the part she couldn't forgive herself for: she'd signed.

The searchlight swept again, closer this time. The smear of it brightened, dimmed, brightened.

Tamsin made her decision the way she made all of them: fast, alone, and without telling anyone she'd agonized. She cracked the valve on the lift bag and let it rise—slowly, ten meters off to the east of the wreck, away from the light, so the box would surface where nothing was looking. Then she swam west along the seabed, low over the shell hash, fins barely kicking, and found the old oyster bed she'd noted on her first survey. She buried herself in it. Literally: scooped the loose shell and silt over her legs, her tank, the shine of her mask, until she was a shapeless hump on a shapeless bottom, breathing through her regulator in sips so small her vision sparked at the edges.

Above her, the searchlight found the lift bag.

She heard the engine surge. Heard voices, thin and distorted, carried down through the water column in fragments—a man's voice, bored and official, and then a splash, a grapnel maybe, or a boathook snagging the line. The bag would come up empty-handed. They'd find a corroded strongbox bobbing in open water with no diver attached, and they'd spend an hour deciding whether it was evidence or garbage, and either way they wouldn't be looking at an oyster bed two hundred meters west.

Her chest burned. She held.

The engine moved off toward the bag's position, and Tamsin gave it ninety seconds, then a hundred and twenty, then shed the shells off herself and swam—not up, but laterally, along the contour of the shelf, following her compass bearing toward the deep channel where the *Magpie* lay anchored beyond the restricted zone. Only when the wreck's mass was behind her did she angle upward, rising through the cold layers, watching the black soften to grey, then to the hammered-silver underside of the night sea.

She broke the surface a quarter mile from the lights, on the dark side of the point, and treaded water while she pulled her mask down and looked back.

The patrol boat sat over the lift site, searchlight pinned to the water, a second smaller boat alongside it now. Men were hauling the strongbox aboard. Even at this distance she could read the posture of it—the unhurried way they handled it, the way nobody seemed excited. Salvage rights on a restricted wreck belonged to the harbor authority by default, which meant the box was theirs now, which meant five dives of her life were theirs now, and she floated there in the cold and let that fact settle over her like the spray settling on her shoulders.

It should have gutted her. What surprised her, floating in the dark with her lungs aching, was how little it did. There was a numbness where the loss should be, a smooth scar-tissue place, and she recognized it the way you recognize your own handwriting. She'd been diving past that numbness for three years. Everything since the funeral had been conducted on the far side of it.

She swam for the *Magpie*.

* * *

Odile was awake, which meant she'd heard the patrol boat too, which meant she'd been sitting in the galley doing arithmetic instead of sleeping, which was how Odile prayed. She didn't say anything when Tamsin came over the transom dripping and empty-handed. She just poured coffee from the dented pot and set it on the chart table next to the ledger, open to the page with the circled number, and waited with the particular patience of a woman who had been waiting for other people her whole life.

"Harbor authority," Tamsin said. "Two boats."

"Strongbox?"

"Gone." She stripped her hood off. Salt water ran down her neck and pooled in her collar. "Along with everything in it."

Odile nodded slowly, the way she nodded at weather reports. "So we're at zero."

"We're at negative fourteen-six, minus tonight's fuel and tank fills." Tamsin wrapped both hands around the mug. It was too hot to hold and she held it anyway. Pain was useful sometimes. It kept other things out. "I can get another contract. Wreck surveys, insurance work. Slow money."

"Slow money doesn't outrun compound interest. You know that better than I do." Odile tapped the ledger once, not unkindly. "I've run it every way it runs, Tam. There's no version of slow that gets you clear of Hale."

"I know how it runs."

"Then say the other thing."

Tamsin drank the coffee and burned her tongue and was grateful for it. Below decks, Bram was asleep in the forward berth, or pretending to be; she could hear him not-snoring, which was its own kind of commentary. Pip wasn't aboard yet—wouldn't be till morning, coming down from the net sheds with whatever the kid had managed to beg, borrow, or liberate. A crew of strays. A crew of people the world had already written off, captained by a woman the harbor board had formally censured and informally laughed about, holding a debt that grew like kelp.

"The other thing is I'm done diving the *Corvane*," Tamsin said. "Five dives. Five. And all I've got to show for it is a list of what's missing."

"Which is?"

"A ship's bell with the name ground off it. A crew manifest that isn't a manifest—it's a single typed sheet, unsigned, listing twenty-two souls and no surnames. And a purser's hold that was cleaned out before she ever went down." She stared into the mug. "Somebody scrubbed that ship, Odile. Before she sank. Somebody wanted her anonymous and empty, and then somebody sank her anyway, and then somebody made her disappear from the record entirely. Three somebodies. Or one somebody with a lot of ink."

Odile was quiet for a moment. Outside, the patrol lights crawled back toward the harbor mouth, dragging their reflection across the water like a wound being closed.

"You could stop asking," Odile said quietly. "People do. People find a way to not know things. It's practically a profession."

"My father didn't stop asking."

"No," Odile said. "He didn't."

And there it was—the thing under the night, the reef under the channel. Tamsin set the mug down before her hands could do anything embarrassing.

* * *

The ritual came last, the way it always did. She went aft to her cabin, a coffin-sized space smelling of diesel and neoprene, and took the compass out of the chart drawer where it lived wrapped in a wool sock that had once belonged to someone else.

It was a brass ship's compass, old pattern, the glass crazed with a spiderweb of fine cracks and the case stained the color of weak tea where river water had gotten in and never fully left. The card still swung, lazy and true. Her father had carried it thirty years, on tugs and tramp steamers and finally on the *Magpie*, and he'd pressed it into her hands the last morning she ever saw him, closing her fingers over it with both of his, saying nothing, which from him was a paragraph.

He'd gone out that afternoon on a job he wouldn't describe, upriver, with a crew that came back without him and swore silence so complete that two of them left the coast entirely and the third one drank himself to death inside a year. The navy had sent a letter. Not a report—a letter, two paragraphs, expressing regret and citing regulations that prevented further disclosure. Her mother had framed it, which told Tamsin more about grief than any book ever had.

Every night, she opened the case and let the card settle, and she read the bearing it chose, and she wrote it down in the back of her logbook. North-northwest, tonight. It never pointed anywhere. It pointed wherever the boat happened to be facing, which was nowhere, which was everywhere, and she knew it was superstition, knew it was grief wearing a instrument's face, and she did it anyway. It was the only conversation they still had. *Where are we pointed, Dad?* *North-northwest.* *Into what?* *Into the dark, sweetheart. Same as always. Breathe slower than the situation deserves.*

She wrote the bearing. She wrapped the compass back in the sock. She lay down in her bunk and listened to the tide turn and did not sleep, and somewhere in the hour before dawn she heard, distantly, the sound of a car door slamming on the quay—too clean a sound for the harbor at that hour, too deliberate—and footsteps on the planks, unhurried, expensive shoes on wet wood, stopping at the head of the *Magpie*'s gangway.

A knock. Three knocks, evenly spaced, confident.

Through the hull, muffled, a voice she hadn't heard in six years and would have known in the dark, in the water, in her sleep:

"Sterling. I know you're awake. We need to talk about Meridian Hollow."

Tamsin sat up in the dark, and the numbness—the smooth scar-tissue place she'd been diving past for three years—cracked straight down the middle.
