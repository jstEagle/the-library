## 2. The Rival's Offer

The Salt Line Tavern smelled like tar, fried fish, and the particular desperation of men who had bet their boats on weather that never came. Tamsin Sterling sat in the back corner with her back to the wall—old habit, her father's habit—counting the take from the night's dive under the table while she nursed a beer she had no intention of finishing.

Fourteen hundred and sixty. Minus the fuel, minus the harbor fee, minus the cut she owed Odile for rebuilding the compressor that should have lasted another season. It came to something under four hundred, and the envelope on her bunk back at the *Maggie's Fault* wanted two thousand by the end of the month.

She stacked the bills, folded them into an oilskin pouch, and told herself the math was a problem for tomorrow. It was the same thing she'd told herself every night for three years. The math never listened.

The tavern door opened, and the room's noise dipped—not much, just a half-beat, the way water dips around a hull before the wake arrives. Tamsin didn't look up. She knew that dip. She'd grown up around men whose money entered rooms ahead of them.

Cassian Voss crossed the floor like he owned the boards under his boots, and in a way he had, once. Pressed charcoal coat, no hat despite the drizzle, that same infuriating stillness in his face that had made him unbearable at nineteen and made him worse now. He stopped at her table. He didn't ask.

"Stirling." He pronounced it the way he always had—carefully, like a word he'd learned to say correctly on purpose.

"Voss." She kept counting. "This table's occupied."

"By one person and an empty chair." He sat anyway. Up close he looked older than thirty-four ought to look, which gave her a small, mean satisfaction. "You're diving restricted wrecks at night now."

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"The *Corvane*, mooring buoy nine, inside the naval exclusion line. Patrol boat came through at 0300. You went out the channel side with your running lights cold." He said it flat, no accusation in it, and that was worse. "I was on the water. I watched your exhaust plume."

Tamsin looked up then. The tavern was loud again around them, safe in its own noise, but she lowered her voice anyway. "You were on the water at three in the morning inside a navy exclusion zone. That's an interesting hobby, Cassian."

"I have interesting hobbies." He signaled the barkeep with two fingers, and the man brought over a bottle of the good rye—the bottle that lived behind the lock, the one dockworkers pointed at and never ordered. Voss poured two fingers into her untouched beer glass, which was such a Voss move she almost laughed. He'd never once in his life drunk from a dirty glass, but he'd happily ruin hers.

"I'm not drinking with you."

"You're not drinking anything. That glass has been full since I walked in." He turned it slowly on the table, a small orbit. "You look tired, Tamsin."

"I look employed."

"You look like a woman doing salvage work with a dive light and a prayer, on a boat held together with epoxy and stubbornness, to pay a man named Grigor Halden two thousand a month on a debt you'll die before you clear." He said it without cruelty, which was the most frightening part. He'd always had that talent—knowing exactly where the knife went and sliding it in like he was taking your pulse. "I did some reading."

"You did some asking." She closed the oilskin pouch. "There's a difference. One of them is legal."

"Most things worth knowing aren't." He leaned back, and for a moment—just a moment—the polish dropped and she saw the boy who'd sat across from her in her father's galley at sixteen, all elbows and ambition, asking questions about currents her father answered patiently because he believed even rich kids deserved to learn. "I have a job."

"I have a job."

"You have a debt. It's not the same thing." He slid an envelope across the table, heavy cream paper, wax-sealed, absurd in a tavern where the coasters were beer flats. "Meridian Hollow. The sunken district, lower town, below the waterline since the '09 flood. There's an archive in the old records annex. Sealed, intact, and I want it brought up."

Tamsin didn't touch the envelope. She made herself not touch the envelope, and it took everything she had, because her hands wanted to know what was in it the way a tongue wants a broken tooth.

"Meridian Hollow is a restricted site. Navy cordon, ten kilometers of it. Divers get fined, boats get seized."

"Which is why the pay is forty thousand. Half up front."

The number landed in her chest like a dropped anchor. Forty thousand. The debt gone. The *Maggie's Fault* re-engined. A year of not doing arithmetic in taverns. She hated that her face did anything at all, and she hated more that Voss was watching it do it.

"No," she said.

"You haven't heard the terms."

"I heard the number. That's the trap. The number's how you know the job's rotten." She pushed the envelope back with one finger. "Nobody pays forty thousand for paperwork, Cassian. Whatever's in that annex, somebody wants it badly enough that the last people who went after it—"

She stopped. The sentence had a cliff at the end of it and she'd been walking toward the edge without noticing.

Voss watched her stop. He had the decency, this once, to let the silence sit.

"The last people who went after it," she said again, quieter, "never came back."

"Your father came back." Voss's voice was careful now, stripped of its polish. "That's the whole point. He came back, and then he went out again, and the second time—"

"Careful."

"—nobody knows what happened, because his own crew wouldn't talk, and the navy scrubbed every record of the job, and a nineteen-year-old girl spent three years being told her father was a smuggler who drowned doing what smugglers do." He turned the beer glass another slow degree. "I'm the only person in this room who knows that's not true."

"You don't know what's true. You know what you've heard."

"I know what I've paid for." He said it simply. "And what I've paid for says the Hollow archive is the reason your father took that last contract. Same annex. Same job. He turned it down once, and then something changed his mind, and he went, and the *Larkspur* came home with a hole in her crew and a story nobody would tell."

Tamsin stood up. The chair scraped, and a couple of dockhands at the near table glanced over, then looked away with the practiced blindness of people who'd learned that looking was expensive.

"Whatever you're selling, Cassian, I'm not buying."

"Your father's name is in that annex."

She stopped with her hand on the back of the chair. The tavern noise seemed to recede, the way sound does underwater.

"You want to talk about my father," she said, "you can do it at his grave. Oh, wait. There isn't one. There's a marker because there's no body, because the river kept him, and you want me to dive back into the exact water that ate him because you've got a wax-sealed envelope and a number with a lot of zeros in it."

"I want to give you the thing you've wanted for eleven years." Voss stood too. He was taller than her, had always been, and she hated that she had to look up. "Your father went down there for that archive. He died for it, or near enough. You can spend the rest of your life diving strangers' wrecks for strangers' money, or you can go get the one thing that tells you why."

"You don't know why he went."

"No," Voss admitted. "But I know who hired him. And I know that the same family that hired him is paying me now. And I know that when I asked what happened to the last crew, the man on the other end of the phone went quiet for eleven seconds, and then said, 'The archive is sealed for a reason, Mr. Voss,' in a voice like he was reading my eulogy." He picked up the envelope and held it out. "I'm not asking you to trust me. God knows you shouldn't. I'm asking you to take the job because you're the only diver alive with a reason to do it right."

Tamsin looked at the envelope. Cream paper, wax seal, forty thousand dollars, and underneath all of it, like a body under ice, the shape of her father's last two weeks—the contract he wouldn't show her, the argument with his crew that she'd heard through the *Larkspur*'s hull, the way he'd held her too long at the dock and said *keep the compass dry, Tams, I'll want it back*.

"Find another diver," she said.

"There isn't another diver."

"Then drown." She took her oilskin pouch and walked out.

* * *

Cassian Voss watched her go, and the strange thing—the thing he would think about later, in the dark, in the expensive quiet of his cabin aboard the *Amaranth*—was that he'd known she'd say no. He'd run the conversation in his head a dozen times on the run downriver, and in every version she said no, and in every version he'd sat there anyway, because some part of him had wanted to watch her do it.

She paused at the door. He saw her shoulders set, saw her almost turn around, saw her decide not to. Then the door swung shut behind her and the drizzle took her, and the tavern noise closed over the space she'd occupied like water over a dropped coin.

"She'll come around," said the man in the corner booth, who had been nursing the same stout for an hour and was very good at not being noticed. Deke. Voss's man, though Voss had never liked the way that word fit him. "They always come around when the money gets hungry enough."

"She's not hungry for money." Voss sat back down and looked at the two glasses on the table—hers untouched but ruined now, his own never poured. He signaled the barkeep to take them away. "She's hungry for the truth. It's worse. Money you can pay off."

"You said the old man's name would hook her."

"I said it would cut her. Hooking and cutting look the same from a distance." Voss turned his glass over, caught himself, set it right. Eleven years since he'd stood on the *Larkspur*'s deck with Tamsin Sterling, both of them sixteen, both of them certain the other was insufferable, both of them right. He had loved her father's boat the way you love a thing that doesn't care about your family's name. He had loved—no. He set that down too. "Did you confirm the annex survey?"

"Contractor's sonar run last week. Structure's intact. Door's sealed, pressure's held." Deke leaned forward. "There's something else. The contractor's crew found dive gear on the site. Old. Pre-flood vintage, but not flood-era placement—somebody's been down there in the last few years. Recent regs, recent brass."

Voss went still. "Whose?"

"Serial numbers filed. But the rig configuration—" Deke hesitated, and Deke never hesitated. "It matched the description in the old incident file. The *Larkspur* crew's configuration. Three-tank setup, left-side primary, the old Meridian salvage pattern."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning either somebody's copying a dead man's rig, or the dead man's crew has been back to the annex since the navy said the site was clean." Deke sat back. "You want to tell her that part?"

Voss looked out the salt-fogged window. Down at the docks, the *Maggie's Fault* sat low on her moorings, a working boat with a working woman aboard, running lights dark, riding at anchor in the gray drizzle like something that had learned to survive by not being seen.

"No," he said. "Not yet."

Because here was the thing Voss could not say out loud, not to Deke, not to the family lawyers, not to the mirror: the contract he carried wasn't his. It came down to him from his grandfather's firm, from the quiet accounts and quieter men who had built the Voss name on river money, and the instructions were specific in a way that made his skin crawl. *Recover the archive. Deliver it sealed. Contents are not your concern.*

His grandfather had signed things in Meridian Hollow. Voss had found the signatures himself, three months ago, in a box of papers he'd been told didn't exist. He had told himself it was old business, dead business, business that belonged to men who were now comfortably beyond consequence. He had told himself that hiring the best diver on the river was simply good practice.

He had not told himself why the best diver on the river was the daughter of the last man who'd tried.

Voss left money on the table—too much, the barkeep would remember that—and stepped out into the drizzle. Across the water, the *Maggie's Fault* rocked gently at her mooring. A single light burned in her wheelhouse, then went out.

She'd come around. They always did. And when she did, he'd be waiting with the contract and the truth he was permitted to tell, and he would not think about the truth he wasn't.

Downriver, past the cordon buoys, past the drowned streets where the river held its secrets at forty feet, something in the old annex waited in the dark with her father's name on it.

Voss turned up his collar and walked the other way.

* * *

Tamsin didn't go straight back to the boat. She walked the length of the seawall with the drizzle in her face and the oilskin pouch heavy against her ribs, and she let herself do the thing she never let herself do in front of anyone: she thought about the dock. Eleven years ago. Her father's hand on her head, rough palm, salt smell, the compass pressed into her hands.

*Keep it dry, Tams. I'll want it back.*

The *Larkspur* had gone upriver with six men and come back with five stories that didn't match and one silence that never broke. She'd been nineteen. She'd stood on this same seawall for three days waiting for a boat that was already at the bottom of something, and when the navy finally sent an officer to say *tragic accident, illegal dive, no further comment*, the whole crew signed a paper swearing silence and scattered like spilled mercury.

She'd spent eleven years telling herself her father died doing something stupid for money. It was a load-bearing lie. It held up her whole life. And Cassian Voss, with his wax seal and his forty thousand, had just put his shoulder against it.

Tamsin stopped at the end of the seawall. Below her, the harbor water moved in the dark, patient, keeping everything it had ever taken.

She took out the compass. The casing was cold and water-stained, the needle swinging lazy and true, and she held it the way she always did—like a question she hadn't earned the answer to.

Forty thousand dollars. Her father's last job. The same annex.

"No," she said aloud, to the water, to the compass, to the man who wasn't there to hear it.

The needle didn't waver. It just kept pointing north, the way it always had, the way her father had pointed her—straight at the thing she was afraid of.

Back at the boat, she'd find the envelope waiting on her wheelhouse steps, wax unbroken, weighted down with a river stone.

He'd known she'd take it. He'd just known she'd take it her way.

Tamsin stood over it a long time in the rain, and then, hating herself with every step, she picked it up.
