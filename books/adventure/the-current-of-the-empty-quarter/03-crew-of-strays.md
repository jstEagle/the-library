## 3. Crew of Strays

I spent two days telling myself I wasn't going to do it.

I told myself that while I pulled the *Magpie*'s bilge pump apart on the dock at Lowmarsh and cleaned three years of silt out of the impeller housing. I told myself that while I re-caulked the seams and argued with a chandler over the price of dive line. I told myself it most convincingly of all while I sat in my cabin at night with the ledger of my own debts spread across the chart table—Voss's contract money sitting in an escrow account like bait in a trap, and my father's compass beside it, needle swinging slow and patient toward nothing.

The math was simple. The math was always simple. A solo diver can work a wreck. A solo diver cannot work a drowned city.

So on the third morning I made coffee bad enough to be honest about what this was going to be, and I started making calls.

* * *

Odile Ferro lived above her machine shop in a part of Lowmarsh where the tide had taken the street signs years ago and nobody had asked for them back. I found her under a decommissioned water-jet drive the size of a rowboat, her boots sticking out from beneath it, a radio playing some station that was mostly static and occasionally, defiantly, music.

"I need an engineer," I said.

"Buy a manual." She didn't come out.

"It's a salvage job. Deep water. Old structures."

One boot withdrew, then the other, and Odile rolled out on her creeper into the light. She was fifty-something and built like something welded rather than born, gray hair cropped to stubble, forearms crosshatched with burn scars from a life spent too close to things that cut. She looked at me the way she looked at a cracked weld: assessing whether it was worth fixing or better scrapped.

"Tamsin Sterling," she said. "You're the one whose crew died."

"My father's crew. And one man didn't die. He just stopped talking."

"Mm." She sat up, wiped her hands on a rag that was dirtier than her hands. "And you want me on a boat."

"I want you on my boat."

"That's different." She stood, joints popping like small-arms fire, and walked to the window. Below us, the harbor lay flat and gray, working boats going out with their running lights still on against the fog. "You know why I left the yards, Sterling?"

"I heard a story."

"You heard a story because the official version is boring. The real version is that I signed off on a pressure hull I shouldn't have, because my supervisor told me the numbers were fine, and eleven days later there were men's names on a memorial wall and my signature at the bottom of the inspection report." She turned around. "I didn't build it wrong. But I said it was right. There's a difference, and it doesn't matter, because both of them end the same way."

"So you've been fixing outboard motors for tourists ever since."

"Tourists don't drown."

"Neither will you. Not on my boat." I put Voss's contract on her bench, face down—I wasn't ready to show anyone the name at the bottom yet—and told her the parts that were true: deep dive, restricted water, good pay, bad odds. "I'll be straight with you because I know you check everything twice. This job is illegal-adjacent. Maybe past adjacent. If we get caught, I'm the one who goes down for it. You were never hired. You were never aboard."

Odile picked up the contract, read it without appearing to read it—that trick she had, eyes moving while her face stayed still—and set it down again.

"Escrow through Marlow & Finch," she said. "They pay out. Even when the client would rather they didn't." She looked at me for a long moment. "Who's the client?"

"You'll meet him."

"That's a no."

"He knew my father."

Odile went quiet. Outside, the static-and-music radio played on, indifferent. She picked up a torque wrench, put it down again. When she finally spoke, her voice had changed—not softer exactly, but aimed differently.

"Then it's not a job, is it. It's a reckoning wearing a job's clothes." She sighed through her nose. "I'll want the hull specs by tonight. And if I find one thing on that boat I don't trust, we're done, and I keep the advance."

"There's no advance."

"Then I definitely keep the advance." She stuck out her hand. Her grip felt like being arrested by someone who liked you. "One condition. Nobody touches my engine room without asking. Including you. Especially you."

"Deal."

"And Sterling." She held my hand a beat longer. "Whatever your father found down there—you make sure it was worth it. Not for him. For the people who have to live with what he left behind."

I didn't have an answer for that yet. I just nodded, and she let me go.

* * *

Bram was harder.

Bram Okonkwo didn't live anywhere, which was the point of Bram. I tracked him down through a fisherman who owed me, then through a woman who ran a pawnshop and owed me less, and finally to a houseboat moored in the reed shallows south of town—a floating shack of scavenged panels and solar film, listing gently, home to a man who had once been the best passive sonar operator on the eastern seaboard and was now, officially, no one.

He opened the door before I knocked. He always did. That was the navy in him, or maybe just the paranoia the navy had installed and then disowned.

"Sterling." He looked past me at the empty reeds, cataloguing threats. "You're loud. Your boat's louder. I heard you coming up the channel twenty minutes ago."

"Good. Then you had time to decide to say yes."

"To what?" He stepped aside anyway, which was Bram's way of saying yes to the conversation even when he hadn't agreed to the question. Inside, the houseboat was a nest of screens, most of them dark, one of them showing weather radar with the loving attention other men gave to sports. Tea steamed on a hotplate. Everything was clean. Everything was arranged so it could be abandoned in ninety seconds.

I sat where he indicated—the chair facing the door, because he'd want his back covered—and laid it out. Meridian Hollow. The drowned district. A sealed archive below the waterline, in structures that predated the flood charts. A client with money and motives I didn't trust.

Bram listened the way he did everything: completely, silently, his stillness so total it became its own kind of noise.

"Navy charts show the Hollow as a hazard zone," he said at last. "Restricted. No survey data published since the flood. That's not normal, Sterling. Even disaster zones get surveyed eventually. Somebody's keeping that water dark."

"I know."

"And you're going in anyway."

"I'm going in anyway."

He got up and poured tea, two cups, which was more answer than most people got from him. He handed me mine and stood at his window, looking out at the reeds.

"You know what they did to me," he said. Not a question. Everyone in that world knew, or thought they did. "Eighteen years listening to the water. I could tell you what kind of boat was passing by the sound of its cooling pumps alone. Then one night I hear something I'm not supposed to hear—an exercise that isn't an exercise, a hull signature that isn't on any registry—and I log it. I do my job. And six months later my clearance is gone, my pension is 'under review,' and a man in a gray suit explains to me very kindly that my hearing must be deteriorating, and wouldn't early retirement be restful?" He smiled, and it was the loneliest thing I'd seen in weeks. "My hearing is perfect. That's the whole problem."

"The archive might have his name in it," I said. "Or whatever he heard. Whatever they buried."

Bram turned from the window. For a moment the stillness cracked and I saw underneath it: a man who had been erased so smoothly he sometimes doubted himself, and hated that he doubted, and doubted that he hated.

"If I come aboard," he said slowly, "I run sonar my way. Passive first, always. And if I say we leave, we leave. No debate. No vote. You can hate me later."

"No debate," I agreed.

"And I want it in writing that anything we recover gets copied before the client sees it. Anything. If it matters enough to sink a city over, it matters enough to have a backup."

I hadn't told him it was a city that got sunk. I watched him realize what he'd just said, and watched him decide not to take it back.

"Copies first," I said. "Always."

We shook on it. His hand was steady. Mine almost was.

* * *

Pip I didn't recruit. Pip recruited me, the way a burr recruits a sock.

I came back to the *Magpie* near dusk to find him already aboard, sitting on my deck coil, eating crackers from my stores like he'd been issued them. Sixteen, maybe seventeen—all elbows and hollow cheeks, hair cut short in a way that suggested either a reform school or an attempt to be unmemorable. Canvas jacket two sizes too big. Eyes that did a full inventory of me before I'd finished stepping off the gangway.

"You're moored badly," he said. "Starboard line's chafing on the cleat. Also your fenders are set for a higher tide than tonight's going to give you, and that pump impeller you rebuilt—" he pointed at the parts drying on my deck box, "—you seated the gasket dry. It'll hold for a week and then it won't."

I looked at him for a long time. He looked back, unbothered, chewing.

"Get off my boat."

"Happy to. After you fix the gasket." He hopped down, produced a tube of sealant from somewhere inside the enormous jacket, and held it out. "Or use mine. It's better."

"Who are you?"

"Pip."

"That's not a name, that's a sound effect."

"Best name I've got right now." Something flickered behind the cockiness—there and gone, like a fish turning under the surface. "Look. I heard you at the chandlery. Asking about dive tenders and line handlers. You need a deckhand. I need—" he shrugged, elaborate and fake, "—experience."

"You need a meal and a bath."

"Those too. Bundle deal."

"Pip." I took the sealant, mostly because he was right about the gasket and I hated that. "I'm not running a charity. And I'm not running whatever it is you're running from. This job goes places that are dangerous in ways that don't advertise themselves. Kids stay ashore."

"I'm not a kid."

"Everyone under twenty-five is a kid. It's a rule of the sea."

"Whose rule?"

"Mine. I just made it." I knelt at the pump housing and started reseating the gasket properly, wet this time, and he crouched opposite me and watched with an attention that was almost painful—like a boy memorizing how a thing was done because nobody had ever bothered to teach him and he refused to die ignorant.

"Here's what I can do," he said, quieter now. "I can handle a tender in current. I can splice, I can read water, I can keep watch eight hours without getting bored, because I don't get bored, I notice things. Ask your fisherman friend. I spotted the customs skiff Tuesday before his lookout did, and that's why he owes you a favor instead of a fine." He paused. "And I work for food and a bunk until you can pay, and I don't ask questions, because questions are how you end up noticed."

"Noticed by who?"

The shrug again, but thinner this time. "Everyone. Anyone. Take your pick."

I tightened the housing bolts and sat back on my heels. The tide was dropping; the *Magpie* settled against her lines with a groan. Somewhere across the harbor a ship's horn sounded, long and low, and neither of us moved until it faded.

"One trip," I said finally. "Trial basis. You step out of line, you steal so much as a wrench, you lie to me about anything that matters, and you're off at the next dock, no discussion."

"Yes."

"I'm serious."

"I know you're serious. That's why I said yes fast, before you remembered I'm a kid." He grinned, and for a second the grin was young and real and unguarded, and it did something to my chest I decided not to examine. "When do we eat? Because I wasn't kidding about the meal."

* * *

We ate that night in the *Magpie*'s cramped cabin, all four of us, because Odile arrived with the hull inspection report in one hand and a pot of stew in the other and announced that no crew of hers sailed on an empty stomach. Bram brought tea. Pip brought appetite enough for two. The coffee I'd made was terrible, which felt appropriate—it tasted like the job ahead, bitter and thin and likely to keep everyone awake.

Odile spread her report on the chart table. "Hull's sound. Through-hulls replaced, seacocks serviceable. Your dive compressor, though—" she leveled a look at me, "—is a war crime. I'll rebuild it tomorrow. We don't go anywhere until it's done."

"We're on a schedule."

"We're on my schedule now. That was the deal." She ladled stew into bowls with the authority of someone distributing rations. "Also, your ballast trim is wrong for a dive platform. We'll need to re-rig. Two days."

"Two days," I said, and didn't argue, because she was right, and because watching her argue with herself about whether to be here had already cost enough.

Bram sat with his tea untouched, studying the chart of the river above Lowmarsh that I'd pinned to the bulkhead. His finger stopped well short of Meridian Hollow, hovering over the blank stretch where the charts went vague and the annotations thinned to nothing.

"Water up there runs cold and green," he said, half to himself. "Deep channel, old industry on the banks. Good acoustic conditions." He glanced at me. "Meaning sound carries far. Meaning if anything big moves down there, I'll hear it long before we see it. Assuming anyone lets me turn my gear on."

"Turn it on whenever you want."

"I'll want it on at night." He said it mildly, but I understood: he slept badly, and listening was how he slept. A man who'd been betrayed by silence needed to fill it.

Pip ate like the food might be revoked mid-meal, and between mouthfuls he asked questions—small ones, practical ones, the questions of someone building a mental model of the boat and the crew and every exit from both. Where did Odile learn jet drives. Did Bram really hear a submarine once. Was the head broken or just temperamental. (Both.) He filed every answer away, and I realized, watching him, that I'd acquired not a deckhand but a witness—someone young enough to remember all of this clearly, whatever *this* turned out to be.

At some point the talk died down and the cabin got quiet, the four of us sitting in lamplight with the river slapping the hull, strangers who had agreed, for their own damaged reasons, to tie their fates to mine.

Odile broke the silence first. "So. Are we allowed to know what we're actually doing, or do we find out when the shooting starts?"

"No shooting," I said. "Probably."

"'Probably' is doing a lot of work in that sentence," Bram observed.

I looked at them—one at a time, because they deserved that much. An engineer carrying a dead crew's worth of guilt. A listener the navy had tried to make deaf. A kid the world had tried to make invisible. And me, with my debts and my compass and a name I couldn't say yet.

"Three days ago a man offered me a contract," I said. "A salvage job in Meridian Hollow. Recovering an archive from the flooded district. The pay is real and the escrow is clean." I turned my father's compass over in my hands, needle trembling. "Twenty-two years ago, my father took a crew into that same water for the same kind of job. One man came back, and he's never spoken since. My father didn't come back at all. The navy called it an accident and sealed the file, and everyone who might have said otherwise kept quiet, and I grew up inside that silence like it was a room."

Nobody spoke. Pip had stopped eating. Bram's stillness had gone total again, but a different quality of total—the stillness of a man hearing his own case being made.

"I don't know what's down there," I said. "I know it killed my father, and I know somebody wants it badly enough to pay me to fetch it, and I know that every instinct I have says walk away." I closed my fist around the compass. "But I'm tired of walking away. So we go upriver, we do the job, and we watch each other's backs, because the only thing I can promise you is that I will not leave any of you in that water. Whatever else happens. That's the charter. That's all of it."

Odile nodded slowly, once, like a stamp on a document. Bram lifted his tea a centimeter—Bram's toast, Bram's blessing. And Pip, sixteen and starving and running from something he wouldn't name, grinned at me across the ruined coffee and said:

"So when do we cast off?"

I was still deciding how to answer when Bram's head came up, sharp, his eyes going to the open porthole and the dark water beyond it.

"Boat," he said quietly. "Out there. Idling with no lights. It's been holding position off our quarter for ten minutes." He met my eyes across the cabin. "It's not fishing, Sterling. Fishing boats move."

I put out the lamp.
