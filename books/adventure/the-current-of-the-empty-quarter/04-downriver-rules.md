## 4. Downriver Rules

The toll bridge at Caster's Bend announced itself an hour before we reached it, a gray lattice of steel rising out the morning fog like the ribs of something that had died standing up. I saw it first from the wheel, then in the water—its reflection broken by our wake, trembling, as if the river itself were nervous about what waited under all that iron.

"Harbor police launch tied off at the second piling," Bram said from the sonar station. He'd been awake since four, running the channel charts Odile had pulled from a data broker in Saltmarsh. "Two officers visible on the catwalk. One of them has binoculars."

"Everyone has binoculars," Pip said. He was coiled on the bench by the galley door, pretending to read a tide table he'd been holding upside down for ten minutes.

"Then everyone sees us." I eased the throttle back until the engine dropped to a murmur you could feel more than hear. *Kestrel* settled into the current like she was glad of the excuse to slow down. "Odile. How do we look?"

Odile came up from the engine compartment wiping her hands on a rag that had started life white sometime before either of us was born. She squinted at the bridge the way she squinted at everything—as if it had personally disappointed her and she was cataloguing exactly how.

"We look like a salvage boat," she said. "Because we are a salvage boat. Permits are forged but well-forged. Registration says we're out of Greywater Basin, which is true enough if you count the two years I actually kept a berth there. The only problem is the cargo."

"The cargo is empty ballast tanks," I said.

"The cargo is empty ballast tanks *now*. But we're going to have to open them for inspection if they ask, and when they ask why a salvage rig is running light with no salvage aboard, one of us is going to have to be very good at lying."

"I'm good at lying," Pip offered.

"You're good at talking," Odile said. "Lying requires a face that doesn't apologize while it does it."

Pip shut up. I almost smiled. Almost.

Here's what nobody tells you about running rivers in the Hollow country: the law isn't a wall. A wall you can see coming. You can climb it, go around it, blow a hole in it if you're desperate enough. The law downriver is a current. It moves. It has moods. It eddies behind toll bridges and pools in the pockets of harbor officers, and it flows in one direction only—against whoever's carrying something worth taking. You don't beat it. You learn its schedule. You pay it respect in the currency it prefers, which is never printed money, and you keep moving before it decides you're interesting.

I'd learned that lesson at nineteen, running fuel up the Sable with my father, watching him slide a sealed bottle of brandy across a chart table to a customs man who'd already decided to find nothing. My father used to say the difference between a bribe and a fee was whether you resented it. He never resented anything. That was the year before Meridian Hollow. Before the job. Before the silence.

The fog thinned as we closed the last half mile. The bridge grew teeth: chain-link fencing along the catwalks, floodlights on rusted gantries, and painted on the concrete abutment in letters meant to be read from a hundred yards—

**HARBOR AUTHORITY CHECKPOINT — ALL VESSELS STOP AND DECLARE**

Underneath, older and smaller, someone had spray-painted: *declare what?*

"Cut to idle," I said. "Bram, kill the transponder. If they ping us, we're a fishing boat that lost her beacon in the fog. Odile, stand by the hatch. Pip—"

"I know," he said. "Be invisible."

"You can't be invisible. You're fourteen and you have a face like a struck match. Go below and stay there."

He went, but not gracefully. The companionway hatch banged twice behind him, which was his way of filing a formal complaint.

We drifted under the first span. Above us, boots rang on steel grating. A voice came down through a bullhorn, flattened by fog and indifference:

"*Vessel under the span. Cut engines and prepare for inspection.*"

We'd already cut engines. I raised one hand so they could see me doing nothing. The launch putted out from the second piling—gray hull, gray tarp over the forward gun mount, two officers aboard wearing the harbor authority blue that was just navy blue with worse tailoring. The younger one handled the lines. The older one stood at the bow with his hands clasped behind him like a man admiring architecture, and I knew before he opened his mouth that this whole checkpoint existed mostly so he could stand somewhere and be admired.

"Salvage vessel," he called up. "Out of where?"

"Greywater Basin," I said. "Light run. We're headed downriver to take a contract on a barge grounding near Tull's Shoal."

"Tull's Shoal." He said it like he was tasting it. "That's forty miles down. You're running empty?"

"Fuel and gear weigh the same going down as coming up. No point loading salvage equipment twice."

It was a good lie. It was even mostly true, which is the best kind. The officer nodded slowly, and I watched his eyes do the thing eyes do when they've stopped looking at your boat and started pricing it—the winch, the dive compressor, the brass fittings Odile kept polished because she claimed corrosion was a moral failing.

"Boarding inspection," he said. Not a question. "Standard for unlisted traffic."

"We're listed," Bram said mildly, from behind me. "Transponder hiccupped in the fog. You're welcome to check the registry."

The younger officer was already climbing the ladder. The older one followed, slower, and came aboard the way men come aboard when they want you to understand the boat belongs to them now. His name tag read **VANCE**. His partner's read **OKONKWO**, and Okonkwo had the particular stillness of somebody doing a job he hadn't chosen, well, and hating every minute of it.

Vance walked the deck like he owned the deed. He looked into the ballast hatches. He looked into the fish hold we didn't have. He ran his thumb along the rail and inspected it for dust.

"Nice compressor," he said. "Military surplus?"

"Civilian," Odile said flatly. "Older than both of you."

"Everything's military surplus down here, one way or another." He smiled at his own joke, then didn't. "Open the aft locker."

My stomach did the slow cold drop it does when a current shifts under you. The aft locker held the dive gear, which was fine—and the strongbox from the night wreck, wrapped in canvas, which was not fine. Not because of what was in it. Because of what it *was*: naval property markings, partially ground off, on a box recovered from a restricted site, sitting three feet from a harbor policeman with a boarding authority and a bad attitude.

"Locker's stuck," Odile said immediately. "Salt in the seal. Takes ten minutes and a pry bar."

"Then take ten minutes," Vance said pleasantly, "and a pry bar."

Okonkwo shifted his weight. Just slightly. And I caught it—not disapproval exactly. Something more like embarrassment. He looked at the water, at the bridge, at Vance's back, and I understood that Okonkwo had done this a hundred times and knew exactly what this was: not an inspection. An appraisal.

Ten minutes. Odile could give us ten minutes with salt and theater, maybe twelve. After that, canvas comes off a box that ends careers, and no amount of forged paperwork survives a man like Vance finding a reason to be thorough.

There was a chronometer in my cabin. Brass-cased, deck-mounted, salvaged off a packet steamer two seasons back. It was worth more than *Kestrel*'s engine. I'd been keeping it against the day the loan shark's patience ran out—a thing of last resort, sellable in any port to any fence in ninety seconds. My father would have called it dead weight. My father also slid brandy across chart tables.

Some inheritances you don't get to choose the shape of.

"Officer Vance," I said. "Before your partner starts prying at my boat—can I show you something in the cabin? Navigation issue. I'd rather sort it with you than with the magistrate at Tull's Landing."

Vance's eyebrows went up a fraction. Men like Vance love being shown things in cabins; it flatters their sense of how the world works. "Lead on."

The cabin was six feet by eight and smelled of coffee and old charts. The chronometer sat on the shelf above the bunk, gleaming the way only maintained brass gleams. I picked it up, felt its weight settle into my palm—heavier than it looked, the way all debts are—and turned to face him.

"This is a Meridian Standard Eight-Day," I said. "Pre-war. They don't make them anymore. Keeps time to two seconds a week, even rolling. I pulled it off a packet steamer with a legal salvage claim, all stamped and filed." I set it in his hands. "But honestly? It's wasted on me. I navigate by radio fixes and guesswork. A man with your responsibilities—checking bridges, keeping schedules—that's a tool that earns its keep."

Vance turned it over. Opened the case. Listened to it tick the way other men listen to music.

"Salvage claims get audited," he said softly.

"Not small ones. Not old ones. Not ones filed in Greywater Basin, where the clerk drinks on Thursdays and loses paperwork on Fridays."

A long moment. The chronometer ticked between us like a small patient heart. Somewhere above us, Okonkwo's boots crossed the deck, unhurried, going nowhere in particular.

"You know," Vance said, closing the case, "your paperwork looks fine to me. Fog plays hell with transponders. Happens all the time." He tucked the chronometer inside his coat with the smoothness of long practice, and clapped me on the shoulder hard enough to mean something. "Safe passage to Tull's Shoal. Mind the current past the third span—it's a liar."

He left the way he'd come, richer and more relaxed. Okonkwo paused at the top of the ladder. He didn't look at me. He looked at the river, downstream, toward Tull's Shoal and everything beyond it, and he said—quietly, just for me:

"Third span. He wasn't joking about that. And there's a second checkpoint at Weir Nine that isn't on the civilian charts anymore. Decommissioned, officially." A beat. "Unofficially, it's the busiest post on the river. Whatever you're carrying—" he finally glanced at me, and his face was carefully, professionally blank, "—carry it lower."

Then he was down the ladder and gone, and the launch pulled away, and the fog closed behind them like a curtain after a play nobody applauded.

* * *

We made the third span without incident, though the current there tried to fold us against the piling exactly as promised, and Odile wrestled us through it swearing in three languages, two of which I'm fairly sure she invented. By noon we were clear of Caster's Bend and running the long straight reach below it, banks sliding past, brown water folding green water, the river doing its endless work of carrying everything downhill and telling no one.

Pip emerged from below around midday, drawn by the smell of Odile's coffee, which was less a beverage than a threat.

"So we paid him," Pip said. It wasn't quite a question. He dropped onto the bench across from me, elbows on the table, studying my face with the frankness of the young. "The watch. We just gave it to him."

"Bribery," I said. "Yes."

"And that's just... how it works?"

"That's how it works downriver."

He chewed on that. I watched him chew on it, actually—you could see the machinery going, the same machinery that had asked me on the dock in Saltmarsh, *is it true about your father?*, the question everyone asks and nobody should.

"My uncle paid a harbor man once," Pip said. "Back home. Paid him every week to look the other way while he moved scrap. Then one week the harbor man didn't look the other way, and my uncle lost his boat and his license and nearly his hands." He shrugged, too casual by half. "Seems like paying them just teaches them where you keep your money."

I looked at him. Fourteen years old, stowaway-turned-deckhand, holding a chipped mug of coffee that was mostly sugar, delivering a smarter critique of corruption than any editorial I'd ever read.

"It does," I admitted. "Every payment buys today and rents tomorrow. You pay because the alternative is worse, and you keep paying until the day it's worse anyway, and then you're in debt to people who don't lend—they *take*." I turned my mug slowly on the table. "You avoid it when you can. You minimize it when you can't. And you remember every name and every face, because someday the current changes direction, and the people who swam with it need to know you noticed."

Pip considered this. "That sounds like a plan for revenge."

"It's a plan for memory. Revenge is just memory with bad timing."

He grinned at that, and for a second he looked young instead of orphaned, which on this boat counted as a victory.

Bram appeared in the doorway, chart tube under his arm, and spread the river survey across the table without asking. His finger found a point thirty miles downstream, where the channel narrowed between bluffs.

"Weir Nine," he said. "What Okonkwo said. I checked the civilian charts—weir's marked decommissioned eleven years ago. But look at the power draw." He tapped a marginal notation, some utility figure Odile had flagged weeks ago. "Something down there pulls enough electricity to light a town. Decommissioned locks don't pull current."

"Checkpoint that doesn't exist," Odile said from the engine room doorway, rag over her shoulder, "manned by people who aren't there, funded by money nobody spent. That's not corruption. That's architecture."

"Can we run it?" I asked.

Bram and Odile exchanged the look they exchange when the answer is yes but the price is high. "At night," Bram said. "Low water lights. There's a side channel through the old fish ladder—shoal draft only, and it hasn't been dredged since the weir was 'decommissioned.' Odile could thread it. Probably."

"Probably," Odile repeated, disgusted. "I thread it *definitely*, or we tear the bottom out of this boat in a place where nobody's allowed to admit we exist."

I looked at the chart. At the narrow pencil line of the fish ladder, threading between the bluffs like a secret the river had kept for itself. At Weir Nine, sitting fat and lit and officially nonexistent astride the main channel.

Everything downriver of Caster's Bend was telling me the same thing the fog had told me at the bridge: the closer we got to Meridian Hollow, the thinner the lawful world wore, and underneath it wasn't chaos. Underneath it was *order*—order bought and paid for, order with payroll and schedules and decommissioned checkpoints that never slept. My father had run into that order eleven years ago. The river had kept his receipt.

I folded the chart. "Night run, then. Fish ladder. Odile threads it, Bram runs dark, Pip stays below and invisible."

"And you?" Odile asked.

"I'll be at the wheel," I said, "owing one less favor and one more memory."

Pip carried his mug forward. Bram rolled the chart. Odile went below to argue with the engine, which was her form of prayer. And *Kestrel* ran on down the long brown reach toward Weir Nine and whatever waited past it, while behind us the fog burned off Caster's Bend and the bridge stood over the water with its hand out, waiting for the next boat that had something worth taking.

By dusk, the bluffs had closed in on both sides, and the first light of Weir Nine showed far ahead—not white, not amber, but a flat institutional blue that made the whole gorge look like it was underwater already.

Nobody official had ever built a light that color.
