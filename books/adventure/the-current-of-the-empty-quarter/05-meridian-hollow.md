## 5. Meridian Hollow

The river changed color two miles before the city did.

Tamsin noticed it from the wheel of the *Magpie's Rest*, watching the brown current ahead of them shade toward something older and greener, like tea left too long in a cup. Odile had said it was algae, temperature stratification, dead water pooling behind the collapsed floodgates. Bram had said nothing at all, which was worse. Bram only went quiet when his instruments told him things he didn't want to repeat out loud.

Pip stood at the bow rail with a pair of binoculars he'd won off a lock-keeper in a card game neither party would admit to.

"I can see it," he called back. "I can see—huh."

"Huh what?" Tamsin said.

"There's a church steeple sticking up out of the water. Just the steeple. Like somebody built a church on a sandbar and forgot the rest of it."

"Nobody forgot anything," Tamsin said, and heard how flat her voice came out, and didn't try to fix it.

She'd been dreading this stretch of river since Voss slid the contract across that tavern table three weeks ago. She'd told herself it was the job she dreaded—the sealed archive, the navy's fingerprints all over the salvage permits, the way the words *Meridian Hollow* made even Voss lower his voice. But standing at the wheel now, watching the green water widen to swallow the banks on both sides, she understood it wasn't the job. It was the logs. Her father's dive logs, the ones she'd burned half of and hidden the rest inside a hollowed-out copy of a maritime insurance codebook, because keeping them felt like evidence and destroying them felt like burying him twice.

She'd read those logs so many times she could have navigated this city blindfolded. That was the problem. She'd never seen it. She'd only ever seen her father's version of it, inked in his cramped hand, annotated with depths and bearings and one recurring note in the margins of every page:

*They don't want this place found.*

* * *

Meridian Hollow announced itself the way drowned things do—not all at once, but in pieces, each one more wrong than the last.

First the channel markers, navy-issue, freshly painted, which meant someone was still maintaining a navigation lane through a city that officially didn't exist. Then a row of lamp posts rising out of the water at perfect intervals, their glass fogged green, following a line that had obviously once been a boulevard. Then the rooftops.

Pip lowered the binoculars slowly, like they'd gotten heavy.

"It's a whole town," he said. "Under there."

"Half a town," Odile corrected from the engine well, not looking up from the fuel line she was nursing. "Look at the waterline. Third story on some of those buildings. The high ground's still dry further east—that ridge. They didn't drown the whole valley. They drowned the part where people lived."

"Why would anyone—" Pip started.

"Pip." Tamsin kept her eyes on the channel. "Not now."

But the question hung in the cockpit air anyway, the way his questions always did, too honest for the space they occupied. Why would anyone drown a town. She knew the official answer; everyone who worked the river knew it. The Hollow Reservoir Project. Flood control. Hydroelectric. A necessary sacrifice, compensation paid, residents relocated. It was in the almanacs. There were even photographs—smiling families on train platforms with cardboard suitcases, new housing blocks waiting somewhere downriver.

Her father's logs told a different story. The relocation trains ran empty. The compensation ledger had forty-one names on it and no payment dates. And the ferry—the ferry that was supposed to carry the last holdouts to safety—had gone down in deep water with its manifest sealed, and when her father had tried to dive it, the navy had been waiting for him at the dock with questions instead of answers, and then not even questions. Just silence, and a crew sworn to it, and a boat burned at anchor as punctuation.

Tamsin brought the *Rest* through the channel markers at idle speed, hands steady, jaw tight.

The city opened around them like a held breath.

* * *

They moored at what had been the commercial district's upper stories—a row of brick facades breaking the surface at the second-floor windowsills, fire escapes descending into green water like ladders into nowhere. Someone had welded cleats onto the cornices years ago. Salvage crews came here, then. Officially sanctioned ones, or ones bribed enough to be unofficial. The water around the buildings was dotted with buoys marking claimed sections, most of them faded, some of them cut loose and drifting.

Bram came up from below with his tablet and a face like a closed door.

"Sonar's going to be useless," he said. "Too much structure. Everything echoes. You'll be diving by feel and memory."

"Memory works," Tamsin said.

He looked at her a moment longer than the comment deserved. Bram had spent eleven years in naval hydrography before they'd scraped him off the bottom of whatever scandal had ended his career, and he had a sonar tech's instinct for reading what people didn't say. "You've been here before," he said. Not a question.

"No."

"But you know it."

"My father dove here. Before I was born, mostly. Some after." She killed the engine and let the quiet come in over the water. "He kept logs. I keep the logs. That's the whole relationship I have with this place."

Odile climbed up onto the deck, wiping diesel off her hands with a rag that was already black. "Then you can tell me why my charts show a ferry terminal two hundred meters north of us, and your father's logs—I'm assuming, since you've got that look—show it somewhere else entirely."

Tamsin went still.

"How do you know about the logs?"

"Because you navigate like a woman reading from a book nobody else can see. Because you've corrected the channel twice since the markers without checking a chart. And because you flinched when Pip said *steeple*." Odile shrugged. "I don't need to know what's in them. I just need to know if they're right."

Tamsin looked north, past the drowned boulevard, to where the water lay flattest and deepest, hiding whatever it hid. On the official charts, the ferry terminal sat on the eastern shore, on high ground, safely relocated along with everything else. In her father's logs, in his handwriting, dated six weeks before the flooding was completed: *Terminal still operating at original site. They are loading people, not equipment. Manifests sealed by military escort. Will dive the approach channel Thursday.*

There was no Thursday after that one. Not for him.

"The charts are wrong," she said. "On purpose. Somebody redrew the city before they flooded it. Moved the terminal, moved the streets, moved the numbers. If you dive this place by the official charts, you'll spend years finding nothing."

"And by your father's?"

"You'll find exactly what somebody paid to make disappear."

Nobody said anything for a while. The river slapped against the brickwork below them, patient, indifferent. Somewhere under that green surface was a street her father had walked, a dock he'd tied up to, a channel he'd swum down with a torch in his teeth and questions in his chest that got him killed. She could feel it all down there like a weight against the hull.

"We moor here tonight," she said finally. "Dive at first light. Bram, I want the approach channel sounded anyway—even if the sonar's noisy, I want to know if the terminal's where the logs say. Odile, keep the engine ready to run hot. This is restricted water whether the signs say so or not, and signs are the least of what they post out here." She turned to Pip, who was watching her with an expression she recognized and hated, because it was the expression of a kid deciding to trust someone. "Pip. Topside watch. Anything moves on the water that isn't us, you wake me before you think. Understood?"

"Aye," he said, and then, because he was fifteen and constitutionally incapable of leaving a thing alone: "Tamsin? Whose town was this?"

She looked out at the rooftops, the fire escapes, the lamp posts marching into the deep. Somewhere below, behind a sealed vault door, was an archive with her name on the contract and her father's ghost on the manifest.

"It was everybody's," she said. "That's what they couldn't allow."

* * *

She didn't sleep. She told herself it was the watch rotation, and then she told herself it was the noise the old buildings made as the current worked at them, groaning and ticking like a house settling, and then she stopped telling herself things and climbed out onto the cornice with her father's compass in her hand and let the dread do what it came to do.

The compass was water-stained, the brass gone cloudy, the needle lazy. He'd carried it on every dive. She carried it on every dive. Neither of them had ever quite trusted it to point north—it drifted, always a few degrees west, like it was looking for something else—and her father had joked once that it wasn't broken, it was homesick. She'd laughed then. She was eleven. She hadn't known yet that he was describing himself.

From the cornice, the drowned city spread out under the moon like a photograph developing in reverse. She could trace the boulevard by its lamp posts. She could see the park—or the treetops of it, a few drowned oaks still standing dead in eight feet of water, branches bare, like the forest had been caught mid-scream and preserved. Further out, the water darkened over the low district, and she found herself counting windows under the surface, lit faintly by the moon's reflection, floor after floor descending into black.

Her father had written about those windows. *Third night. Dived the low quarter. Faces in the glass, silt-caked. Counted 200+ dwellings. Where are the people, if the trains took them?*

Where are the people.

She'd asked herself that question for twenty years and answered it with work. Dive jobs, salvage contracts, a reputation built on being the best diver on the river and the worst person to ask about her past. She'd let the loan shark's debt sit on her like ballast because debt was simple, debt was arithmetic, debt could be paid. Whatever her father had found here couldn't be paid. It could only be carried.

And now Voss had hired her to carry it straight back into the water.

She thought about the contract clause again, the one she'd read four times before signing: *Recovery of sealed municipal archive, location per attached survey. Contents to be delivered unopened.* Unopened. Voss wanted the box, not the knowledge. She'd told herself that was standard—clients always wanted sealed deliveries, plausible deniability, clean provenance. But standing here, above the city her father had died asking questions about, the clause looked different. It looked like a lid.

Below her, at the edge of the lamplight, the water bulged once—something moving under the surface, big and slow, or maybe just the current finding a new way through a doorway that used to be a street. The needle of the compass swung lazily in her palm, drifting west, drifting toward the deep water and the terminal that wasn't supposed to be there.

Homesick, she thought. Yeah. Join the club.

"Tamsin." Pip's voice, low, from the deck below. Not scared, but close to it. "You need to see this. There's a light out there. Out on the water. It's been circling the terminal site for ten minutes."

She pocketed the compass and came down the fire escape two rungs at a time.

Through the binoculars, past the drowned boulevard, she saw it: a single white light moving in slow, deliberate sweeps over the darkest part of the harbor—right over the approach channel, right over the place her father's logs said the ferry went down. No running lights. No navigation pattern. A search pattern.

Somebody else was looking for the ferry tonight.

And they were diving it first.
