## 6. What the Navy Won't Discuss

Bram didn't sleep. Tamsin had learned that about him somewhere around the second night out of Meridian Hollow's outer channel, when she'd come up on deck at three in the morning and found him sitting in the wheelhouse with a salvaged navy terminal balanced on his knees like a sleeping cat, its screen the only light for a mile of black river.

"You're going to burn your eyes out," she said.

"Already did. Years ago." He didn't look up. His fingers moved over the keys with the particular tenderness of a man touching something he'd been ordered never to touch again. "Go back to bed, skipper."

She didn't. She poured two cups of Odile's terrible coffee and sat on the chart table across from him, boots dangling, watching the terminal's glow crawl across his face. Outside, the river breathed against the hull. Somewhere upstream, a lock siren moaned once and went quiet.

"What are you working on?"

"Nothing."

"Bram."

He exhaled through his nose. On the deck below, Pip was supposed to be asleep in the forepeak but she could hear him shifting, listening the way teenagers listen—pretending not to, missing nothing.

"When I got mustered out," Bram said finally, "they took my clearance but they couldn't take what's in my head. Sonar signatures. Hull profiles. The way different yards weld their seams." He turned the terminal so she could see it. "I've been running the scan data from your wreck against the naval registry fragments I still remember. Off the books. Don't ask me how I still have access, because I'll lie to you."

"Would it be a good lie?"

"Very convincing."

The screen showed two hull schematics side by side, overlaid in ghostly green lines. They matched almost perfectly—except where they didn't, and the places where they didn't were the interesting places. Weld patterns altered. Compartments reconfigured. A section amidships that had been cut open and rewelded with cruder hands than the original yard would ever have employed.

"That's the ferry," Bram said quietly. "The *Corvid's Rest*. The one that went down in the Hollow flood with—" He stopped himself, the way men do when the number is too large to say casually. "The one from the Hollow."

Tamsin felt the deck tilt under her, though the river was flat as poured glass.

"No," she said. "The wreck I've been diving is a coastal freighter. Registry says—"

"The registry says whatever someone wanted it to say. Look at the keel line. Look at the frame spacing. That's a passenger vessel, Tamsin. Refitted as a freighter on paper, refitted *back* in the water. Someone welded new bulkheads into her and repainted her name twice—the old name's still under the current one, I can see it in the sonar return where the paint layers reflect differently." He tapped the screen. "Your restricted wreck is the *Corvid's Rest*."

The coffee had gone cold in her hands. She drank it anyway.

"They told everyone the ferry sank during the evacuation," she said. "Overloaded. Bad weather. That's the story. That's the story my father—"

"That's the story everyone tells." Bram closed the terminal halfway, then couldn't quite make himself finish closing it. "But here's what's eating me. If she sank in the flood, in the channel, she'd be where the flood put her. Silt patterns, impact damage, all of it consistent with going down in moving water full of debris." He reopened the screen. "Your wreck is sitting upright on a shelf in forty meters of still water, keel level, oriented north-south, in a spot where the current doesn't run. That's not a sinking. That's a *placement*. Somebody put her there."

* * *

They gathered in the saloon at dawn because Bram refused to say the rest of it twice. Odile came up from the engine room wiping her hands on a rag that had been white during the previous administration. Pip sat cross-legged on the bench with his elbows on the table, trying to look like crew instead of cargo.

Bram set the terminal in the middle of the table and turned it around.

"I pulled fragmentary logs," he said. "Not the official record—that's sealed or shredded. But ships talk to each other, and shore stations log everything, and some of those shore station backups got sold for scrap when the coastal command consolidated. I've been buying corrupted drives off scrap dealers for three years. Most of them are garbage. This one—" he touched the terminal, "—this one has pieces."

The screen filled with text. Green characters on black, some lines whole, most of them chewed to fragments, half-sentences hanging in digital amber.

**[STATION MERIDIAN APPROACH — SURFACE LOG — EXTRACT]**

> **0412 —** ...vessel *CR* departing berth 3, authorized... [corrupt] ...passenger manifest NOT attached per order...
>
> **0530 —** ...weather clear, visibility good... no distress...
>
> **0907 —** ...unidentified subsurface event grid K-9... consistent with scuttling charge... [corrupt] ...no vessel reported lost in area...
>
> **0915 —** ...patrol assets directed to grid K-9... salvage/salvage-denial protocol... deny all queries...

Nobody spoke. The river slapped the hull. Down in the engine room, Odile's spare generator ticked over like a slow heart.

"Scuttling charge," Odile read aloud. Her voice was flat, which was how Tamsin knew she was furious; Odile went flat when other people went loud. "That's a controlled detonation. That's somebody opening the seacocks or setting charges and letting her go down *deliberately*, in calm weather, with a passenger manifest that nobody was allowed to file."

"The flood was three days before that," Pip said. He'd gone pale. "My uncle used to talk about the Hollow. Said the water came up fast and the ferries ran all night getting people out. There were supposed to be—there were boats running the whole time."

"There was one boat," Bram said. "Running one night. And the log says she left the berth at 0412 with no manifest, in clear weather, and nine hours later something blew a hole in her nine miles downstream." He looked up at Tamsin. "And then patrol boats cordoned the site and denied all queries. That's not an accident report, skipper. That's a burial."

Tamsin stared at the screen until the green letters swam. She thought of the dive. Forty meters of cold green silence, the wreck sitting neat as furniture on her shelf, hatches open, holds empty—holds *empty*, she'd noticed that and filed it away without understanding why it mattered. A ferry overloaded with fleeing civilians should have been a tomb. Personal effects everywhere. What remains.

There was nothing. She'd told herself the river took things, rivers do, silt takes things—

Or the navy took things first. Salvage-and-denial. Everything off the boat that could identify anyone, everything that could testify, and then the boat herself, sunk properly this time, in still water, out of the channel, where no dredge would ever find her by accident.

"They sank her twice," Tamsin said. "Once with charges. Once with paperwork."

* * *

Odile went back to the engine room and came back twenty minutes later with a printout she'd made on the ancient dot-matrix wired to the ship's systems, because Odile trusted paper the way other people trusted God.

"Here's what bothers me," she said, spreading it out. "Everyone keeps saying *the flood*. The flood drowned the Hollow, the flood killed the people, the flood is a tragedy and an act of God. But look at the timeline." She laid her finger along the log fragments. "Flood crest, here. Ferry departs *after* the crest, after the emergency, when there's nothing left to evacuate. So who was on board?"

"Officials," Pip said. "My uncle said the last boats took the officials out first. Before anybody else."

"Right. So the *Corvid's Rest* wasn't an evacuation vessel by the end. She was the last boat out, carrying whoever mattered, in clear weather, with no manifest." Odile straightened up. "And then somebody sank her. With witnesses' families still alive in the camps downstream. You don't sink a boat full of officials unless the officials are the problem. Unless what they knew goes down better than what they'd say."

The saloon was very quiet. Through the porthole, Tamsin watched the brown water slide past, patient as anything, taking everything downstream toward the sea and keeping nothing.

"My father dove that wreck," she said. It came out of her before she could stop it. "Before I was born—or when I was small, anyway. He had charts. Hand-drawn. He kept them in a case he only opened at night. My mother caught him looking at them once and they fought, and afterward he told me: *some water isn't water, Tammy. Some water is a locked room.*" She laughed, and it came out wrong. "I thought it was a diver's superstition. I built a career on diving his locked rooms and I never once asked who locked them."

Bram reached over and rested one scarred hand flat on the table—not touching her, just near her, the way ex-navy men comfort people, adjacent and silent.

"The registry entry for the *Corvid's Rest*," he said. "You want to know what it says?"

"Tell me."

"'Lost with all hands, cause undetermined, location unrecorded.' That's it. No grid reference. No investigation number. Every other loss in that registry gets pages. Court of inquiry, witness statements, wreck position so no one snags their nets on her." He shook his head slowly. "Hers is four lines. I've seen denials shorter than that, but not many."

"Because there was never an inquiry," Odile said. "You can't inquire into a ship that officially never sank where they say she didn't sink."

* * *

Pip was the one who broke the silence, because Pip was sixteen and hadn't yet learned which questions adults agreed not to ask.

"So where did everybody go?" he said. "The people on the ferry. The officials. If the navy sank her with them aboard—"

"We don't know they were aboard," Tamsin said quickly.

"Skipper." Bram's voice was gentle and terrible. "Empty holds. Stripped personal effects. Scuttle charges. Denial protocol. Either they evacuated her first and sank her empty—which means the sinking served no purpose except destroying the *ship*—or they didn't evacuate her." He let that sit. "And if the ship needed destroying, it's because the ship was evidence. Which means what happened on that boat was evidence too."

Pip looked down at his hands. When he spoke again his voice had dropped its bravado entirely, and for a moment he sounded exactly as young as he was.

"My town's on maps," he said. "It's small, but it's there. You can drive to it. If the Hollow's not even on the maps anymore—if the ferry's not even in the registry—then somebody didn't just kill those people. Somebody *edited* them." He looked up. "How do you edit a whole town? How does that even work?"

No one answered him, because none of them knew, and because the answer was sitting right there in the green light of a corrupted shore-station log: slowly, in filing cabinets, one deleted manifest at a time, by men whose names would appear on no list either.

* * *

Tamsin took the last watch alone. She stood at the wheel with the river running dark on either side and the compass open in her palm, its needle swinging lazy arcs, drunk on the wide water, finding north and losing it and finding it again.

Her father had drawn charts of that wreck. Had known it was there, in still water, on its shelf, placed rather than fallen. Had known—or suspected—and had gone diving it anyway, years later, on a job that killed him, in a city the maps had forgotten.

*Some water is a locked room.*

She'd spent her whole life picking locks and calling it salvage. Calling it a living. Calling it a debt paid down a little at a time to a man in a glass office who smelled of cologne and counted other people's losses in percentages.

The needle steadied. North. Upstream. Toward the Hollow.

Below her feet, in the hold, wrapped in oilcloth inside a padded crate, lay the strongbox from the restricted wreck—the one she'd bled and bribed and nearly died for, the one Voss was paying her to open. She hadn't opened it. She'd told the crew it was corroded shut. It wasn't. She'd simply stood in her cabin the night they raised it with a pry bar in her hands and found she couldn't make herself do it, the way you can't make yourself knock on a door when you already know who's dead behind it.

Tomorrow, she thought. Tomorrow we open it.

The compass needle trembled, held true, held north.

Upstream, nine miles past a lock that no longer appeared on any chart, a ferry full of edited people sat upright in the dark on her shelf of still water, waiting forty years for someone to say her real name out loud.

Tamsin meant to be the one who said it.
