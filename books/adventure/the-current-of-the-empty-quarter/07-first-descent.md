## 7. First Descent

The archive vault sat beneath the old customs house like a swallowed heart.

Tamsin had spent the morning on the roof of the customs house, or what remained of it—a ridge of green-slicked tile breaking the surface of the Hollow's drowned quarter, gulls arguing over the chimney pots. From there she could see the whole shape of the submerged district through forty feet of tea-colored water: streets running to nowhere, doorways exhaling silt, a church spire leaning like a drunk against nothing. Her father's dive logs had described it in careful, clinical language. *Structural survey incomplete. Visibility poor. Do not attempt without topside support.* The last line was underlined twice.

She'd thought about that underline all night.

"You're doing the thing," Odile said beside her. She was lying flat on the tiles with her cheek against the moss, peering down into the water as if she could read it. "Where you go quiet and start planning your own funeral."

"I'm assessing risk."

"You're planning your own funeral. I've known you eleven days and I already know the difference." Odile rolled onto her back and squinted at the sky. "Bram says the vault is under the east wing. Says the navy's own salvage surveys flagged it as 'inert storage'—which is what they call things they don't want anyone opening."

"Convenient."

"Everything about this place is convenient," Odile said. "That's what worries me. A city drowns, and the one building with a sealed sub-basement just happens to be the one Voss wants emptied." She sat up, started checking her rig for the third time that hour. "So we do this clean. Line tied at the roof, descent line to the stairwell, thirty minutes bottom time, no heroics. You go first because you're better in the dark and I'm better at doors. We touch nothing we can't carry."

"And if something goes wrong?"

"Then Pip screams into the radio and Bram pretends he isn't terrified, and we come back up and argue about it like professionals." Odile snapped her mask over her face. "In and out. Like we were never here."

Tamsin looked down one more time. Somewhere below, past the green murk and the drifting curtains of algae, her father had swum these streets. He had come back from this place changed—quieter, harder, carrying rules he never explained—and then he had gone back a second time and not come back at all. The water below her held the answer to both trips. She could feel it the way you feel weather coming in your teeth.

"Comms check," she said, and dropped her voice into the regulator.

Pip's crackle came through the earpiece, thin and tinny from the tender above. "Loud and clear. Bram's got the sonar watch. He says—" a pause, muffled consultation "—he says there's a hard return under the customs house, rectangular, consistent with a vault door. Also says, quote, tell them not to die, unquote."

"Tell him we'll do our best."

She spat in her mask, rinsed it, settled it against her face. The world went green and framed. Odile gave her a thumbs-up, then a fist—their signal for *no matter what happens down there, we come up together*—and Tamsin returned it, and then she leaned forward and let the river take her.

* * *

The first ten feet were warm and blind.

The Hollow's flooded quarter didn't have water so much as it had suspended earth—silt held in solution, turning every beam of light into a brown smear. Tamsin descended hand over hand down the line, watching her own fins vanish beneath her, listening to the doubled rhythm of her breathing amplified inside the hood. At fifteen feet the customs house materialized around her like a developing photograph: first the ghost of a cornice, then a window frame furred with weed, then the whole wall resolving out of the murk, windows staring at her with the patient blindness of a skull.

A streetlamp hung sideways off its post at twenty feet, still holding its glass. A child's tricycle stood upright in a doorway, handlebars furred white. She made herself look at it. Made herself log it, the way her father had taught her—*name what you pass, or it will follow you*—and moved on.

At thirty feet she found the stairwell.

It opened through a collapsed section of floor where the customs house's grand hall had given way, a wide stone staircase descending into blackness, the banister draped in a beard of gray silt-vines. Her light cut a cone through the gloom and found steps going down further than it should—down past where a basement should end, down toward whatever the navy had built when they decided a customs house needed a second, secret floor.

Her depth gauge read thirty-eight feet. The stairs went to sixty.

She keyed the comm. "Found it. Descending."

"Copy," Odile said, close behind her, her own light swinging across the walls. "Nice and slow. Watch your ceiling—that archway looks like it's shedding."

It was. Fine grit sifted down through their light cones like snow falling upward wrong. The building had been drowning for years, but it hadn't finished dying; every wall was a decision the river hadn't gotten around to making yet. Tamsin kept her left hand trailing the banister and counted steps the way her father had taught her—counting was an anchor, counting was a rope back to the surface even when no rope existed. Twelve steps. Twenty. Thirty-one, and the staircase turned, and beyond the turn the water changed.

It got colder. It got *clearer*. Whatever fed this depth came from somewhere else—seepage through foundations, maybe, filtered through decades of stone—and in that clearer water her light finally reached out and found what she'd come for.

A door. Steel, set into dressed stone, painted institutional gray and furred at the edges with orange iron bloom. No handle. A wheel, like a submarine, crusted but intact. And above it, stenciled in letters that thirty years of water had failed to erase:

**SUB-LEVEL 2 — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL — RECORDS**

Records. Not inert storage. Records.

"Odile." Her voice came out flat through the comm. "You seeing this?"

"Seeing it." A pause. "Hating it. Navy doesn't stencil warnings on things it plans to admit exist." Odile drifted up beside her, and her light joined Tamsin's, two cones crossing on the steel. "Wheel'll be seized. Give me room."

While Odile worked—hammer taps, penetrating oil from a squeeze bottle, the patient violence of a woman who had spent her life persuading stuck metal—Tamsin swept her light along the corridor. And stopped.

There were marks on the silt of the floor.

Fins. Two sets, recent—the silt hadn't resettled over them, edges still crisp, lines still sharp. They led away from the door, down the corridor, and vanished around a corner where the light couldn't follow. Whoever had come through here had come through within days. Maybe hours.

Her mouth went dry behind the regulator. She keyed the comm before fear could finish forming into words.

"Pip. Topside. Anyone else on the water? Any other boats?"

Static. Then Pip's voice, pitched high and fast: "Negative. Just us and a barge two miles downriver. Why? What's wrong?"

"Nothing. Keep watching." She killed the channel and hung there in the cold clear dark, breathing recycled air, looking at fin marks that meant everything she'd believed about this job was wrong. Voss had said the archive was untouched. Voss had said no one else knew. Voss had said—

Voss had said a lot of things.

"Line's moving," Odile announced. The wheel groaned, shed a century of rust in orange flakes, and turned. "Door's open. Not locked—unlatched. Someone closed it behind them, careful-like." She looked at Tamsin, eyes wide behind her mask. "Careful means coming back. Careful means they're not done."

They went in.

* * *

The vault was a single long room, maybe eighty feet by thirty, and it had been beautiful once in the way government rooms are beautiful—ordered, numbered, certain. Shelving ran in ranks down its length, steel frames bolted to the floor, and on the shelves: canisters. Hundreds of them. Sealed cylinders the size of wine bottles, racked in rows, each tagged with a paper label that time had eaten to pulp. The water in here was dead-still, preserved somehow, and in that stillness the scene had the terrible dignity of a tomb that had been robbed.

Because it had been.

An entire section of shelving near the far end stood empty—racked slots bare, tags torn away, the dust of dissolved paper hanging in the water like breath. Dozens of canisters, gone. Taken recently, carefully, by someone who knew exactly which ones they wanted.

"They beat us here," Odile said. Her voice had gone very quiet. "By weeks, maybe. Look at the corrosion on those empty brackets versus the full ones—they pulled them fast, though. Fast and organized."

Tamsin swam the aisle slowly, her light walking down the shelves. Most of the remaining canisters were corroded husks, their seals breached, their contents long since dissolved into the water that had killed the town above them. But here and there one survived—sealed in wax-painted steel, intact, patient. She checked tags as she passed. Most were illegible. One, on a canister near the middle rank, still held legible characters under a skin of slime:

**MH-FERRY / MANIFEST SUPPLEMENT**

Ferry. The word went through her like a fishhook. Every diver knew the story the navy told: the *Meridian Star*, lost with all hands in a storm, records lost with her, tragedy, closure, memorial plaque. Her father had died diving a wreck the navy swore was exactly where it wasn't.

And here, in a vault beneath a customs house in a town the navy also swore had simply flooded, was a manifest supplement labeled with the ferry's name.

"Tamsin." Odile's voice again, sharp now. "Ceiling."

She looked up. Above the empty shelving section, a crack ran across the vault's ceiling—a long, weeping fracture, older than their visit, older probably than the flooding. But from its ends, new cracks branched like frost ferns, and fine sediment pulsed gently from one of them with each passing second, timed to some deep movement of the building. The vault was breathing. The vault was failing.

"We take what we can carry and we go," Tamsin said. "Now. Two each. Intact seals only."

They worked fast after that, fast and silent, the way people move when the building itself has become an adversary. Tamsin pulled four candidates, tested each seal with a thumbnail, rejected two whose wax showed hairline fractures. The two survivors went into her mesh bag: one tagged **MH-FERRY / MANIFEST SUPPLEMENT**, one tagged only with a number and a date—**LOT 44 / 11 MAR**—a date that meant nothing to her and felt like it should mean everything.

Odile found hers in the last rank, wedged behind a collapsed shelf strut: **HYDRO-SERIES / CORRESPONDENCE**, seal perfect, cylinder cold and heavy in a way the others weren't. "This one's different," she said. "Thicker casing. Military spec." She weighed it in her palm. "Somebody wanted *this* kind protected."

The crack overhead chose that moment to speak.

Not a sound—water doesn't carry sound so much as it carries *intent*, a pressure change, a shudder through the whole room like the building clearing its throat. Sediment sheeted down from the fracture in a gray curtain. Somewhere above them, in the drowned floors of the customs house, something structural surrendered.

"Out," Tamsin said. "Out, out, out—"

They hit the corridor at speed, bags cinched, lights swinging wild. Behind them the vault door stood open, and Tamsin made herself stop—one second, two—to swing it shut and spin the wheel until it seated. Not to trap anyone. To slow the collapse, to keep the surge of a failing room from racing up the stairwell after them. It was the kind of thing her father would have done. It was possibly the kind of thing that had killed him, this instinct to close doors behind himself in places that wanted him dead.

Then the stairwell began to come apart.

It didn't collapse all at once. It collapsed the way old things do—reluctantly, then all at once. The first sign was the banister in Tamsin's hand jumping like a live wire. The second was the sound, finally arriving through the water: a deep, grinding groan from directly overhead, the voice of tons of drowned masonry reconsidering its position. The third sign was the silt.

It came down the stairwell ahead of the collapse like smoke before fire, a brown avalanche in slow motion, erasing their lights, erasing the steps, erasing the world. Tamsin's visibility went from ten feet to three feet to nothing in the space of four breaths.

"Line!" she shouted into the comm, knowing even as she did it that Odile might not hear her, that the comm might be silt-choked too, that the line itself might be buried. She grabbed for the descent line with both hands and found it—thank God, thank God—still taut, still anchored somewhere above in the roof tiles Pip had set. She wrapped it twice around her forearm and started up into the blind.

The building fell behind her.

She felt it more than heard it—a pressure wave rolling up the stairwell like a train in a tunnel, slamming against her back, shoving her up the line whether she willed it or not. Masonry thundered somewhere below, close enough that she flinched from impacts she couldn't see. Her fins struck something solid. Debris rained past her arms. Once—only once—something large swung past her shoulder close enough to touch, and she refused, absolutely refused, to think about what part of the building it had been.

Thirty feet of zero visibility. She climbed it by count and by feel, wrapping the line, pulling, kicking, counting again. Her gauge said she was burning air faster than she should. Her ears told her the pressure wave had broken into echoes, which meant open water somewhere above. Her lungs told her nothing, because her lungs were trusting the regulator, and the regulator was trusting her, and that chain of trust was the only thing in the world.

Then her head broke into green-gray light and the river took her weight and she was coughing around her mouthpiece, treading water beside the customs house roof, alive, in daylight, in the ordinary breathing world.

"Odile!" Nothing. She spun in the water. "ODILE!"

The surface boiled ten feet away and Odile came up like a breaching thing, ripped her mask off, and laughed—a raw, shocked, furious laugh. "Stairwell's gone! Whole flight, pancaked!" She spat river water. "Your bag okay?"

Tamsin clutched it. Both canisters, sealed, cold against her ribs. "Yours?"

"Held onto it like it owed me money." Odile treaded water beside her, catching her breath, and then her expression changed—flattened into something grim. "Tamsin. Those fin marks. That unlatched door. Somebody emptied half that vault on purpose, recently, and they were careful about it. Organized. Equipped." She nodded back at the collapsing ruin behind them. "We just dove into somebody else's operation. We're carrying what they left behind."

Tamsin looked down through the water at the settling cloud where the stairwell had been, at the vault now sealed under tons of fresh rubble, at the few surviving canisters entombed there that no one would reach easily again. Then she looked at the mesh bag pressed against her chest—at the manifest supplement from a ferry the navy said never sank wrong, at a lot number dated the month her father disappeared.

They rowed back to the tender in silence. Pip hauled them aboard, chattering questions neither of them answered. Bram met them at the rail with his sonar tablet and a face like a man reading his own death warrant.

"Before you ask," he said quietly. "I've been watching the channel all day. That barge two miles downriver?" He turned the screen toward her. On it, a heat signature idled at the barge's stern, motionless, patient. Watching. "It hasn't moved in six hours. And it's rigged with a crane."

Tamsin set the mesh bag on the deck between them, water pooling around the two sealed canisters, and understood that the question was no longer whether they'd find the truth at the bottom of the Hollow.

Someone else had gotten there first—and they were still here, waiting to see what surfaced.
