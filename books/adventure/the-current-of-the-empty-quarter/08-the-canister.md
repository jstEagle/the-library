## 8. The Canister

They didn't open it on deck.

Tamsin didn't have to explain why; Odile saw her face and simply started the engine and took them two miles upriver, into a dead slough behind a collapsed levee where the dredger sat low in the reeds like something that had been there so long it had grown roots. Bram killed every light but one—a red lamp clipped to the bulkhead of the galley, the kind that wouldn't bleed through a window seam. Pip was sent forward to watch the channel, which was a kindness dressed up as an order, because nobody wanted him in the room for what came next.

The canister sat on the galley table between a cold pot of coffee and a coil of spare line.

It was naval issue, or had been once—titanium cylinder, forty centimeters, the kind designed to survive depth charges and decades of silt. The seal ring was intact. The exterior stamping had been ground off with a tool, not worn away by water, which told Tamsin someone had wanted it anonymous before it ever went down. Her father's world, she thought. A world of things that had been made nameless on purpose.

"Pressure's equalized," Odile said. She'd already checked twice. "It's been flooded since day one, or it's dry as a bone. No way to know till we crack it."

"Then crack it."

Odile worked the release collar with a strap wrench wrapped in cloth, slow, deliberate, the way she did everything that mattered. The mechanism fought her for a moment—corrosion welding steel to steel—and then gave with a sound like a knuckle popping, a hiss of gas escaping that smelled of river mud and old rubber and something else underneath, faintly chemical, faintly sweet.

Bram turned his head away from the smell. He didn't say anything about it. He didn't have to. Tamsin had watched him read that scent the way he read sonar returns—instantly, professionally, and with a tightening around the eyes that meant *I know what this is and I wish I didn't*.

Inside, packed in waxed linen gone stiff as bark: a bundle of papers. And beneath them, wrapped separately, a small brass cylinder the size of a thumb—film canister, pre-digital, the kind that hadn't been manufactured in thirty years.

Nobody touched anything for a long moment. The red light made the papers look like they were already bleeding.

"Gloves," Tamsin said finally, and her voice came out steadier than her hands felt. "And photographs first. Everything, before we separate a single sheet."

Odile laid out the bundle on a dry towel under the lamp while Bram shot frame after frame with the salvage camera, flash diffused through a scrap of frosted plastic. Tamsin stood back and let them work, because if she leaned in now—if she looked—she was afraid of what her own hands would do.

She knew before she saw it. She'd known since the stairwell collapsed behind her and Odile screamed her name through the comm and the only thing Tamsin could think, alone in the dark with the water rising, was *he was here, he stood here, this is his air I'm breathing.*

The top sheet was a shipping manifest. Dated nineteen years ago. Consignee: Meridian Hollow Municipal Ferry Authority. Contents column: blank. Weight column: filled in to the kilogram. That discrepancy alone would have been enough—the navy did not ship things whose contents it declined to name—but it wasn't what stopped Tamsin's breath.

It was the margin.

Someone had annotated the manifest in pencil, small, precise letters slanted hard to the right, the hand of a man who wrote fast and hated wasting paper. A notation beside the weight figure: *ballast? no. sealed crates, 14 units, stbd hold aft. crew not informed.* And below that, underlined twice: *ask R. why ferry manifest routes through Naval Ordnance ledger.*

R.

Rourke. Her father's dive partner. The man who'd sworn silence at the burning boat and kept it until the day he died, and who had never once, in all the years after, answered the question Tamsin had asked him a hundred different ways: *what was my father carrying?*

Her hands were shaking. She noticed it the way you notice weather—as a fact about the room, remote from herself.

"That's—" Odile started.

"I know whose writing that is." Tamsin picked up the second sheet with gloved fingers, careful as a surgeon.

A list. Not a manifest this time. Names. Columns of them, handwritten, each entry followed by a date and a string of numbers she couldn't yet parse. Forty-one names visible on the first page. She turned it over. Forty more on the reverse. Ages, some of them—she realized with a slow, cold drop in her stomach that some of the entries weren't ages at all but birthdates, and several of those birthdates were less than a year before the dates written beside them.

Children.

The Hollow had been a working town. Ferries ran twice a day; there'd been a school, a parish, a fish cannery, a lock-keeper's union hall. When the district flooded—when the levee "failed," when the navy's report called it an act of God compounded by municipal negligence—eighty-two people had officially drowned. She knew the number because everyone on the river knew the number; it was the kind of tragedy that got a memorial plaque and a chapter in a regional history and then, within a decade, a fence and a restricted zone and a generation of kids warned away from the water.

Eighty-two. She counted the names on the page. Eighty-three.

One extra. One name too many for the official count, written in her father's hand, dated three days *after* the flood.

She read it four times. It didn't change.

*Iles, M. — evacuated upstream, unregistered. alive.*

Mara. The name meant nothing to her yet. It would come to mean everything. But standing in the red-lit galley with the river pressing against the hull, all Tamsin felt was the shape of the fact itself: her father had known someone survived who wasn't supposed to exist on any record, and he had written her down anyway, in pencil, in a canister bound for the bottom of the world.

He hadn't just found evidence. He'd found *people*. And he'd decided they mattered more than the mission, more than the money, more—God help him—than coming home.

"Bram." Her voice cracked on the second syllable. She cleared her throat. "The chemical designation. You smelled it. Tell me."

Bram had the last sheet, the technical one, held close to the lamp. His jaw worked. "HX-9," he said. "Stabilized precursor. Binary component—it's inert until it meets its partner compound, and then it's—" He stopped. Started again, flatter, the way men talk when the alternative is shouting. "It's a persistent agent. Not a weapon on its own. It's a *contaminant*. You don't deploy it, you seed it. Groundwater, reservoirs, a town's drinking supply. Deniable. Untraceable after ninety days. Nobody signs for it because officially it doesn't exist."

"And the navy was testing it."

"The navy was *shipping* it." Bram set the sheet down like it burned. "Through a civilian ferry authority. On a passenger route. With crews not informed." He looked at her, and in twenty years of knowing ex-navy men she had never seen Bram look like this—not angry, worse than angry. *Complicit-adjacent.* "Tamsin. This isn't a weapons program. This is a field trial. The Hollow wasn't an accident they covered up. The flood was the *test result*."

The galley was very quiet. Somewhere forward, Pip's boots creaked on the foredeck, keeping his watch, thirteen years old and guarding a secret he couldn't pronounce.

Tamsin looked down at the ledger. At the columns of names in handwriting she'd have known anywhere—in the dark, underwater, dissolved to nothing—and understood, with the terrible clarity that comes only once or twice in a life, that she had spent fifteen years grieving the wrong thing.

She'd thought her father died for money. For a salvage contract, a payday, a debt—that was the story the surviving crew told, the story that kept a nine-year-old girl from asking questions that got adults hurt. *Your dad took a job too big for him. Bad luck. Bad water.* She'd built her whole life inside that story. The loan shark's ledger on her desk, the numbers she owed, the wrecks she dove at night to chip away at them—all of it resting on the belief that her father had been a man chasing a payout and drowning in it.

But the debt was never money.

The debt was eighty-three names. It was a man who'd found out what his employers really were and chose, in the space of a few days, to steal their proof instead of finishing their job—to hide it where only a diver could reach it, and to annotate it in pencil like a man leaving instructions for whoever came after. *Ask R. why.* He'd known he might not make it home. He'd left the trail anyway. Fifteen years long, leading from a strongbox on a scuttled ferry to a corroded canister in a breached vault to this table, this lamp, this shaking-handed daughter who'd spent half her life diving his wrecks without knowing she was reading his mail.

"He hid it here," she said aloud. Not to anyone. To him. "You hid it in the one place they'd never send divers, and then you went back for it, and they sank the ferry on top of you."

"Tamsin." Odile's voice, gentle. Odile, who never used gentle. "There's more. Under the manifest."

There was. A final sheet, folded in quarters, thicker stock. Tamsin opened it and found a letter—or the start of one, unfinished, undated, addressed to no one:

*If you're reading this, then either R. kept his word or the river keeps better promises than people do. The full ledger is copied. Three sets. One goes to the Hollow families if I can find who's left. One stays with me. The third—*

It ended there. No signature. No third location. Just the indent of a pen pressed hard and then lifted, mid-sentence, forever.

Tamsin read it twice. Then she did something she hadn't done in front of another living person since she was nine years old standing on a dock watching a boat burn.

She put her forehead down on the table, next to the ledger, and cried.

Not quietly. Not prettily. The kind of crying that comes up from somewhere below the diaphragm, fifteen years deep, and takes the body's whole architecture with it. Odile put a hand on her shoulder and left it there. Bram turned away and studied the coffee pot like it contained classified material. Outside, the river moved against the hull in its patient, indifferent way, carrying everything downstream toward the sea, keeping nothing, forgiving nothing, forgetting nothing either—that was the thing about rivers people always got wrong. They didn't forget. They just held everything where no one could see it, and waited.

* * *

It took her twenty minutes to stop and five more to wash her face and come back to the table looking like someone who could be trusted with state secrets, which, as of tonight, she was.

"We photograph everything," she said. "Originals stay sealed in the second canister—we swap the contents, vacuum-seal it, and it doesn't see daylight again until it's in front of someone who can't be bought. Bram, the film canister—can you develop it?"

"If I can rig a darkroom out of the head and the chemical locker, yes. Give me six hours." He paused. "Tamsin. If that film is what I think it is—"

"It's the other copy. Or part of it. My father wrote *three sets*." She tapped the unfinished letter. "One for the families. One with him. One somewhere else. We've got whatever this is, plus the originals. Which means at least one set is still unaccounted for, and Voss didn't hire us to find a canister. Voss hired us to find *all of them*."

"You think he knows what's in it?"

"I think his family name is going to be in that ledger's margins somewhere, same as mine is in these pages' history. And I think a man like Cassian Voss doesn't spend his own money recovering his grandfather's crimes. He spends it *erasing* them." She looked at the red lamp, at the wet towel, at the two canisters—one empty, one full, one future, one past. "We assumed we were racing the navy. We're not. We're racing a cleanup crew."

Odile was quiet a moment. Then: "The barge. The crane. They haven't moved in six hours because they're not salvaging. They're waiting to see what we bring up. And when we surface with it—"

"They take it. Quietly. A salvage accident, a boat lost with all hands, another erased record." Tamsin heard her own voice go flat and cold, the voice she used for dive plans and funerals. "Same play they ran on my father. Same play they've run for nineteen years. It works because nobody survives to contradict it."

"So we don't surface with it," Bram said slowly. Understanding dawning across his scarred face. "We move at night. Shallow draft, lights dead, down the east channel where the sonar's blind behind the old rail bridge—"

"No." Tamsin shook her head. "That's what they'll expect. A runner runs. We've got a dredger that looks like a floating junkyard and a crew of ghosts. We don't run *past* them." She allowed herself the ghost of a smile, the first in hours. "We run *at* them. Close enough to read their paint scheme. Because I want to know who bought this secret—and nobody guards a barge for six hours without getting sloppy about who's watching them back."

She gathered the ledger sheets, squared their edges with a care that bordered on ritual, and slid them into the second canister. Her hands had stopped shaking. That was the strange part. All those years of tremors over loan payments and patrol boats and the small daily terrors of a life built on borrowed time—and now, holding the actual weight of what her father died for, her hands were steady as bedrock.

Because the debt was never money. And debts like this didn't get paid in installments.

They got paid in truth.

She was screwing down the seal when Pip's voice came over the intercom, pitched high and fast, a boy trying very hard to sound like a man:

"Skipper? Lights on the barge. They're moving—and skipper, there's a launch running ahead of them, running dark, heading straight for us. Two minutes, maybe less."

Tamsin closed her eyes for one breath. Her father's letter, unfinished, rose behind them: *the river keeps better promises than people do.*

"Kill the lamp," she said. "Everyone to stations. Whatever happens next—nobody touches that canister but me."

The red light died, and the river came in black at the windows, and somewhere out in the dark, running silent, whatever Cassian Voss had set loose on the water was already closing the distance.
