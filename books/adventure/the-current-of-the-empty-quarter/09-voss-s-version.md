## 9. Voss's Version

Cassian Voss had rehearsed this conversation for eleven years, and every version of it had ended with him lying.

He sat in the stern of the *Kestrel's Debt*—Tamsin's boat, a name that still needled him—and watched her read the canister's contents by lamplight. Her hands were steady. He remembered when they hadn't been. Twenty-two years old, both of them, competing for the same salvage certification, and she'd dropped a lift bag on the exam wreck because her gloves were too thin and he'd said nothing, just watched her fail and felt something warm and shameful bloom in his chest. That was the first thing he'd ever stolen from her, and it had cost him nothing. Everything since had cost more.

The ledger lay open between them on the chart table: forty-one names, dates of "resettlement" that were dates of drowning, and a chemical designation—*Compound VX-9, stabilized precursor, Meridian Hollow facility*—written in a clerk's careful hand. And in the margins, cramped and slanting, annotations Tamsin had been staring at for an hour without speaking.

Her father's handwriting. Cassian recognized it too. He'd seen it once, years ago, on a manifest Aldous Sterling had signed before the last dive anyone would ever admit to.

"You knew," Tamsin said finally. Not a question. She didn't look up. "When you hired me. You knew what was in the archive."

"I knew there was an archive." Cassian turned his glass on the table, a slow quarter-rotation, watching the lamplight swim in the whiskey. "I didn't know you'd find your father in it."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have that's true." He heard how it sounded—evasive, lawyerly, the voice of a man who'd spent a decade in contract negotiations learning to say everything while saying nothing—and he hated it. He tried again. "Tamsin. Look at me."

She looked up. Her eyes were red-rimmed but dry, and there was something in them worse than anger. Recognition. She was looking at him the way you look at weather coming in off the water—the way you look at something you'd already braced for.

"My grandfather," Cassian said, "was Undersecretary for Procurement in the Naval Materials Directorate. Nineteen sixty-eight through nineteen seventy-four. You've never heard his name because no one was supposed to. Edmund Voss. He administered the supply chain for a program that didn't exist, at a facility that wasn't on any map, producing a compound that was banned by three treaties before the first barrel left the spring."

The words came out of him flat and practiced, and he realized with a kind of distant horror that he'd rehearsed this part too—not the confession, but the tone of it. The controlled delivery. Even his shame was curated.

"He signed the transport orders," Tamsin said quietly.

"He signed everything. Including—" Cassian stopped. Drank. Started again, because she deserved the whole weight of it, not the edited version. "Including the requisition for the ferry. The *Corvid*. It was flagged as a civilian evacuation vessel. It wasn't. It carried eleven officials out of the Hollow the night before the gates locked, and then it was towed upriver and scuttled in deep water so that no one would ever ask why a passenger ferry was sitting at the bottom of a channel with its seacocks opened from the inside."

Tamsin's hand went flat on the table. Not a fist. Flat, the way you press down on something to keep it from lifting off and taking you with it.

"The wreck I've been diving," she said. "The restricted one. Off Gannet Shoal."

"The *Corvid*."

"Three years. Three years I've been diving that wreck illegally, trying to figure out why the navy erased it, and it was because my father—" Her voice caught, and she mastered it, and Cassian watched her master it and thought: *she is stronger than I will ever be.* "My father died on the job that covered it up. And you knew. You've known since before you walked into the Salt Line Tavern and slid that contract across the bar like it was a salvage job."

"I did know." He set the glass down. "And I'm going to tell you why, and then I'm going to make you an offer, and you're going to want to hit me, and I'd deserve it."

* * *

Here was the thing Cassian Voss had never told anyone, not his lawyers, not his brother, not the woman he'd almost married before she read the room and left: he had found out about Edmund Voss at his grandfather's funeral, at twenty-six, in the least dramatic way imaginable.

He'd been sorting the old man's papers for the estate lawyers—boxes of them, decades of committee minutes and correspondence, most of it mind-numbing bureaucratic sediment. And in the third box, under a folder marked *Hollow Municipal Water Project*, he'd found a memorandum. One page. Dated nine days after the flooding of Meridian Hollow.

*Re: residual documentation. Recommend incineration of all local records. The settlement was regrettable but necessary. No further action required. — E.V.*

*Regrettable but necessary.* Five words. His grandfather's whole soul in five words, filed and initialed like a lunch order.

Cassian had sat in the dead man's study until dark, holding that page, waiting to feel something enormous. What he felt instead was smallness. A specific, corrosive smallness—the understanding that the family name he'd been raised to carry like a coat of arms was stitched over rot, and that everyone who'd ever told him he came from good stock had either known and lied or not known and been lied to, and that either way the lie now belonged to him.

He could have burned the memo. That was the sane option. That was what any reasonable man would have done—slip it into the fireplace, close the box, go back to his life of contracts and harbor leases and respectable gray money.

Instead he'd kept it. For eleven years. In a fireproof case behind the paneling in his office, where he saw it every day and told himself he kept it as evidence, as leverage, as insurance—as anything other than what it actually was, which was a stone he carried in his chest to punish himself with.

Because here was the arithmetic he'd done a thousand times and never balanced: if the Voss name was built on the Hollow, then every dollar of it was blood money, including the trust fund that had paid for his education, his first boat, his first three salvage contracts—including, God help him, the money he'd offered Tamsin Sterling to do this job. He was paying her with drowned people. He had been paying everyone with drowned people his entire life.

"So when the audit notice came through," Tamsin said. She'd listened to all of it without interrupting, which frightened him more than shouting would have. "Navy Inspector General. Decommissioned records review. Your lawyers flagged it."

"They flagged it six weeks ago." He nodded toward the ledger. "That audit is going to surface the program whether anyone wants it to or not. Too many files, too many retirements, too many clerks with guilty consciences reaching pension age. When it breaks, there are two ways it breaks. Way one: the navy releases a sanitized summary, blames a deceased contractor, seals everything for another fifty years, and the forty-one people in that book stay ghosts forever. Way two—"

"Somebody leaks the real record."

"Somebody leaks the real record, and the navy does what navies do when cornered. They burn the evidence chain. Every document connected to the program gets pulled and destroyed to protect the institution—*and* every document connected to the cover-up gets destroyed right along with it. Including the exculpatory material. Including the manifests proving certain divers were contracted under false pretenses and abandoned." He met her eyes. "Including whatever would clear Aldous Sterling's name."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere above them, on deck, Pip was doing some chore badly, boots scraping, blessedly oblivious.

"You're saying the truth coming out kills the truth," Tamsin said slowly.

"I'm saying the truth coming out *unmanaged* kills the parts of it you care about most." He leaned forward. This was the pitch, the one he'd built in his head on the train ride down, and it sounded exactly as rotten aloud as it had in his head, and he made himself say it anyway. "The audit surfaces the program. My family's lawyers get ahead of it—we control the narrative, we take the fall publicly, Edmund Voss posthumously becomes the villain, clean and contained. The program gets named, condemned, closed. And in exchange, the operational records—the ones naming contractors, the ones documenting who dove what and why—get sealed as part of the settlement. Protected. Your father comes out of this with his reputation intact. Officially cleared. A name on a memorial instead of a footnote in a classified annex."

"And the ledger?"

"Goes into the sealed collection." He held her stare. "With everything else."

"For fifty years."

"At minimum."

"So the families wait another half century to learn their mothers had names." She pushed back from the table and stood, and for a moment he thought she was going to strike him after all, and he found he wanted her to—it would have been cleaner than the look she gave him instead, which was pitying. Pitying. From Tamsin Sterling, whose life he'd helped break. "You're offering me my father back in exchange for keeping forty-one people buried. That's the trade."

"It's not—" He stopped. Breathed. Because the honest answer was *yes, that is exactly the trade*, and he'd dressed it in procedure and legal strategy because he couldn't stand to say it plain. "Yes," he said. "That's the trade. And I'll tell you why I'm making it, and you can decide what it makes me."

* * *

His grandfather had died believing he was a good man.

That was the detail that had undone Cassian, more than the memo, more than the ferry, more than anything in the boxes. Edmund Voss had gone to his grave at ninety-one surrounded by grandchildren, honored by his service, genuinely beloved—and in all those years he had never once, as far as Cassian could find, lost a night's sleep over Meridian Hollow. There was no hidden confession in the papers. No letter of remorse. Just the memo, and the smooth continuation of a life.

*Regrettable but necessary.*

Cassian had spent eleven years trying to locate the difference between himself and that old man, and the terrible joke of it was that the difference kept shrinking. He'd hired Tamsin not to recover the archive but to control it—to be the man standing between the truth and the daylight, managing it, curating it, deciding which victims got named and which stayed convenient. He'd told himself it was mercy. He'd told himself destruction was protection—that a burned ledger couldn't be weaponized against the families, that silence was a kind of shelter.

But he'd stood in the Salt Line Tavern and watched Tamsin refuse his contract, and he'd used her father's name like a crowbar, and he'd known, even then, walking back to his car in the rain, that he was not protecting anyone. He was protecting the name. The same name. The same five words, wearing his face now instead of Edmund's.

"I'm offering you the deal because it's the deal I can deliver," he said. "Not because it's right. I don't know anymore what's right. I know that if I let that ledger surface raw, my family's name dies in public, and I will feel—" He laughed, short and joyless. "Do you know what I'll feel? Relief. That's the obscene part. Part of me wants the fire. And I don't trust a man who wants the fire to build anything afterward. So I'm offering you the managed version, the survivable version, the version where your father gets his name back and nobody else has to burn."

Tamsin was quiet for a long time. Outside, the river moved against the hull with that patient, indifferent sound it made, the sound of something that would still be moving long after both of them were names in someone else's ledger.

"My father annotated that book," she said at last. Her voice had changed—lower, steadier, and Cassian understood with a sinking feeling that he'd lost her somewhere in the last ten minutes and was only now hearing the door close. "He didn't just carry it. He corrected dates. He added initials. He was building a case, Cassian. He went back for this. He went back knowing what it cost, and the crew that survived swore silence around a burning boat rather than give up what he was doing." She closed the ledger gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping child. "And you're asking me to seal it in a drawer so a dead undersecretary can keep being a good man in his grandchildren's memories."

"I'm asking you to choose the version where somebody wins."

"No." She picked up the canister, cradling it against her ribs like something alive. "I'm choosing the version where everybody's counted. You should think hard about which side of that line you're standing on, Cassian. Because I've seen what happens to men who manage rivers. The river doesn't negotiate."

She went up the companionway, and the lamplight swung, and Cassian Voss sat alone in the stern of her boat with his untouched whiskey and his grandfather's five words ringing in his skull like a bell that wouldn't stop.

*Regrettable but necessary.*

He took out his phone. Deke answered on the second ring, as he always did, loyal and incurious and bought.

"Change of plans," Cassian said, and heard his own voice go quiet and cold, the voice of procurement, the voice of the memo. "There's a second copy of the archive. In the Hollow itself. I need you to find it before she does."

He hung up before Deke could ask the obvious question—*before who does?*—because he already knew the answer, and the answer was a stone in his chest, and he had learned, at his grandfather's knee without ever meeting the man, how to carry stones.

Above him, Tamsin's silhouette crossed the deck, heading upstream in her heart already, and Cassian Voss sat in the dark below and told himself, one last time, that what he was doing was mercy.

The river, patient as arithmetic, said nothing.
