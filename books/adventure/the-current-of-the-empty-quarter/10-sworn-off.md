## 10. Sworn Off

The summer her father took the Hollow job, Tamsin was fourteen and certain of three things: that the river was honest, that her father was careful, and that careful men came home.

She would spend the rest of her life unlearning all three.

* * *

It started with a smell — tar and diesel and the particular sweetness of fresh-caulked seams — drifting up through the floorboards of their houseboat before dawn. Tamsin knew that smell the way other kids knew the smell of pancakes. It meant work. Good work, usually, because nobody wasted good caulk on bad pay.

She found him in the wheelhouse of the *Maren Cole*, running his thumb along a chart he'd spread across the helm and weighted down with four brass fittings he'd never let anyone else touch.

"You're going out," she said.

"Didn't want to wake you."

"You woke the whole dock." She climbed onto the cooler by the door and sat, making herself part of the furniture, which was how she'd learned to stay in rooms she wasn't invited into. "Where?"

He didn't look up. That was the first wrong thing. Her father looked at people when they talked to him; it was a rule with him, one of the small dignities he believed in. Now his eyes stayed on the chart, on a stretch of river marked with nothing at all, just clean paper where a city should have been.

"Upstream," he said. "Meridian Hollow."

Tamsin frowned. She knew the river the way she knew her own hands — every lock, every shoal, every town with a fuel dock and every town without. Meridian Hollow was on none of her maps. She'd heard the name maybe twice in her life, both times from old men who lowered their voices to say it, the way you'd lower your voice in a church or a morgue.

"That's not a place," she said. "That's a story people tell to scare deckhands."

"It's a place." He folded the chart along its old creases, precise as surgery. "Was."

"What's the job?"

"Salvage." He said it too fast, the way you say a word you've been told to say. "Recovery contract. Sealed archive, insurance paperwork, boring as ballast. Two weeks, maybe less."

"Who's the client?"

"Tamsin."

"I'm asking a normal question."

"And I'm telling you it's boring." He finally looked at her then, and what was in his face scared her more than any answer could have. Not guilt exactly. Guilt she knew — she'd seen it on men coming off jobs they shouldn't have taken, and it looked like a man avoiding his own reflection. This was different. This was a man who had already decided something terrible and was now deciding whether to tell his daughter about it.

He decided no.

"Watch the boat," he said. "Keep Odile fed. She forgets to eat when she's inside an engine. And if Bram comes by wanting to borrow tools—"

"—tell him no, because he doesn't return them. I know." She slid off the cooler. "You're being weird."

"I'm being paid," he said, and smiled, and the smile almost worked. "Two weeks. I'll bring you back something from upstream. Something you've never seen."

"Everything upstream I've seen."

"Not this." He kissed the top of her head, salt and caulk and coffee, and went down the ladder two rungs at a time like a man half his age, and Tamsin stood in the wheelhouse of the *Maren Cole* and watched him cast off into the gray morning water, and the last thing he did before the current took him was touch two fingers to the compass mounted above the helm — not checking it, just touching it, the way you'd touch a saint's medal or a lover's cheek.

She didn't know then that it was goodbye. Nobody ever knows. That's the whole trick of it.

* * *

The first week was fine. Better than fine — it was the week she learned to bleed the fuel lines alone, because Odile said if she was going to hang around engines she might as well be useful, and Odile's idea of teaching was handing you the wrench and walking away. Tamsin bled the lines, fouled two injectors, fixed them, and by Friday the generator ran so smooth Odile grunted at it, which from Odile was a parade.

Bram came by Tuesday, borrowed a socket set, and returned it Thursday, cleaned, which shocked everyone including Bram.

"He called," Bram said, leaning in the doorway of the engine room, trying for casual and missing by a mile. "Your dad. From upstream. Says the job's bigger than quoted."

"Bigger how?"

"Wouldn't say on an open line." Bram turned the socket over in his big hands. "Says don't worry."

"Do you worry?"

Bram was ex-navy sonar, twenty years on the water, a man who'd heard torpedoes in training exercises and drowned men on real ones, and he considered the question longer than any easy question deserved.

"I worry when a careful man won't say what he's doing," he said. "But he's your father, and he's the most careful man on this river, so I'm going to go home and sleep like a rock, and you should too."

Tamsin slept like a rock. That was the last easy night of her childhood.

* * *

Day nine, the calls stopped.

Not slowed down. Stopped. The relay station upstream that handled traffic past the toll bridges went quiet on the same day, which Odile swore was routine maintenance and Pip — no, not Pip yet, Pip came later — anyway, Odile swore it was routine, but she checked the relay's schedule against the harbor authority bulletin board three times, and Tamsin saw her do it, and neither of them said anything.

Day eleven, a navy patrol boat came downriver at speed, running dark except for its running lights, moving like a man leaving a room quickly. Tamsin stood on the deck of the *Maren Cole* and watched it pass, and counted — eleven crew visible, twin mounts fore and aft, a tow cable rigged but nothing on the tow. Coming back empty. Going out heavy, coming back light. She was fourteen and she already knew enough salvage arithmetic to hate what that equation meant.

Day thirteen, Odile stopped pretending to eat.

Day fifteen — two weeks, the promised two weeks, the day he was supposed to walk up the dock with something she'd never seen — a man came instead.

Tamsin never learned his full name. He was crew, one of the six who'd gone upstream with her father, and when he stepped off the bus at the downstream depot he moved like a man carrying something breakable inside his chest. He found her at the dock because of course he did; there was nowhere else she'd be. He was maybe thirty and he looked sixty, and he wouldn't come down onto the floating dock itself, wouldn't step on the *Maren Cole*, wouldn't even touch the rail.

"Where is he?" Tamsin asked.

The man opened his mouth. Closed it. Looked at the water.

"Where is my father?"

"There was an accident," the man said, in a voice like a recitation, like words handed to him by someone else. "A diving accident. The boat was lost. There were— there were no survivors among the dive team."

"The boat," Tamsin said. "You mean the *Maren Cole*. You're standing next to the *Maren Cole.*"

"No." His voice cracked on it. "No. A different boat. A charter. Your father's boat is—" He gestured helplessly at the hull under their feet, the hull her father had caulked and loved and named after Tamsin's dead mother, the hull that was currently holding up his daughter's weight as surely as the earth held up a house. "This is fine. This is untouched. They didn't—"

"They." Tamsin heard her own voice go flat and cold, some older self stepping forward to take the controls while the girl fell away behind. "Who is they?"

The man looked at her, and Tamsin watched him make a decision, and she would spend years replaying this moment, trying to read what passed across his face — fear, or pity, or the specific shame of a survivor doing arithmetic about who lived and why.

"Take the boat somewhere else," he said. "Don't dive the Hollow. Don't ask the navy questions. Don't—" His voice failed and rebuilt itself. "There are people who will come around asking what we discussed. Tell them we talked about weather. Tell them I came to say the job was lost and the client defaulted. Weather and money. Nothing else."

"You're not answering me."

"I know." He wiped his mouth with the back of his hand. "I'm sorry. About all of it. He was—" The man's face did something complicated and unfinished. "He was the best diver I ever worked with. Remember that. Whatever else you hear, remember that."

Then he walked away up the dock ramp, and Tamsin stood alone on her father's boat in the last light of the fifteenth day, and the river moved beneath her, patient and honest, saying nothing.

* * *

They didn't hold a funeral, because there was nothing to bury. The navy issued a statement — commercial diving mishap, vessel loss under investigation, families notified — and then, with a thoroughness Tamsin only understood much later, declined to investigate anything at all. No wreck location published. No recovery report. No manifest of the charter boat's crew, as though six men had walked into the water off the edge of the map, which in a sense they had, because Meridian Hollow was not on the map.

What there was instead was the burning.

Odile organized it, because Odile organized everything, because grief with nowhere to go will find work. She sent word down the river through channels Tamsin didn't know existed, and on a windless October evening, boats began arriving at a bend miles below the harbor authority's reach — a coal barge with a family aboard, a rusted tug, two skiffs, Bram's cabin cruiser riding low with silence. Men and women Tamsin had grown up around and others she'd never seen, salvagers and lock-keepers and smugglers, people whose living depended on knowing when not to know things.

In the middle of the anchorage, tied off alone, floated the charter boat's surviving tender — the little aluminum workboat her father had towed behind the *Maren Cole* on every job since before Tamsin was born. Someone had brought it back downstream. Tamsin never learned who. It sat in the black water with its paint scorched and its name plate pried off, and everyone understood without discussion what it was for.

Odile stood at the bow of the tug with a lantern raised, and the fleet went quiet the way water goes still.

"We don't have bodies," Odile said. Her voice carried flat across the water, stripped of everything but function. "We don't have a wreck. We don't have answers, and we're not going to get them, and every one of us knows why, and every one of us knows better than to say it out loud. So here's what we have. We have each other, and we have a fire, and we have the truth, which we'll keep the way you keep anything valuable on this river — down deep, and dry, and out of sight."

She looked at Tamsin then. Everyone did. Fourteen years old, standing at the rail of Bram's cruiser in a coat too big for her, and the whole hidden community of the river watching to see what grief would make of her.

"Tamsin," Odile said. "You can stop this. Say the word and we take the tender home and we grieve like land people, with paperwork and a stone. But if we light it, we light it for all of them, and we swear on it, and a swearing on the water doesn't come undone."

Tamsin looked at the tender. Her father had taught her to row in that boat, on mornings when the fog was thick enough to hide them from the world, and he'd told her — she remembered it with total clarity, the way you remember things that turn out to matter — he'd told her that the river keeps every promise made on it, and collects every debt, and never loses the ledger.

"Light it," she said.

They poured the fuel and Odile struck the match and the tender caught with a soft rushing sound, flame climbing the aluminum like ivy, orange light rolling out across the faces of the anchored boats. And one by one, as the fire took hold, the crew spoke.

Bram went first. "I swear silence," he said, to the fire, to the water, to whatever was listening. "Not because I'm afraid. Because talk gets people killed, and I'm done burying divers."

Odile: "I swear silence until the truth can survive being spoken. Not a day less. Not a day more."

The coal barge family. The tug captain. The smugglers, who swore in a dialect of nods. Sixteen voices, sixteen oaths, and the smoke going straight up into the windless sky like a column marking a country that appeared on no map.

When they got to Tamsin, she found she couldn't speak. Odile knelt beside her, and waited, and the fire crackled, and finally Tamsin leaned close to the flames and gave the river the only oath she had:

"I'll remember," she whispered. "Whatever you all forget, whatever you bury, I'll remember. Somebody has to keep the ledger."

Nobody corrected her. Nobody knew yet what she'd meant by it — least of all her. But the river heard, and the river keeps accounts, and eleven years later, standing in a flooded street with a canister of names in her hands, Tamsin Sterling would understand that the oath she'd sworn at fourteen was the one she'd been breaking her whole adult life, rule by rule, dive by dive, and that breaking it was the only way to keep it.

* * *

The fire burned down to the waterline by midnight and the fleet slipped away in the dark, each boat to its own berth, each mouth to its own silence. Tamsin stayed until the aluminum was awash and glowing, until the last ember hissed out, until there was nothing left on the black water but a skin of ash drifting downstream toward the sea.

Bram found her there at dawn, stiff with cold, and put a blanket around her shoulders and coffee in her hands, and didn't say anything, because there was nothing left that either of them was allowed to say.

But before he left, he pressed something into her palm and closed her fingers over it. Small, brass-cased, heavier than it looked. Her father's compass — the one from above the helm of the *Maren Cole*, the one he touched every time he cast off, the one that should have gone down with everything else.

"Recovered from the tender," Bram said quietly. "Before tonight. Water-stained. Case won't open — I tried. Maybe it's jammed. Maybe it's not." He held her eyes. "Some things get sealed for a reason, Tamsin. Doesn't mean they're meant to stay sealed forever. Means they're meant to be opened by the right person, at the right time, when opening them costs less than keeping them shut."

He squeezed her shoulder once and walked up the dock, and Tamsin sat alone in the gray morning with her father's compass in her fist, feeling its weight, feeling the case resist her thumb, feeling — though she couldn't have said how — that it was not empty.

Eleven years later, a rival's contract in her hand and a ledger of the dead waiting upstream, she would finally understand what her father had sealed inside it.

And why he'd trusted the river to deliver it.
