## 11. Upstream

The dredger had been rotting in a cut below Tollgate Reach for nine years, and she smelled like it.

Tamsin found her at low water, half-sunk in the reeds with her name burned off the bow and her superstructure furred green to the wheelhouse roof. Somebody had stripped the copper years ago. Somebody else had stripped what was left of the copper from them. But when Odile went down into her engine flat with a headlamp and came back up twenty minutes later, she was grinning the way she only grinned at machinery other people had given up on.

"She's a *Kestrel*-class," Odile said, spitting rust-water off the ladder rungs. "Navy auxiliary. They built four hundred of these things and forgot about three hundred and ninety of them. Twin diesels, direct drive, no electronics worth spoofing. She's dumb as a fence post and she'll run until the sun burns out."

"That's exactly what I want to hear."

"Course you do." Odile wiped her hands on a rag that was more grease than rag. "She'll take two days to make riverworthy. Three if you want her to survive the cordon."

"I want her to survive the cordon."

"Then three."

* * *

They worked through the heat of the day and into the blue hour, and by full dark the *Marrow* — Pip named her that, after the barge his uncle used to run, and nobody argued because arguing with Pip about names was like arguing with the tide — sat low and steady in the cut, breathing diesel smoke through a stack Odile had patched with part of a grain silo.

Bram came aboard last, carrying a canvas bag and an expression Tamsin had learned to read over the past week: the particular set of his jaw that meant he'd been listening to navy frequencies and hadn't liked what he heard.

"Cordon's moved," he said, dropping the bag on the chart table. "Was at Weir Nine. Now it's a running picket between the Nine and the old ferry landing. Two gunboats, rotating. They're not screening traffic anymore. They're looking for something specific."

"How specific?"

"Mid-sized vessel, heading upstream, unregistered cargo." He looked at her. "That's us down to the rivets."

Tamsin spread the chart across the table and weighted the corners with coffee mugs. The river ran up out of the south, wide and brown and lazy on paper, but the paper didn't show the current, and the current was the whole argument. Going downstream, you were a leaf. Going upstream, you were a stone that had decided to walk. Every knot they made against the flow was a knot the navy could spend catching them.

"We go through the picket at slack water," she said. "Three a.m., between rotations. Odile says the *Marrow* makes six knots clean. We'll be making four against the current, and we'll look like it."

"Look like what?"

"A dredger," Tamsin said. "Because we are one. Dredgers work at night. Dredgers are slow, ugly, and boring. Nobody boards a dredger unless she's doing something wrong, and we are going to be so aggressively boring that boarding us will feel like a career mistake."

Pip, who was coiling line by the rail with the exaggerated care of someone pretending not to listen, said: "What does aggressively boring look like?"

"It looks like me standing on deck in a hi-vis vest holding a clipboard," Tamsin said. "It looks like Odile running the engines at exactly the speed that produces exactly the wake a working dredger produces. It looks like Bram sitting on a bucket eating cold beans where the searchlight can see him, looking miserable, which will require no acting whatsoever."

"I resent that," Bram said, "because it's true."

* * *

They cleared the cut at two-forty and turned upstream into the dark, and the river took their measure immediately.

The *Marrow* pushed against the current the way her name suggested — stubbornly, gracelessly, with a vibration in the deck plates that got into your teeth. Tamsin stood at the wheel with the running lights dimmed to embers and watched the banks slide past, black walls of willow and dead pilings, and felt the old animal alertness come up in her chest, the one that lived somewhere below thought. Somewhere ahead, around a bend she couldn't see yet, there were men on a gunboat with a searchlight and a list, and her name was close enough to the top of that list now that she could feel its warmth.

Odile had done something to the stack that made the smoke run heavy and oily, the way a tired old workboat's smoke should. She'd also rigged the flood lamps to throw a working cone off the bow onto empty water, buckets lowered on lines, all the theater of industry. From two hundred yards, in the dark, they were exactly what a bored watch officer expected and dreaded: contract dredging, some county sediment order, nothing to do with him.

At 3:07 the searchlight found them.

It came off the water first — a white road laid across the current, walking toward them — and then the gunboat swung out from behind a spoil island with her running lights blazing, and Tamsin's heart did the thing it always did, the drop, the cold bloom, and then the settling, because fear was a tool like any other and she'd spent eleven years learning to hold it by the handle instead of the blade.

"Steady," she said, though nobody had wavered. "Nobody changes anything. Boring. Be boring."

The light pinned the deck. Bram sat on his bucket in the glare, spoon halfway to his mouth, and gave the gunboat the single most defeated look Tamsin had ever seen a man produce. Odile leaned out of the engine hatch wiping her hands, unhurried, faintly annoyed, a woman whose night was being interrupted by paperwork. Tamsin raised the clipboard.

A voice came across the water through a hailer, flattened and official: "*Dredger, hold position. State business.*"

Tamsin keyed the handheld she'd borrowed from a lock-keeper two days ago and pitched her voice into the register her father had used with harbor officials — tired, cooperative, faintly aggrieved. "Dredger *Marrow*, contract seven-seven-four, sediment survey, Tollgate to Nine. Want my paperwork?"

A pause. On the gunboat's bridge, shapes moved behind glass.

"Paperwork's fine," the voice said finally, with the weariness of a man who had checked forty boats tonight and would check forty more before dawn. "Keep your cone off our channel."

"You keep your wake off my buckets," Tamsin said, and killed the radio, and did not let her hand shake until the searchlight swung away and the gunboat slid back behind its island like a shark losing interest.

For a full minute nobody spoke. Then Pip, from the shadows by the rail, exhaled so loudly it was nearly a whistle.

"You said 'keep your wake off my buckets,'" he said. "To a *gunboat*."

"They were going to wave us through either way. I just wanted the last word."

"That's not a personality, that's a diagnosis."

"Coil your line, Pip."

But he was grinning in the dark, and she caught herself grinning too, and then the bend took them and the cordon fell away astern and the river narrowed and quickened, and the night became simply the night again — water, banks, the diesel heartbeat underfoot — except that all of them knew they had just crossed a line drawn on no chart, and there was no crossing back.

* * *

Pip found her at the wheel near dawn, when the sky over the eastern hills had gone the color of dishwater and the first birds were working the shallows.

He brought two cups of the crew's coffee, which was less a beverage than a threat, and handed her one without asking, which was also new. A month ago he'd have waited to be offered. A month ago he'd been cargo. Somewhere in the past hundred miles he'd become crew, and Tamsin wasn't entirely sure when it had happened or whether she'd approved it, and she suspected that was how family always happened — sideways, while you were watching the channel markers.

"You slept?" she asked.

"Two hours." He leaned on the rail beside her, elbows on the worn wood, chin on his fists, watching the water slide by. He'd grown in the weeks since Meridian Hollow. Or maybe she'd just started seeing it. "You didn't."

"I don't much, before a run."

"The cordon's behind us."

"The cordon's never behind us. It just moves." She drank the coffee and regretted it on principle. "Get some more sleep."

"In a minute." He was quiet for a while. The *Marrow* thumped and shuddered beneath them, patient as livestock. Downstream, behind them, the drowned streets of Meridian Hollow lay under their green water with their doors shut and their staircases going down into nothing, and the canister with the ledger rode lashed in the forward hold, wrapped in oilcloth, packed in Odile's spare gaskets, guarded like a relic.

When Pip finally spoke, he didn't look at her. He looked at the water, the way people do when they're asking something they've been carrying a long time and don't want to see the answer land on your face.

"Why can't we just sell it?"

Tamsin kept her eyes on the channel. "Sell what?"

"The ledger. Come on." He straightened, turning to face her, and his voice had the edge it got when he thought he was being handled. "There's collectors who'd pay for that. There's newspapers. There's — Voss alone would pay double whatever anyone else offered just to keep it quiet, and you know it, because he already tried. We could take the money, split it four ways, buy real boats, real papers, names that aren't ours. Disappear somewhere the navy's never heard of. That's the whole game, isn't it? That's what salvage *is*. You find the thing, you sell the thing, you run. Everybody on this boat has spent their whole life running. So why are we steaming *upstream*, toward the guns, toward the place where the thing came from, instead of cashing out and vanishing?"

It was a fair question. It was the fairest question anyone had asked her since the Hollow, and she had known it was coming, had felt it building in Pip for days the way you feel weather building behind hills, and she still didn't have an answer that would hold weight.

So she gave him the honest one.

"I don't know yet."

His face fell. Not with disappointment, exactly — with something worse, the look of a boy discovering that adults were improvising too.

"You don't know," he repeated.

"No." She set the cup down on the ledge by the wheel. "I know what the answer's supposed to be. I've got the speech. Justice, and the dead, and names that deserve to be spoken, and all of it's true, Pip, every word of it's true, and if you want the speech I'll give you the speech and you can believe it and it'll get us through the next hundred miles fine."

"But?"

"But the speech is what my father believed, and it put him at the bottom of the Hollow." The words came out level, which surprised her; she'd expected them to cost more. "And the speech is what the old crew swore on, around a burning boat, and then they spent the rest of their lives keeping their mouths shut and dying poor and calling it honor. And I've watched what believing costs. I've paid it myself, in installments, for eleven years. So when you ask me why we don't sell the ledger and vanish — the true answer is that selling it and vanishing is the smart play, and I can't make myself want it, and I don't yet know whether that's because I'm right or because I'm my father's daughter and we're both idiots about rivers."

Pip stared at her.

"That's the worst pep talk I've ever heard," he said.

"It's not a pep talk. It's a weather report."

"And you're still steering upstream."

"Yeah."

"Why?"

Tamsin looked at the water. The current came down at them steady and brown and endless, carrying silt from country none of them had seen, from farms and fences and drowned parishes, everything the river touched eventually ending up in the sea, nothing the river carried ever truly lost — just moved, just held, just waiting for someone downstream to pull it out and read it.

She thought about her father's compass in its sealed case, riding in her jacket pocket against her ribs. She thought about the handwriting in the margins of the ledger pages, cramped and sure, annotating the names of the dead like a man correcting a misprinted chart. Her father had known exactly what the ledger was. Had held it in his hands. Had gone back for it, or gone down with it, and either way had chosen it over the smart play, over the money, over coming home to her.

And she had spent eleven years telling herself she wasn't like him.

"Because of the names," she said finally. "Not as a speech. As a fact. There's a woman in that book — page three, fourth column — Mara Iles' mother, if the dates line up the way I think they do. The navy wrote her out of existence, Pip. No record, no wreck report, no grave. She doesn't exist anywhere in the world except in ink that's currently wrapped in oilcloth in our hold. If we sell it, she exists for whoever buys it and nobody else. If we burn it, she stops existing forever." She turned back to the wheel. "I can't be the second person to erase her. That's the whole answer. It's small and it's not noble and it doesn't pay, but it's mine, and I'm steering by it until something better comes along."

Pip was quiet for a long moment. When she glanced over, he was looking at her with an expression she couldn't file — something between respect and grief, the way young people look when they realize the grown-ups are just people who kept going.

"Okay," he said. "That answer works."

"It shouldn't. It's a terrible business plan."

"Most true things are." He picked up both cups — hers included, drained or not — and headed aft, then stopped at the hatch. "Tamsin?"

"Mm."

"When we get to wherever this ends — the source, the facility, whatever's up there making the navy this nervous—" He hesitated. "You'll tell us before you do anything stupid? Alone, I mean. You'll tell us before you do it alone."

The question sat in the morning air between them, and Tamsin understood that he already knew the shape of her, the way crews come to know a skipper — knew that her instinct, carved deep by eleven years of solo wrecks and solo debts, was to go into the dark by herself and leave the people she cared about safely ashore and safely lied to.

"I'll tell you," she said.

It wasn't quite a promise. They both knew it wasn't quite a promise. But Pip nodded once, like a man accepting partial payment on a debt, and went below, and Tamsin stood alone at the wheel as the sun came up gray behind the hills and the river bent north into country none of the charts marked honestly.

* * *

They made the upper reach by mid-morning, running the long slow pool above the Nine with the banks closing in, willow and alder and the occasional roofline of a farmstead that had given up on the flood years. Bram came up from the radio corner twice with his jaw set, reporting patrol chatter moving upstream faster than any gunboat could steam — orders propagating, checkpoints multiplying, somebody at the top of the river very interested in who was coming.

By early afternoon the sky had thickened, and the rain came on soft and steady, and the channel markers grew sparse and then wrong, and then absent altogether, as though the river were gradually withdrawing its own directions.

It was Pip who spotted the steeple first.

It rose out of the trees on the northern bank like a finger raised in warning — a slim stone spire, slate-dark with wet, standing clear of the flood line while everything around it sagged toward the water. Below it, half-drowned in the rising river, the roofs of a village showed like the backs of sleeping animals: a chapel, a row of cottages, the gable end of something larger, all of them abandoned-looking, all of them wrong in a way Tamsin couldn't immediately name.

Then she could. There was laundry on a line between two cottages. And smoke, thin and gray and deliberate, rising from the chapel's side flue.

"Somebody lives here," Pip said.

"Not somebody." Tamsin slowed the *Marrow*, eyeing the water depth off the steeple, the ruined quay below it, the one stretch of stone wall that might still take a mooring line. "Somebody who knows exactly what this river is, and stayed anyway."

She brought the dredger in against the current, gentle as setting down a sleeping child, and threw the line herself to a rusted iron ring older than any of them.

Above them, in the rain, a figure had appeared at the steeple's high window — a woman, motionless, watching them come in with the stillness of someone who had been waiting a long time and had learned not to hope about it.

She did not wave. She did not go away, either.
