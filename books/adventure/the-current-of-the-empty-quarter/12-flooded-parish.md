## 12. Flooded Parish

The steeple appeared out of the fog like a mast from a shipwreck that had decided to keep sailing.

Tamsin saw it first from the dredger's wheel, a gray finger rising off a drowned hillside, and for three full seconds she thought her mind was playing tricks—the way it did sometimes at the end of a long watch, offering up shapes the river had no business holding. Then Pip called out from the bow, voice cracking with something between wonder and unease.

"Church. There's a whole church up there."

Not a whole church. Half of one. The nave had gone under decades ago, its roofline just a dark suggestion beneath the green water, but the bell tower stood on high ground—a knuckle of hill the flood had never quite swallowed—and someone had patched its windows with plastic sheeting that caught the dying light like fish scales.

"Nobody lives out here," Bram said, coming up beside her. He said it the way he said everything now, flat and careful, a man who'd spent twenty years in navy rooms learning to make his voice into a wall. "This stretch is marked uninhabited. I checked the charts twice."

"Charts," Odile said from the engine hatch, not looking up from the fuel line she was splicing. She'd been in a black mood since the cordon, since the dredger's engines had coughed them past the navy picket by sounding, to any sonar operator dumb enough to be listening, exactly like the dead hulk she'd disguised them as. "Charts are stories people tell when they want the water to behave."

Tamsin brought them in slow. The dredger drew too much to approach the knuckle directly, so she anchored forty meters out in what had once been, according to the faded sign still legible on a leaning post, the town of Corren's Hollow—no. She squinted. *Corren's Hollow* wasn't right either. The letters had been shot through, deliberately, years ago, until only fragments survived: **R N'S H LL**.

A town whose name someone had taken the trouble to erase.

"Nobody goes ashore," she said. "We watch till dark. If there's really someone living in that tower, they've stayed invisible this long, which means they're better at it than we are, which means we don't spook them."

"And if they spook us?" Pip asked.

"Then we'll have learned something about who patches windows on drowned churches."

* * *

They watched through the afternoon. What they saw rearranged itself slowly, the way a face emerges from a crowd.

First: a rain catchment rigged along the steeple's north face, tarps funneled into barrels, all of it neat as a navy installation. Then: a smoke haze from the belfry venting sideways, thin and controlled, someone who knew how to burn without being seen. And then, as the light went copper over the water, a figure moved across the tower's gallery—a woman, broad-shouldered, moving with the unhurried economy of someone walking rooms she'd walked ten thousand times. She carried a bucket. She paused at the rail. She looked directly at the dredger, across forty meters of darkening water, and did not wave, did not retreat, did nothing at all except look, for a long time, before turning back inside.

"She's known where we are since we dropped anchor," Bram said quietly.

"Probably since we passed Weir Nine," Tamsin said. Her chest felt tight in a way she couldn't name yet. "Bram. Those charts you checked. Who publishes them?"

"Hydrographic Office. Navy contract."

"And this town?"

He was quiet a moment. When he answered, his voice had gone carefully neutral, the tone he used when the answer was worse than the question. "Corrin's Hollow. Population four hundred eleven. Struck from the register in—" He stopped. Started again. "There's no date given. The entry just ends. Like a sentence somebody walked away from."

Four hundred eleven. Tamsin turned the number over. It sat in her mind next to another number, one she'd carried since the canister, since she'd spent two nights cross-referencing her father's cramped marginal handwriting against the ledger's columns: the ferry manifest listed one hundred six souls unaccounted for. Four hundred eleven minus whatever evacuation had or hadn't happened—

"We're going ashore," she said.

"Tamsin." Odile straightened, wiping her hands. "We're carrying the only copy of something three governments would burn a country to bury, and you want to knock on the door of the one lit window in a navy ghost town."

"I want to know why the window's lit," Tamsin said. "Pip stays aboard. You and Bram too. If I'm not back by midnight, run downstream and don't look back."

Odile held her gaze for a long moment. "You always say that. 'Don't look back.' You know none of us ever listen."

"That's why I say it."

* * *

She crossed alone in the skiff, oars muffled, the canister locked below in the dredger's bilge safe where Odile had welded it shut around itself. The water over the drowned town was shallow enough that her keel whispered over rooftops; twice her oar blade struck something soft and gave her a jolt that took minutes to fade. A chimney here. A weather vane there. Streetlamps still standing in rows beneath the surface, their glass clouded green, like candles lit for a service no one had ended.

The church steps rose out of the water at an angle, the lower six drowned, and she climbed them dripping, past a door hanging open on darkness, up an interior stair that smelled of wax and river mold and, faintly, impossibly, of bread.

At the top, a trapdoor stood open onto lamplight.

"You took your time."

The woman from the gallery sat at a table near the window, a shotgun resting against the wall within easy reach—not aimed, not hidden, just present, the way a fact is present. She was perhaps sixty, gray-haired, built like the moorings she'd grown up around, and she was pouring tea from a dented pot into two cups. Two cups. She'd seen the skiff coming and made tea.

"Mara Iles," she said. Not a question. An introduction offered on her own terms. "Sit down, girl, you're dripping on my floor. Such as it is."

"How do you know my name?" Tamsin didn't sit.

"I don't." Mara set the pot down. "I know your boat. Dredger, decommissioned, hull number scraped but the weld pattern's still navy yard. I know your engineer runs her quiet enough to embarrass a submarine. And I know you came upstream past a cordon that turns back everything with a hull, which means whatever you're carrying matters more than your lives." She pushed the second cup an inch toward the table's edge. "I also know you've been diving things you shouldn't, because the navy's been asking questions in the downstream towns for a month. Questions about divers. About a woman diver." Her eyes were pale gray, the color of the water at dawn, and entirely without mercy. "So. Sit. Drink. And tell me what my mother's name is doing in your pocket."

The floor seemed to tilt. Tamsin gripped the back of the chair.

"What did you say?"

"You heard me." Mara's voice didn't rise. It lowered, if anything, going quiet and dense as deep water. "Word travels on this river the way silt does—slow, and settling on everything. Three weeks back a lock-keeper's boy sold a story to a fuel barge cook: a diver pulled a canister out of Meridian Hollow, and the canister had names in it, and one of the names got repeated downstream like a prayer. *Iles.* My name. My mother's name, Adria Iles, who was thirty-four years old when the water came, and who is not on any list the government ever published, because the government never published a list, because officially there was nothing to publish." She finally looked away, out the window, at the drowned rooftops catching the last light. "Forty-one years I've lived above her grave, girl. Forty-one years watching the water that took her. So you'll understand when I say: sit down, drink your tea, and tell me everything, because I have waited longer than you've been alive for someone to come up this river carrying the truth, and I am done waiting politely."

Tamsin sat. She drank. The tea was bitter and strong and steadied her hands more than she wanted it to.

And she told her. Not everything—not Voss, not yet, not the bargain or the betrayal still waiting downstream—but the shape of it. The wreck. The archive. The ledger of names. Her father's handwriting in the margins. Mara listened without interrupting, and when Tamsin described the chemical designation, the program, the scuttled ferry, something moved behind the older woman's face that was not surprise. Recognition. Confirmation arriving decades late.

"The sirens," Mara said at last, when Tamsin ran out. Her voice had gone somewhere far away. "That's the part nobody believes. They believed the flood—floods happen, rivers are rivers. But the sirens sounded at two in the morning, and the gates were locked. All of them. Every road out of the Hollow, chained, with men standing at them. And the ferry ran all night, lights burning, back and forth—but it only ever stopped at the company dock. Officials. Records. The strongboxes from the works. I was nine. My mother put me on the church roof with my aunt and told me to hold the baby, and she went back down for the deeds, for the papers, because papers mattered, she said, papers were how they'd prove what was theirs." Mara's hand had closed around her cup hard enough to whiten her knuckles. "The water came up faster than the papers did."

The belfry was silent. Below them, somewhere under forty years of green water, the nave of her church held its congregation.

"They didn't drown," Tamsin said quietly. "The record says the levee failed naturally. Act of God."

"The record says a lot of things." Mara turned back, and her eyes were dry and terrible. "My father spent eleven years writing letters. To the ministry, to the newspapers, to anyone with a title. Eleven years, and every reply was the same: *no record of a settlement at this location.* No record. Four hundred eleven people, and no record. He died still writing them. And do you know what I learned from him, girl? What I learned is that you can't argue with a man who says you were never born. You can only make him say it in front of witnesses."

She leaned forward.

"So. You're going to tell me what's in that canister, exactly, and how many copies exist, and who else knows. Because I've spent forty-one years keeping supplies cached along this river for the day someone came upstream with the truth—and I will get you to the source, past every gate and gunboat between here and the spring—but first you're going to look me in the eye and swear that those names see daylight. All of them. Every last one."

Outside, full dark had come down over the drowned town. Far downstream, so distant it might have been imagination, a patrol light swept the water once, twice, and went dark again.

Mara didn't even glance at it.

"They're getting closer," she said. "Which means we're done talking, and started planning. Tell me about your crew, girl. And then tell me—who else is looking for that ledger?"

Tamsin opened her mouth to answer, and found, for the first time in her life, that she was afraid to say Cassian Voss's name out loud.
