## 13. Mara's Testimony

Mara Iles made tea the way people do when they've had nothing else to offer strangers for a long time—carefully, like it was a ceremony she'd invented to keep her hands busy.

The steeple room was small and dry and smelled of dust and lamp oil. A cot in the corner. A shelf of tinned food with labels gone soft. A map of the Hollow pinned to one wall, hand-annotated, streets inked over in blue where the water had taken them. She'd been living above the floodline for eleven years, give or take. She didn't say so. The room said it for her.

She poured three cups—one for me, one for Pip, who sat by the window pretending he wasn't listening to every word—and set the kettle back on the little spirit stove.

"You came upriver," she said. "Past the cordon."

"We did," I said.

"Then you already know something's wrong at the source." She sat down across from me, knees together, hands wrapped around her cup though the tea was too hot to drink. "Everyone downstream knows something's wrong. Nobody knows what. So tell me why you're here, Tamsin Sterling, before I decide whether you're the first honest stranger in a decade or just the best-dressed vulture."

I set the canister on the table between us.

Her eyes went to it and stayed there. Something moved behind her face—not surprise. Recognition. The way you look at a door you'd stopped believing existed.

"Where did you get that?"

"The archive vault. Under the old records office, in the drowned district."

"Nobody's been down there since—" She stopped. Recalibrated. "My mother worked in the records office. Civil administration. She used to say if anything ever happened to the town, the truth would be in that vault, because the navy never thought to drown their own paperwork." Her thumb traced the rim of her cup. "They thought wrong, apparently. Or they sent someone down after it."

"They did," I said. "Eleven years ago. He didn't come back up."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Pip had gone very still by the window.

"Mara," I said, "before I open this, I need you to understand what I am. I'm not press. I'm not an inspector. I'm a salvage diver who took a contract to recover that canister for a man who wants it destroyed. I'm telling you that because you deserve to know exactly what kind of person is sitting at your table holding your mother's name in a rusted tube."

She looked at me for a long moment.

"And why are you telling me that?"

"Because my father died on that ferry job, or under it, or because of it—I don't even know which, and neither does anyone else, because everyone who might have told me swore silence and kept it until it killed them too. And because I spent twenty years thinking that silence was about money. It wasn't. It was about this." I put my hand flat on the canister. "And I think you're the only person left who can tell me what actually happened, so that whatever I do next, I do it for the right reason."

Mara set down her cup. She looked older suddenly, or maybe just tired in a way she'd gotten good at hiding.

"All right," she said. "Open it."

* * *

The ledger was worse out of the water than in it.

Odile had done what she could with the pages—rinsed them in fresh water, pressed them between boards—but paper remembers drowning. The ink had run in places, held in others, and the handwriting that survived was cramped and official on the printed lines and then, in the margins, in a different hand entirely, urgent and slanted: annotations. Dates cross-checked against tide tables. Ferry passenger counts circled. And beside certain names, small marks—a dash, a question mark, once a single word: *confirmed?*

My father's hand. I knew it the way you know a voice through a wall.

Mara read standing up, both hands braced on the table, the way you read something you expect to hit you.

"It's a manifest," she said quietly. "A partial one. These are the families from the lower parish—the river side. The Delacroix family. The Okonkwos. The Ferreiras." Her finger stopped. "This is my mother. Sefa Iles. Age thirty-four. Occupation: civil registrar." Her voice didn't break. It went flatter, which was worse. "There's a mark next to her name."

I looked. In the margin, in my father's writing: *confirmed.*

"She got off the ferry," Mara said. "That's what the mark means, isn't it. Someone verified she boarded."

"I don't know what it means," I said, which was true, and also a lie, because I could feel what it meant pressing up through the page like cold through wet stone.

Mara pulled out the chair and sat down slowly, the way old people sit, though she couldn't have been more than forty-five.

"Then let me tell you what happened," she said. "Since nobody's ever asked me before. Since asking was apparently illegal."

* * *

She talked for two hours. I didn't interrupt once. Pip fell asleep against the wall around hour one and I let him, because some things a sixteen-year-old shouldn't have to hear awake, and some things nobody should have to hear at all, but somebody has to, or they get to keep pretending they never happened.

It started, she said, three weeks before the end. Contractors arrived at the headwaters—private security, not navy, though they wore navy surplus and drove navy-pattern boats. Water tests started coming back strange. Fish dying belly-up in the spring pools, then downstream, then not dying but changing—wrong-colored flesh, lesions. The clinic filled up. Kids first. Kids are always first; their bodies are smaller and closer to the ground where the water sits.

"People complained," Mara said. "Of course we complained. We wrote letters. My mother filed them—she was the registrar, everything went through her office, every petition, every complaint, every death certificate. She kept copies. She was that kind of woman. She believed paper was a promise." A thin smile. "She was right, in the end. Just not the way she meant."

Then the sirens. One Tuesday night in October, no warning, no drill—every siren in the valley going at once, and loudspeakers: mandatory temporary evacuation, contamination event, proceed to the ferry landing. Gates locked behind them as they went. Not locked ahead of them—behind them. So nobody could go back for anything. Photographs. Documents. Medicines. Grandmother's ring.

"There were maybe four hundred of us at the landing," she said. "And one ferry. And officers with lists. And the list had maybe sixty names on it, and every name was an official or a contractor or someone related to one. They called the names. People begged. A man named Okafor tried to put his daughter on the boat and they pushed him into the water. That's the last time I saw the Okonkwo girl—she was on that ferry, waving at us from the rail, and her father was in the river, and the boat pulled away upstream toward the source facility, and the gates stayed shut."

"What happened to the rest of you?" Pip asked. He was awake after all. Of course he was.

"Nothing," Mara said. "That was the horror of it. Nothing happened for six days. They kept us at the landing, then moved us to a camp outside the valley—'for our safety'—and fed us and counted us and told us the contamination would be resolved. And on the sixth day the navy arrived. Real navy, proper uniforms, and they told us the settlement was being condemned under emergency order, structural risk, no reentry permitted, compensation schedules to follow." Her mouth twisted. "Compensation schedules to follow. Eleven years. I have received a letter. One letter. It says my claim is under review because, quote, 'no record of residency at the referenced address could be located.'"

"No record," I said.

"My mother was the registrar," Mara repeated. "Every birth, every deed, every death in that parish went through her office. There were records. There were four hundred years of records. And then there weren't." She looked at the ledger. "Because the records office is forty feet underwater, and the ferry that carried the last officials out was scuttled with the registrar's duplicates aboard—or the navy's copies of them, anyway—and the original ledger of who lived there, who complained, who died of what, went down with the town. Erasure isn't burning paper. Burning paper leaves ash. Erasure is water. Water leaves nothing."

Except, I thought, it doesn't. Not always. Not if a diver goes down against orders. Not if a man with a compass and a strongbox decides that some things float back up.

"My father dove that wreck," I said. "Eleven years ago. He brought up documents. Names. I think—" My throat closed. I made myself finish. "I think he was verifying the manifest. I think these marginal notes are his. I think he died doing it, and his crew was paid and threatened into silence, and I grew up inside that silence without knowing what it was built around."

Mara reached across the table and turned the ledger toward herself again. Found the page. Found the line.

*S. ILES — confirmed.*

"He marked her confirmed," she said softly. "Which means he found proof she boarded. Which means somewhere in that wreck or that vault there's more—boarding records, the ferry's own log, maybe the order itself." She looked up at me, and her eyes were dry and terrible. "You said a man hired you to destroy this."

"Cassian Voss."

"The shipping family?"

"You know the name."

"Everyone in the valley knew the name. Voss Marine ran the ferry concession. Voss barges hauled the contractor freight up to the source. When the town was condemned, Voss Marine bought the salvage rights to the drowned district—for pennies, from a government that had just erased it—and then never salvaged a thing. Just fenced the water and posted signs." She tapped the table. "His family didn't just know, Sterling. His family was the plumbing. They were how the program moved its material and how the evacuation moved its chosen few. Of course he wants it burned. This ledger doesn't just name the dead. It names who profited."

The lamp flickered. Somewhere below us, the river moved through the drowned parish, patient as arithmetic.

* * *

Pip slept in the bell loft. Mara insisted on taking the cot's blanket and giving us the room, and I lay on the floor with my jacket under my head and the ledger an arm's length away, and I did not sleep.

Guilt is a strange cargo. It doesn't weigh what you expect. I'd carried grief for my father for twenty years and it had settled into me like ballast—dense, familiar, mine. But this was different. This was lighter and sharper, like a hook: the guilt of having been comfortable. Of growing up poor and angry about the wrong thing. Of thinking my family's tragedy was the tragedy, when four hundred people had lost towns and mothers and names, and the difference between us was that my father had tried to do something about theirs and I'd spent my life diving wrecks for men like Voss and calling it survival.

I'd taken the contract. That was the hook's barb. Whatever I told myself now—about justice, about finishing what he started—I'd said yes to Cassian Voss for money, for the debt, for the clean slate. I'd needed a rival to buy my way back into respectability, and the price of respectability was going to be four hundred names burned in a brazier somewhere while auditors signed off and the river kept its secret one more decade.

Mara appeared beside me in the dark. I hadn't heard her move. She sat down against the wall, an arm's length away, and handed me half of something—hard bread, honey, a steeple-keeper's midnight supper.

"You couldn't have known," she said.

"That's not absolution. That's just the truth. It doesn't weigh less for being true."

"No," she agreed. "But it points somewhere. Guilt with a direction is just fuel." She chewed. Swallowed. "What will you do?"

I looked at the ledger in the dark. I couldn't see the names, but I could feel them, the way you feel current under a hull.

"Voss expects delivery in nine days. He thinks I'm coming downstream with this." I turned the bread over in my fingers. "There's a second copy. Deke's been asking questions about it—my crew caught him transmitting, and what he was transmitting about was a duplicate hidden somewhere in the Hollow. Voss wants both burned before a federal audit reaches the source country."

"And you're going to find it first."

"I'm going to find it first, and then I'm going upstream, to the source, where the master records live. Because a ledger proves people existed. It doesn't prove who killed them. For that I need the order. The signature. The whole machine, photographed and copied and spread so wide that no audit, no director, no Voss can ever stuff it back underwater."

Mara was quiet a long moment.

"My mother believed paper was a promise," she said finally. "You're describing making it true." She stood, joints cracking, and looked down at me. "There are survivors, Sterling. Scattered from here to the coast—lock-keepers, fishermen, people the system forgot because officially we never existed. Some of them have been waiting eleven years for someone to ask. If you're serious, you won't be doing this alone. You'll have a network. Eyes on every lock between here and the source. And when the moment comes—" her voice hardened, "—witnesses. Not just paper. People, standing up, saying *we were here.*"

"Why help me? You don't know me."

"I know your father's handwriting," she said simply. "I've seen it now. He spent his last dive putting marks next to our names so we'd count as real. You're carrying his work upstream." She paused at the stairwell door. "That's recommendation enough. Sleep. You leave at first light—and Sterling? Before you go, there's someone downstream you need to meet. A lock-keeper at Weir Nine who saw the ferry come through that night. He counted the passengers."

"How many?"

"Sixty-one," Mara said. "Sixty-one souls on a ferry licensed for two hundred. And he wrote the number down, because he's that kind of man too." She closed the door behind her. "We seem to breed them in this valley."

I lay back in the dark and listened to the river working beneath the floorboards, and I understood, finally, what my father had known on his last dive: that the dead don't need avenging. They need counting. And somewhere downstream, at Weir Nine, a man with a notebook had been keeping count for eleven years.

First light was five hours away. I didn't sleep. I started planning the route instead.
