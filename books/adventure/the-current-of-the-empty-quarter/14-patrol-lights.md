## 14. Patrol Lights

Bram heard the gunboat before he saw it—thirty years of sonar work had left his ears tuned to engines the way a priest's ears are tuned to silence. It was the pitch that gave it away. Smugglers ran shallow-draft outboards, high and buzzy, like wasps in a coffee can. This was different. Low, heavy, diesel-driven, with the particular harmonic of a hull designed to take punishment rather than avoid it.

He set down the coil of rope he'd been splicing and stood very still on the steeple's lower gallery, listening the way other men prayed.

"Bram?" Odile's voice came up through the hatch, low. "You've got your listening face on."

"Gunboat," he said. "Coming upriver from the Weir Nine direction. Big one. Not a patrol skiff—a proper cutter."

Above him, boots crossed the belfry floor. Tamsin appeared at the top of the ladder, Pip behind her, both of them wearing the same expression: not fear exactly, but the quick recalculation of people who had learned that bad news usually arrived with paperwork attached.

"How far?"

"Two miles. Maybe less if they're pushing current." He checked his watch out of habit, though the numbers didn't matter. What mattered was what he could hear and what he couldn't. "No second engine. No helicopter. One boat, running dark-ish—they've got running lights but no searchlight yet."

"No searchlight means they're not looking for us specifically," Tamsin said. "Sweep pattern."

"Sweep pattern," Bram agreed. "Which still puts them inside this parish inside twenty minutes, and this steeple is the only structure for half a mile with a roof above the waterline. If they're mapping occupied buildings—"

"They'll map this one first," Tamsin finished.

The canister sat where it had sat for two days, wrapped in Odile's spare tarpaulin in the corner of the gallery, looking for all the world like what it wasn't: nothing. A cylinder of salt-corroded alloy containing a partial ledger of names that the navy of Meridian Hollow had spent thirty years pretending didn't exist. Bram had held it once, briefly, when they'd transferred it from the dredger's false bilge to the church. He'd been surprised by how light it was. He'd expected the weight of it to be greater. All those names. His own hands had felt too casual around it, like carrying an urn in a grocery sack.

"We move it now," Tamsin said. "Before they anchor. Once that boat is sitting off our doorstep, nothing goes in or out of this building without being seen."

"Move it where?" Pip asked. "There's nowhere. The nave's flooded to the third pew. The crypt's underwater. Everything else is open sky."

Tamsin looked at him, and Bram watched her do the thing she did—the thing that had made her famous once and infamous twice—where she stopped seeing the church as a church and started seeing it as a container with certain properties. Her eyes went up. Way up. To the niche above the belfry arch where, in another century, someone had decided that the safest place in any building was the small stone alcove built to house the patron saint's relics.

"The reliquary," she said.

* * *

They'd noticed it their first night, all of them, and none of them had remarked on it, which Bram recognized now as its own kind of instinct. The niche was maybe eighteen inches deep, arched, set into the wall twelve feet above the belfry floor, facing away from the river. Inside it sat a stone coffer the size of a breadbox, its lid carved with a cross worn nearly smooth by weather and time. Whatever saint or fragment of saint had once rested there had been gone so long that even Mara Iles—who remembered everything about this parish, who could name the families buried under every flooded row of pews—had only shrugged when Odile asked. "Gone before my mother's time," she'd said. "Maybe gone before the water. People don't steal relics from a drowning town. There's nothing left to steal them *for*."

An empty reliquary. A box built by people who believed that some things were worth sealing in stone against fire, flood, and theft.

It was almost funny. Almost.

Odile went up first with a line, then lowered the canister, wrapped now in oilcloth and bound with cord, swinging gently against the old stone. Bram belayed from below while Tamsin guided the package into the niche, and Pip—small, quick, indispensable in ways he hadn't been two weeks ago—climbed the ladder to the belfry and then the narrow stair to the arch itself, wedging the canister back behind the coffer where the shadow swallowed it whole.

"Nobody looks up," Pip called down, breathless with something between terror and delight. "That's the rule, right? Nobody ever looks up."

"Get down before somebody looks up," Tamsin said.

Bram kept his hands on the line and his ears on the river. The gunboat's harmonic had shifted—slower now, the engine dropping into that patient surveying idle he knew from a decade of standing watches aboard vessels exactly like it. They were coming into the parish at inspection speed. Somewhere on that deck, a young officer with a clipboard was preparing to log the coordinates of a derelict steeple, note the state of its roofline, flag anything anomalous, and move on. Bram had been that officer once. He remembered how it worked from the inside: the sweep wasn't a hunt, it was a census. The navy didn't come looking for trouble in drowned country; it came counting, because counting was cheap and hunting was expensive, and the whole doctrine of the inland patrols rested on the assumption that nobody upstream had anything worth finding.

Thirty years ago, that assumption had been true. Then they'd scuttled a ferry full of civilians to bury a weapons program, and the assumption had become a lie the entire service told itself in unison.

He'd told himself a version of the same lie for eleven years, from the inside. *If it were important, someone would have said something.* That was the sentence he'd carried out of the service like shrapnel. It had taken Tamsin Sterling, of all people—Tamsin Sterling, whom he'd once testified against in a deposition he still dreamed about—to hand him a way to put it down.

"Line's clear," Odile said, coiling rope. "Canister's seated. Coffer lid's back in place. From below you'd need a mirror and a reason."

"And we don't have either," Tamsin said. "Positions. Same as the last drill. Bram, you're on the gallery with the glasses. Odile, hatch. Pip—" She paused. Pip was fifteen and had the survival instincts of a raccoon in a bakery, but he'd earned the next part. "Pip, you're with me in the vestry loft. If they board, we're salvage surveyors. We have papers. Bad ones, but papers."

"And I'm what?" Odile asked.

"You're my engineer, asleep in the dinghy because you drink."

"I don't drink."

"That's why it's convincing. Nobody suspects a sober engineer of anything."

Bram almost smiled. Almost. The gunboat rounded the drowned tree line at the edge of the old churchyard, and there was no room in him for smiling anymore.

* * *

She was bigger than he'd expected. Riverine cutter, coastal refit, the kind of platform the navy had started deploying after the Hollow scandal—not that anyone called it a scandal, not officially, not in any document Bram had ever signed or read. On paper the post-Hollow buildup was about smuggling. On paper everything was about smuggling. But he knew procurement, and he knew hull classes, and he knew that you didn't station a cutter with a mounted gun and a boarding team on a stretch of river whose principal commerce was catfish and contraband cigarettes unless you were worried about something that smoked worse than tobacco.

She came in slow, bow wake folding out in long gray fans across the flooded street, her searchlight still dark, her deck crew moving with the unhurried economy of men doing a job they'd done four hundred times. Through the glasses, Bram counted six visible crew, a coxswain, and an officer on the wing—lieutenant, junior grade, by the single thin stripe, young enough that the Hollow would be history-book material to him rather than memory.

The cutter anchored two hundred meters off the steeple, broadside to the current, and killed her main. In the sudden quiet Bram could hear everything: water lapping the flooded gravestones, the tick of the cutter's cooling engine, a radio squawking somewhere on her bridge, and—faintly, carried flat across the water—a voice. The lieutenant, giving orders. Calm voice. Routine voice.

Then the launch went over the side.

"Boarding party," Bram said quietly, without turning from the glasses. "Four plus the coxswain. Coming to us first."

"Why us first?" Pip whispered from the loft. There was a crack in the whisper. He was scared, and he wasn't hiding it well, and Bram found he loved him a little for it.

"Because we're the biggest thing above water," Tamsin whispered back. "And because the last three structures they logged probably didn't have fresh footprints on the stairs. Stay in character. Let me talk."

The launch came alongside the base of the steeple where the old churchyard steps vanished into green water. Bram watched through the glasses as a sailor in a flotation vest threw a grapnel—textbook, lazy, the throw of a man who'd done it until his shoulder ached—and hooked the stonework below the gallery. The launch rose and fell against the church like a boat against a dock, and the first boots hit the wet steps, and Bram's heart was doing something he resented deeply, which was remembering.

Eleven years ago he had climbed aboard a civilian wreck in exactly this manner. Different river, different century, same boots, same grapnel, same young officer saying *log it and move on*. The wreck had been a trawler officially lost to weather, unofficially stripped of anything that might float to the surface and testify. He'd stood in her flooded wheelhouse and seen the chart table bolted shut with government seals, and he'd asked his CO about it, and his CO had said, "Not our compartment, Bram," and Bram—twenty-six years old, career-minded, good at his job—had written *no anomalies* in the log and gone home and slept.

He'd slept fine for years. That was the part that still amazed him. The sleeping had been easy. It was the waking up that took a decade.

Boots on the gallery stairs now. He closed the glasses, set them down, and arranged himself against the rail with the boneless boredom of a man who had been awake all night on watch and resented the interruption. It wasn't even acting. He'd spent enough years standing navy watches to know exactly what a tired, irritated, harmless man looked like from the outside, because he'd been one, because he'd *been* one—

The lieutenant came up the last step and stopped.

He was younger than Bram had guessed. Twenty-four, maybe. Clean-shaven, careful, with the particular alertness of someone doing a job slightly above his pay grade and knowing it. Behind him, two sailors fanned out along the gallery with the bored professionalism of men who expected to find pigeons and rust. The fourth stayed at the boat.

"Evening," the lieutenant said. "This building occupied?"

"Salvage survey," Tamsin said, coming out of the vestry door with her papers already in hand, her voice pitched at exactly the register of a woman who had answered this question forty times and would answer it forty more. "Contracted structural assessment of flood-zone churches. You want to see the permit?"

"I want to see everything eventually," the lieutenant said, and took the papers, and Bram watched his eyes move down the page and saw the exact moment the boy decided the papers were boring. Good papers. Odile's forgery work had improved since the toll bridges.

"Structural assessment," the lieutenant repeated. "Of a church."

"Of the roofline, mostly. Diocesan insurance. Nobody wants a steeple coming down on a fisherman." Tamsin shrugged. "There's not much left up here worth insuring except things falling on people."

The lieutenant almost smiled. Bram catalogued it: the boy was human, tired, end-of-shift, wanting this entry logged as routine. Every advantage lay in confirming his expectations. Bram let his shoulders sag a degree further and scratched his jaw and did not look at the reliquary niche, which was behind him and above him and twelve feet up, and which no one had looked at, because nobody ever looked up.

Except.

One of the sailors—older than the others, a petty officer with salt-bleached eyebrows and the unhurried eyes of a man who'd swept a lot of rivers—was looking up. Not at the niche. At the bell yoke. At the beams. Working the geometry of the place the way an old tech works a space, top to bottom, out of habit older than thought. And Bram watched his gaze travel, beam by beam, rafter by rafter, descending in that methodical sweep toward the arch, toward the niche, toward—

Bram coughed.

It was a tremendous cough, a consumptive's cough, a cough with a maritime career behind it, and it bent him double over the rail and turned every head on the gallery including the petty officer's, including the lieutenant's, including Tamsin's for one flickering instant of pure murder.

"You all right?" the lieutenant asked.

"Watchman's lungs," Bram rasped. "Forty years of river fog. You want to hear something interesting? Listen to this." And he coughed again, longer, uglier, hawking and wheezing with a commitment that would have embarrassed a dying man, while the petty officer's eyes came off the arch and settled on him with faint disgust, and the moment passed, and the geometry of the gallery went back to being just a gallery.

The lieutenant handed back the papers. "Roofline's stable, then?"

"Corners are sound," Tamsin said. "Mid-span's soft. Write whatever gets you home fastest, Lieutenant."

He wrote whatever got him home fastest. The sailors clattered down. The launch pulled away, its engine whining up to speed, and Bram watched it go with his lungs burning from performance and his hands, when he finally looked at them, perfectly steady.

* * *

They should have waited. Everyone agreed later that they should have waited—waited for the cutter to weigh anchor, waited a full hour, waited for dark to deepen from gray-black to black-black. But the cutter didn't weigh anchor. She sat there off the steeple, lights dimmed to running minimum, and Bram, back on the gallery with the glasses, heard the thing he'd been afraid of hearing since the launch first touched the steps: the radio traffic changing texture. Shorter transmissions. More of them. A relayed position report going downstream, and then, twenty minutes later, a reply—and replies meant a chain of command, and chains of command meant decisions, and the decision, when it came, arrived as a second engine note on the night air, upstream this time, from the direction of Weir Nine.

Two cutters. A closing box.

"They're not sweeping," Bram said, coming down into the vestry. His voice sounded strange to him, flattened, the way it always did when the professional part of his brain took over from the person. "They're holding position. First boat's a marker. Second boat's coming down to sit on the other end of the parish. That's a containment pattern. Somebody flagged this grid."

"Flagged it how?" Odile demanded. "We've been ghosts. Cold camp, no lights, no transmitter—"

"Not us." Tamsin was quiet. Bram watched her arrive at the same conclusion he'd been circling for twenty minutes and hated watching her get there. "Somebody else in this parish. Somebody who saw us come in and made a call."

"Mara?" Pip said, and then looked stricken for suggesting it.

"No," Tamsin said, too fast, and then, slower: "I don't know. I don't think so. But it doesn't matter tonight. Tonight matters is that in"—she looked at Bram—"how long?"

"Second boat's eight miles out, running with the current. Forty minutes. Maybe fifty if they slow for the shallows past the old millrace."

"Then we leave in thirty-five, and the canister leaves with us, and we get it off this rock before the box closes." She was already moving, pulling the tarp, checking lines. "Odile, fuel state on the dinghy."

"Half tank. Enough to reach the dredger if the current's kind."

"It won't be kind. Nothing about tonight will be kind. Go."

They went up together, all five of them—Mara emerging silent from her corner of the vestry, face unreadable, saying only "I know a way down the north face, through the rood loft, if the beam holds"—and Bram took the canister's weight on his shoulders via a rope harness and started down the outside of the steeple in the dark, hand over hand, boot on wet stone, with forty minutes of grace bleeding away beneath him.

The north face was the bad face. Everyone knew it; Mara had said *if the beam holds*, and the beam was the rood-loft joist, black with sixty years of damp, and it held for Mara, and it held for Tamsin, and it held for Pip, and it held for Odile, and it did not hold for Bram.

Later, reconstructing it—and he reconstructed it many times, in many watches of many nights—he understood that the failure was his. Not the wood's. His. He'd taken the canister's weight on a harness slung for climbing, not descending, and when the joist let go with a crack like a rifle shot, the load swung him out from the wall instead of pressing him to it, and the wall was what his feet needed, and the wall was suddenly four feet of empty black air away, and then he was falling.

The river took him the way the river takes everything: completely, without malice, without interest.

Cold. The cold was total and immediate, a fist closing around his chest, and the harness dragged him down with the canister's weight and his own, and the flooded churchyard opened beneath him like a mouth. Gravestones drifted past in the murk, pale rectangles, names he couldn't read. His boots found bottom—silt, soft, treacherous—and he pushed up and the harness pulled down and the arithmetic was simple and terrible: eighty pounds of sealed ledger, one hundred and ninety pounds of ex-navy sonar tech, ten feet of rope, and one knife on his belt that might as well have been on the moon.

He got the knife out. That much he managed. His fingers were already stupid with cold, and the blade found the harness webbing and sawed, and the webbing was new, Odile's best, cut for strength, and it did not want to cut, and above him—muffled, warped, coming through water the way voices come through dreams—he could hear shouting. Tamsin's voice. Odile's voice. A splash, close, someone coming in after him, and a furious, irrational, crystal-clear thought arrived through the roaring dark:

*Don't you dare. Don't any of you dare.*

Because he knew what rescue cost in water like this. He'd pulled two men out of a flooding compartment once, in another life, and the second one had nearly taken him down too, clinging, panicking, dragging them both toward the bulkhead where the dead collected. Loyalty in the water was a currency that spent fast. He would not be the coin that bought Tamsin Sterling's death. He would rather—

The webbing parted.

He came up like a cork, gasping, alive, the knife still in his fist and the canister sinking away below him into the green dark, and he had one heartbeat—one clean, ringing heartbeat of relief at the weight gone, at the air, at the beautiful stupid gift of breathing—before the second thought landed:

The canister. The ledger. Thirty-one names, sinking into the flooded nave of a drowned church, in a parish boxed between two navy cutters, and the box was closing, and the water above him was filling with flashlight beams, and Tamsin was swimming for him with her jacket already off, and Pip was shouting from the rocks, and somewhere out on the river the second cutter's engine note dropped into that patient surveying idle, and its searchlight came on for the first time that night, a white blade sweeping the water toward the steeple, toward the splashing, toward them.

Bram got an arm around a gravestone, spat river, and shouted the only order that mattered:

"Dive! Everybody dive—it's the only way out of this light!"

And went under again, into the dark, after the names.
