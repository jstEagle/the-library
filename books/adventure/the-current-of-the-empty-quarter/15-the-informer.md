## 15. The Informer

The gunboat's wake was still rolling through the flooded nave when we found the transmitter.

It happened the way these things always happen—not with a shout but with a silence. Bram was below decks in the dredger's old engine room, running diagnostics on the bilge pumps that had saved his life two hours earlier, and Odile was up top nursing the port engine that had coughed black smoke all the way out of the parish channel. Pip was asleep in the galley, curled under a chart table like a dog that had learned not to trust beds. And I was doing what I did after every close call: inventory.

We'd been seen. That was the arithmetic I couldn't make go away. The patrol boat hadn't been sweeping—it had come straight at us, straight to the steeple, straight to the one stretch of drowned parish where a crew of nobodies was sheltering with a canister full of dead people's names. Gunboats don't find things by accident on a river that wide. Somebody had told them where to look.

So I inventoried. Rations, fuel, charts, the reliquary box with its false bottom and its cargo of paper that weighed more than lead. And then, because I'm thorough when I'm frightened, I went through the lockers aft—the ones we hadn't opened since Odile welded them shut outside Meridian Hollow, back when we were still pretending this was a salvage job.

The third locker stuck. Not rusted—stuck the way a latch sticks when someone has worked it open and shut it again without quite seating it. I got a knife blade into the seam and levered, and the door swung out, and there it was: a gray plastic case the size of a cigarette pack, wedged behind a coil of spare hose, its little green status light blinking like a pulse.

A burst transmitter. Military surplus, contractor-grade encryption. The kind of thing you buy from the same suppliers who sell sonar arrays to men like Bram's former commanding officers.

I stood in the engine room holding it in my palm and felt the deck tilt under me, though the river was flat as poured glass.

* * *

I didn't say anything right away. That's the part I'm not proud of. I climbed back up to the wheelhouse, set the case on the chart table under a folded chart, and watched my crew for an hour while the light went copper and then violet over the water.

Odile came down first, wiping grease off her hands with a rag that was more grease than rag. She clocked my face before she clocked anything else—she always does—and her eyes narrowed.

"Out with it," she said.

"Someone signaled the patrol."

She didn't argue. She didn't do the thing most people do, which is to say *no, impossible, we're careful*. She just leaned against the doorframe and looked at the four of us—me, her, sleeping Pip, and Bram coming up the companionway with a wrench in his hand—and I watched her do the same math I'd been doing.

"It wasn't me," she said simply.

"I know."

"You know how?"

"Because if it were you, they'd have found us in Meridian Hollow instead of missing us by half a mile." I said it flat, and she almost smiled, and that almost-smile hurt worse than an accusation would have.

Bram set the wrench down on the workbench very carefully, the way he did everything now, like a man relearning how much force his hands contained. "You're saying one of us called in a navy patrol on a boat we're all standing on." His voice was even. Too even. "That's a hell of a theory, Tamsin."

"It's not a theory. It's hardware." I lifted the edge of the chart. The gray case sat there blinking its patient green blink.

Nobody moved. The engine ticked as it cooled. Somewhere upstream a lock gate groaned through its cycle, a sound like a giant clearing its throat.

"That's a Type-4 locator," Bram said at last. "Contractor issue. Range is—" He stopped himself, jaw working. "Range doesn't matter. What matters is it needs a code key to pair with a receiver. You don't just find those in a locker."

"No," I agreed. "Somebody brings them aboard."

Pip had woken. I don't know when—he made no noise getting up, another skill of his—but suddenly he was standing at the foot of the companionway stairs, hair smashed flat on one side, looking from face to face like a boy watching adults argue about which parent gets the house.

"Deke," he said.

And there it was, hanging in the air between us, the name none of us had wanted to say first.

Deke Marlowe had come aboard at Meridian Hollow with Voss's contract in his pocket and a handshake for everyone and a story about being a pilot who knew the upper river. He'd been useful—that was the worst of it. He'd read the shallows like a man reading his own handwriting, gotten us through two toll bridges and a wire-fence narrows without a scratch. Useful people are how you get betrayed. Nobody ever gets stabbed by the incompetent.

"He's topside," Pip said. "Been up there since we anchored. Said he was keeping watch."

"Keeping watch," Odile repeated, in a voice that could have stripped paint.

"Bram," I said. "With me. Odile, stay with Pip and the canister. If anything happens—if Deke so much as sneezes wrong—you get that box into the water and you swim it downstream yourself. Understood?"

Odile nodded once. Bram picked his wrench back up, considered it, and left it on the bench. That told me something about him too. He wanted to be innocent badly enough to leave the weapon behind.

* * *

Deke was at the stern rail with a mug of coffee going cold in his hand, watching the last light die on the water. He heard us coming—I made no effort to be quiet; quiet would have been a lie about what this was—and he turned around with that easy half-grin that had gotten him fed at every dockside tavern from Hollow to the Reach.

"Tamsin. Coffee's fresh if—"

"Locker three, aft. Behind the hose coil."

I watched his face the way I watch a wreck come up out of green water: looking for the shape of it, the places where the truth shows through the silt. And here's the thing I'll swear to until I'm silt myself: for one full second, his face went blank. Not guilty-blank. Surprised-blank. Then the grin came back up like a diver who'd been ordered down and didn't want to go.

"That's mine," he said.

"I know it's yours. That's why it's interesting."

He set the mug down on the rail cap, slowly, giving his hands something honest to do. "Before everybody starts throwing each other to the fish, maybe you let me explain."

"We're listening," Bram said from behind me, arms crossed, filling the companionway.

Deke looked past us at the wheelhouse windows, at the shapes of Odile and Pip behind the glass, and something in his posture shifted—the charm draining out of him like air out of a punctured suit, leaving behind a tired man in his forties who'd slept badly for years.

"Voss gave it to me," he said. "In Hollow. Before we cast off."

"To signal the navy."

"To signal *away* from the navy." He laughed, short and bitter, no humor in it. "Christ, Tamsin. You think I want a gunboat finding this boat? I'm standing on it."

"Then explain the light," I said. "Explain why your locator was transmitting tonight, during the sweep, pointed at the parish."

For the first time, real fear moved across his face. Not fear of me. Fear of the answer to the question, arriving faster than he could arrange it.

"It shouldn't have been transmitting. It's keyed to a schedule—a window every third night, four minutes, short burst. Voss's auditors pick it up, confirm position, that's it. It's how he keeps tabs on his investments. Tonight isn't a window night." He pulled a battered pocket calendar from his jacket, thumbed it open to a page dense with his own cramped marks, and held it out. "See for yourself. Marked like everything else. Tonight's blank."

Bram took the calendar, studied it, handed it to me without comment. The marks were consistent. Either Deke was telling the truth or he'd prepared this defense days ago, and I couldn't tell which, and that was the whole problem with trust—it doesn't come with a gauge.

"So if tonight wasn't your window," I said, "who transmitted?"

"Nobody. Or—" He stopped. Rubbed his jaw. "Or somebody else has a unit. A second one. Voss wouldn't run just one; he's not a one-lock kind of man. If there's another box on this boat, it's not mine, and I'd very much like to help you tear this vessel apart rivet by rivet to prove it."

"You'd love that," Odile said from the wheelhouse doorway. She'd come down without a sound, Pip behind her, and now the whole crew stood in a half-circle around Deke Marlowe at the stern rail, and the river ran dark and indifferent beneath us all. "Because while we're tearing apart rivets, nobody's watching you swim."

"If I jump, shoot me," Deke said. "I mean that. I'd rather take a bullet than thirty miles of this river at night with whatever's out there that pinged that patrol." He looked at me directly, and his eyes were steady, and I hated him a little for how steady they were. "Tamsin. Ask yourself the question that matters. Who benefits? I'm on this boat. Everything that happens to this boat happens to me. Voss pays me to keep an eye on his asset, not to sink it with myself aboard. Whatever signaled that gunboat wanted us found—or wanted us flushed. Think about who wants us flushed."

"The director," Bram said quietly. "The facility. If they know what we're carrying—"

"They don't know what we're carrying," Odile snapped. "Nobody knows what we're carrying except the five people on this deck and a man in a dry suit who thinks it's paper worth burning."

Silence again. The lock gate groaned. Pip, at the edge of the group, spoke up in the small clear voice he used maybe twice a week, the one that cut through everything:

"Somebody lit us up tonight. It wasn't luck. So either it's Deke lying, or it's a second transmitter, or—" he hesitated, and I saw him decide to finish it, "—or it's somebody ashore. Mara's people. The parish. Maybe somebody there sold us."

I felt that one land in my chest like a hook. Because it was possible. Because Mara Iles had lost everyone and trusted no one, and desperate people make terrible allies and excellent informants, and I had walked into her steeple and shown her exactly what we carried.

But no. I'd watched her face when she read the names. You can fake a lot of things. You can't fake the way her hand went to her own mouth, like she was holding words in that wanted out as a scream.

"It wasn't Mara," I said.

"You can't know that," Odile said—not cruelly, just precisely, because precision was how she loved people.

"I can't. But I can rank my suspicions, and a woman who waited twenty years beside her mother's name doesn't sell it to the men who put it there." I turned back to Deke. "Here's what happens now. The transmitter comes with me. Your calendar says your next window is Thursday night. Thursday night, we transmit exactly what Voss expects to hear—position, heading, nothing else. You send your report, word for word as scripted, and if Voss's reply contains so much as a syllable that smells like a tasking, we'll know whose side the second box is on. Meanwhile, Bram strips this boat to the stringers looking for it. Agreed?"

Deke exhaled. "Agreed."

"And Deke. If I find out you're smarter than you look—if there's a third play, a fourth box, a code phrase I haven't heard—you won't get a trial. You'll get the current. Are we clear?"

Something flickered behind his eyes then, and I recognized it, because I'd seen it in mirrors: the look of a man carrying a secret heavier than the one he'd confessed. He covered it fast, but I've spent my life reading faces underwater, where nothing covers well.

"Clear," he said.

* * *

We didn't sleep, any of us. Bram tore through the hull till dawn and found nothing—no second box, no wires, no bugs—which somehow made it worse, because it meant the signal had come from somewhere we weren't, somewhere ahead of us or behind us, and the river was full of somebodies.

Near morning I found Pip at the bow, legs dangling over the anchor chain, watching the fog burn off the water. I sat down next to him. For a while neither of us said anything. That's mostly what our friendship was built on—the comfortable silence of two people who'd learned early that talk is the first thing the world takes from you.

"Do you think it was him?" Pip asked finally.

"I think he's lying about something. I don't think it's the thing he lied about."

Pip chewed on that. "My uncle used to say a crew is just a wreck that hasn't happened yet."

"Your uncle sounds like a laugh."

"He drowned." Pip shrugged, matter-of-fact, terrible. "Reservoir project. Whole town gone under in a summer. He stayed to the end, though. Said somebody had to keep the lights on so the houses knew they'd been lived in." He looked at me sideways. "Is that what we're doing? Keeping lights on?"

"Something like that."

"Then we can't afford to throw anybody overboard yet. Even him." He nodded back toward the stern, toward Deke, invisible in the fog. "Even if he's cargo."

I looked at my deckhand—sixteen years old, orphaned by an official map, wise in the specific way that only children of erasure are wise—and I thought about trust, and how on this river it had stopped being a feeling and become freight. Something you lashed down. Something you jettisoned when the water rose. Something that could shift in the night and capsize you.

"Yeah," I said. "Even if he's cargo."

Behind us, Deke's voice floated up from the stern, casual as Sunday: "Fog's lifting. We should make the old locks by noon if we push."

And I noticed—because I notice everything, it's the only inheritance my father left me that never corroded—that Deke Marlowe knew our route. Knew the locks. Knew the timing.

And I had never told him about the locks.

Not once.
