## 16. Rapids of the Old Locks

The dredger was never meant to run rapids. She was built in a government yard forty years ago to chew silt out of shipping channels, all iron belly and no grace, and every hour Odile kept her alive above the Old Locks was an argument with that fact.

"Tell me again why we didn't take the towpath," she said, hands moving across the throttle quadrant without looking, because looking was for people with spare attention.

"Because the towpath has checkpoints," Tamsin said from the wheelhouse doorway, "and checkpoints have cameras, and cameras have faces that end up in navy briefing rooms."

"And this has rocks."

"This has rocks," Tamsin agreed. "But rocks don't testify."

Odile muttered something in her mother's language that would have gotten her fined in three jurisdictions, and went back to the engine telegraph. Below her feet, through two decks of riveted steel, the old diesel turned over with a sound like a man clearing his throat before saying something he'd regret. Port bank ran hot. She'd been running hot since Smugglers' Reach, and no amount of coaxing had brought her temperature down. Odile had compensated by feeding her cooler water, richer mixture, and the kind of verbal abuse that machinery sometimes responded to.

Ahead, the river narrowed into the first of the derelict locks. The Old Locks had been abandoned when the navy rerouted barge traffic downriver—before the Hollow drowned, before everything—and nobody had spent money on them since. The gates stood open like broken teeth. Water poured through the chambers in white braided ropes, and the sound reached them a full minute before the sight did: a long rolling thunder, patient as weather.

"Bram," Odile said into the intercom. "Soundings."

"Four meters over the sill at Lock One," Bram's voice came back, calm as a man reading a grocery list. He'd been a sonar tech for nineteen years and he delivered depth readings the way other people delivered small talk. "Then it drops off fast. Chart says three-two after the gate, but the chart is older than I am."

"Nothing on that chart is older than you are," Pip said somewhere behind him.

"I heard that."

"You were meant to."

Odile allowed herself half a second to be grateful for them—the ex-navy man who'd walked away from a career rather than falsify one report, the kid who'd appeared on her deck one morning smelling of river and secrets—and then she spent the rest of her attention where it belonged, which was on the water.

Here was the thing about rivers that most people never learned: they weren't obstacles. They were arguments. Every reach of water wanted something specific, and if you listened, it told you what. This stretch wanted speed. It wanted to take their hull and slam it against the lock walls like a dog shaking a rat. Fine. You didn't argue with a river. You gave it what it wanted in the exact measure it could stand, and not one liter more.

"Tamsin. Come steer."

Tamsin crossed the wheelhouse and took the helm without question, which Odile noted and filed away. A year ago Tamsin Sterling would have argued about who steered. Now she just did it. Grief had burned a lot of things out of her, but apparently not the ability to trust.

"On my mark we go through the left third of the opening," Odile said. "Not center. Center looks clean but there's a drowned crane arm just under the surface—I can see the boil over it. Left third, then hard starboard the moment we're through, because the current will want to pin us on the downstream gate abutment."

"You've done this before?" Pip asked over the intercom.

"No. But I've read water my whole life, and this water is shouting." She gripped the telegraph. "Mark."

Tamsin put the nose into the gap and the river took them.

* * *

There were maybe eleven seconds between entering the lock cut and knowing they were going to lose the barge, and Odile spent all eleven of them working.

First second: the hull hit the standing wave at the gate throat and the whole dredger shuddered like a struck bell. Something below deck—a chain, a fitting—let go with a bang.

Third second: the stern swung six degrees wide, exactly as predicted, and Tamsin corrected with a touch so light it barely registered on the wheel. Good. Better than good. Odile had taught her that correction two years ago on a different boat in a different life, and here it was, living in her hands.

Fifth second: the boil over the drowned crane. They cleared it by less than a meter. Odile felt it more than saw it—a change in the vibration coming up through the deck plates, the water's grip shifting along the keel.

Seventh second: the tow cable sang.

That was the sound she'd been dreading since Smugglers' Reach. They'd lashed the dredger's old work barge alongside—not towing it, that would have been suicide, but carrying it nested against the port side because it held half their fuel drums and all of Bram's salvaged equipment. Nested cargo was fine in slack water. In rapids, a nested barge became a second opinion about where the vessel should be heading, and it expressed that opinion through the lashings.

The cable didn't snap. It *sang*—a rising note, thin and terrible, the sound of steel deciding how much more it could take.

"Ninth second," Odile said aloud, because counting helped her think, "we're going to lose her."

"Can't we—" Pip started.

"No. Cutting her loose now means she goes through the next gate sideways and jams it, and then we're locked in a concrete box with a flood pulse coming down the gorge. Keeping her means she takes us over when she goes." Odile was already moving, dropping down the ladder to the main deck with one hand on the rail and her boots finding the treads by memory. "Bram! Axe!"

"Which axe?"

"The big one! The ugly one!"

"That describes all your tools!"

She rounded the deckhouse and he was there, holding out the fire axe, rain and spray already plastering his gray hair flat. Behind him the barge ground against the dredger's hull with a shriek that made her fillings ache. The lashings had parted everywhere except the bow line, and the bow line was the problem—it was dragging the barge's nose around so the whole mass pivoted toward them like a door swinging shut.

"Get clear of the pivot!" she shouted, and Bram went, and she didn't, because someone had to cut the line before it wrapped the propeller shaft housing, and everyone else on this boat had people waiting for them.

She got two swings in. The third swing never landed, because the cable chose that moment to part on its own with a crack like a rifle shot, and the recoil took the loose end across her forearm and threw her against the rail.

The world simplified. There was the pain, bright and specific. There was the barge, peeling away to port, its fuel drums lashed down and its nose already shipping water. And there was the river, taking it—gently, almost tenderly, the way the river took everything—sweeping it back into the white water and downstream toward the second gate.

"My arm's fine," she said, before anyone could ask. "Barge is gone. Fuel's gone. Get below and check whether we're taking water, because when that cable parted it may have opened us up along the stringer."

"You're bleeding," Tamsin said from the wheelhouse window.

"I'm aware. Steer."

* * *

They made the second lock chamber by miracle or momentum—Odile genuinely couldn't tell which, and she distrusted both—and then the third, and then the river spat them out below the last gate into water gone suddenly, shockingly quiet.

The dredger drifted in a long green pool beneath walls of cut stone going soft with moss. Upstream, the rapids muttered to themselves. Somewhere back there, their fuel was lodged in the rocks or already in the next county.

Odile sat down on the engine room hatch, peeled back her sleeve, and looked at the welt the cable had laid across her forearm. Deep bruise, skin unbroken. She'd have a stripe like a tattoo for a month. She pressed it once, catalogued the pain, and decided nothing was broken.

Tamsin came and sat beside her without asking. That was another new thing. The old Tamsin would have stood over her, arms crossed, delivering some speech about risk. This one just sat, shoulder to shoulder, watching the water.

"Half our fuel," Tamsin said finally.

"More than half. The drums that stayed aboard are the ones I'd already flagged as contaminated. We've got maybe sixty kilometers of range at economical speed, and the source country is ninety upstream, minimum, through water that gets meaner the higher we climb." Odile flexed her fingers. They worked. "I should have cut the barge loose at the top of the reach. I knew the lashings were marginal. I kept telling myself we'd need the fuel more than we'd need the margin."

"You made the call any engineer would make."

"Any engineer would have been wrong too." She said it without self-pity, which took practice. "That's the thing nobody tells you about this work. Being competent doesn't save you. It just means you fail for interesting reasons."

Tamsin huffed something that was almost a laugh. "My father used to say competence was just love with its sleeves rolled up."

"He sounds like he stole that from somebody."

"He absolutely stole that from somebody."

They sat with it. Down in the pool, the water was so still they could see the drowned orchard below them—rows of apple trees standing in the green dark, thirty years dead and still in formation, still orchard-shaped. Odile found she couldn't look away. Everything in this country was like that: things that had been erased on paper but refused to stop existing underwater.

"Pip's making dinner," Tamsin said eventually. "It's going to be terrible."

"It's always terrible."

"He put cinnamon in the rice last night."

"I know. I ate it anyway. That's what family is." She stood, wincing at her arm. "Get me to a town with a fuel dock that doesn't ask questions. I'll get us range. That's my job. Yours is the ledger. Bram's is the listening. Pip's is the rice."

"And none of ours is resting?"

"Resting is what you do when the river lets you." She looked upstream, where the gorge walls closed in and the water ran fast and narrow between them. "This river isn't letting us do anything. So we keep moving."

* * *

They found the smuggler moorage at dusk, tucked behind a wing dam where the current slowed enough for a floating shanty-town of barges and houseboats to cling to the bank like barnacles. Lantern light on black water. Music from somewhere, out of tune and unbothered about it.

A woman with a shotgun slung across her back and a ledger book under her arm met them at the gangplank. Her eyes went over the dredger the way Odile's went over engines—professional, unsentimental, pricing everything.

"Fuel," Odile said. "Diesel. Sixty kilometers' worth, minimum. Clean, not cut with anything that'll eat my injectors."

"Clean costs."

"Everything costs. What I want to know is what it costs in something other than money, because money we have short."

The woman smiled, and it wasn't unfriendly, exactly. It was the smile of someone who had been waiting all season for customers with problems interesting enough to be worth her time.

"Money's fine," she said. "What I want is information. You came down through the Old Locks. Nobody comes down through the Old Locks unless they're headed up past Weir Nine." She tapped her ledger book. "And everybody headed up past Weir Nine lately has been either contractor or corpse. Which are you?"

Behind Odile, she heard Tamsin go very still.

"Neither," Odile said. "We're salvage."

"Salvage." The woman wrote something in her book. "Sure. Everyone up there is salvage. Tell you what—I'll sell you your fuel, and I'll throw in something free, because I like the look of your engineer's arm and I hate the contractors personally." She leaned closer, voice dropping. "The men at Weir Nine aren't navy anymore. They wear no colors at all. And three weeks back, a dredger much like yours went up that way with a crew of five, and only the river ever saw it again."

Odile felt the words land in her chest like the cable had landed on her arm—one bright, specific strike.

"Only the river saw it," Tamsin repeated quietly. "You're certain?"

"Honey," the woman said, closing her ledger, "around here, the river sees everything. Ask it nicely sometime. It answers."
