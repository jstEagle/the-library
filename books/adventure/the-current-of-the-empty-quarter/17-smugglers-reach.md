## 17. Smugglers' Reach

The smugglers' camp announced itself the way a knife announces itself: too late to matter.

One moment we were running the dredger through a channel of drowned orchards—apple trees standing hip-deep in brown water, their fruit gone soft and black—and the next there were men on the banks with rifles slung casual as fishing rods, and a woman on a flat-bottomed skiff cutting across our bow with an engine that sounded like it had been stolen from something much larger.

Odile killed our throttle. "That's a signal," she said. "We're being signaled."

"I can see we're being signaled."

"Then why are you still holding the wheel like you're going to outrun them?"

Because I'd been thinking about it. I'll admit that. The Reach narrowed ahead into a stand of dead cypress, and past the cypress I could see tarps and stovepipe smoke and at least three boats worth taking seriously. We had maybe forty liters of fuel left in cans that leaked when you looked at them wrong, a barge we'd lost back at the locks, and a crew of four including a teenager who was currently standing on the bow rail for no reason I could identify.

"Pip," I said. "Off the rail."

He came down grinning. He'd been grinning since the locks, since Odile had stood at the helm of a dying dredger and threaded us through derelict gates with the fuel gauge flatlined and half the machinery screaming, and come out the other side whooping like she'd won something instead of losing a barge. It was the grin of people who had decided they were already dead and might as well enjoy the trip. I recognized it. I'd worn it for years.

The woman on the skiff pulled alongside, close enough that I could see her face: sixty-something, sun-cured to leather, a gold tooth that caught the gray light. She looked at the dredger the way a butcher looks at a limping cow.

"You're burning diesel like a navy boat," she called up. "And you're flying no colors."

"We're not a navy boat."

"No," she agreed. "Navy boats don't look like grief." She jerked her chin toward the cypress. "You want fuel, you come to the Reach. You don't want fuel, you keep going and find out what's upstream without any."

I looked at Odile. Odile looked at the fuel gauge, which had achieved a kind of eloquent emptiness.

"We'll come to the Reach," I said.

* * *

They took our weapons at the edge of camp, which I expected, and they took our names, which I hadn't. Not real names—the woman running the ledger just wrote down whatever we gave her, and I watched her do it, watched her write *Tamsin Sterling* without blinking, and understood that the name itself was currency here. Word travels on rivers faster than boats. Somewhere between the parish and the locks, someone had talked about the diver from downriver, the one carrying paper the navy wanted.

Or about her father. That was the other possibility, and it sat in my chest like swallowed ballast.

The camp filled a bend where the river widened and slowed, a natural harbor ringed by drowned farmland. Houseboats, barges cut down to hulls, shipping containers stacked two high with walkways welded between them. A crane made of scaffolding pipe lifted crates off a scow. Kids ran the catwalks barefoot. Everything smelled of diesel, woodsmoke, and river mud—that particular Meridian Hollow mud smell, sweetish and mineral, that I'd been breathing since we entered the old city's watershed and couldn't seem to get out of my lungs.

The woman led us to a container that had been gutted into a galley and office both. A map of the upper river covered one wall, hand-annotated, layers of ink going back years. She poured coffee that tasted like it had been brewed during the previous administration and introduced herself as Sefa.

"Sterling," she said, tasting the name. "Your father ran this stretch. Before the closure."

"He did."

"Good man. Paid his tolls." She said it without warmth or accusation, just fact, the way you'd note the depth of a channel. "Bad luck, what happened to him."

"What exactly do you think happened to him?"

Sefa looked at me over her cup for a long moment. Around us the camp went on with its noise—generators, haggling, a dog losing an argument with a gull—but inside the container it got very quiet, the quiet of people deciding how much truth a stranger has earned.

"I think," she said finally, "that you should talk to me about what you're buying before I decide what you're selling."

* * *

The deal was simple enough: forty liters of clean diesel, water, provisions, and a guide through the narrows above the Reach, in exchange for the chronometer.

Not the one I'd given the harbor cop at the toll bridges—I wasn't rich enough to lose two of those. This was a ship's chronometer off the dredger, brass-cased, pre-Closure, the kind of thing that shouldn't have been on a working vessel at all except that Bram had stripped it off a wreck two seasons ago and Odile had cleaned its movement until it kept time better than anything on land. It was beautiful. It was also ballast now; the dredger ran on GPS units older than Pip and a compass I didn't let anyone else touch.

Sefa turned it under the lamp, opened the caseback, checked the movement with a loupe she wore on a chain around her neck like jewelry. "This is worth more than the fuel."

"The fuel is worth more than the fuel. Upstream prices are upstream prices."

"They are." She closed the caseback gently, the way you'd close a coffin lid. "And the guide is extra."

"Extra?"

"You want to go up past Weir Nine, you don't want my guide, you want a priest." She set the chronometer down between us. "But first. Tell me what you're actually doing, because I've had three parties through here this season asking after the source country, and two of them worked for the same company, and neither of them was navy."

The word landed and sat there. *Company.*

"Navy runs the source," I said. "Everyone knows that."

Sefa smiled, and the gold tooth did its work. "Everyone knows what everyone knew ten years ago. Sit down, diver. You've been swimming in old water."

She spread the annotated map on the table and put her finger on the headwaters, the fenced blank space where every official chart simply stopped drawing details—a cartographic shrug, miles of nothing above the last lock.

"This is the navy station. Was. Closed it officially—" she tapped a date written in faded ink, "—here. Decommissioned, sealed, listed as an environmental remediation site. That's the story your father would have known. But remediation sites don't need this." Her finger moved to a cluster of marks she'd added herself, fresh ink, within the last year by the color. "Barges. Fuel convoys. Armed contractors rotating on thirty-day tours. And this—" a mark near the discharge channel, "—a private security firm called Corvane Group. You know them?"

I did. Everyone in salvage knew Corvane. They guarded pipelines, mines, cable landing stations—anywhere valuable met remote. They were good, expensive, and utterly deniable.

"That's not a remediation site," I said.

"That's a purchase," Sefa said. "Somebody bought the secret. Bought it whole—site, records, access, the works. Navy keeps the fence on paper so the maps stay clean, but the guns inside the fence rent by the quarter. My people run cargo up that stretch. We see the faces. Those aren't sailors, diver. Sailors complain about the food. These ones don't complain about anything."

Odile, who had drifted in halfway through and was leaning against the doorframe with her arms crossed, said: "Who buys a secret?"

"Someone who has to own it," Sefa said. "A secret nobody owns is a leak waiting for a price. Somebody paid to make sure the price never gets set." She looked at me. "Which means somebody thinks there's something down there worth more than what they paid. Or worth more than their freedom, if it ever surfaces."

I thought about the canister lashed in the dredger's hold under false flooring, wrapped in oilcloth, full of names. Names and dates and a chemical designation my father had annotated in his cramped, patient hand. I thought about Voss's grandfather's signature on the order that had sunk a ferry full of civilians, if Bram's decrypted fragment told the truth I believed it told. And I thought about Cassian Voss somewhere behind us on the river, wanting that paper burned, and wondered—not for the first time—who he answered to besides his own shame.

Shareholders, I thought. The conspiracy has shareholders. Shame doesn't burn that hot on its own.

"How many guns?" I asked.

"At the facility? Nobody counts. On the water approaches? Two patrol skiffs, Corvane-marked, plus a checkpoint at Weir Nine that isn't on any chart I'll show you." She rolled the map closed. "My guide can take you past the skiffs. Nobody takes you past Weir Nine by water anymore. Not since spring."

"What happened in spring?"

"A man tried. Cargo runner, good man, ran this stretch twenty years. They found his boat burned at Nine and him—" she paused, and for the first time the merchant fell away and something older and wearier looked out of her face, "—they didn't find him. His family lives on a houseboat two berths down. His daughter runs his route now, but she stops at Nine. Everybody stops at Nine."

The container felt smaller. Outside, the camp noise went on, indifferent, and I understood suddenly that this whole floating town existed in the shadow of that blank space on the map, that every barge load and fuel drum and smuggled crate moved through a corridor owned by men whose names appeared nowhere, guarding a wound nobody was allowed to name. My father had died in that corridor. The Hollow's dead had died in it. And the people profiting had never once gotten wet.

"Sefa," I said. "The ferry. The one that went down off the Hollow. Do your people remember it?"

The silence stretched long enough that I heard the generator change pitch outside.

"We remember," she said quietly. "We remember everything on this river. That's most of what we are." She picked up the chronometer, weighed it, and put it in her pocket. "Fuel, water, provisions, and my guide to the narrows. After that you're on your own current." She stood. "One thing free, diver, since you paid fair and your father paid fairer."

"What's that?"

"The company men came through asking questions too. Three weeks back. Two of them, Corvane contractors, civilian clothes, very polite." She held my eyes. "They weren't asking about the source. They were asking about a woman diving restricted wrecks downriver. Description matched you close enough to make my skin crawl. And they asked one question I haven't stopped turning over since."

"Which was?"

Sefa walked me to the container door. Evening was coming down over the Reach, lamps lighting along the catwalks, the river gone the color of hammered tin.

"They asked whether the woman traveled alone," she said. "And when I said I hadn't seen her, they said, tell her the debt comes due at the source either way. Either way. Like it was already decided which side of it she'd be standing on."

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat in the dredger's wheelhouse with the lamp off and the canister's weight somewhere below my feet, and I listened to the Reach settle into night—diesel generators dropping to idle, a baby crying two houseboats over, the river itself making its endless argument with the banks.

*The debt comes due at the source either way.*

Voss's phrase. I knew it was Voss's because it was the kind of thing he said—elegant, fatalistic, designed to sound like fate while being a threat. He'd used versions of it on me since we were nineteen years old and competing for the same salvage contracts, both of us hungry, both of us pretending we weren't. *The river settles all accounts, Tamsin.* He'd said it the day my father's boat came back empty.

But Voss didn't have Corvane contractors. Voss had Deke, and money that was old rather than deep, and a family name he wanted to save. Contractors meant budget lines. Budget lines meant someone upstream signing off, someone whose pension and position depended on the blank space on the map staying blank.

Unless Voss had found deeper pockets. Unless the shame I'd seen in him at the tavern, real as it was, had simply been outbid.

Pip found me there past midnight. He didn't knock; he never knocked, a habit I'd stopped correcting because I suspected it came from a childhood where knocking got answered badly. He climbed into the wheelhouse, dropped onto the bench across from me, and sat with his knees up, watching the dark water.

"You heard," I said.

"Camp this size, everybody hears everything." He was quiet a moment. "My town's on a map like that."

I looked at him.

"Back home. The reservoir project. There's a version of the map with our town on it, in the county office, and there's the version everywhere else, where it's just—blue." He shrugged, elaborately casual, fifteen years old performing indifference. "Blue eats things. Whole towns. Nobody asks where they went."

"Pip—"

"I'm not asking you to turn around." He picked at a flake of rust on the bench. "I'm telling you I know what it is we're carrying. It's not paper. It's the part of the map that got eaten." He stood, stretched, headed for the door, then paused. "Mara says the names have to go somewhere. She says that's the whole job. Not justice—she doesn't believe in justice, she grew up on this river—but somewhere. A place for the names to live."

"And what do you say?"

He grinned, that already-dead grin, though it looked different tonight. Softer at the edges.

"I say we need more fuel than forty liters," he said, "if we're going to deliver mail to the dead."

* * *

We cast off before dawn, guided by a narrow, silent woman named Yara who piloted a skiff ahead of us through the narrows, reading the water like scripture. The fog lay thick on the river, and the drowned orchards slid past on either side like a congregation, black trees standing in gray water, and somewhere behind us the Reach woke up and began its day of moving everything except the truth.

At the mouth of the narrows, Yara brought her skiff alongside one last time. Through the fog ahead, faint as a bruise, I could see the shape of the high ground rising toward the headwaters—and, just visible on its flank, a light that blinked in a rhythm no lighthouse ever used.

"That's Nine," she said. "Checkpoint. Nobody stops at Nine." She handed Odile a folded chart, hand-drawn, showing a portage route overland around the weir through country the charts left blank. "Walk it. Take you a day. Lose the dredger or hide it—your choice, but Corvane's drones fly the waterline, not the ridge."

"Yara," I said. "The men who came through asking about me. Which way did they go?"

She looked up at the blinking light in the fog, and then at me, and her face said everything her employer had declined to.

"Upstream," she said. "Three weeks ago. They're still there."
