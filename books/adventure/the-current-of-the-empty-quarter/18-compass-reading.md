## 18. Compass Reading

The river went quiet after Smugglers' Reach.

Not still—rivers this far upstream were never still—but quiet in the way a room goes quiet when someone important enters it. The water narrowed between slate banks, running green-black and fast, and the dredger's engine settled into a low working hum that Odile swore was the happiest sound it could make. Above us the gorge walls rose sheer and wet, streaked with old flood lines like tide marks on a drowned man's chest.

I stood at the wheel through the middle watch because I didn't trust anyone else to hold us against the current, and because standing at the wheel was easier than sleeping. That had been true for eleven years. Some people grieve in bed. I grieved standing up, hands on wood, eyes on water.

The compass sat in my breast pocket where it always sat, riding over my heartbeat like a second, smaller heart.

My father's compass. Brass cased, glass cracked in one corner, the needle housing fogged with forty years of condensation that no amount of drying ever cleared. He'd given it to me the morning of his last run. I'd been fourteen and furious about something—I couldn't even remember what now, only that I'd taken it without saying thank you, which was the last thing I ever took from him without cost.

*It doesn't point north,* he'd told me, tapping the case. *Not really. It points home. You'll know the difference when it matters.*

For eleven years I'd assumed that was just a thing fathers said. Sentiment dressed up as instruction. I'd used it as a compass anyway, because it was his, and because a compass that pointed mostly north was better than no compass at all, and because holding it was the closest thing I had to holding his hand.

Then I'd found his handwriting in the margins of a ledger of dead people, and everything I thought I knew about what he'd left me had started to shake loose.

"Skipper." Pip's voice from the companionway, careful. "Odile says we've got maybe four hours of flat water before the next set of locks. She says you should sleep. I said I'd tell you." A pause. "She also said if you don't sleep she's going to start calling you Sterling instead of Skipper, and nobody wants that."

"Tell her I heard her."

"You want me to tell her you *obeyed* her?"

"Pip."

"Going." But he lingered at the top of the steps, sixteen years old and all elbows, silhouetted against the running lights. "You've been holding that pocket all night," he said. "Like it's going to bite you or fly away."

"It's not going to do either."

"So open it."

I looked at him. He looked back with the particular fearlessness of kids who have already lost the things most people are afraid of losing. His town was a reservoir now. He knew what it was to have your history filed under infrastructure.

"I will," I said.

He nodded once, satisfied, and went below, and I was alone with the wheel and the water and the small brass weight of everything my father hadn't told me.

* * *

Here is what I knew by then, laid out in the order I'd learned it:

The ferry *Corvid's Rest*, missing from every naval record, scuttled deliberately in Meridian Hollow with civilians aboard—civilians whose names appeared nowhere because the settlement they came from appeared nowhere. A chemical designation, VX-series adjacent, that my father had annotated in his own hand: *not weapons-grade. Worse. Persistent.* A program run out of a research station at the headwaters, buried under a fenced facility, its costs shared among men who preferred their money clean and their conscience outsourced. And Cassian Voss, whose grandfather had signed the order, offering me my father's cleared name in exchange for the ledger's destruction—as though a name were a debt that could be paid off, as though clearing it wouldn't just be another entry in somebody else's book.

And underneath all of it, the question I'd been carrying since the vault: how did my father get into that archive? How did he annotate a ledger he should never have seen?

Because he'd been there before me. That was the answer I kept circling. He'd been inside the Hollow before the navy flooded it, or inside the program's records somehow, and he'd come home changed, and then he'd gone back for the job that killed him, and the crew had burned a boat and sworn silence rather than say why.

The compass had been in his pocket on that last run. Mara Iles had confirmed it—he'd shown it to her mother once, up in the parish, turning it over in his fingers like a man checking a watch. *He said it was the only honest instrument left,* Mara had told me, around the fire in the steeple. *Her mother laughed at him.*

An honest instrument. I'd been carrying an honest instrument for eleven years and using it to find wrecks.

I brought the dredger through a bend, past a collapsed fish weir, the current shouldering us sideways and Odile's engine answering, and when the channel straightened I lashed the wheel and took the compass out of my pocket and held it under the binnacle light.

The brass was warm from my body. It always felt warmer than it should, like something living. The crack in the glass caught the light and threw a thin white line across the dial. The needle swung lazily, agreeing with the river's pull more than the pole's, pointing vaguely upstream and slightly wrong, the way it always had.

*It points home. You'll know the difference when it matters.*

I found the seam.

You have to understand—I'd handled this object ten thousand times. I'd dropped it, dried it, oiled the hinge of the lid, replaced the cracked glass cover twice. I knew its weight to the gram. And in all those years I had never once looked for a seam, because you don't look for seams in a gift. You don't take apart the last thing your father put in your hands. Taking it apart would mean admitting there might be nothing inside but parts.

But my father had been a salvage man, and salvage men don't hide things in obvious places, and they don't leave messages in words when instruments will do. *It doesn't point north. Not really.* He hadn't been being sentimental. He'd been leaving me a fault report.

The seam ran along the bezel, hairline-fine, filled with eleven years of salt. I worked a thumbnail into it. Nothing. I got Odile's smallest pick from the chart table drawer—the one she used for corroded terminals—and set it in the groove, and my hand was shaking so badly the pick chattered against the brass like a tiny telegraph key.

*Steady,* I told myself, in his voice, because whose else would it be.

The bezel gave with a click I felt in my teeth.

Inside, beneath the needle card—which was not a needle card at all but a printed disc sitting loose over a flat cavity—there was no mechanism. No pivot, no jewel bearing, no magnet. Just a shallow recess machined into solid brass, and lying in it, sealed under a disc of mica, a strip of microfilm the size of a matchstick, dense with lines of characters too small to read by eye.

The compass had never pointed anywhere. There was nothing in it to point. For eleven years the needle had been moving on nothing but the river's iron pull and my own wishful physics, and my father had known, and he'd let me believe, because the believing was the point. Carry this. Keep it close. Don't open it until you're ready to know what I knew.

*I'll know the difference when it matters.*

It mattered.

I stood at the wheel of a stolen dredger in the dark above Smugglers' Reach and I laughed, once, a broken sound that scared a heron off the bank, and then I did what I'd been doing since I was fourteen, which was cry without making any noise about it, tears running hot down a cold face while the river ran on indifferent below me.

* * *

Bram had the reader rigged within the hour—a salvaged document scanner cannibalized from the dredger's dead navigation suite, its output patched to a screen mounted crooked on the bulkhead of the wheelhouse. He worked without questions, which was Bram's way; he'd spent enough years in navy signals to know that some things you decrypt first and ask about after.

"Microfilm strip, standard archival format," he said, feeding it through with cotton gloves. "Whoever cut this knew what they were doing. This isn't camera film and a darkroom. This is professional duplication stock." He glanced at me. "Your father have contacts in records?"

"My father had contacts everywhere. That was the problem."

The screen flickered, resolved, and filled with text.

It was a cipher key.

Not a message—a key. Column headers, substitution tables, date conventions, a checksum protocol. Everything needed to read the ledger's encrypted annotations, the ones my father had written in a shorthand I'd assumed was personal habit, the ones that had looked like marginalia and were actually a full second layer of documentation, legible only to someone holding this strip.

"He encrypted his own notes," Bram said slowly. "On a ledger of victims' names. He built a lock around the truth and then he—" Bram stopped. His jaw worked. Ex-navy men don't finish sentences like that easily. "Then he hid the key in a gift for his daughter."

"He didn't hide it," I said. My voice came out strange, level, underwater-calm. "He *gave* it. Eleven years ago. On a dock. With instructions." I touched the screen, over the column header, over the designation repeated there in the same chemical notation as the canister. *Persistent. Bioaccumulative. Do not allow public record.* "He just couldn't tell me what he was giving me. Because telling me would have made me a target, and I was fourteen, and he was already—" 

Already what? Already marked. Already followed. Already a man who showed a stranger's wife his compass and called it the only honest instrument left, because he was carrying proof of a massacre in his pocket and he needed one person outside the program to know he had it.

"He was already out of time," I finished.

Bram said nothing. On the screen, the key scrolled, line after line, patient as sediment.

* * *

I decoded the ledger's margins that night, alone at the chart table, while the crew slept and the autopilot held us in the slow water above the Reach.

It took hours. The cipher was elegant but not cruel—my father had built it to be solved by someone with patience and grief and a reason, not by a cryptographer with a deadline. Every annotation resolved into plain language. Dates of discharge tests. Quantities. Disposal sites—three of them, all underwater, all chosen because rivers forget. And names—not just the victims' names from the ledger proper, but the other kind. Officials who signed. Contractors who hauled. A purchasing chain that led, page after page, toward a single family name that I read four times before I let myself understand it.

Voss.

Cassian's grandfather had signed the disposal orders. Had signed the ferry order. Had signed, in his own careful hand, the authorization that turned a civilian settlement into a non-place, a hole in the map, a current with no record.

I sat back from the screen. The lamp hummed. Somewhere below, Odile turned over in her bunk and the hull ticked as it cooled.

My father had known all of it. Had carried all of it. Had come home from the Hollow knowing, and had gone back anyway—for the full ledger, for the master copy, for whatever would make it undeniable—and had died in the water with it, and his crew had burned the boat and sworn silence because the alternative was following him, and they had children, and I was one of the children.

Every rule I'd ever lived by—never dive the Hollow, never ask about the ferry, never say his name near water—every one of them had been a wall built around this. Around a strip of film in a compass, waiting.

He meant for someone to finish this.

Not necessarily me. Maybe he'd hoped I'd never need to open it. Maybe the gift was a failsafe, a letter to be opened only in the event of his death becoming unbearable to ignore. But he'd built it so it *could* be finished—by me, by anyone I trusted, by whoever the compass found its way to. He'd made the truth portable. He'd made it survivable. That was the inheritance: not the grief, not the debt, not the name. The means.

I wiped my face with the heel of my hand and keyed the final checksum, and the screen confirmed what I already knew in my marrow: the key matched the ledger. Complete. Valid. My father's last work, finished by his daughter at a chart table on a stolen dredger, eleven years late and exactly on time.

"Okay, Dad," I said to the empty wheelhouse. My voice cracked in the middle and I let it. "Okay. I'm picking it up."

Outside, the current ran fast and green toward the headwaters, toward the fence, toward the facility bleeding its poison into the spring itself. Toward the wound at the world's beginning.

I took the wheel again and turned our bow upstream.

Below decks, a hatch banged, and Deke's voice drifted up—easy, joking, asking Pip to pass the coffee—and I noted it the way you note a change in the water's color over a wreck you haven't mapped yet. Something about the rhythm of him was half a beat off tonight. Something about the way he'd asked, twice now, exactly how far it was to Weir Nine.

But that was a problem for daylight. Tonight I had a heading, a key, and my father's voice in my hands, and for the first time in eleven years the compass in my pocket weighed exactly what it was: not a memory.

A mission.
