## 19. Deke's Confession

They tied him to the dredger's mast because there was nowhere else to put him.

Deke Marlow had been tied to worse things in worse places—a piling off Cane Reach, a radiator in a Harrow Street basement, once, memorably, a carousel horse while a man named Fitch explained interest rates to him with a length of hose. The mast was almost dignified. The river slid past below him, brown and patient, and the morning fog came off it in rags, and nobody had hit him yet, which by his personal accounting made this the fourth-best interrogation of his career.

The problem was that he deserved this one.

He'd known that for three days now, ever since the patrol lights swept the parish and he'd felt the transmitter buzzing warm against his ribs like a wasp in a matchbox. He'd told himself the signal was short enough, coded enough, deniable enough. He'd told himself Voss would never let it come back on him. He'd told himself a lot of things at two in the morning over bad coffee, and every one of them had the structural integrity of wet paper.

Tamsin Sterling came up the deck toward him and stopped just outside arm's reach, which told him she'd thought about what he could do with arm's reach. Smart woman. Terrifying woman. She looked like she hadn't slept, and she looked like sleep was something other people needed.

"Odile wants to put you ashore," she said. "Bram wants to put you under. Pip voted for the mast until further notice."

"Kid's got instincts."

"Pip's twelve." She pulled a crate over and sat on it, forearms on her knees, close enough that he could see the salt-crack in her lips and the thing underneath her face that wasn't tiredness at all. "So talk fast, Deke. Why did you signal a navy gunboat to my position?"

"I didn't." He held up his bound hands as far as they'd go. "I swear on my mother, who is alive and terrible and will find you if you doubt me. I signaled *away* from you. I gave them a bearing four miles south and a vessel description that matches half the boats on this river. That was the job. Misdirection. You want to check the transmitter log, check it. I sent one burst, encrypted, and then I was supposed to drown the unit in the cistern."

"You were supposed to."

"The cistern had a lock on it." He shrugged as much as the ropes allowed. "I was going to come back for it. I'm telling you the truth, Sterling, and I know how that sounds coming out of my particular mouth."

She didn't blink. "Whose job, Deke?"

And there it was. The question he'd been circling since the parish, since before the parish, if he was honest, since the day in Voss's office when the old man had slid a photograph of Tamsin across the desk and said *she'll lead us to it* and Deke had said *to what?* and Voss had said nothing at all, which was its own kind of answer.

"Voss," Deke said. "Cassian Voss hired me six weeks ago. Before you ever took his contract. Before Meridian Hollow. Maybe before he even asked you—maybe asking you *was* the plan, I don't know how his mind works anymore. But I wasn't tracking you. I was tracking the ledger."

* * *

The fog thickened while he talked, and Odile drifted near the rail pretending to work on a winch that did not need working, and Bram stood at the gangway with his arms crossed like a door that had learned to frown. Pip was supposed to be below but was absolutely not below; Deke could hear the kid breathing behind a coil of rope. Fine. Let the whole crew hear it. He was done carrying it alone.

"Voss has auditors," Deke said. "Not accountants—auditors. Men who audit things that don't officially exist. Family retainers, third generation, same as him. When the navy announced a records review of the Hollow disposal—internal, quiet, the kind that finds nothing ninety-nine times out of a hundred—the family panicked. Because their name isn't just on some paperwork, Sterling. Their name is *in* the program. His grandfather signed things. Real signatures, real dates, on documents that were supposed to have burned with everything else."

"So he hires me to recover the archive," Tamsin said slowly, "and hires you to—"

"To make sure there's only ever one copy. The one you bring him. He wanted your salvage operation to do his burning for him, with your hands, so it never touched his. Clean chain of custody: diver recovers document, document is destroyed, no fingerprints. And me on the periphery making sure nobody else had gotten there first." Deke worked his wrists against the rope, not to escape—he'd stopped wanting to escape somewhere around dawn—but because his hands needed a job when his mouth was doing this much work. "There was supposed to be nobody else. That was the intelligence. One archive, one vault, one canister. Then you cracked it open and found it already breached, and Voss went gray as drowned meat on the comm call, and I knew the intelligence was wrong."

"Breached by whom?"

"That's the part I couldn't give him, because I don't know. But here's what I can give you, and it's why I'm still tied to this mast instead of swimming for it." He leaned forward as far as the ropes let him, and lowered his voice even though there was no one on this stretch of river but herons and the dead. "There's a second copy."

The winch stopped. Odile wasn't pretending anymore.

"Voss's grandfather kept duplicates—that was his whole method, insurance against everyone including himself. The program copy went into the facility. The ferry copy went down with the ferry, or so everyone believed until your father's crew started pulling things up twenty years ago. And the family copy—" Deke exhaled. "The family copy went back to the Hollow. Hidden in the district, before the flooding. In a place only the grandfather knew, and he died without telling anyone where, except he left a sealed letter with the family solicitor, to be opened 'when the water gives up its dead.' Everyone assumed that meant never. Then the audit notice came, and Voss opened the letter."

"And?" Tamsin's voice was very level, the way water is level right before it goes over the edge.

"And the letter says the second ledger is in the Hollow. Physical location, described in full. A strongroom under the customs house, behind a wall that used to be above the waterline and isn't anymore. Voss has the location. What he doesn't have is divers he trusts, because—" Deke gestured at himself with his chin. "Because the last people he trusted are you, and me, and look how that's going."

"Why tell me this?" Bram said from the gangway. First words all morning. "You're confessing to a crew you sold out. What's your angle, Marlow? There's always an angle."

"Yeah," Deke said. "There is. Here it is." He took a breath, and it hurt going in, the way truth always does after years of breathing lies, which are cheaper and fit better. "Voss didn't hire me to mislead the navy forever. He hired me to mislead them *until the ledgers were destroyed*. Both copies. And then—" He looked at Tamsin directly, because she deserved to hear it looking at her. "Then the loose ends get tidied. That's what 'auditors' means, Sterling. It means men who make problems stop existing. Your father was a loose end. The Hollow was a loose end. You're a loose end with a boat and a witness list, and when the second copy comes up out of that strongroom, Voss doesn't need a salvager anymore. He needs a story with an ending. Diver lost to the river. Tragic. Unfortunate. Nobody asks questions about rivers."

The silence after that had weight. Somewhere downstream a lock horn sounded, long and low, and nobody moved.

"He offered you my father's name," Tamsin said finally. "Cleared. Restored. I know, because he offered it to me too."

"He offers everybody the thing they want most. It's not even lying, really. It's inventory." Deke sagged against the ropes. "I took the job because the money was good and the work was easy and I told myself nobody gets hurt watching a paper trail. Twenty years in this business, and I still write that sentence like it means something. But I've been on the wrong end of a tidy-up before, Sterling. I know what the ending of that story looks like from the inside. So when the gunboat came up the parish channel and I realized I wasn't misdirecting the navy away from you—I was holding the door open for whatever comes after—I sent the false bearing and I started figuring out how to get on the right side of this boat."

"You could be lying," Odile said. Not unkindly. Which was somehow worse.

"I could. Check everything. The transmitter log, the burst timing, the encryption key—it's Voss-family cipher, third variant, Bram can crack it if he still remembers the navy's files on them, and he does, because I've seen him look at the name Voss like it owed him money." Deke turned back to Tamsin. "But ask yourself the question that actually matters. If I'm lying, you lose a day. If I'm telling the truth, there's a copy of the ledger sitting in a flooded strongroom under the customs house, and Cassian Voss knows exactly where it is, and the only reason it's still dry is that he hasn't got hands he trusts to reach it."

"Until he does."

"Until he does. And he will. Voss doesn't quit, Sterling. Quitting is for people who can live with themselves. He's spent his whole life buying his way out of that particular requirement."

* * *

They cut him down eventually—not free, but mobile, roped to the rail with enough slack to sit, eat, and be watched. Odile brought him coffee that tasted like engine coolant and regret, which he understood was a promotion. Pip materialized nearby with the studied casualness of a twelve-year-old who had definitely heard everything and would definitely repeat all of it, and Deke let him, because kids should learn early that the world runs on information and most people guard the wrong kind.

Tamsin stood at the wheel for a long time, saying nothing, watching the current take the dredger around the bend. Deke knew that silence. It was the sound of a person re-pricing everything they owned.

When she finally spoke, she didn't turn around.

"My father dove the Hollow," she said. "Before the flooding. His logs had the customs house in them. Marked, annotated, flagged—'structural survey, sub-levels.' I thought it was salvage assessment." Her knuckles whitened on the wheel. "It wasn't. He was mapping it. He was finding places to hide things."

"Or finding where someone else hid them," Deke said. "Your father annotated the margins of that ledger, Sterling. In his own hand. He wasn't just carrying the names. He was *working* them. Cross-referencing. Maybe he knew about the second copy. Maybe the second copy is why he went back."

She was quiet a moment longer. "You're saying my father died over a piece of paper that has a twin."

"I'm saying the dead don't file duplicates for fun. Somebody wanted that record to survive the flood, the fire, and the family. Two copies, two hiding places, two chances." He shrugged. "That's not paranoia. That's craftsmanship."

Tamsin turned from the wheel then, and Deke saw the decision land in her face before she said a word—the specific stillness of a woman who has stopped choosing between options and started choosing between obligations.

"We split," she said, loud enough for the whole deck. "Odile, Bram, Mara—they take the original canister and run it downstream, out of Voss's reach, to people who can copy it and spread it. Pip and I go back upstream. Back to the Hollow."

"The customs house is inside the navy exclusion zone," Bram said. "Patrols every forty minutes. Sonar sweeps the submerged streets twice a day."

"Then we dive between sweeps." She looked at Deke, and there was no warmth in it, but there was something more useful than warmth. "You know Voss's methods, his cipher, his timetables, and his auditors. You're going to earn your keep, Marlow. Every hour, out loud, everything you know. And if you signal anyone—if you breathe sideways—I won't tie you to the mast."

"No?"

"I'll give you to the river. It's owed more than it's collected." She said it without heat, which was the frightening part, and then she turned back to the wheel and the fog and the long brown current running north, and Deke understood that he had just been promoted from prisoner to instrument, and that instruments get used up.

He was fine with that. It was more than he'd expected, and less than he deserved, and in his long professional experience those two amounts usually arrived in the same envelope.

Behind the coil of rope, Pip whispered to himself, rehearsing the story he'd tell later. Deke closed his eyes and listened to the river, and somewhere far downstream, in a flooded customs house behind a drowned wall, a dead man's duplicate waited in the dark—and so, he'd have bet his last clean shirt, did somebody else.
