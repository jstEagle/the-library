## 20. Split Current

The dredger sat low in the water at the mouth of Smugglers' Reach, tied off to a rotted piling that groaned every time the current shifted. Below decks, the lamp swung on its hook and threw shadows across the chart table where the ledger canister lay open, its contents spread out like evidence at a trial.

Which, Tamsin thought, was exactly what it was.

She'd laid the photostat pages in rows—names, dates, the chemical designation that Bram had finally decoded down to a facility designation: SOURCE STATION NINE, HEADWATERS OF THE MERIDIAN. And beside them, Deke's confession still hanging in the air like diesel smoke: *there's another copy. Hidden in the Hollow. Voss's auditors want both burned.*

Odile stood with her arms crossed, grease to the elbows, staring at the pages as if she could will them into order. Bram sat hunched over his salvaged sonar rig, which he'd repurposed into a decryption station and which now did nothing but hum at them accusingly. Pip perched on an ammunition crate near the door, too young for this room, too stubborn to leave it.

"So," Odile said. "Two copies. Two directions."

"Two directions," Tamsin agreed.

"Voss has the original." Bram's voice was flat. He still hadn't fully forgiven himself for nearly drowning in the parish, and he covered it by being merciless with facts. "He took it off us downstream while we were playing nursemaid to Mara's photostat. Which means if we go back for the Hollow copy, Voss is already ahead of us toward the source. And if we go upstream, whoever his auditors send for the Hollow copy gets there first."

"Unless they don't know exactly where it is," Pip said.

Everyone looked at him.

"Deke said copies," Pip went on, picking at the crate's splintered edge. "Not locations. Voss hired him to *track* them, right? That takes time. Surveys, informants, old-timers who remember where people hid things before the water came up. We've been in the Hollow two days. They might not have found it yet."

"He's not wrong," Bram admitted.

"He's usually not wrong," Odile said. "That's what worries me."

Tamsin let them talk. This was the part nobody taught you about running a crew—that the captain's real job wasn't giving orders. It was standing still long enough to hear what her people already knew, and then choosing which truth to betray.

Because that was what it came down to. Whichever way she pointed the dredger, she was going to abandon something. A copy of the ledger left to burn, or the source left running, pumping whatever it pumped into the spring that fed the whole river—the river that fed Meridian Hollow, the flooded parishes, the smuggler towns, all of it, all the way down to the sea where she'd started this with a strongbox and a debt.

Her father had gone upstream. She knew that now the way you know a thing in your bones, the way you know weather coming. The compass, the microfilm key, the bolt-cutters she hadn't found yet but would—he'd walked toward the headwaters and never come back. Every instinct she had said follow him.

But instinct was how crews died. Instinct was how her father's crew had sworn silence around a burning boat while she watched from the treeline, thirteen years old and learning that loyalty could be a shroud.

"We vote," she said.

* * *

They voted around the chart table because that was where decisions got made on the *Magpie's Debt*, and because Pip insisted votes needed ceremony or they didn't count.

Bram went first. "Upstream." He tapped the facility designation. "You don't cut a weed by pulling leaves. Everything—every name in that ledger, every reason Voss wants it burned—comes out of that building. Kill the source, the copies stop mattering. Nobody burns what nobody's hiding anymore."

"You're assuming we can kill it," Odile said. "It's a fenced contractor site at the headwaters of a river they've militarized. We've got half a tank of fuel, one working winch, and a teenager."

"I'm nineteen," Pip said.

"You're a teenager with a knife."

"My vote's the Hollow." Odile turned to Tamsin. "Hear me out. The source is proof of *what happened*. The Hollow copy—if it exists—is proof of *who*. Names, signatures, the men who signed the scuttle order. You want hearings? You want inspectors? Inspectors need culpable humans, not chemistry. We secure the second copy, we control the whole record, then we go upstream together with everything in hand."

"And if Voss's people find it first?"

"Then we've lost nothing we had."

"We've lost time," Bram said. "Time is fuel and fuel is the Reach's exchange rate. You want to trade our last diesel for a maybe?"

"I want to trade it for something better than a maybe," Odile shot back. "I want to trade it for the *whole* archive instead of half of it."

Pip raised his hand like they were in school. "Split the difference?"

"There's no split," Bram said. "The boat goes one way."

And there it was. The whole problem in one sentence, sitting on the table between the ledger pages like a third document.

Tamsin looked at the lamp swinging on its hook. She thought about her father's compass in her pocket, the microfilm key inside it that matched the ledger's cipher—a key meant for the source, she was almost certain, meant for whatever master records lived behind that fence at the headwaters. She thought about Mara Iles standing in the steeple of the drowned parish, saying her mother's name out loud for the first time in twenty years, and how the sound of it had been like a keel breaking water.

She thought about what her father would do, and then she deliberately stopped thinking about it, because following a dead man's ghost upstream had gotten enough people killed already.

"No," she said.

They all went quiet.

"No split-the-difference, no re-vote, no committee. Here's what happens." She put both hands flat on the table, over the names of the dead. "We are not going to be in one place when this breaks. If we run the Hollow together and Voss hits the source while we're digging through flooded basements, we come back with a complete archive and nowhere to use it, because the facility will have purged itself clean. If we run upstream together and his auditors burn the Hollow copy, we've got proof of process and no proof of persons, and lawyers can lose process. They can't lose forty names."

"So we split," Odile said slowly.

"We split." She kept her voice level. Cold, if she was honest, because cold was the only temperature that worked for this. "Bram, Odile—you take the dredger back down. Mara knows the Hollow streets better than anyone living; she can take you to wherever her mother's generation hid things. Find the second copy. Protect it. Don't engage Voss, don't engage auditors, don't be heroes. If you get the copy, hold it somewhere with witnesses. A lock-keeper's house, a church, anywhere with more than three people who can swear to seeing it."

"And you?" Bram asked, though his face said he already knew.

"Pip and I go upstream. Overland, from Weir Nine. The dredger can't make the upper locks anyway—not with half a tank—and a boat draws eyes. Two walkers draw less."

"That's suicide dressed up as tactics," Odile said.

"It's arithmetic dressed up as tactics. Four people on a boat is a crew. Two people in the gorge is a rumor." She straightened. "Bram, strip the sonar rig down to the portable unit. If I can get close enough to the fence, I want to map their emissions—power draw, pump cycles, anything that tells us the layout before we're inside it. Odile, I need every liter of fuel you can beg, borrow, or trade in the Reach, because when this is done, whether it goes well or badly, I want a fast boat at the weir. That's the extraction. Be there."

"When?" Odile asked.

"Five days. Six at the outside. If we're not at Weir Nine by the sixth dusk—" She stopped. Made herself say it. "If we're not there by the sixth dusk, you take the copies and Mara's people and you go public without us. Hearings, inspectors, newspapers, whatever Mara's network can reach. You don't come looking. You don't wait. You finish it."

Silence. The lamp swung. Somewhere above them, the piling groaned against the hull.

"You're asking us to plan your funeral," Pip said quietly.

"I'm asking you to plan the *victory*," Tamsin said. "My part in it is optional."

* * *

They loaded her at first light, which was less a light than a gray suggestion through the Reach fog. Odile pressed a canvas pack into her arms—ration bars, water tabs, the portable sonar unit wrapped in oilcloth, and, wrapped separately at the bottom, a coil of cutting charge that Tamsin didn't ask about and Odile didn't explain.

"For doors," Odile said.

"For doors," Tamsin agreed.

Bram shook her hand, which he'd never done, and held it a beat too long. "Sixth dusk," he said. "I'll have the boat hot."

Pip was already in the tender, sitting very straight, wearing a borrowed jacket two sizes too big with the collar turned up, trying to look like someone who belonged on an overland run through contractor country. He looked, Tamsin thought, exactly like what he was: a kid from a town that had been erased by a reservoir project, going to look at the machinery that erased towns.

She cast off the tender line herself. Stood at the dredger's rail as the gray water took them.

Odile raised one hand—not a wave, just an acknowledgment, a surveyor marking a point. Then the fog closed and the *Magpie's Debt* was a sound, an engine note fading downstream, and then not even that.

Just the river. Just the two of them. Just the current, running the wrong way, toward the beginning of everything.

Pip didn't talk for the first hour. Neither did she. The gorge road was a shelf of broken asphalt stitched to the cliffside, and it demanded attention; one bad step went into white water fifty feet below, and the river up here didn't give things back.

It was Pip who finally broke the quiet, somewhere past the second collapsed culvert, his voice careful:

"Tamsin? Back there. When you split us up." He scrambled over a slab of fallen concrete, regained the road. "Was that the right call? Or just the lonely one?"

She kept walking. Watched the water tear itself white on the rocks below, cold and certain and going where it was going regardless of what anyone aboard it wanted.

"Ask me at the sixth dusk," she said.

Ahead, through a break in the gorge walls, she could see it: a fence line climbing the far slope, and beyond it, on the shoulder of the mountain, the flat gray geometry of buildings that had no business being beautiful in morning light.

Source Station Nine. Her father's last heading.

She checked the compass in her palm—needle steady, pointing home—and started down toward the checkpoint at Weir Nine, where the first drone of the morning lifted off the wire and began to drift along the road ahead of them, patient as a heron.
