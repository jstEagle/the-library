## 21. The Gorge Road

The dredger's wake was still fading on the water behind them when Tamsin shouldered her pack and turned away from the river for the first time in weeks.

It felt wrong. Her body had learned the current's grammar—how to read a bend before it came, how to feel a shoal through the soles of her boots. Walking was silence without information. The gorge road climbed away from the water in switchbacks cut into the rock by some forgotten public-works crew, and every step took her further from the thing she understood and closer to the thing she didn't.

Pip walked ahead of her, light-footed, his pack riding high between his shoulder blades like it had been packed by someone who expected to run. Which, she supposed, he had been.

"Keep off the ridgeline," she said. "Drones fly the spine of the gorge on a loop. We stay under the tree cover until the road drops toward Weir Nine."

"You've done this before."

"No." She adjusted the strap digging into her collarbone. "My father did. I'm working from his notes."

Pip glanced back at her, and she saw him decide not to ask. Good kid. The notes were a slim oilskin packet she'd carried for eleven years without opening more than a dozen times—hand-drawn contour maps of country she'd never seen, annotated in her father's cramped left-slanting hand: *drone loop, 40 min, unreliable in rain*; *spring below the third switchback, drink upstream of the sheep*; *if stopped, you are a surveyor*. She'd thought, when she was younger, that the maps were evidence of some secret life. Now she understood they were something sadder. He'd planned this route the way other men planned retirements. He'd always known he'd have to come back up this gorge.

The trees closed over them and the river disappeared, though they could still hear it somewhere below, talking to itself in the dark of the gorge.

* * *

They made eight miles the first day, which Tamsin considered respectable given that Pip was fourteen and carrying more curiosity than gear. They camped in a hollow beneath a shelf of rock, no fire, cold rations. Tamsin took first watch because sleep had become a negotiation she kept losing anyway.

The drone came at dusk.

She heard it before she saw it—a thin insect whine threading down through the canopy, wrong for a bird, too steady for wind. She put two fingers on Pip's shoulder and pressed him flat against the rock, and they lay there while the sound circled once, twice, and moved on down the gorge toward the water, where the dredger would be sliding dark and unlit past whatever cordon Odile and Bram were supposed to slip.

"Contractor?" Pip breathed.

"Has to be. Navy hardware doesn't bother with gorges." She waited until the whine faded entirely before she let them move. "They bought the source. That means they patrol like a company, not an army. Companies are lazy about anything that doesn't show up on a spreadsheet."

"What shows up on a spreadsheet?"

"Fuel. Wages. Incidents." She smiled without much humor. "We're going to be very careful not to be an incident."

They walked the next morning in fog so thick the world shrank to twenty feet of wet rock and dripping branches. It was good cover and bad footing, and somewhere in the middle of the morning Pip went down hard on a scree slope, skinning his palm open from heel to finger. Tamsin cleaned it with water from his canteen and wrapped it with a strip of shirt, and he didn't cry out, which told her something about him she'd already suspected—that the kid had a history of injuries nobody had been around to kiss better.

"Your turn," he said, when she'd tied off the knot. "Truth for truth. You said on the boat. You never paid up."

"I don't remember saying that."

"You said it. Night we passed the second toll bridge. You were tired."

Tamsin sat back on her heels and looked at him. Fog beaded in his hair. His bandaged hand lay in his lap like something borrowed. She felt the old reflex rise—the one that turned questions into walls—and then she thought of Mara Iles standing in the steeple saying *the ledger lists my mother*, and how the whole weight of that ledger was nothing but people deciding, one after another, to stop keeping their mouths shut.

"My father worked a job here," she said. "Upstream. Before I was born, or just after—I've never been able to pin it down, because everyone who could tell me signed an oath or died. He dove the Hollow. He came back wrong. Quiet in a way that wasn't natural for him. And then years later he went back, and the river kept him." She tied off her own pack strap, buying her hands something to do. "The navy erased the wreck from its records. Erased the settlement. Erased him. I've spent my whole career diving wrecks nobody will admit exist, trying to find the shape of the hole they left."

"That's not a truth," Pip said. "That's a résumé."

She laughed despite herself—a short, surprised bark that startled a bird off the rocks above them. "Fine. Here's the truth underneath: I'm not doing this for justice. Not primarily. I'm doing it because if the names get read out loud, then my father existed. That's the small, ugly engine at the center of all of it. I want my dad back, and the only way to get him is through a room full of drowned strangers' names." She stood, shouldered the pack. "Your turn. Why does a fourteen-year-old sign onto a suicide run upstream?"

Pip looked at the fog. For a long moment she thought he wouldn't answer.

* * *

He answered at noon, but not with words—not at first.

The road dropped through a cutting where the rock had been blasted apart decades ago, and on the cut face someone had painted a mural. It was old, weathered to ghosts, but legible: a row of houses with red roofs, a church spire, a water tower, and beneath them, in letters that had once been bright and were now the color of dried blood:

**MILLBROOK — EST. 1902 — REMEMBER US**

Below that, smaller, angrier, added later by a different hand: **THEY SAID IT WAS FOR THE GREATER GOOD.**

Pip stopped walking.

Tamsin watched him look at it. She'd seen people stand in front of graves with less expression than that. His jaw did something complicated, and his good hand came up and touched the lowest corner of the mural, where a child's chalk drawing had been added sometime after the paint—a stick figure holding a balloon, faded almost to nothing.

"Pip," she said quietly.

"My town's under a reservoir," he said.

The words came out flat, rehearsed, like something he'd said to himself many times in the dark until it wore smooth.

"Not flooded like the Hollow. Drained first, then drowned—there's a difference, apparently, when the government does it. Millbrook sat in a valley, and the valley was worth more full of water than full of people. Dam project. Reservoir for the industrial belt downstream. They did the assessments for three years and then one spring the notices went up and the appraisers came through offering insult money, and anybody who held out got condemned anyway." He traced the water tower in the mural with one finger. "My granddad held out. He was the last one out. There's a photo of him sitting on his porch while the water comes up the steps. It ran in the regional paper. Then the paper got bought, and the photo stopped existing, and eventually Millbrook stopped existing, except it's still right there, forty feet down, whole streets of it. You can dive it if you know where. I used to swim over my own street."

Tamsin said nothing. There was nothing to say that wouldn't have been smaller than the silence.

"When the reservoir filled, they scattered us. Compensation checks and bus tickets. My mum chased work across three counties. Nobody ever said sorry. Nobody ever put it in a book anywhere that Millbrook existed. You go to the county archive and there's a dam report and a fish-stocking schedule and that's it. An entire town, and the official record is a fish-stocking schedule." He finally turned to look at her, and his eyes were dry and furious in a way that was worse than crying. "So when I heard you talking on the boat—about a ledger, about names, about people the navy erased—I stowed away. I'm not here for wages. I'm not even here for you. I'm here because if somebody writes those names down somewhere permanent, then maybe somebody, someday, writes mine."

"Pip—"

"I know what you're going to say. I'm fourteen, it's dangerous, I should've stayed with Odile." He lifted his chin. "Odile's got Bram. Bram's got the boat. Mara's got her survivors. Everybody in this story has somebody except us, and I figured orphans of the same map ought to travel together."

Tamsin looked at him for a long time. The fog was burning off above the cutting, and somewhere far up the gorge a drone whined along its loop, patient as a clock.

"You're not fourteen," she said finally. "You're fifteen. You told Odile fourteen so she'd pay you less."

Something flickered across his face—guilt, then defiance, then, faintly, relief at being caught. "Fifteen," he admitted.

"Fifteen gets a bigger share." She touched the mural once herself, below the angry letters, where the paint had flaked away to bare stone. "And it gets the truth: I can't promise you your town goes in any book. I can promise you that when this is over, we dive Millbrook together, and we document every street, and we make copies, and we mail them to every library in three counties until somebody has to file them. That's not justice. That's just refusing to let the water win."

Pip nodded, swallowed hard, and picked up his pack.

They walked on. But something had shifted in the geometry between them—the distance of employer and deckhand collapsed into something else, something with no name yet and no need of one. Two orphans of official maps, walking a road that appeared on none of them, toward a facility that officially did not exist, carrying a ledger of people who had officially never lived.

* * *

They camped that night in the ruins of a lock-keeper's cottage halfway up the gorge, a stone shell with a hearth still black from somebody's recent fire. Tamsin risked a small flame, screened by the broken walls, and they heated tinned soup and drank it straight from the cans.

"Surveyor," Pip said, around a mouthful. "Your dad's note. *If stopped, you are a surveyor.* How do you survey?"

"Badly, probably." She turned the oilskin notes over in her hands. "But I know the terminology now. Contractor survey crews run the gorge checking slope stability near the facility fence line. If we're stopped, we're a two-person team from the southern office, behind schedule, hating our lives. Contractors hate each other's paperwork more than they love security. It's the one universal law."

"And if they check with the southern office?"

"Then we're an incident." She fed another twig to the fire. "Which is why we're not going to get stopped."

Pip was quiet for a moment, turning his soup can. "There's a checkpoint at Weir Nine. Mara's network mentioned it. Full contractor post, vehicle gate, drone mast."

"I know."

"You knew, and you still split the crew and sent everybody else downstream." He wasn't accusing her. He was studying her, the way Bram studied sonar returns—looking for the shape underneath the signal. "Why not take the boat around? Why the gorge?"

Because Voss would be watching the water. Because the dredger was half fuel and all luck. Because every route she trusted had already been sold to somebody. But what she said was: "Because the gorge is my father's map, and nobody alive knows it better than I do now. And because sometimes the only safe road is the one nobody bothered to put on theirs."

Pip accepted that, or seemed to. He rolled into his blanket, and within minutes his breathing went long and even, the way it only did in people who had learned to sleep wherever sleep found them.

Tamsin kept the watch.

The fire burned down to a red eye. Above the cottage walls, the stars came out hard and cold over the gorge, and the river spoke to itself far below, and she thought about her father drawing these contour lines by lamplight, planning a route he must have known he might die on, annotating it for a daughter who might someday need to walk it. *If stopped, you are a surveyor.* He'd written her a disguise before he wrote her a eulogy. That was the kind of man he'd been—practical all the way down, grief managed through logistics.

She checked the compass out of habit, though the needle meant nothing overland. It swung, settled, pointed north toward the source, toward the wound at the headwaters, toward whatever was waiting at Weir Nine.

In the morning they'd drop out of the trees onto the old haul road, and two miles beyond that, according to Mara's people, the contractor post would rise out of the mist with its drone mast and its vehicle gate and its bored men with rifles.

Tamsin banked the fire, woke the boy, and led them down into the fog toward the checkpoint—wearing her father's notes like armor, and lying to herself about how afraid she was.

The drone mast came into view an hour later, exactly where the map said it would be, and beneath it, blocking the road, a striped barrier arm and two figures in gray contractor jackets who raised their hands for her to stop.
