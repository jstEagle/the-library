## 22. Checkpoint at Weir Nine

The gorge road gave them exactly one day of grace before it ended at a fence.

Tamsin saw it from the ridge above the switchbacks: a line of chain-link running bank to bank across the river's throat, floodlights on steel poles, a low concrete building with a radio mast, and two vehicles parked nose-out like they'd been placed there by someone who thought about angles. Weir Nine. Her father's notes had a hand-drawn map of this exact spot, tucked between pages of tide tables, the checkpoint marked with a small, careful X and a single word in his cramped script: *Talk first.*

Below her, Pip was crouched in the sagebrush, binoculars pressed to his eyes, breathing through his mouth the way he did when he was scared and didn't want anyone to know.

"Two guards visible," he said. "Maybe three inside. There's a barrier arm across the road. And—" he adjusted the focus, "—a drone rack. Empty rack, but it's there."

"Which means drones are flying."

"Which means drones are flying."

Tamsin lay back in the brush and let the sky do its slow rotation overhead. Two days out from the Hollow, one day since the last drone pass had buzzed them like a curious wasp and moved on, satisfied that two hikers with survey packs were nobody's problem. That luck had a shelf life. She could feel it expiring.

The plan she'd made on the ridge last night had been simple to the point of insult: go around. Climb high, cross the weir itself on the maintenance catwalk if it wasn't gated, drop down on the far side, keep walking. But the binoculars said the catwalk was gated, and the gate had a padlock the size of her fist, and the far side of the gorge had a second vehicle parked where a second vehicle shouldn't be.

So. *Talk first.*

She sat up and pulled her father's notes from the waterproof sleeve inside her jacket. She'd been reading them for two days now—not the dive logs, those she knew by heart, but the other pages, the ones he'd written in a different hand, flatter, more careful, the handwriting of a man transcribing something he'd been told rather than discovered. Survey notes. Contractor shorthand. She hadn't understood why a salvage diver had pages of hydraulic head measurements and discharge schedules memorized until she'd stood at the headwaters fence and seen the facility built over the old research station, and understood that her father hadn't just broken into that place. For a while, some years before the end, he'd worked in it. Or beside it. Or under it. The notes didn't say which, and he never had.

But they told her what a contractor surveyor carried, how she talked, what she'd know. And right now that was worth more than a boat, more than a gun, more than the truth.

"Pip." She kept her voice level. "How do you feel about being my trainee?"

He lowered the binoculars slowly. "Bad," he said. "Why?"

"Because you're about to be one."

* * *

They spent an hour on it, sitting in the shade of an overhang while the sun crawled toward the checkpoint and the river below ran green and quiet over the weir's lip, pretending to be harmless water.

The cover was simple because it had to be. They were a two-person survey team, subcontracted, doing pre-maintenance flow assessment on the upper weirs ahead of the autumn drawdown. It was plausible because it was almost true—they *were* walking the gorge road, they *did* have instruments, and contractors ran crews this thin all the time because the parent company treated field staff like consumables. Tamsin had learned that much from the notes and confirmed it from the smugglers at the Reach, who'd laughed when she asked what the source security actually checked for. "Papers," one had said. "And whether you complain. Complainers get waved through faster than liars. Nobody wants a meeting."

So: no complaints. Boredom. The specific boredom of people who have done this walk too many times.

Her name for the day was Sterling—no reason to burn her real one when it was already half-true—and Pip became Adeyemi, off a name stitched into the collar of the spare pack Odile had packed him, some dead man's gear passed down through three owners. He hated it. He practiced saying it anyway, flat and bored, until it stopped sounding like a costume.

The instruments helped. Bram had sent them upstream with a case of salvaged survey equipment he'd bought off a drunk lock-keeper for the price of a story: a total station missing its tripod mount, a handheld sonar fathometer, clipboards, a laminated set of gauge tables that were twenty years out of date and therefore perfect. Tamsin had added the finishing touch herself, working by headlamp at midnight—a work order, printed on the portable printer Odile insisted they carry, on letterhead she'd reconstructed from a contractor invoice in her father's notes. The logo was close enough. The document number followed the format. If anyone looked hard enough to catch the forgery, they'd already caught her, so she made it good instead of perfect, because perfect was what forgers did and tired subcontractors made typos.

She left one typo in deliberately. A transposed digit in the section code. Her father's note, next to a paragraph about inspection culture, said: *Nobody trusts a clean form.*

"Okay," she said, when the light was getting long and gold and the guards' shadows stretched east across the road. "Run it again. Who are you?"

"Adeyemi. Trainee. Third week." Pip recited it staring at a point six inches from her face, jaw tight. "I don't talk unless you talk first. I hold the clipboard. I look stupid."

"You don't have to look stupid."

"I'm sixteen and carrying a clipboard. It's free."

Despite everything—the fence, the drones, the fact that somewhere behind that checkpoint was a payroll signed by the same company that had bought her father's grave—she almost smiled. "One more thing. If I say 'gauge,' you start writing numbers. Any numbers. You just write."

"What does that do?"

"It gives their eyes somewhere to be that isn't your face. People trust a busy trainee." She stood, brushed off her knees, and felt the fear arrive all at once, the way it always did, a cold hand closing around the back of her neck. She let it. Fear was information. Her father used to say a diver who wasn't scared was a diver who'd stopped reading the water. "Come on. Walk like we're underpaid."

* * *

The last hundred meters of road were the longest of her life.

She made herself amble. That was the trick of it, she realized—everything in her wanted to march, to get it over with, to cross the distance like a swimmer driving for the surface. But guilty people crossed distances fast. So she ambled, boots scuffing, and let Pip fall half a step behind her with the clipboard, and she even stopped once to squint at a benchmark bolt set in the rock shoulder, making a show of checking it against nothing.

The guard at the barrier arm was young. That was the first mercy. Young guards performed authority; older ones just applied it. This one had a rifle slung muzzle-down and a tablet in his hand and the posture of someone who'd been standing here since morning and would stand here until dark, and who resented every minute of it.

"Afternoon." She pitched her voice low, unhurried, already reaching into her jacket for the folder. "Sterling, Hydrosurvey East. We're on the upper-weir assessment. Should be a crew notification from Tuesday."

The young guard took the folder the way people take things they've taken ten thousand times. He flipped it open. His eyes went to the work order, then to her face, then back to the paper, and Tamsin stood in the space between those glances and did not breathe.

Here was the thing she hadn't expected, the thing no amount of rehearsal prepared her for: the shame arrived before the fear did. Standing there in a forged livery of competence, wearing her father's stolen knowledge like a badge, she felt—dirty. Not afraid. Dirty. Because the uniform she was impersonating belonged to the machine that had drowned a town, erased a ferry, put a bullet or a lock or a lie on her father's name, and here she was, asking to be let inside it. Asking to be mistaken for it. Somewhere under the adrenaline a small cold voice said: *you fit.* The posture fit. The paperwork fit. The bored, competent, complicit face fit. And she hated how well it fit, hated that her father's notes had taught her this fluency, hated that everything she knew about honesty she'd learned from a man who'd learned dishonesty first.

The guard scrolled. "Upper weirs," he said. "Nine through twelve?"

"That's the scope."

"You're walking? Vehicle didn't come through."

"Company wouldn't spring for a truck." She let one degree of bitterness into it, real as blood. "We're the cheap bid."

Something in his shoulders loosened. Recognition, maybe—fellow laborer in the great machinery of people who got billed by the hour and paid by the year. He glanced past her at Pip. "He new?"

"Three weeks."

"They still sending kids up the gorge?" He handed the folder back, and then, instead of lifting the barrier, he leaned on the arm and lowered his voice, and Tamsin's whole nervous system went to red alert. "You'll want to watch yourselves past Eleven. Drone schedule changed last month. They fly the gorge at dusk now, low passes. Survey teams keep filing complaints and nothing happens." He shrugged. "Not my department."

"Gauge," Tamsin said.

Pip's pen started moving.

She smiled at the guard, tired and grateful, the smile of a woman who has heard bad news before and will hear it again. "Dusk passes. Thanks. We'll log it." She tapped the clipboard without looking at it. "Anything else change? We've been out of signal for two days, and my office assumes silence means progress."

"Gate code's the same as the sheet says." He straightened, lifted the barrier arm with one hand, and jerked his chin at the road beyond. "Water's up a little over the weir. Don't get clever on the catwalk."

"We're never clever," Tamsin said. "That's why we're cheap."

He almost laughed. The barrier came down behind them, and they walked through, and Tamsin counted her heartbeats—one, two, five, ten—before she trusted her legs to keep the same lazy rhythm. The checkpoint building slid by on her left. Through its window she caught a glimpse of a third guard, older, heavyset, watching a screen, and she fixed her eyes on the middle distance and gave him nothing to see but a woman thinking about flow rates.

Only when the road bent and the checkpoint dropped out of sight behind the shoulder of the gorge did she allow herself to exhale, long and shaking, into the collar of her jacket.

"Gauge," she said quietly.

Pip stopped writing. He looked down at the clipboard, then held it up. Page after page, dense columns of numbers in his careful teenage hand—depths, heads, discharges, dates. He'd written for nearly a full minute.

"What are these?" he asked.

Tamsin took the clipboard and read a few lines. Then she stopped reading, because the numbers had arranged themselves into something she recognized, and recognition hit her like cold water in the ears.

They were discharge schedules. Outflow volumes from the facility at the source, logged by season, by year. Her father's notes contained the same figures—not copied, annotated, corrected, argued with in the margins. And the pattern in them, the slow seasonal pulse, matched the pattern Mara had described from memory: the weeks each year when the river below the Hollow ran strange, when fish floated, when the old people said the water tasted like pennies.

"He wasn't just diving that place," she said, mostly to herself. "He was reading it. For years. Before the job. Before—" She closed the clipboard. "These aren't fake numbers, Pip. You wrote down what the gauges actually say. Somebody's going to want these."

Pip looked at her with an expression she couldn't parse for a moment—then could. It was the look of a kid realizing the adults' map has edges. "That guard," he said. "He was nice."

"Yeah."

"He thinks we're them."

Tamsin looked back down the road, at the checkpoint dissolving into the evening haze, at the barrier arm standing straight and official against the sky. The shame was still there, seated in her chest like swallowed river water. She suspected it would be there for a while. Maybe forever. Passing as the enemy turned out not to be a door you walked through; it was a current you swam in, and some of it got into your mouth.

"He thinks whatever keeps us alive," she said. "Come on. Dusk is coming, and we've got a date with a drone."

* * *

They made two miles before the light failed, and camped in a culvert under the old haul road, sharing a heat pack and half a brick of trail food. Pip fell asleep mid-sentence, the way teenagers do, guiltless as a cat, and Tamsin sat awake with the clipboard across her knees and her father's notes open beside it, matching column to margin, figure to furious annotation.

That was when she found the entry she'd missed.

Near the back, in the flatter transcription hand, a single line she'd skimmed past twice before, buried among valve specifications:

*W-9 gate code rotates quarterly. Current: 4471-K. Verify before use.*

The same code the young guard had waved them past without a second glance. Her father had known it. Twenty years ago, her father had stood at this same checkpoint—or one just like it, earlier, smaller—and written down the number that opened the way to the source, the way he'd written down everything, hoarding the enemy's grammar against the day someone would need to speak it.

Which meant the route she was walking wasn't hers. It was a relay. He'd run this leg and died before the handoff, and the baton had waited in a waterproof sleeve for two decades until his daughter happened to pick it up.

Tamsin sat in the dark of the culvert with the river murmuring below and let that settle over her—not grief exactly, something drier and stranger. Purpose, maybe, with grief folded inside it like salt in water.

Then her satellite messenger buzzed against her hip, and the feeling curdled.

One message. From Odile's unit, downstream, routed through the smugglers' relay. Four words:

*Voss has the canister.*

Tamsin read it twice. Below her, through the culvert's mouth, the river ran black and steady toward the sea, carrying everything—silt, secrets, the outflow of the springs at the world's beginning—toward a place where none of it would ever be seen again, unless somebody stood in the current and refused.

She put the message away, woke Pip with a hand on his shoulder, and said, "Change of plans. We move tonight."
