## 23. Downstream Betrayal

Cassian Voss had always believed that the worst decisions of a man's life arrived dressed as kindness.

He stood on the aft deck of the *Meridian Star*, a chartered patrol cutter with a navy-gray hull and a civilian registry, watching the river bend below Weir Seven. The morning fog was burning off in rags. Somewhere upstream, Odile Ferre was running his dredger through the derelict locks, and somewhere downstream of her, Mara Iles was carrying a photostat of a ledger that could end three generations of his family's good name.

He had told himself, all the way down from Meridian Hollow, that this was mercy. That was the word he kept reaching for. Mercy. He turned it over like a coin he suspected was shaved.

"Contact," said Deke, at the rail glasses. "Dredger, coming out of the lock cut. She's riding light—lost something back at the rapids."

"I can see her."

"She'll see us in about ninety seconds."

Voss lowered his own glasses. The dredger was an ugly, honest machine, all rust and function, and he could pick out the figures on her deck even at this distance: Odile at the wheel, big shoulders hunched over it like she was wrestling the river personally. Bram in the stern, one arm still bandaged from the parish. And a third figure, slighter, standing apart, wrapped in a man's coat two sizes too large.

Mara Iles. The witness. The woman whose mother's name sat in the ledger in ink that had been underwater for thirty years.

His grandfather's ink. His grandfather's order, countersigned in a hand Cassian knew better than his own father's, because he'd grown up tracing it on the framed commission that hung in the front hall of the Voss house in Meridian Hollow—the house on the hill above the drowned streets, where the family had watched the water rise over the parish and called it progress.

"Run out the lines," he said. "Signal flag. Salvage inspection—we're claiming right of search under the old harbor statute."

Deke gave him a look. "The old harbor statute died with the harbor authority."

"It died," Voss said, "which means no one alive remembers exactly what it covered. That's the whole art of law, Deke. It's not what's written. It's what's forgotten." He heard himself say it and thought: my grandfather would have liked that sentence. The thought sat in his chest like swallowed ice.

* * *

The interception went the way these things go when one side has a gunboat and the other has a dredger with half its fuel burned off in the locks. Odile brought the *Old Bess* alongside under protest, arms crossed, jaw set, and let the boarding line come across because the alternative was a warning shot and a hole in her hull with Bram still limping around aboard.

Voss came over the rail himself. He wanted to see their faces when they understood. He told himself he owed them that much honesty—he'd spent enough of his life behind intermediaries.

"Tamsin's not here," Odile said flatly. She planted herself between him and the deckhouse like a door with opinions.

"I know where Tamsin is. I'm not interested in Tamsin." A lie, or half of one; he was always interested in Tamsin Sterling, the way a man is interested in the current that nearly drowned him as a boy. "I'm interested in the canister."

"There's no canister."

"Mara." He said the name gently, and saw the woman flinch inside her borrowed coat. "Ms. Iles. You made a copy. Photostat, wet-process, done on Bram's field kit—I know because I supplied that kit to the navy survey office eleven years ago, and I know what it does in damp air. The original is in a brass canister with a green pressure seal, and it is on this boat, because Tamsin doesn't trust anyone enough to split them further than she already has." He paused. "She trusts you least of all, Ms. Iles, because you're the one with the most to lose. That's not an insult. It's how she loves people. She keeps them at the distance she can afford to grieve."

"You don't know anything about how she loves people," Odile said.

"No," Voss admitted. "I know how she hates them. It's adjacent."

Bram laughed once, short and ugly, from the rail. "You rehearse that stuff?"

"In the mirror," Voss said. "Every morning. Along with my excuses." He held out his hand, palm up. "Give me the canister and everyone walks away rich. I mean it. There's money in the escrow account—more than the salvage contract paid, more than any of you would see in five honest years of pulling dead men's tools out of the mud. Take it, disappear, live well. The ledger stops being your problem and becomes mine."

"And then what?" Mara's voice came out low and steady, and it was worse than shouting. "You burn it."

"I destroy it. Yes."

"That's the same thing."

"It isn't." He heard his own voice tighten and made himself loosen it. This was the speech. He'd built it plank by plank on the run downstream, and now he laid it out before them like a man laying out tools. "Listen to me. All of you. In six weeks there is a naval audit—quiet, internal, the kind that exists so that things can be found and then un-found. My family's name is attached to the Hollow program through my grandfather's signature. If that ledger surfaces through official channels, it doesn't just convict the dead. It triggers the indemnity clauses. The settlements get restructured. The survivors' compensation fund—the one that pays for Mara's neighbors' medicine, for the rebuilt school at the parish—gets frozen pending litigation. For years. Maybe a decade. I have read the statutes, Ms. Iles. I have read them at night, when I couldn't sleep, which is most nights." He let that land. "If I burn the ledger quietly, before the audit, the fund stands. The names stay buried, yes—but the living keep getting paid. That is the arithmetic. I didn't write it. I'm just the only one willing to do the sum."

For a moment nobody spoke. The river slapped against both hulls. Somewhere inland a lock horn sounded, long and mournful, like the whole valley clearing its throat.

"You've been telling yourself that story a long time," Mara said.

"Every story I tell myself, I tell a long time. It's the only way to make myself believe the bad ones."

"The fund was frozen anyway," Bram said suddenly. He was looking at his own hands. "Two months ago. I processed the paperwork before I mustered out. Interim suspension, pending review. Nobody told the survivors because—" He stopped.

"Because someone wanted them calm," Voss finished softly. "Yes. I know. I found that out after I started lying to you, which I believe makes me a slow learner." He kept his hand out. "So the arithmetic changed. But the rest holds. Names buried, or names burned in open court with lawyers picking the bones. Those are the options the world has left us. I'm choosing the kinder one. God help me, I actually believe that."

"You believe it," Odile said, "because believing it lets you keep the house on the hill."

And that—that was the blade, because it was true, and because she'd said it without heat, almost kindly, the way you'd note a man's limp.

"My grandfather signed the scuttle order," Voss said. "Did you know that? Not authorized it from a distance. Signed it. Ferry manifest, passenger list, disposal authorization—all in his hand. Thirty-one names on the manifest marked *not embarked*, which was a lie, because they were embarked, and the ferry went down with them in it, and the settlement was flooded the same week, and my family got the water rights." He looked at the river. "I grew up drinking tea in a house that was paid for with those thirty-one people. I was taught to say grace over it. So yes, Ms. Ferre. I am keeping the house. I am keeping everything. Because if I give it away, I will have to become a different person, and I am fifty-one years old, and I have discovered that I do not know how."

The honesty did what honesty does in a negotiation: nothing useful. But Odile's shoulders dropped a centimeter, and Bram looked away, and Voss understood he had, for one strange moment, been seen—and that being seen was not the same as being forgiven, and might even be the opposite.

"The canister," he said again.

* * *

It was Mara who moved first, and Voss never learned whether it was surrender or strategy, because in the confusion of the next minute both were possible.

She went into the deckhouse. They all followed—Deke covering the companionway, Voss behind him—and she came out with the canister held in both hands, the green seal intact, the brass dulled by river damp. She held it out. And then, at the last second, she stumbled—a genuine stumble, or a masterpiece of one—against the coaming, and the canister spun from her hands, and Deke lunged, and Bram shouted, and in the tangle of bodies at the deckhouse door Voss caught the canister against his chest with both arms like a man catching a child dropped from a window.

When he straightened, Mara was three steps back, breathing hard, empty-handed.

And her sleeve was wrong. Her right cuff hung heavy, folded oddly, and there was a smudge of fixative on her wrist that hadn't been there a minute ago.

The photostat. Folded small, inside the coat, against her skin. She'd copied it days ago—of course she had; Tamsin would have insisted—and the canister was real but it was not the only ledger in the world anymore, and she had let him take it because taking it was the thing that would send him hurrying downstream to burn it while she walked, unhurried, in the other direction.

"You planned that," he said.

"I planned nothing," Mara said. "I'm a schoolteacher. I plan lessons." She pulled the coat straight. "But my mother taught me never to carry all the eggs in one basket, and she should know. Your grandfather drowned her in the other kind."

Voss stood with the canister against his chest and felt the full architecture of his error assemble itself around him, elegant and load-bearing. He had come to take the original so that no copy could exist. Instead he had confirmed, in front of witnesses, that copies existed, that he feared them, that the Voss name had a price on it—and he had let the witness walk free because stopping her meant violence, and violence was the one thing he had promised himself he would not inherit.

"Let her go," he said quietly, before Deke could ask.

"Boss—"

"*Let her go.*" He watched Mara step across to the dredger, watched Odile put a hand on her shoulder, watched Bram cast off the line without being told. The *Old Bess* fell away downstream, toward the Hollow, toward whatever Tamsin was doing at the source, and Voss stood on the deck of his hired gunboat holding thirty-one names in a brass tube and understanding, finally, the shape of the trap he had built for himself out of mercy.

Because here was the arithmetic now, and he could do nothing else with it: the photostat would reach Tamsin. Tamsin would reach the source. And if she published—if she flooded the world with those names—then the only version of events left standing would be the one where Cassian Voss, grandson of the signatory, chased a grieving woman down a river to burn the evidence of her mother's murder.

There would be no audit gentle enough to survive that story. There would be no settlement, no fund, no quiet. There would be his grandfather's signature projected on courtroom walls, and his own face beside it, and the house on the hill sold at auction to pay the judgments, and every night for the rest of his life the river sounds.

Unless he got ahead of it.

Unless he went to the source himself—not to stop her this time, but to be there, to stand in the room when it happened, to say the words aloud that no document could force him to say. *My grandfather did this. I knew. I tried to bury it. I was wrong.*

Or to stop her. He genuinely did not know which. He stood at the rail with the canister under his arm and examined himself the way he'd examine a recovered artifact—turning the self over, checking for cracks—and could not determine, even up close, even in the good light, whether the man holding it intended to confess or to destroy.

Both, probably. Both, in that order, depending on who reached the server room first.

"Set a course upstream," he said. "All speed."

Deke hesitated. "Upstream's the cordon. And past that—" He nodded at the gray line of hills where the headwaters ran behind contractor fencing. "That's bought ground, boss. Private security. Men who shoot first and invoice later."

"I know what I purchased with my silence, Deke. I'd like to see the property." Voss looked down at the canister, at the green seal, at the dull brass that held thirty-one people who had never been allowed to exist on any record at all. "And I'd like to arrive before she does. There's something I have to say to Tamsin Sterling, and for once in my life I want to say it before she has a reason never to listen."

The *Meridian Star* came about, bow swinging toward the hills, and the wake unspooled behind it, white and brief, closing over itself the way all wakes do—as if nothing had passed.

In his cabin that night, Voss opened the canister. Just to look. Just to hold the pages his grandfather had tried to drown.

He read until dawn. He read every name twice.

Then he took out a sheet of paper and began, in a hand that shook only a little, to practice writing the hardest sentence he owned: *My name is Cassian Voss, and my family sank the ferry.*

He wrote it eleven times. He kept none of them.
