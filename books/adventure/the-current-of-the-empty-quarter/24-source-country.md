## 24. Source Country

The river got younger as we climbed.

That was Pip's word for it—*younger*—and I'd stopped correcting him because he was right. Downstream the water moved like something that had seen things, brown and patient and heavy with everything it carried. Up here it was all noise and innocence, white over stones, cold enough to hurt your wrists when you cupped it to drink. A river that hadn't learned what it was going to be asked to hold.

We walked the last six miles on the old service road, keeping to the tree line where the gravel gave way to pine needles and the drone traffic wouldn't silhouette us against open sky. Pip counted them under his breath the way other kids counted sheep. "Two o'clock. High. Not circling." He'd gotten good at it in the gorge—good enough that I'd stopped flinching at every buzz of wings overhead. Good enough that I trusted him with my life without ever having said so out loud.

The trees broke at noon.

I'd built a picture of it in my head for twenty years, out of fragments: my father's notes, a smuggler's rumor, the shape of a secret everyone knew but no one said. The picture was smaller than the truth. It always is.

The facility sat in a bowl of cleared land where three streams braided together into the first honest water of the river—the spring itself, the source, the place every map agreed the current began. Except it didn't begin there anymore. The springs had been fenced. All of them. Chain-link topped with razor wire, running in long silver arcs up the slopes, enclosing maybe forty acres of ground that had once belonged to nobody because it had belonged to everybody. Inside the fence: low concrete buildings, a helipad, antenna masts, and a water-treatment complex that looked municipal until you noticed the discharge pipes.

There were four of them. Great riveted steel throats as wide as trucks, emerging from the hillside above the main spring pool, and they were *running*. Even from two hundred meters I could hear them—a sound I felt in my back teeth before I properly heard it, a subsonic groan of volume and pressure. They emptied into the headwater pool in four columns of churning foam, and the foam was wrong. Not dirty, exactly. Not oily. Just *off*—a faint iridescent sheen spreading across the surface in rainbow continents, dissolving, reforming, sliding downstream toward everything and everyone the river would ever touch.

"That's the source," Pip said quietly. "That's where it starts."

"Yeah."

"It's bleeding."

"Yeah." I put my hand on his shoulder because he looked like he might walk straight down there, sixteen years old and furious, into forty acres of razor wire and contractors with rifles. "It's been bleeding since before you were born. We're not going to stop it by dying today."

He swallowed whatever came next. But his jaw stayed set, and I thought—not for the first time—that the drowned towns keep making children exactly like this. Children who count drones. Children who know what an erased map looks like from the inside.

We worked our way along the slope to a granite outcrop I'd marked on the survey plates Odile had pulled from the dredger's old charts. From there, through Bram's salvaged binoculars, I could read the facility like a page.

The modern buildings sat on top of something older. You could see the seams if you knew how to look—my father had taught me that much, at least, before he taught me nothing else forever. The contractor's structures were prefabricated, modular, bolted together with the cheerful ugliness of things meant to be temporary and then kept anyway. But underneath them, breaking through at odd angles like bones through skin, were walls of poured 1940s concrete. A research station. The original one. The navy hadn't built their secret out here in the empty country—they'd buried it inside an older secret, the way you hide a body in a graveyard.

And the discharge pipes came out of the old station's foundations. Whatever they'd been doing down there in 1948, they were still doing it, or still paying for having done it, and the river was still carrying the bill.

"Chemical designation," I said, mostly to myself. The words from the ledger canister, the ones that had made Bram go quiet and then go pale. "Whatever they were testing. It never stopped leaching. They just built a bigger pump."

"To push it downstream faster," Pip said.

"Or to keep the spring from pushing it out on its own terms." I lowered the binoculars. "Either way, somebody's been managing this wound for seventy years instead of healing it. That's not neglect, Pip. That's maintenance."

*

We waited out the daylight in a hollow beneath the outcrop, taking turns watching while the other slept, which neither of us managed well. Around four, a supply truck ground up the service road and I watched the gate procedure through the glasses: guard post, barrier arm, a scanner arch the truck idled through, a second gate fifty meters inside. Two fences. Two checkpoints. This wasn't a navy outpost anymore—the uniforms were wrong, charcoal-gray corporate fieldwear, no insignia, weapons carried with the bored competence of men paid by the month. Smugglers' Reach had told me true. Someone had bought this secret outright, and whoever held the deed wanted the navy's fingerprints kept off it.

Which meant the records were here. Somewhere below those 1940s foundations, behind whatever doors the old station had grown around its own guilt, was the master archive—and Voss's auditors, and the audit that would burn it, and my father's name still sitting in the margins of a ledger that only half existed.

"You're doing the thing," Pip said.

"What thing."

"The face. Like you're already underwater." He passed me the coffee tin, which contained tea, because we'd run out of coffee somewhere in the gorge and neither of us had mentioned it since. "Odile does it too. Before a dive. Bram says it's the brain rehearsing."

"Your brain should rehearse staying topside."

"My brain," Pip said, "already knows I'm coming with you tonight."

I looked at him. He looked back with the flat stubbornness of someone who had lost a town to a reservoir project and had decided, at some age too young for the decision, that erased places deserved people willing to walk into them. I could have ordered him to stay. He'd have followed anyway, quieter and angrier, and alone in the dark that was worse than beside me.

"Culvert route," I said finally. "You watch the fence line and the drone schedule. If anything moves wrong, you don't come find me. You go back down the gorge to Mara's people and you tell them where I went in. That's the job. Can you do that job?"

"Yes," he said, which is how I knew he intended to do more.

*

Night came up cold and starless, low cloud pressing down like a lid. By ten the facility had settled into its rhythm—floodlights on the gates, a walking patrol on a fifteen-minute loop I'd timed twice, the drone hangar dark. The discharge pipes ran loudest after dark, or seemed to; the sound filled the whole bowl of land, and I understood suddenly why the contractors had chosen this valley. Nobody could sneak up on a place that roared.

Except through the roar itself.

My father's notes had drawn it in a hand I'd have known anywhere: a storm culvert on the western slope, cut into the hillside in the forties to carry runoff away from the original station's foundations, later half-swallowed by the contractor's earthworks but never fully sealed—because sealing it would mean admitting the old station's plumbing mattered, and admitting that meant questions. His sketch showed the grate, the drop, the crawl, and a single annotation in pencil that I'd read a thousand times without understanding until this year:

*They never fixed what they don't look at.*

We found the culvert where his map said it would be, behind a curtain of blackberry bramble and sixty years of silt. The grate was there. So was the hole cut in it—torch-cut, ragged, edges furred with rust that had been rust for decades.

My father had stood here. My father had knelt in this mud with a cutting torch and gone through this fence, and everything after—the Hollow, the ferry, the ledger, the burning boat, the silence, my whole life shaped around the absence he left—had started on the far side of this hole in the wire.

I put my hand through the gap and touched the cut metal. Cold. Rough. Real. Twenty years of grief, and it came down to a jagged edge I could hook my fingers into.

"Tamsin?" Pip's voice, very small against the roar of the pipes.

"I'm here." I took out my light, shielded it, and played it into the culvert mouth. Concrete throat, dry floor, running maybe thirty meters into the hillside before the dark ate it. No new welds. No sensor brackets. They truly never looked.

I went through. The rust caught my jacket and let me go, and the hillside closed behind me, and the sound of the discharge pipes changed—it was inside my chest now, inside my skull, the heartbeat of the wound at the world's beginning.

*

The culvert ended at a junction chamber where three drainage runs met, and from there my father's penciled lines became a language I could suddenly read fluently, because he'd written it for someone exactly as desperate as he'd been. A maintenance ladder behind a loose panel. A service gallery running under the modern buildings' footings, its 1940s tile walls furred with mineral bloom, white and orange, like the earth itself trying to heal over the infection. Stenciled numbers on doorways in a style two generations dead. And everywhere, the smell—chemical and mineral and old, sharp enough to taste, the same smell I'd pulled up from the Hollow's flooded streets on dives I'd never told anyone about. The smell of the thing the river had been carrying all my life.

I moved slow. Counted my breaths between steps the way I did on deep dives, because terror and patience use the same clock and only one of them keeps you alive.

The gallery climbed gently and opened into a room that stopped me cold.

It was the old station's central hall, preserved like a fly in amber beneath the newer construction—vaulted ceiling, tiled walls, a floor of terrazzo worn into paths by feet that had been dead longer than I'd been alive. Somebody in the intervening decades had used it for storage: shelving racks, crates stenciled with logistics codes, plastic sheeting over equipment nobody had bothered to remove. But the walls were the reason I stood there with my heart slamming.

They were covered in plaques. Commemorative brass, dozens of them, green with age, each one bearing names and dates and unit designations of the research staff who had served here. A wall of honor. Men who had built the program. Men who had signed the orders. Their names cast in metal and mounted forty feet underground where no rain could ever reach them, while the people downstream—the ferry's passengers, the Hollow's families, Mara's mother, my father—had been struck from every record that existed.

Two kinds of permanence, and they'd given all of theirs to the guilty.

I found a space on the lowest row, near the floor, where the brass ran out. Where the wall was just bare tile. I pressed my palm flat against it, cold and damp, and thought: *there's room.* There was room for every name in the ledger. There was room for the truth, if anyone ever made it fit.

Then I heard the footsteps above.

Not in the gallery—heavier, closer, through the ceiling. Voices, muffled by concrete, two of them, unhurried, passing over the hall and fading toward the north end of the complex. Routine patrol, probably. Probably.

But one of the voices said a word that came through the structure clear as a bell, because the building carried it the way water carries sound:

"*Audit.*"

And the second voice laughed, short and ugly, and said: "*Then we burn it all before Friday.*"

I stood in the dark under the wall of honored names, my father's map trembling in my hand, and did the math. Days. Maybe less. Everything—the master records, the order with Voss's grandfather's signature, the proof that the Hollow had been murdered on paper before it was murdered in the water—was down here, somewhere behind these tiles, and the men upstairs had just scheduled its funeral.

I turned north, toward the oldest part of the station, and started walking.
