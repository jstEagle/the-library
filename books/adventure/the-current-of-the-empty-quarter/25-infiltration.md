## 25. Infiltration

The facility showed itself in pieces.

From the ridge above the gorge road, it was nothing—a smear of sodium light against the black shoulder of the mountain, a fence line that caught the moon like a scar. From the waterline below, where the spring came out of the rock and the discharge pipes bled their gray plume into what should have been the cleanest water in the country, it was a hum. A low industrial heartbeat you felt in your molars before you heard it with your ears.

Tamsin lay flat on the granite at the tree line and let it hum through her.

"Three towers," Pip whispered beside her. He'd gone quiet an hour ago and stayed that way, which for Pip was its own kind of alarm. "Two of them are cameras. The third's just a light."

"How do you know?"

"The cameras sweep on a count. I've been counting." He held up his watch, face dimmed under his sleeve. "Forty seconds dark, twenty lit. The light tower doesn't move at all. Somebody wanted it to look like coverage without paying for a second camera."

Tamsin looked at him sideways. In the dark she could just make out the sharp line of his jaw, the way he'd stopped flinching from her attention somewhere back on the gorge road. Two orphans of official maps, she'd thought, when he told her about the reservoir that had eaten his town off the survey maps. She hadn't known then how much she'd come to rely on the fact of him.

"You've been counting since we left the treeline," she said.

"Somebody had to. You were doing your thing." He meant the staring, the long stillness she fell into before a dive or a job, the ritual Odile called *Tamsin loading*. He didn't say it unkindly. "What is your thing, anyway?"

"Reading my father's notes until they read me back."

She opened the oilskin packet one more time. The pages were soft as cloth now, handled so many years, and the last page—the only one about this place—was a single paragraph in her father's cramped hand:

*Discharge culvert, south face, below the pipe outfalls. Grate cut and reset by hand; they never check it because nobody believes anyone would swim up the outfall. Cold enough to stop your heart if you rush it. Go slow. Go slow.*

Go slow. He'd written it twice, underlined the second time, as if he'd known who would be reading it someday and how fast she'd want to go.

Below them, the spring ran silver over the rocks toward the pipes. Three outfalls, each wide as a road tunnel, each breathing warm mineral steam into the night air. And beneath them, half-hidden by a shelf of stone and twenty years of silt, the culvert mouth: a concrete throat maybe four feet high, barred with a grate that—

Tamsin raised the binoculars. Lowered them. Raised them again.

"Pip," she said. Her voice came out strange. "Look at the grate. Left side, bottom bar."

He took the glasses. She watched his shoulders change.

"It's cut," he said. "It's been cut and put back."

"I know."

"That's—how long has that been there?"

"Twenty-two years," Tamsin said, "give or take."

* * *

They went down through the trees in the camera's dark window, forty seconds at a time, freezing in the open stretches while Pip counted under his breath like a rosary. The ground steamed faintly where the spring's warmth leaked up through the soil, and the smell was chemical-sweet, wrong for a mountain, wrong for water. Every step closer to the outfalls made the hum louder until it wasn't sound anymore, just pressure, the whole hillside thrumming like the hull of a running boat.

At the culvert mouth they crouched behind the stone shelf and Tamsin finally saw it whole.

The grate was steel, galvanized once, rusted to lacework now along its lower edge. But the cut was clean. One vertical seam, left side, bottom corner, made by a bolt-cutter worked patiently through three bars—and then the section had been hinged back into place, wired at the top with soft aluminum wire gone white with corrosion. Anyone walking past would see an intact grate. Anyone looking closely would see a door.

Her father had looked closely. Twenty-two years ago he'd stood exactly here, in exactly this cold, and cut his way into the same building she was about to enter, and he had wired the grate shut behind him because he'd meant to come back out this way.

He hadn't come back out.

Tamsin knelt in the shallows and put her fingers on the wire. It crumbled at her touch, flakes of oxide drifting away downstream like gray snow. Underneath, the metal was still solid enough to hold. She could feel the twist he'd given it, two turns with pliers, a workman's habit, the same twist he'd put on every shackle and lashing on every boat she'd grown up on. Her hands knew that twist. She'd learned it standing on a deck at seven years old, small fingers inside his big ones, both of them turning together.

"Dad," she said, very quietly, to no one.

Pip didn't ask what she'd said. He crouched next to her and started working the cut section free, and neither of them spoke again until it swung open on its ruined hinge with a sound like a coffin lid.

* * *

The culvert swallowed them by degrees.

First the water—shin-deep, then knee-deep, then waist-deep, and cold in a way that wasn't temperature but intent, the spring's original chill preserved down here in the dark like something kept in a cellar. Then the light, falling away behind them until the rectangle of night sky at the culvert mouth shrank to a coin, then a slit, then nothing. Then the sound, the outside world gone entirely, leaving only the drip of seepage and the deep subsonic pulse of the facility's machinery transmitted through the concrete into their bones.

Tamsin swam with her chin above water, one hand trailing the wall. The concrete was slick with biofilm, soft as skin. Her dive light stayed off; they moved by touch and by the faint gray glow that began to bleed up from somewhere ahead, emergency lighting buried in the throat of the building.

Pip breathed close behind her. She could hear him controlling it—in, four counts, out, six—the way she'd taught him on the dredger, the way Bram had taught her, navy discipline passed hand to hand down a chain of people who'd all needed it to stay calm in the dark.

"Talk to me," she murmured. "Keep talking."

"You said go slow."

"I know what I said. Talk anyway."

A pause. Water dripping. Then: "When they flooded my town—for the reservoir—they gave everyone ninety days. Posted notices in the feed, like anybody checks the feed. My mom spent those ninety days photographing everything. Every room, every street, the scratch on the school door where I carved a stupid arrow when I was six." His voice echoed strangely off the wet concrete, flattened and intimate at once. "After the water came up, she'd look at the photos instead of sleeping. Like the town was a patient and she was keeping vigil."

"What happened to the photos?"

"Flooded. Our house was above the line but the basement wasn't." A breath, controlled, four in, six out. "That's why I stowed away, back at the locks. Not adventure. I just—I keep meeting water that took things, and I can't stand it winning."

Ahead, the gray glow strengthened. The culvert began to rise, the ceiling lifting, the water shallowing until they were wading, then walking bent-double, then upright in a corridor of raw concrete that ended at a second barrier: an interior grille, newer, unpainted steel, secured with a padlock the size of a fist.

Tamsin played her light over it—one second, no more—and felt her stomach drop and settle.

The padlock hung open. Hasp unlocked, body dangling, untouched for years if the dust film across it told true. Someone had come through here before them too. Recently enough that the facility hadn't relocked it. Carelessly enough that they'd left it hanging like an invitation.

Or a trap.

"Could be maintenance access," Pip whispered.

"Maintenance locks get checked. This one hasn't been touched since the dust settled." She turned the lock over in her gloved hand. Inside the shackle, stamped small: a contractor mark she recognized from her father's notes—Halvern Security Group, the private outfit the smugglers had named, the ones who'd bought the secret along with the source. "Somebody with a key comes through here sometimes. Somebody who isn't supposed to be seen using the front door."

"Smuggler? Inspector? Ghost?"

"Maybe the same ghost I'm following." She eased the grille open on silent hinges—recently oiled, which answered some questions and raised worse ones—and looked through into the facility's underbelly.

* * *

The discharge gallery ran fifty feet to a junction where pipes the width of trucks climbed into darkness and catwalks stitched between them like nerve fibers. Emergency lights burned every thirty feet, amber and grudging. The hum lived here at full volume; Tamsin could feel it climbing her ribs, rearranging her heartbeat around it.

And on the near wall, at eye height, someone had scratched a mark into the paint.

Not graffiti. A compass rose, three inches across, drawn with something sharp and sure—a drafting point, maybe, or a diver's knife. North star at the top, the cardinal points, and in the center a tiny letter S with a line through it, the old hydrographic shorthand for *sounding*: depth measured, bottom confirmed, safe water here.

Her father's mark. He'd used it on charts her whole childhood, in the margins of his logs, on the lintel of the kitchen door at home where she'd measured her height against it every birthday. *Sounding.* It means I was here. It means the water is known.

Tamsin stood in front of it for a long time.

There were others. As they moved up the gallery, hugging the shadow side of the pipes, she found them at intervals—a sounding mark at the junction, another at the base of a ladder, another beside a hatch labeled SUBLEVEL ACCESS — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY. Each one placed where a person moving through the dark would need to make a decision. Each one saying: *this way, this is safe, I checked.*

He hadn't just broken in. He'd mapped it. For himself, or—

"For whoever came after him," she said aloud.

"Huh?" Pip had gone ahead to scout the ladder.

"He marked the route. He knew he might not be the last one to need it." She pressed her palm flat against the painted steel beside the hatch, over the place where his knife had cut the little S twenty-two years ago, and the metal was cool and indifferent and absolutely real. "He was leaving a chart for us, Pip. Before I was even born, he was charting the way in for me."

Pip came back and looked at the mark, and then at her, and did the kindest thing he could have done, which was nothing. He just held the hatch's wheel and waited.

The hatch was locked too—but from this side the lock was a simple interior bolt, and the bolt was thrown open. Of course it was. Her father's route ran clean end to end, a channel cut through the facility's security by a man who'd understood, even then, that the truth would need a second attempt.

She spun the wheel. The hatch swung inward on a breath of warmer air that smelled of ozone and old paper and, underneath it, faintly, impossibly, the green-water smell of Meridian Hollow—of drowned streets and river silt—as if the whole river system had followed her upstream and pooled here at its own beginning.

Beyond the hatch: a stairwell descending, concrete steps worn pale at their centers by decades of boots. A sign on the wall, hand-lettered over the printed one, older than both:

RECORDS — SUBLEVEL 4

Tamsin clicked off her light and stood in the amber dark with her hand still on the wheel, listening to the facility breathe around her. Somewhere above, cameras swept their counted circles. Somewhere deeper, servers held the names of people the country had agreed never existed—including, possibly, the name of the man who'd cut this path for her and died somewhere between this stairwell and the river.

"Sublevel four," Pip whispered.

"Yeah." She stepped through, and the hatch sighed shut behind them, and the dark took them gently, like water. "Let's go meet the dead."

Two flights down, the emergency lights changed—from amber to a color she'd never seen in a facility like this, a thin clinical blue—and Tamsin realized, with a cold that had nothing to do with the spring, that the blue was coming from below.

Someone was already working on Sublevel 4.
