## 26. The Archive Below

The corridor smelled of ozone and old paper, which was wrong on both counts. Facilities like this were supposed to smell of nothing—scrubbed air, recycled a thousand times through filters until history itself was strained out. But the lower levels had been sealed since before Tamsin was born, and behind the pressure doors the building had been quietly rotting into its own secrets. Paper decayed. Ink faded. The truth, apparently, got moldy just like everything else.

She moved with her headlamp dialed low, a coin of light on the floor ahead of her boots. Behind her, thirty meters back and holding position at the junction as they'd agreed, Pip waited in the dark with the radio and his heart probably going like a drum line. She'd told him to stay put. He'd told her he'd stay put. Neither of them had believed it for a second, but it was the ritual that mattered.

The stairwell down had been marked on her father's notes in his cramped hand: *SUB-3 — RECORDS — ASK FOR HALLORAN.* No explanation of who Halloran was. Her father had a habit of writing instructions like messages in bottles, addressed to a person who didn't exist yet.

*Ask for Halloran,* she thought, passing a door with a brass plate so tarnished it read only *H LL R N*. Well. She'd asked. Nobody answered.

The records level opened out of the stairwell into a long gallery, and Tamsin stopped dead when her light hit it, because she'd imagined filing cabinets and got a cathedral instead.

Row after row of shelving ran off into the dark, floor to ceiling, industrial gray steel, each bay labeled in stenciled block letters that had gone brown with age. HYDROGRAPHIC SURVEYS. SETTLEMENT INTAKE. POPULATION MOVEMENT — MERIDIAN BASIN. And at the far end, past the others, past everything, a single freestanding cage of chain-link and padlocked gate, the kind banks used for bond storage. Stenciled above it, in letters that someone had tried to scrub and given up on:

**PROJECT LEDGER — DO NOT DUPLICATE**

Her father's handwriting floated up in her memory, unbidden: *They named it after what it killed. That's the joke nobody at the facility gets.*

Tamsin stood in the aisle a long moment, breathing the dead air, listening to the building tick and settle around her like a ship at anchor. Somewhere overhead, footsteps crossed a floor—contractors on night rotation, indifferent, forty tons of concrete between them and her. Then silence again.

She checked her watch. Ninety minutes until the shift change brought fresh eyes to the perimeter cameras. The microfilm key sat in its waterproof sleeve against her sternum, warm from her body, small as a communion wafer. Her father had carried it inside a compass for twenty years so that it could make this one trip. She intended to honor the freight.

* * *

The cage lock was a joke—a 1970s pin tumbler that surrendered to Odile's tension wrench in under a minute, because of course Odile had packed picks in a dive kit, because of course she had. Tamsin filed that away as a debt to repay with coffee and insults, and slipped inside.

Inside the cage, the shelving was different. Denser. Climate-controlled once, though the unit had died years ago and the air hung thick and sweet with decaying cellulose. And the boxes were not gray. They were navy black, government-issue, each one tagged with a string tag and a typed label.

She pulled the first box and set it on the work table someone had left in there decades ago—a scarred oak thing with a magnifying lamp, an ashtray, and a coffee mug fossilized to the surface. Someone had spent years of their life in this cage, alone, cataloguing the end of a town.

Box one: **FERRY MANIFEST — M.V. CORIOLIS — FINAL SAILING.**

Her hands went still on the cardboard.

The Coriolis. Bram's decrypted fragment had named it—the Hollow's missing ferry, the wreck she'd been diving blind for two years, the hull her sonar had mapped and her hands had touched without ever knowing what she was touching. The navy's incident report said lost with all hands in a storm. The manifest in her hands said otherwise. It said the ferry had sailed half-empty by design, its passenger list struck through and re-struck, names removed in a bureaucratic hand until only officials remained—and then, in a final column added by a different typewriter entirely, the names that had been *loaded as cargo*.

Not passengers. Cargo. A town's worth of people, entered in a shipping ledger between crates of equipment and drums of compound, each entry stamped RECEIVED.

Tamsin sat down on the stool because her legs had opinions about standing. She made herself read three entries all the way through. One of them was a family name she'd seen carved into the steeple shelf in Mara's parish, fresh-cut, recent, grief still wet in the grain.

"Okay," she whispered to the empty cage. "Okay." It was what she said to herself at ten meters down, when the dark pressed in and the body wanted to bolt. Okay. You're here. Breathe. Work.

She unclipped the microfilm key from its sleeve and fitted it into the document camera she'd carried the whole way—a battered field unit, ex-surveyor stock, the kind of machine that photographed pages onto 16mm film one agonizing frame at a time. Odile had rebuilt its motor. Bram had calibrated the exposure against every kind of paper they could steal samples of. It would work. It had to work, because there was no second night, no second descent, no coming back down this stairwell.

She fed in the first page. The shutter clicked. The film advanced. Click. Advance. Click.

The sound filled the cage like a heartbeat, like a clock, like a sewing machine stitching something shut.

* * *

Page by page, box by box, the program assembled itself in front of her, and it was worse than anything she'd let herself imagine, because it wasn't chaos. It wasn't a crime of passion or a rogue officer's madness. It was *minutes*. It was agendas. It was a quarterly review.

The weapons program hadn't been hidden *at* Meridian Hollow. Meridian Hollow had been built *for* it—a civilian settlement raised around the research station as cover, population recruited with housing subsidies and river jobs, a living screen of families arranged in front of a chemical works like scenery in a play. And when the program came up for audit, when the first inspectors started asking questions about discharge levels and worker illness, the answer hadn't been to shut down. The answer had been in a memo she now photographed twice, because her hands shook so badly the first frames blurred:

**RECOMMENDATION: REDUCTION OF PERMANENT RESIDENT POPULATION PRIOR TO INSPECTION WINDOW. FERRY CAPACITY SUFFICIENT. TIMING COORDINATED WITH SEASONAL TRANSIT.**

Reduction of permanent resident population. She read the phrase four times. Somewhere in a conference room, men with pensions had used those words, and other men had minuted them, and a third man had signed them, and the words had walked down through the hierarchy gaining signatures the way a river gains tributaries, until they reached a ferry dock and became locked gates and sirens that never meant rescue.

And at the bottom of the memo, in the signature block, the name she'd known was coming and still wasn't ready for:

**A. VOSS, UNDERSECRETARY. APPROVED.**

Cassian's grandfather. Not a footnote. The pen itself.

Tamsin pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes until she saw stars. Then she loaded the next page. Click. Advance. Click. Grief could wait. Grief had waited twenty years; it could wait ninety more minutes.

There was a folder at the back of the last box, thinner than the rest, and she almost missed it because it had no stencil label—only a handwritten one, in ink gone sepia: *S. STERLING — CONSULTANT — SITE ACCESS LOG.*

Her father's name. On a government file, in the cage, in the deep of the place that killed him.

She opened it with fingers that did not feel like hers.

It was his access log—every visit he'd made to this facility under contract as a hydrographic consultant, dates and gate times in a security clerk's neat hand. Eleven visits over fourteen months. And clipped to the log, a single surveillance photograph: her father at the records counter, younger than she'd ever known him, squinting down at a document with the exact expression she wore reading charts—the expression of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like.

On the back of the photo, a clerk's annotation: *Subject requested materials outside scope ×3. Flagged. Escalated.*

Escalated. Two weeks after his last logged visit, the Coriolis went down. Six weeks after that, a salvage crew including Samuel Sterling took a contract they never should have touched, and only the silence came home.

He hadn't stumbled into it. He'd *found* it—here, in this room, maybe at this table, sitting where she sat. He had held these same pages. He had known. And he had spent his last months carrying fragments out through the discharge culvert, annotating ledgers in the margins, hiding a cipher key inside a compass for a daughter who wouldn't understand it for twenty years.

"You knew you might not get out," she said to the photograph. Her voice cracked the dead air of the cage. "You knew, and you sent it forward anyway."

Click. Advance. Click. She photographed his file too. All of it. Especially the flag. Especially the escalation. Let the record show what happened to consultants who asked for materials outside their scope. Let the record show it in his own daughter's hand, on film that would outlive everyone who signed these memos.

* * *

She worked until the film spool ran thin, and then she kept working, triaging now—manifests, memos, the scuttle order itself, which existed as a separate document with its own classification stamp and its own signature and which she photographed first among equals, twice, three times, checking each frame against the lamp. The order authorizing the Coriolis to be sunk at mooring with its cargo aboard, dressed as storm loss. Signed. Countersigned. Filed.

Filed. That was the part that finally broke something loose in her chest—not rage, exactly, but a cold, clarifying astonishment. They'd *filed* it. Somewhere in all these years, some clerk had put this paper in a folder and shelved it in the dark, and the dark had kept it safe, and the dark had waited, and the dark had sent her father's compass to lead his daughter down to it.

Truth doesn't drown. That was the thing the navy had never understood, the thing Voss's grandfather had never understood, the thing every administration since had built its silence on top of and hoped would hold. Truth is buoyant. You can weight it, you can sink it, you can mark the chart NO DUPLICATE and lock the cage—but it waits at the bottom with its arms open, and sooner or later somebody dives.

Tamsin spooled the last of the film, sealed the cassette in its waterproof sleeve, and pressed it flat against her sternum where it had lived all night. Warm. Real. Twenty years of a debt paid down to zero in ninety minutes of shutter clicks.

Then she heard the radio click twice in her earpiece—Pip's signal, the one that meant *movement topside, not emergency, but move*—and the cold came back all at once.

She repacked the boxes exactly as she'd found them, squared the tags, closed the cage, and left the lock hanging open a finger's width, the way she'd found it. Let the archive keep its secrets a few hours longer. It had kept them this long.

At the top of the stairwell, Pip's face appeared around the junction wall, pale in the green glow of the exit sign, and the look on him told her everything before he said a word.

"Shift change came early," he breathed. "Two trucks at the main gate. And Tamsin—" he swallowed, "—one of them's flying Voss family colors."
