## 27. Alarm

The master archive room smelled of ozone and old paper, and Tamsin wanted to stay in it forever.

She didn't have forever. She had maybe eleven minutes before the shift change Odile's stolen schedule promised, and she'd already spent four of them photographing the first drawer.

The room sat three levels below the facility's main floor, a concrete throat at the bottom of the building, lit by strips of fluorescent that hummed like trapped insects. Filing cabinets in military gray ran the length of one wall. Server racks lined the other, their status lights blinking green and amber in the dark like a slow heartbeat. Between them, long tables held boxes—cardboard, water-stained at the corners, labeled in fading marker with years going back further than she'd dared hope.

*1959. 1961. 1963.*

The Hollow drowned in '64.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. She'd expected them to shake the way they'd shaken in the canister, holding her father's handwriting up to the light. But this was different. This was work. This was the job her father had started twenty-two years ago with a pair of bolt-cutters and a route through the discharge culvert, and she was finishing it, and her body understood that as competence, not grief.

The microfilm key fit into the portable scanner Bram had rebuilt from a fish-finder and two dead laptops. She fed it pages from the second box: requisition orders, chemical manifests, transport schedules for the ferry *Corvid's Daughter*—the ferry that was supposed to carry three hundred people out of Meridian Hollow on the last night and carried forty-one instead, most of them officials and their families.

There it was again, in a memo dated six days before the scuttling: *Program assets to be consolidated prior to site closure. Civilian presence complicates closure timeline.*

Civilian presence. Three hundred people. Her father's whole town, reduced to a scheduling problem.

She photographed everything. She photographed the order signed by Aldous Voss, Undersecretary for Special Materials, authorizing "hydrological remediation" of the Hollow district—a signature so casual it could have been approving a catering budget. She photographed the test results from the research station upstream, the ones showing what the weapons program had been leaching into the spring for a decade before anyone decided the evidence was worth more than the town sitting on top of it.

She photographed the list. The full list. Not the partial ledger from the canister—all of it, every name, every date, cross-referenced against ferry manifests that showed who boarded and who was turned back at the gates.

Mara Iles's mother was on page nine. Tamsin checked twice, because Mara deserved to know someone had counted her properly.

"Pip." She keyed the radio low, barely above breath. "Status."

His voice came back thin through the static. "Maintenance corridor's still clear. There's a guy doing rounds on two but he's got headphones in, he's basically a walking playlist. You good?"

"Two more minutes."

"You said that four minutes ago."

"I'm aware." She turned the last page, photographed it, slid it back into its folder exactly as she'd found it, angled the folder two degrees left the way it had been sitting. Her father would have done the same. Leave no wake. That was the first rule of salvage: you take, but you don't disturb. The river always knew.

She killed the scanner, stowed it in the dry bag against her chest, and looked around the room one last time—the gray cabinets, the humming racks, the boxes of names nobody had been allowed to read for twenty-two years.

"Hold on," she told them quietly. "We're coming back for you."

Then the lights changed.

Not off—changed. The fluorescent hum stuttered, dropped an octave, and every status light on the server racks flipped from green to red in the same instant, a wall of small red eyes opening all at once.

Tamsin's stomach dropped before her mind caught up.

"Pip. What did you touch?"

"Nothing!" His voice cracked on it. "I'm touching nothing, I'm standing in a corridor touching nothing—"

An alarm began. Not a klaxon—something worse, something polite. A soft chime, three notes, repeating, the sound of a building clearing its throat. Then a voice over the intercom, calm as a flight attendant: *"Attention. Containment protocol in effect. All personnel proceed to designated stations."*

Containment protocol. Her eyes went to the ceiling, to the pipes running along it, thick ones, sweating condensation—and she remembered the discharge pipes bleeding into the spring outside, the facility built over the old research station, the whole complex married to the river at the water table. A place like this didn't have fire suppression. It had flood suppression. And if the system thought there was a breach—

Water hit the floor.

It came from slots in the base of the walls, thin silver tongues of it, spreading fast across the concrete toward her boots. Testing the space. Learning it.

"Pip, run. Now. Get to the culvert, don't wait for me."

"Tamsin—"

"*Run.*"

* * *

The corridor outside the archive was already ankle-deep and rising.

She went the wrong way. That was the decision she made in half a second, cold and clear: Pip had gone right, toward the maintenance stairs, and the alarm meant whoever tripped it—or whatever sensor had tripped itself—would be answered from the main stairwell, the fast way down. So she went left, deeper, following the emergency map bolted by the door that she'd memorized on the way in without knowing why. SUB-LEVEL 4 — PUMP GALLERY — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY.

Authorized personnel. She'd been unauthorized since birth, practically.

The water climbed her shins. Behind her, somewhere above, doors were banging open—boots on stairs, radios crackling, men shouting coordinates. The contractors weren't chasing her yet. They were securing. Sealing. She heard a heavy mechanical thunk through the concrete, then another, and understood with a lurch that they were dropping blast doors between levels, sectioning the building like a ship taking damage.

Which made her the damage.

The pump gallery door was a steel wheel, the old kind, and it took both hands and her shoulder to turn. She slipped inside and hauled it shut behind her just as the corridor behind filled with flashlight beams sweeping white through the flooded dark.

She stood in blackness, chest heaving, listening to them pass.

"—sensor grid flagged movement on three, near the east riser—"

"—nobody came down the main stair, check the freight lift—"

"—flood protocol's got sub-levels filling, we've got maybe twenty minutes before four is under—"

Twenty minutes. She did the math the way her father had taught her, back when the lessons were disguised as games on the boat: volume of the level, inflow rate, displacement of the equipment. Twenty minutes until the pump gallery was a swimming pool. Less, probably. The river wasn't waiting politely anymore. The river had heard its name called after twenty-two years and it was coming home.

Tamsin felt along the wall in the dark. Pipes, valves, a control panel dead under her fingers—the gallery had gone onto backup power, or no power. Somewhere below her, pumps that hadn't run in decades were either trying to start or had given up entirely. The water around her calves was cold in a way that felt familiar, felt like the Hollow, felt like every wreck she'd ever entered, and she caught herself almost laughing at the absurdity of it.

She'd spent her whole life diving into drowned places. Now the drowning had come to meet her halfway.

* * *

Her radio was a problem. If she keyed it, they might triangulate. If she didn't, Pip wouldn't know where she was, and Pip—she knew him now, knew the set of his shoulders when he was scared and refusing to show it—Pip would come looking.

She keyed it anyway. One click. Two clicks. Their signal: *alive, hiding, don't talk.*

Static. Then, faintly, two clicks back, and her lungs unlocked.

He was out. He had to be out or close to it, moving toward the culvert, and if he had any sense he'd keep going, get to the dredger, get to Odile. She pictured him running through the gorge dark, sixteen years old and furious at the world for giving him another erased town, and she made herself a promise with the water climbing her thighs: she was going to be there when he needed someone to be furious with him.

First she had to not drown in a basement.

The gallery had one other exit—she'd seen it on the map. A service tunnel running laterally, connecting the pump systems to the old research station's infrastructure, which meant, if her father's notes were right, connecting eventually to the discharge culvert. His route. He'd come in through the river's own plumbing, and maybe the river would let her leave the same way.

The tunnel door was jammed. Of course it was. Twenty-two years of rust and disuse, and the wheel wouldn't give more than a few degrees no matter how she threw her weight against it. Water surged around her knees as something upstream opened wider—a valve, a breach, the facility's containment protocol eating its own floors.

Think. Her father's voice, in the old game: *When the boat's sinking, stop bailing and find out where the hole isn't.*

Where the hole isn't. The water was coming from the walls, from the risers—but the gallery's floor drain, the big central sump the pumps used to feed from, was dry. Which meant the outflow side was blocked too, sealed by the same protocol, but the sump shaft itself went *down*, and down was where tunnels went when tunnels were honest about themselves.

She pried the sump grate up with a valve handle. Below it, a vertical shaft dropped into darkness, rungs bolted to the wall, slick with old grease. Cold air breathed up out of it, wet and mineral, and beneath the smell of rust she caught something else—river smell. Silt and weed and living water.

The shaft connected to the outflow line. The outflow line connected to the discharge culvert. Her father's road.

Above her, muffled through steel, she heard the blast door at the top of the gallery grind open, and flashlights cut down through the high grating, and a voice said, "Gallery's flooding, forget it, nothing alive down there anyway."

Nothing alive down there anyway.

Tamsin swung her legs into the shaft.

* * *

The rungs were murder. Grease and rust and cold, and the water pouring in above her soon found the shaft too, trickling past her shoulders, then streaming, then falling in earnest as the gallery gave up its fight. She descended faster than was safe, palms burning, counting rungs because counting kept the panic in a box. Forty. Sixty. Eighty. At ninety her foot found not another rung but a horizontal surface—a ledge, a junction, the outflow tunnel branching away into the dark.

She pressed herself into it as the shaft above became a waterfall. The sound was enormous, total, a column of river hammering down the throat of the facility, and she crouched in the side tunnel with her arms over her head and felt, absurdly, hidden, spared. The river was reclaiming its own, yes. But it seemed willing to negotiate about which parts.

The outflow tunnel ran flat for thirty meters and then began to rise, gently, toward the distant gray suggestion of daylight. Toward the culvert mouth. Toward the spring and the fence and the night.

She waded it. Then she swam it, when the ceiling came down to meet the water and there was nothing left but the gap her father must have known about, the gap he'd timed his breathing for, the gap she now trusted with her life because he had trusted it with his. She took three breaths and went under, pulling herself along the low concrete by feel, the dry bag clutched to her chest with the photographs and the key and every name on page nine, and the dark pressed in and the old fear rose up—the Hollow, the ferry, her father's last dive, all of it circling her like eels—and she counted. Four strokes. Seven. Eleven. Fifteen, and her head broke water in the culvert's upper reach, and there was air, real air, moving air, and beyond it a coin of night sky and the chain-link of the outer fence.

She hung there in the shallows, gasping, laughing once—a single broken syllable that echoed down the pipe—and behind her, deep in the hill, the facility groaned as the river took another floor.

Then she saw the light.

Not the facility's. Not the moon. A handheld beam, bobbing along the fence line outside, coming toward the culvert mouth, and with it a voice she knew, young and ragged and furious with relief:

"TAMSIN! Oh my god, oh my god, I thought—I couldn't get back in, they sealed everything, I've been out here for—" Pip's face appeared at the culvert grate, dirt-streaked, tear-tracked, sixteen years old and trying very hard to be neither. "Are you okay? Are you—did you get it?"

She lifted the dry bag out of the water. It was heavy as a heart.

"Got it," she said. "All of it."

Pip made a sound that was half laugh, half sob, and started working the bolt-cutters—her father's bolt-cutters, still wedged in the fence after twenty-two years, and she watched this kid who'd never met him use his tools to open the way home.

But even as she swam for the grate, even as her hands closed on the rusted steel, part of her stayed down in the dark, in the flooding rooms, in the gray cabinets full of names. Because the facility was drowning, yes. But drowning things float. Papers float. And somewhere above her, men with radios were already deciding what to salvage.

Behind her, through the culvert, she heard the blast doors opening far above—and boots, many boots, starting down.
