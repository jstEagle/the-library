## 28. Drowned Room

The water came up to her chin before she understood the room was a trap.

Tamsin had heard the alarm as a distant, mechanical scream—Pip's name torn out of her mouth by the corridor's echo—and then the contractors had flooded the passage behind her like blood filling a wound, and she'd run the only direction left: down.

Down was always the wrong answer. Her father had taught her that before he'd taught her anything else. *The river forgives everything except going down.*

Now she stood on a maintenance platform in a sub-basement that smelled of rust and old concrete and something chemical-sweet underneath, and the water rose past her knees with the calm inevitability of a thing that had all the time in the world. Above her, the hatch she'd come through had sealed itself with a hydraulic sigh. Somewhere beyond it, boots hammered across grating. Voices. A radio crackling numbers.

She swept her light across the room. Pipes along the walls, thick as her waist, wrapped in insulation gone gray and sagging. A control panel dead for decades. Drainage channels cut into the floor, running toward a sump pump that hadn't turned since before Pip was born. And in the far corner, half-hidden behind a rack of corroded junction boxes, a steel door with no handle.

No exit. No way up. The water at her thighs now, cold enough to make her breath go shallow.

*Okay,* she told herself. *Think.*

But thinking was hard, because her hands were shaking, and because the last time she'd seen Pip he'd been turning back down the corridor toward her, shouting her name, and then the bulkhead had dropped between them like a guillotine. He was sixteen. He was alone up there with men carrying rifles, and it was her fault, because she'd brought him, because she'd let him stow away and then let him stay, because—

*Stop.* She pressed her palm flat against the cold pipe. *You can't help him from inside a drowning room. Breathe. Solve one problem at a time.*

Problem one: air. The ceiling of the sub-basement was maybe three meters above the platform, and the room's volume meant she had—she did the math without wanting to—perhaps forty minutes of breathing space above the waterline, less if the inflow accelerated. Problem two: exit. There wasn't one she could see. Problem three:

Problem three arrived through her earpiece, a burst of static and then Odile's voice, ragged, half-swallowed by interference.

"—Tamsin. Tamsin, if you can hear me, the flood control is automated, it's purging the lower levels, they've triggered a containment flush—" Static ate a full five seconds. "—can't override from outside. You have to find the vent. Bram says every level has one, it's in the original specs, a pressure relief—"

"Odile." Her voice came out thin. "I don't have the specs."

Silence. Then, quieter: "Your father did."

The line died.

Tamsin stood in the rising water and felt the sentence settle over her like the cold. *Your father did.* Of course he had. Aldous Sterling had walked this exact route twenty-two years ago, through this exact culvert, down into this exact basement, and he had found a way out because he'd had to, because he'd been carrying names in a waterproof case against his chest and a navy audit team at his heels.

And somewhere in the facility's systems—maybe still cached, maybe archived, maybe looping forgotten on some maintenance server—there might be a record of how.

Her dive computer had a storage chip. So did the camera mounted on her shoulder rig. Both were rated to depth; both might survive a dunking. But she needed a terminal, and the control panel in front of her was a corpse.

Except.

She played her light over the panel again, slower. Beneath the dead display, behind a hinged cover crusted with mineral scale, there was a diagnostic port—a standard marine-grade coupling, the kind used on dredgers and survey barges for thirty years. The kind she could plug a dive computer into blindfolded.

Her father had used the same port. She knew that the way you know things you've never been told: because he'd taught her the coupling's quirks when she was fourteen, his big hands over hers on the barge's console, saying *the trick is you seat it crooked first, Tammy, always crooked first, or the pins bind.*

She waded to the panel. The water was at her waist now, dragging at her like hands. She flipped the cover, broke the scale away with the heel of her knife, and seated the cable crooked-first.

The screen flickered. Died. Flickered again—and woke, glowing a soft institutional green, displaying a directory tree older than she was.

* * *

She searched for *vent* and found nothing. She searched for *relief*, *pressure*, *egress*, *maintenance*. Nothing but dead ends and corrupted files, the digital equivalent of the rusted pipes around her. The water climbed her ribs. Forty minutes became thirty-five in her head, and she could feel panic circling her like a shark, patient, waiting for her to tire.

Then she tried the search her father would have tried. Not a word. A number.

The culvert route. His route. He'd numbered his passages in his logs—she'd memorized them as a girl, tracing the figures in the margins of his forbidden notebooks while he slept. Culvert access: Route 9. Sub-level approach: Route 11. And the third number, the one he'd never explained, circled twice in red ink like a warning or a promise:

Route 13.

She typed it in.

One file. Unlabeled except for a timestamp dated twenty-two years and four months ago—the date her father went into the Hollow and didn't come out. Helmet-cam archive. 340 megabytes. Status: *unindexed — pending review.*

Pending review for two decades. Nobody had ever looked at it. Or nobody who mattered had.

Her thumb hovered over the key. The water touched the bottom of her chin. In the dark above the hatch, the boots had stopped moving, which was somehow worse than when they'd been moving.

*This is his last hour,* she thought. *Whatever's in this file is the last thing my father ever saw.*

She pressed play.

* * *

The image bloomed green and grainy, resolution like looking through dirty ice. A helmet-cam view, head-mounted, bobbing with each stroke. Water everywhere—not the clear black of deep open water but the chalky, particulate haze of a flooded interior, lit in stuttering cones by a dive torch.

A voice, breath-distorted, narrating in the clipped cadence of a man conserving air and words alike. She knew the voice the way she knew her own heartbeat.

"—route thirteen, descending. Sump level higher than projected. Note for the record: they're flushing the sub-levels. They know I'm in the structure."

Her knees nearly gave. She caught herself on the junction-box rack and made herself watch.

On the screen, her father swam through a corridor she recognized—the same pipe-lined walls, the same sagging insulation, seen from inside a dead man's eyes. The camera swung as he checked his gauges. She heard him exhale, long and controlled.

"Ledger secure. If anyone finds this: the ferry *Corvane* was scuttled under direct order, reference file—" a burst of bubble noise swallowed the designation "—and the discharge program runs on schedule. I'm going to try the vent."

*The vent.* Tamsin's whole body went electric. "Where," she said aloud, to a man twenty-two years drowned. "Where is it, Dad."

As if he heard her, the camera tilted up. On-screen, Aldous Sterling's gloved hand rose into frame, pointing—at nothing, at the memory of a wall—but Tamsin spun, matching the angle against her own surroundings, counting junction boxes the way he'd counted them. One. Two. Three. Behind the third, where on the recording the torchlight caught a seam in the concrete—

There. Here. A vertical seam, hairline-thin, invisible until you knew to look, running floor to ceiling behind the corroded rack.

She shoved the rack aside. It came away screeching, shedding orange flakes into the water. Behind it: a panel, bolted, and stamped into the steel in faded letters almost too shallow to read:

**PRESSURE RELIEF — EMERGENCY EGRESS — MAINTENANCE ACCESS ONLY**

Bolts. Six of them. She had a wrench in her kit and arms full of adrenaline, and the water was at her mouth.

* * *

She got two bolts free before she had to stop thinking in minutes and start thinking in seconds.

The problem was simple and absolute: the panel opened inward-sealed, meaning the water pressure against it—the room filling, the pressure building—was actually helping hold it shut, and every bolt she removed increased the load on the rest. Her father's recording showed him solving it differently. On the tiny screen, he braced his back against the opposite wall, planted both feet on the panel, and pushed with his legs while pulling the last bolt, letting the panel blow inward and riding the surge through.

He'd been stronger than her. He'd also had a full tank. She had maybe six minutes of regulator air left in her backup bottle and a room closing over her head.

She clipped the wrench to her wrist, took three deep breaths of the shrinking pocket of air, and pulled bolt five.

The sixth bolt was stripped. Of course it was. Everything in this place was stripped, rusted, abandoned—except the secret it had been built to keep, which had been maintained perfectly for twenty-two years.

The water passed over her head.

For a moment she floated in the middle of the dark room, weightless, her torch beam swinging wild arcs through the murk, and the panic rose in her throat like her own scream trying to leave. This was how it happened. This was exactly how it happened, in the dark, in the cold, alone, with the surface a rumor—

The helmet-cam file was still playing. Her computer, sealed in its housing, glowed an inch from her face, and in the green light she saw her father's on-screen hand do something she'd missed: tap the panel twice, low, near the floor. Then the recording showed why. A secondary release—a manual shear pin, a small recessed lever, the kind of redundancy engineers build for exactly the person she was right now: alone, underwater, out of options.

She dove. Found the recess by touch, by memory, by inheritance. Set her fingers on the lever the way she'd once set them on her father's compass, and pulled.

The panel blew inward with a boom she felt in her teeth, and the room's entire volume of water funneled through the gap, and Tamsin Sterling went with it—tumbled, scraped, rolled through a concrete throat barely wider than her shoulders, into darkness deeper than the room she'd left.

* * *

The vent shaft climbed at a steep angle, a service crawl built for men with tools and patience, not divers with spent air. She followed her bubbles upward by feel, one hand on the guide rail her father's gloves had gripped two decades ago—she could feel the wear in the metal, a smoothness worn into the rust, and she hated how much comfort she took from it, from being held by the ghost of his grip.

Halfway up, her backup bottle ran dry.

The regulator gave its last wheeze and went silent, and the silence was enormous. She hung in the black water of the shaft, lungs burning, and the old fear—the one from the wreck, from every night dive since, the fear that lived in the space where her father should have been—rose up and said, *this is it, this is how you join him.*

And then, absurdly, she heard his voice again. Not from the computer—from memory, from a summer afternoon on the barge when she was twelve and scared of her first open-water descent, crying into her mask. He'd crouched beside her on the swim step, mask to mask, and said:

*Panic spends air faster than work does, Tammy. Fear is just your body telling you it wants to live. Thank it, and get back to work.*

She thanked it. She got back to work.

Hand over hand up the guide rail, kicking, counting strokes because counting kept the panic paid off—one, two, three, four—her vision narrowing to a gray tunnel, her chest a fist closing around an ember—and then the ember flared white, and her body made the decision her mind wouldn't: she exhaled the last of her reserve in a silver stream and kicked harder than she'd ever kicked, into the narrowing dark, into the—

Light.

Her head broke the surface of a narrow overflow channel, a concrete runnel no wider than a bathtub, open to the night sky, and she dragged herself onto its lip and vomited river water and screamed once—just once, a raw animal sound she'd deny later—and lay there gasping at the stars like a landed fish, alive, impossibly, stupidly alive.

Above her, the facility's fence lines cut the sky into geometric slices. Below her, somewhere under all that concrete, the servers were drowning in the truth, and the river was doing what rivers do.

She laughed. It came out cracked and wet and it sounded nothing like laughter, but it was.

* * *

It took her ten minutes to remember how to be a person, and another five to realize her dive computer had survived the ride, still clipped to her harness, its screen cracked but lit. The helmet-cam file was still loaded. Still paused—frozen on the last frame her father's camera had recorded before the archive cut off.

She looked at it for a long time.

The frame showed the inside of the vent shaft, from below, looking up. At the top of the frame, faint in the torch glow, was a square of lesser darkness—an opening. Daylight, or something like it. The way out.

He'd made it this far. Twenty-two years ago, Aldous Sterling had crawled up this same shaft, through this same channel, and surfaced—maybe here, maybe close—and then he'd gone back. Back down into the facility, back toward the ledger vault, because the surviving crew's testimony said he radioed from inside the perimeter one last time, and whatever he went back for, he never came out with it.

Or he came out with it, and someone made sure he didn't come out at all.

Either way: he'd found the exit. He'd touched freedom. And he'd turned around.

Tamsin sat on the concrete lip with the river singing somewhere below her and understood, finally, the shape of the debt she'd carried her whole life. It was never money. It was never even the ledger. It was this: a man who found the way out and chose the way back, so that the names would survive. And a daughter who had spent her whole life diving wrecks, looking for him in every dark room, when really she'd been looking for the answer to one question—

*Was it worth it?*

She looked at the cracked screen, at the frozen square of light at the top of the frame, and she answered him the only way that mattered.

"It will be," she said. "I'll make it worth it."

Then she was moving—up the maintenance catwalk, along the fence line, toward the tree cover on the facility's eastern flank and the long dark miles between here and the weir where Mara's people would be waiting. Her legs shook. Her hands shook. Her chest hurt with every breath, and every breath tasted like iron and river.

She was halfway to the treeline when her earpiece crackled to life—static, then a voice, young and breathless and so familiar her heart stopped and restarted in the same second.

"Tamsin? Tamsin, it's me, I'm okay, I'm okay—they didn't catch me, I got out, I'm at the—" Pip's voice broke, steadied. "I'm at the weir. Mara's here. Tamsin, you need to see who else is here."

Behind her, the facility's flood sirens finally wound down into silence, and ahead of her, through the trees, the gorge road ran north toward the sound of many voices gathered at the water.
