## 29. Reunion at the Weir

The gorge let us go grudgingly.

Pip and I came down off the ridge in the last grey hour before dawn, our boots slick with dew and the mud that never quite dried this high up. Below us, the river widened out of its stone throat and spread flat and silver toward Weir Nine, and for the first time in two days I heard something other than wind and drones: voices. Human voices, low and deliberate, carrying across the water the way sound does when people are trying not to be heard.

I put a hand on Pip's shoulder and we both went still behind a shelf of granite.

"Is that—" he started.

"Wait."

Three skiffs slid along the weir's upstream face, running dark, their hulls patched with tarred canvas. At the stern of the lead boat stood a woman with a lantern hooded to a coin of light, and even at this distance I knew the set of her shoulders—square, unyielding, the posture of someone who had spent years refusing to be moved. Mara Iles.

Beside me, Pip exhaled like he'd been holding his breath since the checkpoint.

"Lock-keepers," I said. "She said she could raise them." I hadn't quite believed her. Belief was a currency I'd stopped spending freely somewhere around the time my father's boat burned on the water with him inside it. But there they were—the keepers of the old locks, men and women the navy had pensioned off or ignored for thirty years, poling their boats through the dark like it was Sunday morning.

We picked our way down the scree. When we were twenty meters from the waterline, the hooded lantern swung toward us once, deliberately, and swung away again. *Come.* No challenge, no hail. Mara had counted us before we arrived; I was sure of it. She had a way of knowing things about you that you hadn't told her yet.

The nearest skiff bumped against the stones. A boy no older than Pip held the painter, and beyond him an old man with forearms like mooring cable sat motionless at the tiller, watching me with eyes the color of the water.

"Tamsin Sterling," Mara said. Not a greeting. An accounting.

"Mara."

"You look like something the gorge chewed and spat back."

"It did." I climbed aboard. My legs were shaking in a way I didn't want examined, so I sat down hard on the thwart and let the boat take my weight. Pip dropped in beside me, and the old man pushed off without a word, and the weir's long spillway murmured ahead of us like a congregation settling into pews.

"Two days," Mara said. "You were supposed to signal from the ridge."

"Drones changed the route."

"Everything changes the route. That's what routes are for." She crouched in front of me, and in the coin of lantern light her face was all planes and patience. "Did you find it?"

"The facility? Yes. The records?" I touched the waterproof sleeve where the microfilm key rode against my ribs, next to the compass casing that had held it for twenty years without my knowing. "Better than found. Photographed. Every page of the master archive. The program, the ferry order, the discharge logs. Signed by Aldous Voss himself."

Something moved through the boats then—not a sound exactly, but a current. The old man at the tiller turned his head. The boy stopped coiling rope. In the second skiff, a woman I didn't know pressed her knuckles to her mouth.

"Names," someone said quietly. It might have been prayer. It might have been a demand.

"Some of them," I said. "Not all. The rest are still in those servers, and servers can be wiped by one man with a keycard and a bad conscience. Which is why we're not done."

"No," Mara agreed. "We're not."

* * *

They fed us first. That was the Hollow way—I was learning it. Before questions, before plans, before grief: food. The lock-keepers' camp sat in the lee of the weir's abutment, a scatter of tarps and cook-fires tucked into concrete that had been poured before anyone thought to guard it. Someone handed me a tin cup of fish broth and flatbread hot off a plate iron, and I ate like a woman who had been running on adrenaline and river water for forty-eight hours, because I had been.

Pip ate beside me, and slept between one bite and the next, mid-chew, his head tipping onto my shoulder. I let him. He was nineteen and he had crossed the gorge on a broken ankle strapped with duct tape and never once complained, and if he wanted to sleep against a stranger's shoulder in a camp full of strangers, that stranger was going to sit very still until he woke up.

Mara settled across the fire from me.

"You got them out," she said. "The photographs."

"We got ourselves out. Barely. Pip tripped a sensor on the way down—we were lucky the response teams were contractor security, not navy. Contractors chase. They don't cordon."

"Lucky." She said the word like she was tasting it and finding it unfamiliar. "My mother used to say luck is just other people's work arriving on time." She stirred the fire with a length of rebar. "There's more coming tonight. Word moves along the locks faster than the water does. By morning there'll be keepers here from Six, Seven, Eight. Some of them worked these gates when the Hollow was still above the waterline. They remember which officials left on the ferry. They remember who was turned away at the gates."

"I know."

"Do you?" Her eyes came up, firelit and level. "Because I've watched you carry that ledger like it's cargo, Tamsin. Weight to be delivered. And it is. But it's also *people*. My mother is entry forty-one. Entry forty-one has a daughter who learned to swim in floodwater because the sirens came too late. When your photographs reach the hearings—and they will reach the hearings—entry forty-one stops being a number. Understand?"

"Understand," I said. And I did, better than she knew. I had grown up on the wrong side of an official record myself—daughter of a diver the navy called a trespasser and a thief, dead in waters he was forbidden to enter, his name scrubbed from every manifest. I knew what it was to be a number in someone else's ledger. I had simply never known how many others there were.

A whistle sounded from the downstream watch—a single low note, passed hand to mouth down the line of boats. Mara rose instantly. "That's the Odile signal," she said, and something in my chest unlocked that I hadn't known was locked.

"She made it."

"She signaled the pattern your engineer gave us at the parish. Two short, one long." A pause. "She also signaled damage. Slow approach."

I was already moving. Pip woke when I lifted his head off my shoulder, blinking, and I said, "Stay," and he stayed, because he'd earned the right to be ordered around by someone who meant it kindly.

* * *

Odile came up the weir channel with her engine coughing like a smoker, and I saw the damage before I saw her face: the dredger's tender riding three degrees down by the bow, a shrapnel star punched through the port gunwale, the whole starboard rail peeled back like a tin lid. Bram stood at the wheel with a bandage wrapped around his throat and jaw, steering with his elbows. Odile stood in the bow, black with oil, one arm cradled against her chest, and when she saw me on the abutment she started laughing—that dry, hacking laugh of hers that meant fury wearing a joke as a coat.

"Well," she called up. "You should see the other boat."

"What happened?"

"Voss happened." She let Bram bring the tender alongside, and I took her good arm and hauled her onto the concrete. Up close she was worse—burns along her forearm, a gash over her eyebrow glued shut with something that was probably not medical grade, and a look in her eyes I'd only seen once before, the night we lost the barge in the old locks. "He intercepted us below Weir Seven. Two boats, fast, professional. Not navy contractors—private. His money, his flag."

"Mara—" I turned, but Mara was already there, already directing two keepers toward the wounded.

"Bram took a graze covering our run. He'll live; he's too stubborn and too navy for anything else. Deke—" Odile stopped. Worked her jaw. "Deke jumped. Went over the side at Weir Six with a bag. Voss's people pulled him out. His choice, far as I could tell. Or maybe he finally picked his employer."

"And the canister?"

Her silence answered before her mouth did.

"They have it," she said. "The original. Voss has the original ledger, Tamsin. He boarded us personally. Put a gun against the hull plating like he was going to shoot the *boat*, which, honestly, was the most honest thing I've ever seen him do. Took the canister, apologized to me—*apologized*—and said, and I quote, 'Tell her destruction is cleaner than justice.'" She spat over the side. "Then he paid for our fuel. Paid! Like we were a toll."

I stood very still. The weir murmured. Somewhere behind me, Pip had come to stand at my shoulder, awake now, silent.

"He has the original," I said slowly, "but he doesn't have the only copy. He doesn't know about the microfilm."

"Doesn't matter if he does," Odile said. "Here's the part that matters. After he took the canister, he didn't turn downstream. He turned *up*. Straight past us, straight up the gorge channel, running lights dark, headed for the source." She looked at me, and under the oil and the blood her face was stripped bare of everything except the thing underneath. "He's going to purge the servers himself, Tamsin. Tonight. Before any audit, before any inspector, before your photographs can be corroborated against the masters. He's going to burn the truth at its root and keep the original as proof that it never existed."

The cold that went through me wasn't fear. It was arithmetic. Voss had a head start, a fast boat, and a facility passcode. I had a microfilm key, a crew of strays, a camp of lock-keepers, and a woman whose mother was entry forty-one.

"How fast can the keepers move?" I asked Mara.

"Faster than the navy thinks. Slower than I'd like."

"Then we don't need faster. We need simultaneous." I turned to the firelight, to the faces turning toward me—keepers and survivors and one sleeping-deprived teenager, all of them waiting, and God help me, none of them looking at me the way the navy had looked at my father. "Listen. Voss is going to the source to erase the master records. If he succeeds, everything we bled for becomes a story nobody can prove. So we're going to make sure that whatever happens inside that fence tonight, the world outside hears about it *while it's happening*. Mara—your network. Inspectors, federal contacts, whoever Mara's photostat reached. They need to be moving *now*, with a timestamp, so that when the servers go dark—or when they don't—there's a record that people knew. Odile—"

"Already thinking it," she said. "The facility sits on the river's flood-control grid. Old system, pre-contractor, municipal hardware. Somebody kept it alive because nobody ever funded replacing it. If I can get to a control junction downstream of the intake..."

"You could open the sluices."

"I could *talk* to the sluices. Whether they listen depends on how much of the grid Bram and I can wake up before Voss's people notice the lights flickering."

Bram, bandaged, voice a gravel rasp: "I've been dreaming about those schematics since the parish. Give me an hour and a working radio."

"Then that's the plan," I said. "Mara's witnesses move first. Odile and Bram hit the flood grid. Pip—" I looked at him, and he straightened like a mast taking wind. "Pip comes with me. We go back up the gorge road. We get inside before Voss finishes what he came to do."

"You just escaped that place," Odile said.

"Yeah." I touched the compass casing through my shirt, the microfilm key warm against my ribs where my heartbeat had been keeping it company for two days. "And I know the way in now. My father drew me a map twenty years ago. It seems rude not to use it."

Mara stepped close. For a moment she said nothing, just studied my face with that terrible patient attention of hers, the look of a woman who had catalogued every survivor's face in a drowned town and never forgotten one.

"My mother's name is Wren Iles," she said. "Entry forty-one. When you're in there, among all that paper and all those machines—remember that the entries breathe. Make them breathe, Tamsin."

"I will," I said. And for the first time since the sirens of my childhood, I believed a promise I was making.

The keepers were already pushing off. Upstream, past the black shoulder of the gorge, the source facility's perimeter lights burned in a thin orange line against the stars—patient, certain, unaware.

Behind them, small and steady, a second light was moving. Coming down to meet us.

Voss was already on the water.
