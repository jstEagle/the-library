## 30. Voss at the Source

The facility had a smell Voss remembered from his grandfather's study—ozone and old paper and something chemical underneath, like a wound dressed too long. He'd been eleven the last time he stood in that study, watching the old man burn letters in a brass dish while explaining, gently, that some families paid their debts in silence.

He had come to pay his family's debt in fire.

The director met him at the security checkpoint on the upper level, a soft-bellied man named Corliss with the complexion of someone who had spent thirty years under fluorescent light and the eyes of someone who had spent them counting. Voss had done his homework on the flight up. Director Aldous Corliss. Twenty-nine years at the Meridian Headwaters Research Facility. Twenty-nine years of quarterly reports stamped and forwarded, of discharge logs signed, of a pension vesting at sixty with the patience of a man who had never once looked directly at what he was administrating.

"Mr. Voss." Corliss extended a hand. His grip was damp. "We weren't expecting you until the audit cycle."

"I came early." Voss smiled the smile that had closed a hundred deals. "Family business."

Corliss's eyes flicked to the two contractors flanking Voss—men Voss had hired in Smugglers' Reach with cash and a story about document recovery—and then to the case in Voss's hand. "Of course. Your grandfather's old office is preserved on sub-level two. We keep it as a—"

"I know where it is," Voss said. "I've seen the photographs."

He hadn't. But he had dreamed of it often enough that the lie came easily.

* * *

Sub-level two smelled worse. The air recyclers down here were original equipment, and the corridors had the tomblike hush of a place that had been sealed rather than finished. Voss walked past door after door of archive rooms, each with a brass plate and a date range, the whole history of the program laid out in filing-cabinet geography. His grandfather had helped build this. Had signed the orders that put the chemical designation into the water table and the settlement of Hollow's Rest into the river.

Hollow's Rest. That was the settlement's true name. Not Meridian Hollow, not the drowned district—Hollow's Rest, population four hundred and eleven, erased from the maps in a single fiscal quarter. Voss had learned the name three weeks ago from a ledger his grandfather had hidden so well that it had taken a rival salvage diver and a dead man's compass to find it.

He reached the server hall at the end of the corridor. It was a long room, cathedral-ceilinged in the utilitarian way of government-adjacent architecture, rows of black cabinets humming under blue status lights. The master records. Everything: the program, the scuttled ferry, the evacuation order that never went out, the signature at the bottom of every document that mattered—*E. Voss, Deputy Director, Special Projects*.

His grandfather's initials. His blood. His name.

Voss set the case down and opened it. Inside, nestled in foam, was a data-spike—a military-grade purge tool he'd bought from the same smugglers who'd sold him fuel passage, guaranteed to scrub a network so thoroughly that even the audit teams would find only formatting errors. He'd told himself, on the flight up, that this was mercy. That some wounds should be allowed to close. That Tamsin Sterling, wherever she was in this facility or already gone from it, would thank him someday for sparing her the long slow bleed of a public reckoning.

He had almost believed it.

He knelt at the first cabinet and found the maintenance port. His hands were steady. That was the thing no one understood about the Voss family—they were always steady, right up until the moment the water came in.

The spike slid home. A status light turned amber. On the small display, a progress bar began to crawl: *SECTOR 1 — PURGE INITIATED — 2%*.

* * *

"Mr. Voss."

The voice came from behind him, and it was not a guard's voice, and it was not afraid.

Voss turned. Corliss stood in the server hall doorway with a sidearm held low and competent, and behind him were four of the facility's own security staff—not the contractors Voss had bought, whose silence had apparently been resold within the hour. Money, Voss thought, was a current too, and it always flowed downhill to the man who controlled the outflow.

"You're wondering about your men," Corliss said. "They're in the parking structure, being paid twice what you offered them to forget your face. It's remarkable how the market works when the person holding the purse strings is also the person who signs the invoices."

"You'd let me purge the records," Voss said. "It protects you too. Every audit lands on this facility. Every name in that ledger was processed through your office."

"I know exactly what lands on this facility." Corliss stepped into the room, and the door sealed behind him with a hydraulic sigh. "I've known for twenty-nine years. Do you know what I also know? That the audit was already coming before you ever bought your spike. That the inspectors have been scheduled for six weeks. That when they arrive, they will find either a facility that cooperated fully with a federal records review—" he gestured at the cabinets, the amber light, the crawling percentage— "or a facility whose director stood by while a private citizen destroyed evidence. One of those men keeps his pension. The other one shares a cell with you."

Voss's mouth went dry. "The purge is at nine percent. You could stop it. You could—"

"I could." Corliss nodded, almost kindly. "And I will. But you're going to stand there while I do it, Mr. Voss, because you're not leaving this room until I understand exactly who else knows what you know. Who else is coming. Who tipped the inspectors." He raised the pistol a few degrees—not aiming at Voss, exactly, but at the space just beside his head, the way one aims at a problem. "You see, my family has been comfortable for a long time because this facility was boring. You've made it interesting. I take that personally."

* * *

They took his case, his phone, his contractor's radio, and his belt, and sat him in a chair by the maintenance console where he could watch the purge reverse. A technician—young, terrified, refusing to meet his eyes—worked at Corliss's direction, and the progress bar began to crawl backward. *SECTOR 1 — RESTORE FROM BACKUP — 14%*. The facility, it turned out, backed up everything nightly to an offsite vault, because Corliss was not a man who trusted even his own building.

Voss sat with his hands cuffed to the chair's armrest and did the arithmetic of his life.

He had told himself, coming up the river, that he was doing this for Tamsin. That destroying the ledger was protecting her, the way you protect someone by taking a bullet meant for them. And beneath that story, in the sediment where the true ones settle, was the older story: the Voss name, polished by three generations, and the terror—genuine, physical, ancestral—of watching it dissolve in public. His grandfather had died comfortable. His father had died comfortable. The whole architecture of Voss respectability had been built on four hundred and eleven people who were never allowed to have existed, and Voss had inherited not the crime but the maintenance of it, the way you inherit a house with a bad foundation and spend your life pretending the floors are level.

And then there was Tamsin Sterling, who had never pretended anything in her life, who dove into wrecks the navy pretended weren't there, whose father had died carrying the names of the dead in a strongbox. He had offered her a trade—her father's cleared name for the ledger—and she had looked at him with something worse than hatred. She had looked at him with recognition. *You and I are both carrying our fathers*, that look had said. *The difference is what we decided they were worth.*

"Who else knows?" Corliss asked again. He had asked four times now, pacing the length of the server hall, and each time Voss had given him a different partial truth, because a man protecting his pension wants specifics, and specifics could be fed to the wrong ears.

"The inspectors come Thursday," Voss said. "That's all I know. I'm not your leak, Corliss. I'm your problem, and my problem is bigger than both of us."

Corliss stopped pacing. "Explain."

"The ledger has copies. Plural. One is already outside this building. One is in the hands of people who have spent the last month running my river, past my checkpoints, through my locks, and every one of those lock-keepers and harbor police and smugglers has now been paid by someone to look the other way, which means every one of them knows something is moving upstream. You can't arrest a rumor, Director. You can only confirm it." Voss leaned forward as far as the cuffs allowed, and for the first time since the chair, he felt something like his old self—the negotiator, the closer, the man who could find the price of anyone. "So here is your position. In three days, federal inspectors walk into this facility. Either they find the records intact and a director who assisted a voluntary disclosure—cooperating witness, immunity discussion, pension maybe dented but pension intact—or they find a hole where the records were and a director who detained a private citizen without authority and destroyed evidence on his own initiative. I'm the only man in this building who can make the first story true."

Corliss was quiet for a long moment. The amber light crawled: *RESTORE — 61%*.

"You're negotiating," Corliss said finally. "Handcuffed to a chair in my basement, and you're negotiating."

"I'm a Voss," Voss said. "It's the family business. We negotiate with whatever's left."

But even as he said it, he heard the hollowness under it—the sound of a man who had spent his whole life buying exits discovering that this room had none. His grandfather had signed the orders from a leather chair. His father had managed the silence from a boardroom. And he, Cassian Voss, third of his name's long maintenance, was cuffed to a maintenance console while a technician restored the evidence of his family's crime, waiting to learn whether the inspectors would arrive to find him a cooperator or a corpse with a tidy incident report.

Somewhere above them, muffled through three floors of concrete, there came a sound that did not belong to the facility's ordinary hum. A low, rhythmic thrumming, deep and hydraulic, like a great heart beginning to beat somewhere in the building's bones.

The technician looked up from his console, frowning. "Sir? The flood-control telemetry just went active. Somebody's in the sluice system."

Corliss turned toward the console, and for the first time, the director's face showed the expression of a man watching his pension walk out of his hands.

Voss sat very still, and felt—beneath the fear, beneath the shame, beneath thirty years of inherited silence—something that took him a moment to recognize, because he had never once felt it in his family's service.

Hope.

The river was coming. And for the first time in his life, Cassian Voss was on the side of the water.
