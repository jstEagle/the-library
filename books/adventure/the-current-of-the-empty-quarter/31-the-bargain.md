## 31. The Bargain

The director's office smelled of old carpet and newer fear.

Tamsin sat across the desk from him with her hands folded in her lap, the way her father had taught her to sit when she wanted a man to underestimate her. Keep your hands still. Keep your eyes soft. A man who thinks he's winning tells you everything.

His name was Aldous Renner. She'd read it off the facility roster Odile had pulled before the comms blackout, and now she watched him live inside the title the way a man lives inside a coat two sizes too small. He was perhaps sixty, silver at the temples, with the careful grooming of someone who still believed appearances were a currency anyone accepted. His desk was immaculate. His hands were not steady.

Between them on the blotter sat a single sheet of paper, and beside it, a black case the size of a breadbox.

"You understand my position," Renner said.

"I understand you've got a problem," Tamsin said. "I'm looking at it."

His mouth did something that in a younger man might have been a smile. "Ms. Sterling. I've read your file. Or rather, the file that used to exist before certain parties had it amended. You were a promising naval diver once. Your father was—" He stopped, chose a different road. "Your father was a complication."

"He was a witness," Tamsin said. "There's a difference. You'd know. You've spent thirty years making sure nobody learned it."

Renner exhaled through his nose and looked at the window. Beyond the glass, the headwaters of the river ran silver over the rocks, clean as a lie, and below the waterline the discharge pipes bled their gray plume into the current. From this office the plume was invisible. From this office, the river looked like what it was supposed to look like.

"Here is what I'm offering," he said. "You give me the ledger originals. The canister, the microfilm, the photographs if you've taken any. In exchange, I give you Mr. Voss—alive, uncharged, and on the first transport downriver. And I give you silence. Your record restored. Your father's record restored. Sterling, R. — honorably discharged, commendation intact, death reclassified as a line-of-duty casualty. There will be a ceremony, if you want one. There are people who owe your family more than they'd like to admit, and they would very much like to pay it in the only coin they have left."

The room was quiet. Somewhere below them, a pump cycled. The building breathed.

"And the names," Tamsin said. "The hundred and forty-one people on that ledger. The ferry. The parish that isn't on any map. What happens to them in your version?"

"They remain what they have always been," Renner said, and here his voice gentled, which was the most obscene thing he'd done yet. "A tragedy. Unattributable. The program is dead, Ms. Sterling. The men who ordered it are dead. What survives is an institution, and institutions don't drown, they only leak. You can plug a leak, or you can sink the whole hull and take every man aboard down with it—including yourself, including Voss, including a teenage boy who is, I'm told, currently sitting in your dredger's hold eating our coffee."

"Pip drinks it black," Tamsin said. "It's a whole thing."

"Ms. Sterling—"

"You want to know what I think?" She leaned forward. "I think you're not offering me a deal. I think you're offering me a receipt. I take your money, I take my name, and somewhere downriver in about four years, a file gets reopened and my father goes back to being a complication, and this time nobody bothers to offer me anything, because I already sold. That's not a trade. That's a subscription."

Renner's composure held, but she saw the calculation shift behind his eyes—the recalculation of a man who had prepared for grief and gotten arithmetic instead.

"Then let me be plainer," he said. "The inspectors Mara Iles's people have been calling for weeks—they're real. I know. I've read the same traffic you have. They arrive within days, possibly hours. When they arrive, I will hand them a facility that is clean, a program that was terminated twenty years ago, and a security incident involving an armed intruder, one Cassian Voss, currently in custody, who will be charged, tried, and buried under a federal facility for the rest of his natural life. Unless."

"Unless."

"Unless the ledger originals are in my hands before they cross the gate. Because the ledger is the only document that ties this site to the Hollow. Everything else—the orders, the manifests, the chemical designations—it's all deniable. Redactable. The ledger is a list of names with dates and a chemical compound, and names are not deniable. Names have mothers. I know that better than anyone in this building." He laid his palm flat on the blotter. "Give it to me, and Voss walks, your name is clean, and the inspectors find a facility that has already cooperated fully with a private recovery effort. Everyone goes home."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I don't need your answer," Renner said. "I have Mr. Voss. And Mr. Voss, despite everything, has been remarkably cooperative since we removed him from his own people's custody. Grief makes men pliable. He wants his family's name buried as much as I do. Perhaps more. Perhaps he's already told me where your crew is moored."

Tamsin let the silence sit. Let him watch her jaw tighten. Let him think the hook had set.

Because that was the thing—Voss *had* been taken. Odile had told her over the wire an hour ago, voice flat as a sounding line: *they moved him to the secure wing, two guards, card access only, and Tamsin, the flood-control console is in the sub-level directly beneath his cell.* Which meant the trade was real, and the trap was real, and both of them were sitting in the same room wearing each other's clothes.

"Give me a minute," Tamsin said.

"Take five."

She stood and walked to the window. Below, the river ran. Somewhere under that silver skin, her father had swum with bolt-cutters in his teeth, and somewhere downstream the Hollow lay drowned beneath forty years of green water, and somewhere in the secure wing below her feet Cassian Voss—traitor, rival, and as of three nights ago, a man who had put a canister of the dead into her hands rather than let the river take it—was sitting in a cell deciding what kind of ending he deserved.

*The originals for the silence. The dead for the living.*

Her father had a rule about trades like that. He'd kept it scrawled inside his dive log in grease pencil, the last page, the page she hadn't found until Meridian Hollow: *Never trade what can't be replaced for what can. The river replaces everything. It just takes its time.*

"Here's my offer," she said, turning around. "I give you the canister. The original ledger, complete, all hundred and forty-one names. Not a copy—your people can verify that themselves, Bram's good but he's not that good. In exchange: Voss released into my custody before the inspectors arrive, my father's record amended in writing, with the amendment order signed and dated tonight, and my crew walks out of this valley without a tail. That's the deal. That's the only deal."

Renner studied her for a long moment. "You're certain the canister is intact?"

"Odile sealed it in wax and steel after the rapids. It's on the dredger. I can have it here within the hour."

"And the microfilm key?"

"Destroyed," Tamsin said, and watched him decide to believe her.

She had never been a good liar. Her father used to say her face was a chart of every current running underneath it. But grief, she was learning, was a kind of mask too—grief made you look broken, and broken people were safe, and safe people were ignored, and ignored people had all the time in the world.

Renner stood and extended his hand across the desk.

"Within the hour, then."

She shook it. His grip was dry and cool and it held on a half-second longer than it needed to, and she let it, because justice, it turned out, wore whatever face the room required. Tonight it wore hers.

* * *

The corridor outside the office was institutional green and lit by humming tubes. A contractor escort fell in beside her—young, bored, rifle slung with the casual negligence of a man who had never been shot at—and walked her toward the visitor's gate without speaking.

Tamsin counted doors. She counted cameras. She counted the turns: left at the fire point, right at the stairwell, down two flights to the sub-level where, if Odile's schematics were right, the flood-control console sat in a room the size of a chapel, wired into the sluice gates that had been installed when the research station was converted—gates built, ironically, to protect the servers from exactly the kind of flood the river had always promised.

At the gate, the escort stopped. "Transport's not back yet. You'll wait in the holding area."

"Fine."

The holding area was a cinderblock room with a bench and a water cooler. Through its single window she could see the motor pool, the fence line, and beyond it the darkening gorge. Somewhere out there, Pip was sitting on a ridgeline with a pair of binoculars and a radio, being fifteen and terrified and absolutely reliable, which was the most dangerous combination she knew.

She took out the radio they'd disguised as a battery tester and pressed the transmit stud twice. Pause. Twice more. Odile's signal for *proceed*.

The reply came as a single click. Then, thirty seconds later, a second click—Odile confirming she was inside the maintenance conduit, moving on the console.

Tamsin sat down on the bench and did the math. Odile needed forty minutes to splice into the flood-control system and set the diversion: river water through the server halls, not to destroy the records—never that—but to force an evacuation, trip every failsafe, and put this facility in front of the inspectors with its doors open and its secrets floating in the corridors. Mara's people had already tipped the federal office. All Odile needed was time.

And time was what a bargain bought.

She thought about Voss in his cell two floors down. She thought about him handing her the canister in the rising water, his face gray, his family's name finally worth less to him than the truth. *I'm not doing it for you,* he'd said. *I'm doing it so my grandfather's victims stop being mine.* And she had taken the canister and hated him a little less, which was its own kind of betrayal of her father, and she was done pretending otherwise.

The water cooler gurgled. Outside, a transport engine started.

Tamsin stood, walked to the window, and watched the motor pool lights come on one by one, and thought: *Hold, Odile. Hold. The river's coming, and this time it's on our side.*

Then the door behind her opened, and a voice she knew said, "Tamsin."

She turned.

Cassian Voss stood in the doorway between two guards, wrists cuffed in front of him, and his eyes went from her face to the window to the radio in her hand, and she saw the exact moment he understood what she was doing—and chose, with a small, exhausted nod, to help her do it.

"Mr. Voss," she said loudly, for the guards' benefit, "you look terrible."

"I've been having a very frank conversation with the director," he said. "About the canister." His gaze flicked to the ceiling, toward the sub-level, toward the sound—faint, almost subliminal—of a sluice gate beginning, somewhere far below, to test its seals.

"It's coming," Tamsin said. "Within the hour."

"I know," Voss said. "I heard the river."

And under the hum of the lights, beneath the concrete, beneath the floor, the headwaters of the current that had started all of it began, very quietly, to rise.
