## 32. Opening the Sluices

Odile had spent thirty years making water go where she told it to, and in all that time she had learned one law above every other: the river does not destroy. It only moves.

People got this wrong constantly. They thought of floods as violence, as catastrophe, as the river turning on them. But Odile had stood in enough pump rooms and read enough gauges to know better. The river was indifferent and it was honest, and those two qualities looked like cruelty to anyone who'd built something they shouldn't have built. You put a town on a floodplain, the river took it back. You sank a ferry full of civilians to hide your paperwork, the river kept it for you—cold and dark and patient—until somebody came asking. The river didn't judge. It just remembered everything and forgot nothing, and eventually it delivered.

Tonight she intended to make it deliver.

She crouched in the maintenance gallery beneath the facility's east wing with Bram's tablet glowing against her knees, tracing the flood-control schematics for the fourth time. The drawings were old—pre-renovation, pre-classification—but water didn't care about renovations. Water cared about elevation, pressure, and openings, and Odile had spent the last six hours finding the openings.

"You're sure about the sequencing?" Bram whispered beside her. He was pale in the green light of the screen, his ex-navy haircut growing out ragged, his hands steady even when his voice wasn't.

"I'm sure about nothing," Odile said. "I'm confident about three things. One: the primary drainage for sub-levels one through four runs through this gallery." She tapped the schematic. "Two: the emergency sluice gates at the old intake are mechanically operated, not networked, which means no contractor can lock them out remotely. Three: the server halls sit eleven feet below the river's current level."

"That last one's the whole plan, isn't it."

"That last one is the entire thesis." She allowed herself the ghost of a smile. "Tamsin wants the records exposed, not erased. So we don't blow anything. We don't burn anything. We invite the river in politely, and we make the building evacuate itself."

Bram rubbed his face. "And the inspectors?"

"Mara's people tipped them twelve hours ago. Federal team's already staged downriver at Weir Nine. All they need is a facility-wide evacuation order—an *emergency*—to walk in without a warrant fight. Contractors can't seal the gates around an active flooding event without explaining why they sealed civilians inside." She snapped the tablet dark. "The river forces the door. We just turn the handle."

* * *

Getting to the old intake meant crossing four hundred yards of open ground between the discharge culvert and the original 1960s waterworks—a squat concrete block half-swallowed by blackberry bramble, decommissioned on paper but never actually demolished because demolition required paperwork and paperwork left trails. Everything about this place was like that, Odile was learning. Nothing destroyed, only buried. The whole facility was a sediment layer of other people's sins.

They moved fence line to fence line while Pip watched the road from the ridge with a pair of binoculars and a radio he'd modified himself, because of course he had. The kid could strip a transceiver faster than Odile could strip a pump, and he'd taken to their little insurgency with the terrifying calm of someone who had already lost a hometown once and refused to lose another.

"Road's clear," Pip's voice crackled. "One drone on the far loop, heading north. You've got maybe six minutes before it comes back around."

"Six minutes," Bram repeated. "For a gate that hasn't been opened since before I was born."

"It'll open," Odile said. "Rust is just iron being honest about its age."

The intake housing smelled of wet concrete and forty years of moss. Inside, the sluice mechanism rose out of the dark like something industrial and holy: a handwheel eight feet across, geared down through a worm drive into a shaft that dropped into the earth, connected to a gate that weighed more than the boat they'd lost in the locks. Odile put her palm flat against the wheel's rim and felt the cold come up through her skin, and for a moment she thought of Tamsin—somewhere above them now, waist-deep in negotiations with a man who held hostages and ledgers both—and made herself stop thinking about it. Worry was ballast. She'd jettisoned hers years ago.

"Bram. Light."

He clipped a work lamp to the gearing and the mechanism leapt out of the dark, furred with corrosion, threaded with dead spider silk. Odile worked her way along the drive train with a wire brush and a squeeze bottle of penetrating oil, clearing the worm gear tooth by tooth, checking the shaft bearings by feel. It was slow, exacting work, the kind she loved—the kind where competence was the only prayer that ever got answered.

"Talk to me about flow rates," Bram said. He'd set up at the gallery junction below, monitoring the pressure differential between the river side and the dry side of the system.

"When the gate opens, we get gravity feed through the old main. Roughly nine cubic meters per second into the distribution manifold. The manifold branches—east wing, west wing, deep storage." She wiped her hands. "We want east wing first. Server halls. Slow fill, controlled. Not a wall of water—a rising tide. Enough to trip every moisture sensor and fire-suppression interlock in the building, force a full evacuation protocol, and make the contractors choose between their servers and their story."

"And if the director cuts power to the whole sub-level?"

"Then the servers die warm and the data dies with them, which is why Tamsin's upstairs keeping him busy." She checked her watch. "Which is why we move now."

* * *

The wheel fought her.

Of course it did. Eight feet of cast iron geared through sixty years of silence, and Odile threw her whole body against it, boots braced on the housing floor, arms burning, breath sawing. It gave a centimeter. Then another. Somewhere below them, deep in the earth, a gate that hadn't moved since the Nixon administration began grinding upward out of its seat with a sound like a whale clearing its throat.

"Flow confirmed," Bram called up from the junction, his voice tight with something between fear and awe. "I've got movement in the old main. Pressure's equalizing. Odile—it's *running*."

She kept turning. Centimeter by centimeter, the wheel came around, and beneath her feet she felt it begin—that low, vast tremor, the sound of a river being asked to do something new. Not a flood. A tide. Nine cubic meters per second of the same water that ran past Meridian Hollow, that lay over the ferry, that held every name in the ledger in its cold green memory, now climbing the stairs of the building that had tried to buy its silence.

"East branch valve," she said, sliding down the ladder. "Your station."

Bram stood at the manual override, hands on the lever, waiting for her nod. His jaw was set. She knew what this cost him—he'd served under officers who'd signed the orders that built this place, had spent years telling himself the navy's sins were bureaucratic accidents rather than choices. Tonight there were no accidents. Tonight he was choosing, with both hands, on the record of his own body.

"Now," Odile said.

The lever went over. Somewhere in the walls, water found the east branch and began to rise.

* * *

They climbed back to the ridge to watch, because there was nothing left to steer—the system would do what gravity and plumbing dictated—and because Odile wanted to see it. She needed to see it.

Below them, the facility lit up like a struck hive. Amber strobes swept the compound. Sirens wound up through the night air, overlapping, contradictory. Flood doors slammed in sequence. Voices crackled over contractor channels, sharp with confusion: *moisture alarm sub-level two—no, sub-levels two AND three—where's the source—negative, no breach in the perimeter—repeat, no external breach—*

"No external breach," Bram murmured, almost smiling. "That's going to be a fun incident report."

"They'll figure it out eventually," Odile said. "By then the inspectors will be through the front gate."

Headlights were already moving on the downriver road—two vehicles, then four, running dark until the last stretch, then blazing. Mara's network had done its work; somewhere a lock-keeper had made a call, a ferry pilot had relayed a message, a survivor of a drowned parish had passed word along a chain of people who had spent twenty years pretending not to remember. The river had witnesses everywhere. It always had. That was the thing the navy had never understood when they scuttled the ferry: you cannot sink a memory. You can only delay the salvage.

Pip lowered the binoculars. "Evacuation order just went out. Whole sub-level complex. They're pulling everyone up to the surface compound—including the security detail from the detention area." He glanced at Odile. "Including whoever's guarding Voss and the director."

"Then Tamsin's window is opening," Odile said. "Come on. We're going down there."

"Down *there*? Into the—" Pip gestured at the strobing chaos below. "You want to walk INTO the—"

"The evacuation corridor passes the east loading bay," Odile said, already moving. "Everyone's running UP and OUT. We're the only idiots running DOWN. Which means the corridors will be empty, the badge readers will be fail-safe unlocked, and nobody will be looking at the one place the water hasn't reached yet." She paused at the treeline, silhouetted against the strobing amber light, and permitted herself a real smile. "Water goes where I tell it, Pip. Right now I'm telling it to clear us a road."

* * *

Inside, the facility was pandemonium in reverse—hundreds of people flowing one direction, sirens screaming, sprinkler heads hissing uselessly in corridors that weren't actually on fire, and three figures moving against the current like salmon. Odile led, following the schematics in her head, counting doorframes. Bram covered their rear. Pip drifted behind them both, small and quick, lifting keycards off distracted contractors with fingers so light they should have been registered weapons.

Sub-level two was already ankle-deep and rising. The water was cold and clear and utterly unbothered by the emergency lighting, and Odile felt something loosen in her chest as she waded into it—not triumph exactly, but recognition. This water had come a long way. From the spring, down through the gorge, past the drowned parish, past the Hollow, gathering witness the whole way. And now it had arrived, right on schedule, at the room where the truth was stored.

The server hall doors stood open, fail-safe released, steam curling off the cooling units as technicians fled. Racks of drives blinked amber in the half-dark, still powered, still humming, still holding twenty years of orders and manifests and signatures—including, if Tamsin's photographs were right, the order signed by a Voss that had sent a ferry to the bottom with its passengers' names struck from the manifest.

Odile stood in the doorway, water swirling around her shins, and let the river lap at the threshold of the archive.

"Not tonight," she said quietly. "Not you. These ones float."

Behind her, Bram had his radio out, relaying to Mara's chain: coordinates, access codes lifted from a fleeing technician's lanyard, confirmation that the master records were intact and the inspection team was through the outer checkpoint. Somewhere upstream, Tamsin was moving. Somewhere downstream, lawyers were waking up. And here, in the rising water, Odile put her hand on the nearest rack and felt it hum against her palm like a living thing.

The river rose around her ankles, patient and honest, carrying everything it had ever been given toward the light.

Then her radio crackled—Pip's voice, stripped thin: "Odile. East stairwell. They're not evacuating the sub-basement. Somebody's sealed it from the surface, and Tamsin's down there."
