## 33. Standoff in the Rising Water

Cassian Voss had spent his whole life learning how powerful men behaved when the water came in, and the answer was: badly.

Director Halloway stood waist-deep in the server hall with a pistol in one hand and a data slate in the other, and he was doing what powerful men always did—negotiating with physics. The flood-control system Odile had tapped was not a weapon; it was a river, and rivers did not take offers. Water sheeted down the stairwells in gray ribbons, pooled across the raised floor grates, climbed the racks of humming machines like something patient coming home to a house it had owned first.

"Ms. Sterling." Halloway's voice carried strangely in the flooded hall, flattened by the water, stripped of its boardroom warmth. "You understand I have no choice."

"Everyone says that," Tamsin said. She stood on the far side of the room, near the breach where the culvert emptied in, her dive knife sheathed and her hands empty and visible. "The navy said it when they scuttled the ferry. Your contractors said it at Weir Nine. It's the most popular sentence in this country, Director. *I have no choice.* Nobody ever means it."

"I have thirty years of pension in this building."

"You have forty years of bodies under your spring," Tamsin said. "You signed the discharge permits yourself. I've seen them."

Voss watched from the gallery above, half-hidden behind a shattered cable tray, his wrists still raw from the zip ties Halloway's security man had used. He should have been gone. That was the plan—his plan, such as it had survived contact with the last three days. Get free of the director, get down the maintenance spine, get to the boat, get out before the inspectors Mara's network had summoned arrived to find him standing in the wreckage of his family's name. His lawyers could manage an audit. They could not manage a photograph of Cassian Voss wading through a drowning archive beside Tamsin Sterling.

But he wasn't moving. He noticed that about himself the way you notice a current taking the boat—not all at once, but as a slow realization that the steering has stopped answering.

Below, Halloway raised the slate. "This is the master index. Everything. If you come any closer, I wipe it."

"You'll wipe a copy," Tamsin said. "Odile already mirrored the array. Ask your own IT contractor—he's the one who sold us the access codes. You bought your secret from people who sell secrets for a living, Director. What did you think they'd do?"

Halloway's jaw worked. The water reached his belt now. Somewhere below the floor, a coolant pump screamed and died, and the sound it made going down was almost human.

*He'll do it,* Voss thought. *Not because it works. Because men like him would rather burn the evidence than be seen surrendering it.* He had grown up watching that instinct at family dinners—his grandfather's generation, the ones who never used the word *program*, who called the Hollow "the site" and the dead "the relocation." Shame, in the Voss house, had never been an emotion. It had been a filing system.

His grandfather had signed the order scuttling the ferry with a fountain pen. Voss knew because he'd held the pen once, at fourteen, turning it over in its velvet case while his father explained that some names were load-bearing. *You don't tear down a wall to see what it's holding up, Cassian. You inherit the wall.*

He had inherited the wall. He had spent forty years adding to it—the salvage contracts, the informers, Deke, the quiet purchase of every copy of every ledger that surfaced. He'd told himself it was containment. He'd told himself it was mercy, even, in the last few weeks: better these families live without knowing, better the country keeps its clean story, better one old man's guilt dies with him than a hundred careers die with him.

Then he'd watched Tamsin Sterling read her father's handwriting in the margins of a drowned ledger, and understood that he had never once been protecting anyone. He had been protecting the pen.

* * *

The water took another step.

It came up the hall in pulses now—Odile working the sluice logic somewhere upstream, feeding the building the river in measured sips, enough to force evacuation, not enough to drown the proof. Voss had to admire it. It was exactly the kind of elegant, furious engineering Tamsin collected around herself the way other people collected crew. Odile didn't destroy things. She made destruction unnecessary.

Halloway's security chief had fled ten minutes ago with the rest of the day shift, which meant the director's pistol was pointed at a woman who had more right to be here than anyone alive, and there was no one left to pretend otherwise.

"The ledger," Halloway said. "The original canister. You have it. Give it to me and I'll lose it in the flood—no theft, no testimony, nothing for the inspectors to find. You walk out clean. Everyone walks out clean."

"That's not clean," Tamsin said. "That's just wet."

"My family—" Halloway stopped himself, and Voss saw the exact moment the man heard what he'd almost said, saw the word *family* arrive in his mouth and taste wrong. Directors didn't have families in moments like this. They had exposure.

Tamsin took one step forward through the water. It dragged at her knees. She didn't flinch.

"Forty-one names on the partial ledger," she said. "Mara Iles can recite eleven of them from memory. Her mother is the fourth. There were six hundred people in that settlement, Director, and your ferry took nineteen officials across and came back empty. My father died carrying their names because your predecessor decided names were a liability. So no. I'm not giving you anything. You're standing in the only honest water in this building, and I'd suggest you decide what kind of man drowns in it."

Halloway raised the pistol.

And Voss moved.

Later he would not be able to reconstruct the decision. There wasn't one. There was just the wall, forty years tall, and the sudden unbearable knowledge that he was tired of holding it up—that the weight had never once been shared, that his grandfather had handed it down like an heirloom and called it duty, and that Tamsin Sterling was the first person he'd ever met who treated the weight as something that could be put down.

He came down the gallery stairs two at a time, boots hitting water, and shouted—"Halloway!"—and the director swung toward the new voice, and in that half-second of divided attention Tamsin closed the distance and had the man's wrist, twisting, the pistol clattering into the flood and vanishing between the floor grates with a sound like a coin dropped down a well.

Then it was the three of them, chest-deep in rising water and history, breathing hard.

"Sterling," Voss said.

She looked at him. Her hair was plastered flat, her face was gray with exhaustion, and her eyes went straight past his face to his hands—to what he was carrying.

The original canister. Sealed, river-marked, her father's salvage stamp still legible on the cap. The one his auditors had paid Deke to find. The one he'd taken off Odile at gunpoint two days ago and told himself, all the way downstream, that burning it was the kindest available act.

"You came back," she said. Not a question. An accusation wearing a question's clothes.

"I came back," he said.

"Why?" And there it was underneath: *so I know whether to hate you.* He had earned that look over a lifetime. He intended to answer it honestly, once, because he might not get another chance and because honesty was the only currency he had left that wasn't counterfeit.

"Because my grandfather signed the order," Voss said. "Because my father buried it. Because I spent twenty years making sure it stayed buried, and I told myself it was mercy, and it was never mercy. It was the pen." He held the canister out over the water between them. "Your father died carrying this. I'm not going to be the reason it stays lost. Take it."

Tamsin didn't move. "What's in it for you, Voss? Your name clears if this burns. You said so yourself. You offered me my father's reputation in exchange for it—you priced both our fathers and thought you were being generous."

"I know what I said."

"So why?"

He looked at the water. It had reached his sternum. Behind them the server racks were dying one by one, each shutdown a small dark star going out, and through the breach the river kept arriving, indifferent and exact, doing what it had done to the Hollow forty years ago and would do long after everyone in this room was a name on somebody's list.

"Because there are six hundred people who were never allowed to exist on any record," he said, "and my family is the reason. You can't clear my name and theirs at the same time, Sterling. I finally did the arithmetic." A breath. "They win."

For a long moment she just looked at him, and he braced for contempt—it would have been fair, it would have been earned—and instead what crossed her face was something worse. Recognition. The look of a woman seeing another carrier set down a load she knew by weight.

She took the canister.

Her hands shook. He pretended not to see, because pretending not to see was the last courtesy he had to offer her.

"Hold it above the water," she said, voice rough. "If we lose it to the flood after everything—"

"We won't." He lifted it high, arm burning, and found that the small absurd effort steadied him. "Where's your crew?"

"Odile's at the sluice controls. Bram's with Mara's people at the weir gate. Pip—" Something moved across her face, quick as a fish. "Pip was covering the retreat. He's either outside or he's the reason the alarms stopped."

"He's fine," Voss said. "I passed him on the service stairs. He had a fire extinguisher and a plan. Terrifying child."

Something that was almost a laugh escaped her, strangled by adrenaline. Then Halloway, who had been standing silent in the water with the particular stillness of a man recalculating, spoke.

"You're both fools," the director said quietly. "The inspectors won't come. I've made calls. This facility has friends at every level that matters, and by morning the story will be a mechanical failure and a tragic accident involving two trespassers." He turned toward the far door, wading, dignity intact even at chest depth, and Voss understood suddenly that this was the true danger—not the pistol, not the slate, but the machine of quiet arrangements that would grind forward the moment they let this man reach a telephone.

Tamsin saw it too. "He doesn't leave this room."

"And how do you propose we stop him?" Voss asked. "We're not police. We're not even good people, depending on the week."

Above them, through the broken skylight, the sky flickered—once, twice—a stutter of light that wasn't lightning. Then a voice, tinny and enormous, rolled across the compound from loudspeakers neither of them had known existed until that moment:

"*This is a federal inspection action. All personnel evacuate the facility immediately. All systems are under sequestration. Repeat: all systems under sequestration.*"

Mara's network. The lock-keepers, the smugglers, the survivors with their photostats and their forty years of memory. Somebody had made calls that outranked Halloway's calls.

Halloway stopped wading.

The water kept rising.

* * *

Voss watched the director's face go through the stages—disbelief, bargaining, and then the last one, the one Voss knew best because he'd practiced it in mirrors: the smooth settling of a man deciding what version of himself survives the night.

"There's a purge protocol," Halloway said. Almost conversational. "Physical. Thermite charges in the sub-basement archive. Manual trigger. If the data can't stay mine, it won't be anyone's."

"You're bluffing," Tamsin said.

"I built this place, Ms. Sterling."

And Voss felt the cold arrive—not fear, exactly, but the recognition of a pattern completing itself. His grandfather had scuttled a ferry rather than let a settlement testify. Halloway would scuttle the truth rather than let it testify. Three generations, same pen, different ink.

"The sub-basement floods first," Tamsin said. "It's already flooding. Your thermite is underwater, Director."

"Waterproofed casing." Halloway smiled, and it was the worst thing Voss had seen all week. "I'm going to walk out of here through the east spine. You're going to let me, because if you follow me into four feet of rising black water in the dark, one of us drowns, and you can't afford to guess which."

He turned. He began to move.

Tamsin looked at Voss. In the strobing emergency light, in the roar of the river coming home through the walls of the building that had poisoned it, she looked—for the first time since he'd known her—young. Scared. Certain anyway.

"Pip's on the east spine," she said.

Voss was already swimming.

* * *

The east spine corridor was a throat of black water and failing lights, and Cassian Voss went down it fighting the current with a stolen fire extinguisher in one hand and forty years of inheritance drowning behind him, and he had never in his life been so certain that he was exactly where he was supposed to be.

Ahead, faint through the flood, he could hear a boy's voice shouting, and beneath it, unmistakable, the click of a manual trigger arming.
