## 34. The Flood

**TAMSIN**

The water came for them the way water always comes—without malice, without hurry, without the slightest interest in what it ruined.

Tamsin heard it before she saw it: a deep, arterial groan running through the facility's bones, the sound of a building remembering it was built on a riverbed. Then the lights died in the east corridor and came back amber, and every speaker on the ceiling crackled with a voice too calm to be trusted.

*Evacuation. All personnel evacuate. This is not a drill.*

"Odile," Tamsin said into her radio. "Tell me that's you."

"Tell me you're out of the archive," Odile answered, and beneath her voice Tamsin could hear the roar—distant, but getting closer, the river finding the doors Odile had opened for it. "I gave it the east sluice first. It'll take the server halls in about four minutes. After that, everything below grade goes under. Including you, if you're still below grade."

"I'm coming up." Tamsin had the ledger canister strapped across her chest, the originals wrapped in oilcloth inside it, and the microfilm key in an inner pocket where her heartbeat could keep it warm. The corridor lights strobed. Somewhere below her feet, a pump tried to start and failed, and the silence after the failure was worse than any alarm.

She ran.

The facility had been built to keep water out, and now it was failing at the only job it had ever been given. Water sheeted across the floor at the base of the stairwell, ankle-deep, then shin-deep by the time she reached the second landing. Behind her, down the long throat of the archive corridor, she could see the first of it arriving—not a wave but a wall, slow and patient, carrying leaves and a dead bird and forty years of river sediment into the place where the river's name had been scrubbed from every record.

*This is not a drill.* She almost laughed. The whole place had been a drill, forty years of pretending the Hollow had never existed, the ferry had never sunk, the names in her canister had never been alive.

She was three steps from the surface door when the lights went out for good.

**PIP**

Pip had been told—specifically, repeatedly, in Odile's most frightening engineer voice—to stay topside with Mara and the lock-keepers and not, under any circumstances, go back inside.

He lasted ninety seconds.

The breach Odile's diversion had opened was on the facility's north face, where the old discharge culvert met the new flood channel, and it was doing exactly what Odile wanted: eating the building from the inside. But it was also the only way in or out that wasn't a locked stairwell full of rising water, and Tamsin had gone in through the stairwell, and Pip had done the arithmetic on that and hated the answer.

"I'm going in," he told Mara.

"I know," Mara said. She was already pulling rope from the lock-keepers' boat, her face set in the expression of a woman who had watched a town drown and had sworn, apparently, to never watch anything else do it. "I'm going with you."

"You're supposed to be the witness. The inspectors—"

"Are ten minutes out, and the building will still be here when they arrive. She won't be." Mara tied a bowline around his waist with hands that didn't shake at all. "Your father's daughter goes in after ghosts. Somebody has to go in after her."

The water inside the breach came to their chests in the first corridor and their shoulders in the second. Pip kept one hand on the guide rope and one hand out in front of him, feeling for the walls, and the dark was total except for the green glow of Mara's chemical stick, and the sound—the sound was the whole building inhaling.

"TAMSIN!" His voice bounced and died. "TAMSIN STERLING!"

Nothing. Just water, and the groan of steel.

Then, faintly, from somewhere ahead and below: a flashlight beam, sweeping, and a shape in it, and a voice he'd know anywhere, hoarse and furious and alive.

"Pip? *Pip?*"

**TAMSIN**

The stairwell door had jammed. Of course it had. The building was dying and it wanted company, and Tamsin stood in the dark with water at her waist and the surface door sealed above her, and for one long, cold moment she was sixteen again, watching a boat burn on a riverbank, learning that the people you love do not come back up.

No. She was not sixteen. She was thirty-four, and she had a canister of names on her chest, and she had spent a year following her father's footprints through this exact kind of dark, and her father had taught her one thing above all others: *the river always leaves a way out. Your job is to be calm enough to find it.*

The emergency vent. In the drowned room, weeks ago—no, days, it felt like years—the helmet-cam file had shown him using a maintenance vent behind the old pump gallery. This was a different wing, but facilities repeat themselves; she'd learned that from Odile. She felt along the wall, hand over hand, past a pipe, past a valve wheel, past—

A hatch. Round, rusted, bolted from her side.

She put the flashlight between her teeth and worked the wheel with both hands while the water climbed her ribs. It didn't move. It didn't move. It moved.

Cold air, thin and chemical, but air. She hauled herself through into a pump gallery that was already knee-deep and rising, and the ceiling here was lower and the water was faster, and she understood with terrible clarity that she had maybe two minutes and no second vent.

"Pip!" she screamed into the dark, because if she was going to die in a pump gallery she was going to do it loudly. "PIP!"

The answer came as a green light, bobbing, and a rope slapping the water near her shoulder.

**PIP**

She was heavier than she looked, or the water was heavier than it looked, or both. Pip braced the rope against a stanchion and pulled and the rope went taut and then slack and then taut again, and Mara was beside him, both of them hauling, and the current was trying to keep her, the way the current had tried to keep the ferry, the way the current had kept everyone the Hollow had ever lost.

"Not this one," Pip said through his teeth. He didn't know who he was saying it to. The river, maybe. "Not this one. You don't get this one."

Her hand broke the surface, grabbed his wrist, and gripped with a strength that would leave bruises he'd be proud of for a month. He pulled. Mara pulled. The water pulled harder, and then—suddenly, insultingly—stopped pulling at all, as if it had considered the matter and lost interest, and Tamsin Sterling came up out of the black water coughing and swearing and holding a metal canister above the surface like a priest raising a relic.

"The ledger," she rasped.

"You absolute—" Pip couldn't find a word bad enough. "You went back for *paper*."

"I went back for *people*." She was shaking now, the adrenaline bill coming due. "They're the same thing. That's the whole— that's the entire point, Pip. That's what my father died trying to say."

"Tell him yourself," Pip said. "Move."

**BRAM**

Bram had never run a flood-control console in anything but a simulator, and the simulator had never had a director screaming in his earpiece, a rival novelist barricaded in a server hall, and federal inspectors forty meters from the gate.

"The east hall's gone," Odile reported from the sluice controls, calm as a surgeon. "Central's taking water. The archive sub-basement will be fully flooded in six minutes."

"Then we're done," Bram said. "Everybody out. Let it drown."

"It's not drowning, Bram. It's *testifying*." A pause, and then, quieter: "Get the inspectors to the north breach. They need to see it while it's happening. A dry report is a memo. A wet one is a scandal."

Bram keyed the radio. "Inspector Onari, this is Bram Kettle, ex-Navy sonar. The facility's flood-control system has been—repurposed. The server halls are flooding. Whatever's in those archives is about to be underwater and unrecoverable, and the man who runs this place knows it. If you want evidence, you have about five minutes to walk in and take it while it's still dry enough to read."

A long silence. Then, in a voice that had clearly been waiting its whole career for this: "We're coming through the north breach. Nobody touches anything. And Mr. Kettle—"

"Yes."

"Good work."

Bram looked at the console, at the water levels climbing in neat green numbers, and felt something in his chest unclench for the first time since the navy had taken his clearance and his career and his name off a report. He'd spent eleven years listening to a river nobody was allowed to talk about.

Now everybody was going to hear it.

**VOSS**

Cassian Voss stood waist-deep in the server hall with the original canister in his hands, and for the first time in his life he was exactly where he meant to be.

The water was cold and rising and full of floating paper—printouts, manifests, personnel files, decades of careful records coming apart into pulp. Somewhere behind him the facility director was in custody, protesting pensions and procedures to inspectors who were not listening. Somewhere above, Tamsin was alive, because Pip had radioed it in the way Pip radioed everything, at maximum volume and minimum grammar.

Voss had come here to burn it. That had been the plan since the dockside tavern, since her father's name, since the whole long con of hiring the one diver in the country with a reason to say yes. And now the fire was going to happen anyway, except it would be water, and it would take everything—the program, the ferry, the order with his grandfather's signature—and he found that he could not lift his hands to help it.

His grandfather had signed the order in a warm office. Voss was standing in cold water. It seemed like the least he could do.

He heard her boots on the catwalk above—she'd come back in, of course she'd come back in, she'd probably been in the building when it flooded—and he held the canister up into the emergency light.

"Sterling." His voice came out strange. Stripped. "It's all here. Everything. I didn't touch it."

She looked down at him for a long moment, water dripping off the catwalk rail between them, and whatever she saw in his face, she didn't gloat about it. That was somehow worse than gloating.

"Bring it up, then," she said. "Both hands. It's heavy."

It was. It had been heavy his whole life.

**TAMSIN**

They came out through the north breach into gray daylight and rain, and the facility was dying behind them in a way that looked almost peaceful from the outside—water pouring from every lower window, the amber emergency lights flickering under the surface like something breathing.

The inspectors were everywhere, in rain jackets and body armor, wading the loading dock, carrying out evidence in waterproof cases with the reverence of men carrying relics. The director sat on a crate in handcuffs, still explaining. No one was writing it down.

Tamsin sat on the flood channel's edge with the canister between her feet and a foil blanket around her shoulders, and Pip sat down next to her, and Mara on her other side, and none of them said anything, because there was nothing to say that the river wasn't already saying better.

Odile came down the slope, soaked to the collar, grinning like a woman who had just argued with a river and won on points. "Central hall's fully flooded. Sub-basement's gone. Every copy of the program records in that building is now silt." She sat down hard. "Which means the only copies left in the world are on your microfilm, the photostat Mara's network has, and whatever the inspectors just carried out. The secret is finally, genuinely, unsinkable."

"Voss?" Tamsin asked.

"Gave the inspectors his grandfather's personal correspondence. Voluntarily. Including letters I really wish I hadn't seen." Odile shook her head slowly. "He's asking for the maximum, Tamsin. I heard him. He told them he'd cooperated too late and he knew it."

Tamsin looked out at the water pouring from the building—the river, running through the halls where the Hollow's death had been filed and forgotten, carrying the paper out in pieces, returning it all to the current that had never agreed to keep anyone's secret.

Her hands were shaking. She let them.

"We got it," Pip said quietly. "We actually got it."

Tamsin put her arm around his shoulders and looked at the canister at her feet—forty years of names, dry, intact, real—and felt the strange lightness that comes not when a weight is lifted but when you finally set it down on purpose.

"No," she said. "We're just getting started. There's a second copy in the Hollow. And there are forty families who've spent their whole lives being told their town never existed." She stood, legs unsteady, and looked upriver toward the source, where the water ran clean over the rocks for the first time in a generation.

"Get the boat. We're going home to tell them."
