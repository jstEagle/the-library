## 35. Aftermath at the Spring

The water came out of the mountain clean.

That was the thing that undid her, standing there on the third morning after everything, watching the spring run bright over pale stone. Not the arrests, not the inspectors' white vans crawling up the gorge road, not even the sight of the facility's fence cut open and its gates hanging wide like a mouth that had finally stopped lying. It was the water. Clear as glass, cold as honesty, moving the way water was supposed to move—downhill, unhidden, toward the sea.

Tamsin crouched at the edge of the outflow channel and let her fingers trail in it. The current pulled at them gently, without agenda. Upstream of her, where the discharge pipes had bled their gray poison into the source for twenty years, contractors in hazmat suits were capping the last of them with bolts and torchlight. She could hear the hiss of their work echoing off the rock face. It sounded almost like breathing.

"You're going to freeze those fingers off," Pip said behind her.

She hadn't heard him come up the trail. That was new. For eleven days she had heard every footstep within fifty meters, every door, every drone's distant whine. Now her body was slowly remembering that it lived in a world where footsteps were just footsteps.

"They'll thaw," she said.

Pip sat down beside her on the flat rock, knees drawn up, and handed her a tin cup of coffee that smelled like it had been boiled twice and regretted once. She drank it anyway. It tasted like home, if home were a crime scene with a galley.

"Odile says the dredger's hull held," he said. "Bram's already re-welding the seam. Says we'll be river-worthy by tomorrow night."

"Tell him to take his time."

Pip looked at her sideways. "You okay?"

It was such a simple question, asked so plainly, that she almost laughed. Three weeks ago nobody on that crew would have asked it. They'd have watched her from the corners of their eyes and adjusted course around whatever they saw there, the way you steer around a snag in the channel. Somewhere between the flooded parish and the rising water in the server halls, they'd stopped steering around her.

"No," she said. "And yes. Both at once. I don't think there's a word for it."

"Odile says there is. She says it's called *Tuesday*."

Tamsin laughed—a short, cracked sound that surprised her more than him—and Pip grinned like he'd won something. He was growing again, she noticed. Or maybe just standing straighter. A boy whose town had been erased from a map, sitting at the headwaters of another town's erasure, drinking bad coffee in the morning sun. Two orphans of official documents, keeping each other company.

Below them, down the gorge, the river gathered itself and began the long journey through the locks, past Weir Nine, past the smugglers' reach, past the drowned parish where Mara Iles had lit a lamp in a steeple window for fifteen years. Past Meridian Hollow, where streets ended in green water and houses stood open-mouthed under the current, waiting two decades for someone to say their names out loud.

Last night, someone finally had.

* * *

The hearing announcement had come over Bram's salvaged radio at dusk, crackling through static the way all important things seemed to arrive in her life—damaged, delayed, but arriving.

*"—federal commission convened under emergency statute. Public sessions to begin in Meridian Hollow within the fortnight. Survivors and families of the Hollow settlement are invited to submit testimony. In related developments, the names of the deceased—"*

Then the names.

They'd all stopped working when the names started. Odile set down her wrench mid-turn. Deke—who had stayed on after everything, because apparently even a man who'd carried a transmitter for money could be remade by a flood—came out of the hold and stood dripping on the deck plates. Bram turned the radio up until the speaker buzzed.

The announcer read them slowly, alphabetically, one hundred and forty-two names of people who had officially never existed. People who had been loaded onto a ferry that only ever made one trip, a ferry scuttled in deep water with its manifest burned, because the manifest would have shown that the passengers were not officials but witnesses—the residents of a settlement sitting too close to an illegal weapons program, people who had seen the discharge, who had complained, who had asked questions that a program like that couldn't afford.

Her father's handwriting had filled the margins of the ledger with their real details. Birthdays. Occupations. The Iles family, listed together. A schoolteacher named Corren. A lock-keeper's daughter. Names inside names, a whole town compressed into a corroded canister that had waited under green water for Tamsin Sterling to come looking for something else entirely.

When the reading ended, no one spoke for a long time. Then Mara, who had come aboard to hear it, said quietly: "My mother hated boats. She went because they told her the ferry was going to safety." And Odile had put her arm around the old woman's shoulders, and Pip had cried without embarrassment, and Tamsin had stood at the wheel with her hands gripping the spokes hard enough to ache, hollowed out and lighter than she'd felt since she was nine years old.

Victory, she was learning, didn't feel like winning. It felt like setting down something so heavy you'd forgotten you were carrying it, and then discovering how tired you were, and then discovering, underneath the tiredness, that you were still standing.

* * *

Midmorning, Mara climbed the trail to the spring with a canvas bag over her shoulder and moved like a woman sixty years younger than her paperwork claimed. She settled onto the rock beside Tamsin with the ease of someone who had decided, somewhere in the last few days, that Tamsin Sterling was worth deciding about.

"The commission sent a courier to the parish this morning," Mara said. "Official seal and everything. First government paper to come up that road that wasn't an eviction notice." She took the coffee tin from Tamsin's hand, drank, and grimaced. "Your boy makes this like he's trying to punish someone."

"He learned from me."

"Mm." Mara looked out over the water. "They want me to speak. At the hearings. Tell what happened the night of the evacuation."

"What are you going to tell them?"

"The truth. Sirens that never sounded. Gates locked from the outside. Soldiers who wouldn't look at us." Mara's voice was level, worn smooth like river stone, like she'd said these words to herself so many times they'd lost their edges. "And the ferry. I saw it load. I was fourteen. I hid in the reeds because my mother told me to run, and I watched them take the officials' families aboard first, and then I watched the gangplank come up while there were still people on the dock." She paused. "I've kept that inside me for twenty-two years. You know what I thought would happen if I ever said it out loud?"

"What?"

"I thought I'd break." Mara shook her head. "You don't break. You just get heavier, and then somebody finally carries it with you, and you get lighter. That's all testimony is. Asking someone to carry it with you."

Tamsin thought about the ledger in the strongbox aboard the dredger—one hundred and forty-two names in her father's careful hand, annotated in the margins with the small human details the navy had never bothered to record. He had carried it alone. He had died carrying it alone. And the crew who survived him had carried the silence alone, sworn around a burning boat by a girl of nine who grew up thinking the weight was hers to haul through life like ballast.

"There's something I should give you," Tamsin said.

She went down to the dredger and came back with the ledger canister, still streaked with green silt, and set it in Mara's lap. "The original. It should go to the commission from the families, not from me. It was never mine. My father just held it for you."

Mara ran her thumb along the corrosion, along the dent where the vault stairwell had nearly crushed it, along the salt-scoured seams. When she spoke, her voice had gone rough. "He dove for this. Your father."

"He dove for a lot of things. This one mattered."

"Then he'll be named too." Mara looked up. "In the record. As the man who kept the list. If the families have any say—and now we do—he'll be named."

Tamsin nodded, because if she tried to speak she was going to do something undignified in front of a woman who had earned the right never to watch anyone fall apart on her account. She looked at the water instead. Clean water, running downhill, minding its own ancient business.

* * *

Voss's name came up the way weather comes up—everyone mentioned it, nobody wanted to stand in it.

The federal inspectors had taken him into protective custody two days ago, which was a polite way of saying they weren't sure yet whether he was a witness or a defendant, and Voss himself seemed genuinely uncertain which he preferred. He had opened the sluice of his family's secrets in front of the director and half a contractor security force; he had handed Tamsin the original canister waist-deep in rising water with an expression she was still trying to file anywhere in her understanding of him. Whatever sentence came out of the hearings, Cassian Voss had already sentenced himself to something longer.

Deke brought word of him, oddly enough. Deke had gone down to the inspection camp to give his own statement—transmitter and all, full confession, the works—and come back looking ten pounds lighter and thoroughly confused about it.

"He asked about you," Deke said, perched on the dredger's rail. "Not much. Just whether you got out of the sub-basement okay. Whether the crew was whole."

"What did you tell him?"

"That you got out. That we're whole." Deke shrugged. "Seemed to matter to him. Can't figure why. Man's got money, family name, connections coming out his ears. Could've bought his way clear years ago. Instead he drowns it all in a server room."

"People drown for stranger reasons," Tamsin said. "I've recovered some of them."

Deke studied her. "You're not angry anymore. About him. I can't tell if that's growth or exhaustion."

"Ask me Tuesday."

But it wasn't exhaustion, and she knew it. Something had shifted in the water between her and Voss during the flood, some deep current realigned. He had spent his whole life managing his family's shame the way you manage a leak—patching, pumping, hiding the stain. She had spent hers drowning in a grief that had no name because the navy had deleted the name along with the town. In the end they'd both just wanted the same thing: for the truth to exist somewhere outside themselves. He'd just needed thirty more years and a near-drowning to admit it.

She didn't forgive him. Forgiveness was a currency she'd stopped trading in. But she understood him now, and understanding was heavier and more useful.

* * *

Evening came gold across the gorge. The contractor crews pulled out in a convoy of dust, leaving the source fenced but unlocked, guarded by nothing but daylight and the federal seal stenciled fresh on the gate. Tamsin walked the outflow channel one more time before dark, alone, the way she did everything that mattered.

At the bend where the channel widened, she found her father.

Not literally—she'd known for weeks that he wasn't coming back, had known it in the drowned room when his helmet-cam footage played against the concrete wall and his voice, young and scared and determined, narrated his own last dive. But his bolt-cutters were still wedged in the culvert fence downstream, rusted solid, exactly where he'd left them twenty-two years ago, and standing here at the water he'd come to expose, she could feel him the way you feel a current under a calm surface: invisible, directional, true.

"We finished it, Dad," she said quietly. It felt absurd to say aloud, and necessary. "Mara's going to testify. The names are going in the record. All of them. Yours too, if she has anything to say about it."

The water said nothing. Water never did. It just kept doing the only honest thing it knew.

She crouched and touched the surface one last time. Cold. Clean. Going somewhere.

Behind her, up the trail, voices—Pip calling that Odile had the engine turning over, that Bram wanted a word about heading downstream, that there was soup if she ever planned to eat again. Her crew. Her strange, damaged, loyal crew of strays, waiting on her the way they always had, except now it was a choice instead of a habit, and that difference meant everything.

Tamsin stood, wiped her hands on her thighs, and took one long look at the spring—the wound at the world's beginning, running clear at last.

Then she turned downhill, toward the river, toward home, toward whatever came next.

She was halfway to the dredger when Bram met her at the gangway with a message flimsy in his hand and an expression she hadn't seen on him since the patrol lights swept the parish.

"Tamsin," he said. "We've got a problem. Somebody's been asking about you in Meridian Hollow. Not the commission." He held out the flimsy. "A private party. Says you owe them money."

She took the paper. Read the name on it. And felt the old current—the other one, the ugly one—begin to pull.
