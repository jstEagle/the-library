## 36. Settling Accounts

The envelope came on a Tuesday, delivered by a man who looked like he ironed his shirts with a ruler.

Tamsin found it pinned under a coffee cup at the crew's table aboard the *Magpie's Rest*, the old dredger they'd been slowly converting into something that could carry a family instead of a secret. Heavy cream paper. A wax seal. Nobody used wax seals anymore except lawyers, governments, and people who wanted you to feel small when you broke the seal.

She opened it with a paring knife and read it standing up, the way she read anything that might knock her down.

*Dear Ms. Sterling,*

*We write regarding the outstanding obligations held against you by Mr. Errol Sandoval, trading as Sandline Credit, in the amount of—*

The number was still there. Of course it was. Forty-one thousand, three hundred and sixty, plus interest that had been breeding in the dark for six years like something in a flooded hold. She'd memorized it the way other people memorized prayers. She'd woken up at three in the morning reciting it. She'd stood on the deck of sinking wrecks and thought, *one more job, one more, and then I'm clear.*

*—said obligations having been acquired in full by the Department of the Navy through an intermediary, and having been discharged as of the fourteenth of this month. You will find enclosed a certificate of satisfaction. No further sums are owed.*

Tamsin read it three times.

Then she sat down, because her knees had opinions, and read it a fourth time out loud to Odile, who was elbow-deep in the galley pump.

"They bought your debt," Odile said, not looking up. "Sandline sold it. To the navy. Who then forgave it." She tightened something, and the pump shuddered and began, for the first time in a week, to actually pump. "That's not forgiveness. That's a receipt."

"For what?"

"For your silence, sweetheart." Odile finally looked at her, and there was no comfort in her face, only the flat competence that Tamsin had come to trust more than most people's love. "Nobody discharges forty-one thousand out of the goodness of their hearts. They did it so that when the hearings start, when some senator asks whether you were ever pressured, ever leveraged, ever *owned*—the answer is no. Clean witness. Clean story. They're laundering you."

Tamsin looked down at the certificate of satisfaction. It was beautiful, actually. Heavy stock, embossed, a real signature at the bottom in blue-black ink. Six years of her life, cancelled with one flourish.

She should have felt free.

What she felt was the specific nausea of a diver who surfaces and realizes the current that carried her up wasn't the current she thought it was.

* * *

Sandoval himself called that evening.

She almost didn't answer. The number was one she'd blocked twice, and both times he'd found a way around it, because that was Sandoval—patient, thorough, the kind of man who treated other people's desperation as a renewable resource. Six years ago he'd bought her father's outstanding debts along with the *Kestrel's* salvage rights, and he'd held them over Tamsin like a hook through the cheek. Work, he'd said. Work and pay. And don't ask questions about the Hollow job, because questions are how your father ended up at the bottom of a river with his logs confiscated.

She answered, because some conversations you owe yourself.

"Miss Sterling." His voice was as smooth as silt. "You've seen the paperwork."

"I've seen it."

"Then I imagine you have questions."

"I imagine you have answers you've rehearsed."

A pause. She could hear him smiling. "The navy approached me. They wanted your obligations cleared before the inquiry. I was compensated fairly. I want you to know that I argued—" and here he actually paused, as if the word cost him something, "—*against* accepting. Your account was one of my better performers."

"Performers."

"Accounts," he corrected, unbothered. "In any case. You're free, Miss Sterling. No liens, no claims, no interest accruing. I'd suggest you treat it as a fresh start."

"Is that what it is?"

"It's what you make of it." A beat. "There is one more matter. A second envelope is coming to you. Larger. I'd advise you to take it, and I'd advise you to take it quietly. The people who cleared your debt would prefer the whole matter settled without a scene. You of all people understand the value of not making waves."

He hung up before she could answer, which was his way of having the last word without risking hers.

The second envelope arrived the next morning. It was not cream paper and wax. It was a plain courier packet, and inside it was a cashier's check for an amount that made her sit down on the *Magpie's Rest* rail with her heart going like a bilge pump.

It was more than the debt. It was more than the debt and six years of interest. It was, she understood immediately, the price of a story. Her story. The Hollow, the ferry, the ledger, her father's name—all of it, signed away in exchange for a number with enough zeroes to make her stop talking.

She carried the check into the cabin and set it on the chart table, and she stared at it for a long time.

* * *

Pip found her there an hour later.

He was sixteen now, or thereabouts—his papers said fifteen, but his papers had been printed by a town that no longer existed, so who was counting. He'd grown three inches since the gorge road. He still flinched at drones. He came in quietly, the way he did everything, and looked at the check, and looked at her.

"That's a lot of money," he said.

"It is."

"Is it yours?"

"That," Tamsin said, "is the question, isn't it."

She thought about her father then—not the drowned version, not the helmet-cam ghost she'd followed through a flooding sub-basement, but the man at the kitchen table with his compass open in his palm, teaching her how to read a bearing. *A compass doesn't tell you where to go,* he'd said. *It tells you what you're turning away from.* She'd been nine and hadn't understood. She understood now. Every heading was a refusal. Every course was a thing you declined to sail toward.

The check was a bearing. It pointed straight at silence.

"Sandline held my debt for six years," she said to Pip. "You know what that means? It means every job I took, every wreck I dove, every risk—I was diving for him. My father's death bought my slavery, and I paid it off one corroded hull at a time. And now the navy buys the debt and calls it settled, and hands me a bonus on top, and what they're actually buying is—" she tapped the check, "—this. My agreement to let the number be the whole story. To let money be the currency. Because money is the currency they understand, and as long as we're trading in it, they win."

Pip was quiet. Then: "What do we trade in instead?"

And that, she thought, was the question. Not *what do we refuse*—refusal was easy, refusal was just another way of staying afloat. The question was what came after. What did you put in the hold when you threw the cargo overboard?

She thought about Mara Iles, standing in the church steeple above the drowned parish, reading names off a photostat with her voice steady and her hands shaking. She thought about the survivors' fund that Mara had started with nothing—literally nothing, a coffee tin and a list—and that had grown, since the hearings were announced, into something with a post office box and a queue of families a mile long. Families who'd spent thirty years being told their town had never existed. Families who needed headstones, and lawyers, and boat fare to the inquiry, and winter coats, and dignity in installments.

She thought about what a check like this one was worth in that world.

"Get me a pen," Tamsin said.

* * *

She endorsed it over to the Hollow Survivors' Fund that same afternoon, and then—because she was who she was—she did it in person.

Mara Iles kept the fund's office in a converted lock-keeper's cottage two miles downstream of Weir Nine, a building that had spent decades underwater and still smelled faintly of river no matter how many windows she opened. There were folding tables everywhere, and boxes, and a hand-lettered sign on the door that read HOLLOW FAMILIES — WALK INS WELCOME. The walk-ins, when Tamsin arrived, were an old man arguing gently with a volunteer about whether his father's name had been spelled *Ilyich* or *Ilyick* on the ferry manifest, and a young woman with a baby on her hip filling out a form for a memorial plaque.

Tamsin stood in the doorway with the endorsed check in her jacket pocket and felt, for a moment, like an intruder. She'd spent her whole life in places like this—the back rooms, the side doors, the economies that ran underneath the official one. But she'd never stood in a room where the unofficial economy ran toward something instead of just away.

Mara saw her and came over, wiping her hands on her trousers.

"You look like a woman carrying something heavy," she said.

"It's not heavy. That's the strange part. It's the lightest thing I've ever held and the hardest to put down."

She took out the check and explained it—Sandoval, the navy, the settlement, the quiet, the price. Mara listened the way she did everything, completely, without blinking, and when Tamsin finished, Mara looked at the check for a long moment.

"They think money settles things," Mara said. "That's the whole disease, isn't it. That's what they did to the Hollow. They ran a number. Thirty-one households, so many tons of equipment, so much liability—cheaper to sink it than to save it. They put a price on my mother's street and the price was lower than the truth." She looked up. "And now they've put a price on you, and you're going to hand it to me."

"If you'll take it."

"Oh, I'll take it." Mara's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "I'll take every cent and I'll spend it on headstones and hearing rooms and winter coats, and I'll send them a receipt so itemized they'll choke on it. But I want you to understand what you're doing, Tamsin. You're not donating. You're converting. You're taking their currency and turning it into ours." She took the check. "Names into stones. That's the exchange rate now."

The old man at the folding table had settled on *Ilyich*. The volunteer wrote it down carefully, in the good pen, the one they saved for permanent things.

* * *

Word got out, because everything got out now; Mara's network saw to that, and the inquiry's clerks leaked like the dredger's old hull. By the end of the week, a reporter from the regional paper was at the docks asking Tamsin whether she'd refused the settlement out of principle, and whether she had a statement, and whether she'd characterize her relationship with the navy as adversarial.

Tamsin gave her the only statement she had.

"I didn't refuse the money," she said. "I refused the *terms*. There's a difference. They wanted a transaction—money for silence, account closed, everyone walks away clean. But you can't close an account with the dead. The ledger my father died carrying had two hundred and six names in it, and every one of those names is still owed. So I took their money and I spent it where the debt actually lives. That's not a statement. That's bookkeeping."

The reporter wrote it down. It ran under a headline Tamsin didn't love—*DIVER REFUSES HUSH MONEY*—but Mara told her the headline didn't matter, because the old man with the ferry manifest had read it and cried, and three more families had walked into the cottage that afternoon.

Bram, when he heard about it, said nothing for a full day, which from Bram was a eulogy. Then he showed up at the *Magpie's Rest* with a bottle of terrible whiskey and two cups and said, "Your old man would've done the same thing, and he'd have done it quieter, and I've been thinking about that a lot lately—how loud we got to be, to get here." They drank the terrible whiskey and didn't talk about it further, which was its own kind of settlement.

Odile said: "Good. Now we can afford a new bilge pump," and put the old one on the scrap pile with a small ceremony.

* * *

There was one more piece of business, and Tamsin saved it for last, alone, the way she did everything that mattered.

Sandoval called again on Friday night. She let it ring twice before she picked up.

"Miss Sterling. I'm told you endorsed the settlement over to the survivors' fund."

"You're told correctly."

A long silence on the line. She pictured him in whatever office he kept, surrounded by the paper architecture of other people's misfortune, recalculating.

"That was unwise," he said finally. "Not the charity—the *gesture*. Men like me, we don't forget gestures. And the people I sell to, they remember them longer."

"Sandoval." She said his name the way she'd say a bearing. "Six years ago you told me the debt was mine and I'd carry it until it killed me, same as my father. You were half right. It did kill something. But here's what you never understood about ledgers." She looked out the cabin window, down the dark river toward the Hollow, where the drowned streets were getting their names back one hearing at a time. "A debt only has the power you agree it has. I agreed for six years. I'm done agreeing. And if you or anyone you sell to comes near my crew, my fund, or my families, I won't make a gesture. I'll make a *record*. I've got two hundred and six names, a federal inquiry on speed dial, and nothing left to lose that you can put a price on. Do you understand me?"

Another silence. Longer.

"I understand," Sandoval said, and for the first time in six years, there was no silt in his voice. Just a man, doing arithmetic, arriving at a number he didn't like. "Good evening, Miss Sterling."

She hung up and set the phone face-down on the chart table, and her hands, she noticed, were perfectly steady.

* * *

Later, much later, she went out on deck alone.

The river moved past the hull, black and patient, carrying everything toward the sea the way it always had—the rain, the runoff, the silt of thirty years of secrets slowly loosening its grip on the bottom. Somewhere upstream, at the source, the facility sat empty under federal seal, its servers drying out in the air, its files in the hands of inspectors who read them aloud in hearing rooms. Somewhere downstream, in a cell in the capital, Cassian Voss was writing letters. Somewhere in between, in a converted lock-keeper's cottage, two hundred and six names were becoming stones.

Tamsin leaned on the rail and did her own arithmetic. Six years of debt: cancelled. One cashier's check: converted. One loan shark: retired, she hoped, from the currency of her life. The column didn't balance—she was honest enough with herself to know it never would. Her father was still dead. The Hollow was still drowned. No number, no settlement, no certificate of satisfaction would ever raise a single one of those streets.

But the current ran clear now. And she was done paying in the old coin.

She was about to go below when she saw it—sitting on the chart table where she'd left it that morning, where she'd left it every morning for a year: her father's compass, its brass casing dulled, its needle swinging slow and true.

She'd been carrying it since she was nine years old. Through the wreck dives and the gauntlets, the gorge and the flooding dark. It had given her a bearing when she had none, and a key when she needed one, and a father's voice when the water closed over her head.

But the debt was settled. The names were spoken. And she had been thinking, more and more lately, about a flooded street in Meridian Hollow, and about the ferry that lay beneath it, and about the fact that some things are not cargo, and not evidence, and not inheritance—but something else entirely, something you can only give away in person, in the water, with witnesses.

Tamsin picked up the compass and held it in her open palm, and the needle settled, and pointed, as it always did, upstream. Toward the source. Toward the spring.

Toward home, if a heading could be a home.

"Okay, Dad," she said quietly, to the dark river and the man somewhere in it. "One last bearing."

Tomorrow, she would need to ask Mara to stand beside her.
