## 37. The Compass Returned

The morning of the naming, the river ran so clear I could see the rooflines.

That was the thing nobody warned you about grief — it didn't wait for the dramatic moment. It waited for the quiet one. For the first still morning after the hearings were announced, after the inspectors' vans rolled out and the cameras packed up and Meridian Hollow went back to being a place instead of a story. I stood on the old ferry landing at first light, coffee going cold in my hand, looking down through six feet of green-gold water at a street that used to be somebody's whole world.

You could see the tram tracks running up the middle of what had been Harbor Street. A lamppost, its glass long gone, furred with weed like a drowned man's beard. And further out, where the channel deepened toward the old ferry slip, a dark shape I knew without needing to see it clearly. Forty years of silt hadn't hidden it from me. Nothing would.

"You're up early."

Mara came along the landing the way she did everything — unhurried, certain her feet knew the boards even if the boards had been underwater for most of her life. She was carrying two mugs instead of one, which was her way of telling me my coffee was wrong without saying so. She handed me the replacement and looked out at the water.

"Couldn't sleep," I said. It wasn't a lie. It just wasn't the whole truth either.

"I know." She sipped. "I've been watching you not sleep for three weeks. You do it standing up, mostly. On deck. Facing upstream."

I almost laughed. Almost. "Occupational habit."

"No," Mara said. "It's a vigil. There's a difference. My mother used to keep them." She said her mother's name the way she always did now — plainly, without flinching, because the hearings had put it in the record and the record had made it real in a way that thirty years of private mourning never managed. *Ilse Vannick. Age 34. Ferry manifest, final sailing.* "She'd stand at the window all night before an anniversary. I thought she was being haunted. She was doing the haunting herself. Making sure the dead knew they'd been seen."

I turned the compass over in my pocket. I'd been carrying it there since the spring, since the flood, since the night I'd stood in waist-deep water in a server hall and understood that my father had died holding proof and I'd nearly died finishing his sentence. The brass casing was warm from my body heat. The needle inside still swung true when I checked it, which felt like a small miracle and also like an accusation.

"I'm going in today," I said.

Mara didn't ask what I meant. She looked at the water, then at my pocket, then at my face, and whatever she saw there settled the question. "Above the ferry?"

"Above the ferry."

"When?"

"After the ceremony. After everyone's gone home." I watched a heron stalk the shallows near the old customs house, patient as a debt collector. "This isn't for an audience."

"It shouldn't be," Mara agreed. Then: "But I'm coming with you."

"Mara—"

"Don't." Her voice stayed gentle and went immovable at the same time, which was a trick I'd never learned. "You gave my mother her name back. You gave all of them their names back. Whatever you're carrying down to that water, you don't carry it alone. That's not how this works anymore. That's not how any of this works anymore."

* * *

The ceremony was held on the new dock — the one the survivors' fund built with the settlement money I refused and redirected, though Odile kept telling me I should take credit and Pip kept telling her that Tamsin taking credit was like the tide taking credit for the moon. Two hundred people came. More than the Hollow had ever held in its living days, Mara said. Carpenters and lock-keepers and smugglers who'd run supplies past navy cordons for us, and families who'd driven in from three provinces away because a letter had finally reached them saying *we know where your people are, we know what happened, we're sorry took forty years but here it is.*

They read the names off a bronze plate bolted to a stone cairn. One hundred and twelve names. It took forty minutes. Nobody left. When they got to Ilse Vannick, Mara stepped forward and touched the letters with two fingers, the way you'd touch a sleeping child to confirm the breathing, and I had to look at the water because if I looked at her face I was going to come apart in front of two hundred people and I had one more thing to do today that required me assembled.

Bram stood at the back with his hands folded, ex-navy straight-backed, eyes wet and daring anyone to comment. Odile cried openly and without embarrassment, which was the most Odile thing possible. Pip held the photostat ledger against his chest like a schoolbook he was proud of. Deke wasn't there. He'd sent word from holding that he'd testify when called and otherwise wanted to be forgotten, and for once I found I could grant someone oblivion without resentment.

Voss's marker went up too — separate stone, separate wording, placed where the victims' families had agreed to allow it. *For those who were failed by men who knew better.* His grandfather's name wasn't on it. His own was, as the one who'd asked for it. Mara had argued for including him. "A warning needs a face," she'd said. I hadn't argued back. Some mercies aren't mine to give or withhold.

Afterward, the crowd thinned the way crowds do — in eddies, in slow spirals of goodbye — until the dock held only us and the gulls and the sound of the current moving under everything, the way it always had, the way it always would.

* * *

I changed into my dive skin behind the boathouse. Old habit said full kit — tank, regulator, mask, the works. But I stood at the water's edge with the gear bag open and couldn't make myself reach for it.

This wasn't a salvage. This wasn't a job. This was a delivery, and some deliveries you make the way the recipient would want them — barefoot, in the water, no equipment between you and the thing you're returning.

I kept the compass in my hand.

Mara waded in beside me, trousers rolled to the knee, and gasped once at the cold and then stopped complaining, which told me more about her spine than any testimony ever had. We walked out along the drowned street itself, following the tram tracks underfoot, the water rising from ankle to knee to waist. Fish scattered ahead of us. Sunlight came down through the surface in shifting lattices and laid itself across the pavement below like lace across a coffin.

Halfway out, my foot came down on something that clinked. I bent, half expecting debris, and came up with a bottle — green glass, intact, sealed with wax. Inside, folded tight, a note gone soft as cloth with forty years of soaking. I could make out maybe three words in faded ink. *Remember the ferry.*

Mara made a sound low in her throat. "Someone threw that from the shore," she said. "The day it happened. Or after. People did that. My aunt did that. Threw things into the water for years. Letters. Flowers. Once a wedding ring." She looked at the bottle in my hands. "The river's been keeping mail this whole time. Nobody ever delivered it."

I set the bottle gently on a windowsill that broke the surface nearby — a little stone shelf, a dead letter office — and we kept walking.

The water reached my chest before we got to the deep channel. I could feel the compass against my palm, and I could feel something else underneath that, something I'd been refusing to name since chapter one of my life, if my life had chapters, which lately it seemed determined to have.

My father gave me this compass when I was nine. He pried it out of a wreck off Cape Verity, cleaned it with vinegar and patience, and put it in my hands and said, *"It doesn't tell you where to go. It tells you where you are. Half of staying alive is knowing the difference."* I'd carried it through every dive, every debt, every dark year of a reputation I didn't earn and couldn't shake. I'd opened its casing in a wheelhouse at midnight and found the microfilm key that unlocked everything, and I'd understood in one vertiginous instant that he had known — that he'd gone into the Hollow knowing he might not come out, and had hidden the ending of his story inside the one object he trusted to find its way back to me.

He'd meant for me to finish it. I finished it.

So why did it feel like putting him down?

"Tamsin." Mara's hand on my arm. The water at our shoulders now, the ferry slip's dark shape visible below us, a great shadow holding its breath. "Tell me what you're afraid of."

"That I'll forget the weight," I said. It came out rougher than I intended. "Thirty years I've carried this. The debt, the silence, the name nobody would say. It's been — God help me, it's been the shape of my whole life. And now it's done, and I don't know who I am without it. Giving this up feels like—" I stopped. Started again. "Everyone keeps telling me I'm free. Nobody tells you free is also empty. There's a hole where the grief used to be and I can hear wind through it."

Mara nodded slowly, and the current moved her hair, and she said, "My mother kept vigil every year for thirty years. And the first anniversary after the hearings, she slept through the night for the first time since I was a girl. And she woke up crying. Not because she missed the grief. Because she'd been afraid the sleeping meant she'd stopped loving him." She squeezed my arm. "It doesn't work like that. Love isn't the weight. Love is what the weight was protecting. Put the weight down and the love stays. I promise you. I'm standing in the proof."

Below us, the ferry waited with its hundred and twelve.

I looked at the compass one last time. The needle swung, settled, pointed north — steady, faithful, telling me exactly where I was, which was here, which was enough, which had always been the whole lesson.

"Dad," I said quietly, to the water, to the man, to the forty-year silence between them. "Job's done. Go home."

I opened my hand.

The compass didn't fall so much as it was received — the river took it the way the river takes everything, without violence, without hurry, turning it once in a column of light so the brass caught fire for a single heartbeat, and then it sank, smaller and smaller, a bright coin, a spent star, down past the lampposts and the tram tracks and the doorways of the street where people had lived and been erased, down to the deck of the ferry where it came to rest on the planking above the engine room, and lay still.

The needle, I knew, would keep pointing north. Down there in the dark, in the cold, among the names. Pointing home. Pointing at all of us.

I hadn't realized I was crying until the water hid it, and then I laughed at that — the river covering for me one last time, running interference between my face and the world — and Mara put her arm through mine and we stood there together, chest-deep in a drowned town, letting the current hold up what we couldn't.

We stayed until our lips went blue. Then we walked back along Harbor Street, past the dead letter windowsill, past the heron, up onto the dock where dry towels waited and Odile had, predictably, put a kettle on.

* * *

Pip met us at the boathouse, trying very hard to look like he hadn't been watching from the dune grass, and failing completely, and I loved him for it.

"Odile wants to show you something," he said. "She's been up since five. She's got charts."

"Charts," I repeated, wringing out my hair.

"Charts of the upper tributaries. And Bram pulled records — there are eleven other settlements in the basin with gaps in their registries. Gaps like the Hollow had. Like my town had." Pip's voice picked up speed the way it did when he was scared of slowing down. "And there's a woman from Ashford Weir who wrote to the fund asking if anyone could look for her grandfather's boat, and Odile says if we're going to be a recovery service we need a proper survey vessel, and there's a dredger at Smugglers' Reach the owner will trade for the right cargo, and—"

"Pip."

He stopped. Braced himself, the way you do.

"Get the others," I said. "Chart table. Twenty minutes."

His whole face lit like a lamp. He ran.

Mara and I walked up from the waterline side by side, wringing the Hollow out of our clothes, and behind us the river ran clear over the ferry, over the compass, over the street where the names lived now — not erased, not buried, just resting, the way the dead rest when the living finally stop digging them up to hide them and start visiting to remember.

Somewhere upstream, at the spring where it all began, the water was already on its way down to us. Clean. Unstoppable. Carrying everything it had witnessed toward the sea.

I had no debts. No secrets. No weight.

Just a heading — and a crew waiting at the chart table, arguing about engines, calling my name.
