## 38. New Charter

The paperwork took three days, which Tamsin found fitting. Everything that mattered in her life had arrived soaked, delayed, and smelling faintly of bilge.

She sat at the scarred table in the Hollow's rebuilt harbormaster's office—rebuilt being generous; the roof was new, the walls were original, and the coffee was a war crime—and read the charter language Odile had drafted for the fourth time.

*The Meridian Hollow Recovery Service. Purpose: location, documentation, and respectful recovery of sites submerged by civic and industrial action. Services rendered to families of the displaced. No salvage for profit. No contracts with military or security entities.*

"No salvage for profit," she said aloud.

"You're reading it like it's a eulogy," Odile said from the doorway, a torque wrench in one hand and a mug of something gray in the other. "It's a business plan. Barely."

"It's the opposite of everything I've done for fifteen years."

"Good." Odile set the mug down on the only dry corner of the table. "Everything you did for fifteen years was running from a name. This is running toward one. Different current entirely."

Tamsin looked out the window. Below, the Hollow's new docks stretched into water that had once been streets. The pilings were fresh-cut pine, pale as bone against the dark river, and every morning Mara Iles walked their length before anyone else was awake, touching each one like she was counting children home from school.

The hearings had been announced eleven days ago. Federal inspectors, a special commission, names read aloud in a hall in the capital—the ferry passengers, the parish records, the ledger Tamsin had carried upriver in a canister worth more than her old boat. Her father's handwriting had been entered into evidence. She had watched a clerk hold those pages with white gloves, and she had thought: *he wrote these in the dark, in a hurry, knowing he might die for them, and now a man in a tie is cataloguing them between lunch and his four o'clock meeting.* It should have made her angry. Mostly it made her tired, and a little bit free.

"Sign it," Odile said. "Then we argue about the boat."

"The boat needs arguing about?"

"The boat needs everything. Bram found rust in places rust has no business being. Pip named the rust 'Kevin.'"

"Of course he did."

Tamsin signed. Her signature looked strange under all that clean language—small, salt-stained, a diver's scrawl at the bottom of a page full of purpose. But it held.

* * *

They registered the service in a rented room above the tavern where Voss had once made his offer, which Tamsin considered either irony or the river's sense of humor. Four chairs, one table, a window with a view of the water. Bram brought a chalkboard he'd salvaged from a decommissioned tender and wrote their first job queue on it in a hand far too neat for a man who'd spent a decade listening to sonar ghosts:

1. **Ferry site survey — Hollow families** (Mara, liaison)
2. **Weir Nine lockhouse — structural record**
3. **Parish reliquary recovery — St. Anselm's** (fragile)

"That third one," Tamsin said. "The reliquary. That's where we hid the canister."

"I know," Bram said. "Mara knows too. She says if it's good enough to hide truth in, it's good enough to hold what comes after it."

Tamsin turned that over. It had the shape of something Mara would say—plain on the surface, deep enough to drown in if you weren't careful.

Pip arrived last, as always, taking the stairs two at a time with the particular energy of a seventeen-year-old who had recently discovered that adults would now legally have to listen to him. He dropped a folder on the table. It was thick. Hand-labeled. Cross-referenced.

"What's this?"

"Applications." He sat down hard, grinning. "Well. One application. Mine. For apprentice. I filled it out proper because Odile said if we're going to be legitimate, we do things legitimate, even the parts that are stupid."

Odile took the folder, flipped through it with visible delight. "He listed his qualifications. Item one: 'can hold breath four minutes.' Item two: 'not afraid of drowned churches anymore, mostly.' Item three—" she paused, and her voice changed, just slightly, "—'my town isn't on maps either. I know how to look for things people wanted gone.'"

The room went quiet in the way rooms do when someone young says something true.

"He also requested wages," Bram offered.

"He requested wages *and* a compass," Odile said. "Item six under equipment requests. 'A real one. Not a toy.'"

Tamsin looked at the boy who had stowed away on her deck because a reservoir project had erased his town the way the navy had erased the Hollow—who had tripped an alarm to save her, hauled her half-drowned through a breach in a flooding facility, and never once asked for anything except to be useful. Who had asked, upstream in the dark, why they couldn't just sell the ledger and vanish, and then stayed anyway when the answer turned out to be worse than selling.

"Apprentice means you start at the bottom," she said. "You'll scrub hulls. You'll log dives you don't get to make. You'll learn charts until your eyes bleed, and when you finally go down, the first thing you'll see is probably mud."

"Okay," Pip said.

"And the compass—I'm not buying it. When you've earned it, someone who loves you will give it to you. That's how they work. That's the whole mechanism."

Pip blinked. Whatever he'd expected, it wasn't that. "Whose compass? Yours?"

"Mine came from my father. Yours'll come from wherever it comes from. That part's not the job." She slid the folder back across the table. "The job starts Monday. Hull first."

Pip grinned so wide it looked like it hurt. "Monday," he said, like it was a word in a language he'd just learned to speak.

* * *

Their first job was the ferry itself.

They went down in pairs over three days, Tamsin leading, mapping the wreck that had started everything—the missing ferry, the erased crossing, the navy's tidy scuttle that had become the navy's public shame. The water was cold and green-gold, and the ferry lay on its side in the silt like a sleeping animal, its hull furred with sixteen years of river.

But this time Tamsin didn't dive it as a thief. She dove it as a witness.

They photographed everything. Hull numbers. Rivet patterns. The scorch marks near the engine room that proved what the official story still denied. Odile ran the sonar and built a picture of the wreck so complete it could be argued with in a courtroom, which was the point. Bram recorded position data with a precision that bordered on devotion.

And on the second day, working the passenger deck, Tamsin found the shoes.

Dozens of them. Silt had taken the leather soft, but they were there—caught in the decking, in the stairwells, in the galley where people had run and slipped and not gotten out. She hovered above them for a long time, her regulator hissing, and thought about the ledger in her pocket-memory: names, dates, a chemical designation, her father's cramped annotations. Names were evidence. Shoes were people.

When she surfaced, Mara was waiting on the dive platform, as she seemed to wait everywhere now, patient as tide.

"Well?" Mara asked.

"There are shoes," Tamsin said. "A lot of them. We documented every one. Position, condition, everything."

Mara nodded slowly. Her mother's name was in the ledger. Tamsin had carried that fact up a river and through a fence and into a flooding server hall, and it had never once gotten lighter.

"My mother wore brown boots," Mara said. "Laced high. She complained they were impractical for a dockworker, and she bought them anyway because they were beautiful." She looked at the water. "If you find them—"

"We photograph them," Tamsin said. "And we leave them. And we mark the spot so you can come down yourself, someday, if you want. Or never. Both are allowed."

Mara studied her for a moment. "You've changed your relationship with the river."

"The river changed it first."

That earned the ghost of a smile. "The families meet Thursday. They'll want to hear it from you. Not the commission—you."

"Why me?"

"Because you're the one who went down and came back up with something true. Around here, that makes you clergy." Mara said it without smiling at all, and Tamsin understood it wasn't entirely a joke.

* * *

Thursday evening, the harbormaster's room was full past capacity. Survivors of the Hollow, children of survivors, lock-keepers who had kept quiet for sixteen years and were done keeping quiet, a woman from two towns over whose brother had vanished in the evacuation that never happened. They sat on crates and windowsills and stood along the walls, and Tamsin stood at the front with the chalkboard behind her showing the ferry's outline in Bram's careful hand, and she told them what was down there.

She told them plainly. Where the wreck lay. What condition it was in. What the scorch marks meant. How many shoes. She did not perform grief and she did not perform professionalism, and somewhere in the middle she realized she had stopped performing altogether—that the woman who had once dived restricted wrecks at night with a fake registration and a debt on her neck had no idea how to be this person, and this person was her.

An old man near the window raised his hand. "My wife was on that ferry. Will you bring her up?"

The room held its breath.

"No," Tamsin said gently. "We document. We mark. If a family wants a recovery, we'll do it, carefully, with everyone's consent. But mostly what we'll do is make sure no one can ever say this place didn't happen. The dead stay with the river unless the river gives them back. Our job is to be honest about where they lie."

The old man nodded slowly, and something in his shoulders let go—not relief exactly, but the end of waiting.

Afterward, the crowd thinned by degrees, the way crowds do when the important thing has been said. Odile counted prospective contracts on her fingers. Bram erased and redrew the chalkboard queue twice. Pip fell asleep sitting up, mid-sentence, still holding a pencil, and nobody woke him because everybody remembered being seventeen and tired in a way that sleep didn't fix.

Mara stayed last, as she stayed first.

"They're talking about a memorial," she said. "On the water. Buoys over the ferry, one for each name. The commission wants to fund it. The families want to build it themselves."

"Both?" Tamsin asked.

"Both. Commission money, family hands." Mara looked at her. "They want your crew to place the buoys. Precisely. Over each name."

Tamsin felt that land in her chest like an anchor settling into good holding ground. "We'd be honored."

"Not honored," Mara corrected softly. "Present. There's a difference, and you of all people know it."

* * *

Late that night, Tamsin walked the new docks alone. The river moved beneath the pilings, dark and patient, carrying spring melt down toward the sea. Somewhere downstream, the source facility sat drained and gutted, its servers hauled away in evidence containers, its discharge pipes cut open to the air like arteries laid bare. Somewhere downstream, Cassian Voss waited in a federal facility for a sentencing that the papers said would be severe and that Tamsin suspected would be complicated. She hadn't answered his letter yet. She wasn't sure the answer existed yet.

Her hands were empty. No debt. No canister. No compass—she had given that to the water above the ferry, and some mornings she still reached for her pocket out of habit and found nothing, and some mornings that nothing felt like grief and some mornings it felt like room.

Boots on planking behind her. Pip, wrapped in a blanket against the night chill, dropping down beside her at the dock's end with the unselfconsciousness of the very young and the very tired.

"Can't sleep?"

"Thinking about the buoys." He swung his legs. "Forty-one names. Odile says we anchor each one within a meter. That's precision work in current."

"You'll learn it."

"Is that what apprenticeship is? Just—learning things until you're useful?"

Tamsin considered. "It's learning things until you belong to the work. Then the work belongs to you. Then you give it away to somebody else someday, and that's the whole religion."

Pip was quiet a while. The river talked to itself below them, the way it always had, the way it always would.

"Tamsin? My town. The reservoir one. Do you think... when the hearings are done, and there's time—could we go there? Document it? Nobody's ever even marked it. It's just a lake with a number."

She looked at him—at the orphan of another official map, asking permission to grieve in the only way either of them knew how to ask.

"It's already on the board," she said. "Job four. I put it there the day we signed."

Pip didn't say anything. He leaned sideways until his shoulder touched hers, briefly, the way boys his age did when words were too big, and then he looked back out at the black water where forty-one buoys would soon rise over a ferry and its shoes and its brown boots laced high, and Tamsin Sterling sat with her crew's youngest at the end of a dock in a city that had come back from drowning, and waited for morning, and work, and the long clear current running east toward the sea.
