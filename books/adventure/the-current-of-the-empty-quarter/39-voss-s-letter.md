## 39. Voss's Letter

The letter came on a Tuesday, which felt like the kind of detail Voss would have planned.

It arrived with the morning supply run to the Hollow docks—Mara's network had grown organized enough by autumn to have its own boat schedule, its own crates, its own quiet system of favors—and it sat at the bottom of a canvas mail sack between a box of water filters and a bundle of newspapers three weeks out of date. Pip fished it out, turned it over twice, and held it up like something that might bite.

"Nobody writes letters," he said. "People who write letters are either dead or about to be."

"Or in prison," Odile said, not looking up from the carburetor she was rebuilding on the dock planks. "Federal holding facility, Fairwater Annex. That's a prison postmark."

Tamsin took it without hurrying. Her name and the Hollow's new coordinates were written across the front in a hand she recognized instantly—Cassian Voss had the kind of penmanship they used to teach boys who were expected to sign things, upright and deliberate, every letter aware of its own weight. No return address beyond the facility's stamp. No lawyer's envelope, no thick paper, no attempt at weight or ceremony. Just a single sheet folded in thirds.

She didn't open it on the dock. She carried it up to the steeple room she'd been borrowing from Mara since spring—still not quite living there, still keeping her gear bag packed by the door out of habit older than sense—and sat on the windowsill where the light came through clean off the water.

The church had been raised on pilings when the river took the parish below; only the tower and a ring of higher ground stayed dry, and from the window you could see the drowned streets laid out beneath the green surface like furniture under a sheet. On clear days you could make out the ferry landing. She'd stopped avoiding the view months ago. Some mornings she even looked for it.

She broke the seal. One page. His handwriting changed halfway through—she could see where he'd stopped, set the pen down, picked it back up. She knew what that looked like. She'd done it herself more than once over the past year, usually at two in the morning, usually over lists of names.

*Tamsin,*

*I've started this four times. The first three versions were apologies, and I burned them, because an apology is a transaction and I have no right to be buying anything from you.*

*You'll hear the official version before this reaches you, so let me give you the true one while I still control any part of it.*

*Deke told you I hired him to track copies of the ledger. That's accurate but incomplete. I hired him before we ever shook hands on the archive contract. The job offer was the tracking operation. You were never meant to reach the source—you were meant to lead me to everything my grandfather touched, so it could be destroyed cleanly, one fire instead of many. When Deke reported that a second copy existed in the Hollow itself, I sent him downstream to intercept your crew because I believed—I genuinely believed—that burning it was mercy. That the families deserved to grieve without their dead becoming evidence, exhibits, a traveling scandal with their mothers' names printed in tabloids next to weapons schematics.*

*I want you to know I no longer believe that. I'm not going to pretend I arrived at it nobly. I arrived at it standing waist-deep in the server hall watching the director try to buy you with the same currency my family has traded in for sixty years—silence for safety, names for comfort—and I understood that mercy was just the word we used for the erasure we profited from. My grandfather didn't drown those people to protect them. He drowned them to protect himself. And I spent my life defending a man's reputation I would not have shaken hands with.*

*So: the hearings. I will testify fully. Not the curated version—the whole ledger of it, including the parts that implicate me. I signed contracts knowing what the archive contained. I paid men to follow you. I put Pip in front of a gunboat to protect a secret I already suspected was rotten. If they take my name, my company, and my freedom, then those are the first honest prices anything in my family has ever paid.*

*I am not asking you for forgiveness. I know the arithmetic. What my name bought me, it cost you a father, a crew, eleven years of being called a liar. Forgiveness would be me stealing something else from you—the last thing left, which is your right to withhold it.*

*I ask one thing, and it is small, and you may refuse it.*

*When the hearings end and the record is corrected, there will be a marker. There always is. And markers get built by people like my family, which means his name will be on it—regent of the program, patron of the river, some carved fiction about a complicated man. I cannot stop them from carving it. But I can ask that somewhere on that stone, in letters no larger than anyone else's, the victims' names stand beside mine. All of them. From the ledger. Spelled correctly. Including the ones whose families couldn't be found to object.*

*Not his redemption. Their presence. Let anyone who comes to admire him have to read past them to do it.*

*If you do this, don't tell me. Don't write back. I'll find out the way everyone finds out things now—from the river towns, from Mara's people, from the current. It's better that way. It keeps the transaction out of it.*

*One last thing, and then I'll stop taking your time.*

*Your father's compass. I saw it once, years ago, at the crew reunion your mother hosted the summer before the Hollow job. He let me hold it. I remember thinking it was a strange gift—a compass doesn't point anywhere useful on a river. It points north, and rivers run wherever they're going. I understand it now. He wasn't giving you a direction. He was telling you that you'd need to know where you stood regardless of which way the water moved.*

*You stood. That's all. You stood, and the rest of us found out what we were made of by watching you do it.*

*C.V.*

Tamsin read it twice.

The first time was fast, the way she read charts—scanning for hazards, checking depths, looking for the hook in it. There wasn't one. That was somehow worse. A hook she could have handled. She'd spent her whole adult life handling hooks.

The second time was slow, sitting on the windowsill with the page flat against her knee while the light moved off the water and the drowned streets kept their silence below. She read the paragraph about her father twice more within the second reading, and had to set the letter down on the sill, face-up, and look at the river for a while.

He'd been at that reunion. She remembered it now that he'd named it—the summer before the Hollow job, the whole crew crammed into her parents' backyard, her father holding court by the grill with a beer in one hand and stories getting bigger with each one. And Voss there, twenty-something, heir-apparent awkward in a linen shirt too nice for folding chairs, laughing at her father's impression of a harbor master they'd both cheated in different ways. They'd been friends once. She'd forgotten that. Or she'd buried it so deep it counted as forgetting.

Her father had let him hold the compass. She could picture it—her father's generosity, that easy way he had of putting the important thing into someone else's hands like it was nothing, like trust was just a habit you kept whether or not people earned it. And Voss had held it and thought it was useless, and her father had laughed, and eleven months later her father was dead in water Voss's grandfather had ordered filled, and the compass had come home to her water-stained and stopped at 4:17, and she'd carried it through every chapter of this story like a bone she couldn't bury.

She thought about forgiveness.

Not the abstract kind—the real kind, the kind with weight and temperature. Whether she had it in her. Whether she wanted to. Whether forgiving Cassian Voss would be a gift or a surrender, whether it would close something or hand him the keys to it. She turned it over the way Odile turned over engine problems, looking for the fault line.

And found she couldn't answer. Not yet. Maybe not ever. And discovered, sitting with the not-answer, that it didn't hurt the way she expected. The old Tamsin—the one from chapter one, diving restricted wrecks at night to service a debt—would have needed an answer. Would have needed to resolve it, file it, be done. This one could hold it. Could carry a letter she hadn't decided how to feel about, the same way she'd carried a ledger of strangers' names up a river to a fenced facility, not knowing what justice would look like until she was standing in it.

She folded the letter along its original creases.

She did not burn it. She noticed herself noticing that—some part of her waiting for the urge, the ritual, the clean ending—and it didn't come. Burning was for secrets. This wasn't a secret anymore. Nothing was.

What she did instead surprised her. She went down to the main room of the steeple house, where Mara kept the parish records—the reconstructed ones, the ones the survivors' network had rebuilt from memory, baptismal certificates, and half a legible census—and asked for a blank page and ink.

"You're writing back?" Mara said. "He said not to."

"I'm not writing back." She flattened the page. "I'm drafting something else."

Because here was what she'd realized on the stairs, in the space between the second reading and the decision: Voss had asked for one thing, and asked it well, and asked it humbly, and none of that changed the arithmetic. But the marker—the stone, the hearings, the public naming—he'd framed it as *his* request, *his* family's penance, something the Voss name would permit or refuse. As if the shape of remembrance belonged to whoever had caused the wound.

It didn't. It belonged to the wounded.

So she wrote—not to Voss, and not a refusal, and not a grant. She wrote a proposal, addressed to the hearings commission, co-signed that evening by Mara Iles on behalf of the survivor network and by Odile, Bram, and Pip as witnesses of record. It laid out the design for the memorial at the rebuilt Hollow docks: the victims' names cut deep and large, facing the water, readable from a boat. And the Voss name—if it appeared at all—cut shallow, small, positioned low on the stone where a person would have to kneel at the waterline to read it. Below the names. Beneath them, literally and otherwise.

Not erased. Erasure was the family trade; she wouldn't practice it, even on him. Present, accountable, subordinate to the truth. Let history keep him the way the river kept wrecks: visible, instructive, and no longer in charge of the current.

Mara read it and smiled for the first time in days. "He'll hate it," she said.

"He said he didn't want to know," Tamsin said. "So he won't. From us."

She sealed the proposal, added it to the supply run south, and then—on impulse she didn't examine until later—slipped Voss's letter inside her chart case, flat against the waterproof backing where her father's dive logs used to ride. Not displayed. Not hidden. Kept. The way you keep a chart of water you've survived: not because you love the memory, but because you refuse to ever again pretend you don't know what's down there.

Pip found her at dusk on the dock, feet dangling over the edge, watching the current move through the channel where the ferry lay.

"You kept it," he said. It wasn't a question. He'd seen the chart case.

"For now."

"That's not an answer."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't." She bumped his shoulder with hers. "Get used to it. Most of the good ones aren't."

Below her feet, the river ran dark and steady toward the sea, carrying everything it carried—names, letters, a stopped compass waiting for tomorrow's tide—and ahead of her, up the dock, Odile was already arguing with Bram about the fuel lines on the *Kestrel*, and Pip's laugh carried over the water, and the rebuilt Hollow smelled at last like paint and woodsmoke instead of silt.

One year, almost to the day, since she'd cast off with no debts and no secrets.

Tomorrow they'd launch again. New charter, first contract: a drowned town two hundred miles south, a family who'd waited forty years for someone to go down and bring their names home.

She watched the water and let the letter stay unread in her mind's third reading—the one she hadn't finished yet, maybe wouldn't for a long time.

Some currents, she was learning, you don't cross. You just learn to swim in them.
