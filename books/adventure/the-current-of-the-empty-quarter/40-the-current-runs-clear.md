## 40. The Current Runs Clear

One year ago I dove a wreck at night with a patrol boat's searchlight crawling across my back and a debt sitting on my chest like a weight belt. Tonight I stand on the rebuilt docks of Meridian Hollow in full daylight, and the only thing pressing down on me is the smell of fresh-cut timber and someone's frying fish drifting up from the market stalls.

The river has changed. That's the thing nobody tells you about water—it remembers being dirty, but it doesn't hold grudges. A year of remediation barges, a year of Odile bullying contractors over flow rates, a year of Bram's testimony read aloud in a hearing room while senators pretended they'd always cared—and the current running past the Hollow docks is the color of bottle glass again. You can see the pilings all the way down. You can see fish.

I can see, if I look hard enough at the right angle off the third pier, the shadow where the ferry lies.

We don't dive her anymore. She's a grave now, officially. Marked on charts. Named on the memorial wall that Mara Iles spent eleven months arguing into existence, stone by donated stone, name by fought-for name. Two hundred and six names. My father's is not among them—he wasn't from the Hollow—but Mara put it there anyway, at the bottom, in smaller letters, under a single line: *He came back for us.*

He didn't come back. But his ledger did. Close enough. It's more than most ghosts get.

"Tamsin." Odile's voice carries across the deck of the *Ledger's Wake*—Pip named her, over my objection, over everyone's objection, and then we all got used to it the way you get used to a scar. "Fuel lines are set. She'll take diesel or she'll take prayer, your pick."

"Both," I say. "In that order."

She grins and goes back to wrestling the coupling, forearms greased to the elbow, hair tied up in a rag that used to be a parish altar cloth. Mara gave it to her. Odile pretends it's just a rag. Nobody believes her.

Bram sits on the rail with a mug of coffee so bad it could strip paint, watching the water the way he still watches everything—like it might lie to him. The navy took his pension when he testified. He says he sleeps better. I believe him, mostly because I've seen him sleep, face-down and snoring, in places no honest man would find restful.

And Pip—Pip is at the bow, sixteen now and pretending he isn't, coiling line with the quick economy of someone who learned knots before he learned long division. His town is on the map again. Not repopulated—the reservoir project was never reversed, some wounds are just geography now—but named. Restored to the official record, which for people like us is its own kind of resurrection. There's a plaque. He sent his mother a photograph of it. She wrote back that she'd always known where home was; now the government did too.

He looks up and catches me watching. "You're doing the thing," he says.

"What thing?"

"The standing-at-the-wheel-looking-like-you're-about-to-deliver-a-speech thing. Don't. We've got a schedule."

"We have a tide," I correct him, "and tides don't schedule."

"Everything schedules," Pip says, with the serene authority of a teenager who has never once been wrong in his own estimation. "Odile scheduled the fuel. Bram scheduled the weather window. I scheduled lunch."

"Lunch," Bram says into his coffee, "is the only part of this operation with redundancy built in."

I laugh. A year ago I didn't laugh much. A year ago I measured my days in installments owed to a man named Corvus Dree, whose ledger of my debts died quietly in a navy settlement the same week the hearings began. They paid to make me quiet. I took the check and turned it over to the survivors' fund before the ink dried, and Dree's collectors found other work, and I found something better than being owed to or owing.

I found a heading.

* * *

Mara comes down the dock road at noon, walking slow the way she always does now, like the ground owes her nothing and she owes it nothing either. She's carrying a paper bag and an envelope, and I know what's in the envelope before she hands it to me, because I've seen that handwriting once before and once was enough.

"He writes every month," she says. "This one asked me to give it to you today. Launch day." Her mouth does something that isn't quite a smile. "He remembered."

Voss remembers things. It's his curse. Cassian Voss, awaiting sentencing in a federal facility with a view of a drainage canal, has spent twelve months writing letters to everyone his grandfather's program ever touched—apologies shaped like information, each one containing a fact, a date, a location of remains, something useful. He asks for nothing. That's what makes them hard to read. A man begging forgiveness you can refuse. A man asking nothing just leaves you standing in an open room with your own thoughts.

I open the envelope on the rail.

Inside: a single page, and beneath it, flat as a pressed flower, a photograph. The old Hollow ferry terminal, pre-flood, crowded with people who didn't know. And on the reverse, in Voss's careful hand:

*My grandfather stood on that platform the morning the sirens failed. I have his diary now. He wrote that the water would be "a clean solution." I am told the marker stone will carry my family name beside the victims'. I did not ask for that. I asked only that it be small, and true, and placed where the river can reach it. If you ever pass it—if the current takes you that way—I would know that someone read it. No reply necessary. None expected. — C.V.*

I read it twice. I keep it. That's all the answer either of us needs, maybe ever.

"He's not asking for forgiveness," Mara says, watching my face.

"No."

"Good. Because it isn't ours to give. It belongs to the two hundred and six." She sets the paper bag on the rail—fried fish, still hot, wrapped in newsprint from the *Hollow Current*, the paper the survivors started, the one that ran the names first. "But a man who spends his sentence making the dead easier to find is doing something. I've decided to let it be something."

From Mara Iles, that's a papal blessing. I take a piece of fish and eat it looking at the water, and the water looks clear, and for a moment I let myself think about a man in a cell by a drainage canal who will never see a clean river again, and I feel—not forgiveness. Something adjacent. Something that lives next door to forgiveness and borrows its tools.

"Pip!" I call. "Add a stop to the schedule."

He appears instantly, suspicious. "We have a route."

"We're going to have a detour. Downstream, past the old ferry landing. There's a marker stone there now."

His suspicion softens. "The one with the small true words."

"That one. We'll read it out loud. All of it. Every launch, every return. Crew tradition."

Bram raises his terrible coffee. "To the two hundred and six."

"To the two hundred and six," we say, and even Odile stops fighting the fuel line to say it, and the river runs past us toward the sea, carrying nothing we need to hide.

* * *

We cast off at the turn of the afternoon tide, and I do it by hand, uncleating the last line myself, because some rituals you keep even after the reason for them rots away.

A year ago my casting-off was escape. Lines thrown, engine coughing, running downstream ahead of a patrol boat with a strongbox full of corroded secrets and a loan shark's interest accruing by the hour. A year ago I read my father's water-stained compass every night like a rosary, asking a dead man for permission to keep going.

Tonight—or rather, this bright ordinary afternoon—I don't need permission. But I take the compass out anyway, because it's not a rosary anymore. It's just a compass. The microfilm key is long gone, surrendered as evidence, copied a hundred times over into the public record. The casing is scratched, the glass clouded, the needle lazy in the way of old instruments that have earned their rest.

It still finds north. That's all I ever needed it to do.

I hold it up at the wheel, let the needle settle, and Pip leans against the rail beside me without being asked, which is how he does everything now—arriving exactly where he's needed and acting like it was his idea.

"You're doing the thing again," he says.

"I'm navigating."

"You're holding a compass and looking like you're about to deliver a speech."

"Maybe I am."

"Odile will mock you."

"Odile mocks me for breathing."

"She's thorough." He squints downstream, where the channel markers run green and red toward the widening light of the estuary. "So. Where first? The commission wants the Kettle Reach survey by autumn. There's the drowned quarry town past Weir Nine—families have been writing in. And there's a reservoir upstate, somebody's town, whole place went under in '61, no records survived except—" he taps his own chest, grinning, "—an apprentice who knows how to find them."

"All of it," I say. "Every drowned place that wants finding. That's the charter. That's the whole charter."

"And after that?"

"After that, whatever the river shows us."

He nods like this is a reasonable business plan, which, for us, it is. Then he goes forward to help Odile with the lines, and Bram settles into the sonar station he rebuilt from salvage, and the *Ledger's Wake* gathers way, and Meridian Hollow falls astern—its new docks, its market smoke, its memorial wall catching the late sun, its people on the shore raising hands or fists or coffee mugs in our direction, depending on their disposition.

Mara stays on the dock longest. She doesn't wave. She stands with her arms folded, watching us go the way she watches everything—like a woman keeping an appointment with history—and when we're far enough out that waving would be pointless, she turns and walks back into the town she refused to let die, and I stop watching, because some things you don't need to see through to trust.

* * *

Past the ferry landing, past the marker stone—we read it aloud, all of it, every word, even the small true ones, even the name at the bottom in smaller letters—the river opens up.

Wide, brown-gold, unhurried. Running clear to the sea.

I stand at the wheel with no debts, no secrets, no patrol boats in my wake and no ledgers in my hold. Just survey gear, bad coffee, good people, and a list of drowned towns waiting for someone to come back for them the way my father came back, the way we all came back, the way you keep coming back until coming back is just called going home.

Home. I spent years thinking it was a place I'd been expelled from—a berth, a reputation, a name cleared or stolen. It took a flooded city and a book of names to teach me different. Home is a heading. You point yourself at it and go, and the going is the having.

The compass rests on the binnacle in front of me, needle steady, pointing the only way it knows.

Ahead, the estuary light opens like a door.

"Helm answers," Pip calls from the bow, grinning into the wind. "Where to, Captain?"

I look at the clear water, at my crew, at the long bright miles between here and everywhere.

"Downriver," I say. "All the way down. Then we start on the up."

The *Ledger's Wake* heels gently, catches the current, and runs—clean, unburdened, and fast—toward the sea.
