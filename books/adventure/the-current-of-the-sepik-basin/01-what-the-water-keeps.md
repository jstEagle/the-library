## 1. What the Water Keeps

The Sepik did not want her down there, and Paloma Thackeray had learned to stop asking its permission.

She hung in the brown-green murk at eleven meters, breathing the last clean air of her third tank, and let the river press against her like a hand testing a door. Silt drifted past her mask in slow ribbons — the river's endless shedding of itself, topsoil from the highlands, rotted leaves, ash from some garden fire upstream, all of it traveling toward the sea with a patience she envied. Visibility was two meters on a good day. Today it was barely one and a half, and the good day was over by noon anyway.

Below her, the wreck resolved out of the gloom the way it always did: not all at once but in pieces, as if the water were deciding how much to show her. A slope of hull plating furred with weed. The black socket of a cargo hold. The ribs of a deckhouse reaching up like something that had tried to climb out and been pulled back down.

The *Kuranda Star*. Forty years drowned, navy-restricted, officially a hazard to navigation and unofficially a grave nobody wanted named. Officially, Paloma was salvaging scrap off the *Merinda*, a rust-bucket freighter two kilometers downstream. Unofficially, everyone on this stretch of the river knew where her boat anchored at dawn, and everyone had decided — for their own reasons, none of them generous — to keep knowing without saying.

She finned down along the hull, gloves trailing the plating the way Tio had taught her. *Never grab. Never trust. The wreck is a stranger until you've touched every part of it twice.* His voice lived in her ears down here, clearer than it ever did above the surface. Down here he was still alive, still cranky, still alive.

Her brother's voice didn't live anywhere anymore.

She found the cut she'd started yesterday and worked it wider with the hydraulic shear, sparks swallowed instantly by the water, a dull orange bloom that died before it finished being light. Inside the compartment she'd opened: copper pipe, brass fittings, a bank of gauges gone green as pond ice. Scrap. Enough to keep Tio's compressor running another month, enough to pay the fuel levy and the harbor man at Angoram who pretended not to notice which wrecks her anchor dragged over. Not enough to change anything. She'd stopped diving for enough-to-change-things years ago. That kind of diving got people killed, or worse — it got them noticed.

*Keep your head down,* her mother had told her, once, the only useful thing she'd ever said. *The river takes what rises.*

Mateo had risen. That was the joke she never told anyone, the joke that lived under her sternum like a swallowed hook. Everyone said the river took what sank, and it did, it took hulls and engines and whole villages in flood years, but what it took from her had risen first — Mateo surfacing after his last free-dive, laughing, waving, and then not surfacing again an hour later when it mattered. They'd found him three days downstream, snagged in a fish fence, his hands still curled around nothing. Seventeen years old and holding onto air.

Paloma breathed out through her regulator and watched the bubbles climb away from her, silver and quick, going up where she couldn't follow yet.

She worked. That was the arrangement between her and the river: she worked, and it let her. Her hands knew the shear's weight, knew the angle of cut that wouldn't jam, knew how long a tank lasted at this depth when her heart rate stayed low and her mind stayed empty. Restless competence, Tio called it. *You dive like a woman running from something,* he'd said, twenty years ago, and she'd said *I am,* and he'd laughed, because everyone ran from something on the Sepik, and the ones who admitted it lasted longer than the ones who didn't.

The cut gave way. The plate swung inward on a hinge of rust, and the compartment exhaled — a slow belch of silt and forty-year-old river breath that clouded everything to milk. Paloma backed off, waited, counted her breaths. Sixty. Eighty. The murk began to settle in layers, thickest at the bottom, and she dropped back into the hole feet-first, one glove leading.

Her fingers found shelving. Jars, most of them broken, their contents long since become part of the mud. Then metal. A box shape, square-shouldered, heavier than the scrap around it. She hauled it into the gray half-light and turned it over.

Not a munitions crate. Not a tool locker. It was a chest — banded iron, wood showing through the corrosion in dark stripes like the ribs of some drowned animal — and across its lid, raised under her glove even through the rust-scale, a seal. She scraped at it with her dive knife, gently, the way you'd scrape ice off glass you didn't want to crack.

A crest emerged. An eagle with spread wings standing on a wheel, and beneath it letters worn nearly smooth: letters in no alphabet used on any ship that sailed these waters now. A dead nation's mark. A country that had existed for eleven years, fought its war, lost it, and been erased so thoroughly that children in Angoram drew maps of the world without it, because their teachers' maps didn't have it either.

Paloma crouched on the wreck's deck with the chest in her lap and felt the river go quiet around her. Not calm — quiet, the way a house goes quiet when someone stops talking in the next room. The current still pushed at her fins. The silt still fell. But underneath all of it, something had shifted its attention toward her, and she had been on this river long enough to know that feeling the way she knew a storm line on the horizon.

The chest was sealed. Not rusted shut — sealed, gasketed, deliberate, the way you sealed something you intended to come back for. Somebody had put this here on purpose, in a restricted wreck, inside a compartment behind a false bulkhead she'd only found because the *Kuranda Star*'s hull plates had shifted in last year's floods and shown her a seam that shouldn't exist.

Somebody had meant to come back. Nobody had. And now the river had handed it to the one diver on this stretch of water who kept her head down, and Paloma understood, with a coldness that had nothing to do with depth, that her careful life had just developed a crack straight through the middle of it.

She could leave it. Set it back in the dark, weld the seam of silt closed with her own silence, go up and sell her copper pipe and be poor and invisible for another season. That was the smart move. That was the move Tio would tell her to make, in his gravel voice, if she were fool enough to ask him.

But her hands were already clipping the chest to her lift line, already inflating the bag, already doing the thing her body had decided while her mind was still arguing. Because the seal was a dead nation's, yes — but the chest had been hidden *here*, in the Sepik's water, among the Sepik's drowned, and things hidden here belonged to the river's memory, and the river's memory was the only ledger this basin had ever kept honestly.

And because Mateo had held onto nothing when the water took him, and she was tired of holding onto nothing too.

* * *

She surfaced into late gold light and the smell of woodsmoke from the far bank, and the world above the water was exactly as small and manageable as she'd left it. Her boat rode at anchor in the lee of a sandbar spit, paint peeling, tarp rigged crooked, the little single-slung crane Tio had welded for her standing against the sky like a gallows that had changed careers. The chest came up dripping, sat in the bilge water among her scrap sacks, and looked — she had to admit — like nothing. Like junk. Like a box any salvage diver would strip for the iron bands and burn the rest.

Paloma sat on the gunwale with her fins off and her legs in the warm river and made herself look at it for a long time.

The eagle on the wheel. She'd seen that crest before, once, years ago — stamped into a corroded strongbox door Tio had bought sight-unseen at a estate auction in Wewak, and when they'd pried it open there'd been nothing inside but a child's shoes and a bundle of letters in a language neither of them could read, and Tio had gone very quiet and then very drunk, and in the morning he'd burned the letters in the workshop stove and told her never to bring him anything with that bird on it again. *That nation ate people,* was all he'd say. *It ate them and then it ate its own name.*

She hadn't thought about those shoes in fifteen years. Now she could see them perfectly, small and cracked, laces still tied.

The light was going. Upstream, past the bend, the navy post at Ambunti would be lighting its generator, and the patrol boat that sometimes idled down this far would be tied up for the night, its crew playing cards under a bulb, its commander — Reyes, the new one, the one who asked questions with a smile — drinking something better than card players drank. She'd seen him twice at the Angoram wharf. Both times he'd watched her boat a moment too long, and both times she'd told herself it was nothing, because a woman alone on this river survived by deciding that most things were nothing.

The chest sat in the bilge, patient as a debt.

Paloma hauled anchor and ran downstream with the last of the light, the river sliding gold and then bronze and then dark beneath her hull, and all the way home she felt it behind her in the boat like a passenger — silent, sealed, carrying whatever it carried — and she caught herself, more than once, checking over her shoulder at a stretch of empty water, as if the river might have changed its mind about letting her keep it.

Tio was waiting at the stilt-house with the lamp lit and rice on the burner, and when he saw her face before she'd even tied off, he set down the ladle and said, "What did you bring up?"

"Scrap," Paloma said.

He looked at the chest. He looked at her. The lamp hissed, and somewhere out in the dark the river moved against the pilings, steady as breathing, keeping — as always — whatever it kept.

"That," Tio said quietly, "is not scrap."

"No," Paloma said, and reached for the crowbar.
