## 2. The Ledger of Captain Voss

The chest sat on Paloma's workbench like a thing that had drowned and refused to admit it.

She'd left it there overnight, wedged between the vise and the coil of lifting bags, and all through the small hours she'd heard it — not a sound, exactly, but the absence of one, the way a room changes when something in it is waiting. The stilt-house creaked on its pilings above the brown water. Rain ticked against the iron roof in the fitful way of the dry season's last storms. And the chest said nothing, which was worse than if it had knocked.

By first light she'd made coffee strong enough to strip paint and told herself she was only going to clean it. That was salvage work: you cleaned what you pulled up, catalogued it, sold it or scrapped it. A routine. Routines were how you stayed alive on the Sepik — Tio had taught her that before he'd taught her anything else. *The river doesn't punish greed, girl. It punishes carelessness.*

But her hands knew better than her head did. They went straight to the seal.

It was stamped into a lead disc set into the lid, no bigger than a coin, and even under forty years of river silt the design held its lines with the arrogance of an empire: a crowned eagle clutching a globe, wings spread over crossed anchors. She'd turned it under the lamp half the night, consulting the battered reference books Tio kept for exactly this purpose — hull plates, engine serials, the detritus of every nation that had ever sunk something in these waters. The eagle-and-globe matched nothing current. It matched nothing recent either. The trading house it belonged to had folded before Paloma was born, its ships scuttled or sold, its name scrubbed from the registries so thoroughly that finding its mark on the Sepik was like finding a footprint from someone who'd never walked there.

A dead nation's seal, on a chest that came up from a wreck the navy had wire-buoyed off-limits.

She worked the lid free with a pry bar and a patient hatred for rust, working the blade along the seam the way she'd learned to open oysters, feeling for the give. Rivets popped one by one, small reports like distant gunfire. Inside, packed in tarred canvas gone hard as shell, lay bundles of paper.

Not gold. Not weapons. Paper.

Her first reaction was disappointment, sharp and childish, and she was ashamed of it almost before she felt it. Then she lifted the top bundle into the light and stopped being disappointed and started being afraid.

It was a ledger. Ship's stationery, watermarked, the pages ruled in faded violet. The cover bore the same eagle-and-globe, and beneath it, in a hand that started crisp and deteriorated page by page as though written by a man running out of time, the name *K. Voss, Kapitän*.

Paloma read standing up, because sitting down felt too much like settling in, and she didn't want to settle in. She wanted to read three pages and put it back and sell the whole thing to some collector in Wewak who liked dead empires, and forget it.

Voss had been a quartermaster's nightmare and a historian's dream. He recorded everything: fuel tonnages, rations, the names of officers transferred out on charges she could guess at, and then — beginning somewhere around the middle of the book, after the entries stopped being about cargo and started being about *cargo* — crates. Crates numbered, weighed, and moved inland. Crates that had come off his ship at night, at a landing no chart would confirm, and gone upriver on barges crewed by men whose names Voss wrote without ranks, as if they'd stopped being military and become something else.

And then the ledger changed again. It became a map.

Not drawn — described. Distances in paddles' strokes. Bearings taken off a hill shaped, Voss wrote, *like a sleeping crocodile*. A waterfall heard before it was seen. A grotto behind it, dry in the dry season, sealed in the wet. And a list — this was the part Paloma read twice, three times, her lips moving though she hadn't spoken aloud in hours — a list of families. Village families. Names written in a colonial clerk's careful script, each with a notation beside it.

*Sheltered. Trusted.* One word repeated down the column like a litany: *Wari. Wari. Wari.*

She knew the name. Everyone on this stretch of the river knew the Waris — the cartographer family from up past Angoram, the ones who'd mapped half the tributaries the government still listed as *unsurveyed*. There was a Wari who ran the fuel dump at Angoram. There had been a Wari schoolteacher, dead now. And there was Elias Wari, whom she'd seen maybe six times in ten years, always alone, always with a survey case strapped to his back, a man who looked at the river the way she looked at it — like something that owed him an answer.

His grandfather's name was in the ledger. Third from the top. *Sheltered. Trusted.*

Paloma set the ledger down on the bench very carefully, the way you set down a snake you weren't sure was dead.

Outside, the morning had gone on without her. A kingfisher staked its claim on a piling. Somewhere downstream a diesel engine knocked in that familiar broken rhythm, boats going about their business, the whole river pretending to be ordinary. She stood in the doorway of her workshop with the smell of rust and old paper clinging to her hands and understood, with the slow cold certainty of floodwater rising over a step, that nothing here was ordinary anymore, and that the chest had not been lost. It had been waiting. Forty years in the dark, and it had waited for *her*, and she did not believe in fate but she believed absolutely in trouble, and trouble had just signed itself into her keeping.

She should burn it. The thought arrived fully formed, sensible, and she picked up the ledger and carried it toward the little kerosene stove and stood there holding it over the flame's reach and could not make her hands let go.

Because Voss hadn't just written down where the crates went. He'd written down *why*. The last pages were different — tighter, smaller, the handwriting of a man writing against discovery. He wrote of things loaded in the dark holds that never appeared on manifests. He wrote of villages burned upriver as reprisal, and of orders to record nothing, and of his own decision to record everything. *If we are to be erased,* the final entry read, *let there remain one pen that refuses.* Then coordinates, ciphered, and a last line in a different ink: *The families know. Trust the families. They have kept faith longer than any flag.*

Paloma lowered the ledger. Her brother's face floated up unbidden — Daniel, seventeen, gone under off the wreck she'd dived yesterday, nine years ago this season, and she had spent nine years diving that river looking for everything except him. She thought: this is what the water keeps. Not scrap. Not gold. This. The accounts nobody wanted settled.

She wrapped the ledger in oilcloth and hid it inside the false bottom of her tool chest, beneath the dive knives, and told herself she'd decide tomorrow.

Commander Reyes arrived at four in the afternoon, which was wrong in itself. Nobody official came at four in the afternoon; the heat was at its meanest, the light flat and merciless, and government men traveled in the cool of morning or not at all. But the grey patrol boat slid up against her pilings anyway, quiet as a shark, and the man who stepped onto her ladder wore civilian clothes that fit him like a uniform trying to behave.

He was maybe fifty, lean, with the permanent tan of someone who'd served on water his whole career and the particular stillness of a man who watched more than he spoke. Two sailors stayed with the boat. He climbed alone.

"Paloma Thackeray." He said it like he was confirming a specimen. "I'm told you dive the restricted sector."

"I dive a lot of sectors." She stayed on the workshop steps, arms crossed, keeping the door at her back. "Mostly the ones that pay."

"The navy pays." He smiled without warmth, a professional expression deployed like a signal lamp. "Salvage rights, hazard fees. We're conducting a recovery operation on the *Karlsruhe* wreck. Any material recovered from the exclusion zone is, of course, state property."

"Of course."

"You brought up something two days ago." He wasn't asking. His eyes went past her shoulder, into the dim workshop, unhurried, inventorying. "A container. Iron-bound. Sealed."

The heat pressed down. Paloma heard her own heartbeat and made her face do nothing at all. "I bring up containers most weeks. Drums, boxes, engine parts. The war threw a lot of junk in this river."

"This one had a seal." He produced a photograph from his breast pocket — her own boat, moored at Angoram, shot from across the water with a lens long enough to count the rivets on the crate lashed in her well deck. "Lead disc. Eagle and globe. You may recognize it."

"I wouldn't know one eagle from another."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "Most people wouldn't. That's rather the point." He folded the photograph away. "The vessel that chest came from carried records belonging to a trading concern liquidated in 'forty-six. Its assets were dispersed under terms that are now considered... incomplete. My government has an interest in completeness, Miss Thackeray. I'm sure you can appreciate that. Bureaucracies hate loose ends."

"And you're the loose-end man."

"I'm the *tidy* man." Something moved behind his eyes then, brief and cold, a current under still water. "Here is my difficulty. Three days ago my office received a report that a diver in your sector recovered material matching that description. The report was specific. Unusually specific. Which suggests that whoever filed it already knew what was down there — and knew it would surface eventually." He paused, letting the silence do its work. "So you see my problem. Either you found it, or someone believes you found it. In both cases, I need to know where it is now."

"And if I say I've never seen it?"

"Then I'll say that's a shame," Reyes said, "and I'll mention that men who recover sealed chests from dead nations' wrecks have historically had short, eventful careers. Fires happen on the river. Boats sink. Divers stay down." He said it the way another man might discuss the weather, and somehow that was worse than a threat shouted. He descended the ladder, then paused, one hand on the rail, and looked back up at her with something almost like courtesy. "You seem like a careful woman, Miss Thackeray. Careful women live long lives on this river. Be careful."

The patrol boat pulled away without wake, sliding downstream into the glare, and Paloma stood on her steps until the engine sound dissolved into the ordinary noise of the water.

Then she went inside and took out the ledger and read the list of names again, all of them, every village family marked *sheltered, trusted* — and understood that Reyes hadn't come because of a report. He'd come because the chest was a marker, and markers get claimed, and everyone who touched it got written onto somebody's list. Voss's list. Or the other kind.

She wrapped it deeper in oilcloth, thinking of fires, of boats that sink, of divers who stay down. Tomorrow, she decided. Tomorrow she'd take it upriver, find this Elias Wari, and put this inheritance back in the hands of the people Voss had trusted with it.

Tomorrow, she thought, and outside, far downriver, a light flickered once against the dark water — too steady for lightning, too moving for a star.

She watched it a long time before she recognized it for what it was.

A boat, coming upstream. Running dark.
