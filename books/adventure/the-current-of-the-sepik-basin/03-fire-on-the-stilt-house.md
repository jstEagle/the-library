## 3. Fire on the Stilt-House

The chest would not leave her alone.

Paloma had wedged it under the floorboards of her workshop, beneath a false panel she'd laid herself from river timber, and still it sat in her mind like a stone in a boot. All through the afternoon she'd told herself the sensible things: that rusted iron was common, that dead empires left their litter everywhere along the Sepik like teeth in a jawbone, that Commander Reyes's questions had been the ordinary questions of an ordinary man doing his job. She told herself these things while she resealed her dive mask with beeswax, while she mended the frayed strap on her weight belt, while Tio sat on her veranda sharpening his filleting knife against a leather strop with the slow patience of a man who had done ten thousand small tasks by the water and expected to do ten thousand more.

"You're listening to the house," Tio said.

"I'm working."

"You're listening." He didn't look up from the knife. The strop whispered. "House has been talking all day. You hear it too — the boards ticking when there's no heat to make them tick."

"It's the river," Paloma said. "It moves everything."

"The river moves everything," he agreed. "That is not always comfort."

She wanted to laugh at him and couldn't. Tio had taught her to dive when she was nineteen and hollowed out with grief for Marco, had taught her that the water forgave nothing but forgot nothing either, and that a diver who confused the two would die of it. He was seventy-one years old and moved like driftwood, slow and unhurried, and he had never once been wrong about the river's moods. She trusted him the way she trusted depth gauges: completely, because her life depended on it.

"Reyes will come back," she said finally.

"He already knows what you pulled up. That's why he came asking instead of sending soldiers." The knife whispered against the leather. "A man who sends soldiers doesn't need answers. A man who comes himself is afraid of something."

"Of me?"

"Of the chest." Tio set the knife down and looked at her across the dimming workshop, lamplight catching the grey in his beard. "You opened it?"

"Not yet."

"Liar," he said gently. "You've had it open since before I came. I can smell the paper. Old paper smells like a church that's been closed a long time."

Paloma knelt and lifted the floor panel. The chest sat where she'd left it, lid thrown back, and inside lay the ledger wrapped in oilcloth — dense, blocky script in a language neither of them could read more than fragments of, columns of figures, and maps. Maps of the Sepik drawn with colonial precision, channels marked, depths noted, and at three points along the river, small symbols like anchors crossed with arrows. And names. Pages of names in careful lists — village names, family names, the families who had sheltered whatever the cache was, written down by some captain's clerk as though gratitude were a debt to be recorded.

One name appeared again and again, inked darker than the rest, as if someone had come back to it:

*Wari.*

"Tio," she said slowly, "do you know a family called Wari? Upstream, past Angoram way?"

He was quiet a long moment. "Old Wari family. Cartographers, some of them. My father traded with a Wari man once — said he could draw the river better than the river could draw itself." He frowned. "Why?"

"Because they're in here. Whoever wrote this book owed them something, or thought it did. And Reyes asked about cargo manifests from '44. He asked about *this stretch* of river." She closed the ledger. "Tio, I think I pulled up something that people died over. People are maybe still dying over."

"Then you have two choices," Tio said. "Sell it fast to whoever pays most, or give it back to the ones it belongs to." He rose, joints cracking like green wood in a fire. "There is no third choice. There never is. Things like this don't let you keep them neutral."

"And which do I do?"

"That's not mine to say." He paused at the door, silhouetted against the last grey light on the water. "But I'll tell you what my father said about the war years. Everyone hid something. The smart ones hid it well. The wise ones hid it and then *forgot where.*" His eyes went to the chest. "You're not wise enough to forget, girl. Neither am I."

He went home along the boardwalk, and Paloma sat with the ledger until the lamp burned low, and somewhere in the small hours she slept at the table with her hand flat on the oilcloth as if holding down a living thing.

* * *

She woke to smoke.

Not woodsmoke — not the sweet haze of cooking fires drifting across the water at dawn. This was sharp, chemical, wrong, and it was coming from below her own floor.

Paloma came off the chair and the world tilted with sleep and terror. Through the gaps in the boards she saw orange light licking up between the stilts, feeding on the kerosene-soaked rags someone had stuffed against the pilings. Below, on the black water, a longboat idled without lights, and a man stood in it with a jerry can, methodical as a farmer sowing seed.

They hadn't come for her to find alive. Or they had, and fire was just how they asked.

She grabbed the ledger — only the ledger, the oilcloth bundle jammed inside her jacket, no time for anything else, no time even to understand that she was leaving behind every photograph of Marco, every tool, every year of her scraped-together life — and went out the back window onto the platform over the water as the front wall breathed flame.

The heat came after her like a hand. She dropped into the river.

The water took her the way it takes everything: completely. She swam under the surface, ledger clutched to her ribs, and came up behind her own dive boat, moored in the shadow of the next stilt-house. Behind her, her home roared — she heard the windows go, heard the distinctive soft collapse of a roof giving itself to fire, and heard something else. Voices. Shouting. Not the mercenaries'.

*Tio.*

He was on the boardwalk in his undershirt, ancient and furious, standing between the burning house and the longboat with his filleting knife in his fist, and he was shouting at them in three languages, giving them the full weight of his contempt, buying seconds the way he'd bought everything in his life — cheaply, patiently, with work.

"No!" Paloma's voice tore out of her. "Tio, get down—"

One of them shot him.

It wasn't dramatic. That was the obscenity of it. No duel, no speech. Just a man lowering a pistol and an old man folding like laundry, and the boardwalk planks taking him, and the firelight moving over his face as though the river were already closing his eyes.

Paloma made a sound she had never made before, animal and enormous, and started for the bank, and hands — her own hands, her own survival, the cold arithmetic Tio had drilled into her across twenty years of diving — hauled her back. *Never surface into danger. Never rise into the thing that killed you. Go deep, go dark, go now.*

She got the dive boat's engine going on the third try, coughing, half-flooded bilge slopping at her knees, and steered out into the current with her home burning behind her and Tio lying on the boards with his knife still in his fist, and the river carried her away from both.

* * *

Dawn found her thirty kilometres downstream, running the narrow channels south of the main stem where the patrol boats wouldn't fit. The boat leaked steadily; she bailed with a bucket while steering with her knees, and the monsoon clouds stacked up over the ranges like a threat being composed.

She should have gone to ground. Every instinct said hide, wait, let the river close over the whole business. But Tio's voice was in her head, and it wasn't saying hide.

*"Everyone hid something. The wise ones forgot where."*

She had the ledger open on her knee whenever the channel straightened, one eye on the water, one on the pages. The Wari name. The crossed-anchor marks. Three points on the river, and the deepest mark — the final cipher, half-torn, a set of coordinates rendered in an old survey system — sat upstream, past Angoram, in country she didn't know and couldn't navigate alone.

She needed someone who knew the river the way Tio had. Someone whose family the ledger remembered.

Grief kept arriving in waves, the way water does. One moment she was reading coordinates; the next she was seventeen again, learning that Marco's boat had been found empty; the next she was watching an old man die for a stranger's curiosity, for *her* curiosity, and understanding that the wilderness had opened an account in her name and would collect.

"The river forgives nothing," Tio used to say. "But it forgets nothing either. So make sure what it remembers of you is worth carrying."

She bailed the boat. She held the course. And far upstream, past Angoram, past the drowned mission church she didn't know existed yet, a government mapmaker was drawing the bends of the Sepik in careful ink — a man named Wari, who did not yet know that his grandfather's name was waiting for him in a dead captain's handwriting, or that a leaking dive boat with a grieving woman aboard was already fighting its way upriver toward him.

Paloma saw the first rain hit the water ahead of her, silver and hard as nails, and turned her bow into it.
