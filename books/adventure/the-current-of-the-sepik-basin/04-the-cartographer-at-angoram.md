## 4. The Cartographer at Angoram

Elias Wari had drawn the Sepik so many times he could trace it blind — every oxbow, every silted channel, every village that the government maps marked with a dot and a name and nothing else. The river deserved better than dots. It deserved its moods, its tempers, its long memory. But the Survey Office in Wewak wanted clean lines and legible labels, and Elias had learned to give them what they wanted while keeping his own maps folded in the back of his mind, dense with everything the official ink left out.

He was in Angoram to resupply and re-ink his field sheets, three weeks into a survey of the middle reaches, when the woman came into the trade store like weather.

She moved wrong for Angoram. Too fast, too low, eyes doing the work of a sentry — checking the door, the window, the two men playing cards by the kerosene shelf. Her clothes were wrong too: salt-stiff canvas trousers, a dive knife on her belt worn down to a sliver, and under the river mud on her forearms, the pale stripe of old tan lines where gloves used to sit. A salvage diver. He knew the type from the lower river. People who made their living off what the water swallowed and never quite digested.

She bought diesel filters with crumpled notes, rope, a tin of matches, rice, tinned fish. Her hands shook as she counted, and she kept glancing at the window as if the river itself might be following her.

Elias went back to his survey sheets. It wasn't his business. In eleven years away from his village he had gotten very good at things not being his business.

Then she said his name.

Not his name — his family's. She said it to the storekeeper, low but not low enough: "I'm looking for someone who knows the upper channels past Korogu. The Wari people. Anyone carry that name up there?"

The storekeeper shrugged the way storekeepers shrug, all commerce and no memory. But Elias's pen had stopped moving.

*Wari.* His grandfather's name, spoken in a stranger's mouth in a trade store two hundred kilometers from home. He watched her shoulders — the way she carried exhaustion like ballast, the way she flinched at a hornet bumping the flyscreen. She wasn't hunting anyone. She was running. He knew the posture. He'd worn it himself once, walking out of his village with a surveyor's satchel and his grandfather's silence packed around him like wet clay.

She turned, scanning the store again, and this time her gaze snagged on him — on the drafting tools spread across the corner table, the half-finished chart, the government stencil on his equipment case. Her eyes did the arithmetic. Government man. Maybe help. Maybe worse.

"I heard you," she said, crossing toward him. Up close she smelled of diesel and river and something scorched underneath, smoke worked deep into her hair. "You're from upriver?"

"Depends who's asking."

"Paloma Thackeray." She didn't offer a hand; her hands were busy holding herself together. "I need passage. And fuel. And someone who knows the water above Korogu, because I don't, and the monsoon's coming early."

"Everyone says the monsoon's coming early. Every year."

"This year it's true." Something flickered behind her eyes — grief still wet, grief only hours or days old. "Please. I can pay. Not much, but some, and I can dive. If your boat needs work below the waterline, I'm worth my keep."

Elias looked at her for a long moment. Then he looked at the doorway, where the light had gone the color of pewter, and asked the question he already dreaded: "Why the Waris? Why upriver?"

She hesitated. He watched her decide — watched the calculation run behind her eyes, the weighing of a stranger against whatever was chasing her. When she spoke, her voice dropped to almost nothing.

"Because I'm carrying a book that names them. Names your family. And because men have already burned one house over it, and killed one good man, and they'll burn more before they're done." Her jaw tightened. "I don't know what the name means. I only know it's in the ledger, next to a place marked with a symbol I've never seen, and that everyone who wants that ledger wants it badly enough to kill."

The store noise receded. The card players, the kerosene smell, the storekeeper's radio muttering weather warnings — all of it thinned to a hum. *A ledger.* Elias thought of his grandfather's hands, which he remembered as enormous and gentle, braiding fishing line, shelling cacao, once — only once — pressing a finger to young Elias's chest and saying words the boy hadn't understood until years after the funeral: *Some things we keep so they can't be taken. Remember that the keeping is heavy.*

He had thought it was about land. About the garden plots, the clan boundaries his grandfather guarded like scripture.

Now a stranger stood in front of him saying the name aloud like a key turning in a lock he'd forgotten existed.

"What kind of ledger?" he heard himself ask.

"A captain's. From the war. A dead trading nation's seal on the chest it came in." She pulled something from inside her jacket — wrapped in oilcloth, tied with cord — and let him see only the corner of a page: columns of figures, a hand-drawn coastline, and there, boxed in faded iron-gall ink, a word written in a careful colonial clerk's hand.

*Wari.*

His own surname looked different in that script. Older. Like a photograph of someone before you knew them.

"Who else has seen this?" Elias asked.

"The man who burned my house. A naval officer named Reyes, who asks questions he shouldn't know to ask. And now you." She wrapped the oilcloth closed again, and her hands were steady now, steadier than her voice. "I didn't come here to drag anyone into it. I came because the ledger points upriver, and upriver is where I'm going whether anyone guides me or not. I'd rather not drown finding it alone."

Outside, the first fat drops hit the tin roof. The storekeeper swore and hauled in the drying nets. The monsoon, early after all, arriving like an accusation.

Elias looked at his survey sheets. Three weeks of careful, meaningless lines. Dots and names and nothing else. He thought about how he'd left home — how his father had called him a man without roots, how he'd told himself a mapmaker needed distance to see clearly, how eleven years could pass while you told yourself the same lie in different fonts.

His grandfather had carried something heavy. Had died carrying it. And the whole time, the answer had been floating downriver in a drowned chest, waiting for a diver with burned hands to pull it into the light.

"I have a boat," Elias said. "A survey boat. Slow, ugly, and registered to the government, which is either the best thing or the worst thing about it depending on who's chasing you."

"Which is it?"

"I don't know yet." He began rolling his charts, sliding them into their brass tube. "That depends on what my grandfather did. And I'm coming with you to find out — not just guiding. Coming. Whatever's in that ledger, the Wari name is mine too, and I've spent eleven years pretending I didn't want to know why my family kept secrets heavier than other families' harvests."

Paloma studied him the way she'd probably studied wrecks — assessing hull integrity, deciding what could bear weight. "You understand they might come after you too. Reyes, the mercenaries. Whoever burned my house."

"They'd come eventually anyway," Elias said. "Men like that don't leave loose threads. Better to be a thread that's moving." He capped the tube. "My boat's at the government jetty. We leave before the rain gets serious, and we run dark past the patrol stations."

"You've done this before."

"No," he said, and found, to his surprise, that he was almost smiling. "But I've mapped every place a man can hide along this river, and every place a boat can sink. Between us, that should be enough."

* * *

The *Kapul* sat low at the jetty, a flat-nosed workboat with a canvas canopy gone green with mildew and a hull that Elias knew down to the last patched rivet. Paloma walked it end to end before she'd step aboard — checked the bilge, ran a thumb along the welds, opened the engine cover and pronounced the fuel line "a disaster waiting for a schedule." He caught himself bristling, then caught something else underneath: the small relief of a person who inspected things because inspecting was how she loved the world. Tio, she'd said on the walk down. The mentor. The man who died covering her escape. She said the name the way you set down something fragile, once, and then didn't pick it up again.

They loaded in silence broken by rain. By the time the last jerry can was lashed down, the monsoon had arrived in earnest, hammering the canopy, turning the river the color of strong tea. Elias cast off and took them out into it, and Angoram dissolved behind them into grey smear and drumming water.

Paloma sat in the bow with the oilcloth bundle on her knees, watching the banks slide past. After an hour she spoke without turning around.

"Why does a government mapmaker know how to run dark past patrol stations?"

"Because I grew up running rivers at night," Elias said, easing the throttle around a half-submerged log. "And because the government doesn't always like what I map. There are places they've asked me to leave blank. Villages that got moved for logging concessions. Creeks that are poisoned but still on the drinking maps. I draw them anyway, in my own copies. Someone should remember."

"And that's worth losing your job over?"

"It's worth being honest over." He glanced at her back. "You dive wrecks. You spend your life going down into what people sank and forgot. Is that so different?"

She was quiet long enough that he thought she wouldn't answer. Then: "I go down looking for things worth saving. Most of it's scrap. Sometimes it isn't." Her hand rested on the oilcloth bundle. "This time it isn't. Or it's the most dangerous thing I've ever pulled up, and I can't tell which yet."

"That's usually the same thing," Elias said.

The rain eased near dusk to a fine grey veiling. They tied up in a side channel behind a screen of pandanus, cold food, no fire, the mosquito coil smoking between them like incense. Paloma slept in snatches, one hand always near the bundle, and Elias sat up with his private map unfolded on his knees, adding tonight's channel by pencil-light: the sandbar shifting since the last survey, the new snag, the flooded gutter that would be a shortcut in high water and a death trap in low.

He found himself drawing a second mark, small, in the margin beside the channel. A symbol he hadn't drawn since he was a boy, sitting beside his grandfather while the old man traced it in the dirt with a stick — a spiral, opening outward, that meant *the water remembers where it was sent.*

He'd asked what it meant, back then. His grandfather had said, *One day a stranger will bring the question back to you, and then I won't be here to answer, and you'll have to answer for me.*

Elias had laughed. Eleven years old, laughing at an old man's ghosts.

Now he touched the spiral in the margin and looked across the dying fire-smell at the sleeping woman who had carried the question two hundred kilometers through fire and flood to lay it at his feet.

In the morning they would run for Korogu. Above Korogu, the ledger pointed. Above Korogu, the village that waited — his village, whose stilt-houses he could draw from memory but hadn't stood beneath in eleven years.

The river slid black and patient past the anchored boat, carrying its cargo of rain toward the sea, and somewhere ahead of them, upstream against the current, waited the weight his grandfather had spent a lifetime calling heavy.

Elias folded the map along its old creases and finally slept.
