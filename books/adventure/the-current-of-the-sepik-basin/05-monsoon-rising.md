## 5. Monsoon Rising

The rains broke three days early, and they broke like something angry.

Paloma felt it first in her teeth, an ache that had nothing to do with the cold river water and everything to do with the pressure dropping over the basin like a lid coming down. The sky over Angoram had gone the color of old pewter, and the air hung so thick with moisture that breathing felt like drinking. Elias had stood at the rail of the dive boat that morning and said, quietly, "It's coming. My grandmother could smell it a day out. I can only smell it half a day."

"Then we have half a day," Paloma said, and threw him the fuel line to coil.

They had bought diesel in Angoram — two drums of it, lashed under the tarpaulin amidships, the sum of everything Paloma had left in the world after Tio's funeral that wasn't the ledger. She kept the ledger wrapped in oilcloth inside her dive bag now, slept with it under her head like a pillow stuffed with dynamite. Every time the engine coughed she thought of Reyes's patrol boat, the grey hull she'd seen once in the distance below Angoram, moving upriver against the current like a shark that had learned to climb.

The first rain hit an hour after noon. Not a shower — a wall. It came down the river valley toward them as a visible thing, a grey curtain dragging across the water, and then it was on them and the world dissolved into hammering white noise. Elias killed the engine and they drifted, shouting to hear each other, while the Sepik rose around them in visible inches, brown water climbing the hull, carrying whole trees past them end over end like matchsticks.

"We can't run the channels in this," Elias shouted. "The markers are gone. The sandbars have moved. We'd end up on a bank or in a snag, and either way we're done."

"So we anchor?"

"So we anchor *well*." He was already moving, feeding the anchor line out, testing the bottom with the lead weight, and Paloma watched him work and understood that this was the part of the river he knew and she didn't. She could read water from below — current seams, silt plumes, the way a wreck's shadow lay on the bottom. He could read it from above, from the color of the surface and the shape of the foam and the smell of the air, and right now all of it was telling him things she couldn't hear.

They anchored in the lee of a flooded copse, the boat's bow into the current, and the night came down like a held breath.

* * *

The fuel drums went at dawn, in the way that disasters go: stupidly, quietly, almost politely.

The flood had lifted them in the night — Paloma woke to the boat swinging loose on its line, the copse gone, the whole landscape remade into brown sea dotted with the crowns of drowned trees. Elias was already up, hauling on the anchor line, and it was he who saw it first: one of the drums, bobbing on its side fifty meters downstream, riding a current seam like it had a destination in mind. The lashings had chafed through in the night. The second drum was gone entirely.

They chased the first one for an hour, Elias poling them through submerged fence lines and the roofs of garden huts, and when they finally gaffed the drum alongside it was a third empty — water had gotten in through a bad bung and the diesel had bled out into the river, a rainbow slick spreading behind them like a bruise. Enough left to idle. Not enough to run.

Paloma sat down hard on the gunwale and laughed, because the alternative was crying, and she'd done enough of that for one lifetime.

"Reyes doesn't need fuel to find us," she said. "He has plenty. We're the ones who just became a floating target with nowhere to go."

"Then we go nowhere fast," Elias said. He was squinting at the treeline, at a shape Paloma had taken for a hill. "There. Do you see it? The bell tower."

She saw it then: a church roof breaking the floodwater, and above it a concrete bell tower leaning slightly, patient, like a man waiting out a storm under an umbrella. "That's the old mission," Elias said. "Kambaramba side. It flooded in '98 and they never rebuilt. The river's been eating it ever since." He paused. "The nave floods every monsoon. The sanctuary's on higher ground. We can shelter there, wait out the worst, and figure out fuel after."

"And if Reyes finds us there?"

Elias looked at her, and there was something in his face she was beginning to recognize — the cartographer's calm, the man who had learned to stand very still while everything around him moved. "Then we'll hear him coming," he said. "On this river, in this rain, nobody sneaks up on anybody. That's worth more than diesel."

* * *

The church was half-drowned and wholly beautiful, in the way that ruins are beautiful when the river gets hold of them.

They brought the boat in through what had been the front doors and moored it to a roof beam that still stood above the waterline. Inside, the nave was a flooded hall, pews floating in loose rafts, a plaster Christ on the far wall with the water lapping at his knees. Mud had climbed the walls in tide-marks, season after season, a ledger of floods written in brown lines. Paloma counted nine of them. The river had been writing its own book here for twenty years and nobody had come to read it.

The sanctuary sat on a raised platform at the rear, dry, its timber floor still sound. Someone had left things, over the years — a rusted kerosene lamp, a coil of fishing net, a stack of hymnals swollen into bricks. Elias found a dry corner for the ledger and Paloma found herself standing in the flooded nave, looking down through a meter of tea-colored water at the tiled floor, and the diver in her woke up the way it always did.

"There's stuff down there," she said. "You can see the tiles. Things drop in a flood and stay. Coins, tools, whatever the mission had when it went under." She was already pulling her fins from the dive bag. "We can't run, we can't hide forever, and we can't buy fuel without money. But I can work. I can always work."

"You want to dive in a flooded church."

"I want to dive *anywhere*. It's the only thing I'm good at." She said it lightly, but it came out with an edge she hadn't intended, and she saw him hear it — the whole history underneath it, Tio and the boats and the brother the river had taken, all of it compressed into one sentence.

Elias didn't flinch from it. He sat down on the sanctuary steps and started unlacing his boots. "Then teach me," he said. "I've mapped a hundred wrecks from the surface. Never been down to one."

* * *

She taught him in the drowned nave, in water so still it was like diving in a held breath.

She started with the basics — how to clear a mask, how to breathe so the regulator didn't chatter, how to sink and rise with the smallest possible movement. He was a careful student, methodical, the way he was with his maps. He wanted to know *why* before he did *how*, and at first it made her crazy, and then it made her laugh, and then she realized she was enjoying herself, which was a thing she had not expected to feel three days after her mentor had died covering her escape.

"Stop thinking," she told him, treading water over the submerged aisle. "The water doesn't care what you know about it. Down there you're just meat and bubbles. Be humble and be slow."

"Is that how you survived?"

"That's how I survived *that*." She pointed at nothing and everything. "Tio used to say the river forgives nothing but forgets nothing either. You work in between those two facts."

They dove together that afternoon and again the next morning, and he was clumsy and then less clumsy, and by the second day he could hold a position in the still water and work a trowel without silting the whole nave. They came up with mission buttons, a brass door handle, a clay pipe bowl, and — the day's treasure — a small tin strongbox, fused shut with rust, that Paloma promised to open with the same care she had *not* used on the chest that started all of this.

In exchange, he taught her the surface.

He'd sit at the bow in the grey light with his notebook and make her look at the water until she saw what he saw. The way the current wrote itself in lines on the surface, and how a line that frayed meant a snag below, and a line that bulged meant a bar. How to read the rain — the difference between a shower that would pass and a system that had settled in for days. How the birds told you where the channels ran, because they followed the fish and the fish followed the clean water. How the river's moods had a grammar, and the flood was not chaos but a sentence, long and violent and grammatical, and you could live inside it if you learned to parse it.

"Your grandfather taught you this?" she asked him once, and he was quiet a long moment, his pencil still.

"The river taught me," he said finally. "He taught me to stay away from it. That's a kind of teaching too, I suppose." He didn't say more, and she didn't push, because she knew about the things families bury, and she was carrying one of them wrapped in oilcloth in her dive bag.

* * *

On the third night the rain slackened and the moon came through the broken roof in pieces, silver coins scattered across the black water of the nave. They sat on the sanctuary steps sharing a tin of sardines and the last of the rice, and Paloma realized she was warm — not from a fire, there was no fire, but from something else. The strange, unearned warmth of not being alone.

"Tio had a saying," she said. "He said you can't pick your family but you can pick your crew, and the second one matters more."

"Was I crew?"

"You're getting there." She bumped her shoulder against his. "You're a slow hire."

He smiled, and in the broken moonlight he looked younger, and tired, and unafraid, and she thought: this is what I ran upriver to find, and I didn't know it was a person.

Then he went still. His head came up, the way a dog's does, and Paloma followed his eyes to the open front doors, to the flooded nave, to the night beyond.

She heard it a moment later. An engine. Not the loose stutter of a dugout's longtail — a deep, disciplined thrum, big and steady, the sound of a boat built by a government. It was still distant, somewhere down the drowned channel, but it was not going away, and it was not passing by.

Elias closed his notebook without a sound. "Patrol boat," he whispered. "Coming up the channel."

Paloma's hand found the dive bag, and the ledger inside it, hard as a heart. The moon went behind the clouds, and the church went dark around them, and the engine sound grew — patient, inevitable, like the flood itself — as it came for them through the drowned world.
