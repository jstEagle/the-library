## 6. The Village That Waited

The river changed its voice two days before they arrived.

Elias felt it in the way the current slowed and widened, spreading itself thin across the floodplain like a man lying down after a long walk. The water went from brown to the color of weak tea, threaded with channels that braided and rejoined, and the banks softened into sedge and pandanus and the pale skeletons of trees drowned decades ago, still standing in three feet of water as if waiting for someone to explain. Somewhere behind them, he knew, Reyes's patrol boat was working its way up through the same maze, slower now, more cautious — but coming.

Paloma slept in the bow of the dive boat, the ledger wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, her hands curled around it even in sleep the way a child holds something taken from them once before. Elias steered with one hand on the tiller and let the other trail in the warm water, reading the temperature of the current against his wrist the way his grandfather had taught him before he'd ever taught him letters. Warm meant shallow. Cold meant deep channel, or spring-fed, or something old underneath. The Sepik had moods the way people did, and you learned them or you drowned.

He had not been back to Wari Point in eleven years.

He told himself it was only geography that made his chest tight as they rounded the last bend — the sudden green wall of the village rising from the water on stilts and pilings, houses stacked like wading birds, smoke standing straight up in the windless morning air. But geography was a lie he'd been telling himself since he was nineteen, when he'd taken the government posting in Angoram and told everyone, including himself, that a man who drew maps for a living needed to see more than one place.

The truth was simpler and uglier: he had left because staying meant being small, and being small meant being his grandfather's grandson forever, carrying a silence no one would name out loud but everyone could feel under the floorboards of every house in the village.

Now here was the silence, floating downriver toward him in an oilcloth bundle.

* * *

They came in past the fish traps, and the children saw them first — of course they did, children always did, running along the walkways shouting *boat, boat* in voices that carried across the whole settlement. By the time Elias tied off at the main landing, half the village had gathered on the platform above the water, and he stood in the boat looking up at faces he knew and faces that had grown new lines since he'd known them, and nobody spoke.

It was old Magdalene who broke it. She was ninety if she was a day, blind in one eye, and she leaned over the edge of the platform and looked down at him with the good eye and said, "Elias. You got thin."

"I got busy," he said.

"Same thing." She turned her head slightly. "And this is the woman from the river stories already? Two days old and she has stories."

"Magdalene," Elias said carefully, "how do the village have stories about Paloma already?"

The old woman smiled, and there was nothing warm in it. "Because Tambo's boy sold word of her to strangers at Timbunke for a radio battery and a case of beer. Word travels faster than boats when money's on it." She straightened with effort. "Come up. The elders are meeting under the big house. They have been meeting since yesterday, actually. Since Tambo's boy came back through and we understood what was coming upriver behind you."

* * *

The men's house was dim and smelled of smoke and old timber, and the elders sat along both walls in the light that fell through the gaps in the woven blinds, striping their bodies like the bars of a cage. Elias counted seven. He had expected five. Two had come in from villages further up, which meant word had moved faster than he had, which meant the ledger's contents were already loose in the world in some form, distorted, growing teeth.

His uncle Anis presided — not the oldest man present, but the one whose family line ran to the big house, and therefore the one whose voice carried weight. He looked at Elias for a long moment before speaking, and what passed across his face was not warmth and not anger but something wearier than either.

"You brought her," Anis said.

"I brought the ledger," Elias said. "She brought it. It's the same thing, maybe."

"It is not the same thing." Anis gestured, and Paloma unwrapped the oilcloth and set the ledger on the central planks between them. It looked smaller in here, among these men, than it ever had in the boat. A dead captain's book. Paper and rot and ink.

Old Magdalene had followed them in and sat apart from the men, as she always had, on her own stool near the door, and it was she who spoke first. "Open it," she said. "Read us the names. We have waited sixty years to hear whether our fathers' names were written down anywhere except in God's book, and God does not show His pages."

Elias opened the ledger to the page he had marked. His hands were steady — cartographer's hands, surveyor's hands, trained to be steady — but his voice was not, and he heard it crack on the third name.

*"Wari. Joseph Wari, headman's son. Keeper of the crossing."*

The room made a sound. Not words. Something lower than words, a sound like wind moving through a hollow tree, and Elias understood that every man on those benches had been holding the same breath for sixty years.

"He never told you," Paloma said quietly. It wasn't a question.

"He told me everything except the thing that mattered," Elias said. "He taught me the channels. He taught me which trees fell which way in a storm. He taught me to read water temperature off my wrist. And when I asked him why our family kept the crossing rights when we didn't even own a canoe anymore, he said—" Elias stopped. Swallowed. "He said, *because we owe the river a debt, and debts don't drown.* I thought it was just an old man's talk."

Anis nodded slowly. "Your grandfather helped hide it. That much the old ones knew, or guessed. In the war years, when the soldiers came through — first theirs, then ours, then theirs again — a foreign ship put into the back channels and unloaded crates. Gold, yes. But also things taken from villages all along the coast and the basin. Carvings. Masks. Drums that belonged to clans that no longer had anyone left to beat them. Records too — papers, photographs, lists. The kind of papers that prove what was done and by whom." He looked around at the other elders. "Joseph Wari and four others took it into the limestone country, into the caves behind the falls, and sealed it. Not to steal it. To keep it from being stolen twice."

"And then he was silent," Elias said.

"And then he was silent," Anis agreed. "Because silence was the price of keeping it. Every man who knew swore the oath of the closed mouth. Your grandfather kept it so well he kept it from his own blood. That is not shame, Elias. Or it is shame, but shame of the honorable kind — the kind that costs."

"The kind that costs," Magdalene repeated from her stool, dry as driftwood. "Tell that to his grandson, who grew up thinking his grandfather was a small man."

* * *

They argued until the light through the blinds went from gold to copper to nothing, and the lamps were lit, and still they argued.

Not about whether the cache should come home — that was settled almost before it was spoken, the way bedrock is settled. It belonged to the villages. It had always belonged to the villages. Even the youngest men in the room, the ones with phones and diesel engines and grievances against the government in Port Moresby, agreed on that without discussion.

What they argued about was what came next.

"We open it and we share it," said one elder, a crocodile-toothed man named Sereva. "Every clan takes back what is theirs. The records go to the archives where the whole country can see what was done. Light. That is what Joseph Wari would want — he hid it in darkness only because darkness was all there was."

"No," said another, younger, a schoolteacher named Bruno. "We open it and we decide alone, clan by clan, and the outside world gets nothing until we say. Every time outsiders learn where it is, outsiders come. Soldiers, traders, archaeologists, navy men. Sixty years it stayed safe because six men kept their mouths shut. Now the mouth is open and the whole river knows."

"And the whole river is coming," Magdalene said flatly. "That is the argument you are all dancing around. The woman's boat burned. Her teacher is dead. Navy men and hired guns are upstream and downstream both. Whatever we decide in this house tonight, by tomorrow morning it will not matter unless we decide faster than they travel."

Through all of it, Paloma sat against the wall with her arms around her knees, saying almost nothing, and Elias watched the villagers watch her — not with suspicion exactly, but with the careful attention people give weather. She was the storm front. She was the reason the strangers were coming. And she had lost everything to bring the ledger here, and everyone in the room knew it, and knowing it did not make her one of them.

Except that slowly, as the night wore on, it began to. When the talk turned to the flooded channels and the approach routes the mercenaries would use, it was Paloma who said, "They'll come up the main channel, fast and loud, because they think loud is safe." And when it turned to the grotto and the caves, it was Paloma who asked the questions a diver asks — how deep, how cold, what the rock does in flood — and the elders answered her the way you answer family, without guarding themselves.

Near midnight, Magdalene rose from her stool, crossed the room, and put her hand on Paloma's head, briefly, the way you'd touch a child. "You eat," she said. "You sleep in my house. Tomorrow we fight about the rest."

* * *

Elias did not sleep. He sat on the landing platform above the black water while the village breathed around him, and he thought about his grandfather's hands teaching his small hands the feel of a current, and understood at last that every lesson had been a message in a cipher he hadn't had the key to read until tonight. *We owe the river a debt.* Not a debt of guilt. A debt of guardianship. Passed down like land, like a name, like a silence.

Footsteps on the planks. Paloma, wrapped in a borrowed blanket, lowering herself to sit beside him. For a while neither spoke. Downriver, far off, thunder muttered — or something that might have been thunder.

"My cousin," Elias said finally. "Tambo's boy. What's his name now?"

"Manu," Paloma said. "I heard the name in the meeting."

"When Manu sold word of you at Timbunke, did he know what he was selling?"

"He knew it was worth selling." She pulled the blanket tighter. "That's usually enough."

Elias nodded. Below them, the river slid past in the dark, patient, keeping count. "Then he'll sell again. And the next time, he won't sell a description of a woman in a boat. He'll sell the location of the caves."

Paloma was quiet a moment. "How do you know?"

"Because I know him. Because I was almost him." He said it plainly; it cost less than he expected. "Eleven years ago I chose the map office over this place because I wanted a life with doors in it. Manu chose the same thing, just worse. People like us don't stop at one sale."

The thunder came again, closer, and this time there was no mistaking it — not thunder at all but the low, rhythmic thrum of an outboard engine, somewhere down in the braided channels, running dark and slow, feeling its way toward them through the flooded country.

Paloma stood. So did Elias. Neither reached for a lamp.

"Dawn," she said. "They'll hit us at dawn, where the channels meet. It's what I'd do."

Below the platform, the river went on sliding past in the dark, carrying their voices away toward the sea, carrying everything away, keeping everything.
