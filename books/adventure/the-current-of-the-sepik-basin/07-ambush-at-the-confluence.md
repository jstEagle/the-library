## 7. Ambush at the Confluence

Paloma woke to the sound of the river changing its mind.

She had learned its moods the way other people learned languages — the low mutter of a falling barometer, the high thin whistle of wind coming down from the ranges, the way the current went glassy and silent before it did something terrible. But this was new. This was the sound of two rivers arguing.

She sat up in the hull of her boat, where the elders had let her moor at the village landing, and looked east. The sky was the color of a healing bruise, and the mist lay so thick on the water that the far bank had been erased, as if the whole world had been reduced to this: the river, the canoes, the smell of woodsmoke and wet sago. The Sepik came down from the west, heavy with mountain silt, and somewhere ahead of them the Karawari came up from the south to meet it, and at the confluence the two currents braided and fought and made a standing wave that the villagers called the Old Man's Teeth, because it ground anything careless down to splinters.

Elias was already awake. He sat on the gunwale with the ledger open on his knees, wrapped in plastic, tracing the cipher with one finger as if he could wear a groove in it. He hadn't slept. She could tell by the way he held his shoulders, like a man carrying water in both hands who has forgotten how to set it down.

"They'll come at first light," he said, without looking up. "My cousin sold them the crossing. Reyes will want to take the channel before the flood comes down. After the flood, the ledger's trail drowns — theirs too."

"Then we move now."

"Where?" He finally looked at her, and in the grey light his face was all planes and exhaustion. "Every canoe in this village is worth more than my life to these people. If we run, they follow us and burn what's behind us. Tio taught you that, didn't he? The river forgives nothing. Neither do men with guns."

She didn't answer. Tio's name sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. Three nights now she had dreamed of the stilt-house burning, of the way the fire had turned the water beneath it orange, of Tio's silhouette in the doorway — her mentor, her only family left above water — turning back for the rifle he never got to fire. The river had a debt against her name now. She felt it the way a diver feels current: not seen, but pulling.

The first canoe came out of the mist at a quarter past dawn.

* * *

There was no horn, no shout. That was the worst of it. The mercenary boat came up the Karawari with its engine cut, drifting on the current the way a crocodile drifts, and the only warning was a dog starting to scream at the far end of the village.

Then the mist tore open and there were three of them — a long aluminium hull, camouflaged netting, a mounted gun that no one in this basin was licensed to own — and the morning went to pieces.

Paloma was moving before she had finished deciding to move. She grabbed Elias by the collar and pulled him flat into the bilge as the first burst went over them and took the splinters off the landing posts. Villagers scattered into the bush with the practised speed of people who remembered what soldiers looked like from the war their grandfathers had survived. A woman scooped a child out of the water like a fish and was gone. Old men she had eaten with two nights ago were shouting in a language she didn't speak, and the meaning was unmistakable in any tongue: *run, run, the river is the only door.*

But the river was full.

The mercenaries' boat swung wide, herding, and Paloma saw the shape of it with a diver's clarity, the way she saw a wreck from above before committing to the descent: they weren't attacking the village. They were penning it. Pinning the canoes against the landing, pinning her boat — *her* boat, the last thing she owned, the leaking dive boat that had carried her out of the fire and down a hundred kilometres of brown water — against the bank where the channel narrowed at the Old Man's Teeth.

"They want the landing blocked," Elias shouted over the gunfire. "They want to come ashore and search house by house —"

"They want the ledger."

"They'll burn the village to find it."

She looked at her boat. The *Mariana* — she had never told anyone she'd named it for her brother — sat low in the water, engine cold, fuel cans lashed under the tarp. Her escape. Her whole plan, the one she'd been building in her head since Angoram: run upriver past the last patrol post, cross into the tributaries, vanish into the basin the way Tio had taught her the basin could make a person vanish. Sell the ledger somewhere far away. Buy a life with no river in it at all.

The mounted gun swung toward the cluster of canoes where the elders' grandsons were still paddling, stupid with courage, trying to draw fire away from the women.

Paloma stopped thinking like a woman planning an escape and started thinking like a salvage diver.

Every wreck she'd ever worked, she'd learned the same first lesson: the hull is a weapon. A boat scuttled in a channel is a reef. A reef is a wall. And the Old Man's Teeth needed only a little help to close its jaws.

"Elias." She was already at the tarp, dragging the dive knife from her kit. "The fuel. Get the fuel off. Everything that floats. Now."

He understood in one heartbeat — she saw it land in his face, saw the grief arrive with the understanding — and then they were both moving, throwing fuel cans into the shallows, shoving the ledger and the cipher and Tio's knife and nothing else into a waterproof bag, and Paloma dropped into the bilge of her own boat with the knife in her teeth and found the seams she knew by heart, the old corrosion scars below the waterline, the places where thirty years of river had already been eating.

"I'm sorry," she said to the boat, to the brother's name on her tongue, to the version of herself that had believed freedom was a direction you could motor toward.

She opened her veins of rust and rot with four strokes of the knife, and the river came in the way it always came in — patient, certain, without anger.

* * *

The *Mariana* went down at the mouth of the narrow channel like a tired animal lying down.

Paloma and Elias hit the water on the far side and swam for the reeds while the boat settled stern-first, and the mercenaries' pilot saw too late what she'd done. The channel at the Old Man's Teeth was barely a boat's width of navigable water in the dry season, and now it was a hull's width less, and the current of the rising Sepik did the rest — swung their bow sideways, drove them onto her drowned deck, and held them there grinding while the standing wave stood up white and angry around them.

The gunfire stopped. The mounted gunner was suddenly very busy with balance. The villagers' canoes slipped away into the mist like fish into weed, carrying old men and children and the whole unbroken memory of the village up the side channels where no aluminium hull could follow.

Paloma floated in the reeds with her mouth and nose above water and watched her boat become a wall, and felt something in her chest break and set at the same time, the way a bone does.

She'd spent six years keeping one hull between herself and the world. Now the hull was a reef, and she was just a woman in the water, and the village behind her was still standing, and it was — she searched for the word and found it with surprise — *worth it.*

Then a hand closed on her collar and hauled her backward out of the reeds.

* * *

Commander Reyes was not what the fire had promised.

She had built him, in three nights of running, into something large and burning. What she got was a lean, wet, tired man in a navy polo shirt gone soft at the collar, standing on the grinding bow of his stranded boat with a pistol held low and steady, water streaming off her hair onto the deck between them. His men had Elias too, kneeling on the foredeck, a mercenary's boot between his shoulders, and Elias was looking at Paloma with an expression that said, clearly, *don't.*

"Thackeray." Reyes said her name like a file number. "You cost me a channel."

"You cost a man his life in Angoram. We're even."

"I don't know anything about that." And the terrible thing, she thought, was that he probably didn't — that men like Tio died as weather to people like Reyes, as background, as cost of doing business. He crouched to her level, careful on the tilting deck, and for a moment his voice dropped into something almost confidential. Almost human. "Let me explain your situation to you, because I don't think you understand it yet. That ledger is a list of names. Families who sheltered stolen property during the war. Property that certain governments have spent seventy years pretending never left a warehouse that burned. You've read it. The cartographer's read it. Elders in that village can recite it. Do you understand what that means?"

"It means it's true."

"It means it's *dangerous*," he said, and for the first time she heard something under the bureaucratic calm — fear, old and institutional, the fear of a nation that had learned to bury things. "There is no version of this where the cache comes up into the light. There is only a version where it goes back down, deeper, and everyone who knows where it went goes with it. I'm offering you the version where you're not in the ground. Give me the ledger, give me the cipher, and you and the mapmaker walk away. I'll even leave your village its canoes."

Paloma looked at him. She thought of Tio's tools hanging in the burning doorway. She thought of the elders' songs, which Elias had translated for her in the dark of the drowned church — songs that were, it turned out, maps, maps made of melody because paper burns and songs don't. She thought of her brother, who the river kept, and of how she had spent six years diving its wrecks looking for him in every hull, and how the ledger had finally given her something better to look for.

"You want to bury it again," she said. "Deeper."

"With everyone who's seen it. Yes."

"And you're telling me this because you think I'll trade my life for my freedom."

Reyes almost smiled. "Isn't that what everyone does?"

"Not here," Paloma said, and meant the whole basin, the whole brown patient country of it. "Here the river keeps what it's given. You should ask it sometime what it's holding for you."

Something moved in his face — doubt, or the memory of doubt — and that was when the flood arrived.

It came the way Elias had promised it would, the monsoon's first true pulse coming down from the highlands, and she saw it before she heard it: a brown wall of water standing up in the western channel, rolling the mist ahead of it, and Reyes turned to look at it and his pistol-hand dropped an inch, and Paloma threw herself backward off the boat into the current as the wall hit the stranded mercenary hull and the Old Man's Teeth closed on all of them at once.

The river took her the way it had always taken her — roughly, completely, like family.

She came up thirty metres downstream in the drowned reeds, coughing, and Elias surfaced beside her with the waterproof bag still strapped to his chest, and behind them the confluence was chaos — hulls grinding, men screaming, the mounted gun swallowed whole. Reyes's voice cut through it, barking orders, still alive, still commanding, already turning his stranded boat toward the only place left to go.

Upriver. Toward the waterfall. Toward the grotto.

Elias spat out river water and looked at her, wide-eyed. "He knows where it is. The cousin told them everything."

Paloma looked back at the flood, at the two rivers grinding together, and felt the last of the old Paloma — the one who ran, the one who sold, the one who kept one hull between herself and the world — go down with her boat.

"Then we get there first," she said. "The elders' songs. There's a back way in. And Elias —" she pulled the bag strap tight across his chest, "— this time, the river's on our side."
