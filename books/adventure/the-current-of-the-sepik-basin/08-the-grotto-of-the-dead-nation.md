## 8. The Grotto of the Dead Nation

The elders' song had seven verses, and the seventh was a map.

Elias had heard it as a boy, sitting cross-legged on the cool clay floor of the men's house while old Kambui's voice cracked through the smoke like a stick breaking over a knee. He had thought it was only a song — the kind of thing sung to make children sit still, to keep the dark from feeling empty. Now, with the ledger open across his knees in the bow of the dugout, he understood that every place-name in the cipher matched a line of that seventh verse, and the verse had been waiting sixty years for someone to read it aloud in order.

*Where the water wears white teeth against black stone,* the elder had sung, tapping the ledger's final page where Voss had drawn a waterfall in careful colonial ink — *there the dead nation keeps its house.*

*Behind the curtain no hand can part.*

*But the river remembers the door it closed, and opens it for those who ask in the old tongue.*

They had asked. That morning, at the base of the falls, Paloma had waded into the churning shallows while Elias stood on the bank and spoke the words his grandfather had taught him before he taught him his own name — words Elias had carried out of the village like stolen goods, half-ashamed of them, never guessing they were keys. The water did not part. But where the falls met the cliff face, the roar changed pitch, dropped by a half-tone, and beneath the white noise they both heard it: a hollow sound, vast and patient, like breath drawn behind stone.

Paloma surfaced grinning, river water streaming off her jaw. "There's a gap under the western lip. Two meters down, maybe three. You dive or I do?"

"I dive," Elias said, surprising himself. "It's my family's door."

* * *

The passage was longer than the song suggested and colder than either of them expected — a throat of black rock that swallowed their torchlight whole. Elias swam with one hand trailing the wall, counting strokes because counting was the only prayer he trusted, and when the ceiling finally lifted and his head broke water, the sound he made was not a word at all.

Paloma came up beside him, coughing, and her torch found the far shore of an underground lagoon, and the lagoon found the grotto.

It was a cathedral. That was the only word his government-trained mind would give him, though no cathedral he had ever surveyed deserved the comparison. The cavern rose forty meters into darkness ribbed with flowstone, and the torchlight moved across it like dawn moving across a valley — slowly, revealing things that should not exist so far from any sea. Crates. Hundreds of them, stacked in terraces along the natural shelves of the rock, their stenciled markings still legible after six decades: the crossed anchor and sunburst of a nation that had ceased to exist before Elias was born, its flag folded into history books, its crimes folded into nothing at all.

He waded ashore onto a shelf of pale sand that crunched underfoot — not sand, he realized, but the fine white dust of coral, carried here somehow, or brought, ballast in the holds of ships that had come up this river to take what was not theirs.

"God," Paloma breathed beside him. She said it the way divers say it, flat and reverent, a word for depths. Her torch swept left. "Elias. Look."

The first crate stood open, its lid propped like a mouth mid-sentence. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, lay masks — dozens of them, spirit faces carved from hardwood, their eyes bored deep, their surfaces still bearing traces of ochre and shell inlay. Elias knew the style. He had grown up two days' paddle from villages whose carvers made these faces, whose grandfathers had danced them at initiations. They were not treasures. They were *relatives*. And here they sat in a dead empire's crate, catalogued in a dead language, numbered like livestock.

Beside the masks, ingots. Gold, dull with tarnish, stamped with assay marks. A strongbox with its lock shot through. And beneath everything, filling a crate of its own, paper — bundles of it, tied with wire, the edges furred with damp-rot but the ink holding. Ledgers within the ledger. Shipping manifests. Requisitions signed in confident colonial hands. And lists — Elias knelt, untied one bundle with fingers that would not quite obey him, and read names. Villages. Dates. Numbers where people should have been. Quotas of labor levied on the Sepik basin, and columns recording how many had been delivered, and how many had died en route, and how many had been written off as loss.

His grandfather's village appeared on the third page. So did the number of men taken from it in 1943.

"So it wasn't just gold," Paloma said quietly. She had come to stand behind him; he felt her shadow fall across the paper. "They hid the receipts too. Proof. Someone in the navy wanted all of this buried where even historians couldn't find it."

"That's why Voss sealed it." Elias's voice sounded strange in the cavern, flattened by stone. "Not to protect the treasure. To protect the evidence. From everyone."

"And your grandfather helped put it here."

"He helped hide it *from the looters.*" Elias stood, and the name came out of him like a splinter working free. "And then he kept the secret so long it became silence, and the silence became shame, and the shame became our family's inheritance. My father wouldn't speak the old tongue because speaking it meant remembering. That's why I ran to the survey office. I thought maps were neutral. I thought drawing lines was safer than inheriting them."

Paloma touched the lid of the mask crate, light as a benediction. "Nothing about this river is neutral," she said. "I've known that since I was nineteen years old. I just pretended otherwise, because pretending paid better."

For a long moment neither of them spoke, and the grotto held its breath around them — the drip of water somewhere high above, the distant mutter of the falls through the rock, the small sounds of their own bodies adjusting to a place that had waited out three generations without company.

Then Paloma's head came up sharply, the way it always did — the diver's instinct, the animal ear. "Do you hear that?"

Elias listened. Under the falls' murmur, something else now. An engine note, thin and metallic, filtering through stone.

"It carries over water," he said. "Sound travels up the gorge—"

"No." She was already moving, dousing crates into shadow with the edge of her hand, killing their torches one by one until only a single flame remained cupped low between them. "That's a patrol boat engine. Twin screws, shallow draft. I'd know it anywhere." Her face in the guttering light was grim and very calm. "Reyes found us."

* * *

They could not have been followed through the falls; the underwater passage admitted one swimmer at a time and no boat at all. Which meant Reyes had not followed them. He had *known* — the same way he had known about the chest before Paloma ever opened it, the same way the mercenaries had known which stilt-house to burn. Somewhere upstream or downstream, someone had sold the seventh verse of an old man's song, and the price had been paid in the coin mercenaries understood.

"The cousin," Elias said. "Tambe. The elders said he took money—"

"Doesn't matter now." Paloma was at the lagoon's edge, testing the water temperature with two fingers, reading the current the way she read everything — professionally, hungrily. "What matters is the flood."

He didn't understand until she pointed her chin upward, toward the vault of the cavern, and he saw what awe had hidden from him. The flowstone walls wore tide-lines. Old ones, high ones — bands of mineral stain marking water levels from other monsoons, other years, some of them four meters above the present surface of the lagoon. This cave flooded. Every wet season, the river backed up through whatever channels fed it, and the grotto filled like a lung.

"The rains broke early," Elias said. "Three days ago."

"And the river's been rising ever since." Paloma turned to face him fully, and in the low light her eyes were the color of the water outside — green-brown, patient, merciless. "We have maybe hours. Maybe less. And Reyes has a boat, a radio, and enough men to carry out whatever he wants and drown whoever objects."

Through the rock, faintly, came the crunch of a hull grounding on gravel. Voices. Boots. The metallic ratcheting of something being assembled — a winch, or worse.

"They'll find the passage," Elias said. "The gap under the lip. It's not deep."

"It's not wide, either. One swimmer at a time, in the dark, against outflow current." Paloma almost smiled. "I've defended worse positions with less."

"This isn't a joke to me, Paloma."

"Who's joking?" She picked up the bundle of atrocity lists and pressed them into his hands, wrapping his fingers around the wire ties with both of hers. Her hands were cold from the water and steady as bedrock. "These go back to the village if we don't. The masks, the gold — the elders can decide all of that. But the paper is the weapon. Paper is the only thing Reyes fears more than drowning, because paper can't be sunk. You're the cartographer, Elias. You know terrain. Get the documents out the way we came in, get them high, get them dry. I'll hold the door."

"You can't hold a door against eight men with guns."

"I held a channel against a patrol boat once," she said, and there was the scar of Ambush at the Confluence in her voice, the scuttled boat, the weeks in the village healing, "and I'm a much angrier swimmer than I used to be."

Above them, muffled by forty meters of stone, a voice called out in clipped naval English, echoing strangely: *Lights! We saw lights! Sweep the water!*

A beam stabbed down through the underwater passage — refracted, broken, but unmistakable, a searchlight's arm reaching into the lagoon and stirring the water like a spoon in tea. The beam crawled across the ceiling, across the terraces of crates, and for one heartbeat it found them both, and Paloma snuffed the last torch with her bare hand.

Darkness total. Darkness ancient. In it, Elias heard his own heart, and the river rising, and boots splashing at the mouth of the world.

"Go," Paloma whispered, already sliding toward the passage, a knife appearing in her hand from somewhere he never saw. "Count your strokes. Don't stop for anything."

"Elias—" she caught his sleeve, one last time, and in the absolute dark her voice was closer than a whisper, closer than skin. "Whatever happens — tell the elders the Wari debt is paid. Tell them their grandfather kept faith. Tell them *I said so.*"

Then she was gone, into the black water, toward the light and the guns, and the first cold finger of the flood slid over Elias's ankles like a hand closing around the past —

— and pulled.
