## 9. The River Takes Its Due

The water came for the grotto the way grief comes — not all at once, but in a rising you could feel in your teeth.

Paloma stood ankle-deep on the shelf of black stone where the crates lay stacked in their rows of eighty years' patience, and she watched the waterfall at the cave's mouth thicken from silver to brown. The monsoon had found them. Somewhere above the basin, a whole sky's worth of rain was funneling down through limestone channels older than any nation, dead or living, and it was coming here, to this throat of rock, to swallow what it had guarded.

"Paloma." Elias's voice, tight. He stood among the crates with his hands full of paper — ledgers, manifests, photographs gone soft as cloth with damp — the evidence of what Voss's empire had done and then erased. "The water."

"I see it." She turned. Behind them, deeper in the grotto, the passage the elders' songs spoke of — the low crawl that climbed toward daylight and the forest path — was still dry. A ribbon of dark stone, maybe forty meters of it. Maybe less time than that.

Boots on stone behind her. She didn't need to look. She'd heard that particular tread before, at Angoram, at the confluence, in her dreams: Commander Reyes, walking like a man who believed the ground owed him its attention.

"You've made this difficult," he said. He had four of the mercenaries fanned out along the shelf, rifles slung now that the fight was over, because the fight *was* over — Paloma had surrendered the ledger an hour ago, and Reyes had spent that hour photographing pages by flashlight while his men pried at crate lids with dive knives. "But difficulties are my profession, Ms. Thackeray. The navy has been trying to forget this place since 1944. You're not going to be the one who makes it remember."

"The village already remembers," Paloma said. "That's the whole point of them."

Reyes smiled the way a patrol boat cuts a wake — smooth, and leaving damage behind it. "Villages are easy to forget too. I've done it before."

She thought of Tio then. She thought of him every hour now, but in that moment his face came up out of her like a diver surfacing without a line — his hands, his laugh like a bilge pump starting, the way he'd shoved her over the rail into the black water and stayed behind with men who meant to burn the world down around him. *Swim, girl. Don't look back until you're past the bend.* She'd looked back anyway. Everyone looks back. That was the river's oldest lesson: looking back is how you learn what you owe.

The water reached their knees.

* * *

It happened fast after that, the way catastrophes do — not as a wave but as a decision the river had already made, merely informing them of it.

The waterfall went from brown to roaring. The level jumped a handspan between one breath and the next, and one of the mercenaries — the big one, the one they called Brannigan, who'd held a machete to Elias's cousin's throat at the confluence — slipped on the algae-slick shelf and went down flailing into the flood pool, and came up coughing and furious and afraid, and Paloma saw fear move through all four of them like current through wire.

Reyes felt it too. She watched him feel it — watched the commander's certainty crack along some hairline seam, because here was the thing about men who commanded rivers from the decks of patrol boats: they had never once been inside one. They knew water as a surface. Paloma had spent her whole life knowing it as a country.

"We need to move," she said. Not to Reyes. To Elias. "Now. The crawl passage. Take the documents."

He hesitated — she saw him weigh leaving her the way you weigh anything precious against anything necessary — and she put both hands flat on his chest and pushed, once, hard. "Go. That's not a request. Your grandfather carried this silence for fifty years. You carry the end of it. *Go.*"

Elias went. She heard him splashing up the shelf, heard the low scrape of his shoulders finding the crawl, and then the dark took even the sound of him, and Paloma turned back to face Reyes alone in a drowning room full of stolen gold.

"Ms. Thackeray," Reyes said, wading toward her, water at his thighs now, his voice pitched to carry over the roar. "You can still be useful. There's a launch outside. Two seats. I'm prepared to be generous —"

"There's no launch," Paloma said. "I scuttled your tender at the falls. Twenty minutes ago. While your men were counting gold bars."

His face did something complicated. She would remember it, later, as the exact expression of a man watching his own map burn.

* * *

Here is what Paloma Thackeray knew, and what Commander Reyes did not:

A grotto is not a room. It is a lung. It breathes with the river — inhales when the flood backs up through the lower channels, exhales when pressure finds a way out. And the elders' songs, which Elias had sung to her three nights running in the drowned mission church, low and patient while the rain hammered the tin roof, were not songs at all. They were instructions. *Where the serpent drinks, the ceiling kneels. Where the serpent sleeps, the floor gives way.* Every verse mapped a failure point in the rock, and the old women who sang them had never needed to say why such knowledge should be kept. You keep flood-songs the way you keep fire-drills. Because someday the water asks.

The serpent's drinking place was a shelf of limestone directly above the main stack of crates, already weeping water in a dozen threads. The serpent's sleeping place was the floor of the eastern channel — the one Reyes's men had waded up, the one that fed the deep pool where the grotto's only other exit drowned itself each wet season.

Paloma had a dive knife, a coil of salvage line, and a lifetime of knowing exactly how much punishment stone will take before it stops being stone.

She cut the line free of her weight belt, looped it twice around the base of a stalagmite thick as a man's thigh — the one the songs called the serpent's tooth — and drove the knife hilt-deep into the weeping seam above the crates, levering with everything Tio had ever taught her about rusted things and reluctant things and things that wanted, more than anything, to come apart.

"What are you doing?" Reyes shouted. He was waist-deep now. His men were climbing onto the crate stacks like men on a sinking deck, because that was what they were.

"Giving the river back its due," Paloma said.

The seam let go with a sound like the world clearing its throat.

* * *

The ceiling came down in slabs the size of trucks, and the water behind it came down with it, and the difference between a grotto and a grave stopped mattering very much.

Paloma dove.

Not away — *through*. Down and left, along the channel the songs called safe, into the black water she had been born to, knife still in her fist, the collapse thundering somewhere above and behind her like a god slamming doors. Debris rained past her. Something struck her shoulder and spun her and she lost the light entirely and kept swimming anyway, because panic is just a story you tell yourself and she refused, today, to be its audience. Her hand found the guide-line she'd rigged at dawn — her own small treachery, strung through the lower channel while Reyes photographed his stolen empire — and she followed it hand over hand into the cold throat of the river.

Behind her, muffled by tons of falling world, she heard shouting. One voice, then another, then none. Then a long grinding roar, and then the sound every diver knows and dreads and answers to: silence, total, underwater, absolute.

She surfaced in the flooded forest two hundred meters downstream, gasping, bleeding from a cut above her ear, alive in the way that felt less like survival than like being issued a debt.

The crawl passage exit was upstream and above the floodline. She hauled herself along the root-laced bank toward it, calling Elias's name into the rain, and when she reached the mouth of the tunnel he was there — soaked, shaking, clutching the oilskin-wrapped documents to his chest like a child, alive, alive, *alive* — and she grabbed him and held on and neither of them said anything, because there are moments language was never built for.

Then, distantly, through the rain, they heard the mercenaries' boat engine catch and fade, fleeing downriver. Some of them had made it out. The river had taken its tithe and released the rest, and Paloma found she couldn't summon hatred for the survivors. Only arithmetic. Tio for Brannigan. The stilt-house for the tender. The grotto for the truth.

And Reyes?

They found no sign of him. Not on the bank, not in the wreckage-choked pool below the falls, not in the days that followed. The elders said the river keeps what it is owed, and Commander Reyes had tried to command it, and there is no debt larger than that.

* * *

They came down out of the hills at dusk, Elias and Paloma and the documents wrapped in oilskin, and the village met them at the water's edge with torches — dozens of them, the whole settlement turned out, elders and children and the cousin who had sold them all, standing apart with his face wet and not from rain.

Old Magdalene Wari, Elias's grandmother's sister, ninety years of river in her voice, took the oilskin bundle in both hands and weighed it, and looked at Paloma for a long moment.

"The grotto?" she asked.

"Gone," Paloma said. "Sealed. The river closed its own door."

Magdalene nodded slowly, and something in her face eased that might have been grief or might have been the end of a vigil eighty years long. "Good," she said. "Some things are carried better by water than by people." She pressed the bundle back against Elias's chest. "But the record travels. The village will decide what is shown, and to whom, and when. That is also the debt."

Paloma stood in the torchlight, bleeding, exhausted, owner of nothing in the world — no boat, no house, no mentor, no chest, no ledger — and felt something settle in her that she had not felt since before her brother died.

It took her a moment to name it. Then she did: she was expected. Tomorrow, and the day after, and the season after that. These people would notice if she was missing.

The rain kept falling. Upstream, under a hundred meters of new stone and brown water, a dead nation finished being forgotten, and a living one began deciding how to remember.

And in the morning, Magdalene said, there would be wood to cut, and a site to choose on high ground, and a stilt-house to raise — and if the diver with the salt-scoured hands wished to stay and help build it, well. The river, the old woman said, does not give back what it takes. But sometimes, if you watch long enough, it gives you something else instead.

Paloma slept that night under a roof that was not hers, among people who were not yet family, and dreamed of Tio's tools hanging on the wall, drying, waiting for hands.
