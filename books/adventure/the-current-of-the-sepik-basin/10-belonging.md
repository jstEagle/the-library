## 10. Belonging

The river took eleven days to fall.

Paloma watched it happen the way she'd once watched for patrol boats — from the doorway of a borrowed house, hands wrapped around a mug of sweet tea gone cold, tracking each morning how much more of the bank emerged, brown and glistening, like something being slowly given back. The flood marks on the village posts climbed down and down until they reached the ordinary high-water line, the one the elders had been reading for longer than anyone alive could remember, and then the Sepik sighed and settled into its old bed as if it had never risen at all.

As if it had not swallowed three men and a commander's ambitions and half a hillside of monsoon-soaked jungle in a single furious week.

The crates came up out of the grotto's throat over four days, ferried by young men who dove with the confidence of people retrieving their own inheritance rather than salvaging another's treasure. Paloma helped rig the lines, checked the knots, taught two of the boys how to equalize their ears against the pressure — Tio's lessons, passed down his chain of hands to hers to theirs. She did not go into the cave again. She had seen enough of what the dead nation left behind: the masks packed in straw, the gold that had been pried off temple doors, the ledgers within ledgers, columns of names written by clerks who had counted human beings the way her brother used to count fish. She had carried one ledger out of the water already. It was enough.

The elders opened the first crate on the fifth day, in the spirit house, with songs that Elias's grandfather must have heard as a boy and carried in silence for sixty years. Paloma stood at the edge of the gathering, an outsider among insiders, and watched an old woman named Magdalene touch a carved figure with both hands the way you'd touch the face of someone returned from the dead.

"This one," the woman said, in Tok Pisin, and then translated for Paloma's benefit though she clearly didn't need to, "this one my grandmother described to me before I could walk. She said it would come home when the river was ready."

*When the river is ready.* Paloma turned the phrase over all evening. Not *when it was safe*. Not *when someone strong enough came*. The river's own timetable, its own judgment. She had spent her whole adult life believing the Sepik was indifferent — a current, a hazard, a thing to be worked around. Now she understood the elders had always known better. The river wasn't indifferent. It was patient. And patience, she was learning, looked a great deal like grief from the outside.

* * *

The navy's men were gone by the second week.

There was no announcement, no apology. A patrol boat came upriver flying different colors than Reyes's, and a lieutenant with clean boots and careful eyes sat in the mission house drinking tea while Elias laid out copies of the grotto records page by page. Paloma watched through the louvers. The lieutenant's face went through a sequence she recognized from years of watching buyers appraise salvage: interest, calculation, and finally the particular stillness of a man realizing the item in front of him is worth more than he's authorized to pay for.

They took the copies. They took statements. They promised, in the flat language of institutions, that there would be *a full inquiry*, and everyone in the room understood that *full inquiry* meant the quiet shredding of whatever Commander Reyes had built his cover from, and the quiet reassignment of whoever had signed off on it, and no names in any newspaper that mattered. The empire that had drowned in the grotto had been dead eighty years; the newer empire covering for it died more quietly still, in filing cabinets, in Canberra or Port Moresby, wherever such things were decided.

But the lieutenant paused at the door on his way out and looked at Paloma directly, which Reyes had never once bothered to do.

"The passage offer stands," he said. "Any port. Anywhere. The government considers you owed."

She thought about it honestly, which surprised her. She'd expected to refuse on reflex. But she let herself feel the shape of it — a ticket south, dry land, a coast where nobody knew her name or her brother's, where the water was clear and warm and held nothing but tourists and coral. A life with no ledger in it.

"No," she said. "Thank you. No."

He nodded as if he'd expected it, and maybe he had. People who came up the Sepik either left changed or didn't leave at all.

* * *

They raised the stilt-house in the third week, on the point downstream of the village where the channel ran deep and the morning light came off the water like hammered tin.

It was Tio's ground — he'd kept a shack there for thirty years, and the fire that took him had taken the shack too, leaving only blackened posts and the smell of char that rain hadn't yet washed from the mud. The villagers claimed the spot belonged to no one and everyone, but when Paloma stood looking at it too long on the first morning, old Magdalene had appeared beside her and said simply, "Build here. He would want his tools near the water." And that settled it, the way things got settled in this country: not by deed or argument but by an elder's word, which was older than deeds.

So they built. Elias drew the plans — of course he drew the plans, precise and beautiful, every joint and brace rendered in ink so fine the house seemed to grow out of the paper — and the young men cut sago-palm battens, and the women wove the walls, and Paloma worked alongside them with her hands blistered and her chest full of something she didn't have a name for until Magdalene gave her one.

"You build like a mourner," the old woman said, watching her set a post. "Good. A house raised by a mourner holds its people tight."

Tio's tools had survived, barely — she'd found the lockbox floating wedged in the roots of a fallen tree six kilometers downstream, dented and scorched but sealed, and inside, wrapped in oilcloth the way he'd always wrapped them, his dive knives, his brass depth gauge with the cracked face, his father's spanner. She hung them now on the new wall where the morning sun would reach them, drying, gleaming, present. When she cooked in the evenings she could see them from the hearth, and it was almost — not quite, but almost — like having him standing watch.

On the night they raised the ridgepole, the whole village came. There was pork and pitpit and a drum that went until the moon was high, and children fell asleep in laps and were carried home, and Paloma sat between Elias and Magdalene and realized, somewhere around the third song, that no one was performing hospitality for her anymore. They had simply stopped treating her as a guest. She couldn't name the moment it happened. Rivers don't announce their crossings; you look up and you're on the other side.

Elias leaned close during a lull. "Magdalene says the house needs a name."

"Does it?"

"Everything here has a name. The channels, the shoals, the crocodiles that matter. Houses especially." He was smiling, that rare unguarded smile she'd coaxed out of him somewhere between the drowned church and the confluence, and it still undid her a little every time. "What should we call it?"

Paloma looked at the tools on the wall, silvered by firelight. At the ledger's empty place on the shelf, where the copy Elias had made for the village now lived in a bark folder, waiting for the schoolhouse to be built that would hold it. At the dark line of the river beyond, moving and patient and keeping everything it had ever been given.

"*Haus bilong Tio,*" she said. "And after that — we'll see. Let the river name the rest."

* * *

Winter, such as the basin knew winter, brought the dry months and the low water, and with them a strange new problem: peace.

Paloma had not been peaceful since she was nineteen years old. Grief had been her operating condition for so long that its absence felt like a missing limb — she kept reaching for the ache the way an amputee reaches for a hand no longer there. Some mornings she woke at dawn convinced something was wrong, lay listening to the water lap the posts, and then remembered: nothing was wrong. Nothing was wrong. Her boat was scuttled at the bottom of the confluence channel and she had not replaced it and did not need to, because the village boats went where she needed to go and the villagers asked her along as if she'd always been one of them.

She dove anyway, some days. Old habits. She swam the deep hole below the village where the catfish hung like gray thoughts, and once she dove the wreck of a mission barge that had gone down in '43, and found nothing in it but freshwater mussels and a child's rusted cup, which she left where it lay. Some things belonged to the river. She was learning the difference between salvage and theft, and it was the same difference, she suspected, between taking your freedom and taking your belonging. You could only carry so much out of the water. Choose wrong, and what you carried chose you back.

Elias worked at a table he'd built by the eastern window, drawing.

Not the government maps anymore — he'd sent his resignation south with the lieutenant's boat, folded inside a letter that took him three nights to write. These were his own maps. Maps of the basin as the elders sang it: the channels under the flood, the sacred holes, the paths the ancestors walked when the sea stood closer. He interviewed Magdalene for hours with his notebook open, cross-hatching a world no colonial surveyor had ever thought to record because it existed in songs instead of coordinates.

One evening in the dry season, Paloma brought him tea and saw, in the margin of the sheet he was working on — a map of the lower basin, meticulous, alive — a small notation beside the deep hole below the stilt-house. Not a depth sounding. Not a hazard mark.

A name. Hers. Written small and sure, in ink the color of the river at dusk, the way cartographers marked the places that mattered.

She set the mug down without a word and he pretended not to notice, and the pen scratched on, and outside the last light went gold and then copper and then the color of old brass, and the Sepik moved past them in the dark toward the sea, carrying everything it carried — the masks going home, the dead at rest, the burned house forgiven, the brother whose face she could finally hold in memory without drowning — patient, patient, keeping what it keeps.

And in the margin of a new map of an old world, in a hand that would outlast them both, the river's newest name waited quietly to be found:

*Paloma Thackeray, of the Haus bilong Tio.*

Here. Belonging. Kept.
