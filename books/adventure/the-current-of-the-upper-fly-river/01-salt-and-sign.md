## 1. Salt and Sign

The desert tells you everything, if you're willing to be lied to first.

That's the first thing I teach the rangers they send me, back when they still send them. The Redglass Flats will show you a footprint that isn't there and hide one that is. It'll grow a bush overnight where a man slept and shave a dune's crest by morning. The trick isn't reading the ground. The trick is knowing which lies the ground tells on purpose and which ones it tells because it can't help itself.

I'd been reading this particular set of lies for three days.

Wren Calloway, if that was even her name — and I'd have bet my commission it wasn't — moved like someone who'd read the same books I had. That was the insult of it. Most thieves I track are sprinters. They burn through water, break stride on the hardpan, leave a signature so loud it's practically a confession. This one walked at night, slept cold, and buried her tracks behind her with a branch dragged on a line. She'd crossed the salt pan at the flats' western edge in the two hours before dawn, when the crust holds and the wind comes off the escarpment to sweep it clean behind you.

She knew the window. She knew the wind. She knew that a tracker coming out of Marrow Bend would expect her to swing north toward the rail spur, because that's where the water was, and instead she'd gone south into country with no water at all, which meant she was carrying more than she should be able to carry, which meant she'd planned this before she ever stole whatever it was she'd stolen.

I crouched over a broken stem of saltbush and felt something I almost never feel out here, which was respect.

The bush had been snapped, not cut, and the break was eight hours old, maybe ten. She'd brushed past it in the dark without slowing, which told me she wasn't navigating by the stars or the terrain — she was navigating by memory. She'd walked this line before, or she'd memorized it from a map so good it might as well have been a memory. And there was a third option, the one I didn't like: that someone had drawn the line for her.

The wind came up the escarpment and moved the heat around without doing anything about it. I drank. Three swallows, no more, because the canteen arithmetic was the only honest math left in my life. Then I stood, and my knees announced their opinions, and I went on south after her.

* * *

You want to know what tracking actually is, out there on the Redglass? It's not sign. Sign is easy. Any fool with a week of training can read a footprint or count a dead campfire's rings. What tracking is, is time. You're not following a person. You're following the difference between what the land was and what it is, and that difference is always closing. Every hour the wind works at the edges of a print, the sun bakes the moisture out of turned earth, the crust heals. The land forgives everything eventually. My whole profession lives inside the gap before forgiveness.

By the fourth morning the gap was narrowing, and I knew it the way you know weather in your bad tooth.

She'd made a mistake. Small, forgivable, the kind of mistake that only shows up on the sixth or seventh day of hard travel, when the body starts negotiating with the mind and the mind starts losing. She'd cut through a stand of desert oak instead of going around it, saving herself maybe two hundred meters and costing her the cover of open ground. In the shade of the oaks the ground stays soft. It keeps what you give it.

I found her sleeping hollow at the base of the biggest tree, still holding the shape of her. A tall woman, narrow through the hips, heavier pack than her frame advertised. She'd dug a seep line at the hollow's edge — a little trench to catch the night condensation off the bark — which is a trick I know and maybe four other people alive know, and one of them is dead.

I sat down against the opposite trunk, in the shade, where the ground still held the cool of the night, and I ate a strip of dried fish, and I let her have her lead, because the sign said she'd broken camp four hours before dawn and I wasn't going to catch her tired. I was going to catch her careful. There's a difference, and the difference matters. Tired people run. Careful people talk.

While I chewed, I did the other thing I do out here, the thing nobody hires me for and everybody gets anyway. I looked at the hollow she'd slept in and thought about the person who'd made it. Alone, like me. Cold camp, like me. Seep line instead of a fire, like me, because fires are for people who expect to be found and we were both in the business of not being found. Somewhere behind us was Marrow Bend, with its wet-stone smell and its river traffic and its rooms where somebody knows your name, and somewhere ahead of her was whatever she'd stolen and wherever she was taking it, and somewhere behind me was a house I paid rent on and entered maybe forty nights a year, where the landlady watered a plant I'd never seen and left my mail in a tin on the step.

The best tracker alive. That's what they called me, when they called me at all. Nobody mentions what the job pays in anything but money.

I finished the fish, brushed the crumbs into the dirt for the ants, and stood up.

* * *

I caught her sign again by noon and lost it by two, and this time I understood why, and understanding it changed the shape of the whole chase.

She'd reached the edge of the Redglass proper — the country where the sand goes the color of old wine and the crust breaks into plates that ring underfoot like cheap crockery — and she'd done the thing I would have done. She'd walked up a dry wash for a kilometer, then out of it on stone, then back down a different wash, and then she'd stopped moving entirely and let the afternoon heat do her hiding for her. Heat shimmer is the desert's oldest accomplice. You can have a woman standing four hundred meters out on the flats and the air will simply refuse to show her to you.

So I stopped too. Found shade in the lee of a stone outcrop that some ancient flood had left standing in the middle of nowhere like a monument to its own bad luck, and I waited, because patience is the only weapon I've ever been issued that never rusts.

I thought about the job. That's a habit. When the waiting gets long, I go over the file in my head, and the file on this one was thin in a way that thin files never are by accident.

A surveyor's journal, stolen from the Amberdale Trust's Marrow Bend office. That was the whole of it, as Vane's clerk had given it to me over the phone: a journal, one item, no ransom note, no attempt to sell it, no pattern of similar thefts. The clerk had been very precise about what was *not* taken — the strongbox untouched, the correspondence files untouched, the maps drawer locked and still locked. One journal. Which meant the thief hadn't come for value. She'd come for that specific book, and she'd known it would be there, and she'd known which one it was among however many sat on those shelves.

And then there was the fee. The Trust had offered me double my rate before I'd quoted one. Double, for a journal. I'd taken the job anyway, because a fee that generous is its own kind of sign, and I read sign for a living. People overpay for two reasons: because they're desperate, or because they want your silence as much as your work. I figured I'd find out which somewhere up the trail, and I always do.

The sun went down the way it does out there, all at once, like someone snuffing a lamp. The shimmer died. And there she was.

Four hundred meters out, exactly where the washes met, a figure rose out of the wine-colored sand and stood a long moment looking back the way she'd come. Looking, I'd have sworn, directly at my outcrop. Then she turned and went on, south and a little east now, angling away from the rail spur, away from the river towns, toward country that got less inhabited the further you went, which was saying something, because the Redglass was already the definition of the word.

I gave her twenty minutes and then I followed, and I didn't try to be quiet about it anymore. The time for quiet was over. The time for conversation was coming, and it's kinder to let a careful person see you coming than to appear out of the dark like a judgment.

* * *

I caught up to her at moonrise, at the foot of a rise where the flats began their long climb toward the escarpment country, and I did it the way I always do it: by walking into her firelight and saying her name.

She didn't have a fire. I walked into her moonlight, then, which is colder but serves.

"Wren Calloway."

She turned with a knife already open in her hand, which I respected, and she held it low and forward instead of high and theatrical, which I respected more. She was taller than the hollow had suggested, or the hollow had lied, which happens. Young, maybe thirty, with the sun-cured look of someone who'd been outdoors so long her face had stopped negotiating with the weather. Her eyes went over me the way mine had gone over her trail — fast, professional, inventorying. Ranger's hat, ranger's coat, ranger's dust. She put it together and I watched her put it together, and I watched her decide not to run.

"Tracker," she said. Not a question.

"Quentin Underhill."

"I know." She said it without pride, the way you'd admit to knowing the name of a storm. "I've been leaving sign for you since the salt pan."

That stopped me. Three days of reading that trail like scripture, three days of grudging respect, and she tells me the scripture was written for me. "The buried prints."

"The buried prints. The oak stand. The washes." She lowered the knife but didn't close it. "You read all of it. I needed to know if you would."

"And if I hadn't?"

"Then you'd have gone home, and I'd have saved us both some walking." She looked south, at the dark country climbing toward the escarpment, and something in her face did a thing I couldn't read, which almost never happens, and which I noted and filed and didn't like. "You're the best there is. Everyone says so."

"Everyone says a lot of things about me. Half of them are that I'm a myth."

"Are you?"

"I'm standing here."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I give on an empty stomach." I nodded at her pack. "You've got water for one more day, maybe. I've got water for two, and I'm not sharing it with a thief."

"Then arrest me," she said, and held out her wrists, and her voice had a strange flat brightness to it, like glass in the sun. "Tie me up and walk me back to Marrow Bend and collect your double fee. That's the job, isn't it? Retrieve the journal, retrieve the thief. The Trust pays for both."

I didn't move. "You know about the fee."

"I know everything about the Trust. That's the problem." The knife finally closed, and she put it away, and she looked at me with an expression I've thought about a great deal since, though I didn't know it yet — the look of someone deciding how much of the truth a stranger can carry. "You haven't asked me what's in the journal."

"I don't care what's in the journal."

"You will." She said it simply, without drama, the way you'd tell a man the bridge ahead is out. "You will, and sooner than you think, and when you do, I want you to remember that I offered to tell you out here, in the open, before anyone paid me to say otherwise."

The wind came off the escarpment, the night wind, the one she'd used on the salt pan, and it moved the dust between us in a slow gray curtain. Somewhere out on the flats, a crust plate settled with a sound like a struck bell. I stood in the moonlight with three days of trail behind me and a canteen half full and the best reading of my career, and I realized I'd been tracking the wrong thing the whole way. I'd been tracking a thief. The thief had been tracking the truth, and the truth had a head start measured in years.

"Show me," I said.

She hesitated — one breath, two — and then she knelt and opened her pack, and what she handed me wasn't the journal. It was a corner of a map, torn ragged along one edge, old paper gone soft as cloth. I turned it to the moonlight.

It was the headwaters of the Upper Fly River. The country above the last surveyed reach, the forbidden country, the blank on every map I'd ever seen rendered here in fine brown ink with contour lines like fingerprints. And in the margin, in a small, slanted, utterly certain hand, a single line of notes — a hand I didn't recognize, and which stopped my breath anyway, though I couldn't have told you why until much later, when it was far too late for it to matter.

"Where did you get this?" I asked.

"Tore it out of the journal," Wren Calloway said, "the night before I stole it. And the reason I tore it out, Underhill, is that it's the only page in that book the Trust doesn't know exists — and the only page that proves what they're going to do to the source of that river."

The moonlight sat on the contour lines like water. Far to the south, past the escarpment, past everything I knew, the Fly was running in the dark toward a sea it would reach long before I did, carrying whatever it was given.

"Start at the beginning," I said.
