## 2. The Trust's Letter

Marrow Bend smelled the way river towns always smell at the end of the dry season: fish, diesel, and the sweet rot of things the water had decided to give back. I'd been in town four days, waiting on a ferry schedule that turned out to be a rumor with a timetable, and I was on my third cup of coffee that tasted like it had been argued out of the bean when the boy found me.

"You're Underhill," he said. Not a question. He was maybe fourteen, shirtless, barefoot, standing in the doorway of the dockside café like he owned the tide.

"Depends who's asking."

"Mr. Vane's asking." He set a card on my table, heavy cream stock, embossed. It cost more than my breakfast. "The Amberdale Trust. He says come now."

"Does he say please?"

"He says double your fee."

I looked at the card a long moment. Aldous Vane. I knew the name the way you know a weather system — from a distance, and with a certain low unease. The Amberdale Trust had been buying up the upper Fly country for a decade: survey rights, water rights, mineral options, whole stretches of riverbank that nobody had lived on until somebody decided they could be sold. In the desert we called men like Vane rainmakers. Out here I suspected they called them something shorter.

"Tell him I'll come when I finish my coffee," I said.

The boy grinned like I'd passed a test. "He said you'd say that. He said tell you the coffee's on him too."

* * *

Vane kept offices above the chandlery, in a room that had been designed to make visitors feel like they were being measured for something. Ceiling fans turned with the slow indifference of things paid by the hour. Maps covered one wall — the whole watershed, from the escarpment down to the delta — pinned under glass like specimens. A second wall held photographs: dredges, concrete pours, men in hard hats shaking hands over ribbon. Progress, arranged chronologically.

Aldous Vane stood at the map wall with his back to me, which told me he wanted me to watch him before he watched me. He was sixty-odd, silver at the temples, dressed in linen that had never known honest work. When he finally turned, his smile arrived a half-second before the rest of his face did, the way lightning precedes thunder.

"Miss Underhill. The famous tracker. They said you'd be sunburnt and rude. You're only one of those."

"I'll let you guess which." I stayed by the door. Old habit. Doors are the only furniture worth trusting.

"Sit, please." He didn't sit himself. Hosts who remain standing want you to feel the meeting will be brief and entirely on their terms. "You've been in Marrow Bend four days. Waiting on the ferry."

"The ferry's late."

"The ferry isn't coming. The Trust bought the company in March. I can have you upstream tomorrow, if you're willing to work."

That landed where he meant it to. I crossed the room and sat, mostly so he couldn't see what my face did about it. Four days of dead ends, and the man across the desk had closed them all with a signature. It wasn't an offer yet. It was a demonstration.

"Now," he said, folding himself into the chair opposite mine at last, hands steepled. "Two matters. One small, one large. The small one first, because everyone always wants to start with the money."

He slid a folder across the desk. Inside: three photographs of a woman — young, sharp-faced, dark hair cut short in a way that said she'd done it herself with a knife rather than pay a barber. Wren Calloway. I'd seen her once before, two years ago, selling forged permits in a border town, and I remembered thinking she had the eyes of someone reading a room for exits even while she laughed.

"Wren Calloway," Vane said. "Formerly a junior surveyor in our own employ. Three weeks ago she broke into our field office here and stole a journal. A surveyor's journal. Field notes, measurements, sketches. Property of the Trust."

"People steal journals all the time," I said. "Usually they're writing them."

"This one belonged to Dr. Imogen Hale."

And there it was.

I want to tell you I took it well. I want to tell you the name went through me like any other name, one more fact in a professional briefing, filed and forgotten. But a name like that doesn't go through you. It lands. Like a stone dropped into still water — the surface stays smooth for exactly as long as it takes you to understand what's happened, and then the rings start, and they don't stop at the edges of the pond.

Imogen Hale.

Seven years since I'd heard anyone say it aloud. Seven years since I'd said it myself, and then only once, into a radio that didn't answer, from a ridge in country I'd promised her I'd leave. I had built a whole life on top of that silence the way silt builds on a drowned thing — layer on layer, flat and stable and load-bearing, and all of it resting on the assumption that no one would ever drop a stone.

"Dr. Hale," I said, and my voice came out level. I checked. It came out level. "Your expedition leader."

"Our expedition leader." Vane watched me the way a man watches water rise against a gauge. Whatever he saw, he chose not to comment on it, which was its own kind of data. "Imogen led our source expedition. Nineteen people. Scientists, surveyors, support staff. They went up the Upper Fly six weeks ago to establish baseline readings at the headwaters. Three weeks ago, they stopped transmitting."

"Equipment failure happens."

"Her equipment was military-grade redundancy. Two transmitters, satellite uplink, scheduled check-ins every twelve hours." He tapped the folder. "She missed thirty-six consecutive check-ins. Then Calloway steals the journal from our office the same week. I don't believe in coincidences, Miss Underhill. I believe in causes, and I pay to find them."

"What do you think happened up there?"

"I think," Vane said, "that Imogen found something at the source she wasn't supposed to find, and that either the river took her or she decided to stop being found. And I think Wren Calloway knows which." He leaned forward. "I want both retrieved. The woman and the journal. Alive and intact, respectively — though frankly, Miss Underhill, if the ordering proves inconvenient, I'm more flexible about the woman than the paper."

The rings were still spreading. I made myself look at the map wall instead of him. The headwaters of the Fly sat near the top of the glass, a smudge of blue lines converging in country marked, in the old surveys, with a single word: *unadministered*. Which was a cartographer's way of saying nobody who drew maps had ever come back from there to correct them.

"Double my fee," I said. "You said double."

"Double, plus expenses, plus a bonus on delivery." He wrote a figure on a card and turned it toward me. It was more than I'd made in the last three years combined. It was, I understood immediately, not really about the money at all. Men like Vane don't buy services. They buy ownership — of your time, your silence, your version of events. The number was just the leash.

"And if I find Dr. Hale and she doesn't want to come back?"

Something moved behind Vane's eyes — quick, cold, gone. "Then you'll have learned something useful about her, and you'll report it. Your job is finding, Underhill. What people want is between them and their circumstances." He smiled again, that half-second early. "Circumstances being, in this case, me."

* * *

I walked the docks for an hour afterward, because I needed my body doing something mechanical while the rest of me caught up.

Here is what I knew about Imogen Hale, and I knew it the way you know a scar — completely, uselessly, by heart.

Hydrologist. The best field scientist I ever met, and I've met men who could taste salinity in groundwater through boot leather. She'd spent nine years charting rivers nobody funded because the rivers ran through nowhere anybody owned. She laughed at bad jokes and cried at good news. She slept in the open without a tent and woke at dawn without an alarm, and she used to say the desert taught her everything except how to leave it.

She was wrong about that last part. I taught her how to leave it. I demonstrated, personally, seven years ago, when the Trust's lawyers made it clear that my commission — the only thing I had ever earned, the only thing that had ever made me legible to myself — would not survive my association with a scientist whose findings were inconvenient to their holdings. There was a hearing I could have testified at. There was a choice. I told myself it was duty. Duty is a wonderful word. It takes whatever shape the hole requires.

I had left her a note. Two sentences. I have thought many times about what those two sentences cost per word, and concluded they were the most expensive prose ever written.

Now her name was sitting in the middle of my still water, and the rings had reached every shore.

There was also this, and I made myself look at it straight: Vane had hired me because I was the best. But Vane had also, apparently, lost contact with nineteen people and responded by hiring one tracker instead of calling a rescue service, and had mentioned flexibility about whether the woman came back alive. Employers who lead with the paper and hedge on the person are telling you their priorities. I'd spent fifteen years reading broken scrub and buried footprints. Men like Vane print their tracks in ink.

* * *

The boy found me again at dusk, sitting on the bollards at the ferry landing, watching the brown water carry the day away.

"He says yes or no by morning," the boy said.

"Did he send a deposit?"

The boy handed over an envelope. Heavy. I weighed it in my palm and did not count it. Counting it would mean deciding, and I wasn't ready to decide, though we both knew what the answer was going to be, the way you know a storm is coming from the color of the light.

"There's one more thing," the boy said, and for a second he looked less like a messenger and more like a kid. "Down at the fuel dock. Woman came through yesterday asking after boats upstream. Paid cash, wouldn't give a name. Dockhand said she had a map with the headwaters marked on it, and—" he shrugged, embarrassed by the drama— "and she asked whether anyone else had been asking. Whether anyone was following her."

Dark hair, cut short. Eyes reading the room for exits.

"When did she leave?" I asked.

"Dawn boat. Pilot named Marr. Old fellow. They say he's the only one who'll take the Upper Fly past the Serrated Reach this time of year."

I stood there while the boy padded off, envelope in my hand, the river running dark and patient below me. Somewhere upstream, past the reach, past the gorge, past everything mapped and everything that refused to be, Imogen Hale was silent, and Wren Calloway was running toward her or away from me or both, and Aldous Vane's money was in my pocket like a hook already set.

I had spent fifteen years finding people who didn't want to be found. It had never once occurred to me, until that moment on the landing, that someday the person I'd most want to find might be the one I'd taught myself to stop looking for.

I went back to Vane's office and said yes.

He shook my hand and told me the boat would be ready at first light, and that he'd taken the liberty of arranging a pilot — an old riverman, very reliable, name of Sefu Marr.

The river, I thought, walking back down into the dark, keeps what it's given.

So, apparently, does the current.
