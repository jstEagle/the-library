## 3. What the River Keeps

The pilot I wanted was dead, and the pilot I could afford was a liar, so I settled on the man in between: Sefu Marr, who was neither dead nor cheap and only lied about his age, which he claimed to be sixty-two in the way a river claims to be shallow.

"Seventy-eight," said the woman who ran the fueling dock at Marrow Bend, jerking her chin at him. "Maybe eighty. He's been running this stretch since before the Trust had a name."

"I'm sixty-two," Sefu said with dignity, "and I have excellent knees."

He did not have excellent knees. He had a flatboat called the *Patient Widow* — I would come to learn why — forty feet of scarred timber and an engine that sounded like a man clearing his throat before delivering bad news. He had a deck stacked with fuel drums, rope older than me, and eyes the color of tea left too long in the pot. And he had one condition of hire, stated before I'd finished explaining what I needed.

"You don't tell me what you're really after."

I'd told him I was chasing a thief upstream. That much was true. What I hadn't told him was whose journal she'd stolen, or whose expedition had gone quiet three weeks ago, or that the Trust's money sat heavy in my pocket like a stone I hadn't decided whether to throw.

"That's a strange condition," I said.

"It's a survival condition." He spat over the side, into water the color of strong coffee. "People who go up the Fly asking questions come back either answered or drowned. I prefer my passengers unburdened. You carry your question, I carry you. We both arrive."

"And if your question is heavier than mine?"

He looked at me for a long moment, weighing something. Whatever he found, he didn't share it.

"Then we sink," he said, and quoted me a price that made Vane's double fee feel less like generosity and more like prophecy.

* * *

We cast off from Marrow Bend on a morning so humid the air seemed to be sweating on my behalf. The town fell away behind us — tin roofs, the Trust's white administrative building standing out like a tooth in a rotting jaw — and the river took us.

I want to say I understood the Upper Fly immediately. I didn't. I'm a desert ranger; sand is my scripture, and sand is honest. It doesn't move unless wind moves it, and when it does, it tells you exactly where everything went. Water is a different religion entirely. The Fly braided and unbraided itself around islands that hadn't existed last season and wouldn't exist next month. Whole trees came downcurrent, upright and green, still convinced they were alive, sailing past us with their roots waving like drowning men. Sefu read all of it without appearing to look.

"Sandbar's moved," he said, two hours out, and swung the boat wide around a stretch of water that looked identical to every other stretch of water. Ten minutes later we passed the bar he'd predicted, sitting where no bar should be, brown hump of silt just kissing the surface.

"How did you know?"

"The birds." He nodded at a line of white egrets standing on nothing, patient as creditors. "Birds remember where the land was. Land forgets faster than birds do." He glanced back at me. "You'll want to learn that. Upstream, memory isn't a comfort. It's equipment."

I wrote that down in my log, because I write things down — it's how I keep myself together, ink as mortar between the bricks of a day. Then I went below to the tiny cabin he'd given me, which smelled of diesel and mildew and somebody else's tobacco, and I took out the map fragment.

Wren Calloway had torn it from something larger — the corner bore a ragged edge and half a Trust stamp, the letters AMBER cut off mid-word. It showed the upper reaches of the Fly: the Serrated Reach, the gorge country, and then, at the top edge, past the last surveyed contour, a blank space where the cartographers had given up and simply written SOURCE — UNSURVEYED in small, defeated type.

And in the margins, in pencil, someone had written notes.

I had read them once aboard Wren's trail, quickly, by firelight, cataloguing them as intelligence: elevations, flow rates, a reference to limestone karst. Useful. Impersonal. The kind of handwriting you see on a thousand survey documents, neat and slanted and anonymous.

By lamplight in the cabin, with the river muttering under the hull, I read them again.

*Flow gauge unreliable above the second bend — spring-fed, not rain-fed. Ask the elders.*

*Terraces on the east bank are maintained. Maintained implies hands.*

*Q — if you ever read this, the water here is colder than any place we were happy. Come find out why.*

The lamp guttered. Or my hand shook. One of those.

I knew that handwriting. You know handwriting the way you know a voice in the dark — not with your eyes but somewhere lower, in the chest, where recognition happens before thought can get its boots on. The loop of the *g*. The way she crossed her *t*s like she was striking them through, impatient even on paper. Seven years, and my body still filed Imogen Hale's hand under *home* before my mind could object.

Seven years ago I had stood in a room very much like this one — small, functional, smelling of fuel — and listened to Aldous Vane explain my options. Option one: continue my association with Dr. Imogen Hale, whose research into the upper watershed was becoming, in his word, *complicated*, and lose my commission, my pension, and the only profession I'd ever been good at. Option two: take the posting he offered me, four hundred miles of desert away, and let the relationship die of distance, which is the gentlest murder there is because nobody has to hold the knife.

I chose option two and told myself it was duty. Duty is a wonderful word. It sounds like armor and works like a cage, and the best part is you build it yourself, so you can't even blame the locksmith.

I had left her a note. A note! Seven years of loving someone and I reduced the ending to eleven lines on Trust stationery, and I remember that the eleventh line was *this is the right thing*, which is the sort of sentence that proves it wasn't.

Now here was her handwriting again, riding a stolen map up the same river, and the last line of the note she'd left in the margin — written years ago, carried by a thief, delivered by a river — was an invitation.

*Come find out why.*

"Well," I said to the empty cabin, because I talk to empty rooms when the alternative is talking to myself honestly, "you always did leave better notes than I did."

* * *

That night I couldn't sleep, so I climbed to the deck, where Sefu kept the first watch under a tarp strung against the drizzle. The river at night is a black country with a border of sound. Insects stitched the dark together. Somewhere far off, something large entered the water with a sound like a door closing.

Sefu poured me tea from a dented thermos without asking. It was terrible. It was perfect.

"Can I ask you something professional?" I said.

"You can ask. Whether it stays professional depends on you."

"The expedition. Three weeks silent. If a boat went missing on this stretch, what would that mean?"

He considered the dark. "Depends on the boat. Depends on the season. Depends on who wanted it missing." He drank. "You're asking the wrong question, ranger. The right question is: what's upstream that makes silence worth keeping?"

"You've been up there."

"Twice." He held up two fingers, then folded one down. "Once to run cargo, once to bring down a man who'd gone up to find God and found fever instead. Both times, the river let me turn around. That's not guaranteed." He looked at me sideways. "There's a village past the Reach. Drowned now — Trust survey flood, five years back. They call it an accident. The people who lived there call it Tuesday." His voice stayed level, but something underneath it didn't. "The old ones say the source has a keeper. Say strangers who take from the river get kept by it. I used to think that was poetry."

"And now?"

"Now I think most poetry is insurance fraud committed by people who noticed something true and had no other way to file it."

I laughed despite myself, and he allowed himself the ghost of a smile, and for a while we just watched the banks slide past, black shapes of jungle breathing in and out.

"Your thief," he said eventually. "The one you're chasing. You talk about her like weather. Like something that happened to you rather than something you're doing."

"She's fast."

"Mm." He refilled my cup. "In seventy-eight years—"

"Sixty-two."

"—in sixty-two years, I have learned that when a person describes another person as fast, or clever, or trouble, what they mean is: I cannot stop thinking about them. So. Which is it?"

I watched the water. The moon had come apart behind cloud, and the river ran silver-black, endless, going somewhere with total commitment.

"There's someone upstream," I said finally. It cost more than I expected. Words about Imogen always had a toll attached. "Not the thief. Someone I—" I tried several words and rejected all of them. "Someone I owed an apology to. Seven years overdue. With interest."

Sefu nodded slowly, as if I'd confirmed a reading he'd already taken.

"Then you understand the boat's name," he said.

"*Patient Widow.*"

"A widow waits. A widow knows the person she's waiting for isn't coming back. And still she keeps the house ready." He tapped the deck beneath us with one bare foot. "I named her after my wife. She waited thirty years for me to stop running cargo and stay home. Then she stopped waiting, if you follow me, and I built this boat the same week." He shrugged, a whole philosophy compressed into one motion. "The Fly forgives nothing and forgets less. Neither do widows. You'd best hope yours forgives, ranger. Because the forgetting —" he shook his head — "the river will handle that part for her. It keeps what people put down. Every debt, every name, every note in a margin. It holds all of it, and one day it hands it back, and you'd better be standing in the water when it does, or it'll knock you flat."

* * *

Two days upriver, past the first rapids, I spread the map fragment out again in the cabin and forced myself to read it the way I should have read it the first time: not as intelligence, but as correspondence.

Every marginal note was a message. Not to me — to whoever came after, to anyone the map might reach, a trail of breadcrumbs laid by a woman who had learned, somewhere in seven silent years, to assume that help would have to find her rather than the reverse. *Ask the elders.* *Maintained implies hands.* Each line was a door left unlocked.

And each line was also an accusation, though she hadn't meant it as one. She'd been up here mapping the truth, and the truth had frightened the Trust enough to make her expedition go quiet, and I had spent those same seven years tracking people across dunes for money, telling myself the desert was neutral ground, that duty had no address.

Duty had an address. It had a white building in Marrow Bend with AMBERDALE TRUST on the door, and I had been paying rent on my conscience to it for seven years.

I sat with the fragment until the light failed, memorizing it — every contour, every penciled line, the position of the terraces, the phrase *spring-fed, not rain-fed* — because a tracker learns early that paper burns and gets wet and gets stolen, and memory is the only document that travels well.

Outside, the current spoke against the hull in its one long syllable. Going, going, going. The river didn't ask permission and didn't apologize, and it was carrying me toward the one person I had never had the courage to row toward on my own.

Somewhere ahead, in the gorge country, Wren Calloway was running with a journal full of dam schematics. Somewhere beyond her, Imogen was alive or wasn't, silent by choice or by force. And behind me, I now suspected, the Trust's patience was not as long as its money.

I blew out the lamp and lay in the dark listening to the water, and I made the kind of promise you make alone at night, knowing morning will try to renegotiate:

This time I'm not leaving first.

The *Patient Widow* rounded the second bend at dawn, and Sefu woke me with tea and news: fresh engine noise downstream, gaining — someone else was running the Fly, and running it hard.
