## 4. Wren in the Reeds

There are three rules to being a good thief, and I learned all of them the hard way, which is the only way anybody learns anything worth knowing.

One: never sleep twice in the same place. Two: never trust a man who smiles before he speaks. Three: when you feel that prickle at the back of your neck — the one that says *someone is watching* — believe it, because it is almost never wrong.

That night, on the fueling dock at Kessick's Landing, the prickle arrived around midnight, right about the time I decided I could risk a fire after all. I want it noted, for the record, that I did not light the fire. I sat there with my hands cupped around an unlit stub of candle and my pack between my knees like a child hugging a schoolbook, and I listened to the river go by in the dark, and I thought: *you're getting sloppy, Wren. You're starting to think like a person with a future.*

Then the prickle came, and I knew I'd been right not to strike the match. Small mercies. The river deals in them, apparently, though I hadn't learned that yet.

I should back up. Because if you're going to understand why I did what I did on that dock — why I didn't run, why I didn't fight, why I sat down across from a woman who had every reason to break my arm and every skill necessary to do it — you need to know what was in my pack, and what it cost me to put it there.

* * *

Six weeks earlier I'd been a surveyor for the Amberdale Trust. Not a thief. There's a difference, whatever Aldous Vane's lawyers would tell you, and the difference is mostly a matter of who signs your pay chit.

I drew maps. That was my whole job. I took instrument readings and turned them into lines on paper, and the lines became charts, and the charts went into reports, and the reports went into a locked room in Marrow Bend where men with clean collars decided what the river was worth. I was good at it. I liked the work. I told myself it mattered — that a map is a kind of honesty, that ink doesn't lie even if the people reading it do.

The journal belonged to Corwin Ashe, senior surveyor, a dry stick of a man who smelled of pencil shavings and gin and who treated his instruments better than he treated his wife. He kept his field notes in a leather-bound journal with a brass clasp, and he was proud of those notes, and he should have been, because they were thorough. Too thorough. Thorough enough to be dangerous.

I wasn't supposed to read them. Nobody below Vane's level was supposed to read them. But Ashe left the journal on his desk one evening while he went to negotiate something with somebody, and I was alone in the chart room with a lamp and a bad conscience and forty minutes to kill, and I opened it.

You have to understand what I expected. Numbers. Elevations. Flow rates. The usual poetry of my profession.

What I found instead was the source of the Upper Fly River, drawn in Ashe's careful hand — the spring itself, rising out of limestone like something out of a church — and around it, terraces. Terraced gardens, centuries old, tended by people whose names didn't appear anywhere in Trust records because as far as the Trust was concerned they didn't exist. And beneath the drawings, folded in on themselves like something ashamed to be seen, the schematics. Dam schematics. A concrete wall ninety meters high, a reservoir that would drown the spring, the terraces, the valley, everything, and a note in Ashe's handwriting that made my skin go cold:

*Vane wants this filed under "hydrological survey, preliminary." It is not preliminary. Groundbreaking within two years. The expedition's findings are to corroborate, not contradict. H. has been instructed accordingly.*

H. Dr. Imogen Hale. The expedition leader. The woman whose silence upstream had Marrow Bend chewing its fingernails.

I sat in that chart room until the lamp burned low, and I did the arithmetic that thieves do, the ugly kind, where you weigh one thing against another and pretend the scale is honest. If the journal went back into the locked room, the dam would be built, and the people on those terraces would come home one day to find their sacred spring at the bottom of a reservoir named after some Trust benefactor. If the journal disappeared —

If the journal disappeared, they would send someone after it.

They sent Quentin Underhill. But I'm getting ahead of myself again. I always do, when I'm scared. My mouth runs forward and my memory runs backward and somewhere in the middle is the truth, trying to keep up.

* * *

So: Kessick's Landing, midnight, no fire, prickle at the neck.

I heard her before I saw her, which is how it works with the really good ones. A shift of weight on the dock planks behind me, so soft it might have been the wood settling. Then a voice, low and even, with an edge of the desert in it — dry, unhurried, the voice of someone who has waited out sandstorms longer than my entire life story.

"Don't reach for the pack."

I reached for the pack. Old habits. She let me get halfway there before she stepped out of the reeds and into the moonlight, and I stopped, because stopping was suddenly the most reasonable idea I'd ever had.

Quentin Underhill. I'd heard the name in Marrow Bend saloons the way sailors hear about storms — with respect, and from a safe distance. The ranger who finds people who don't want to be found. They said she once tracked a murderer across the Redglass Flats for eleven days with half a canteen and came back carrying him over one shoulder and the man's boots over the other, because the boots were Trust property. That last detail is what frightened me most, honestly. Anyone can be relentless. Only a certain kind of person is relentless *and* punctilious.

She stood maybe four meters away, and she looked exactly like the stories except for her eyes, which were tired in a way the stories don't mention. Desert-tired. Long-road tired. The kind of tired that isn't about sleep.

"Wren Calloway," she said.

"You have me at a disadvantage," I said. "Which is fine. Most people do."

"Underhill." She didn't sit down. She stood with her weight balanced the way I imagine she stands on a horse or a dune or the edge of anything — like the ground was a suggestion she was humoring. "You've been hard to follow."

"No, I haven't. You followed me the whole way. I just made it boring." I nodded at the dark water sliding past the pilings. "Nobody interesting travels the Upper Fly. They just endure it."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. An acknowledgment, maybe, that I'd said a true thing. I filed that away, because I was already learning something important about Quentin Underhill: she respected true things. It might be the only currency she took.

"The journal," she said.

"Is in my pack."

"I know it's in your pack. I've known since Marrow Bend. What I don't know is why a Trust surveyor with six years of clean service stole it, ran three hundred kilometers through country that eats strangers, and is now sitting on a fueling dock at midnight without a lit fire, which tells me you knew I was coming." Her head tilted slightly. "You don't strike me as stupid, Calloway. So convince me you're not crazy instead."

And there it was. The moment. I'd rehearsed versions of it for six hundred kilometers — on the boat, in the reeds, in the sweaty dark of a dozen borrowed beds — and every version ended differently. In some of them I talked my way out. In some of them I lied. In one particularly humiliating version I cried, which I include here only to demonstrate the full range of my desperation.

In none of them did I do what I actually did, which was tell the truth.

Maybe it was the way she looked at the unlit candle. Maybe it was that she'd said *I've known since Marrow Bend* and hadn't taken the pack off me yet, which meant she wanted something more than the recovery. Or maybe it was simpler than that: I'd been carrying the journal for six weeks like a stone in my chest, and I was so tired of being the only person alive who knew what was in it.

"Open it," I said. "Read page forty."

She didn't move. "You first. Tell me."

"Tell you what?"

"Why you stole it."

I laughed. I couldn't help it — a short, ugly sound that startled a night bird out of the reeds. "Because I read it," I said. "Because I'm the one person in the Trust's employ who actually read the damn thing, and now I can't unknow it, and neither will you, and that, Ranger, is your problem now too."

* * *

Here's what I told her, there on the dock, while the river ran past us black and patient and the moths beat themselves against the fuel-house lamp:

I told her about the schematics. Ninety meters of concrete across the gorge above the spring. A reservoir flooding the upper valley. I told her about the terraces in Ashe's drawings, and the keeper-tracks worn into the limestone, and the fact that none of it appeared in any Trust filing because the Trust's surveys of the headwaters had been, shall we say, curated. I told her about the note — *the expedition's findings are to corroborate, not contradict* — and watched her face when I said Imogen Hale's name, because I'd done my homework too, and I knew things about Quentin Underhill and Imogen Hale that Underhill probably thought were buried deeper than the spring itself.

Her face didn't change. That's the thing about desert people; they keep their weather inside. But her stillness changed quality. It went from professional stillness to the stillness of a woman standing very carefully on ice she suspects is thin.

"The expedition went silent three weeks ago," she said.

"It went silent because it was never meant to come back." I said it flat, because I'd earned the flatness, six weeks of it. "Think about it. Vane sends Hale upriver with instructions to corroborate. Hale finds the spring, finds the keepers, finds a living culture sitting on top of the reservoir site — and Hale, by every account I ever heard, is not a woman who corroborates on command. So the expedition stops transmitting. And Vane doesn't send a rescue. He sends you. One tracker, after a thief. Ask yourself, Underhill — why does a missing expedition rate less urgency than a stolen notebook?"

"Because the notebook is evidence," she said slowly.

"Because the notebook is evidence *against him*, and the expedition—" I spread my hands. "The expedition is either already dealt with, or it's a problem he'll deal with when he knows where it is. And you, Ranger, are how he finds out. You're not the retrieval. You're the reconnaissance. I'd bet the journal on it."

For a long moment she said nothing. The river filled the silence, that low constant conversation water has with itself, and somewhere downstream a fuel barge clanged its bell against the dock at Serrit Point, five kilometers off, the sound carrying flat across the water.

"You expect me to believe," she said at last, "that your employer — your *former* employer — sent a missing expedition upriver to die, and that you, a junior surveyor, figured it out from a notebook, and that the only person standing between Vane and a drowned valley is you and a leather journal with a brass clasp."

"When you say it like that it sounds insane."

"It sounds insane any way I say it."

"Yes," I agreed. "It does. Welcome to my last six weeks." I pulled the pack around, slow, showing her my hands the whole time, and set it on the planks between us. "But you're a tracker. So don't take my word for anything. Track it. Read the journal. Read Ashe's marginalia — the man had a conscience and a drinking problem and the second was eroding the first on a schedule. Count the supply manifests for the expedition against the distance traveled and the resupply points. Do the arithmetic on why a hydrological survey needs armed escort permits. You're the best tracker alive, they say. So track the lie. It's got a wide trail. Vane's been dragging it behind him for years."

She looked at the pack for a long time. Then at me. And I want to be fair to her, because history has been fair to her and I contributed nothing to that, so let me at least be accurate: she did not believe me. Not then. Belief is a thing trackers don't carry much of; it's heavy and it spoils. What she had instead was doubt pointed in a new direction, and for a woman like Quentin Underhill, redirected doubt is worth more than another person's faith.

"If I open that pack," she said, "and it's not there—"

"It's there."

"—and if I read it, and it says what you say it says—"

"Then you'll have a choice," I said. "Same one I had. Except yours comes with a commission attached, and mine only came with a conscience, so arguably yours is harder."

That got the almost-smile again, thinner this time. "You talk too much, Calloway."

"I've been alone for six weeks. It's backed up."

* * *

She opened the pack. She read by the fuel-house lamp, cross-legged on the dock with the journal on her knees, and I watched her read, and I'll tell you what I saw, because I've thought about it often since.

I saw a woman who reads the way she tracks — fast, then slow, then fast again, doubling back to a line the way she'd double back to a print. I saw her stop at page forty. I saw her stop longer at the folded schematics, and I saw her thumb move along the contour lines of the reservoir like she was measuring the drown herself. And when she got to Ashe's note about Hale — *instructed accordingly* — I saw her close the journal, gently, the way you close a door on a sickroom, and sit very still looking at the water.

"That village," she said finally. "On the terraces. Are there people there now?"

"There were people there when Ashe surveyed it. Whether there are now depends on whether Vane's other team got there first."

Her head came around. "What other team?"

"The one that isn't answering radios either." I shrugged. "I don't know that there is one. I know the Trust chartered two boats out of Marrow Bend in the same week, and only one of them was logged as a survey vessel. I know Captain Rook's name was on the other manifest, because Rook drinks at the same dockside bar I cased before I skipped town, and he was buying rounds and not paying for them, which means someone else was. Draw your own map, Ranger. You're better at it than I am."

She was quiet for a while. A moth landed on the closed journal and she let it stay.

"Give me one reason," she said, "not to take the journal and you both — you and it — back to Marrow Bend tonight. It's my job. It's what I'm paid for. It's what I'm *good* at."

"Because you haven't done it yet," I said. "You've had the chance since you walked out of the reeds. You could've taken me down before I finished my second sentence. Instead you're sitting on a cold dock listening to a thief tell you your employer is a monster, and you haven't argued once about the facts — only about whether to believe them." I leaned forward. "You already believe them, Underhill. You just haven't caught up to it. I know the feeling. It takes about six weeks."

"And if I catch up? Then what? I burn my commission. You burn whatever's left of your life. And Vane sends someone worse than me next time."

"Now you're doing the arithmetic," I said. "Welcome aboard."

She looked at me then — really looked, the tracker's look, the one that takes inventory — and whatever she found in my face seemed to settle something, or at least postpone it. She tied the journal's clasp shut and slid it back across the planks toward me, which surprised me so much I actually flinched.

"Hold onto it," she said. "If Vane's people board us, it's safer in the pack of a thief nobody's watching than in the hands of a ranger everybody's looking for." A pause. "Also, if we're doing this, you're going to learn to be quiet, paddle straight, and follow orders from a pilot named Sefu Marr who is going to think I've lost my mind for bringing you."

"Sefu Marr." I tested the name. "Should I know him?"

"He pilots the Upper Fly. He'll tell you the river forgives nothing and forgets less." She stood, and the dock boards barely registered her weight leaving them, which unsettled me more than any threat had. "He's right. About both halves."

I gathered the pack and stood too, and my legs had gone stiff and stupid from sitting, and I wobbled like a drunk, and she pretended not to notice, which was its own small kindness.

"One question," I said, as she turned toward the reeds. "Why? Why believe me? You trusted me exactly as far as you could throw me, thirty seconds ago."

She considered that. Downstream, the bell at Serrit Point rang again, farther off now, the current taking the sound away.

"I didn't believe you," she said. "I believed the journal. It's the only one of us with a consistent story." And she stepped off the dock into the dark, toward the boat, toward the river, toward everything that came after, and I stood there a moment longer with the pack against my chest and my heart going like a drum in a parade.

Defiant fear, my mother used to call it — the condition of being terrified and refusing to show it, which she considered the family disease. I'd carried it my whole life. But standing on that dock, watching the best tracker alive choose doubt over duty, I felt something new underneath it. Not safety. Nothing on the Upper Fly was ever safe.

More like company.

Behind us, far upriver, thunder muttered once in a sky that still looked clear, and I remember thinking: *early for the season.* I remember thinking it and dismissing it, because I was twenty-six and immortal and I had a ranger on my side and a river to run.

The monsoon was already coming. None of us knew it yet. But the river knew. The river always knows first.
