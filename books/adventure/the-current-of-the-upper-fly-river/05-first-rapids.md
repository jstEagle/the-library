## 5. First Rapids

The Serrated Reach announces itself the way a bad marriage does — quietly at first, then all at once.

We'd been on the water two days when the river changed its mind about being polite. The Fly above Marrow Bend is brown and slow and forgiving in the way of rivers that have already drowned everyone worth drowning. But at dawn on the third morning, Sefu Marr cut the engine, stood up straight as a heron, and said, "There she is," with the tone of a man introducing you to his ex-wife.

Ahead, the river narrowed between walls of black limestone that rose like the ribs of something enormous that had lain down to die a very long time ago. The water between them was white. Not foam-white — *teeth*-white. It came through the gorge in braided channels, splitting around stone spires that gave the Reach its name, each one polished smooth by centuries of insisting it would not be moved.

"That's where we're going?" Wren asked from the bow, where she'd spent most of two days pretending not to watch me and mostly failing.

"That's where we're going," Sefu said, and there was something in his voice I didn't like, which was the particular pleasure of a man who has survived a thing many times and enjoys watching newcomers do the arithmetic.

I'd run rapids before. Desert rivers are short-tempered things — flash floods that come down a wash and take everything that didn't read the sky. But they're honest. They rise, they rage, they're gone by Tuesday. This looked different. This looked like a river that had been angry for ten thousand years and had gotten *good* at it.

"Sefu," I said, "how many times have you run this?"

"Forty-one."

"And how many times have you gone through without touching?"

He smiled, which answered the question better than any number would have. "The trick," he said, "is not to fight her. You don't steer down the Serrated Reach. You ask politely, and you mean it."

* * *

He meant it, apparently, because we went through.

I won't pretend I remember it cleanly. There's a stretch of maybe twenty minutes that exists in my memory as sensation rather than sequence: the roar coming off the walls and doubling back so the sound had nowhere to go but through your chest; spray so thick it was less like rain than like being inside the river's argument with itself; Sefu's hands moving on the wheel with a gentleness that seemed obscene given what the water was doing, small corrections, patient, like a man calming a horse rather than wrestling one. Wren whooped once — actually whooped, this thief who'd spent two days flinching whenever I looked at her — and I caught myself almost smiling before the boat dropped into a hole the size of a chapel and my stomach arrived somewhere behind my ears.

Then it was over, and the river widened, and the roar fell away behind us like a door closing, and we were floating in green water under green light, and Sefu lit a cigarette with hands that did not shake even slightly, which told me everything about how much of that had been performance.

"You've done that forty-one times," I said.

"Forty-two." He exhaled. "You count the ones you survive."

Wren wrung out her shirt over the gunwale. "You could have warned us."

"I did warn you. In Marrow Bend. You weren't listening."

She hadn't been. Neither had I, if I'm honest — I'd been too busy reading the manifest, counting fuel drums, doing the paranoid arithmetic of a woman who'd just taken a job from a man whose handshake felt like a contract clause. Vane had offered double my fee, which in my experience means one of two things: the job is twice as dangerous as advertised, or the employer wants you twice as compromised. Usually both.

I was still doing that arithmetic when I saw the smoke.

* * *

It was a thin line of it, way upstream, hugging the left bank where the jungle came down to the water. Woodsmoke, gray-green, the color of a cook fire someone thought was hidden. A traveler alone doesn't hide a cook fire. A traveler alone doesn't need to.

"Sefu. Smoke. Two points off the bow."

He looked longer than I liked. "That bank's a fueling stop. Old logging cut, closed ten years. Nobody stops there now except—"

"Except people who don't want to be seen stopping."

He said nothing, which was its own kind of agreement. I got the binoculars out of the dry box — Vane had provided them, along with the rifle, along with the radio, along with every piece of equipment that made me feel less like a tracker and more like an employee, which is to say expendable and observed.

Through the glass: a landing, rotted pilings, and the stern of a boat tied up in the shadow of the cut. Aluminum hull, military surplus, the kind of craft that costs more than most people's houses. Two figures moving on the bank, quick and purposeful. One of them carried a radio handset, and the way he carried it — antenna down, casual, like a man going through motions — told me he wasn't expecting anyone to answer.

Because nobody was answering. That was the thing that sat wrong in my chest like a swallowed stone. Three weeks ago, Dr. Imogen Hale's expedition went silent. Vane had told me it like weather — storms happen, radios fail, the Upper Fly eats signals whole. And I'd believed him, because grief makes you credulous and I'd wanted the job for reasons I wasn't examining.

But here was a second boat. Fresh equipment. A crew that moved like soldiers dressed as surveyors. Heading upstream on the same river, toward the same silence, and if Vane had sent them ahead of me — *ahead*, days ahead, while he was sitting across a desk telling me I was the only hope —

Then Imogen's expedition wasn't lost. It was *avoiding* someone.

Or someone had found it first.

"What are you looking at?" Wren asked. She'd come to stand beside me, and I didn't lower the binoculars fast enough, and whatever she read in my face made her go still. "Trust people?"

"I don't know yet."

"You do know. You just don't want to say it, because saying it makes it real, and if it's real then you're working for the people who—" She stopped herself, jaw tight, and I understood she'd been about to say something about dams and sacred ground and the journal in her pack, and had decided, wisely, that mid-river was not the place.

But she'd said enough. Because here was the problem I'd been carrying since Marrow Bend like ballast: Wren Calloway was a liar and a thief, and I'd arrested four people exactly like her, and none of them had ever looked at me the way she looked at me when she talked about the source of the Fly. Thieves look guilty or greedy. She looked like a woman carrying something heavy that she believed was worth carrying.

And now there was a second team on the river that Vane hadn't mentioned, and the math only worked one way.

"Sefu," I said. "How far to the next place we can raise Marrow Bend on the radio?"

"Two days. Maybe three, if the Reach's mood holds."

"And the patrol channel? Trust boats carry a band, don't they?"

"They do." He tapped ash into the water. "You want me to call somebody?"

I watched the smoke thinning above the treeline. I thought about Aldous Vane's office, the maps weighted flat with brass, the way he'd said *Dr. Hale went silent* and then watched me — not the map, not the window, me — when he said her name. He'd known. He'd known exactly what that name would do to me, and he'd used it, the way you use a lever on a stuck door.

"No," I said. "Don't call anybody."

Wren turned to look at me fully for the first time in two days. "Ranger Underhill. Are we mutinying?"

"We're gathering information," I said. "Mutiny requires a ship worth keeping."

Sefu laughed from somewhere deep, the laugh of a man who has heard passengers say worse things and acted on fewer of them. He fed the engine a little throttle and we slid upstream into the green dark under the canopy, quiet now, running without lights, and if the boat behind us noticed us pass, they gave no sign.

* * *

That night we tied up in a side slough, a backwater where the current forgot us, and I took first watch because I didn't trust my own sleep and certainly wasn't going to trust anyone else's.

The jungle at night is nothing like the desert at night. The desert goes silent the way a held breath is silent — listening. The jungle never stops talking. Insects, frogs, something large and unhurried moving through the understory, the river itself muttering against the hull. It should have been unbearable to a woman who'd spent fifteen years falling asleep to wind. Instead it was almost restful. Noise means nothing is sneaking up on you. It's the quiet stretches that kill you.

Around midnight, Wren couldn't sleep either. I heard her come forward along the gunwale and settle near the bow, and for a long time neither of us spoke, which was its own kind of conversation.

"The boat today," she finally said. "You recognized the hull type."

"Military surplus. Trust fleet uses them for their mineral surveys."

"So Vane sent a second team."

"It looks that way."

"And you're still working for him."

I let that sit in the insect noise for a while. "I'm still *paid* by him," I said. "Working for him is looking increasingly negotiable."

She laughed — a short, surprised sound, quickly smothered, like she hadn't authorized it. Then: "Why'd you take the job? Really. You knew his name, you knew the money was wrong, you knew the story had holes in it big enough to pilot a barge through. People like you can smell a lie through a wall. So why?"

Because the letterhead had said Amberdale Trust and underneath it, in Vane's neat hand, *Dr. Imogen Hale, expedition leader*. Because seven years ago I'd stood in a doorway with a duffel bag and chosen a commission over a woman, and I'd told myself duty was a good reason, and I'd spent seven years discovering that duty is a fine reason for everything except the thing you actually did it to avoid feeling.

"Professional curiosity," I said.

"Liar," she said, without heat.

"Tracker's privilege."

She shifted, and I heard the journal's oilcloth wrapping rustle in her pack, and we both pretended I hadn't. "Whatever you think I stole," she said, "you should know I read every page before I took it. Twice. I was the one who drew half those schematics." A pause. "Vane didn't hire you to recover a journal, Underhill. He hired you to recover his version of events. There's a difference, and it's the width of this river."

I didn't answer. Out in the dark, something splashed — fish, probably, though out here I was learning to hold my assumptions loosely — and the current muttered on, and I kept watch over a thief, a pilot, and my own fast-collapsing certainties.

* * *

The next morning, the patrol boat found us.

Not the aluminum hull from the logging cut — this one was bigger, white-hulled, the Trust crest stenciled on the bow, and it came up behind us at mid-morning running easy, the way a shark swims when it isn't hungry yet. Sefu saw it first and killed our speed without being asked, an old man's instinct for making himself small.

A voice came across the water on a loudhailer, flat and official. "*Flatboat on the Upper Fly, this is Trust Patrol Vessel Corryvreckan. Identify yourself and prepare to be boarded.*"

"Charming name," Sefu murmured. "They named her after a whirlpool."

"Boarded for what?" Wren hissed. "We haven't done anything."

"*Yet,*" I said, and stepped to the rail.

The Corryvreckan slowed alongside. On her deck stood six figures in matching khaki, and at their center a man I placed immediately as trouble: mid-forties, built like the equipment he represented, sunburn line sharp as a stencil across his forehead. Captain's insignia. Eyes that did the thing I dislike most in another professional's eyes — they catalogued me, dismissed me, and filed me, all in about a second and a half.

"Quentin Underhill," he said. Not a question. "Captain Rook, Amberdale Trust security division. You're a long way from your contract parameters, Ms. Underhill. Vane's instructions were to track a fugitive to the lower settlements. Not to run the Serrated Reach."

"Instructions change when the facts do, Captain."

"Do they." He looked past me at Wren, and the look had weight to it, the specific weight of a man who has been told what he's carrying home and intends to carry it. "Ms. Calloway. You've caused a great deal of paperwork."

"She's been cooperative," I said.

"Has she." Rook smiled, and it was the smile of a man for whom cooperation is a technical term meaning *compliance*. "Here's how this goes, Underhill. You hand over the thief and the stolen property. You turn your boat around. You go back to Marrow Bend and collect your fee, and everyone's ledgers balance. My team continues upstream to render assistance to Dr. Hale's expedition, which is our actual mission, and none of us needs to write reports about irregularities."

"Assistance," I repeated. "Is that what the team at the logging cut is doing? Rendering assistance?"

Something moved behind Rook's eyes — small, fast, quickly filed away. But I'd seen it. I'd spent fifteen years reading faces the way other people read weather, and I knew what that flicker was. It was the look of a man whose plan has just acquired a witness.

"I don't know what you're talking about," he said.

"No," I agreed pleasantly. "You wouldn't."

"Underhill." His voice dropped, and the loudhailer was off now; this was just for us, man to woman, professional to professional. "I've read your file. Redglass Flats, the Kestrel run, the Marchetti recovery. You're good. Better than good. Which is why I'm offering you this once, plainly: get off the river. Whatever Vane promised you, he promises everyone, and he pays out to no one who's seen the upper water. You're a smart woman standing on a slow boat. Do the arithmetic."

"I've done it," I said. "It doesn't balance."

For a moment I thought he'd order his men aboard anyway. Six against three, on open water, with a patrol boat's advantage — he'd have won, and we'd all have known it. But something stopped him. Maybe the narrow water ahead. Maybe the fact that bodies float downstream and stories travel faster. Maybe just the arithmetic of his own mission, which apparently had a schedule tighter than his temper.

"Last chance," he said.

"Noted," I said.

The Corryvreckan pulled away with a wash that rocked us against our mooring lines, and Rook stood at her rail watching us until the bend took him, and I understood that I had not seen the last of that man, and that next time there would be no loudhailer and no offer.

"Well," said Sefu, relighting his dead cigarette with the calm of a man watching weather he'd predicted days ago. "That went well."

"That went well?" Wren's voice cracked upward. "He knows my name. He knows the boat. He's going to radio ahead and—"

"He's not going to radio ahead," Sefu said. "Did you notice the antenna?"

I had. I'd been watching it the whole conversation, the way you watch a man's hands. The Corryvreckan's mast was bare. No whip antenna. No radio traffic at all, inbound or out.

"Nobody on this river is answering radios," I said slowly, and the pieces came together with the ugly click of a trap shutting. "Hale's expedition went silent. Rook's patrol doesn't transmit. The second team runs dark. Vane's got people all over the upper Fly, and not one of them wants a record of what they're doing."

"Which means," Wren said, and her voice had gone quiet and certain, the voice of a woman watching her worst claim turn true, "whatever they're doing upstream, it's not the kind of thing you put in a report."

The current carried us on, north and up, toward the gorge and the silence and whatever waited at the head of the water. I stood at the rail and watched the jungle close behind us over the wake, and I thought about Imogen — alive, I was nearly sure of it now, alive and hiding from her own employers — and I thought about the word *assistance*, and I made myself a promise with the flavor of iron in it:

By the time I reached the source of the Fly River, I would know exactly who the enemy was.

I was beginning to suspect it was the man who signed my checks.
