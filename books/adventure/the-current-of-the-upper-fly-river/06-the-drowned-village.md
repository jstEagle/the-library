## 6. The Drowned Village

I want it on the record that I never asked to be a hero. I asked to be a thief, which is a much more honest profession. A thief knows exactly what she is. Nobody writes ballads about thieves, nobody expects them to save anybody, and when one gets caught — as I fully expected to be, eventually, by someone with better boots than Quentin Underhill's — the sentence is at least proportionate to the crime.

What I had not bargained for was the drowned village.

We came around a bend in the Upper Fly two days past the Serrated Reach, with Sefu Marr poling us through water the color of strong tea and Quentin sitting in the bow like a figurehead that occasionally asked uncomfortable questions, and there it was: rooftops rising out of the river like the backs of sleeping animals.

"Survey flood," Sefu said, without turning around. He said most things without turning around. It was his way of making you earn his face. "Two years back. The Trust needed readings on how far upstream they could push water before anyone downstream noticed."

"You mean before anyone downstream *mattered*," Quentin said.

"I mean what I mean," Sefu said, "and the river means what it means, and neither of us is going to change the other's mind today."

I sat very still in the middle of the boat and looked at those rooftops. There were eleven of them that I could count, some still whole, their thatch gone green with algae, a fishing net draped over one like a shroud somebody had hung out to dry and then forgotten why. A church bell tower stood in four feet of water, its bell silent, its cross tilted like a man leaning against a wall after bad news. Children's clothes on a line that no one would ever take down. A dugout canoe moored to a window frame, because apparently even abandonment needs somewhere to tie up.

Here is the thing about guilt. It doesn't arrive all at once, like weather. It arrives the way water rises in a hull — quietly, below the waterline, in places you don't check until your boots are floating. I had stolen a journal. One journal. I'd told myself a hundred stories about it on the way upstream: that it was evidence, that it was leverage, that it was a small sharp thing I could hold against a large soft institution. I had not told myself the story where the institution had already done this. Where the journal wasn't the beginning of anything. It was the receipt.

"That village didn't flood," Quentin said. She was reading the waterline on the buildings the way other people read newspapers. "Floods recede. This one was made to stay."

"The Trust calls it an impoundment trial," Sefu said. "Very technical term. Means they closed a coffer dam downstream for six weeks to see what would happen."

"What happened?"

"What you're looking at happened." He poled us closer, close enough that I could see into a window, close enough that I wished he wouldn't. "Four hundred people moved to Marrow Bend and points south. Some of them took payment. Most of them took nothing, because there was nothing offered but paper, and paper doesn't float."

I thought about the journal in my pack. I had wrapped it in oilcloth like it was something precious, something worth saving, and I suppose I'd been half right — it was precious, just not to me. To me it had been a weapon. Sitting there looking at a bell tower standing in a drowned street, I understood for the first time that it was also a confession, and that I had been carrying somebody else's confession around like luggage, and that luggage, as any traveler knows, always ends up belonging to whoever carries it longest.

* * *

We put in at what used to be the high end of the village, a shelf of ground where the water hadn't quite won, and that was where we found Maren Oduya.

She was an old woman sitting on a stool outside the last dry house, mending a net, and she watched us beach the boat with the expression of someone who has seen every kind of stranger the river can deliver and has stopped being impressed by any of them. Behind her, the house was packed floor to ceiling with things salvaged from the water: chairs, lamps, a sewing machine, framed photographs drying in rows like specimens.

"You're going upstream," she said. Not a question. Old women on rivers don't ask questions; they issue verdicts early so you have time to appeal.

"We are," Quentin said.

"Mm." She bit her thread and tied it off. "The Trust boys went through here in the spring. Big boat, radios, the young one with the jaw like a hatchet. They also went upstream." She looked at Quentin over her spectacles. "You'll be wanting to know if they came back."

Quentin didn't answer, which was itself an answer, and Maren Oduya nodded as if Quentin had spoken a full paragraph.

"They didn't come back," she said. "None of them did. And now here's you three, going up after them, which makes you either brave or stupid, and I've known brave people and stupid people my whole life and I can tell you the river eats both in roughly equal portions." She stood, joints cracking like green firewood, and looked at each of us in turn. When her eyes got to me, they stayed. "You're carrying something."

It wasn't a mystical statement. My pack was misshapen around the journal's rectangle; I might as well have worn a sign. But it landed on me like a mystical statement anyway, the way a guess does when it's too accurate to be comfortable.

"A map," I said, because lying to old women on riverbanks seemed like a good way to die badly.

"And papers."

"Papers."

She grunted and turned toward her house. "Come inside before the rain decides for you. And mind the step. Everything in this house is from the bottom of the river, and the river doesn't like being copied."

* * *

Her house smelled of lamp oil and dried fish and, underneath it, faintly, of mud — the honest smell of a place that had been underwater and knew it. She poured tea that tasted like boiled bark and sat us down among her salvage, and Sefu Marr, who I had by then concluded feared exactly nothing on earth, took off his hat when he crossed her threshold, which told me more about Maren Oduya than anything she said afterward.

"You work for the Trust?" she asked me.

"No."

"You worked for the Trust."

There was no getting around this woman. "I was a surveyor," I admitted. "Three years. I drew the maps they used to decide where the water would go." I looked at the photographs drying on their lines — families, weddings, a boy holding up a fish nearly as long as himself. "I didn't draw this one. But I drew ones like it."

"And then you stole their book."

"How do you—"

"Because trackers track and thieves thieve and surveyors survey, and none of you can help it." She sipped her tea. "Also, the Trust posted a notice about you in Marrow Bend with a drawing that flatters you considerably. Whoever drew it should be arrested for fraud."

Quentin made a sound that in a lesser person would have been a laugh. I shot her a look that promised a long conversation later.

"Why did you steal it?" Maren Oduya asked me, and it was not idle curiosity. Her eyes had the flat patience of deep water.

And here's the thing — I could have given her the speech. The one I'd rehearsed. Evidence, leverage, justice, courts. I opened my mouth to give it and found that in this house, surrounded by a drowned town's belongings, the speech died in my throat like a fish in air.

"Because I read it," I said instead. "All of it. The dam schematics, the flow calculations, the memorandum about the source valley. And then I couldn't unread it. I kept thinking — somebody has to make sure this doesn't happen again. Somebody who isn't paid by them." I turned my cup in my hands. "That's the flattering version. The true version is that I was scared, and angry, and stealing it was the only thing I could think of that felt like doing something instead of nothing."

Maren Oduya considered this for a long moment.

"Honesty," she said finally. "From a thief. The river sends me stranger cargo every year." She set down her cup. "You want to know what's at the top of this river. Everyone who goes up wants to know, and everyone who comes back — which is few — tells it wrong."

"Then tell it right," Quentin said.

The old woman looked at her for a while. Then she got up, slowly, and took down one of the photographs — the boy with the fish — and held it in both hands the way you'd hold a bird.

"This is my grandson, Tam. He was eleven when the water came. He caught that fish the morning of the last day the village was a village." She set the photograph face-down on the table, gently, like putting a child to bed. "You want to know what's at the source. I'll tell you what my mother told me, and her mother told her, back before there was a Trust or a commission or a single white man with a theodolite within a thousand miles of this water."

She sat back down.

"There is a keeper at the source," she said. "Not a person. Not only a person. The river keeps a keeper the way a house keeps a hearth — something has to tend the fire or the house goes cold. The keepers' people live up there, in the hidden valley, and they have tended the spring since before my people had names for the seasons. They don't guard it because it's valuable. They guard it because it's *alive*, and everything alive near it belongs to it."

"Belongs to it how?" Quentin asked. Her voice had changed — flatter, more careful. Tracker's voice. The voice of a woman recalculating a route.

"The law is simple," Maren Oduya said. "Simpler than any law your commissions ever wrote. *Those who take from the source are kept by it.* Take its water, take its stone, take its names, take its dead — whatever you carry away from that spring, you carry as its property. It will come for what's its. Maybe slow. Maybe fast. But the river forgives nothing and forgets less—" she tipped her chin at Sefu, who inclined his head as though receiving a compliment, "—and it holds on."

The rain started then, right on cue, drumming on the tin roof she must have pulled from the flood herself, and I swear the sound of it was different inside that house. Heavier. Like listening through a wall to something pacing.

"That's a story," I said, and hated how thin my voice came out. "A useful story. Scare the surveyors off. I understand why you'd—"

"Do I look like I need to scare surveyors?" she asked mildly. "My village is already underwater, girl. What worse could your kind do to me?" She let that sit. "I tell you the law because you asked, and because you stole the book, and because a woman who steals a book out of fear is dangerous, but a woman who steals a book out of guilt is *useful*. Fear runs out. Guilt compounds."

I looked at my hands. Surveyor's hands, ink-stained, steady enough to draw a contour line that would decide whether four hundred people kept their homes. I had signed those drawings. I had taken the Trust's wage and drawn the Trust's lines and told myself maps were neutral, that lines on paper weren't choices, that I was just describing the world, not sentencing it.

Then I'd read the journal, and the journal described a future, and futures are never neutral.

"The second team," Quentin said. "Vane sent another team up ahead of us. Rook's people. Do you know where they crossed?"

"Two days ahead of you, maybe three now. They bought fuel at the dock and didn't buy so much as a word of greeting." Maren Oduya's mouth tightened. "The one with the jaw asked me about the source valley. Asked me like I was simple. I told him the truth."

"And?"

"And he wrote it down in his little book, under *local superstition*, and ordered his men back to the boat." She almost smiled. "Men like that think a thing written down is a thing controlled. Your Trust especially. They write and write and write. Surveys, charters, memoranda, minutes." She gestured at my pack. "And then along comes a thief with a knife and a grudge, and all their writing fits in one oilcloth bundle, and suddenly they're sending boats and guns up a river that has drowned everything they ever built on it." She picked up her grandson's photograph again. "The river reads better than they do."

* * *

We stayed the night on her shelf of dry ground, because the rain showed no interest in stopping and Sefu refused — absolutely, contemptuously refused — to pole the Upper Fly in a night storm. "The river forgives nothing," he reminded us, settling into his spot under the boat's awning like a man taking his rightful throne. "Why would I test the policy?"

I didn't sleep. I lay in the boat listening to the rain and thinking about compounding.

Guilt compounds. That's what she'd said, and she was right, and she was wrong, because guilt doesn't compound like interest — it compounds like floodwater. It starts below the waterline. A forged expense report, a contour line drawn to please a supervisor, a memo you initial without reading. Then one day you look up and there's a bell tower standing in a street where children used to run, and the water is at your chest, and you realize it didn't rise all at once. It rose every time you chose the easy line. Every time you told yourself paper isn't real. Every time you described the world instead of defending it.

I had thought I was running from the Trust. Lying in that boat, I understood I was running toward something — upstream, toward the source, toward the place where all my drawn lines began. Every contour I'd ever plotted for the Amberdale Trust ultimately referenced the headwaters. Every number in every memorandum flowed down from that spring like everything else. If I wanted to find the first page of my own confession, that was where it was kept.

*Those who take from the source are kept by it.*

I had taken. Not water, not stone — worse. I had taken measurements. I had taken the shape of a living thing and flattened it onto vellum so that men in offices could decide its fate with a stamp. If the old woman's law was true, then I had been kept for years already; I just hadn't known my keeper's name.

Somewhere past midnight, Quentin stirred in the bow. I heard her check her rifle's action — a small, oiled sound, habitual as breathing — and then her voice, low, pitched for me alone across the dark boat.

"You believe her? The keeper business."

I thought about it honestly, which was becoming an unpleasant habit around that woman.

"I believe she believes it," I said. "And I believe the Trust's second team is three days upstream with guns and a man who writes 'superstition' in his notebook, and I believe Imogen Hale's expedition went silent up there, and I believe that village didn't flood by accident." I shifted my pack under my head. The journal pressed against my skull like a second conscience. "Whether the keeper is a spirit or a woman with a spear or just the river itself doing what rivers do — I believe something up there is keeping accounts. And I believe we're all of us in arrears."

A long silence. Rain on tin, rain on water, rain on the drowned roofs across the channel.

"That's the first honest thing you've said since I caught you," Quentin said.

"It's the first honest thing I've said in about three years," I told her. "Don't get used to it."

But she laughed — quiet, surprised, real — and I lay back down and stared at the awning, and I made myself a promise in the dark, the kind of promise thieves make precisely because we know how easily promises get stolen.

Whatever was at the source — keeper, spring, or grave — I was going to see it with my own eyes. And whatever the journal cost me in the end, I would not draw one more line for men who thought a thing written down was a thing controlled.

The rain eased near dawn. And when we pushed off into the brown current, Maren Oduya stood on her shelf of dry ground and called out one last thing across the water, in a voice that carried the way old voices do when they've decided to be heard:

"When you meet the keeper — and you will — remember that the river brought you. Nothing comes to that spring by accident. Ask it what it wants of you. And for once in your clever lives — *answer*."

Sefu poled us into the current. Quentin scanned the banks. And I sat in the stern with a stolen confession on my back, watching the drowned bell tower slide away astern, its silent bell hanging over water that had risen without permission and never once apologized —

— and three bends upstream, faint but unmistakable, we heard an engine.
