## 7. Radio Silence

The camp announced itself before we saw it — the smell of wet canvas gone to rot, and under that, faintly, the chemical ghost of fuel. Sefu cut the engine and let the boat drift into the bank on its own current, the way he did everything, as if the river were a horse that spooked at sudden hands.

"Camp's been dead a while," he said.

"I noticed."

"You noticed the smell. I'm telling you what it means." He nodded upstream, toward a bend where the jungle leaned in close over the water like men reading over someone's shoulder. "Dead camps out here mean one of two things. Either the people left in a hurry, or they didn't leave at all."

"And you can tell which?"

"Not from the smell." He looked at me with the mild patience of a man who has spent sixty years being asked obvious questions by people who think they're clever. "From the birds."

I listened. The canopy was loud — the usual argumentative racket of the Upper Fly, parrots and something larger and more opinionated deeper in. But nothing near the camp itself. No birds at all in the trees directly overhead, which is the kind of absence I'd spent twenty years learning to read in the desert, where silence is a language and every dialect means *something is wrong*.

In the Redglass Flats, an empty stretch of sand meant the vultures had already eaten. Here it meant the jungle was holding its breath.

We tied off and went up the bank single file, Sefu first because he knew where the ground lied about being solid, Wren second because she'd stopped arguing about protocol somewhere around the Serrated Reach, and me last because I like a rear guard position and because I've never trusted anyone else's back as far as my own.

The camp sat on a shelf of ground above a side channel: three tents, two still standing in the boneless way of canvas that's given up, one collapsed entirely. A cook fire long drowned. Crates stacked under a tarp that had become more tarp-adjacent than tarp. And there, lashed to a frame of cut saplings, the radio.

Or what had been the radio.

I've seen storm damage. I grew up in country where hail falls like thrown gravel and lightning treats power lines as personal enemies. Storms break things indiscriminately — a mast snapped mid-pole, a casing cracked, water in the works. What storms don't do is *specific*. What storms don't do is pick up a radio transceiver weighing eleven kilos and bring it down against a rock face hard enough to drive the tuning dial into the chassis like a fist through a window, then do it again, and again, until there was nothing left in the box worth breaking.

Someone had done that. Someone had taken their own lifeline — the one object in this wilderness that connected them to doctors, resupply, extraction, the whole breathing world — and murdered it with a rock.

Wren crouched beside the wreckage without touching it. "That's not rage," she said quietly. "Look at the placement. Every strike is on the transmitter housing. The receiver's intact. If you were just furious, you'd hit whatever was closest. This person wanted to make sure nobody could *call out*." She glanced up at me. "Or call for help they didn't want coming."

"Vane's help," Sefu said, and spat, with the economy of a man who'd already made up his mind days ago and saw no reason to reopen the file.

I stood there with rain starting to stipple the canopy overhead and felt the two halves of me pull in opposite directions like a rope under load. Because Wren was right — this was deliberate, considered, almost surgical — and deliberate meant Imogen had done it herself, or stood watch while someone she trusted did it, which meant she was alive three weeks ago and making decisions. That was the hope half, and it hit me in the sternum like a dropped anchor.

The terror half was quieter and worse: deliberate also meant she'd known exactly who would come looking when the calls stopped. She'd known Vane would send someone. She'd known he'd send *me*, maybe — no. Not that. She couldn't have known that. Seven years is long enough to assume you've been forgotten, and I'd earned the assumption.

Except the crate said otherwise.

It was the last thing I found, which is how these things always work — the important detail hiding behind the boring ones while you inventory tent pegs. I went through the camp the way I go through everything, slow and unglamorous, lifting the collapsed tent flap and finding it empty except for mud and one boot, size nine, left foot, heel worn on the outside edge the way boots wear when their owner walks with her weight rolled slightly inward.

Imogen walked like that. I used to tease her about it. *You walk like you're apologizing to the ground.* She'd said she wasn't apologizing, she was listening. Said the desert talked through your feet if you shut up long enough to feel it. I'd laughed, back then, because I was twenty-nine and thought duty was a ladder instead of a debt.

One boot. Left foot. Which meant either she'd lost it or she hadn't needed pairs anymore, and both possibilities sat in my chest like swallowed stones.

Then the crate. Standard Trust issue, stenciled AMBERDALE SURVEY EXPEDITION along one side, and across the lid, scratched deep with something sharp — a knife point, probably, worked over and over so the letters wouldn't weather away smooth:

**DON'T TRUST VANE. TURN BACK.**

Not written. Scratched. Cut into the wood like she wanted it to outlast the wood.

I put my hand flat on the lid, over the letters, the way you'd check a fever. The grain was rough under my palm and the rain was coming harder now, drumming the tarp, filling the world with white noise, and I stood in an abandoned camp in a drowned green country seven thousand kilometers from any desert I understood and felt my whole professional composure come apart at the seams.

Because it was her hand. I'd know it anywhere. Imogen Hale wrote the way she did everything — fast, slanted forward, impatient with ornamentation, every letter leaning into the next like each word was trying to catch up with the one ahead of it. I'd read her field notes for two years. I'd read a letter she wrote me once, the only one, which I still had in a waterproof sleeve in the bottom of my pack like a relic I was too stubborn to be embarrassed about. Same lean. Same urgency. Same woman, cutting her warning into a supply crate for whoever came next.

For me. Maybe for me specifically. There was no name after TURN BACK, but the message had been left facing the river approach, angled so a boat landing at the bank would see it first thing, and Vane's people would have come by air or come heavy, and neither of those lands quiet at a bank. Only a tracker comes up that way. Only a tracker reads scratches on crates.

She was thinking of whoever came. And some traitor part of me, the part I keep locked up and fed sparingly, insisted on doing the arithmetic anyway: *she knows you'd be the one Vane sends.*

"Don't," I said aloud, to myself, and Wren looked over from the radio wreckage with an expression I couldn't parse in the gloom.

"Don't what?"

"Nothing. Nothing." I took my hand off the crate. "We should look at the rest of it. Tracks, sign, anything that tells us which way they went and how many."

Sefu had drifted to the edge of the shelf, studying the side channel with his hands behind his back like a man inspecting a garden. "Three weeks dead," he called back. "Maybe four. The tarp rotted through from the top, not the sides — nobody folded it when they left. People leaving in a hurry fold things. Habit. You fold even when you're running." He turned. "Nobody folded anything here. They walked out standing up, in daylight, taking what they wanted."

"Which direction?"

He pointed up the side channel, toward the wall of the gorge, where the trees climbed in terraces and the air had that mineral coldness that comes off deep stone. "Up. Toward the high country." His mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "Toward the source, if you believe old Maren Oduya. Toward the place strangers get kept."

* * *

We made camp that night under the expedition's surviving tarp, which felt like trespassing and probably was, and I took first watch because I don't sleep well in other people's abandoned lives.

The rain had settled into its long monsoon rehearsal — not the real landfall yet, just the sky clearing its throat — and I sat with my back against the murdered radio's frame and listened to the river work in the dark. It never stops talking, the Fly. In the desert, silence is the default and sound is the event. Out here it's inverted; the noise is constant and the meaning hides inside it, and I'm still illiterate. Sefu can hear a rapid change its mind a kilometer off. Wren can tell floodwater from rain-swollen current by the pitch. I can track a man across bare rock in a dust storm, and in this country I'm a child sounding out words.

Around midnight, Wren couldn't sleep either. She came and sat a few feet away, knees up, arms wrapped around them, and for a long time neither of us said anything, which is the kind of company I actually prefer.

"He smashed it himself," she said finally. "Hale. Didn't she."

"The strikes were right-handed and low. She's right-handed." I paused. "And she's shorter than most of her team. The height of the impact marks fits her."

"You know a lot about her."

"I know her handwriting and her boot size and which foot she rolls onto when she walks. That's not knowing someone. That's an evidence file."

"It sounded like more than an evidence file."

I let the river answer for a while. Then: "Seven years ago I had a choice between her and my commission. I chose the commission. Vane made sure of it — he had leverage, and I let him use it, because I told myself the work mattered more. Finding people. Bringing them home. You can dress up almost anything as a duty if you try hard enough." I picked a splinter of radio casing out of the mud and turned it over. "So yes. I know a lot about her. None of it does me any good now except to confirm she's alive and she hates the same man I'm starting to hate, and honestly, Wren, that's more than I had yesterday."

Wren was quiet. When she spoke again her voice had lost its usual barbed edge. "The message says turn back."

"I saw."

"It doesn't say *help*. It doesn't say *come find me*. It says turn back." She turned to look at me, and even in the dark I could feel the weight of it. "Imogen Hale doesn't want to be rescued. She wants to be *left alone*, up there, wherever she is. She broke her own radio so Vane couldn't send anyone. And then Vane sent you anyway. So what happens when you find her? You going to drag her home to face him? Because that's what he's paying for."

"That's what he's paying for," I agreed.

"But?"

"But I haven't decided whose money this is yet." I flicked the splinter into the dark. "Go sleep, Calloway. Tomorrow we climb."

She went. I stayed on watch, and somewhere past two in the morning I took out the letter — the only letter, seven years old, waterproof sleeve soft with handling — and read it by feel more than light. *If you ever stop choosing the job,* it ended, *you know where the water is.* I'd assumed she meant the coast. Some town with a harbor. Some life.

Sitting in her ruined camp with her warning cut into a crate three feet away, I understood, with the slow cold certainty of a flood rising, that she'd meant this water. This river. She'd known even then where she'd end up, and she'd left the door open the exact width of a sentence, and I'd spent seven years not walking through it.

The birds started up again near dawn — first the small ones, then the big opinionated ones, the canopy reopening like a fist unclenching. Alive again. Whatever had held its breath had moved on.

Sefu stood at the bank, looking up at the gorge wall, where the terraces of stone rose into cloud.

"Keeper country starts past that ridge," he said. "After that, we're not tracking anymore. We're being tracked." He glanced back at me, and for the first time since Marrow Bend the old pilot looked genuinely uneasy. "And Miss Underhill — whatever's watching us up there? It already knows whose camp this was. Question is whether it thinks we came to take something."

I looked once more at the scratched crate, at the letters leaning into each other like words chasing each other uphill.

*Turn back.*

Sorry, Imogen. I never have learned to follow that particular instruction — not yours, not anyone's. But I promise you this: I'm not coming the way Vane sent me.

I'm coming the way the letter said.
