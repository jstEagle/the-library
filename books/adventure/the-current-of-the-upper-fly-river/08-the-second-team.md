## 8. The Second Team

I had been a soldier for nineteen years and a captain for six, and in all that time I had learned one lesson above all others: the people who cause problems are almost never the ones you expect. It's the quiet ones. The competent ones. The woman standing at the rail of a flatboat with her hands folded like she's waiting for a train.

Quentin Underhill. The Trust's file on her was thin and I'd read it four times between Marrow Bend and the Serrated Reach, which tells you something about how entertaining I find my own company. Desert ranger. Tracker of some renown. A commendation here, a disciplinary note there, a resignation letter dated seven years back that someone at the Trust had apparently talked her out of, because there she was, back on the payroll, doing our dirty work up a river that eats engines and spits out propellers.

The file said she was good. The river was about to tell me how good.

* * *

We caught sight of her boat at midday, or rather we caught sight of the smoke from her boat, a gray thread rising out of the green wall of the bank where the Upper Fly bends north around a spur of black rock the charts called, with a poetry I did not appreciate, the Widow's Tooth. My pilot, a nervous man named Okonkwo who had the unfortunate habit of narrating his own fears, pointed with a finger that was not entirely steady.

"There, Captain. That'll be them."

"That'll be them," I agreed, and put the binoculars up.

The flatboat was beached on a gravel bar, half in the shallows, and three figures were moving along the bank. I picked them out one by one, the way you do, cataloguing threats. An old riverman — Sefu Marr, per the manifest I'd lifted from the Marrow Bend harbor office, a pilot with a reputation for being unbuyable, which I intended to test. A younger woman, wiry, dark-haired, moving like someone who expected to be shot at from the reeds. Wren Calloway, the thief herself, right there in the open, which meant either stupidity or a bodyguard.

And the third figure, standing at the waterline with her boots in the current, watching our boat come around the bend. Not hiding. Not running. Watching.

She raised one hand — not a wave, exactly. More like a man hailing a taxi. Come on, then, the gesture said. I've been expecting you.

I have been called many things in my career, most of them behind my back and all of them accurate, but nobody has ever called me a fool. I knew a trap when I saw one shaped like an invitation. I also knew I had orders, and orders from Aldous Vane have a particular flavor — they arrive wrapped in civility and they taste of iron.

"Take us in," I said.

"Captain, that bar's got maybe a meter under the keel, and the current through there—"

"Take us in, Mr. Okonkwo. If we ground, we ground. If we're meant to drown, no amount of your caution will save us."

He took us in. We grounded, briefly, embarrassingly, and came off the bar with the engine screaming, and by the time we warped alongside the gravel bar Underhill had not moved an inch. She was shorter than I'd imagined, broader through the shoulders, sunburned in the way of someone who has spent her life outdoors and never once thought to apologize for it. Her eyes went over my boat, my men, my weapons, in about two seconds, and I watched her arrive at conclusions I would have paid good money to read.

"Captain Rook," I said, and offered my name the way you offer a warrant.

"Quentin Underhill." She didn't offer her hand. "You're a long way from Marrow Bend."

"We could say the same of you. You're a long way from your contract, Ms. Underhill. The Trust engaged you to retrieve a stolen journal and a missing surveyor. Instead I find you traveling upstream in the company of the thief, at a pace that suggests you're in no hurry to find anyone." I let that sit. "Vane wants an explanation."

"Vane can want things," she said. "It's a free country, more or less."

One of my men — Deverell, a big stupid reliable piece of equipment — shifted his weight, and Underhill's eyes flicked to him and back to me with the unhurried contempt of a cat watching a dog decide whether to bark. Behind her, the thief had drifted toward the tree line, and the old pilot had drifted toward his boat, and between the three of them they had arranged the geometry of the encounter so that any violence would begin at a disadvantage. I noted it. I note everything. It's the only talent I've ever had that I'm proud of.

"Here's how I understand the situation," I said. "You have the thief. The Trust wants the thief. You will hand her over, you will return downstream, and you will be paid in full, plus whatever Vane calls a bonus this quarter. In exchange, I don't write a report describing how the Trust's own tracker went native on the second week. Everyone keeps their commission. Everyone keeps their pension. The river stays beautiful and we all go home."

"You've rehearsed that," Underhill said.

"On the boat. It's a long boat."

"It's a bad speech." She looked past me at the water, at the black spur of the Widow's Tooth, at the green country climbing away toward the gorge, and something moved through her face like weather. "There's an expedition upriver, Captain. Three weeks silent. A woman named Imogen Hale. Does your briefing include her, or does Vane only tell his people the parts that fit?"

I confess the name did something to the air between us. I had read the file. I knew who Hale was, and I knew — because Vane had told me over brandy in a room with the windows shut, in the tone of a man discussing a leak in a roof — that the expedition's silence was not entirely an inconvenience. Some silences are purchased.

"Dr. Hale's welfare is the Trust's concern," I said.

"Is it." Not a question. A weighing.

"Ms. Underhill. I have eight men, a patrol boat, a radio, and a charter signed by the colonial water authority. You have a flatboat, a felon, and a man old enough to be your grandfather. Whatever you think you're doing up this river, it ends here, and it ends politely, or it ends the other way."

She considered me for a long moment, and I want to be honest in this account, because honesty is the one luxury this river has left me. In that moment I did not dislike her. I recognized her. She was doing what I do — measuring, weighing, deciding how much of the truth a situation could carry without splitting at the seams. The difference, I would learn too late, is that she was measuring for something larger than a patrol boat's reach.

"Give me until the squall passes," she said, and nodded at the sky, where a bruise-colored cloud was building over the western ridge with the patience of something that has all the time in the world. "Nobody moves a boat in that. You know it and Okonkwo knows it. Tomorrow morning, I'll give you your answer."

"Tonight," I said. "Dawn, if you like. But you'll give it from my deck."

"Fine." She turned, then stopped, and looked back at me with an expression I have thought about every day since. Pity, maybe. Or a warning delivered in the only language she had. "Captain. Ask Vane what happened to the first survey team. The one before Hale's. Ask him why the charts stop at the gorge."

"I don't take instructions from fugitives."

"No," she said. "You take them from men who pay you to stop asking questions. I used to be one of those. It doesn't wear well."

She walked up the gravel bar and into the trees, and Calloway and the old man folded in around her like a hand closing, and I stood on my own deck in my own command and felt, for the first time in six years as a captain, the specific chill of being the one who is being managed.

* * *

I anchored in the lee of the Tooth, because Okonkwo was right about the squall if wrong about everything else, and I spent the evening doing what I do best, which is arithmetic. Eight men against three. A patrol boat against a flatboat. A charter against — what? Against a tracker's word. Against a thief's. Against whatever was waiting upriver in the green dark that had swallowed one expedition and, by Underhill's tone, swallowed one before that.

I did not like the arithmetic. Not because it was unfavorable — it wasn't, on paper — but because it kept refusing to stay on paper. Every time I summed it, some new term crept in. The smashed radio at the abandoned camp my scouts had found two days prior, smashed from the inside, deliberately, with a boot heel. The message scratched into the supply crate, which my corporal had copied out for me in his terrible hand: DON'T TRUST VANE. TURN BACK. The fact that Vane had sent me upriver three days *after* Underhill, on a boat that could not catch her, with orders that amounted to *make sure she doesn't come back with the wrong ideas*.

I am not a sentimental man. I have never once confused my employers with my friends. But there is a difference between serving a man and being used by him, and the distinction, I find, tends to arrive at the moment you notice the shape of the tool you've become.

The squall came down off the ridge around midnight, and it came the way the river does everything — without negotiation. Rain like gravel, wind that walked my boat sideways on its anchor, lightning turning the gorge walls into brief white negatives of themselves. I put double watches on the fuel cache we'd staged on the bank — forty drums of diesel, the whole expedition's reach, the thing that made us masters of this stretch of water — and I went below and did not sleep, because I have never slept well on the eve of a decision, and I knew, in the way you know a bone is broken before the swelling comes, that a decision was coming.

* * *

They hit us at two in the morning, or I should say *it* hit us, because I never saw more than one silhouette, and I have my suspicions — which I will keep to myself, since this account is the only weapon I have left — that the silhouette was not Underhill at all but the old pilot, who moved through that storm with the unhurried familiarity of a man walking his own hallway.

What I know is this. The sentry on the fuel cache — Brandt, a decent soldier, a poor sentry — was found at dawn lashed to a tree with his own belt, gagged with his own sock, unharmed except in his dignity, and the forty drums were open. Not blown up. Not burned. Opened, bled into the storm-swollen river, forty drums of diesel unspooling into the current in a silver ribbon that the rain washed flat before the sun was up. The caps were lined up on a rock in a neat row. Somebody had taken the trouble to line up the caps.

I stood on the bank in the gray morning with my boots in the mud and understood, with a clarity that felt almost like being struck, what I was looking at. Not sabotage. Not an escape. A message, written in the only language that could reach me. *You are not going up this river on fuel. You are going up this river on will, and I am telling you now, before you spend it, that you do not have enough.*

Deverell wanted to burn the flatboat. Okonkwo wanted to go home. My corporal wanted, in the way of corporals, whatever I wanted, which is the most dangerous want in any army.

And I stood there in the rain-washed morning and found that I was not angry.

That frightened me more than anger would have. Because what I felt, looking at that silver ribbon of my expedition's reach dissolving into the river, was a kind of recognition. Underhill had read my orders the way I read a battlefield — correctly, coldly, all the way down to the ugly bottom — and she had answered them not with a bullet but with an arithmetic of her own. She was telling me the cost. She was giving me the chance to be the kind of man who takes a hint.

I have thought about that morning many times since, from my current accommodations, which are not improved ones, and I have tried to locate the exact moment when the hint became instead a challenge. It was not dramatic. There was no lightning, no revelation. I simply stood there with the river running silver and diesel past my boots, and I heard Aldous Vane's voice saying *the source, Captain, at any cost, the source is worth more than the men who find it* — and instead of hearing the warning in it, I heard the offer.

Any cost. Whose cost, Vane had not said. I had assumed he meant mine. Standing on that bank, I understood he had never once meant his own, and something in me — some cold little pilot light I had kept burning through nineteen years of other people's wars — flared up and said: *then the cost will be mine to set.*

"Corporal," I said. "Break out the reserve drums in the hold. We have enough for the gorge and back, if we don't idle. We go upriver."

"Captain, the flatboat—"

"Let them go. We know where they're going. There's only one source." I looked up the river, at the green country closing over the water like a door, at the rain still falling out of a sky that had not finished with us, and I felt the obsession settle into me the way a splinter works into a palm — small, painless at the entry, and destined to fester. "And when we find them, Corporal, we are going to have a conversation about property."

* * *

We went upriver. Of course we went upriver. You know this already, or you would not be reading this account, and I will not pretend the decision was harder than it was. The truth is that some men are built to turn back, and some are built to keep going, and the river — which forgives nothing and forgets less, as the old pilot kept saying, as if repetition could make it a proverb instead of a threat — the river does not care which kind you are. It only keeps the difference in its ledgers.

I did not know then what the ledgers held. I did not know that the gorge ahead of us had walls carved with the names of dead keepers, or that the woman I'd been sent to retrieve had already stopped being retrievable, or that the journal in Wren Calloway's pack contained the one document in the world that could make Aldous Vane's empire die of the truth.

I knew only this: somewhere above the Serrated Reach, in a country where the charts went blank and the rain was coming early, three people were climbing toward a spring that a man with a charter wanted to drown. And I had fuel enough to reach them, and orders that fit inside a brandy glass, and a cold little flame in my chest that Vane had lit without ever once striking a match.

The river rose all around us as we climbed, brown and patient and full of everything it had ever been given.

It was, I understand now, already counting.
