## 9. Monsoon Signs

The first sign was the birds. Not the absence of them — I'd been told to expect that, the way you're told a rattler shakes before it strikes — but the direction of them. All morning they'd been moving downstream in ragged, purposeful skeins, like commuters who'd heard the trains were stopping. I'd spent twenty years reading the desert, where the sky tells you almost nothing and the ground tells you everything. Here the ground was a rumor under a hundred feet of green, and the sky was a broadsheet screaming the news to anyone literate enough to read it.

I wasn't literate enough. Not yet.

"Four days," Sefu said.

He said it the way a man announces a funeral, without drama, without hope of appeal. He was standing in the bow of his flatboat with his face tipped up, eyes half-shut, doing whatever it was he did up there. Smelling, maybe. Listening. The old pilot had spent the better part of two weeks teaching me the river by demonstration and contempt in roughly equal measure, and this was the first time he'd offered a forecast without me asking.

"Four days until what?" Wren asked. She was crouched over the outboard, doing something to it that I pretended not to be worried about.

"Until the sky comes down." Sefu lowered his chin and looked at us both. "Monsoon. Early. The river told me three days ago. The birds told me this morning. Now the water's telling me, and the water is never wrong."

"The water told you," I said. "The water."

"Girl, I have been reading this river since before your mother was a disappointment to somebody. The water speaks. You just haven't learned the language." He tapped the gunwale with one bare foot. "The current's gone lazy. Sluggish. It's backing up in the bends like it's holding its breath. That's the upper country filling. When the monsoon lands, everything between here and the gorge becomes a lake with opinions."

I looked at the river. It looked like a river. Brown-green, patient, carrying its freight of leaves and secrets. I looked at the sky, which looked like a sky, gray wool stacked to the horizon. In the Redglass Flats, a gray sky meant nothing at all; you could stand under one for a month and never feel a drop. I was, I realized with a sensation like a stone dropping into still water, completely out of my jurisdiction.

"Four days," I said. "To the gorge?"

"On the water, maybe. If the water lets us." Sefu's mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "But the water will not let us. Not now."

Wren looked up from the outboard. "You want to go overland."

"I want to live," Sefu said. "Overland is how we do it."

* * *

I want to be clear about what abandoning the boat meant, because at the time I did not understand it, and my failure to understand it nearly killed all three of us.

That boat was our engine, our bed, our fortress, our pantry. It was the last piece of civilization we had, if you could call a flat-bottomed scow with a temperamental outboard civilization. On the river, we were fast. In the jungle, we would be slow, blind, and edible. Every instinct I had — every instinct a desert ranger has, which is to say every instinct shaped by open ground and long sightlines — said stay with the boat.

Sefu beached it in a stand of bamboo above a shingle bar, ran a line up to a root the size of my thigh, and began unbuckling a pack that looked like it had been packed for exactly this contingency since roughly the invention of packs.

"You're just leaving it?" Wren said.

"I'm leaving it *here*," Sefu said. "The river will keep it. The river keeps everything. That's the whole problem with the river."

We took what we could carry: rice and dried fish in oilcloth, a tarp, rope, the medical kit, Sefu's machete, my rifle, Wren's pack with its terrible cargo. The journal. I'd taken to thinking of it as a live grenade she was carrying against her spine. Maybe that was unfair. Everything about this trip was unfair, starting with the fact that I'd taken the job.

We went up the bank and the jungle closed behind us like water.

* * *

Here is what I knew about jungle before that day: approximately nothing. Here is what I thought I knew: it's like a desert but wet. I would like to formally apologize to the desert, which I have slandered by comparison.

The Redglass Flats taught you to read a landscape the way you read a page — sparse text, wide margins, every mark deliberate. A broken scrub stem. A beetle track fanned by a boot heel. Sand holds a story for a day, two if the wind's kind, and I had made my living as the fastest reader in a very slow library.

The jungle was not a page. The jungle was a thousand pages being written and erased simultaneously by ten thousand authors who hated readers. Everything grew, everything rotted, everything moved. The ground itself was soft, a sponge of root and leaf mold that took a footprint and healed it in minutes. My tracking skills — the skills that had a reputation, that had gotten me this cursed job — were worth approximately as much here as a sun-dial in a cave.

Within two hours I was lost. Not geographically — Sefu had the compass and the bearing and I trusted him the way you trust a lighthouse — but professionally. Existentially. I did not know where I was in the only language I'd ever been fluent in.

Wren, meanwhile, was having a fine time.

"See this?" She crouched by a game trail, pointing at a smear of mud on a root at knee height. "Pig. Small one, came through this morning. And this—" she moved two meters, touched a broken fern, "—that's not broken, that's *browsed*. Different thing. Deer. You can tell because—"

"Because the stem's cut clean, not snapped."

Wren blinked at me. "Okay. So you're not hopeless."

"I'm a ranger, not a tourist."

"You're a *desert* ranger." She said it without malice, which somehow made it worse. "Out here you're a tourist with a rifle."

She was right, and being right, she got to be insufferable about it, and I let her, because the alternative was dying stupidly. That was the arithmetic of the jungle: humility or death, and it collected daily.

* * *

The leeches deserve their own paragraph, and possibly their own chapter, and possibly their own religion.

They were in the leaf mold, in the water, on the leaves — *on the leaves*, hanging there like small dark commas waiting to punctuate you. They found the gap between boot and trouser cuff with a precision I found almost flattering. By the first evening I had eleven of them. Wren had four. Sefu had none, and when I asked him how, he said, "They know better," and declined to elaborate.

Wren taught me the salt trick, and the flick trick, and the trick where you don't scratch, ever, no matter what your skin is telling you, because scratching turns a leech bite into a sore and a sore into an infection and an infection, out here, into a story Sefu would tell other pilots about the idiot desert ranger. I learned them all. I learned them with the specific gracelessness of a proud woman being taught to walk.

"You know," Wren said that first night, while I salted my ankles by firelight and tried to preserve some shred of dignity, "in the Trust's files, you're listed as 'the finest tracker in the territory.' Vane said it himself. I read your file before I ran."

"You read my file before you robbed my client."

"Before I robbed my *employer*," she corrected. "There's a difference. And I remember thinking, fine, let her come, let the famous tracker come. I'd outrun her in a week." She poked the fire. "I didn't figure on the river changing the rules. Or on you being willing to lose."

"I'm not losing."

"No," she agreed. "You're *learning*. It looks the same from the outside. It feels very different from in here." She tapped her own chest. "From out here it looks like losing."

I didn't have an answer for that, so I salted another leech bite and watched the dark close in around our fire like a crowd around a street fight.

* * *

The second day, the rain came early — not the monsoon, just its advance scouts, a warm hammering downpour that turned the trail into a stream and the stream into a torrent and taught us all, in about forty minutes, why Sefu had wanted altitude. We climbed. The jungle climbed with us, indifferent. My boots, which had crossed the Redglass Flats without complaint, began to rot. I mean that literally. The stitching gave up. By evening I was wrapping them with strips of tarp, and Sefu watched me do it and said nothing, and his saying nothing was the loudest pity I have ever heard.

That night I sat my watch and listened to the jungle breathe and thought about Imogen.

Seven years. Seven years since I'd stood in her doorway in Marrow Bend with my commission in one hand and my cowardice in the other and chosen the commission. Vane hadn't even had to threaten much. He'd just made it clear that the Trust's goodwill kept the ranger service funded, and that the ranger service was the only thing I had, and that a woman who chose a surveyor over the Trust would find her world getting smaller one permit at a time. I'd told myself it was duty. Duty is such a useful word. It's the word you use for the thing you were too afraid to want.

And now I was slogging through a drowned green hell toward her — or toward the place where she'd stopped answering radios — carrying a stolen journal and a thief I was starting to trust and an old pilot who talked to rivers, and the whole ridiculous caravan had been set in motion by a man who I was now nearly certain had sent a second team upstream to do something worse than find her.

I thought about the message on the crate. DON'T TRUST VANE. TURN BACK. She'd scratched it knowing who might come looking. Knowing *I* might come looking. Seven years of silence, and her first word to me was a warning.

I found, sitting there in the dark with the rain ticking through the canopy, that I was smiling. It felt like a fever.

* * *

On the third day I earned back a little of myself, and I want to record how, because it mattered.

We crossed a saddle of broken limestone above a side gorge, and below us, in a fold of the green, I saw what my desert eyes were actually built for: not tracks, but *absence*. A line where the birds didn't go. A hush in the insect noise, a corridor of quiet maybe thirty meters wide, running wrong — running against the grain of the terrain, where no game trail had any business being.

"Stop," I said.

Sefu stopped. Wren stopped.

"Something's cleared a path down there. Recently. The birds won't cross it." I pointed. "That's not a trail. That's a patrol route. Somebody's cutting lines of sight along this ridge."

Sefu studied the fold for a long moment. Then he looked at me differently — the way you look at a mule that has suddenly done arithmetic. "Rook's people," he said. "Or Vane's. They're moving toward the gorge from the east."

"Ahead of us," Wren said. Her face had gone the color of old ash. "They're ahead of us."

"They're ahead of *us*," I said, "and they don't know we're behind them, and right now that's the only advantage we have in the entire world. So we go quiet, we go slow, and we go around."

We went around. It cost us half a day and most of my remaining dignity, because going around meant a descent through a ravine that was less a ravine than a vertical riverbed, and I slid the last twenty meters on my back like a turtle on a waxed floor. But we went around unseen, and when Sefu looked at me that evening and said, "Good eyes, ranger," I understood that I had just been graded, and passed, in a school whose curriculum I had not known existed a week ago.

The desert hadn't made me useless. It had made me a reader of absence — and the jungle, it turned out, was mostly absence, wearing a costume of noise. The birds that didn't sing. The mud that didn't show tracks. The quiet where the quiet should have been loud. I could work with that. I could work with anything, if I stopped insisting it be the desert.

* * *

The fourth day, the sky made good on its threat.

We felt it before we saw it — a pressure drop you felt in your teeth, in the old break in my collarbone, in the way the fire wouldn't catch that morning. Sefu stood on a granite shoulder above the treeline, sniffed the wind from the south, and said one word: "Tomorrow."

"Yesterday you said four days."

"Yesterday was yesterday. The storm found its legs." He turned. "We make the gorge mouth tonight or we make it never. After landfall, these valleys drown. Everything low goes under. Everything steep comes down." He meant landslides. He said it the way you'd say *weather*.

We moved. God, how we moved. Wren set a pace that shamed me and I matched it out of pure spite, and Sefu glided along behind us both like a man out for a stroll through his own garden, because that's what it was — his garden, his river's garden, and we were two tourists and a desert rat being walked through it by the only native in the party.

Near dusk, the trees broke.

The gorge opened below us like a wound in the world — a mile-deep cleft running north into country no map in Marrow Bend rendered as anything but blank paper and a warning. The river we'd abandoned was a brown thread at the bottom, already swollen, already loud. Mist rose out of the depths in slow columns. And on the far walls, catching the last red light, I could see them: pale scars in the black stone, rows of them, regular as script.

"Names," Wren said quietly. She'd seen them too. "The keepers' dead. The gorge of names."

Sefu stood at the edge with the wind pulling at his shirt, and for the first time since I'd met him, the old pilot looked old. "I have crossed that river four hundred times," he said. "I have never gone up there." He nodded at the blank country. "Nobody goes up there who expects to come back the same."

The first fat drops of the monsoon hit the stone around us, one, two, three, like knuckles on a door.

Then the sky opened, and the gorge swallowed the light, and somewhere up in that drowned dark, past the flood and the falling rock and the second team and whatever waited at the source, Imogen Hale was alive — I had decided she was alive, and I have found that a tracker's decisions are the closest thing she has to prayers — and she had no idea that the storm, the Trust, and the only woman who ever left her were all arriving at once.

We went down into the rain.
