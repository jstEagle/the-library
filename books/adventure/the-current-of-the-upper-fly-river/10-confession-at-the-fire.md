## 10. Confession at the Fire

### Wren

The rain started an hour after dark, a fine hissing drizzle that turned the jungle into a listening thing, and Sefu built the fire small and tucked it under the overhang of a fallen strangler fig like a man hiding contraband. Which, I suppose, is what we all were by then.

I sat with my knees drawn up and my pack between my feet, the way I'd been sitting for six days, ever since the dock at Kiln Landing when Underhill stepped out of the reeds with a machete and a question. I'd had a knife too. I'd done the arithmetic on it in about half a second and put both hands up instead. That was the smartest decision I made all year, and I want it noted somewhere that I make them occasionally.

Underhill sat across the fire sharpening nothing in particular. She did that sometimes — drew the stone along a blade that was already keen, just to have her hands busy while her eyes worked. Desert people, Sefu told me once, are like that. They hoard stillness the way river people hoard dry matches.

"You were going to ask," she said, without looking up.

"Was I?"

"You've been winding up to it since the drowned village. You get a line right here—" she touched the space between her own brows— "when you're rehearsing."

I looked at the fire. The rain ticked on the fig leaves overhead, patient as a clerk counting out change. Somewhere downriver, behind us, Captain Rook's patrol was presumably regrouping around the fuel cache we'd ruined, and ahead of us was four days of leech country and a gorge full of dead keepers' names, and I realized with a strange, floating clarity that we were probably all going to die in this valley or the next one, and I did not want the last true thing I said to anyone to be a lie.

"I worked for them," I said. "The Trust. Four years."

Underhill's whetstone stopped moving.

"Junior surveyor, then hydrographic assistant, then surveyor proper. I drew the charts they're so anxious to get back. I measured the Upper Fly's flow rates at eleven stations. I sat in Vane's office while he explained, very reasonably, over very good coffee, that the data would inform 'sustainable infrastructure planning.'" I laughed, and it came out ugly. "Sustainable. You know what they call the reservoir behind the Kessing Dam? On the maps they sell tourists? Lake Progress. There's a village under it. Two villages. Nobody calls them anything anymore."

"You forged the journal," Underhill said. Not accusing. Testing.

"No." I made myself look at her across the fire. "That's the thing you've been circling since Marrow Bend, ranger, so let me save you the trip. I didn't forge a single line of it. The dam schematics are real. The elevation figures are real. The flood-stage projections for the source valley — real, and conservative, because I fudged them downward hoping someone senior would catch the error and I'd get a chance to explain why the numbers should be higher. Nobody caught it. Vane signed off on my work personally. He shook my hand." I held mine up, palm out, as if the handshake might still be visible on it, some brand or residue. "Everything in that journal is true. That's why I took it. You can't discredit a forgery if there isn't one."

The fire popped. Sefu, at the edge of the light with his back against the fig's buttress roots, said nothing, which from Sefu was a whole paragraph.

Underhill set the whetstone down. "Vane told me a thief stole it. He didn't tell me the thief was the surveyor."

"He wouldn't. 'Thief' is a much better word for a woman than 'whistleblower.' Cheaper, too." I pulled my pack around and dug past the rice and the rope and the spare socks until my fingers found the oilcloth bundle, and I held it a moment before I tossed it. It landed in the dirt between us with a soft, final sound, like a verdict. "There. Since we're apparently trading sins tonight."

She didn't touch it. She looked at it the way you look at a snake you've been sent to catch and have just realized is venomous in a way nobody warned you about.

"Why tell me now?" she asked.

"Because tomorrow we climb into a gorge where the walls have names carved in them, and the day after that we meet people who keep a spring their grandmothers' grandmothers kept, and I've decided I'd rather walk in there as a liar who confessed than a liar who didn't get the chance." I shrugged, and it cost more than a shrug usually does. "Also because you scuttled a Trust fuel cache for me, and I've been trying to figure out why, and I can't, unless you already know something's rotten. And if you know something's rotten, then you deserve to know how rotten."

Underhill was quiet for a long time. Long enough that Sefu added a stick to the fire without being asked, which was his way of saying *keep talking, this concerns me too.*

Then she picked up the oilcloth bundle, weighed it in her hand like a stone she was deciding whether to skip, and said: "Seven years ago, I left someone at a bus depot in Marrow Bend because Aldous Vane told me he'd take my commission if I didn't."

And that, I did not expect.

### Quentin

I hadn't planned to say it. I want that on the record, wherever records go when nobody keeps them. But she'd handed me the truth like a live coal, cupped in both hands, and there's only so long you can hold your palms closed around your own lies before somebody notices the smoke.

Wren stared at me. Even Sefu shifted forward out of the dark.

"Someone," she repeated.

"Dr. Imogen Hale." Saying the name out loud, to another person, for the first time in seven years, was like pulling a splinter that had grown a tree around itself. "Limnologist. River systems. She was leading the Trust's source expedition because she was the best freshwater scientist on this side of the range, and Vane knew it, and he also knew she'd already flagged the upper gorge as ecologically irreplaceable. Which is exactly why he wanted her report. A dam proposal needs an environmental assessment, and an assessment signed by Imogen Hale is worth ten signed by anyone else."

"So he sent the woman who'd object," Wren said slowly, "to write the justification."

"He sent her to find the source and measure it. What she wrote after that was supposed to be a formality." I fed the fire a stick, mostly to give my hands something honest to do. "And seven years ago, before any of this, she got a posting offer. Permanent. Field directorate, northern watersheds. She asked me to come with her. Properly asked. Had it all worked out — I could transfer, or resign, or take leave, she'd run the numbers every way there were numbers to run."

"And you said no."

"I said duty first. I said the desert needed me. I said a lot of words that sounded noble because I'd rehearsed them in front of a mirror until they stopped sounding like what they were." The fire cracked. Out in the black, something moved through the wet understory and went still again. "What they were was scared. Vane had called me in two days earlier. Sat me down in that office with the good coffee and told me that rangers who developed entanglements with Trust personnel had a way of finding their commissions 'reviewed.' My commission was everything. I'd clawed up out of nothing to get it. Redglass Flats doesn't forgive much either, whatever Sefu thinks of his river, and a discharged ranger goes back to the Flats with nothing but her boots and her reputation, and reputations rot fast when there's no badge holding them up."

"So you chose the badge."

"I chose the badge." I heard how it sounded. Seven years of hearing how it sounded, and it had never once sounded better out loud. "She stood at that depot window with her bag packed and waited for me to say something else. Anything else. And I gave her the speech, and she got on the boat, and I went back to the dunes and told myself I'd made the strong choice. Then three weeks ago Vane says her name across a desk and I swear to God, Calloway, the room tilted. Like the floor had been waiting seven years to do that."

Wren was quiet. In the firelight her face had lost its usual braced, cornered-animal set, and what was underneath was younger and more tired and, I thought, not so different from mine.

"She doesn't know," she said finally. "About Vane threatening you."

"How would she?"

"Because she spent three weeks upstream thinking you took the job for the money." Wren wrapped her arms around her knees. "I read her file. Everyone at the Trust reads everyone's files, it's practically a hobby. Imogen Hale requested you by name for the retrieval. Vane told her no such request existed, or she assumed it didn't. Either way—" she gestured vaguely at the dark wall of jungle between us and the hidden valley— "she's up there believing the person she loved most sold her out twice. Once at a bus depot and once for double fee."

The words went in under my ribs and stayed there, cold, like river water finding a crack in boot leather.

"I didn't know she requested me," I said.

"I know. That's the worst part." Wren poked the fire, sending up a fistful of sparks that the drizzle killed immediately. "You know what I figured out, sitting in Vane's outer office for four years watching him ruin people politely? Fear doesn't look like fear. It looks like professionalism. It looks like *duty first.* It looks like a tracker taking double fee and telling herself it's just business." She glanced up at me, and there was no cruelty in it, which somehow made it worse. "I'm not exempt. I watched the schematics get drawn and told myself I was just doing my job too. Right up until I read the final flood projections and understood that 'my job' meant putting a hundred meters of water over a living cathedral."

Sefu spoke from the edge of the light, his voice gravel rolled smooth by forty years of current. "The river has a word for this hour. When the fire is low and the rain talks. *Bwawa la moyo.* The pool of the heart. Everything you sank floats up." He nodded at the oilcloth bundle lying in the dirt between Wren and me. "Old man Oduya's grandmother used to say: what you carry secret, carries you."

"That's cheerful," Wren said.

"It is not meant to be cheerful. It is meant to be true." He settled back against the roots. "Sleep when the rain sleeps. Tomorrow the gorge decides what kind of people we are."

He closed his eyes with the terrible ease of a man who had made his peace with rivers, and left the two of us with the fire and the bundle and the things we'd said.

Wren reached over and nudged the oilcloth toward me with one finger. "Keep it tonight."

"You stole it fair and square."

"I stole it to hide it. There's a difference, and I think you're the only person I've met who'd understand it." She lay back against her pack, hat tipped over her eyes, and for a minute I thought she was done. Then, from under the brim: "For what it's worth, ranger — if she'd asked me to choose between the commission and her, I'd have burned the commission. But I've never had one. Easy virtue, standing on the shore."

"It's not easy," I said. "It's the hardest thing there is. That's rather the point."

"Yeah." A pause. Rain on leaves. "Well. Maybe we get a second attempt. Some of us."

I picked up the journal. Through the oilcloth I could feel the geometry of it, the dense rectangular certainty of everything Vane wanted buried — the schematics, the elevations, the flood lines drawn in Wren's careful hand, all of it true, all of it damning. Somewhere above us, past the leech country and the gorge of names, Imogen Hale was alive and hating me on accurate information, and there was nothing in the world I could do about that tonight except carry the truth up to her and see if seven years of silence could survive being contradicted.

I slept badly, which was appropriate, and woke before dawn to Sefu standing at the edge of camp, utterly still, head cocked at the eastern sky.

"What is it?" I whispered.

He didn't turn around. His voice, when it came, was quieter than I had ever heard it.

"The birds are wrong," he said. "They're coming down off the high forest. All of them. At once."
