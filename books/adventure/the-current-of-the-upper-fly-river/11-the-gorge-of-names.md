## 11. The Gorge of Names

I have been piloting the Upper Fly for fifty-one years, and in that time I have learned exactly three things worth teaching. One: the river forgives nothing. Two: the river forgets less than any woman I have ever known, and I have known some memorable ones. Three: when the river offers you a choice, it has already chosen for you, and your only job is to look dignified about it.

The gorge offered us no choices at all.

We left the boat tied to a strangler fig two miles below the gorge mouth, because Sefu Marr — that is me, in case you were wondering who is narrating this disaster — looked at the water running white through the narrows and said what I always say: "Not there." Quentin Underhill, desert ranger, asked why not, and I told her the truth, which is that the Serrated Reach is a Sunday picnic compared to what the gorge does to boats, and that my hull had already given everything it was willing to give on the way up. Wren Calloway said nothing. She had been saying nothing since the drowned village, in the particular way of a person doing arithmetic she doesn't like the answer to.

So we went overland, the three of us, into country where the jungle grows so fast it seems to be chasing you uphill, and by the second morning we stood at the foot of the gorge itself, looking up at walls of grey limestone going up and up until they stopped being rock and started being weather.

"There," said Quentin, pointing at a thread of light far above. "That's the sky."

"That's the sky," I agreed. "We are not invited to it yet."

* * *

Now, about the names.

Every young pilot on the Fly learns the story before he learns the channels, though most of us learn neither properly. The keepers of the source — the people Maren Oduya belongs to, the people whose village the Trust's survey flood half-drowned — have tended that spring for longer than anyone can count, and their law is simple enough to carve in stone, which is precisely what they do. *Those who take from the source are kept by it.* And every keeper who dies in the river's service gets his name cut into the gorge wall, ledge by ledge, century by century, so that the water runs over them forever on its way down.

I had seen the lower carvings once, forty years ago, from the deck of a boat I should not have been standing on. Rows of them. Names in a script older than any map I own, and beneath them, in more recent decades, names in letters I could read: pilots, mostly. Men like me who thought the gorge owed them a favor.

It owes nobody a favor. That is the whole theology of the place, carved in ten thousand names.

But I will confess something, since I am an old man and old men get to confess things nobody asked for: I wanted to see the upper ledges. I wanted to read the names the way you read a guest book at a house you've been visiting all your life without ever getting past the porch. Fifty-one years on this river. I figured I had earned a look at the ledger.

The river heard me thinking that, I believe, and took it personally.

* * *

The climb itself was jungle work, which is to say it was leeches with occasional views. Quentin led, because that woman leads the way other people breathe — automatically and without discussion — and she read the slope the way she reads broken scrub, finding handholds in root systems and old flood-scars that none of us would have seen. Wren came behind her, moving better than she talked, which is high praise from me. I brought up the rear, carrying the maps.

Ah. The maps. Let us pause a moment on the maps, so that later events have proper weight.

Forty years of charting, those maps. Every channel of the Lower and Middle Fly drawn in my own hand, soundings taken in dry season and wet, notes in the margins in three languages because I could never decide which one I thought in. And folded among them, the thing that mattered most now: the surveys of the upper gorge, the passages through the Serrated Reach, the deep-water lanes below the falls — knowledge that existed nowhere else, because I had never trusted paper enough to copy it and never trusted people enough to share it. If the Trust wanted to run barges up to the source after their dam, they would need my maps to do it. I had told Quentin this on the boat, and she had looked at me with those flat ranger's eyes and said, "Then don't lose them."

"I have lost nothing in forty years," I told her, "except two wives and a good deal of money."

"And how did that work out?"

"Cheaper than losing the maps."

She almost smiled. On Quentin Underhill, an almost-smile is a festival.

* * *

By midday we reached the first carved ledge, and even Wren stopped talking her silent arithmetic to stare.

The names ran along a shelf of stone as smooth as a church floor, cut deep enough to hold shadow even in that dim green light. Hundreds of them. Thousands. They climbed the wall above us in terraces, following the ledges up into the gorge the way the river's history climbs a man's memory — further and further back, the letters changing, the language changing, until the oldest marks weren't letters at all but something older, spirals and strokes that made my teeth ache to look at.

Quentin walked the shelf slowly, reading. Of course she read them; she reads everything, tracks, tides, faces. She stopped at a cluster of names in newer characters and crouched.

"These are recent," she said. "Within living memory."

"Keepers die young," I said. "The river keeps what it loves, and it loves carelessly."

Wren touched one name — just touched it, fingertips, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal — and pulled her hand back like the stone was warm. "Maren said strangers who take from it are kept by it," she murmured. "She didn't say the keepers themselves get kept too."

"Everyone gets kept eventually," I said. "The difference is the keepers sign the ledger willingly. That is the entire distinction between devotion and theft, Miss Calloway, and I recommend learning it before the next chapter of your life."

She gave me a look that said she'd already learned it, expensively.

* * *

The rope bridge came in the late afternoon, at the third ledge, where the gorge pinches and the wall goes vertical and the only way up is across.

I want to describe it honestly, because I am told honesty is fashionable in books now. It was not a bridge. It was a suggestion of a bridge. Two cables of braided vine-and-fiber, older than my career, sagging across maybe thirty meters of empty air with slats of split bamboo lashed at intervals, roughly half of which existed. Below it, the river ran white and loud through the pinch, loud enough that we had to shout, and the spray off the rapids had painted the near rocks green with slime.

"Nobody built this recently," Quentin said.

"Somebody built it a long time ago and the river has been editing it ever since," I said. "Like everything else here."

She tested the near cable, then the slats, then went across first, because of course she did, distributing her weight like a woman who had crossed worse things in worse places. Wren followed, lighter, quicker, holding the cable with both hands and not looking down, which is the correct technique and also the technique of someone who knows exactly how much she deserves to fall.

Which left me. Sixty-eight years old, forty years of maps in a waterproof roll on my back, and a bridge the river had been editing since before I was born.

An honest man would tell you he considered leaving the maps. I did consider it — one clean moment, standing on the bank, weighing the roll in my hands. The maps were the most dangerous thing on this river. In Vane's hands they were a road to the source; in the keepers' hands they were a shield; at the bottom of the gorge they were nothing at all. There was a version of wisdom that said drop them now, spare myself the weight.

But they were also mine. Forty years of my life, drawn in ink. And I am an old man, and old men are permitted one vanity, and mine has always been that what I know matters.

So I kept the maps, and I stepped onto the bridge, and I will let you draw your own moral.

* * *

Halfway across, the river chose.

I felt it before I heard it — a shiver in the cable under my hands, a wrongness in the rhythm of the sway. Then a crack, somewhere behind me, deep and fibrous, the sound of forty years of patience ending, and the far anchor let go.

Not all at once. The river is not cruel; it is thorough. The cable peeled away from its mooring strand by strand while I stood there in the middle of the air with my arms full of my life's work, and the whole bridge swung out over the white water like a door opening, slow, almost polite, and Quentin was screaming something from the far ledge and Wren was screaming something else from behind me and I could not hear either of them over the water, which was fine, because I already knew what they were saying.

The cable in my hands went slack.

Here is what I remember, and I remember all of it, because a man gets very economical with his attention at moments like this. I remember deciding, in the half-second of falling, not to let go of the maps. Not bravely — don't dress it up as bravery. It was pure stubbornness, the same stubbornness that kept me on this river when sensible men retired to Marrow Bend to drink and complain about the government. Those pages were mine. If I was going into the gorge, I was going in complete.

I remember the water hitting me like a building falling on me sideways. I remember the white noise, total, absolute, the river shouting every name on every ledge at once directly into my skull. I remember stone — my shoulder, my hip, my head, in an order I never established. I remember the roll tearing loose from my grip at last, the straps ripped away, and watching — actually watching, underwater, in the brown-green roar — my maps open like a bird, page after page lifting out, forty years of ink dissolving into the current, the soundings and the channels and the secret deep-water lanes unspooling downstream toward the sea.

And I remember laughing. God help me, I laughed. Down there in the drowning dark, with my shoulder hanging wrong and my lungs full of the Fly, I laughed, because it was suddenly, perfectly clear: the river wasn't taking anything from me. It was borrowing. It borrows everything — the rain, the silt, the salmon, the men. It had borrowed fifty-one years of my labor and now it was calling in the principal, and what kind of fool lends to a river and expects repayment?

The river only borrows. Everything comes back to it eventually. Even me, apparently, because the next thing I knew there were hands on my collar — Quentin's hands, iron, furious — dragging me out of an eddy onto black stone, and Wren kneeling on my chest asking me questions I couldn't answer, and the sky above the gorge going the color of a bruise.

* * *

They laid me on the third ledge, among the names, which I found appropriate and told them so, though it came out as mostly bubbles. My left shoulder was dislocated and my right arm was opened from elbow to wrist on the rocks, and I had cracked at least two ribs, and I was colder than I have been since the winter of '61. But I was alive, and I knew it, and I knew something else besides, something that arrived whole the moment the maps went into the current:

It was all right.

Not "it could have been worse." Not "at least you survived." It was *all right*. The Trust could not buy my charts now. Vane's barges would have to find their own way up the gorge or not come at all. Every sounding I had ever taken was now the exclusive property of the water it measured, and if that isn't the best possible home for a map, I don't know what is.

Wren bound my arm with strips of her shirt, working fast, her hands steadier than her face. Quentin reset my shoulder without warning and without apology, which is how I knew she liked me — kindness with ceremony is for strangers.

"The maps," she said. "Sefu. The maps."

"Gone," I said. "All of them. Opened up and gone south." I started to laugh again and it turned into coughing, and I rode it out against the stone. "Don't make that face, Ranger. You're mourning paper. Paper was always going to lose."

"You spent forty years—"

"Fifty-one," I corrected. "And the river spent rather longer than that, and the river keeps better records. Look around you." I nodded at the ledges, the names climbing into the dark. "That's the only archive that matters on this water. I'm just pleased to be filed."

Quentin stared at me. Wren stared at me. Somewhere below, the river went on shouting.

"You could have dropped the roll," Quentin said finally. "On the bank. Before the bridge. Why didn't you?"

And there it was, the question, arriving on schedule. I looked up at the thread of sky between the walls, going grey now, going early-dark, and I thought about giving her the noble answer, the sacrifice answer, the answer for the book she'll probably write someday.

"They were mine," I said instead. "That's all. Old men hold on. It's our one remaining talent." I worked my good hand into my pocket and found, miracle of miracles, one scrap — a corner of the upper-gorge survey, torn loose in the water, dried flat against my ribs. A single passage through the final narrows, in my own handwriting, the last thing I ever drew. I pressed it into Quentin's palm and closed her fingers over it. "One page survived. The river kept it for you. Mind the difference — kept, not given. It'll want it back someday, and when it asks, you give it."

Above us, on the fourth ledge, a shape moved against the darkening stone. Figures. Still, patient, watching — the way stone watches, the way the names watch. Quentin saw them a heartbeat after I did and came up off her knees with her hand at her side, tracker-quick, but the figures didn't move, didn't call out, didn't threaten. They simply stood at the edge of the carved names, at the boundary of the ledger, waiting for us to decide whether we were readers or entries.

One of them raised a hand — not a weapon, a summons — and turned, and began to climb.

"Well," I said, from my bed of dead keepers, with my arm in a sling of shirt-rags and my life's work singing somewhere in the current below. "I believe we've been given directions."
