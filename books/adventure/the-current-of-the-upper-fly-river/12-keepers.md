## 12. Keepers

I had been dead for three weeks, officially. It's remarkable how much paperwork a person can get done once the world has filed her under *lost, presumed*.

The morning the keepers found us — found *them*, I should say, though I was standing right there in the rain with my arms crossed like a woman who had opinions about it — I was on the third terrace above the spring, mending a net that belonged to a girl named Ashai who had decided, with the unassailable logic of the very young, that mending nets was beneath her and climbing the limestone spires was not. The net smelled of fish and river weed. My hands had stopped being a doctor's hands somewhere around week two and become something rougher, more useful. I was almost grateful. Hands that have sutured a man's scalp by lantern light while he tells you about his daughter's wedding tend to remember too much.

Then Ashai whistled twice from the ridge — the signal for strangers, not trouble; there is a third whistle for trouble and we had all, mercifully, not heard it yet — and my chest did something complicated and unwelcome.

Because I knew. Before anyone said a word, before the blindfolds came out, I knew who was walking up that trail. You can track a person across seven years of silence if you've loved them enough. Quentin Underhill moved through jungle the way she'd moved through my life: quietly, efficiently, leaving everything rearranged.

* * *

Let me back up, because exile has a way of compressing time until you forget which grief came first.

Seven years ago, Quentin Underhill left me at a bus depot in Marrow Bend with two tickets to the coast and one person boarding. She'd gotten a letter from Aldous Vane that morning — I never read it, but I didn't need to. I watched her face do the arithmetic: her commission, her pension, her entire identity as the woman who could find anyone, weighed against a shared future with a field researcher whose grant money wouldn't cover a decent bottle of wine. Duty won. Duty always won with Quentin; it was her best quality and her worst one, often in the same hour.

I told myself for about a year that I'd been left for something noble. Then I told myself I'd been left for nothing at all, which hurt less in some ways and more in others. Then I stopped telling myself things and got very good at hydrology.

When Vane offered me the leadership of the Upper Fly expedition — full funding, full autonomy, my name on the published findings — I took it for the same reason Quentin had taken her commission: because it was the shape of the life I'd wanted, and I'd convinced myself the wanting hadn't changed. I even told myself I might see her. The Fly headwaters were technically outside her patrol territory, but rivers don't respect jurisdiction any more than hearts do, and I am only now, at forty-one, learning to stop lying to myself in complete sentences.

The journal changed everything. Halfway upriver, our surveyor — a nervous, decent man named Pell — showed me what the Trust actually intended for the source: not a research station, not a protected reserve, but a dam. A hundred and forty meters of concrete across the gorge, a reservoir drowning the terraces, the valley, the villages downstream that Vane's earlier "survey flood" had already given a dress rehearsal. The schematics were dated. They predated our departure. We weren't an expedition. We were the last piece of due diligence, a rubber stamp with a doctorate.

So I made my choice, and this time nobody had to threaten me to make it. I smashed our own radio so Vane couldn't call us home, sent half my team downstream with a cover story about equipment failure, and stayed with the four who volunteered — Pell among them, to his lasting credit and visible terror — to find the keepers before Rook's people found the spring.

Which is how I came to be mending nets in a hidden valley, legally deceased, when the woman who taught me that duty could be a kind of cowardice came walking out of the jungle with two companions and a look on her face like she'd been carrying my name uphill for seven years.

She probably had. That was the unforgivable part. Not that she'd left. That she'd carried it.

* * *

They brought the strangers into the valley at dusk, blindfolded, hands bound loosely with river-cane — the keepers' bindings are less about restraint than about message: *you are held, gently, whether you like it or not.* I stood where the light from the cookfire would reach me and made sure my expression was set to something between indifference and mild administrative annoyance. I had rehearsed this. I want that on the record. In the three hours between Ashai's whistle and their arrival, I rehearsed being unmoved the way other people rehearse wedding toasts.

The blindfolds came off. Quentin blinked against the firelight, and I watched recognition move through her like weather — first confusion (she was expecting a stranger), then disbelief, then something rawer that she tried to file under professionalism and failed. Her hair was shorter than I remembered. There was a new scar over her eyebrow, thin as a signature. She looked tired in the specific way of someone who has been sleeping badly for years and calling it rest.

"Imogen," she said. Just that. As if the name were a rope bridge and she wasn't sure it would hold.

"Dr. Hale," I corrected, because I am petty, and pettiness is a load-bearing structure. "You're trespassing on sacred ground, Ms. Underhill. Also, you're supposed to be finding a thief."

"I found her." A gesture at the young woman beside her — Wren Calloway, sharp-faced, watchful, radiating the particular tension of someone who has bet everything on a stranger's better nature and is still waiting for the odds to resolve. Behind them stood an old river pilot with white braids and the calm of a man who has already survived everything worth fearing. "Wren Calloway. And Sefu Marr. They're not prisoners. Well." He glanced at his own bound wrists. "Mostly not."

"You brought thieves and boat pilots to the source of the Fly," I said. "Vane must be proud."

Something flickered across her face at Vane's name — quick, involuntary, gone. Filed away. Quentin Underhill files things; it's how she loves, or how she avoids loving, the distinction having eroded sometime around the bus depot.

"We need to talk," she said. "Not here."

"Here is fine. Everyone in this valley already knows the worst thing about me." I turned to the keeper elder — a small, iron-spined woman named Oduya whom I had come to love with the uncomplicated devotion I'd once wasted on people who deserved it less — and asked in her own tongue for permission to take the strangers to my shelter. Oduya looked at Quentin for a long moment. Keepers read people the way Quentin reads ground. Whatever she saw, she must have found tolerable, because she untied the bindings herself and said something dry that made the younger keepers laugh.

Later, Wren would translate it for me, embarrassed: *"The river sends her back the ones she wants."*

I chose not to examine that too closely.

* * *

My shelter was a lean-to of split bamboo and palm thatch I'd built myself, badly, in the first month, and which the keepers had since quietly rebuilt whenever they thought I wasn't looking. There was room for exactly two people to sit without touching, which I noted with the clinical detachment of a woman cataloguing hazards.

Quentin sat close enough to touch anyway. Of course she did. Proximity was always her native language; words were the foreign tongue she spoke with an accent.

"The radio," she said. "At your camp. You smashed it."

"I smashed it."

"The message on the crate. DON'T TRUST VANE. TURN BACK."

"I wrote it." I fed a stick into the small stove, mostly to have something to do with my hands. "It took me twenty minutes to decide whether to write 'turn back' or 'go to hell.' I went with the one that might actually save your life."

"It nearly didn't save mine." And then she told me. All of it, in order, in that flat ranger's cadence that makes catastrophe sound like a supply inventory: the thief, the map fragment, Vane's letter, the patrol boat, Captain Rook and his second team, the drowned village, the scuttled fuel cache, the monsoon coming early, Sefu reading the sky like scripture. She told it well. She has always told things well; it's one of the thousand ways she keeps people at a comfortable distance, narrating her own life instead of inhabiting it.

I listened with my fury arranged around me like furniture. But fury, I've learned, is just grief wearing armor, and mine kept slipping.

"You knew," I said, when she finished. "Before tonight. On the trail — you knew it was me up here."

"Sefu guessed. The camp, the radio, the handwriting." A pause. "I hoped. Hope isn't knowing."

"Don't." I stood up, because sitting down had become untenable. "Don't do that. Don't stand in my house after seven years and hand me *hope* like it settles anything. Do you know what I thought? For three weeks? I thought Rook's team had found you. I thought you were dead, and I caught myself — do you understand — I caught myself grieving, and underneath the grief, this appalling relief, because if you were dead then I'd never have to say any of this—"

"Any of what?"

"*Any of it.*" My voice cracked on the second word, which was humiliating, and I pressed on regardless, because I had rehearsed dignity and lost it, so honesty was all that remained. "That you chose a letter from Aldous Vane over me. That you'd do it again. That I spent seven years becoming the kind of person who doesn't wait at depots, and it worked, it worked perfectly, and then you walk up that ridge and every single wall I built comes down like it was made of paper—"

"It was made of paper," Quentin said quietly. "I know. I lived behind it too."

And that — that quiet, unbearable gentleness — was worse than anything. I sat back down. The stove ticked. Outside, the spring murmured its one long syllable, the sound I had fallen asleep to for months, the sound I had chosen over her voice and then spent seven years pretending I hadn't.

"Why are you here, Quentin?" I asked finally. "Really. Not the job. Vane hired you to retrieve a journal and a missing expedition leader. Which one are you?"

She was silent long enough that I thought she wouldn't answer. When she did, she didn't look at me; she looked at the fire, tracker's habit, reading it.

"When I got your name from Vane," she said, "I felt the floor go. He knew. That's why he hired me — he knew what your name would do to me, and he figured I'd work faster with a hook in me." Her jaw tightened. "He was right. I climbed faster, walked longer, slept less. Every mile I told myself it was about the commission." She turned then, and looked at me straight, no narration, no inventory. "It stopped being about the commission somewhere around the drowned village. Maybe before. Maybe the whole time."

"That's not an answer."

"No," she agreed. "But it's all I've got that's true. The rest I'm still digging out."

Seven years ago she would have given me a clean answer, polished and hollow. This ragged, unfinished thing was worth more. I hated that. I hated how much it was worth.

* * *

Near midnight, a soft knock — keepers don't knock so much as announce themselves with a knuckle, once, politely — and Oduya entered, took in the two of us sitting in the wreckage of our conversation, and expressed no surprise whatsoever. She spoke to me in her tongue, low and formal.

"What did she say?" Quentin asked, when Oduya left.

"She said the council will hear the strangers tomorrow at the water's edge. And she said—" I hesitated, translating honestly for the first time instead of conveniently. "She said that I have been kept by the river long enough to know its law, and that tomorrow I will be asked to speak for you. All three of you. Whether I speak for or against is my own grief to carry."

Quentin absorbed that. "Will you speak for us?"

"I haven't decided." I lay back on my mat and stared at the thatch. "Ask me again after I've slept. Or don't. Seven years ago you didn't ask me anything, you just left a note."

"There was no note."

"There should have been a note." I closed my eyes. "A bad one. I'd have kept it."

I heard her settle onto the far mat, heard the small sounds of her checking — out of habit, out of love, indistinguishable — that the knife was reachable and the door latch held. The spring sang its single note. Somewhere below, the river gathered itself toward the gorge, patient as a debt.

And in the last gray moment before sleep took me, I understood what Oduya had already seen: that whatever I said tomorrow at the water's edge, I had already chosen. The only question left was whether I'd say it out loud.

Then Ashai's whistle split the dark — three notes this time, high and urgent, the one we had all been spared until now — and the valley lit with moving torches, and someone shouted the word for boats.

Rook had reached the gorge.
