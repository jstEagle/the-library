## 13. The Forbidden Source

Imogen Hale woke me before dawn by not waking me at all — just standing there at the edge of my sleeping mat in the keeper's longhouse, backlit by a single oil lamp, waiting with the patience of someone who had learned to wait for seven years and was very good at it now.

"You wanted to see it," she said.

"I wanted to see you," I said, before my brain could file the appropriate objection. "The spring too. Both."

"Then come. The keepers say the source is best seen before the sun climbs over the eastern wall. They say it's honestest then." She almost smiled. "I've learned their word for it. *Honestest.* There isn't an English equivalent that doesn't sound like a sermon."

I got up, found my boots where I'd left them — which is to say, nowhere near where I'd left them; the keepers moved things the way water moves things, quietly and according to laws of their own — and followed her out into the blue-black hour before morning.

The valley at night was a different country than the valley I'd been marched into blindfolded two days ago. Without the blindfold, without Rook's patrol behind us and Wren's hand gripping my sleeve, I could feel its shape: high limestone walls on three sides, the fourth open toward the gorge we'd climbed, the whole bowl of it holding air like a cup holds wine. Somewhere below us, the river made its low continuous argument with itself.

Imogen walked ahead with a lantern she didn't need. I'd watched her move through camp these past two days, and I kept catching myself cataloguing her like terrain — sure-footed, economical, changed. Seven years ago she'd walked like a woman who expected the ground to cooperate. Now she walked like a woman who had stopped expecting anything from anything and been pleasantly surprised ever since.

"You're staring," she said, without turning around.

"I'm navigating."

"In the dark. In a valley you've never seen."

"I'm a desert ranger, Imogen. Navigating in the dark in places I've never seen is the entire job description."

That got a real laugh out of her, short and startled, as if the laugh had escaped custody. It did something to my chest that I decided not to examine until after breakfast, or possibly after the end of the world, whichever came first.

* * *

We descended through terraces.

I'd seen them from above on our arrival and thought, professionally, *agriculture* — rice paddies or something like them, cut into the valley floor in wide silver steps. In the dark they were something else. Each terrace held water, still as poured glass, and each one reflected the last stars overhead, so that walking down between them felt like descending through the sky itself, level by level, the stars rising around us instead of falling.

"The keepers built these," Imogen said quietly. "Not in one lifetime. Not in ten. Maren Oduya says her line has tended them for four hundred years, and that they were old when her line took them up." She lifted the lantern so its light touched the nearest pool without breaking it. "Every terrace filters the spring water on its way down. Every one is planted with something specific — reeds that pull the silt, a flower I can't pronounce that tests the purity. If a terrace goes cloudy, they know the mountain has shifted. It's a gauge. A living instrument older than any survey office in Marrow Bend."

"And Vane wants to put a dam on top of it."

"Vane wants to put a dam *in front of* it," she corrected. "Which is worse. You don't drown this — you strangle it. Hold the headwaters back, let the terraces dry, let the limestone crack in the heat. Then flood what's left when the reservoir fills." She said it flatly, the way you read coordinates off a map, and I understood she'd said it that way many times, to herself, in the dark, because saying it any other way would have broken something in her.

We came down the last terrace and the ground opened out, and there it was.

* * *

I have spent my life in country that lies about its size. Deserts do it constantly — a dune field will look like a mile and cost you three days. So I don't impress easily, and I want that on the record, because what happened next deserves an honest witness.

The spring rose out of the earth like a building.

There's no other word for it. Limestone had dissolved underneath the valley over some unimaginable span of time, and what remained was a hollow cathedral of pale stone, arched and ribbed and columned, half above ground and half below, and through its center, from a depth the lantern couldn't find, the river came up. Not trickling. *Rising.* A column of water wider than Sefu Marr's whole boat, ascending through the rock with a sound like slow thunder heard through a wall, breaking at the surface into a crown of white that fed the first terrace, which fed the second, which fed all the rest, all the way down to the dark river arguing with itself below.

The water in the column glowed. That's the part I still can't make plain. Some mineral in the limestone, some accident of light — the lantern caught it and the whole ascending body of water lit from within, green-gold, like a lamp made of current. The keepers' carvings ran up the columns of stone on either side, hundreds of names, generations deep, and the light moved across them so they seemed to breathe.

"Well," I said, after a while, because I am a professional and professionals say something.

Imogen didn't tease me. She stood beside me with the lantern lowered, watching me watch it, and whatever she saw in my face seemed to be enough.

"How deep does it go?" I managed.

"No one knows. The keepers have a story about a diver who went down with a rope and came back at the rope's end, alive, and refused ever to speak of it. Maren told me he lived another forty years and would leave any room if the subject came up." She turned to face me fully. "Quentin. This is what the journal proves. Not just that the source exists — anyone with a barometer could guess that. It proves the geology. The limestone aquifer extends under the entire upper valley. Dam here and the reservoir doesn't just cover the spring. The weight of the water changes the pressure in the aquifer. The terraces fail first. Then the caves. Then—" she gestured upward, at the whole invisible architecture of the mountain, "—who knows. Vane's own surveyor wrote it in the margin and Vane filed it under *acceptable losses.*"

"Acceptable losses," I repeated.

"That's the phrase. I've had three weeks upstream to turn it over." Her voice stayed level but her hands didn't; she gripped the lantern handle tighter. "You know what I thought about, out here? Not the science. I thought about the day you left. Because it was the same thing, exactly the same thing. Duty as a ledger, and love filed under acceptable losses."

The spring thundered softly beneath us. Somewhere above, the first birds started up in the walls of the valley.

"I'm not going to defend that," I said. "There's nothing to defend it with. Vane threatened my commission and I chose the commission, and I've spent seven years telling myself it was more complicated than that, and it wasn't. It was fear wearing duty's coat."

She looked at me for a long moment. The light from the water moved across her face, gold and green, gold and green.

"You always were better at saying it than being it," she said, but there was no blade in it. Almost the opposite. "Come on. I want to show you the terraces in the light. And I want you to meet the water properly. There's a rite. Maren insisted, after what your Captain Rook did at the lower camp."

"What kind of rite?"

"A small one. You put your hands in the first terrace and you say your name to the water." A ghost of the old academic smile. "I told them it sounded like a formality. Maren said, *all vows are formalities until they're tested,* and I've been thinking about that for three weeks too."

* * *

The sun came over the eastern wall while we walked the terraces, and the valley performed its morning transformation — grey becoming green becoming almost unbearably vivid, water flashing on every level, keeper children already out on the lowest paddies with baskets, calling greetings to Imogen in their own tongue and, to my surprise, a few words of trade-English they'd clearly learned for her benefit. One girl, maybe eight, looked me over with frank appraisal and informed Imogen that I walked like a heron.

"She's right," Imogen said gravely.

"It's a desert walk. It conserves energy."

"It conserves dignity, is what it does. Loosen up, Underhill. You're among people who think a good day is one where nobody drowns and everybody eats."

"That's... also my definition of a good day."

"Then you might survive conversion yet."

At the first terrace — the highest one, fed directly by the overflow of the spring's crown — Maren Oduya was waiting. Of course she was. I had begun to suspect the elder didn't sleep so much as relocate. She stood on the stone lip with her feet bare and her staff planted, and she watched us come with those eyes that had weighed me on arrival and apparently hadn't finished.

"You bring her to the water," Maren said to Imogen. Not a question. An observation, filed.

"She asked to see it."

"They always ask to see it." But there was no contempt in it — something wearier and kinder. She looked at me. "Desert woman. You tracked across the Redglass to get here. Tell me what you read there."

I blinked. Nobody outside the service had ever asked me that question, and nobody inside it had asked it with this much weight behind it.

"Broken scrub tells you direction," I said slowly. "Buried footprint tells you weight and hurry. A fire ring tells you how scared someone is — scared people build small fires, close to their bodies. The land keeps a record of everyone who crosses it. You just have to know how to read."

Maren nodded once, satisfied, and pointed her staff at the spring.

"This is also a record. Four hundred years of names on the stone. Every keeper who tended these terraces, every stranger who took from the water, every flood and every drought. Your desert keeps records in sand. Ours keeps them in water and stone. Sand blows away, desert woman. Water remembers." She stepped aside from the lip. "Put your hands in. Say your name. Not for us. For you."

I knelt at the edge of the first terrace. The water coming off the spring's crown was cold in a way that went past the skin — cold like altitude, like depth, like something that had been underground since before there were names to say. I put my hands in.

"Quentin Underhill," I said.

It should have felt silly. Standing in a foreign valley at dawn, talking to water, while an old woman watched and the woman I'd abandoned seven years ago watched harder. It should have felt like a formality.

It didn't. It felt like signing a ledger I'd been dodging my whole life.

Maren made a sound low in her throat — approval, or amusement, or both — and said something to Imogen in the keeper tongue. Imogen translated, her voice careful: "She says the water accepted the name. She says it accepts most names. What it does with them afterward is the interesting part."

* * *

We spent the morning walking the terraces, and Imogen talked, and I listened, and somewhere in there — I couldn't tell you where, some turning between the third paddy and the fifth — the last of the hired help drained out of me and something else settled in its place.

It wasn't dramatic. I want to be clear about that, because I've read enough adventure novels to know how conversion is supposed to look: trumpets, lightning, a man on his knees in the rain. This was quieter. It was Imogen crouching at a terrace wall, showing me how the keepers read cloudiness in the water the way I read cloudiness in a footprint. It was a child handing me a basket and demonstrating, with immense patience, how to hold it so the seedlings didn't spill. It was arithmetic I couldn't stop doing: Vane's reservoir line, drawn on the journal's schematics, laid over this valley. The terraces gone. The longhouse gone. The children with their baskets gone. The spring sealed under sixty feet of standing water, its cathedral drowned, its record — four hundred years of names — erased not even maliciously, but as a rounding error.

I had taken contracts for twenty years. I'd retrieved stolen property, chased runaways, escorted men I despised because the fee cleared. I'd told myself the work was neutral — that tracking was tracking, that what happened after delivery wasn't mine to carry. Standing on the fifth terrace with a basket of seedlings in my hands and a child correcting my grip, I understood finally that neutrality was the most expensive thing I'd ever bought, and I'd bought it with exactly the currency Maren's people measured everything in: memory. I had agreed, twenty years running, to forget on schedule.

No more. The thought arrived without ceremony, the way the river arrives — continuously, and then all at once.

"Imogen," I said. "The journal. Wren's copy, the original pages. Where are they?"

Her head came up sharply. "Why?"

"Because I memorized half of it on the boat and I'll memorize the rest tonight. And because I want it somewhere Rook can't reach it, and I want to know, plainly, what happens to it when this is over. Whether it goes to a court in Marrow Bend or stays here with the water. I need to know which side of that I'm standing on before he comes. And he's coming. Men like Rook don't lose a fuel cache and go home. They lose a fuel cache and get *angrier*, and angrier men travel faster."

Imogen was quiet for a moment. Below us, the keeper children had gone quiet too — not frightened, just attentive, the way animals go attentive before weather.

"Maren says the journal belongs to the water now," she said at last. "Anything taken from the source is kept by the source. That's the law. But Quentin—" she stepped closer, and the morning light caught the grey at her temple that hadn't been there seven years ago, "—if it comes to a choice between the courts and the keepers, whose side are you on? Say it out loud. I've spent seven years learning not to assume."

I looked past her, down the long silver stair of the terraces, to the pale cathedral where the river rose out of the earth, thundering gently, keeping its four hundred years of names.

"Mine," I said. "The river's. Whichever way that breaks."

From the rim of the valley, far above, came a sound I knew from a hundred bad mornings in a hundred bad places: the thin, carrying crack of a flare pistol, and then a red star climbing into the morning sky, hanging there, burning.

Rook had found the gorge.
