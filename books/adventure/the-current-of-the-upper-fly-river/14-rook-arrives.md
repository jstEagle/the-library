## 14. Rook Arrives

I have been called many things in my career — hard man, careful man, a man who reads maps the way priests read scripture — but nobody has ever accused me of being lucky, and I want that on the record before I describe what happened at the valley.

Because luck was involved. Bad luck, mostly mine, arriving on schedule like everything else in my life.

We had been climbing for six days behind Underhill's little band of ghosts. Six days of leeches and rot and my second-in-command, Ferro, complaining about his boots as though the boots were the problem. The Trust's money bought good boots. What it could not buy was a dry sky, and by the fifth day the clouds had come down so low they felt like a ceiling pressing on the back of your neck. Sefu Marr would have read that sky and turned around. I am not Sefu Marr. I had orders, and orders are a kind of weather too — they fall on you whether you like it or not.

Vane's instructions had been simple enough to fit on an index card: recover the journal, secure the source survey, neutralize any obstruction. "Neutralize" is a lovely word. It has the softness of a pillow and the weight of a headstone. I'd used it myself in reports for eleven years and never once thought about what it meant until it started meaning me.

The breach came easier than it should have. That should have told me something. In my experience, when a door opens too easily, it is because someone on the other side wanted you to walk through it.

* * *

The keepers' blindfold trick worked both ways, it turned out. We had captured one of their scouts two days back — a boy, sixteen maybe, all elbows and silence — and he had led us, or we had followed him, or some arrangement between those two things had occurred, up a goat track that no map acknowledged. The monsoon was behind us by then. Not coming. Behind us. You could hear it working its way up the gorge like a crowd moving through a house, doors slamming room by room, getting closer to the one where you were standing.

Ferro heard it first. He stopped on the trail with his head cocked like a dog hearing a whistle only dogs can hear, and said, "Captain. You hear that?"

"I hear rain."

"That's not rain."

He was right, and I hated him for it. Rain falls. This thing *arrived*. It came down the gorge with a sound like freight — a long, rolling, industrial roar, the sound of a river deciding to be somewhere else. The boy scout looked back at us once, and there was no fear in his face, which frightened me more than the noise did. He looked almost satisfied. Like the sound was a name being called and he recognized his own.

"We go fast," I said. "We go now."

The valley opened below us the way secrets do — all at once, and too late to unsee. Terraces cut into the hillside, green as pool tables. Smoke from cook fires. And at the center, rising out of the limestone like something the earth had been keeping in its mouth, the spring: white water standing up out of a hole in the world, tall as a church, loud as judgment.

Even then — even soaked, even hunted by the sky — I remember thinking: *Vane will build his monument here and call it progress.* And I remember thinking, a half-second later, in a voice that sounded suspiciously like my late mother's: *You'll drown it, and it will still be louder than you.*

* * *

They knew we were coming. Of course they knew. The camp emptied toward the terraces with the calm of people who had rehearsed this, which meant Underhill had gotten through, which meant my timetable was already worthless. There was a moment — I'll be honest, since honesty costs me nothing anymore — when I considered turning around. The roar behind us had doubled. The trail we'd climbed was already running with brown water ankle-deep and rising.

Then I saw her. Hale. Dr. Imogen Hale, standing on the lowest terrace with her arms crossed, watching us come down into her valley like a landlady watching tenants arrive with bad references.

Three weeks dead, according to Vane. Three weeks silent, according to the radio logs. And here she stood, dry-footed and furious, very much among the living.

"Hale," I said, when we were close enough to shout over the water. "Captain Rook, Amberdale Trust security. You're ordered downstream."

"I don't take orders from the Trust anymore." Her voice carried clean over the flood-noise, the way voices do when they've been sanded down by something hard. "And neither should you."

"The journal," I said. "Where's the journal?"

Something moved behind her eyes — not fear, exactly. Recalculation. She glanced past my shoulder at the wall of weather eating the gorge mouth, and I watched her do arithmetic with it, and I understood she was measuring how long she could stall.

That's when I made my mistake. Not the taking of the hostage — that was strategy, ugly but sound, and I'll defend it to anyone who hasn't heard the river. My mistake was believing the day still belonged to me. I had crossed a jungle, lost two men to fever and one to a landslide, spent my reserve rations, and arrived at the edge of the world just as the world decided to end — and I still thought I was holding the cards. Triumph does that. It gets in your ears like water and you can't hear anything else.

Including, as it turned out, the river.

* * *

Wren Calloway was easier to find than I expected. She wasn't hiding. She was standing near the supply lean-to with a pack on her shoulder like a woman waiting for a bus, and when I put the muzzle of my sidearm against her ribs and told her not to make noise, she said, "You're Rook," in the tone other people use for *you're late*.

"You know me?"

"Vane talks about you. 'My captain Rook, my reliable captain Rook.'" She shifted the pack off her shoulder, slowly, and held it out. "Here. It's what you came for. Congratulations on your jungle hike."

I took the pack and found the journal inside, wrapped in oilcloth, fat and real and heavier than paper has any right to be. Eleven days of pursuit closed up in my fist. I allowed myself three seconds of satisfaction, which was three seconds too many, because in those seconds Ferro shouted my name and the valley's population chose that exact moment to stop being cooperative.

Keepers came out of the terraces — not charging, nothing so useful as charging, just *converging*, quiet as tide. Underhill was among them, and I'll say this for Quentin Underhill: she moved like the terrain was a rumor she'd already disproven. She was ten meters away and closing, and between me and the high ground stood Dr. Imogen Hale, who had not run, who had deliberately not run, and I understood in one cold flash that she was offering herself as an obstacle because she knew I wouldn't shoot her.

She was right. Vane wanted her alive — "the expedition leader's testimony legitimizes the survey," his words, his index card — and more than that, I needed what she had. She knew the paths above the spring. She knew where the high ground went when the low ground drowned. Every soul in this valley was about to become a swimmer except the people who knew the way up, and I intended to be one of them.

So I did the thing. The ugly, sound thing.

"Dr. Hale," I said, and took her arm, and put the journal under my other arm, and raised my voice over the water: "Nobody follows. Anybody follows, the doctor and I discuss the survey somewhere less crowded."

Hale didn't struggle. That was the worst part. She looked at my hand on her arm, then at my face, and she smiled — a small, terrible smile, the smile of a woman who has already seen how this ends.

"Captain," she said, almost kindly. "Do you hear that?"

"I hear the river."

"No," she said. "Listen. It's been getting louder since you entered the valley. It's been getting louder the whole time you've been talking." She leaned closer, and the rain began in earnest then, fat drops the size of coins, and her voice dropped to something meant only for me. "It's not background noise, Captain. It's a countdown. And you're standing in the bowl."

* * *

I got her moving uphill. Ferro covered our flank; the rest of my team — four left, out of nine — fell in behind, and the keepers let us go, which I noted, and disliked noting. Armies that retreat in order are armies planning something. So are valleys.

The climb above the spring was stone stairs older than any nation I could name, slick with spray, and the spring roared beside us the whole way up like a thing giving testimony. Hale walked ahead of me without being pushed. That bothered me too. Hostages who cooperate are either broken or scheming, and nothing about this woman was broken.

At the third terrace she stopped, and I let her, because the view stopped me first.

Below, the valley was changing. The river that fed the terraces had come up a full meter in the time we'd been climbing — I could see it against the marker stones, could see water where grass had been. The gorge mouth we'd entered through was gone. Not blocked. *Gone* — replaced by a rolling brown cataract with trees in it, whole trees, traveling sideways. Somewhere in that soup was our descent route, our extraction point, and every plan Vane had drawn in his tidy office in Marrow Bend.

"Your boat's not coming," Hale said, reading my face. "Nothing's coming. For days, maybe a week. The monsoon owns the gorge now."

"I have the journal." I patted the oilcloth bundle. "I have you. Vane gets his survey, the courts get their legitimacy, and we wait out the weather on high ground like reasonable people."

"Do you know what's in that journal, Captain? Beyond the dam figures?" She turned to face me fully, rain streaming off her chin, and her eyes were bright with something I initially took for anger and later understood was pity. "There's a list. Of names. Keepers who died defending this spring, going back four hundred years. The surveyor recorded them because he couldn't help it — the terraces are carved with them, every ledge, every generation. And there's a line in the margin, in his handwriting, that I think about every day." She paused, letting the river fill the silence, which it did generously. "It says: *The water keeps what is given to it.*"

"Folklore."

"Everything is folklore until it happens to you."

Ferro called from below — the keepers were advancing up the stairs after all, unhurried, inevitable, and behind them, small in the rain but unmistakable, came Underhill, moving like the answer to a question I hadn't finished asking. I pulled Hale higher. The stairs narrowed. The spring thundered. And for the first time in eleven years of service to the Amberdale Trust, standing on wet stone above a cathedral of water with a hostage who pitied me and a prize that weighed like a sin, I heard it — really heard it — under the roar:

A rhythm. Patient. Enormous. Rising.

Triumph, I have read, curdles quickly at altitude. Mine began to turn right there, on the keeper's stairs, in the rain, with the whole valley flooding below me like a verdict being read aloud.

Behind us, gaining, unhurried as the water itself, came Quentin Underhill — and I realized I had taken the wrong hostage, stolen the right book, and climbed directly into the one place on earth where Captain Rook was worth precisely nothing.

The river was louder than my orders now.

It was louder than everything.
