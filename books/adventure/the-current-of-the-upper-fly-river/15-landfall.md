## 15. Landfall

The monsoon did not arrive. It *landed* — the way a dropped piano lands, the way a verdict lands, all at once and everywhere at once, with no negotiation beforehand.

We had four days of warning from Sefu's sky-reading, and we used maybe two of them badly, which is about par for any expedition I've ever been on. The keepers moved us up-valley to a stone shelter cut into the cliff above the terraces, a low-roofed place that smelled of woodsmoke and old rain, and there we waited while the sky came apart over the Forbidden Source.

I've lived my whole life in deserts, so let me tell you what I know about water: I know what it does when there isn't enough of it. I know how it makes people lie, cheat, kill, and marry badly. What I learned in that valley was what water does when there is too much, and the short version is: everything, all at once, and none of it negotiable.

The first hour was noise. The second hour was louder noise. By the third hour the sound had stopped being sound and become weather in the way that a fist stops being knuckles once it connects with your face. The river below us — the modest, glassy spring-fed current we'd followed for two hundred miles — went brown and insane, rising through the gorge like something escaping a cage it had outgrown years ago.

Imogen stood at the shelter's mouth with her arms crossed, watching it, and she wasn't afraid. That frightened me more than the storm.

"You get used to it," she said, without looking at me. Seven years of silence and then three weeks of guarded fury, and now this: conversation, casual as passing salt. "The first landfall is always the worst. After that it's just arithmetic."

"Arithmetic."

"How fast the water rises. How long until it finds the low ground. How many people are standing on the low ground." She turned then, and her eyes had that flat shine they got when she was doing sums she didn't like. "Rook's team is on the low ground."

* * *

Here is how the board looked, as best I could read it through the rain:

Captain Rook and six of his Trust soldiers held the lower terraces, where they'd breached the valley two days earlier behind a wall of floodwater that would have killed a less disciplined man. They'd taken the journal off Wren in the chaos — Rook himself had gone through her pack with the thoroughness of a customs officer and the manners of one, too — and they'd taken Imogen briefly as well, before the keepers' counterattack and the rising river had scrambled everyone's plans into the same wet pile.

Now the river had done what rivers do: it had closed every door. The gorge below us was a churning brown throat, thirty feet of new water where yesterday there'd been rapids you could pick your line through. The patrol boat Rook had come upstream on was somewhere downstream of that, or wrapped around a rock, or both. His radio worked about as well as mine, which is to say it produced static with the occasional ghost of a human voice, like a séance conducted by someone who didn't speak the language.

Which meant Captain Aldous Vane's hand-picked retrieval specialist was stranded on sacred ground he'd invaded, holding a stolen journal worth more than his commission, with six men, dwindling rations, and a wall of water between him and every road home.

I stood at the shelter mouth next to Imogen and thought: *I know exactly how that feels.* Minus the six men. And the journal. And, thank every dry god, the conscience.

"He'll bargain," Imogen said.

"He'll bargain," I agreed. "Question is what he thinks he's bargaining with, and what he thinks he's bargaining for."

Wren Calloway sat inside the shelter with her back against the wall and her knees drawn up, watching us talk through the doorway. She'd been quiet since Rook took the journal — not sulking, exactly. Recalculating. Wren's whole life was recalculation; she'd been the Trust's own surveyor before she became its thief, and she carried betrayal in both directions like ballast. When she finally spoke, her voice came out steady, which cost her something. I could see it costing her.

"The journal's no good to him now," she said. "You understand that, right? Even if he gets out. Even if he gets it back to Marrow Bend. The commission's already suspended, the inquiry's already started, and your testimony—" She stopped. Looked at me. "Your testimony is already in motion. He's holding a document that's worth less every day, and it's costing him food, ammunition, and six men's patience."

"So why hold it?"

"Because it's the last thing he can still deliver," Imogen said quietly. "Men like Rook don't hold objects. They hold purposes. Take away the purpose and you'll find out what else was in there underneath."

* * *

He came at dusk, or what passed for dusk under a sky the color of wet slate. Two of his men flanked him, rifles slung but hands near, and the other four stayed visible on the terraces below, because Rook understood theater even if he despised it. Rain ran off his cap brim in a solid rope. He stopped ten paces from the shelter, which was close enough to talk and far enough to suggest he'd thought about distances.

"Underhill." He said my name like an item on an invoice. "You're a hard woman to bury."

"You haven't tried very hard."

"I sent eight men up that gorge in monsoon season. You're standing here. Either you're very good or my men are very bad, and I've decided it's the former, because the alternative insults my recruiting." He wiped his face with a sleeve that gave up halfway through the job. "I want to talk terms."

"We don't have terms. We have a council of keepers who say nobody leaves this valley with anything taken from the source, and a river that agrees with them enthusiastically."

"The council isn't here right now." His eyes flicked past me to Imogen, to Wren in the doorway, cataloguing. "And the river doesn't negotiate. I do. So listen. I have the journal. I have six soldiers. I have, as of approximately ninety minutes ago, no extraction, no resupply, and no route down that doesn't involve swimming through a gorge that's currently eating trees whole. What I need is a guide who knows the descent routes, and enough goodwill from the people who live here that my men aren't shot crossing the lower terraces."

"And what you're offering?"

"The journal. Unharmed. Back to the keepers' custody, witnessed by everyone present, so that when the inquiry comes asking, the record shows Captain Rook returned stolen property voluntarily under adverse conditions." A thin smile, the kind that costs extra. "I'm offering to be reasonable, Underhill. It's the most expensive thing I own."

Inside the shelter, Wren made a small sound — half laugh, half cough. I understood her. There was something almost admirable in the audacity. Almost. In the desert I'd known men who'd trade you their last water for the promise of shade, and Rook was that species, refined: a man selling back what he'd stolen and calling it commerce.

"You want safe passage," I said. "Down a flooded gorge. With six men. During landfall."

"I want a *chance* at passage. I'm realistic. Sefu Marr knows the river better than any man alive, and I'm told he survived a fall into it recently, which means either the river likes him or it's saving him. Either way, I want him, I want a route, and I want the keepers' word that my men walk out rather than float."

"And the journal goes back before any of that happens."

"It goes back when my men are on the water."

"That's not a bargain, Captain. That's a hostage situation with paperwork."

For a moment something moved behind his eyes — not anger. Something wearier. "No," he said, and surprised me by agreeing. "It isn't. But it's what I have." He glanced up at the sky, at the rain hammering down with the persistence of a debt collector. "Underhill, I've served the Trust nineteen years. I've burned villages for survey access and called it clearing brush. I am not going to pretend to you that I'm a good man having a bad week. But I can count. My extraction is gone. My employer is under investigation. Every day I stand on this mountain, the journal I'm carrying becomes less valuable and the men I'm responsible for become more expendable. I'd like to stop being the instrument and start being the captain again. That's all this is."

Behind me, Imogen said, softly, just loud enough: "Ask him about the second team, Quentin. Ask him what happened to the men Vane sent up before us."

Rook's jaw tightened. The rain filled the silence.

"There was no second team," he said.

"There was," Imogen said. "Three weeks ago. Eight men. We found their camp. We found what was left of their fire, and their ration tins, and one boot. Ask him where the rest of them went."

"I said—" Rook stopped. Closed his eyes for one second, a man resetting a ledger. When he opened them, he looked older, and I realized with a small cold shock that he'd been old all along and carrying it well. "There were eight men in an advance party. I inherited their contract, not their bodies. As far as I know, they descended ahead of the floods and are sitting in Marrow Bend drawing hazard pay. If Dr. Hale found evidence otherwise, then I'm sorry, but I won't answer for ghosts I didn't make."

"But you'll bargain with them hanging over the table anyway," I said. "Because a dead second team makes the Trust look worse than a dammed river does, and you need us to need you gone quietly."

He didn't deny it. I'll give him that. Most men would have reached for a denial the way a drowning man reaches for anything. Rook just stood in the rain and let the accusation sit on him, and adjusted his cap, and said:

"Terms, Underhill. Before dark. Because after dark, my men start making decisions without me, and I promise you they're not as sentimental as I am."

* * *

We argued about it for an hour, the four of us — me, Imogen, Wren, and Sefu, who'd hauled himself up from the keeper's village on a leg the river had nearly kept, moving with the grim economy of a man who refused to be an argument for staying put.

Sefu heard Rook's terms and spat into the fire. "Safe passage down." He said it the way you'd say *free lunch*. "Little captain wants me to take six soldiers down the Fly in landfall season. I have run this river forty-one years. I have pulled drowned men out of it. I have pulled drowned *boats* out of it. In landfall, the gorge is not a river. It is a rumor of a river with rocks in it."

"Can it be done?" I asked.

He looked at me for a long time. Old Sefu Marr, whose face was a topographic map of every river he'd ever lied to. "Anything can be done once," he said. "The question is whether the person doing it gets to do anything else afterward."

"That's not a no."

"It is not a yes. It is a price list." He held up fingers. "One: we wait for the water to crest. Two days, maybe three. The river tells us when — it goes quiet before it drops, like a liar thinking. Two: we go down the far channel, the keeper's channel, the one that isn't on any Trust map, because the maps are wrong on purpose. Three: the journal comes back to the keepers *before* we take a single step downhill. Not on the water. Before. If Rook will not give it up first, then he stays on this mountain until the inquiry sends someone with better manners."

"And if he won't?" Wren asked.

Sefu shrugged, a whole philosophy in the gesture. "Then he learns what the elder Oduya told you at the drowned village. Those who take from the source are kept by it. The mountain has room."

Imogen had been silent through all of it, sitting with her forearms on her knees, watching the fire the way she watched data — for the pattern underneath. Now she spoke, and her voice had the particular clarity it got when she'd already decided something and was merely informing the room.

"He's telling the truth about wanting out," she said. "That's what frightens me. A man who lies bargains hard and settles cheap. A man who's telling the truth about being trapped will escalate, because escalation is the only lever left. If we stall him past tomorrow, he stops negotiating and starts taking hostages, and the only hostages on this mountain are keepers and us." She looked at me. "So we don't stall. We set terms he can accept, and we build the trap into the terms."

"What trap?"

"The same one he built for us." Her mouth curved, and it wasn't a warm expression. "He wants the journal returned 'witnessed.' Fine. Witnessed it will be — by the full keeper's council, by Maren Oduya, by every carved name in that gorge. And the moment it changes hands, the council's law applies to *him*: those who return what they took may leave. Those who took anything else—" she paused delicately, "—and his men stripped the lower terraces of supplies two days ago, Quentin, they took food, tools, a canoe—"

"—are kept by the river," I finished.

"Are kept by the river," Imogen agreed. "Or they make restitution. Their choice. The law is old, but it has provisions. Maren explained them to me my first month here, when I still thought I might leave."

Something in my chest turned over. *My first month here.* As though exile were a neighborhood and she'd simply gotten to know it.

"You've been building toward this," I said. "Since the day he took the journal. You let him think he'd won."

"I let him think he'd won because he had won, temporarily, and it's cheaper to let a man hold a winning hand than to bleed trying to take it back." She fed a stick to the fire. "You taught me that, actually. Redglass Flats, seven years ago. You tracked that smuggler for nine days and never once tried to take him head-on. You just arranged the ground so that by the time he understood he was caught, he already was."

I stared at her. "You remember that?"

"Quentin." She looked at me across the fire, and seven years and three weeks and one catastrophic monsoon were all in her voice at once. "I remember everything about you. That was always the problem."

* * *

I went out to Rook alone, because the terms needed a single voice and because I didn't trust myself to keep my face straight with Imogen watching. The rain had settled into its long shift, the endless middle watch of a storm that intended to stay employed. Rook stood under an overhang on the third terrace with his men drawn back, and when I laid out the conditions — crest, then descent by the keeper's channel, journal surrendered to the council before boots touched a boat, restitution negotiated for what his squad had stripped — he listened without interrupting, which in my experience is the most dangerous thing a soldier can do.

"The restitution clause," he said finally. "Define it."

"Talk to Maren Oduya. It's her law. I just carry messages."

"You're enjoying this."

"No, Captain. I'm surviving it. There's a difference, and it matters to me that you know the difference." I stepped closer, under the overhang, out of the rain, close enough to see the gray in his stubble and the exhaustion banked behind his discipline like coals. "Nineteen years you said. Here's what I know about serving something that long. Somewhere around year fifteen, you stop being able to tell whether you serve the thing or the habit. This—" I nodded upward, at the rain, the valley, the hidden cathedral of water somewhere above us in the dark, "—this is your chance to find out which it was. Hand over the journal. Make restitution. Walk down that river as a man instead of an instrument. Or stay here and be kept. Those are the options. I didn't write them. I'm just the messenger who's tired of getting shot at."

Rook was quiet a long moment. Water dripped off the overhang between us, patient as a metronome.

"If I agree," he said, "and the council reneges — if they keep my men after restitution — I will burn this valley down around their ears. I want you to carry that to them. Not as a threat. As a fact they should weigh."

"They'll weigh it," I said. "They weighed the Trust's dam. They'll survive your displeasure."

"And you, Underhill? Where do you stand in all this? You were hired to bring the journal home. Instead you're brokering its surrender to a pack of tribesmen on a mountain the maps say doesn't exist. Vane will strip you. Court-martial, blacklisting, the works. You understand that."

"Oh, I'm counting on it." I smiled, and it felt strange on my face, like a door opening in a house I'd thought was empty. "Turns out I own a lot less than I thought I did, Captain. It's the freest I've felt in years."

He studied me for a long moment, and whatever he saw, he filed it somewhere permanent. Then he put out his hand, and I shook it, and his grip was dry and strong and honest, which was somehow the saddest thing about him.

"Two days," he said. "When the water crests. Tell the old pilot to be ready."

"He'll be ready. He's always ready. That's not the same as alive at the end of it, mind."

"Nothing is." Rook looked out past the overhang at the drowned gorge, at the brown thunder of it, and for just a moment his professional mask slipped and I saw what was underneath: a tired man on a mountain that wanted him gone, listening to a river that had already voted. "Two days, Underhill. God help us all if the sky disagrees."

* * *

The sky disagreed.

It happened a little after midnight, when the storm had worn itself down to a steady roar and most of the shelter was sleeping. I was on watch — some habits survive even the loss of everything they were attached to — when I felt it through the stone floor before I heard it: a tremor, deep and long, like the mountain clearing its throat.

Then the sound came. Not the rain. Not the river. Something upstream, in the high country where the source cathedral rose out of its limestone, where centuries of terraces held back what the sky was dropping. A groan, then a crack, then a rush that made the midnight river sound like a garden hose.

I was running before I'd finished deciding to run, and Imogen was beside me before I'd taken ten steps — she'd felt it too, in her feet, in her blood, in whatever seven years on this mountain had put into her. We came out onto the overlook above the terraces, and the lightning gave us one clean white frame of it:

The upper weir — the ancient stone terrace the keepers had tended since before anyone had written down a name — had let go. Not failed. *Let go*, deliberate as a gate opening, and behind it the held water of the high valley came down the mountainside in a single brown shoulder, and ahead of it, lit by the next lightning stroke, I could see figures on the lower terraces. Keeper horns sounding. Rook's men scrambling for high ground. And on the lowest terrace, at the edge of the flood's path, a lone figure bent over something at his feet — dragging it, hauling it, refusing to let go of it —

The journal. Rook had gone back for the journal.

"ROOK!" Imogen's voice tore through the rain. "THE WATER! LEAVE IT!"

He didn't leave it. Of course he didn't. Nineteen years of service, and he couldn't leave the last thing he could still deliver.

The flood came down the terraces like judgment with a schedule, and I was already moving, down the wet stone steps two and three at a time, toward the man and the water and the paper that had started all of it — knowing, with the terrible clarity the storm had promised me, that whatever happened in the next sixty seconds, nothing on this mountain would ever be easy again.

The river rose to meet me, and I ran toward it.
