## 17. Climax: The Rising Water

The river announced itself the way a debt collector does — politely at first, then all at once.

I was on the lower terraces when I heard it: a sound like a freight train that had gotten lost in the jungle and decided to take its frustrations out on the valley. Sefu had told me once, back on the boat, that you never hear the flood coming. You hear the *before* of it. The water upstream backing up like breath drawn in. The birds going quiet or going mad, no in-between.

The birds had gone mad an hour ago. I'd been too busy watching Rook's men ransack the keepers' camp to listen properly. That's the thing about being the best tracker alive — sometimes you track the wrong thing.

"Quentin!" Wren's voice from up-slope, ragged with rain. "The gorge — it's coming down the gorge!"

I looked at the spring first. Habit, maybe. Or something older than habit. The cathedral of water rose out of the limestone as it always did, silver columns of it, serene as a judge while the sky came apart overhead. The terraces around it — those long green steps the keepers had tended for centuries — were already taking water, spilling it terrace to terrace in thin bright sheets, the way they were built to. The whole valley was a machine for handling water. The keepers hadn't just lived beside the source. They'd been having this conversation with it for six hundred years.

Rook's camp, pitched flat and square in the middle of the lowest terrace like a man planting a flag on a glacier, was not part of that conversation.

* * *

Let me back up four hours, because the fight started before the fists did.

Rook had come through the eastern breach at dawn with nine men and the monsoon's first tantrum behind him. He'd taken Imogen at knifepoint — not because he needed a hostage, but because he needed a guide, and Imogen was the only person in the valley who knew the paths well enough to be worth threatening. By the time we'd regrouped, he'd fortified the low terrace, strung his men across the approaches, and put the journal in a waterproof case strapped to his own chest like a breastplate.

He'd stood there in the rain with the surveyor's journal against his heart and made a speech about duty and the Trust's charter and the light of progress. I've heard better speeches from drunk prospectors. But he believed his, which is always more dangerous.

"You're a contractor, Underhill," he'd said. "You know how this works. Deliver the asset, collect the fee, go home."

"I quit," I said.

"When?"

"Just now. Felt good."

He'd almost smiled. There was a version of Rook, I think, that could have been a friend — another professional who read country the way I did, who respected competence, who understood that the wilderness forgives nothing. That version had drowned somewhere between Marrow Bend and here, and what was left was a man who heard the river rising and thought about deadlines.

Now the river was rising for real, and I was crouched behind a keeper's stone wall with Wren, watching nine men try to hold ground that water had already decided to take.

"There," Wren said. She pointed through the rain to where Rook had posted two men at the foot of the stair that climbed toward the spring. "He'll move Imogen up when the water hits. High ground. He thinks like a soldier."

"He should think like a river."

"What does that mean?"

"It means water doesn't attack. It finds the low place and waits." I watched the sheet of runoff coming off the upper terraces, splitting around a granite spine, rejoining below. "Your journal. Is it worth dying for?"

Wren looked at me like I'd asked if the sky was worth falling through. "It's worth *everything*. It's the only proof—"

"Then stay alive and let me do the stupid part."

* * *

The flood hit the valley at mid-morning, and I want to describe it honestly, because everything after happened inside it.

It didn't look like water. It looked like the gorge standing up. A brown wall maybe three meters high came funneling out of the narrows carrying trees, boulders, half a keeper's footbridge, and — I swear this — one extremely surprised wild pig, which crested the wave like a surfer and vanished again. The sound stopped being a sound and became a condition, something you existed inside of rather than heard.

Rook's camp lasted eleven seconds. Tents went to ribbons. Crates went to splinters. Two of his men went into the current and were gone before anyone finished shouting their names, and I will not pretend that didn't register somewhere cold in me. They were doing a job I'd been hired to do. The difference between us was one resignation and one afternoon.

The rest of them scrambled for high ground, exactly as predicted. Soldiers. Rook grabbed Imogen and started up the terrace stairs with three men, the waterproof case banging against his chest, and the other four held the wall to cover him.

Which left me a choice: follow the crowd or go where the water wasn't looking.

Here's what seven years of desert tracking teaches you that river people learn differently: water always leaves a dry line. Somewhere in every flood, there's a seam — a ridge, a rock spine, a channel edge — where the current splits and can't quite reach. You don't fight the flood. You find the seam and you walk it.

I'd spent the last two days walking the terraces with Maren Oduya's granddaughter, ostensibly helping repair storm damage, actually memorizing every contour the way I'd once memorized dune fields. There was a granite spine on the western side, slick as glass but above the surge line, running parallel to the stairs. If I went up the spine, I'd come at Rook from above while everyone else came at him from below and got shot at for the trouble.

"Give me your knife," I told Wren.

"Why?"

"Because I'm about to do something that requires a knife and no witnesses."

"That's the worst plan I've ever heard."

"High praise. Go."

* * *

The spine was everything I'd hoped and worse. Rain hammering sideways, water sheeting across the granite in ropes, my boots finding holds by faith rather than evidence. Halfway up I slipped — proper slip, both feet, stomach-drop — and caught myself on a keeper's carving, some name worn nearly smooth by centuries of hands, and I remember thinking, absurdly, *hold on, whoever you were, I need this ledge more than you need your dignity.*

I came over the top of the upper terrace behind a fallen fig tree, thirty meters above Rook's position, and looked down at the fight nobody knew had started.

Imogen was there. Bound at the wrists, standing in the rain on the drowning terrace with her chin up, furious and magnificent, arguing with Rook even now — I could see her mouth moving, see him not listening. Three of his men clustered near the stair. Below, the flood was chewing at the terrace lip, eating it grain by grain, and the spring roared behind them all like a congregation that had finally run out of patience.

And Rook stood at the center with the case against his chest, and I watched him do the arithmetic. The extraction point was downstream. Downstream was now underwater. His radio was dead — drowned with the rest of the camp. He was a captain with no army, a mission with no exit, holding a journal on a terrace that would not exist in an hour.

He didn't panic. Credit where due. He turned to Imogen and shouted something, and she shook her head, and he raised the knife—

—and that's when I dropped onto him from the fig tree.

Not gracefully. Let's be honest about that. I came down on his shoulders with all the elegance of a sack of wet cement, and we both went down hard on the flooded stone, and the knife skittered away into a runoff channel and was claimed by the river forever. His elbow found my ribs. My forehead found his nose. We rolled, and the world was rain and brown spray and the taste of blood that might have been either of ours.

He was stronger. No point pretending otherwise; the man had forty pounds on me and had spent his career doing whatever Trust captains do instead of starving in deserts. He got on top. He got a hand on my throat. And through the red haze I saw the case strap still across his chest, and I stopped fighting like a person and started thinking like water.

You don't beat a flood with strength. You give it somewhere to go.

So I gave. Went limp under his grip, let him commit his weight forward — and hooked my heel behind his knee and rolled us both toward the terrace lip, toward the runoff chute where the floodwater was pouring off the edge in a solid rope. Not into it. *Toward* it. Because I'd walked these contours for two days, and I knew something Rook didn't: the chute ended two meters down on a shelf of limestone the keepers had cut as a spillway, and beyond the shelf the water split around a granite horn and ran quiet.

We went over together. The world became noise and pressure. The flood tried to peel me off the shelf and I wrapped both arms around the horn and held, cheek against wet stone, breathing in the gaps the way Sefu had taught me — *the river gives you a breath between waves, girl, if you're rude enough to take it.*

Rook didn't find the gap. Or didn't take it. He surfaced once, ten meters down-channel, in the fast water past the horn — the water I'd have avoided, the water that goes somewhere. He had the case. Of course he had the case; the man had strapped his mission to his heart and meant to die holding it. Our eyes met across the current, and I saw him understand, in that final second, that I hadn't beaten him. The river had. I'd just known which door to stand beside.

"Let it go!" I shouted, and I don't know if I meant the case or himself.

He meant to throw it to me. I believe that. I saw the decision cross his face — the professional's calculus, asset recovered, mission salvaged, arm cocked back to hurl the case toward the shelf.

Then the side channel took him.

It came out of nowhere, the way side channels do — a backwater surge, the flood finding an old path through the limestone and remembering it. It hit him broadside and folded him under, and the case tore free of his grip and rode the surface toward me, and I lunged and caught the strap with two fingers and then my whole hand, and hauled it against the horn while the current argued.

Rook did not surface again. I stayed on that shelf until my arms burned, watching the brown water, and the brown water gave nothing back. The river only borrows, Sefu says. But some things it borrows permanently.

* * *

Getting up was its own small war. The terrace stairs had become waterfalls; I went up the keeper's spillway on hands and knees, the case strapped across my own chest now, feeling absurdly like a knight in plastic armor. When I crested the upper terrace, Imogen was there — she'd gotten loose, or someone had cut her free; I never learned which — and she caught my arm as I staggered.

"The journal?" she said.

"Got it." I coughed out half the Fly River. "Rook?"

She looked past me at the flood, and her face did something complicated. "Gone?"

"Gone."

She nodded slowly. Whatever she felt about it, she filed away to feel later. Seven years of loving me had taught her that much about my habits, and mine about hers.

Below us, the valley was unrecognizable. The low terraces were gone — not destroyed, *rearranged*, the river having opinions about landscaping. Rook's remaining men clung to the high ground, weaponless and gray-faced, and the keepers moved among them with ropes, pulling them to safety one by one, because that was the law and the law did not care whose fault the weather was.

And the spring — the spring hadn't changed at all. It rose out of the limestone, column after silver column, serene as ever, as though the flood were a rumor it declined to confirm. Maren Oduya stood at its edge with the council, watching the water carry the intruders' camp away board by board, and when she saw me with the case she didn't smile. She just looked at the spring, then at me, and inclined her head slightly, the way you acknowledge a colleague.

The rain kept falling. Somewhere upstream, the sky was still emptying itself, and the gorge was still deciding how much room the rest of us deserved.

I sat down on the wet stone with the journal against my chest, right where Rook had carried it, and Imogen sat down beside me without a word, her shoulder against mine, and for a full minute neither of us said anything, because the river was speaking and it's rude to interrupt.

Then Wren came scrambling up the stairs, soaked, bleeding from a cut on her brow, and stopped dead at the sight of the case.

"You got it," she breathed.

"I got it."

"And Rook?"

"The river has him."

Wren looked at the water for a long moment. Then she said, quietly, "The council will want to decide what happens to that journal now. All of us. Tonight."

I tightened my grip on the strap. Above us the spring thundered, indifferent and eternal, keeping its own counsel — and I had a bad, wonderful premonition that whatever the keepers decided, the easy part of this river was already far behind us.
