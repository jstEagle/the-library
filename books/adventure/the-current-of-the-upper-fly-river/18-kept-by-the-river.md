## 18. Kept by the River

The rain stopped the way a judge calls order — suddenly, and with the expectation that everyone would behave.

For three days it had come down like the sky was settling a grudge, and now, in the drowned hush that followed, the valley of the source lay steaming under a washed sky, and the keepers' council came down from the terraces to tell us what we already knew: that nothing was going to be simple, and that I, of all people, had no right to expect otherwise.

I had spent seven years learning how to wait. Waiting for a supply boat that never came. Waiting for a radio that would never crackle with my name. Waiting, if I was honest, for a woman with desert dust in her boots to walk back into my life and explain herself. So I knew how to stand still while the world rearranged itself around a verdict. What I had never learned was what to do afterward.

The council gathered on the second terrace, where the water ran shallow and clear over pale limestone, and the elders sat on stones worn smooth by centuries of other verdicts. Maren Oduya stood among them, and though she was the youngest of the seven, it was she who spoke, because the river had apparently decided — as rivers do — that she was its translator.

I stood with my people. My expedition, what was left of it: two hydrologists with trench foot, a cartographer who hadn't stopped shaking since the flood, and me, Dr. Imogen Hale, who had come up this river with a charter and a conscience and had watched the first eat the second alive. Beside us, slightly apart, stood the strangers: Wren Calloway with her arms crossed and her chin up, daring the council to find her guilty of the one honest thing she'd ever done; old Sefu Marr, soaked to the bone and serene as a man who had already made peace with every outcome including the wet ones; and Quentin.

Quentin Underhill, standing in the shallows' edge with her ruined boots and her ruined commission and her face doing that thing it does — nothing at all, absolutely nothing, a locked door with the lights on behind it.

Seven years, and she still couldn't look at me while I was looking at her.

"The journal stays," Maren said. Her voice carried the way water carries — gently, and to every corner at once. "What was taken from the source returns to the keepers of the source. This is not a punishment. It is a tide."

Wren exhaled. I watched the tension go out of her shoulders and something else arrive in its place — relief, but also a strange bereavement. She had carried that journal across half a country, stolen it, bled for it, nearly drowned for it. Handing it over was like watching someone give up a child. But she nodded, and when the council's runner came for the satchel, she surrendered it with both hands, the way you'd return something borrowed from a church.

"The strangers may go," Maren continued, and her eyes moved across us without hurrying. "When the river lowers. The gorge will hold you here four days, perhaps five. The river is not finished with its argument." A ripple of grim acknowledgment went through my team. The gorge below us was a churning brown throat; no boat, no rope, no prayer was going down it for the better part of a week. "You will be fed. You will be housed. You will not wander above the fifth terrace. These are courtesies, not rights. The courtesies end where the water begins."

"And the Trust's men?" I asked. "The ones the river took?"

Maren was quiet for a moment. Around us the spring murmured up out of the limestone, that impossible cathedral of water, and I thought — not for the first time — that it sounded less like a spring than like a congregation, hundreds of voices saying the same word under their breath.

"The river keeps what it takes," she said. "Some of Captain Rook's people were pulled from the side channels this morning. They are alive. They are being tended. They will go down when you go down, and they will carry the same story you carry." Her gaze settled on me, and there was something in it that was not unkind but was very old. "Whether they carry it willingly is their own affair."

* * *

They gave us a longhouse near the third terrace, and the afternoon arranged itself into the strange domesticity of the stranded: my team drying socks over a fire, trading theories about the flood, sleeping in the sudden luxurious safety of a place with walls and guards and no patrol boats. I should have slept too. I had earned sleep the way you earn scars.

Instead I went looking for Quentin.

I found her at the valley's edge, past the last terrace, where the keepers' paths gave out and the mountain began. She was standing with her hands in her pockets, watching the water come out of the earth, and she had the look of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like.

"You're supposed to be resting," I said. "Council's orders. Fed, housed, not wandering."

"I'm not wandering. I'm standing." She didn't turn around. "There's a difference. Ask any ranger."

"Ex-ranger," I said, and immediately wished I'd chosen a different opening move, because her shoulders did the small, controlled flinch that meant I'd scored and she wasn't going to admit it.

"Ex," she agreed. "Funny how cheap a commission turns out to be. Twenty years. One bonfire."

"You burned the journal."

"Pages of it. The rest is in my head, which I'm told is also flammable, eventually." Now she turned, and there it was — the wry mouth, the level gray eyes, the whole infuriating architecture of a woman who had walked out of my life with a duffel bag and a two-line letter and had the nerve to look exactly the same afterward. "You came all the way up here to enforce the council's curfew?"

"I came to ask what you're going to do."

"Ah." She looked back at the water. "The question."

"It's the only question," I said. "The commission's suspended you. Vane will have you blacklisted from every outfit between here and the coast by the time the flood drops. You can't go back to what you were. So. What are you going to be?"

"That's not the question," Quentin said quietly. "That's the question you're asking because it's the one I can survive answering. The real question is standing right behind you in a longhouse, and it has to do with a woman named Hale."

My heart did something unprofessional. "Quentin—"

"You asked." She held up a hand, not unkindly. "You get the answer. I've been doing the arithmetic since the flood. Here's what I have. Option one: I go down with the river, walk into Marrow Bend, and testify before the water commission from memory. Schematics, survey lines, the drowned village, all of it. I take Vane down. It costs me whatever's left of my name, because a disgraced ranger reciting a journal she admits she burned is a witness with a credibility problem, but it's a chance. It might save the river on paper."

"And option two?"

"Option two is I stay." She said it flatly, the way she'd report a water source or a body. "The keepers said it themselves. Those who take from the source are kept by it. I took. I burned pages of a thing that belonged here, and I carried the rest in my head, and either way I'm carrying the source inside me now. Maren knows it. The council knows it. They're giving me the polite version of a choice." She almost smiled. "Stay and be kept, or go and be a memory that testifies."

"And which do you want?"

And there it was. The locked door, and behind it the lights. She looked at me for a long moment, and seven years of silence stood in the space between us like a third person, and I understood suddenly that I had spent seven years waiting for this exact conversation and had never once rehearsed it, because I'd always assumed I'd be the one who had to apologize first, or the one who had to forgive, and I had not prepared for the possibility that we would simply stand here, two ruined women above a sacred spring, and one of us would have to say the plain thing.

I had spent seven years being careful. I was done.

"Stay," I said.

Quentin blinked. "That's — I was building up to—"

"I know you were. You were going to lay out the costs and the risks and give me a dignified way to let you decide on your own, because that's what you do, Quentin, you make duty into a wall and then you stand behind it and call it a choice. Well. I'm not letting you." I stepped closer, over the wet stone, close enough to see the flood still in her eyes. "Stay. Not for the council's law, not for the river's arithmetic. Stay because I spent seven years being the woman you left for duty, and I am telling you, plainly, with no apology attached, that I am done being her. I chose exile over letting Vane's second team reach this spring. I chose the river over the charter. I have already paid the price of choosing, and I would pay it again, and the only thing that made those seven years survivable was the belief that somewhere downriver there was a woman who was good at finding people, and that one day she'd find me."

"Imogen." Her voice had gone rough. "I left to protect you. Vane said—"

"I know what Vane said. Wren told me. Quentin, I was a grown woman with a doctorate and a field team. I didn't need protecting. I needed you." The spring murmured behind us, that congregation of voices, and I thought: even the water agrees. "So here is my demand, and I'm making it without apology, because love that apologizes for itself isn't worth the name. Stay. Be kept by the river, be kept by the keepers, be kept by me — I don't care which, I'll take all three. Testify when the river lets you down, if you must — go, say your piece, burn Vane's charter to the waterline — and then come back up. Come back the long way, the honest way, carrying nothing but yourself. That's the only thing I ever wanted you to bring me, and you've never once managed it."

The rain started again — a fine, warm drizzle, the monsoon clearing its throat, reminding everyone whose valley this was. Quentin stood in it and didn't move, and I watched the arithmetic finally finish behind her eyes.

"You know the worst part," she said at last, and her mouth did that wry thing, but her voice didn't hold it. "The worst part is I already decided. Up on the gorge wall, when Sefu fell. I decided somewhere between watching him survive and watching you walk out of that blindfold. Everything since has been me looking for a dignified way to say it."

"Say it anyway."

"Stay," Quentin said. "I'm staying. I'll go down when the river goes down. I'll stand in front of that commission with nothing but my memory and my disgrace, and I'll recite that journal until Vane's charter comes apart like wet paper. And then I'll come back up. The long way." She paused. "The honest way."

"Good," I said, and discovered my own voice wasn't entirely steady. "Because the keepers are going to want to teach you the terraces, and you're going to be terrible at it, and I intend to watch."

"I'm a quick learner."

"You're an arrogant learner. There's a difference. Ask any doctor."

She laughed — short, surprised, real — and for a moment we just stood there in the warm rain at the edge of the world, two women the river had decided to keep, while below us the spring went on saying its one word under its breath.

Then Sefu Marr came around the terrace path at a pace I had not seen the old man move in three weeks of rapids, and the look on his face emptied the valley of everything else.

"Dr. Hale," he said. "Tracker. You'll want to come. Now."

"What is it?"

"The flood dropped early," Sefu said. "Overnight, near enough. The gorge is passable — a day ahead of any reckoning I've ever made." He caught his breath, and his eyes went from me to Quentin and back. "And there's a boat coming up it. Trust colors. Flying a commission pennant." He paused, and the old pilot's serenity cracked just enough to show the man underneath. "It's not a patrol boat. It's a court. Vane's sent the inquiry to you — and riding at the bow, in a white suit, dry as a sermon — is Aldous Vane himself."

Quentin and I looked at each other, and I saw the same thought land in her face that had landed in mine: the river had given us four days of peace, and it had decided we'd only earned one.

"Then he's early," Quentin said, and turned toward the terraces, and the rain came down harder, as if the valley had heard him coming and was preparing its welcome.
