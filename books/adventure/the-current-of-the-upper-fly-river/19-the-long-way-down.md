## 19. The Long Way Down

The keepers gave the journal back to me at dawn, which surprised me until I understood it was a test dressed as a courtesy.

"This is yours to carry," Maren Oduya said, pressing the oilcloth bundle into my hands. Her fingers were old and strong, the knuckles swollen like river stones. "Down as far as the gorge mouth. Then it comes back to us. That is the law."

"And if I just keep walking with it?" I asked. I had to ask. She'd have thought less of me if I hadn't.

Maren smiled the way water smiles right before it takes your footing. "Then you would be taking from the source, Quentin Underhill. And those who take from the source are kept by it." She turned away, then paused. "But you already know that. You've been kept since the day you arrived. You simply haven't admitted it yet."

I stood there in the gray light with the journal heavy against my chest — Imogen's surveyor's journal, Vane's dam schematics, the drowned village's coordinates, three weeks of evidence that could hang the Amberdale Trust from the highest court in Marrow Bend or drown in my memory forever, depending on what I did next.

The monsoon had come early and come angry. The valley above us was still draining, the terraces streaming, the spring itself roaring like something with an opinion. Somewhere downstream, past the gorge where Sefu had nearly died and Rook actually had, the Fly River was running brown and fast and full of everything the storm had torn loose.

I had four days of travel, one flooded river, two companions, and a head that had better start working like a filing cabinet.

Because here was the arithmetic nobody else could do: the council would take the journal back at the gorge mouth. The courts needed proof. And the only bridge between those two facts was going to be me — standing in front of the water commission with nothing but what I could carry between my ears.

So I did what any sensible desert ranger does when handed an impossible job. I got up before the sun, made bad coffee, and started memorizing a book.

* * *

Sefu Marr found me on the second morning hunched over page forty-something, lips moving, muttering figures like a madwoman doing sums for God.

"You know," he said, lowering himself onto a rock with the groan of a man whose knees had opinions, "in forty years on this river, I have memorized exactly three things. Where the sandbars sleep. Which villages sell good rum. And the face of every woman who ever left me."

"How many is that?"

"Two." He held up two fingers, considered them, put one down. "One and a half."

"Which half?"

"The one who only left most of the way." He nodded at the journal. "How much do you have?"

"Forty pages. Schematics, flow rates, the survey grid for the dam wall. Wren's confession notes. The drowned village bearings."

"And how long will it stay?"

"That's the question, isn't it." I rubbed my eyes. In the desert, tracking was memory work — every broken twig, every shifted stone, filed away and cross-referenced. But that was pattern. This was *text*. Numbers don't leave footprints. "Ask me tomorrow."

He grunted. "You'll remember. People remember what they're willing to bleed for." He looked out at the river, high and brown and moving like it had somewhere important to be. "We go today. Water's dropping, but not fast. Two days to the gorge mouth if the Fly is kind."

"It isn't kind."

"No," Sefu agreed cheerfully. "It isn't. But it is fair, sometimes, to people who pay attention. Get your book. We're burning daylight we can't afford."

* * *

Wren Calloway sat in the bow of the battered skiff the keepers had lent us, and she didn't touch the journal once the whole first day. I noticed. Of course I noticed — noticing things is the entire job.

She'd stolen it. She'd carried it through jungle and rapids and a war's worth of trouble. And now she sat with her hands folded like a woman at a funeral, watching me read.

"You could look at it," I said, around midday, when we pulled into an eddy behind a drowned fig tree to let a debris raft pass. Whole trees went by us, roots first, like corpses being escorted downstream.

"I looked at it for six months," Wren said. "In the Trust archive, under a lamp, copying numbers I told myself were just numbers." Her jaw worked. "You know what I learned? I'm very good at telling myself things. It's practically a profession."

"So why give it up now?"

She turned and looked at me, and there was something raw under all that defiance, something younger than the thief I'd chased across the Redglass Flats. "Because Maren Oduya looked at me and said 'those who take from the source are kept by it,' and for the first time in my life somebody meant it. Not as a threat. As a *description*. Like gravity." She faced forward again. "Vane taught me that evidence is power. The keepers taught me it can also be a leash. I'd rather hand the leash back than hold it wrong."

I turned another page. Flow rates at the confluence. Terrace elevations. I repeated them to myself twice, then a third time, because the third time is the one that sticks.

"You're doing it right now," Wren said suddenly. "Aren't you. Reciting."

"Seventeen point four cubic meters per second at low season," I said. "Dam crest at elevation 1,120. Reservoir drawdown flooding everything below 1,090." I tapped my temple. "Including a village with two hundred years of graves in it."

Wren was quiet a moment. "Does it help? Saying it against the river noise?"

"Actually, yes. The current gives me a rhythm. Numbers ride it down easier than silence." I glanced at her. "Why do you think monks chant by water?"

"I thought monks chanted by mountains."

"Monks chant wherever the rent's cheap."

That got the first real laugh out of her in nine days, brief and startled, gone as quick as it came. But it came. I counted it as progress and went back to page fifty-one.

* * *

The gorge took its toll on the second day, as gorges do.

The floodwater had dropped maybe a meter but the Serrated Reach was still chewing — standing waves the color of milk chocolate, holes that opened and closed like mouths, and Sefu reading it all aloud in his low pilot's voice: "*Left of the tongue, hard left, now pull — pull, girl, PULL—*" while Wren hauled on the oars and I bailed with a dented kettle and recited concrete specifications to myself, because if I stopped reciting I would start screaming, and screaming is bad for memorization.

We came through the worst of it at dusk, bruised and soaked and laughing in that unhinged way people laugh when the alternative is crying. Sefu killed the engine in the long slack water below the Reach and just breathed for a while.

"My maps," he said finally. "Lost in the gorge. Forty years of soundings."

"We'll get new ones," Wren said.

"New ones." He shook his head slowly. "Child, those maps had my handwriting. My handwriting is irreplaceable. It took decades to get that illegible."

But he said it lightly, and I remembered what he'd said falling: *the river only borrows.* Old man had made his peace with the water a long time ago. I envied him that. I was still making mine, ledger by ledger, number by number.

That night, camped on a gravel bar with the fire small and the rain holding off like it was waiting for permission, I tested myself. No journal. Just dark, and firelight, and the river talking in its sleep.

"Page sixty-one," I said. "Spillway design. Three gates, radial type, concrete gravity section anchored into the limestone—" I closed my eyes and walked through it, gate dimensions, anchor depths, the load tolerances Vane's engineers had calculated for a spring they'd never seen. When I opened my eyes, both of them were staring at me.

"What?" I said.

"Nothing," Wren said. "It's just — you sound like the journal. Same flat voice the numbers have. Like the paper got up and grew a conscience."

"Flattery will get you nowhere."

"It wasn't flattery." She poked the fire. "It was the saddest thing I've heard all week, and this week included a man drowning in a side channel."

I didn't answer that. Because she wasn't wrong, and because some truths you file away too — not for a courtroom, but for later, for the person you intend to become. I lay back on the gravel and ran the spillway design again, end to end, and fell asleep somewhere in the anchor bolt schedule.

* * *

On the last morning, I finished.

Page eighty-nine, final entry, Imogen's handwriting cramped and urgent: *They were never meant to return. Vane's expedition plan has no provision for descent. Ask why.*

I had asked why. I knew the answer now. Some knowledge you memorize with your eyes; this line I memorized with my whole chest, the way you memorize a promise.

I read the last page three times. Then I closed the cover, wrapped the oilcloth tight, and tied it with the knot Maren had shown me — the keeper's knot, the kind that tells a story to whoever unties it.

"That's all of it?" Wren asked.

"All of it."

"In your head. All of it."

I looked upstream, toward the hidden valley I couldn't see, toward the spring I'd never forget, toward a woman standing on terraces her people had tended for centuries, who had asked me once — plainly, without apology — to stay. And I'd said I had to go down first. Duty owed love a debt, and the debt's name was testimony.

"In my head," I said. "Every gate, every grave, every lie Aldous Vane signed his name to. It doesn't need paper anymore. Paper burns. Paper gets seized by Captain Rooks of the world." I tapped my temple again. "This version, they'd have to take me apart to get it, and I've gotten harder to disassemble lately."

Sefu snorted from the tiller. "Modest, too."

"It's one of my thirty-seven virtues. I memorized those as well."

We pushed off into the last stretch of brown water, and I began drafting.

Not on paper — there'd be no paper until Marrow Bend, and even then I'd write it out seventeen times before I trusted a single copy. I drafted in my head, against the rhythm of the current, the way I'd memorized: *To the Honorable Commission on Waters, I, Quentin Underhill, formerly senior ranger of the Redglass District, do state as follows—*

The reservoir extent. The village. The expedition with no return provision. The second team under Captain Rook, sent ahead, radios silent. The smashed radio at the abandoned camp. DON'T TRUST VANE. TURN BACK. The schematics, the elevations, the spring itself — though I suspected the commission would strike the parts about carved names and keeper's law as the ravings of a sunstruck tracker, and I'd say them anyway, because leaving them out would be one more theft from the source.

*Item by item,* I told myself, riding the current's cadence. *Say it plain. Say it all. Let them call you a liar. Liars get investigated too.*

Wren watched me mouthing words for an hour before she spoke. "What happens after? If it works. If Vane falls."

"Then I go back up," I said. "With gifts. Openly. And I ask the keepers if there's room at the valley's edge for a woman with no commission and no home worth the name."

"And if Imogen asks again?"

"She already asked. I already answered." I kept my eyes on the water. "I'm just making sure I arrive with clean hands. Or as close to clean as mine get."

The gorge mouth opened ahead of us in the late afternoon light — the place where the cliffs stepped back and the river widened and the keepers' territory began. A figure stood on the shingle bar, small in the distance, wrapped against the damp. Maren Oduya, waiting, as if she'd known our hour the way she seemed to know everything the river did.

Sefu brought us in slow. Wren handed me the oilcloth bundle, and I held it a moment longer than I needed to — eighty-nine pages, three weeks of truth, the heaviest thing I'd ever carried and the lightest, now that it lived somewhere safer than paper.

I waded ashore and held it out to Maren Oduya with both hands, the way you return something borrowed.

She took it, weighed it, and looked at me with those old river-stone eyes. "Do you have it still?" she asked. Not the journal. The other thing.

"Every word," I said. "Every number. Every name."

Maren smiled, and this time it reached all the way up. "Then the river keeps what it keeps," she said, "and you keep what you keep. Go testify, Quentin Underhill. And when the men in Marrow Bend ask who sent you—"

She tucked the journal under her arm and turned toward the water.

"—tell them the source did."
