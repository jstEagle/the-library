## 20. Testimony

The hearing room in Marrow Bend had once been a wool warehouse, and it still smelled faintly of lanolin and old rain, which struck me as fitting. Wool remembers water. So do I.

They'd set up a long dais for the commission — five members, four of them men whose boots had never touched anything muddier than a courthouse step, and one woman, Commissioner Achebe, who had the look of someone who'd actually been to the Upper Fly and hadn't enjoyed it. Behind them hung a map of the river basin so large it covered the whole back wall, and someone had drawn the proposed dam site on it in red ink: a neat little arc across the gorge, like a smile drawn on a corpse.

I sat at a folding table with a glass of water I didn't drink. Old habit. In the desert you don't drink what you might need later.

Aldous Vane sat three rows back with his lawyers arranged around him like cairns marking a trail he intended to follow all the way to the top. He wore grey. He always wore grey, the color of a man who wanted to be mistaken for weather. When our eyes met he gave me a small nod, almost fond, the way you nod at a horse you're about to have put down.

The clerk read my name into the record. Quentin Underhill, formerly senior ranger, Redglass District, commission surrendered by order of the Trust's charter office eleven days prior. Formerly. The word had a taste to it, like river silt.

"Ms. Underhill," said the chairman, a soft-voiced man named Pell whose family money came from dredging, "you've submitted no written testimony. No documents. No survey materials. The commission understands you were in possession of the Amberdale journal for some weeks."

"I was."

"And you returned it to — whom, exactly?"

"To its keepers." I let that sit a moment. "That's not a figure of speech, Commissioner. There are people at the source whose whole job, going back further than any of us can count, is keeping that water. The journal is theirs now. Or rather, it was always theirs, and we borrowed it, and borrowing has terms."

Pell's mouth did something disapproving. "You'll appreciate the commission's difficulty. You are asking us to act on evidence no one can examine."

"No," I said. "I'm asking you to act on evidence you can hear. Every word of it. I spent nineteen days coming down that river with the thing open on my knees, and I have a tracker's memory, which is the only kind worth having. Ask me anything in that journal. Any page. Any line."

He glanced at Vane's lawyers. One of them passed up a leather folio — they'd brought their own copy, of course; Vane had never needed me to find the truth, only to bury it where convenient.

"Very well," Pell said. "Plate seven. The dam footprint."

And here is the thing nobody warns you about memory: it isn't a warehouse, it's a river. You don't retrieve. You wade in and let it come to you, and if you've truly lived inside a thing, it comes fast and cold and complete.

"Plate seven," I said. "Concrete arch, two hundred and six meters at the crown. Crest elevation nine hundred and forty. Reservoir full pool, eight hundred ninety-one. Which puts the waterline—" I closed my eyes, because seeing it helped, the way Imogen's voice used to help, dictating figures while I pretended to sleep, "—forty meters above the lowest terrace of the source valley. Forty meters over terraces that were built before this commission's great-grandparents learned to spell *drainage*."

The room went quiet in the particular way rooms go quiet when a number lands wrong.

"You've memorized elevations," said Achebe. Not a question. Almost an accusation.

"Nineteen days," I said. "Not much else to do but read. And the reading was good. Your surveyor — Wren Calloway, though the journal credits her under her old name, Wren Amberdale-Voss — drew the best cross-sections I've ever seen. She also drew, on plate twelve, the seepage analysis your engineers chose to leave out of the summary report. Care to hear it?"

Vane shifted. It was small, a man adjusting his weight, nothing more. But I'd spent twenty years reading bodies in country where a shifted weight meant snake or storm, and I knew what his meant.

"The abutment limestone is karst," I went on. "Honeycombed. The journal says it plainly: *significant subsurface flow paths; grouting program estimated at three times budget; long-term seepage risk rated unacceptable.* Unacceptable. That's not my word. That's the word of the man the Trust paid to say otherwise, before the Trust decided he hadn't said it quietly enough."

Pell cleared his throat. "Ms. Underhill, the commission is aware the Trust disputes—"

"Let me tell you about the drowned village instead."

* * *

I stood, because some things you say better on your feet, and because I wanted them to look at the map behind them while I talked.

"Follow the Fly up from Marrow Bend. Past the Serrated Reach. Where the river widens just below the second gorge — there's a bend shaped like a hook. On your map it's blank. On the ground there was a village. Odunmo. Two hundred and thirty-one people in the last honest census, which was eleven years ago, before the Trust's 'hydrological survey flood.'"

"'Survey flood,'" repeated Achebe.

"That's what the incident report called it. A test release from an upstream weir, they said. Except the weir wasn't theirs to open, and the release wasn't a test, and the timing — first week of dry season, when every soul downstream believes the river is done rising — wasn't an accident. I've read the internal correspondence. Plate forty-one through forty-four, appended memoranda. They wanted to know how high the water would reach at the future dam site if they moved a lot of it at once. Odunmo was between the weir and the answer."

"Allegations of this nature are criminal matters," Pell said carefully. "Outside this commission's—"

"They're outside everyone's jurisdiction," I agreed. "That's the point of doing them upriver. But you asked why I burned nothing and kept everything in my head, so let me finish. Maren Oduya is the elder who survived. She lost a grandson. She told me — she told all of us, Wren Calloway and Sefu Marr and me, sitting in a half-drowned church with the waterline still stained on the wall at head height — that the river keeps what's taken from it. I used to think that was poetry. Then I watched Captain Rook walk into a side channel in flood stage carrying a stolen journal, and I stopped thinking it was poetry."

I looked at Vane then. I made myself. He was watching me with the expression of a man doing arithmetic, and I understood, finally, that he had never once thought of Odunmo as people. We were all line items. The expedition, the thief, the ranger, the drowned. Costs of getting to the source.

"One more thing from the journal," I said. "Plate fifty-eight. Cultural heritage annex. It lists the carved names in the Gorge of Names — the keepers' memorial, every keeper who ever died holding that water sacred, going back past the oldest date anyone can read. The annex recommends, quote, *documentation and salvage of representative carvings prior to inundation.* Representative. As in, save a few samples for the lobby of the new headquarters. The rest goes under forty meters of water, along with the spring itself, which — plate sixty — the surveyor describes as, quote, *a cathedral of rising water, irreplaceable, and incompatible with project objectives.*"

"Incompatible," Achebe said softly.

"She meant the dam couldn't be built without killing it. And the Trust read that sentence and scheduled the concrete."

* * *

They gave me a break after that, which I spent in the corridor watching rain come down on Marrow Bend like the sky had a grudge. Sefu would have laughed at it. *City rain,* he'd have said. *Rain that doesn't mean anything.* I missed him the way you miss a hand you've gotten used to holding onto in current.

Wren found me there. She'd come downriver separately, ahead of me, and testified the day before — the Trust's own former surveyor, standing up in front of the commission that had employed her and saying, in a clear voice, that she'd checked the schematics twice because she'd hoped she was wrong. She looked tired and clean and terrified in the way of people who have recently stopped being afraid of the correct things.

"They're going to try to make it about you," she said. "Your commission. Whether you're credible."

"I'm not credible. I'm a woman with no badge, no property, and a head full of other people's secrets. It's the most credible I've ever been."

She almost smiled. "Imogen sent word. Through the mission boat. She says—" Wren hesitated, and I braced, because messages relayed through third parties from women you abandoned tend to arrive as wounds. "She says the valley held through landfall. Nobody died. And she says, *tell Quentin the river kept its side of the bargain.*"

I nodded and looked at the rain until my eyes behaved.

* * *

The afternoon session was Vane's.

He took the stand in his grey suit and did what powerful men do, which is narrate history as though it were weather — regrettable, natural, nobody's fault. The survey flood had been a "legacy infrastructure event." The expedition's silence had been "regrettable but consistent with monsoon-season communications failures." The dam would bring power to forty thousand people, and he said the number like a man laying cards on a table, and maybe he even believed it, which is the frightening part about Aldous Vane: somewhere under the arithmetic there may have been a man who started out wanting to light lamps.

Then they asked him about me, and the fondness drained out of him in front of everyone.

"Ms. Underhill was retained as a tracker," he said. "A competent one. She exceeded her mandate, consorted with persons hostile to the Trust, destroyed Trust property — a patrol fuel cache, Captain Rook's report is quite detailed — and ultimately surrendered a document of enormous value to parties with no legal claim to it. The Trust will be pursuing remedies."

"Remedies against whom?" asked Achebe.

"Against Ms. Underhill personally, should she persist in this performance. I'd advise her to consider her position. Her pension. Her standing in the districts — she'll want references, I imagine, for whatever work she fancies herself qualified for next. Ruin is a slow process, Commissioner, but I am a patient man, and Ms. Underhill has cost me a great deal of patience."

The chairman turned to me. "Ms. Underhill. Do you wish to respond?"

I thought about it honestly, for about four seconds.

"Mr. Vane mentioned my pension," I said. "So let's take inventory. My commission: stripped, eleven days ago, by his office, before I ever opened my mouth here. My pension: forfeit with it. My boat passage home: paid for out of pocket, because the Trust cancelled my return authorization. My reputation among the district contractors: currently worth whatever Mr. Vane says it's worth, which is to say nothing, since he owns half the paper it's printed on." I spread my hands. "Commissioner, I've been up the Upper Fly. I've seen what a man does with a ledger when the ledger is the only thing he loves. I own a bedroll, a knife, a compass I traded Sefu Marr a bottle of rum for, and a debt to a river. Mr. Vane is welcome to ruin any part of that he can lift."

There was laughter in the gallery. Not much, and quickly hushed, but enough. Enough to see Vane understand, in real time, that he had reached for a lever and found the machine gone. He'd spent his whole life ruining people who owned things. He had no procedure for someone who'd already given hers away.

Achebe leaned toward her microphone. "One final question, Ms. Underhill. You've recited plates, elevations, memoranda, annexes — from memory, unverified. Why should this commission believe you?"

It was the fairest question anyone asked all week, and I'd had three days to prepare the answer, and it still came out plain.

"Because I'm the only person in this room who doesn't get paid either way," I said. "You suspend his charter, I stay ruined. You approve his dam, I stay ruined. There's no version of tomorrow where testifying was the smart move for Quentin Underhill. I'm not here because it profits me. I'm here because I promised a dead man's mother I'd carry the names down, and because a woman I love is living in exile above a flood line to keep a spring from men like him, and because I burned my one chance at being comfortable the moment I decided the truth shouldn't live only in my head." I paused. "Also, Commissioner, because the river forgives nothing and forgets less, and I'd rather face a courtroom than it."

* * *

The vote came the next morning, and it wasn't close.

Charter suspended, pending inquiry. All Trust operations on the Upper Fly halted. The dam site sealed to entry. Criminal referral on the Odunmo matter to the territorial magistrate — which would take years and probably end in settlements and silence, but which would at least put the word *Odunmo* into a record that outlived all of us.

Vane left the chamber before the count finished. I caught him in the corridor, alone for once, his lawyers having evaporated the way lawyers do when the arithmetic turns.

"Well," he said. "You've had your victory."

"It doesn't feel like one. It feels like a levee holding. It'll need holding again."

"You could have had everything, Underhill. Double fee, reinstatement, my genuine respect — I don't give that often, and it's worth more than you think. All you had to do was bring me the journal and forget the rest."

"The rest was the job."

"No," he said, and for one strange moment he sounded almost wistful, a man looking at a door that had closed sometime in the last thirty years without his noticing. "The rest was the *point*. The job was the journal." He straightened his cuffs. "You'll be poor, Ms. Underhill. Poorer than you know yet. Men like me survive inquiries. We always have."

"Maybe," I said. "But you'll do it downstream of her now. And the river keeps what's taken from it."

I walked out into the Marrow Bend drizzle feeling lighter than a woman with no income, no badge, and a magistrate's subpoena in her pocket had any right to feel. Wren was waiting under the warehouse awning with two tickets in her hand — deck passage, upriver, the long slow boat, three weeks if the water was kind and longer if it wasn't.

"They granted you a place?" she asked. "At the valley edge?"

"Maren Oduya did. Conditional." I took my ticket. "The condition is that I learn the river's grammar properly this time, instead of pretending the desert taught me everything."

Wren grinned. "Sefu says the boat leaves on the morning tide. He also says—" she paused, savoring it, "—he says there's a message waiting for you at the pilot's office. Came up from the gorge with the supply run. Sealed. Smells like woodsmoke."

I knew the handwriting on it before I broke the seal. Seven years of silence, and Imogen Hale still wrote her name the same way, with the tail of the *e* curling like water finding its line.

The note was four words long.

*Come home before thaw.*
