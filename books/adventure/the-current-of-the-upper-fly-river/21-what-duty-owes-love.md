## 21. What Duty Owes Love

*Quentin*

The badge weighed less than I remembered. That was the strange part. Seven years of pinning it to my chest every dawn, seven years of polishing it when the desert dust got into everything, and now, sitting in Aldous Vane's outer office while his secretary watched me with the expression of a woman witnessing a public execution, it felt like nothing at all. A coin. A bottle cap. A small brass lie I'd been telling myself since I was twenty-two.

I set it on the desk between us and slid it across with one finger.

Vane didn't pick it up. He looked at it the way a man looks at a snake that has somehow gotten into his boot. "You understand what you're forfeiting."

"I did the arithmetic on the river," I said. "It came out different."

"Your commission. Your pension. Your standing in every jurisdiction from Marrow Bend to the coast. You'll be a woman without papers, Underhill. No authority. No protection." He leaned back, and for a moment I saw past the tailored calm to the thing underneath, which was fear wearing a very good suit. "And for what? A valley you can't even find again without help."

"That's the other thing," I said, and stood. "I'm going back. Openly this time. With gifts."

He actually laughed. It wasn't a kind sound. "They'll keep you, you know. The keepers. That's their law. Take from the source and you're kept by it. You think they'll let you walk out twice?"

"They let me out once," I said. "Because I gave something back instead of taking. Turns out that's the whole religion. You should try it sometime."

His secretary opened the door for me before he could answer, which told me whose side the building was already on. Word travels downriver faster than water.

Outside, Marrow Bend smelled of wet timber and frying fish and the particular green rot of a town learning to live with its river again. The commission's inquiry had put scaffolding on half the government offices, metaphorically and otherwise. Men who had spent a decade calling the Upper Fly a resource were suddenly calling it a neighbor, loudly, in print. I bought what I needed from a market woman who refused my money twice and took it the third time only because I threatened to leave it on her stall anyway: salt, in blocks wrapped in palm leaves. A coil of good rope. A knife with a blade Sefu Marr would approve of, which is to say one that could be sharpened on any stone in the gorge and would outlive its owner. And cloth — bolts of it, dyed the deep red the keepers wore at their ceremonies, because Maren Oduya had looked at my travel-stained shirt once with an expression I did not care to see repeated.

Wren Calloway met me at the dock with two bags and no apology for being early.

"You didn't have to come," I said, though we both knew the words were ceremonial, like the gifts.

"No," she agreed. "But I stole from these people's river, and then I helped save it, and I've decided those two facts cancel out into an obligation. Also," she added, swinging her bag onto the flatboat, "Sefu says if I don't come he'll teach me nothing further, and I intend to learn everything. The man reads clouds like ledgers."

Sefu himself stood at the tiller of his boat, which had been patched in six places and looked, if anything, more honest for it. He nodded at me the way old men nod when they've decided you're worth the trouble of nodding at. "Tide's right," he said. "River's high but honest. We go up slow."

"How slow?"

"Slow enough to say goodbye to everything you pass," he said, pushing off. "That's how you know you're going somewhere that matters."

*Imogen*

I had been watching the gorge mouth for three days before anyone believed me.

"You're seeing boats," Tomas said gently, the way you talk to someone who has been in exile too long. "There are no boats. There are never boats. Rook burned the last ones."

"There are boats," I said, "and one of them is being poled by a man older than the river, and there's a woman on the bow deck pretending she isn't scanning the banks, which means it's Quentin, because nobody else on earth scans like they're apologizing to the landscape."

He followed my pointing finger, squinted, and went quiet in the way people go quiet when the impossible turns out to be merely late.

Seven years. I want to be precise about that number because precision was all I had for most of them. Seven years since she'd stood in my doorway in Marrow Bend with her hat in her hands and said *the Trust made an offer I can't refuse* in a voice that meant *they made an offer I was afraid to refuse*, and I had been young enough to think love should survive cowardice, and she had been proud enough to think duty was a place you could live. We were both wrong. It took a dam schematics and a flood and a man named Rook to prove it, but we were both wrong, and the proof cost enough that I intended to spend it well.

I did not run down to the terraces when the boat tied off. I want that recorded. I walked, at a dignified pace, and then I jogged, and then dignity lost entirely and I was running through the terraced gardens with the spring thundering behind me like applause, and she was coming up the stone path carrying a bolt of red cloth over one shoulder like a soldier with a rolled bedroll, and she stopped when she saw me the way you stop at the edge of water you've decided to enter.

"Dr. Hale," she said. Her voice did something complicated on the second word.

"Ranger Underhill," I said. "Except you're not. I heard."

"I'm nobody," she said. "I surrendered the badge yesterday. Vane's secretary looked relieved. I think she'd been asked to prepare paperwork for worse outcomes."

"And you came up here with no authority, no protection, and no way home except the goodwill of a pilot and a thief."

"And gifts," she said, and held up the cloth, and the salt, and the rope, as if presenting evidence at a trial where she already knew the verdict. "Maren Oduya taught me you don't come to the source empty-handed. I've been empty-handed my whole life, Imogen. I thought it was time I learned to carry things properly."

There is a version of this scene where I make her wait. Where I list the seven years like charges. I had rehearsed it, honestly, on the terraces, in the dark, listening to the spring. But she was standing there with river mud to her knees and sunburn peeling on her nose and the expression of a woman who had done the hardest thing she knew how to do and was braced for it not to be enough, and every speech I'd written dissolved.

"You look terrible," I said.

"You look," she said, "like someone who's been eating well and sleeping under a roof, and I resent it deeply."

I closed the distance. She smelled of engine oil and salt and the road, and underneath all of it, unchanged, like a signature under seven years of forgery, of herself. Her arms came around me with the carefulness of someone handling something they'd broken once and never forgiven themselves for.

"I'm not asking you to stay," I said into her shoulder. "I want to be clear. The council will ask. Maren will ask. Everyone will ask. But I'm not asking. I'm just — informing you of the local geography. This terrace faces east. The light comes up over the gorge wall at about five. There's a bench."

"A bench," she said.

"The best bench in the valley. I've tested it extensively. It has excellent sightlines and terrible acoustics, so no one can eavesdrop on whatever happens on it."

She laughed, and I felt it more than heard it, and the spring behind us roared on like it had opinions about the matter, which, knowing this river, it did.

*Quentin*

The council sat us down that evening under the great shelter roof, and Maren Oduya presided with the unhurried gravity of a woman who had outlasted every empire that had ever sent men up this river. The firelight did things to the carved names on the shelter posts — the dead keepers, generations of them — that made the whole room feel like the inside of a memory.

"You testified," Maren said. Not a question. News traveled, even up here. Especially up here; the keepers had their own ways of knowing things, and I suspected carrier pigeons were the least of it.

"From memory," I said. "Three days before the commission. The dam schematics, the survey flood numbers, the drowned village. Wren corroborated. Your council's testimony arrived by messenger the day after. Vane's charter is suspended pending inquiry. It won't survive it."

"And your badge?"

"Surrendered. Voluntarily, before they could take it."

Maren considered me for a long moment. Around the fire, the keepers watched — Imogen among them, seated on the far side with the expedition survivors, close enough to touch and far enough to give me room, which was its own kind of message.

"You understand our law," Maren said. "Those who take from the source are kept by it. You took nothing. You gave back what was stolen. By our law, you are free to go anywhere, forever." She paused, and something moved behind her eyes that might have been humor, or might have been the same current I'd felt in the gorge. "But free to go is not the same as having somewhere to go. So we offer a third thing. There is land at the valley's edge, above the flood line, near the gorge mouth. It has a spring that isn't sacred, merely good. It has room for a house, a boat shed, a signal post. The river sends people down that gorge who need pulling out. It always has. It always will. We cannot watch the mouth and tend the source both."

"You're offering me a job," I said.

"We are offering you a place," Maren corrected. "The job comes with it, the way weather comes with sky. Rescue work. No commission. No badge. Only the river's own authority, which is older than Vane's charter and will outlast whatever replaces it." She spread her hands. "Think on it. Or don't. You strike me as a woman who decided at the dock."

I had, in fact, decided at the dock. Possibly in Vane's office. Possibly seven years ago in a doorway in Marrow Bend, when I chose wrong and had been walking it back ever since.

"I'll take the place," I said. "And the job. And I'll need Wren, if she'll have it — she knows the river's grammar better than I do. And Sefu, though he'll pretend the decision took him a full minute."

Behind me, Wren snorted. Sefu, at the edge of the firelight, examined his knife blade with tremendous interest and said nothing, which from Sefu was a signed contract.

Maren smiled — briefly, like sun through cloud — and the council murmured, and that was that. No handshake. No oath. In this valley, things were settled the way sediment settles: slowly, and then all at once, and then permanently.

*Imogen*

Later, much later, we sat on the bench.

She had been right about the light, in the end — it came up gold over the gorge wall and laid itself across the terraces in long stripes, and the spring caught it and threw it back broken into a hundred moving pieces, and neither of us talked for a long time because there was nothing that needed saying that the morning wasn't already saying better.

"I keep thinking about the doorway," Quentin said finally. "In Marrow Bend. When I left."

"I try not to."

"I know. I'm going to say it anyway, once, and then never again." She turned the surrendered-badge absence on her chest over, some ghost reflex of touching a pin that wasn't there. "I told myself it was duty. And it was. But it was also fear, dressed in duty's clothes, and I let them both walk out that door together and I've been tracking myself ever since. Worst quarry I ever followed. Kept doubling back."

I reached over and took her hand. Her knuckles were scarred from the fight on the drowning terraces, new scars over old ones, a topography of every hard year. "For what it's worth," I said, "you found yourself eventually. Most people never do."

"Found myself," she said, "and then got offered a house at the edge of a sacred valley by a council of people who communicate with birds, next door to a cathedral of water, with a view of the best sunrise in the hemisphere." She shook her head slowly. "Duty finally pointed in the right direction and it turns out it was pointing here the whole time. I feel like I should write that down. File a report."

"To whom?"

"That's the beautiful part," she said. "No one. For the first time in my adult life, no one."

Below us, at the valley's edge, Sefu and Wren were already arguing about where the boat shed should go — I could hear fragments drifting up, *flood line* and *you call that a flood line* — and the keepers moved through the terraces on their morning rounds, tending water the way other people tend gardens, and the river ran silver through the middle of everything like a spine holding the world upright.

Quentin squeezed my hand. "So," she said. "The bench. The east-facing light. Tell me about the winters."

"Mild," I said. "Wet. The river rises and everyone works, and afterward there's a festival where they float lamps down the gorge for the names on the walls."

"Names on the walls," she said quietly. "The keepers' dead."

"And the river's guests, sometimes. If they're owed." I looked at her. "There's space on those walls, Quentin. Space gets carved every generation. Nobody decides in advance who it's for. The river does."

She was silent a moment, watching the water. Then, wry as ever, but softer than I'd ever heard her:

"Well. Let's give it a few years to get to know me first."

Down at the gorge mouth, where our house would stand, the mist was lifting off the water, and far downstream — too far to be anything yet, just a smudge, a rumor, a shape against the current — something was coming up the river toward us. Sefu saw it first. He always did. He straightened at the tiller of his imagination and shaded his eyes, and I heard him say, clearly, across the whole waking valley:

"Huh. Company."
