## 22. The Current Remembers

The river woke me at dawn, the way it always does — not with sound exactly, but with a change in its voice. A year ago I'd have said a river only had one voice. Now I know better. The Upper Fly speaks in dialects. There's the low murmur it uses when it's satisfied, and there's the flat, glassy hush it uses when it's lying in wait, and this morning it was doing both at once, which meant someone downstream was going to have a bad day.

I lay in the cot a moment longer than I needed to, listening. Old habits die hard; desert habits die hardest. In the Redglass Flats I used to wake to silence so complete you could hear your own blood working, and I'd learned to read that silence like scripture — which way the wind had turned in the night, how far off the nearest water was, whether the day intended to kill me or merely inconvenience me. The river runs on the same curriculum. It just talks more.

From the kitchen end of the post came the smell of Sefu's coffee, which is less a beverage than an act of aggression. I got up, dressed, and went out onto the deck.

The rescue post sits where the gorge spits the river out into the wide brown country below — a stilted timber building, a winch, two boats, a signal mast Wren had rigged from salvage and stubbornness, and a bell we ring when the fog comes down the gorge thick enough to swallow boats whole. On paper, we're nothing. No charter, no commission, no funding worth mentioning. On the water, we're the difference between a stranded trader's family lighting funeral fires and lighting dinner.

"River's talking," Sefu said without turning around. He was pouring coffee into three mugs with the precision of a man defusing something. "You hear it?"

"I hear it."

"Then eat fast." He slid a mug across the counter to me. "It's going to want an answer today."

* * *

A year is a strange unit of measure out here. The Trust's inquiry ran eight months; Vane's charter was suspended in the third month of it, and by the sixth he'd stopped sending lawyers upriver to threaten us and started sending surveyors instead, who were easier to frighten and more useful when frightened. The water commission's ruling came down in the dry season: the source valley closed to development, the keepers' tenure recognized, the drowned village entered into the record under Maren Oduya's name so that no future clerk could pretend it had never existed. Wren testified for two days straight and cried exactly once, afterward, behind the commission house, where she thought nobody could see her. I saw her. I let her think I hadn't. That's a skill the desert taught me too — knowing what not to look at.

My own testimony took four hours. I recited the dam schematics from memory — every gauge, every floodline, every calculated acre of terraces and graves and spring-cathedral that would have gone under the reservoir pool. The commissioners kept asking me to slow down. I told them I couldn't; memory doesn't have a throttle. When it was done, they took my ranger's commission with one hand and shook mine with the other, and Vane, in the corridor outside, informed me that I had ruined myself beyond repair.

He wasn't wrong about the ruin. He was wrong about the repair. Some things you don't repair. You rebuild different.

Wren came upriver with me because, as she put it, somebody had to make sure I didn't drown in the first week, and because her name was still mud in Marrow Bend and gold at the source. Sefu came because the Fly had borrowed his maps and he intended to spend the rest of his life collecting interest. And Imogen — Imogen stayed where she'd chosen to stay, in the keeper's valley, tending the terraces and cataloguing the spring with a rigor that made the old keepers laugh and then, grudgingly, admire. We see each other the way the river sees its tributaries: constantly, in passing, never entirely apart. Every second week she comes down to the post with dried fish and corrections to my river grammar. Every second week I go partway up to meet her, and we stand on the same rock at the gorge mouth where the water runs fastest, and we don't talk about seven lost years, because the river already carries them and it carries them well.

* * *

The bad day arrived at midmorning, as promised.

Two trading boats, lashed together against the current, had tried the gorge mouth in weather that no sane pilot would call weather — that gray-green bruising light that means the high country got rain in the night even if the sky above you is clean. The lead boat's steering oar snapped on the standing wave off the north wall, and the current did what the current does, which is everything.

I heard the shouting before I saw them. Wren heard it too; she was up the signal mast before I'd finished my coffee, ringing the fog bell in the pattern that means *distress, north channel, two hulls.* Sefu already had the big boat untied. Nobody gave orders. A year of pulling people out of this water had worn our crew smooth as river stone — everyone knew their work the way the water knows its bed.

We caught the first boat in the eddy below Split Rock, six souls aboard, all accounted for, all screaming productively. The second boat had wrapped itself around the submerged shelf we call the Liar, because it looks like safe water and isn't. Three traders clung to the hull. One had been swept loose and was going down the channel toward the Serrated Reach, which is not a sentence, it's a verdict.

I went in after him.

Here is what the desert taught me about moving water, though it took me years and a woman I loved to translate: water is honest. It cannot lie about where it wants to go. Sand lies, scrub lies, a buried footprint will tell you three stories before it tells you the true one — but current only ever says one thing, over and over, and your whole job is to stop arguing with it. I read the seam where the fast water met the slow, angled across it the way Sefu had drilled into me until I dreamed in ferry angles, and came up on the swimmer from behind, which is the only direction a drowning man will let you save him.

He fought me, of course. They always do. Fear makes people cling to the very thing killing them. I got the line around him, got my boots braced on the shelf edge, and let the winch do what arms can't. When they hauled us both aboard, he coughed up half the Fly, looked at me with the wide offended eyes of the recently dead, and asked who I was.

"Nobody," I said. "Just the river's staff."

* * *

That evening the traders camped on our bank, drying their clothes and their dignity by turns, and Wren sat on the deck rail cleaning the winch cable and pretending she hadn't spent the afternoon white-knuckled at the mast.

"You hesitated," she said finally. Not accusing. Noting.

"On the throw? Half a beat."

"Half a beat is a long time down there."

"It is." I watched the water go by, bronze now in the lowering light. "I was listening to it."

She snorted. "Superstition."

"Reading. There's a difference." I tapped my ear. "It changed voices this morning. Low and glassy at the same time. That means rain up high and a lying surface down here. I wanted to know which channel it was favoring before I committed you to a rope."

Wren considered this the way she considers everything — as a surveyor first, a believer second, and only lately, reluctantly, as someone who has seen enough of the river's tricks to grant it a professional courtesy. "Vane used to say the same thing about ledgers," she said. "Everything tells you what it is if you read it right. Then he went and misread a whole valley."

"People misread what they've already decided to believe. Vane decided the river was a resource. You can't read a resource. You can only count it." I shrugged. "Out here we count too. Boats saved, boats lost. But the counting serves the reading, not the other way round."

She was quiet a moment. Then: "Imogen coming down this week?"

"Day after tomorrow. She'll want to inspect your signal mast. She'll find three things wrong with it. Try to be gracious."

"No promises."

* * *

The anniversary fell four days later, on a morning so clear the gorge walls stood sharp as knife cuts against the sky.

The keepers observe it simply. They walk the lower terraces, they pour a bowl of the spring water back into the spring, and those who lost someone in the flood — and the flood took keepers too, three of them, names I carry now alongside my own debts — go up to the gorge walls and carve. The walls are crowded with carvings, centuries deep, every ledge bearing the names of the river's dead keepers, and every year the living add their lines to the record. The Gorge of Names, Sefu calls it. The river's ledger, Wren says. Both are right.

I climbed up with them because they asked me to, and because a year ago I stood on these rocks while the water rose and understood, finally and completely, what I was for. Rook went into the side channel that day. The river keeps him. I don't say that with satisfaction — I've had a year to learn the difference between justice and appetite, and the river has only the second. But I don't grieve him either. Some arithmetic you just carry.

Maren Oduya stood at the carving ledge, ancient and upright, watching each keeper make their mark. When my turn came, she looked at me with those eyes that had weighed the Trust's whole expedition and found it wanting, and she said, in the trade tongue, slowly:

"You lost no one here. Whose name will you cut?"

And I thought about it honestly, the way I try to do most things now. I thought about cutting Rook's name, and rejected it — the river keeps whom it takes, and the walls are for keepers, and whatever else Rook was, he wasn't that. I thought about cutting the three keepers' names myself as an offering, and rejected that too; grief is not mine to perform on behalf of others. I thought, briefly and dangerously, about cutting my own name — the woman I had been, the tracker for hire who took Vane's coin and pointed her compass wherever the money faced, the one who left Imogen standing in the dust of Marrow Bend seven years ago because a commission felt like a spine.

But the walls are for the dead, and that woman isn't dead. She's just retired. She lives on in the flinch I still feel when someone offers me double my fee, and in the habit of checking exits, and in the way I love — carefully, belatedly, with both hands.

So I carved nothing.

Instead I knelt at the ledge, wet my fingers in the spring water Maren carried, and touched them to the stone among all those names, leaving a print that would dry and vanish by noon. "The river already keeps mine," I said. "It's had it since the day I chose the water over the courts. I'm not owed a place on the wall. I'm just borrowing a place on the bank."

Maren studied me for a long moment. Then she did something she had never done in my hearing: she laughed, short and dry as a stone skipping. "The desert girl learns," she said. "Sefu owes me a chicken."

Behind us, Sefu pretended profound interest in the horizon.

* * *

We came down off the gorge in the late afternoon, and Imogen was waiting at the post.

She'd come a day early — she does that, and pretends it's because the trail conditions were favorable, and I pretend to believe her. She stood on the deck with her sleeves rolled and her hair tied back the way it was the last morning in Marrow Bend, seven years and one lifetime ago, and when I stepped off the ladder she didn't embrace me. She took my hand, the one with the ghost of spring water still on it, and turned it over and examined it like a page.

"No name," she said.

"No name."

"Good." She looked up at me then, and the guarded fury of the first reunion is long gone now, worn down to something plainer and harder-wearing, the way good rope wears. "Because if you'd cut it, I'd have had to argue with you on sacred ground, and I hate arguing on sacred ground. Everyone's so certain of themselves up there."

"Wren says you found faults in her mast again."

"Four, actually. She's improving." Imogen folded my hand closed around nothing, around the shape of a name I hadn't carved, and held it. Below us the river went on saying the only thing it knows, patient and total, the voice I fell asleep to and expect to fall asleep to for the rest of my life. "Come inside. Sefu's making coffee. It's terrible."

"It's an act of aggression."

"That too." She pulled me gently toward the door, toward the lamplight and the noise of the family we'd assembled out of wreckage, and paused with her hand on the frame. "Happy anniversary, Quentin."

"Of the flood?"

"Of the day you stayed."

Outside, the current went on remembering — every boat, every name, every choice made on its banks — holding on to all of it, forgiving none of it, carrying all of us anyway.
