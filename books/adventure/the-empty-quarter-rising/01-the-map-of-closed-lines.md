## 1. The Map of Closed Lines

The rain had been coming down on Grayling Junction for three days, and Ada Crane had spent all of them in the rail yard office with the stove lit and the windows fogged, redrawing a line that no longer existed.

The office had been her father's once, before it was hers, before it was nobody's. It still smelled faintly of him — pipe smoke soaked into the cedar wainscoting, machine oil in the grain of the desk she sat behind. The Northern Cascade & Interior had abandoned this junction forty years ago, pulling up steel and moving its money south, but the paper stayed. Paper always stayed. That was the whole trouble with the north: everything left except the records of what it had been. Ada had made a living from that fact for eleven years, drafting surveys for logging concerns and mining outfits that wanted to know where the old grades ran, where the culverts were buried, where the river had eaten a trestle and moved on like nothing happened.

The map spread across her desk now was one of those. The Grayling–Kestrel Extension, surveyed 1909, closed 1913. Her father's handwriting was in the margin notes — small, patient, exact. *Culvert at mile 14 washed out spring freshet. Grade passable by handcar only.* He had written like a man talking to someone who would come after him and need to know. She had never thought much about who that person would be until lately.

The knock came at four in the afternoon, when the light through the fogged glass had gone the color of dishwater.

She said, "It's open," without looking up, because in Grayling Junction the door was always open; there was no point locking anything in a town of forty people where everyone owed everyone money.

Two men came in shaking rain off their coats. The first was young, soft through the middle, wearing a city overcoat that had already given up against the damp. The second was older, sixty maybe, silver at the temples, with the stillness of a man who had spent his life being waited on. He didn't drip. He stood just inside the door and let the younger man do the dripping.

"Miss Crane?" the young one said. "Corven Hale's office, Meridian Salvage and Recovery. I'm Mr. Fenn. This is Mr. Hale."

Ada set down her pen. "We don't get many visitors."

"No," Hale said, and his voice was quiet and even, the kind of voice that didn't need volume because it assumed you'd lean in. "I imagine you don't."

He crossed the room without invitation and looked at the map on her desk. Not touched it. Looked. His eyes went over the linework the way a man reads a face.

"The Kestrel Extension," he said. "Closed line."

"You know your railroads."

"I know my wrecks." He turned to her then, and up close he had the kind of face that was hard to hold in memory afterward — ordinary features arranged with unusual care, like a building designed by someone very competent and very cold. "Miss Crane, my company is mounting a salvage expedition upriver. Forty days' run from the coast, into country no commercial vessel has worked since before the war. We're after a steamer that went missing with all hands in 1913. The *Kestrel*."

Ada kept her hands flat on the desk. Outside, the rain ticked against the glass, and somewhere down the yard a loose coupling clanked in the wind, the yard talking to itself the way it did every night.

"There's no wreck called the *Kestrel*," she said carefully. "There's a closed rail line called the Kestrel Extension. Named for the valley. If you've got a steamer, you've got a story I haven't heard."

"Then let me tell it." Hale drew out a chair with one finger and sat, uninvited again, and Fenn took notes. "In 1913 the Interior Navigation Company ran a packet boat upriver carrying equipment and payroll for the extension's construction. She never arrived. Company records say she broke up in the narrows above Grayling. Insurance was paid. Families were paid. Everyone agreed the river took her." He paused. "Everyone agreed too quickly, Miss Crane. My company has recently acquired certain documents suggesting the *Kestrel* did not break up in the narrows at all. She went much farther upriver than her route allowed. And she may still be sitting where she stopped."

Ada looked at him for a long moment. "You want me to believe a steamboat traveled forty miles off her course and nobody noticed."

"I want you to believe what your own maps will tell you," Hale said, "once you look at them the way I'm about to ask you to."

He nodded to Fenn, who produced a document case, and from it a single sheet of heavy paper, which he laid on the desk beside her father's map. A chart fragment. River survey, northern reach, contour lines fading into blank paper at the top edge where the surveyors had turned back. Someone had marked a point on it in red ink — a coordinate, precise, deliberate.

"We recovered this from an estate sale in Vancouver," Hale said. "Former property of the Interior Navigation Company. The mark indicates where we believe the *Kestrel* lies."

Ada looked at the coordinate. And the bottom dropped out of the afternoon.

She knew that bend in the river. Not from any chart — from a letter. From the last letter her father ever sent home, postmarked Grayling, dated six weeks before the expedition he'd joined went silent. He had written to her mother, and her mother had read it aloud at the kitchen table while Ada, nine years old, sat swinging her legs, and one line of it had lodged in her like a splinter that never worked its way out:

*They want me to run the survey past the Devil's Elbow, up where the maps go blank. Strange thing to ask a railroad man. But the pay is triple, and triple pay means we finish the house.*

Up where the maps go blank. The red mark on Hale's chart sat exactly in the blank.

Her father had gone upriver with a salvage-and-survey crew in the autumn of 1913 — hired, as she understood it growing up, to locate the *Kestrel*'s cargo for the insurance settlement. Eleven men. None came back. The company declared them lost to the river, same as the boat, and paid out, and the file closed, and the north swallowed the whole business the way it swallowed everything. Ada had grown up in the shadow of that silence. Her mother had grown old inside it. And every map Ada had ever drawn, every closed line she had traced and re-traced, was in some way she'd never admitted aloud a search grid — a slow, methodical sweep of the country for the place where her father had stopped existing.

Now a stranger had walked into her father's office and put the place on her desk in red ink.

"Miss Crane." Hale's voice, gentle as a hand on the shoulder. "You've gone pale."

"It's the stove," she said. "Too hot in here."

He studied her, and she had the distinct feeling of being read — not her face but her file, as if he'd carried it in with him. "Your name came to us through the land registry," he said. "You hold title to more closed-line surveys than anyone in the territory. Including several segments of the upper river that appear on no other surviving charts. Your father's work, I understand."

"Some of it."

"He was Elias Crane. Surveyor. Lost with the recovery expedition of 1913."

"That's public record."

"It is." Hale folded his hands. "And I'll be direct with you, because I find directness saves time. I'm offering you employment as pilot-guide for our fleet. Six vessels, shallow draft, running the upper river to the marked coordinate. You know the narrows better than any living soul — the charts prove it, and the charts are yours. The fee is substantial. Triple the going rate." He let that sit. "Triple, Miss Crane. I understand the number has history in your family."

The rain filled the silence. Ada became aware that she was gripping the edge of the desk, and made herself let go.

"Why does a salvage company pay triple for a guide," she said, "when the coordinate's already marked?"

"Because marks on paper and boats on water are different animals. The upper river shifts every spring. Channels close, bars move, the narrows rewrite themselves. A wrong reading costs us a hull and a season. Your maps are forty years old and they're still the best in existence. That's not flattery. That's engineering."

"And the wreck itself? What are you after?"

"Cargo manifest, hull condition, whatever the river left. Standard recovery." He rose, buttoned his coat, and the interview was clearly over in his mind, though nothing had been settled in hers. "Fenn will leave the contract. Take a day. But not longer — we run on the next high water, five days out, with or without you."

At the door he paused, half-turned, and for the first time something moved behind the careful arrangement of his face — something that might have been sympathy, or might have been bait being checked for sharpness.

"For what it's worth, Miss Crane — if the *Kestrel* is where we think she is, then so is the answer to what happened to eleven men in 1913. Including, possibly, why they were sent up where the maps go blank." He tapped the chart fragment once, beside the red mark. "Someone ordered that detour. Companies don't wander off-route by accident. Whoever signed the order knew the risk and spent eleven lives against it. My company would very much like to know who. So, I suspect, would you."

Then he was gone into the rain, and Fenn laid the contract on the desk like a man laying down a card he wasn't sure of, and followed.

* * *

She didn't sleep that night. She told herself it was the contract, the numbers, the plain arithmetic of a winter's wages in a single signature. But past midnight she had her father's last letter out of the tin box under the floorboard, and she read it by lamplight with the rain coming down, and the splinter-line stood up off the page the way it always did:

*Up where the maps go blank.*

She went to the flat files along the back wall and pulled the drawer marked UPPER REACHES — UNSURVEYED, and there they were: her father's field sheets, the ones Hale's people couldn't have known the extent of, because she had never listed them in any registry. Sheets he'd drafted in the months before he died, covering water no chart had ever recorded. She unrolled one across the desk and weighted the corners with the ink bottles, and the lamplight fell across penciled soundings, channel notes, a hand-drawn reach of river forty miles long that officially did not exist.

And there, at the top of the sheet, in the blank space beyond the last sounding, her father had written a single word and boxed it twice, the way he boxed things that mattered:

*KESTREL.*

Not the valley. Not the extension. The boat. He had known. Before he left, he had already found her — or guessed so precisely that knowing and guessing were the same thing — and he had gone upriver anyway, with eleven men, toward a wreck he could already draw.

Ada stood over the sheet for a long time, her breath loud in the cold office, the stove ticking down to ash.

Every instinct she owned said the contract was wrong. Men like Corven Hale didn't cross a thousand miles of bad water for standard recovery; they crossed it for liability, for leverage, for things that needed burying. The letter in her hand said the same thing in her father's voice: *triple pay*, a detour into blankness, eleven men spent. Meridian Salvage was walking the same road with the same arithmetic, and they wanted her to lead them down it.

But the red mark was on the chart. And her father's word was on the sheet. And she had spent thirty years drawing lines toward a place she'd never had the courage or the reason to actually go.

Ada took the pen, signed the contract in the yellow lamplight, and blotted it dry.

Then she rolled her father's field sheets tight, slid them into the oilskin map case she'd carry against her own body the whole way upriver, and stood a moment listening to the rain, and to the yard below, where the fleet's lanterns were already coming up the grade through the dark — six vessels' worth of lights strung out along the old right-of-way like a train returning to a line that had been dead for forty years.

Five days to high water. Forty days upriver. To the elbow where the maps went blank, where her father had written *Kestrel* and boxed it twice, and gone.

She put out the lamp and let the dark take the office, and outside the rain went on falling on the closed lines, filling the channels, raising the river — raising it toward her.
