## 2. Six Tides

The river did not begin at Grayling Junction. That was the first thing Ada had tried to explain to Corven Hale, and the last thing he would ever believe.

It began, as all working rivers do, in paperwork. In gauge widths and load limits, in soundings taken by men whose names had worn off their own instruments. The north's abandoned lines were not abandoned because they failed; they were abandoned because someone upstream decided the cost of remembering them exceeded the value of what they carried. Ada kept those maps the way a widow keeps letters — not out of sentiment, exactly, but because no one else would, and because paper is patient in a way people are not.

She stood now on the foredeck of the *Meridian Dawn*, lead barge of a salvage fleet that smelled of diesel and new money, watching the junction fall away behind them. The yard office where she'd signed the contract was already a gray smudge against the treeline. Her father's maps were rolled in a brass tube lashed to her pack, and she caught herself touching it every few minutes, the way you touch a bruise to confirm it's still yours.

"Engineer Crane."

Hale came up the deck plates without hurry, which she was learning was worse than if he hurried. A man who rushed betrayed appetite. Hale moved like a ledger — everything entered, nothing spent. He was broad through the shoulders in the way of men who had once done physical work and now paid others to remember it. His coat was too clean for the third day out.

"You'll want to see this." He held out a folded sheet of flimsy. "Advance team's morning position. Voss reports they made the old Kestrel Narrows beacon by first light. Ahead of schedule."

Ada took it. The handwriting was cramped, confident, the script of a man writing in a moving boat who didn't think the boat could hurt him. She recognized the coordinates before she finished reading them, and something cold slid down between her shoulder blades.

"These aren't narrows," she said. "Not the way your charts show them. Your hydrographic survey is forty years old. It was wrong when it was printed."

"It got Voss's team through the lower reach."

"The lower reach forgives. The narrows don't." She handed the flimsy back. "There's a gravel bar below the second beacon that wasn't there when my father ran this line. Rivers move their furniture, Mr. Hale. They don't ask permission."

"Noted." He said it the way a man notes weather he intends to ignore. "You'll have the fleet through regardless."

"I'll have the fleet through *if* we run my marks and *if* we slow down past the second beacon. Those are the same conditions I put in the contract."

"And I signed them." For a moment something moved behind his eyes — not warmth, but the acknowledgment of a useful instrument. "You're worth the terms, Crane. Your father's line maps are worth double. Meridian doesn't spend money on sentiment. We spend it on results."

*Your father's line maps.* Not *your father*. She watched him walk aft to shout at a winch operator about cable tension, and understood with a clarity that felt like cold water that Hale had hired a dead man's daughter for the same reason he'd hire a dredge: because she could reach things other equipment couldn't.

The fleet was six vessels, counting hers. Behind the *Dawn* came two barges loaded with salvage gear — cranes folded flat, cutting torches in steel coffins, enough chain to moor a cathedral. Then the tender *Grayling Star*, then two fuel scows riding so low their wash came green over the gunwales. Forty-one men and three women, most of them rivermen of the cheap and cheerful kind who treated the north as a season of high wages between winters elsewhere. They waved at her when they passed on the catwalks. She hadn't learned their names yet. She told herself there would be time.

* * *

By the fourth day the river had stopped pretending to be a road.

They passed the first of the closed-line ruins that afternoon — a trestle standing hip-deep in the current, its pilings furred with moss, one span dropped into the water like a man kneeling to drink. Ada called for a slow bell and took the *Dawn* through the gap with her hand resting on the rail, reading the water the way her father had taught her: foam tells you where the rock is sorry, but the color tells you where it isn't.

Hale appeared beside her as the trestle slid past.

"My survey shows a clear channel here."

"Your survey shows a lot of things." She nodded at the drowned span. "That's the Old Quarter Line. My father surveyed it twice. The second time was six weeks before he shipped out with the Kestrel expedition. He wrote to me about it." She heard herself say it and wished she hadn't.

"Did he." Hale's voice didn't change, but something in it went very still, the way water goes still over deep ground. "A careful man, then."

"The most careful man I ever knew. And the river took him anyway." She turned to face him. "Which is why I'm telling you for the third time: the advance team is running too fast. Voss has the best engines in the fleet and the worst judgment. Call him back to station until we catch up."

"He's finding the wreck. That's his function."

"His function is to still be alive when the rest of us arrive."

For the first time Hale smiled, and it was the smile of a man looking at a balance sheet. "Crane. Every operation loses some percentage to attrition. River work, more than most. Meridian insures against it. The families are compensated. The work continues." He said it without cruelty, which was the frightening part. There was no cruelty in it at all — only arithmetic. "I don't say that to wound you. I say it so you understand what you've signed onto. You're an excellent instrument. Don't make yourself a broken one."

He walked away. Ada stood at the rail a long time, watching the trestle recede, and thought about her father's last letter, the one that had come upriver instead of down, arriving eight months after the Kestrel vanished, postmarked from nowhere, four lines long:

*The wreck is not what they told us it was. If anything happens to me, look under the floor of the pilot house. Don't trust the company. Don't trust anyone who says the river keeps its secrets.*

She had been nineteen. She had read it perhaps ten thousand times since, and each time the last sentence rearranged itself slightly, the way a nightmare does in daylight.

*Don't trust anyone who says the river keeps its secrets.*

Because the river doesn't keep secrets. People do. The river just holds what they give it.

* * *

The radio began dying on the fifth day.

It didn't die all at once. It frayed. Voss's transmissions came in longer silences between words, the way a rope frays strand by strand before it parts. Static crept into the gaps like frost into a crack.

*"—Dawn, Voss. Made the second beacon, running the east channel, bar must have shifted, we're seeing—"*

Then nothing for eleven seconds. Then: *"—seeing bottom where there shouldn't be bottom. Over."*

Ada stood in the wheelhouse with the handset pressed to her ear while the pilot, a gray-whiskered man named Ostrander, conned the barge through the growing dusk. Rain had started at noon, fine and needling, the kind that didn't fall so much as hang in the air waiting for you to move through it.

"Tell him to anchor," she said. "Tell him to anchor above the bar and wait for light."

Ostrander relayed it. The reply came back shredded: *"—no anchorage, holding course, we can see the narrows mouth from here, tell Crane her father's marks are—"*

Static swallowed the rest. When Voss's voice returned it was thinner, farther, a man speaking through wool.

*"—marks are good. Tell her they're good. We're going in on the flood. Six hours of water. Plenty. Over."*

"Plenty," Ada repeated, to no one.

The wheelhouse lamp swung gently with the barge's motion, throwing shadows across the chart table where her father's map lay open, weighted at the corners with coffee mugs. She looked down at it and saw what Voss could not: the narrows mouth was a throat, and throats narrow behind the entrance. Six hours of flood tide meant six hours of rising water pushing *into* the constriction — which meant standing waves at the second bend, which meant the gravel bar she'd warned about would be submerged and invisible and exactly as solid as ever.

"They're going in on the flood," she said again, louder, and Ostrander glanced at her.

"That's what he said."

"That's what he *thinks* he said." She grabbed the handset. "Voss. Voss, listen to me. The flood sets *against* you at the second bend. The bar reads as flat water from upstream. Anchor. *Anchor.*"

The static breathed. Then, faintly, almost amused: *"—appreciate the concern, Crane, but we're salvage men, not tourists. Going in. Out."*

The channel clicked off. Ada set the handset down very carefully, the way you set down something you want to throw.

* * *

They anchored for the night above the narrows mouth, per her contract terms, and Hale did not come argue with her, which she understood to mean he intended to win the argument later, on his own schedule. Through the wheelhouse windows she watched lightning walk the ridgelines to the north, silent at this distance, heat lightning stitching the clouds above the Empty Quarter.

She slept badly in the engineer's bunk, which still smelled of someone else's tobacco, and woke before dawn to a sound she knew too well: the radio, alive, speaking in a voice she didn't recognize.

"—anyone receiving, this is *Kestrel Star*, advance team tender, we've lost contact with Voss's boat, last transmission was—" the voice stopped, gathered itself, started again, younger now, frightened now, "—last transmission was cut off mid-word. Mid-*word*. We're at the narrows mouth, we can see debris on the east bank, we're asking permission to—"

Ada was already moving, barefoot on cold deck plates, taking the wheelhouse stairs two at a time. Ostrander was there, and behind him Hale, dressed, awake, composed, holding a cup of coffee he was not drinking.

"How long ago?" Hale asked the radio.

"Forty minutes. Maybe less."

"What was cut off?"

The young voice on the radio hesitated. "The word was 'help.' He got as far as 'hel' and then the carrier dropped. We've tried raising them on every band. Nothing."

Ada stood in the doorway, rain-wet air moving past her into the warm room, and felt the dread settle into her the way silt settles in still water — gradually, then all at once, then permanent. She knew the river's habits. She had grown up on its habits. And the river had a habit she had never told anyone, because it sounded like superstition and she was an engineer: sometimes, when the Empty Quarter took something, it didn't leave wreckage. It left nothing. No bodies, no planking, no fuel sheen. As though the water had considered the matter and decided to keep the whole of it.

Her father's expedition had left nothing either. Forty years of searching, dredging, diving, claiming — nothing. And now Voss's boat, the best engines in the fleet, gone mid-word.

Hale set down his untouched coffee. "We go in at first light," he said. "Full fleet. We force the narrows."

"We *wait*," Ada said. "We take the marks slow, on the ebb, with leadsman calling depth every length. That's how it gets done or not at all."

"Every hour we wait, whatever's left of my advance team gets colder." He turned to her, and in the gray pre-dawn light his face was all ledger again, balanced, final. "You'll take us through, Crane. That's what I'm paying for."

And Ada opened her mouth to answer — to refuse, to insist, to do something other than stand in the doorway of a warm room while a young voice on the radio asked permission to go look for ghosts — and found that the answer had already formed somewhere beneath her, in the place where she kept her father's letter and her father's maps and forty years of not knowing.

She was not going to let Hale's arithmetic decide this.

But she was also not going to wait for his fleet.

"I'll take us through," she heard herself say. "My way. But I'm going in ahead of you."

Hale's eyes narrowed. "That's not in the contract."

"No," Ada agreed, turning toward her cabin and the brass tube lashed to her pack. "It's in the *letter*."
