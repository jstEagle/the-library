## 3. What the River Keeps

The narrows began where the map said they would, and that was the last thing that went according to anyone's plan but Ada's.

She had waited for the third watch, when Corven Hale's deck crew traded the wheel for coffee and the pilot — a broad, careful man named Ostrander who deferred to her only when the water turned mean — dozed in the pilothouse chair with his boots up. She had taken the skiff from the starboard davit, the one with the cracked thwart nobody used, and she had left behind a note she did not sign: *Narrows ahead. Fleet must hold at the lower bend until I signal. Trust me on this or lose the hulls.* Hale would read it and rage. Hale would read it and understand, in the way that ambitious men understand, that she had made herself indispensable by leaving. That was the only currency he respected.

The skiff's outboard was a Meridian machine, new and loud, so she ran it only in bursts and drifted the rest, letting the current do the quiet work. The river here was older than the maps that described it — older, even, than her father's maps, which were older than the company's, which was why the company needed her. On paper the channel ran wide and lazy between forested bluffs. On paper, the advance team's boat, the *Marten*, should have come through here four days ago with room to spare.

On paper. Her father used to say the word like a curse. *Paper drowns people, little bird. The river only tells the truth.*

She killed the motor at the first bluff and took out the closed-line survey, her father's own hand, ink faded to the color of old tea. He had drawn this reach in the spring of his last year, and he had drawn it differently than the company charts. Where the official survey showed a clean channel along the north bank, his pencil had wandered south, marking a submerged shelf he called *the liar's bar*, and beyond it a cut in the bluff — a flooded slot, maybe six meters wide, that the rail crews had blasted a century ago and the river had since swallowed whole. He'd written one word beside it, small and slanted: *door.*

Ada had spent her whole life reading her father's handwriting, and she had learned that when he wrote a word like *door* in a place like this, he meant it.

The fog came off the water in ropes. She poled the last hundred meters, the skiff's hull whispering over gravel she could feel through the shaft — the liar's bar, exactly where he'd said, a shelf of stones that would open any deep-drafted hull like a letter. Beyond it the channel doglegged hard to starboard, and there, half-hidden by alder and the low gray light, was the door: a slot in the rock where the river ran green and slow and deep, wide enough for the skiff, too narrow for anything Hale commanded.

She went in.

* * *

The cut was a cathedral of wet stone. Water seeped down the walls in silver threads, and the air was ten degrees colder, smelling of iron and moss and something older — the mineral breath of a place the sun had never properly reached. She shipped the oars and drifted, her hand trailing the water, and let the silence tell her what it knew.

Rivers talked. That was the first thing her father had taught her, before knots, before compasses. A river under strain hissed. A river over a shoal chuckled. A river running deep and free made a sound like a held breath, and a river with something in it — a log, a hull, a body — went quiet around that thing the way a room goes quiet around a stranger.

This stretch of river was holding its breath.

She found the *Marten* two bends past the door, on a gravel bar that hadn't existed on any chart drawn in forty years, because the river had built it recently and built it on purpose, the way a man builds a wall.

The boat lay on its side, wedged among the stones like something the water had chewed and spat. Ada grounded her skiff and stood a long moment in the shallows, just looking. The *Marten* was a Meridian survey boat, aluminum, thirty feet, fitted with sonar and a winch and a locked chart locker. She had watched them load it at Grayling Junction. She had stood close enough to touch it.

It was gutted.

Not wrecked — gutted. The winch was gone, unbolted with tools, its mounting bolts stacked neatly on the gravel as if someone had intended to return for them. The sonar transducer had been cut free of the transom. The chart locker stood open, empty, its hasp pried rather than broken. Even the cushions were gone. Whoever had done this had not been in a panic and had not been in a hurry. They had stripped the boat of everything that could tell a stranger where it had been and what it had been looking for, and they had done it with the patience of people cleaning a kitchen.

The engine was still mounted. That was the detail that raised the hair on her arms. A boat stripped of everything except its engine was a boat someone expected to use again — or a boat someone wanted found seaworthy, so the story of it would be believed.

She waded closer, boots filling with river water, and put her hand on the hull. Cold, but not night-cold. The dew on the aluminum had dried on the upstream side only, which meant the boat had been rolled upright sometime after the fog burned off. This morning. Someone had been here this morning, and the fog had covered their work the way it was covering hers.

No blood. No bodies. No gear abandoned in flight — no packs, no rifles, no boots. The advance team was five men and a woman; Ada had signed their provisioning chits herself. Five people did not walk out of the bush leaving everything, and five people did not drown without leaving so much as a hat.

The river was hiding them. She understood that with a certainty that had nothing to do with evidence and everything to do with the way the current bent around the bar, the way the fog had pooled here and nowhere else for the last three miles, the way the silence lay over this reach like a lid. The river was keeping something. It had been keeping something for forty years, and it had just added five more souls to its collection, and it did not want company.

Ada Crane had spent her adult life among engineers, and engineers believed in three kinds of facts: the ones you measured, the ones you calculated, and the ones you felt in your hands. She stood knee-deep in the shallows with her hand on a gutted boat and all three kinds told her the same thing.

They were alive. And someone on this river did not want Meridian Salvage to find them.

* * *

She searched the bar until the light went from gray to gold, working methodically the way her father had taught her to work a site — grid it, walk it, trust nothing to memory. She found cigarette butts under a drift log, three of them, filter down, stamped out hard: a man waiting, and waiting unhappily. She found a boot print in the clay at the bar's upstream tip, size eleven, deep at the heel — someone standing watch, facing downriver. Toward the fleet. Toward her.

And she found the thing that stopped her breath.

Wedges of birch bark, tucked into a crevice in the bluff where the water couldn't reach, deliberate as a signature. Seven of them. On each, scratched with a knife point in a crude but steady hand, were marks she recognized because she had invented half of them: the closed-line notation system, her father's, the one that existed in no textbook and no company archive. A bearing. A distance in skiff-lengths. A word.

*KESTREL.*

Ada stood with the bark in her hands and the river talking its held-breath talk around her, and she felt the fear in her chest change shape — not lessening, but hardening, the way water hardens when it turns to a wheel. Someone out here knew her father's codes. Someone out here had watched the *Marten* come upriver, had stripped it, had taken its crew, and had then left a message in a dead man's private shorthand, addressed to whoever was clever enough and stubborn enough to come alone through the door.

They had known she would come. Not Hale — Hale would come with guns and a fleet and a bullhorn. *She* would come alone, because she was the only one who could read the narrows, and the only one alive who could read those marks.

The message was a hook, and she had swallowed it whole, and the strange thing — the thing she would think about later, in the dark, in the years after — was that she wasn't sorry. The fear was still there, humming in her hands like a live wire. But underneath it was something she hadn't felt since the letter arrived at Grayling Junction: the sense of being *expected*. Of walking into a room where someone had kept a chair for her for forty years.

The bearing pointed upriver, into the cut country where the maps went blank and the river braided into a dozen channels that no chart, not even her father's, had ever fully tamed. The distance was a long day's pole and paddle.

She looked back downriver once. Far off, past the door, past the liar's bar, she could just make out the haze of the fleet's funnel smoke, hanging over the trees like a smudge on glass. Hale was coming. He would find her note, and he would come anyway, because he was the kind of man who believed paper over rivers, and the river was going to teach him otherwise at a price he couldn't imagine.

Ada tucked the birch bark into her jacket, over her heart, and pushed the skiff off the bar.

The current took her upstream without being asked, as if the river had finally decided she was allowed.
