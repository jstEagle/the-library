## 4. The People of the Wreck

The blindfold smelled of spruce pitch and old canvas, and beneath it all, faintly, of river mud — the smell Ada had known her whole life, the smell of her father's coat collar when he came home from survey season.

She had not fought them when they took her. That was the strange part, the part she kept turning over as the ground rose and fell beneath her boots. Three figures out of the alder shadow, moving with a silence no salvage crew could ever learn, and she had stood in the shallows beside her gutted skiff and let them come. She had seen the boat first — the advance team's longboat, dragged half onto the gravel bar with its planks pried open like a ribcage, engine gone, fuel gone, every brass fitting stripped to bare wood. No blood. No bodies. A boat that had been *harvested*, not wrecked.

And then the voice behind her, low and even: "Don't reach for anything."

So she hadn't. She had let the cloth come down over her eyes, had let hands that were surprisingly gentle steer her by the shoulders into the trees, and now she walked inland counting her steps because counting was what she did instead of panicking. Two hundred and forty steps since the gravel bar. The ground climbing. The air changing — less water in it, more resin, more rot-sweet humus. Somewhere ahead a fire, because she could smell woodsmoke now, real smoke, not the chemical stink of Meridian's diesel lamps.

Her father's maps were still folded against her ribs, inside her jacket, wrapped in oilcloth. They had taken her rifle, her knife, her radio — the radio dead anyway, drowned with the skiff — but they had not found the maps. Or they had found them and decided they didn't matter. She couldn't decide which possibility frightened her more.

"Watch the root," said the voice at her shoulder. It belonged, she thought, to a woman. Younger than her, maybe. There was an accent under the words, something coastal and worn smooth, the way speech got after two generations of hearing nothing but wind and water.

Ada stepped over the root. "You're the advance team," she said. "The ones from the barge. You're alive."

No answer. Just the sound of feet on needles, four sets of them, and the fire-smell strengthening, and somewhere off to the left the groan of metal — a deep, structural groan, the kind a hull makes when it settles into itself.

Then the light changed. Even through the blindfold she felt it: open sky above instead of canopy, warmth on her face, and a vast echoing quality to the sounds around her, voices bouncing back from something enormous and curved.

"Hold here."

Hands at the blindfold's knot. The cloth came away.

Ada blinked against the glare of late afternoon slanting through a gap in the forest, and what she saw made her forget, for a moment, how to breathe.

A ship. Not a wreck — or not only a wreck. The steamer *Kestrel* lay on her starboard side in a long meadow of silt and moss, half-buried like a whale beached a century ago, but she had been *inhabited*. Windows cut into her hull where portholes had been, glazed with glass salvaged from who knew where. Smoke rising from three stovepipes along her flank. Rope ladders up to the boat deck. Gardens — actual gardens, bean rows and potato hills — laid out in the black soil between the hull and the treeline, fenced with driftwood. Laundry on lines strung from the fallen funnel to a birch. Children. Two of them, small and quick as otters, frozen mid-game on the deck of a rowboat turned playground, staring at her with the frank hostility of cats.

And scattered among the gardens, watching her with the same expression: perhaps thirty people. Men and women, most of them young, some unmistakably old — an ancient man seated on an overturned dory with a blanket over his knees, his face seamed like dried river clay.

"You're alive," Ada said again, stupidly, to no one in particular. "All of you. The whole team."

"They're resting," said the woman beside her. She stepped forward into Ada's line of sight — mid-twenties, dark hair braided tight to her skull, a scar through one eyebrow, eyes the flat grey-green of the narrows. She held Ada's rifle loosely across her own body, the way someone holds a tool they've grown tired of carrying. "Six of them. Sleeping it off. What you put in their tea will wear off by morning."

"I didn't put anything—"

"We did." The woman almost smiled. "Ilsa Vann. You'll want to hear this sitting down. Everyone does."

* * *

They sat on the *Kestrel*'s exposed port rail, which had been planed smooth and cushioned with netting, and Ilsa Vann talked while the settlement moved around them in the unhurried rhythm of people who had nowhere else to be.

"Meridian sent six people upriver with a contract and a lie," Ilsa said. "The lie being that there was ever going to be a rescue, or a survey, or whatever story they told your fleet master to get him moving. We watched them come through the narrows for two days. We know every boat that passes those rocks, because we put the rocks where they are."

"The channel markers," Ada said slowly. "The false markers. My charts showed the old line running wide of the eastern bar, but the water says otherwise."

"The water is ours." Ilsa shrugged. "We move the stakes. We sink the snags where we want them. The river does the rest. Your advance team came through the Narrows of the Dog at night, in flood, following a chart nobody living should have owned. We took them off the gravel bar before they froze. That's the whole of it. Nobody died. Nobody was going to die. But we needed to know why they'd come, and men with guns answer questions badly, so we asked them the other way."

"And me? You drugged me too?"

"You drank from your canteen after we returned it to your skiff. Two days ago." Ilsa's eyes flicked to hers, unreadable. "You've been asleep in the guest bunk below for most of it. You woke up angry, so we blindfolded you for the walk. Old habit."

Ada's hands closed slowly around the rail's edge. Two days. Two days lost, and Hale's fleet already a day behind her, maybe less if he'd forced the narrows — and he would force them, because Corven Hale treated rivers the way he treated crews, as things to be spent. "You can't stop him the way you stopped us. He has forty men and a dredge barge with a steam winch. He'll drag the channel open if he has to pull the mountains into it."

"He might." Ilsa looked out over the gardens, at the children, at the old man on the dory. "That's a conversation we're having tonight. All of us. And you're part of it now, whether either of us likes it or not."

"Why me?"

"Because of whose daughter you are."

The words landed like cold water down the back of Ada's neck. "You know my name."

"We knew it before you launched your skiff." Ilsa turned to face her fully, and for the first time Ada saw something underneath the wariness — something that looked almost like grief held carefully, the way you hold a lamp in wind. "Jonah Sandoval's daughter. The mapmaker. Do you know what he looks like? Because you have his exact way of standing, like you're braced against weather that isn't there yet."

"My father disappeared forty years ago. On this river. With this ship." Ada heard her own voice go flat and careful, the voice she used for load calculations. "If you're telling me—"

"I'm telling you that you're sitting on the reason he never came home." Ilsa stood. "Come below. There are things we show everyone eventually. For you, we kept them ready."

* * *

Below decks, the *Kestrel* was warm and dim and impossibly alive. Lanterns hung from riveted bulkheads. Bookshelves built into the frames of old cabins. A galley that smelled of fish stew and baking bread, bread, forty miles past the last road, and Ada thought absurdly of Grayling Junction, of the rail yard office with its single hotplate, and had to press her tongue against her teeth until the ache passed.

They gave her tea she was afraid to drink, and she drank it anyway, because refusing hospitality in a place like this would cost more than any drug.

Ilsa led her to a cabin near the bow — the former purser's office, by the fittings — where a steel document case sat open on a desk bolted to the tilted floor. Inside, wrapped in waxed linen, lay papers. Logs. Charts. A ledger with a water-stained cover bearing the mark of a company Ada knew only as a ghost-name in her father's files: *Northwestern River & Salvage*. Meridian's predecessor. Meridian's *origin*.

"This is what your father came to find," Ilsa said. "Or rather — this is what he found, and then chose what to do with. Forty years ago he came upriver with a survey crew, same as you. Same company behind him, wearing a different name. He was twenty-six years old."

"That's not possible. My father worked the southern lines his whole—"

"His whole life *after*," Ilsa said gently. "After he left here. After he went downriver and told the world whatever he told it, and the world wrote down that the *Kestrel* was lost with all hands due to spring ice, and the insurance paid out, and Northwestern became Meridian, and everyone agreed to forget." She lifted the ledger and set it in Ada's hands. It was heavier than paper had any right to be. "Read the last entry. Then read the letter folded inside it. It's addressed to a woman named Marta Crane. I believe that's your mother."

Ada's fingers had gone numb. She opened the fold with the care of someone handling a live charge.

The handwriting hit her before the words did — that narrow, forward-leaning script she knew from a hundred margin notes, from the one letter that had reached home before the silence began. Her father's hand. Unmistakable. Undeniable.

*I have seen the manifest,* it read. *The Kestrel was overloaded on orders, her pumps disabled before departure, and the crew was never meant to reach the coast. Eleven men. I have counted eleven graves on the point. If I send this down, the company burns these people's children out of their homes to bury it deeper. If I stay, I am the only witness they trust, and the only one the company cannot find. Tell Marta I chose. Tell her it was not the river.*

The cabin seemed to tilt further. Ada gripped the desk.

"He stayed," she whispered. "He wasn't lost. He *stayed*."

"He stayed nine years," Ilsa said quietly. "He taught our grandparents to read current and steel. He drew the first true charts of the narrows — the ones we still use. Then the fever winter took him, and we buried him on the point, next to the eleven. His grave faces downriver." She paused. "Toward home. That was his request."

Above them, faint through the hull, a bell began to ring — not the ship's old bell but a new one, iron, urgent. Voices gathered. Boots on decking.

Ilsa straightened, and all the softness went out of her face at once.

"Lookout signal," she said. "Smoke on the lower channel. A lot of it." She met Ada's eyes. "Your fleet master just forced the narrows. Whatever you're going to feel about that letter, Ada Crane — feel it fast. He'll be here by slack water."
