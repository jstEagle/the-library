## 5. Her Father's Last Letter

Ilsa Vann had watched many strangers come up the river in forty years — some by accident, some by hunger, one or two by design — and she had learned to read them the way her mother read weather: by the set of the shoulders, the angle of the chin, the way a person's eyes moved across the Kestrel's rusted flanks. Most of them looked at the wreck and saw only ruin. The girl with the map-calloused hands saw something else. She saw it the way you see your own face in dark water: startled, searching, already half-recognizing.

Her name was Ada Crane, and Ilsa had known that before anyone spoke it aloud.

They had kept her blindfolded through the cut and the alder tunnel and the long gravel spine where the river braided itself into patience, and when they finally took the cloth away, the girl had not gasped or wept or demanded answers. She had stood very still in the grey light under the tarpaulin awning, looking at the hulk of the steamer rising out of the willows like the ribcage of something that had once been magnificent, and she had said, quietly, "She's bigger than the drawings."

The drawings. As if she'd grown up with them on her wall. As if she'd traced their lines with a finger until the paper wore soft.

Now, three days later, Ilsa sat across the cookfire from her and watched her eat smoked whitefish with the controlled appetite of someone who had decided not to trust comfort. Around the fire sat the others: old Tam Oduya, who had been born aboard; the Ferreira sisters, who ran the drying racks; young Pell, still bruised from the scuffle at the boat, watching Ada with the wary fascination of a boy meeting a story he'd been told all his life. The advance team's four men were recovering in the far cabin — alive, chastened, fed — and their silence hung over the fire like smoke that would not rise.

"You knew," Ada said at last. Not accusing. Worse than accusing. Certain.

"We knew someone would come eventually," Ilsa said. "We didn't know who." She turned the fish over the coals. "Your father wrote letters for years. We answered none of them. That was the agreement."

"What agreement?"

"The one he made." Ilsa set down the skewer. "You should see it yourself. Words get bent when they pass through other mouths. Come inside."

* * *

The Kestrel's forward hold had become their archive, which was to say their church. Ilsa led Ada up the boarding ladder — iron rungs welded on by hands two generations dead — and through the hatch into a space lit by oil lamps and window-glass salvaged from a drowned chapel. Shelves lined the curved steel walls: logbooks, chart tubes, tin boxes of photographs gone amber at the edges, a sextant wrapped in oiled cloth like a sleeping bird.

Ada stopped just inside the door. Her breath did something small and broken that she tried to hide.

"This is where he worked," Ilsa said. It wasn't a question either of them needed answered. "Third table from the stern bulkhead. He asked for the one with the lamp."

Ada crossed the hold slowly, touching nothing, her fingers hovering an inch above every surface as if the objects might startle. At the third table she laid her palm flat on the wood, and Ilsa looked away, because some griefs are private even in company.

Tam brought the box. It was cedar, silvered by damp, its hinges replaced twice. Inside, wrapped in waxed linen, lay a ledger bound in green canvas — the Kestrel's official log, its spine cracked, its pages furred at the edges — and beneath it, a single envelope softened by decades of careful handling.

"Read the log first," Ilsa said. "Then the letter. In that order. He was specific about it, and we've honored worse instructions than that."

Ada sat. She opened the log.

Ilsa had read those pages so many times she could recite them, but she watched the girl's face instead, and it was like watching weather move across a plain. The early entries were ordinary: soundings, cargo tallies, the small poetry of a working river — *fog till nine, ran the bars at half flood, engineer Crane reports starboard injector temperamental.* Then the dates climbed toward the last week of October, forty years ago, and the handwriting changed. Tighter. Faster. The ink blotched where a wet thumb had dragged.

*Oct 23. Cargo manifest amended by order of Mr. Aldous Wren, Meridian & Northern. Weight certificates do not match hold contents. I have counted the crates myself.*

*Oct 24. Wren insists we run the narrows at night to make the coast schedule. River is at minimum stage. I have told him what the channel does below the old rail bridge. He has told me my contract is contingent.*

*Oct 25. We are loading ballast above the marked line. I have written to my daughter. If anything happens, the manifest in the strongbox is the true one. The one in the wheelhouse is a forgery. God forgive me for waiting this long to say so plainly.*

Ada's hand had gone white around the page's edge. "He knew," she whispered. "He knew before they ever left Grayling."

"He tried to stop it," Tam said from the doorway, his voice like gravel rolled smooth. "Wren owned the insurance. Forty souls on the books, and the hull insured for triple what the cargo was worth. A wreck in the Empty Quarter, blamed on the river — no inquiry would ever come this far north. The river makes a convenient villain. Always has."

"But the Kestrel didn't sink clean," Ilsa said. "Twelve people made it to shore. Your father was one of them. And instead of walking out with them — the narrows were passable, that first week, barely — he stayed."

"Why?" The word came out of Ada like something torn.

"Because if he left," Ilsa said, "the true manifest left with him, and Meridian's men would have burned this ship to the waterline within the month. They tried twice anyway. There are scorch marks on the aft rail you can still put your hand against. He stayed because the evidence needed a keeper, and because twelve survivors needed an engineer, and because he understood — better than most men ever do — that a lie can be a load-bearing wall. Take it out wrong and everything standing near it comes down."

Ada opened the envelope then. Ilsa had seen the letter a hundred times and still had to turn toward the lamp when it came out, because there was a line in it that always caught in her chest like a fishhook.

The girl read in silence. The lamp hissed. Somewhere above them, wind worried the tarpaulins, and the old hull groaned the way it did every evening at slack water, settling deeper into its own history.

*My Ada,* the letter began. *If you are reading this, then you found the river, which means you never believed the story they sold us, and I have never been prouder of anything in my life.*

*I am staying. Not because I am brave — I am afraid all the time, ask anyone here — but because I did the arithmetic and it came out this way. Twelve lives against one reputation. A truth hidden safely against a truth shouted too soon. I chose the twelve. I chose the hiding. If that makes me a liar, then let it be said plainly: loyalty is a kind of lie, and I have chosen mine, and I will keep it until the river or the years take me.*

*Do not come looking. Do not let them use you to find what I buried. They will try. They will be kind to you, and offer you contracts, and tell you the past is best left professional. When that day comes, remember the swing bridge at mile forty-one, and remember that I taught you every closed line on this map so that someday you could open the one that matters.*

*All my love, longer than the river. — Your father.*

Ada folded the letter along its worn creases with the tenderness of someone handling a wound. When she looked up, her eyes were dry and terrible.

"He let me mourn him," she said. "Forty years. He let me grow up with an empty chair and a flag and a grave marker with no body under it, because the truth needed guarding more than I needed a father."

"Yes," Ilsa said simply. She would not soften it. Softening it would dishonor the man who wrote it. "And he grieved you every day. There are eleven more letters in that box, one for each year he thought he might live. Tam reads them aloud every midwinter. You are not a stranger here, Ada Crane. You are the ending of a letter we've been keeping."

Ada pressed the backs of her hands against her mouth for a moment. Then, because she was her father's daughter, she straightened, and her voice came out level.

"The true manifest. The strongbox. Where is it?"

Ilsa exchanged a glance with Tam. This was the question they had dreaded and prepared for, the one the agreement had always pointed toward like a compass needle.

"It exists," she said. "That's all I'll say tonight."

"And Meridian knows it exists. That's why they sent a fleet. That's why they hired me — not for my maps." Ada laughed once, without any humor in it. "For my name. For my blood. I'm not their guide. I'm their key."

Before Ilsa could answer, Pell came down the ladder two rungs at a time, breathless, the radio handset clutched in his fist.

"Ilsa. The fleet's on the water. They're coming up the narrows — *tonight* — and the man leading them is asking for Ada Crane by name."

* * *

Outside, the river had gone the color of hammered pewter, and far downstream, around the first bend, Ilsa could hear it: the deep diesel stutter of barges forcing water that did not want them, engines driven hard against current and common sense. Corven Hale was not waiting for the tide charts. He was spending boats and men to buy speed, and he was spending them gladly.

Ada stood at the rail beside her, the letter folded against her heart like a second pulse.

"He named you," Ilsa said. "Hale. He knows exactly whose daughter he brought."

"Then he knows," Ada said, and her voice had changed — grief burned down to something harder and cleaner beneath it, "that I inherited more than his maps."

Below them, in the flooded dark between the ribs of the Kestrel, the strongbox waited with its paper truth, and upriver the engines grew louder, tide by tide, like a debt coming to collect.
