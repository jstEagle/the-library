## 6. Hale Comes Upriver

Ada heard the fleet before she saw it — a low, grinding insistence carried up the channel like a toothache, the sound of engines being asked to do what engines should never be asked to do. She was on the Kestrel's hurricane deck, mending a net with Ilsa's daughter Wren, when the sound arrived, and her hands went still in the mesh.

"That's not the narrows," Wren said. She was fourteen and had ears like the river herons. "The narrows makes them stop. The narrows always makes them stop."

Ada stood. Below her, the wreck of the steamer Kestrel lay on its starboard side in the shallows like a sleeping animal, its hull furred with forty years of moss and mussel, its superstructure a village of tarps and rope bridges and cooksmoke. The people of the wreck had gone about their morning the way they did every morning — hauling water, gutting whitefish, arguing about the weather — but now they were stopping, one by one, turning their heads south the way deer turn toward a sound in the timber.

"The narrows makes *smart* boats stop," Ada said.

She went down the ladder two rungs at a time and found Ilsa at the signal post on the old cargo deck, a spyglass to her eye, her gray braid whipping in the wind that always came upriver before trouble did.

"How?" Ada asked. "The narrows can't be forced. Not by anything Meridian floats. I mapped it. I *showed* them the map with the channel marked closed —"

"You showed them a map," Ilsa said, without lowering the glass. "Hale read it the way men like him read everything. As a list of things standing between him and what he wants." She passed the spyglass over. "Look."

Ada looked. The fleet came around the last bend in a ragged line of three — the lead barge *Meridian Dawn*, the pushboat, and the flat scow that carried the crane — but they did not come the way the river intended. They came down the eastern chute, the one the closed-line maps marked with her father's own handwriting: *NO. Rock at low water, rock at high water, rock at all water. — E.C.* They had come through anyway, and they had paid for it. The pushboat's starboard paddle box was stove in and hung like a broken wing. The *Dawn* rode low by the bow, patched with something pale — canvas, maybe, and prayer. And lashed to the scow behind the crane, wrapped in tarps that could not hide the shape of them, were the dredging pontoons. They had cut timber on the way up. They had built a road through her narrows, plank by plank, in the dark, while she slept forty miles downstream of them believing the river would do her guarding for her.

"Two days early," Ada said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "They're two days early."

"Then you have two fewer days than you thought," Ilsa said. "And so do we."

* * *

The fleet anchored a mile below the wreck, in the wide water where the advance team's gutted boat had lain on the gravel bar, and Ada watched through the spyglass as a single skiff put off from the *Dawn* and started upriver against the current, unhurried, its engine at half throttle. There were three men in it. One of them stood at the bow with his hands folded, and even at that distance, even through the heat shimmer off the water, she knew the set of his shoulders.

"He's coming to talk," she said.

"He's coming to measure," Ilsa said. "Talking is what he does with the tape afterward."

They met him at the landing, because not meeting him was not a thing the wreck could survive. Ilsa chose the delegation herself: herself, Ada, old Soren Brandt who had been a boy on the Kestrel when she went down, and two of the younger men with shotguns carried openly, which was a message, and slung low, which was another one.

Corven Hale stepped onto the gravel bar alone. He had left his men in the skiff, and he had left his coat in the skiff too, and he stood there in his shirtsleeves with the river running past his boots, looking at the wreck the way a man looks at a house he has already bought. He was not a big man. He had the kind of face that was easy to look at and impossible to remember, and his voice, when he used it, was pitched exactly to the size of the conversation.

"Miss Crane," he said. "Or should I say — Ada."

"You shouldn't," Ada said.

"Fair enough." He smiled at Soren, at Ilsa, at the shotguns, cataloguing each of them without appearing to. "I'll come to the point, because the river's rising and we all have work to do. Forty years ago a steamer went down in this channel carrying eleven hundred tons of registered freight and a cargo manifest worth more than every man on her deck. The company that owned her collected on her loss. My company inherited that paper, and that paper, and the manifest that settles it, are the last open items on the oldest ledger in the north. I'm here to close the ledger."

"The men you sent ahead," Ilsa said. "Your advance team. We found them."

"I know. I have them." Hale's eyes didn't move. "All six, fed and breathing, thanks to your hospitality. I'd call that a debt, but I suspect you'd call it a hostage exchange, so let's not insult each other." He turned, finally, and looked at the Kestrel — the whole ruined length of her, the tarps, the cooksmoke, the children's laundry strung between the davits. "Forty years," he said, almost gently. "Forty years your grandparents' people have lived on a rotting hull to keep a secret that was never theirs. That's not a life. That's a sentence. And I'm the only man on this river with the power to commute it."

"And the price," Ilsa said.

"The manifest. The original. The log pages that go with it." Hale said it the way other men said *good morning*. "You give me those, and I give you this: the company's claim on these waters lapses. The salvage writs lapse. This channel becomes what your charts already say it is — closed, unprofitable, beneath notice. No one comes upriver again. Not in my lifetime, not in my successor's. You keep your town. You keep your dead. I take my paper and I go."

"And the truth," Ada said.

Hale looked at her then, fully, for the first time, and she understood that everything before this — the landing, the speech, the careful gentleness — had been performed for the others, and that this, now, was the actual meeting.

"The truth," he said, "is a liability with a forty-year tail, Miss Crane. Eleven hundred tons of registered freight on a hull that was surveyed as unseaworthy three weeks before she sailed. A company that knew and sailed her anyway because the insurance was worth more than the crew. A wreck that wasn't an accident — it was a *decision*, made in an office in Grayling Junction, signed by men with good penmanship." He let it sit there in the wind between them. "That's what your manifest proves. That's what I'm offering to bury, and burying it is the only thing I know of that keeps it from burying everyone it touches. Including you."

"I don't scare," Ada said.

"No," Hale agreed. "You don't. That's what I paid for."

The words went into her like cold water into a boot. "Paid for."

"Your father's line maps," Hale said. "The closed lines. There are four sets in existence. One burned with the Grayling yard office. One's in a state archive, sealed. One is in your father's effects — which the company purchased from his widow's estate eleven years ago, quietly, through a land agent whose name you'd know." He paused. "And one is in your cabin on my barge, because you brought it, because I asked you to, and because you have never once in your life been able to walk past your father's handwriting without following it. I didn't hire a river engineer, Ada. I hired a Crane. The last one. The company has been trying to get a Crane back up this river since the year you were born, because your father is the only man who ever saw this wreck and walked away from it, and we have spent forty years and a great deal of money wanting to know why."

The gravel bar seemed to tilt. Ada heard the river, and under the river she heard her own blood, and between the two there was a silence where her certainty had been. She thought of the contract in the Grayling yard office, the generous terms, the way the Meridian man had not haggled. She thought of her father's last letter — *the coordinates, if you ever need to find me* — and how conveniently, how *findably*, those coordinates had appeared in the company's salvage writ. She had thought she was following him. She had been on a leash the whole time, and the leash was made of him.

"You knew his letter," she said. "You've had it. All these years."

"We've had everything he wrote. He was a company man to the end." Hale's voice did not soften, because there was nothing in it to soften. "Which brings us to the offer, and this part is only for you. Produce the manifest — find it, hand it to me, no one ever knows whose hands it passed through — and you walk away with a partnership. Salvage rights on the closed lines, every abandoned spur from Grayling to the coast. Your name on the charts, Ada. The Crane lines, reopened, under Crane authority. It's what he wanted. It's what he died within sight of. All you have to do is give me the paper that says what kind of man he was, and I'll make sure no one ever reads it."

"And if I don't?"

Hale looked past her, at the wreck, at the smoke, at the small figures of children on the rope bridges, and did not answer, and the not-answer was the largest thing on the river.

"Two tides," he said at last. "I'll anchor below the bar and wait two tides. Then the crane comes upriver, and we do this the way the company did it forty years ago — by deciding, in advance, what this channel is worth and what the people on it are worth, and letting the arithmetic do the rest." He turned back toward the skiff, then stopped, and spoke without turning around. "For what it's worth — and I know it isn't worth much — your father made the same choice you're facing. He had the manifest in his hands. He could have carried it out, sold it, spent the rest of his life a rich man and an honest one. Instead he hid it where no one would ever find it, and he stayed with the people who were hiding it, and the company called that desertion for forty years." Now he did turn, and his face was as easy and as empty as a locked door. "I'm offering you the chance to be what he wasn't. Think about which of you the river will remember."

The skiff went downriver. The fleet's lights came on as the dusk came down, three strings of them strung along the dark water, patient as bait.

Ada stood on the gravel bar a long time after, until Ilsa came and stood beside her, and neither of them spoke, because there was nothing yet that was true enough to say out loud. Behind them the wreck breathed its woodsmoke into the evening. Ahead of them the engines idled, counting.

"Two tides," Ilsa said finally.

"He'll give us one," Ada said. "He told us two so we'd spend the first one arguing." She looked at the dark water, at the channel she alone had mapped, at the flooded cut through the narrows that no chart but hers and her father's had ever marked — and she felt the cold recognition settle over her and harden into something else, something with an edge. "They used me to find you. Fine. Then they can learn what else I'm used for."
