## 7. Loyalty Is a Kind of Lie

The tide had turned twice since the fleet's lamps appeared at the mouth of the narrows, and still the Kestrel's great cabin smelled of lamp oil and wet wool and the particular sourness of men arguing past the point where argument could help.

Ilsa Vann stood with her back to the iron stove, arms crossed, watching the settlement divide itself in front of her like a river splitting around a stone. On one side of the long table: Tam Oduya, the fish-weir man, his knuckles white on a copy of the manifest they'd recopied so many times the ink had gone soft. Beside him, old Pietr, who had been born aboard this hulk and had never slept a night on land in his life. Behind them, maybe a dozen more — the ones who had children, the ones whose lungs were bad from the winters, the ones who had always kept a bag packed by the door.

On the other side: Ilsa herself, and behind her the ones who had buried people here. The ones whose grandmothers' names were carved into the Kestrel's rail. The ones who understood that what lay in the strongbox under the wheelhouse was not paper but the only headstone forty dead men would ever get.

And between them, sitting slightly apart with her father's letter open on her knees, was Ada Crane.

"You want to hand it to him," Tam said again, not quite shouting but close. "You want to give Hale the one thing that keeps us safe, because you think he'll honor a bargain. He came upriver with guns, Ilsa. He forced the narrows. What part of that man honors anything?"

"He came upriver for salvage," Tam shot back. "That's all we are to him — salvage. And salvage gets taken or it gets left. If we trade, we choose. We walk out with our lives and our children and we let the past stay drowned, which is where it's been for forty years anyway."

"Where your grandmother drowned," Ilsa said quietly.

Tam flinched. It was a low blow and she knew it, and she took it anyway, because fear made people cruel and she was afraid down to the marrow. "Where my grandmother drowned," he admitted. "And where my grandfather chose to stay silent about it, if the log tells true. Silence didn't save her. Maybe silence isn't sacred. Maybe it's just the shape cowardice leaves behind."

The cabin went very still. Somewhere above them, hull plates ticked as the river cooled against the iron. Ilsa felt every eye slide toward Ada Crane, the way water finds the lowest ground.

Ada hadn't spoken in an hour. She sat with the letter — the real letter, the one that had been hidden in the Kestrel's false sternpost for four decades — and her thumb moved along its folded edge like a woman worrying a rosary. When she finally looked up, her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely steady.

"Tam's right about one thing," she said. "Silence didn't save anyone. My father stayed silent for twenty years before he wrote this, and it got him nothing but a grave without a name." She smoothed the page flat. "But Ilsa's right too. Handing the original to Hale doesn't buy passage out. It buys one trip downriver and then a bullet at the fuel dock, because a man who'd let forty sailors die for insurance money doesn't leave witnesses holding copies of his grandfather's sins."

"So what?" Pietr rasped. "We burn it? That's Ilsa's answer. Burn the truth so nobody can use it against us. You know what burns just as easy as guilt, girl? Memory. You torch that manifest and in fifty years there'll be nothing here but a rusted hull and a story the old ones can't prove."

"I know," Ada said. "That's why I'm not proposing we burn it. And I'm not proposing we trade it."

She stood, and Ilsa watched the whole room lean toward her without meaning to — the way you lean toward a fire, toward a lamp, toward the one voice in the dark that sounds like it knows the way out.

"There are three ways a truth can live," Ada said. "It can be owned — that's Hale's way. He takes the original, locks it in a Meridian vault, and it becomes leverage, then becomes nothing. It can be destroyed — that's the burning, and Pietr's right about where that ends. Or—" she held up the letter, and the lamplight shone through the thin paper so the words showed ghosted on the reverse, "—it can be copied. A hundred times. A thousand. Sent downriver in pieces, in different hands, on different boats, until no one person and no company can ever gather it all back up. You can't bury a flood, Mr. Oduya. You can only try to outrun it."

Tam's jaw worked. "Copies can be dismissed. Forged. Called lies."

"The original stays here," Ada said. "Hidden the way my father hid it, better than he hid it. The copies go out as testimony — signed statements, the log pages, the insurance filings Ilsa's family kept. Let Meridian call their own dead men liars in court. Let them explain why forty widows' pension claims were denied the same season the payout cleared." She looked around the cabin, at each face in turn, and Ilsa saw her do what her father must have done on this same deck forty years ago: weigh strangers' lives and find them worth the weighing. "Hale can't chase what's already scattered. But he can shell this hull to splinters while we're all still aboard arguing. So whatever we decide, we decide fast, and we decide together."

* * *

They did not decide together. Not at first.

The argument broke apart into smaller arguments after midnight — knots of two and three, voices dropped low, the way families fight when the walls have ears. Ilsa walked the Kestrel's spine deck under a sky scoured clean by wind, and everywhere she went she heard the fracture working deeper.

By the forward cargo hatch, Pietr and the Maren sisters were counting supplies in whispers: how many days of smoked fish, how much lamp oil, whether the skiffs could carry eleven children downriver through the narrows if the water stayed kind. By the stern, Tam stood alone at the rail looking south, toward the coast none of them had seen, and Ilsa pretended not to notice him wipe his face.

She found Ada amidships, sitting on the hatch combing with her boots off, rubbing feet that had been wet for two days. Without asking, Ilsa sat beside her and passed her the flask of spruce tea that passed for luxury upriver.

"My grandmother helped hide your father," Ilsa said. "Did you know that?"

Ada shook her head.

"After the wreck, when the company men came through asking questions, she put him in the ice cellar and told them the crew was all lost. All forty-one. She lied to their faces with a living man ten feet below her feet." Ilsa turned the flask in her hands. "She never told me if she thought she was doing right. She died before I was old enough to ask the question properly. But I've thought about it every day since your boat came up our channel. Whether keeping him was loyalty or just a lie we could live inside."

"And what did you decide?"

"That it was both." Ilsa laughed, short and without humor. "That's the trick of it, isn't it? Loyalty is a kind of lie. You lie to the company men, you lie to yourself, you tell the children the river took everyone fairly — and you do it because the truth, in the wrong hands, kills. My grandmother's lie kept one man alive. Your father's silence kept forty families from being hunted down for what they knew. Every lie in that strongbox was told to protect somebody." She looked at Ada sideways. "The question isn't whether we lie. It's who we're lying for."

Ada was quiet a long moment. Downriver, faint as a struck match, a lamp moved in the narrows — Hale's picket boat, walking its patrol, patient as a spider.

"When I was nine," Ada said at last, "my mother told me my father died a hero. Railroad man, lost with his survey crew, bridge collapsed, terrible tragedy. She believed it her whole life. She died believing it." Her voice didn't break; it just went thin, like wire pulled taut. "This letter says he lived twenty years after that bridge. Twenty years hiding on this wreck, guarding proof that the collapse wasn't an accident — that the company let it happen and paid themselves for the corpses. He let my mother grieve a hero rather than hand her a truth that would have put her in danger." She turned to Ilsa, and her eyes in the dark were fierce and wet at once. "So don't ask me whether loyalty is a lie. I'm the daughter of both halves of that answer. Ask me instead how we make sure no one else ever has to be."

* * *

Near dawn, the tide slack and gray, the settlement gathered one final time in the great cabin, and this time it was Tam Oduya who spoke first.

"I've been awake all night doing sums," he said. "Not fish sums. Fear sums." He laid his hands flat on the table. "Every path costs. Trading costs us the truth and probably our lives. Burning costs us the past and buys us nothing but a shorter argument. Staying silent costs another generation exactly what ours cost." He looked up, and to Ilsa's astonishment there was no anger left in his face — only exhaustion, and under it something like relief. "Crane's way costs the most work and the most risk, but it's the only one where we're not the ones paying forever. Copies downriver. Originals hidden. And us—" he exhaled, "—us deciding, for once, to stop being a secret."

"It means leaving," Pietr said softly. "Some of us. Carrying the testimony out through the narrows with Hale's boats on the water."

"It means some of us staying, too," Ilsa said. "Someone has to keep the originals. Someone has to keep the light burning so the ones who come back can find home." She looked around the cabin — at the Maren sisters, at Pietr, at the children asleep in the corners wrapped in sailcloth, at Ada Crane standing at the table's end like a keel holding a boat straight. "My grandmother lied to protect one stranger. I'm not going to lie to protect myself. We split the truth into fragments and we send it downriver in every hull that will float, and we trust the river to deliver what the company can't confiscate."

For a moment nobody moved. Then Tam nodded, once, and Pietr, and the Maren sisters, and the room broke apart — not fracturing now but dividing, the way a crew divides when the order comes and every hand knows its station.

Ada caught Ilsa's arm as the others filed out. Her voice was low. "There's a flooded cut on the east bank, three miles up. My maps show it. At high water it runs deep enough for loaded skiffs, and it comes out below Hale's picket line." She hesitated. "If we're going to send the truth downriver, we'll need a channel he doesn't know exists."

Isla looked at her — this stranger who had arrived blindfolded five days ago and was now, impossibly, family — and felt the old fear turn over inside her and become something else entirely.

"And if he finds out?" she asked.

Ada folded her father's letter closed, gently, like closing a coffin.

"Then tomorrow's tide," she said, "is going to be the longest one of our lives."
