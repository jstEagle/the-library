## 8. The Fifth Tide

Slack water came at dawn, and with it the sound Ada had been dreading for two days: engines.

She heard them first as a tremor in the soles of her boots, a vibration traveling up through the Kestrel's ancient hull and into the bones of the settlement itself. The children felt it too — she watched them pause in their morning work among the net racks, heads tilting like birds, before scattering toward the palisade without being told. They had grown up listening to the river the way other children listened to lullabies. They knew what an engine meant.

Ada climbed to the old pilothouse, where Ilsa was already at the glass, her gray braid tight against her back, her hands steady on the rail in the way of a woman who had spent forty years refusing to be afraid.

"Two barges," Ilsa said. "He forced the narrows."

"He'll have lost half his draft doing it." Ada took the glass herself. Through the river mist the lead barge emerged the way a bruise emerges — slowly, then all at once, black steel riding low and mean, Hale's pennant snapping at its mast. Behind it came the second, and behind that, skiffs. Men with rifles slung across their backs the way a farmer carries tools: casually, professionally, without apology.

"He'll take the hulk," Ilsa said. "It's the only deep water left after the narrows. He knows we know that. He's counting on us having nowhere to go."

Ada lowered the glass. Her palms were sweating against the brass. "There's one place."

Ilsa turned to look at her, and in the flat early light her blind eye — the one the river had taken from her mother, the story went — caught the sun like a coin at the bottom of clear water.

"The swing bridge," Ada said.

* * *

Her father had drawn it for her when she was nine years old, on the back of a supply manifest, in the shorthand of a man who thought in rails: *Old line crosses the channel at mile 41. Swing span. Counterweighted. Last turned in '61 to let the dredges through; seized since.* He had drawn the gear train too, the great ring of teeth that rotated the span on its center pier, and he had written beneath it, almost as an afterthought: *If you ever need to stop a river, this is how.*

She had thought it a game then. A father's fancy. She had not understood until Grayling Junction, holding his last letter in a stranger's office, that everything her father drew was a message, and every message was addressed to her.

Now she stood in the bridge tender's house at mile 41, a shack of rotten boards and pigeon droppings perched on pilings above the channel, with Ilsa's son Tam prying at the floor hatch beside her and the engines growing louder below.

"It's under the floor?" Tam asked. He was sixteen, all elbows and urgency, and he looked at her the way the whole settlement had begun to look at her — as if she were a map they'd found washed up on the bank, full of lines leading somewhere none of them had ever gone.

"Under the floor, under the mud, under forty years of rust." Ada got her fingers into the hatch seam and pulled. The board came up with a shriek, exhaling air that smelled of iron and rot and time. Below, half-swallowed by silt, lay the gearbox housing — a drum of riveted steel the size of a grave.

"We can't turn that," Tam said flatly. "Not by hand. Not in a year."

"We're not turning it." She knelt, feeling along the housing's edge until her fingers found what her father's drawing had promised: the counterweight cable, tarred hemp over wire core, running up through a sheave into the dark of the span overhead. The swing bridge did not need power to close. It needed only to be *unlocked*. The span weighed four hundred tons, but it hung balanced on its center pier like a scale waiting for a thumb. Forty years of rust had welded it shut — but rust was just another kind of lock, and locks could be persuaded.

She needed fire, and she needed the tide.

* * *

Hale's men came ashore at the Kestrel within the hour.

Ada watched it from the bridge house through the glass, and the watching was its own kind of wound. They came in three skiffs, fanning out across the slack water with the smooth coordination of men who had done this before, in places whose names never made the newspapers. They boarded the hulk at her breached stern, and within minutes the settlement's people were being driven out onto the open deck — Ilsa last, walking slowly, hands visible, her chin lifted in a defiance that cost her nothing because it was not performance. It was simply who she was.

"They won't hurt them yet," Ada said to Tam, though her voice shook. "Hale wants the manifest. Dead witnesses write no confessions." Even as she said it she heard the flaw in it, the ugly arithmetic, and hated that she had learned to think in Hale's numbers.

The manifest was already moving. That was the only mercy of the morning. While the raiders swept the forward decks, Ilsa's people passed the oilskin packet hand to hand down the Kestrel's spine — through the coal bunker, along the flooded bilge walkway, out through the scuttle where the children swam in summer — to the gravel bar, to the cookfire, to Old Sef, who sat wrapped in blankets pretending to be nothing more than an old man warming himself, while beneath his blanket the truth of forty years pressed against his ribs like a second heart.

And the fire was already laid. That had been Ada's work before dawn, in the hour when the tide stood still and even the river seemed to hold its breath: kindling and lamp oil packed around the gearbox housing, around the main locking pin, around the sheave where the counterweight cable ran. Rust surrendered to heat the way ice surrendered to spring. Her father had known it. She was betting the lives of everyone she had come to love that he had been right.

Below her, the lead barge slid into the channel below the bridge, riding the last of the flood, and Corven Hale stood on its foredeck with his hands clasped behind his back, looking up at the derelict span the way a man looks at a door he intends to walk through.

Ada struck the match.

* * *

Fire is patient in the way water is patient. It does not hurry. It finds the dry places, the oiled places, the places where forty years of grease have soaked into wood and waited. Ada crouched at the edge of the burned hatch with Tam gripping her arm, and together they watched the flames walk down into the machinery, and she counted.

Her father had taught her to count, too. *A riverman counts tides, a railman counts miles, and a woman who is both counts seconds.*

At ninety seconds the grease caught — a soft whump she felt more than heard, a bloom of orange light inside the housing like a lantern lit in a cellar. At two minutes the tarred cable began to smoke. At three minutes and some seconds that she would remember for the rest of her life, the main locking pin — a shaft of forged steel thick as her waist, seized solid since before she was born — gave up its argument with physics.

The sound was thunder inverted. It came from below and rolled upward through the piers, through the pilings, through the soles of her boots and the cage of her ribs, and then the span moved.

Four hundred tons of riveted steel, balanced for forty years on a knife-edge of engineering, swung out over the channel like the arm of God closing a gate. Ada saw it happen in fragments: the counterweight descending past the bridge house window, trailing burning rope; the far end of the span sweeping down toward the water with a grace no wreck should own; Hale's barge, mid-channel, too deep-laden from forcing the narrows to turn, too proud to try.

The impact threw her off her knees. The bridge house lurched on its pilings; Tam went down hard; the glass cracked in its frame. Out on the river the world had become noise — the scream of steel on the barge's superstructure, the span grinding home across the channel with a groan that seemed to come up from the riverbed itself, steam bursting white from the barge's ruptured flank.

Then, strangely, quiet. The span rested across the channel at a drunken angle, its far end buried in the shallows, its near end crumpled into the barge's deck like a hand pressed flat on a table. The channel was closed. Nothing larger than a skiff would pass mile 41 again this season, perhaps ever.

"We did it," Tam breathed. He was laughing, or crying, or both. "We stopped him—"

Ada was already at the glass, and what she saw there killed the words in her throat.

The barge was settling, yes — listing hard, water pouring from her wounds, Hale's crew spilling into their skiffs. But the span had not fallen clean. Its near end had come down across the Kestrel's approach, and between the wreckage and the hulk lay a trap of twisted steel and shallow water that would hold the settlement's boats as surely as it held Hale's. And on the gravel bar, where the fire had spread from the bridge house timbers along the driftwood line, the cookfire had become a wildfire, and Old Sef was running — running with the oilskin packet clutched to his chest, running through the smoke with raiders behind him, and somewhere in the tumble of flame and black wind the packet had torn, or dropped, or opened—

She saw it through the glass: pages lifting on the thermal, dozens of them, her father's handwriting rising into the morning air like a flock of startled birds. Some drifted toward the river. Some fell into the fire. Raiders lunged and snatched at the falling ones, stuffing them into jackets, trampling others into the mud, and Old Sef stood in the middle of it all with empty hands and an expression Ada could read even at four hundred yards, even through smoke: the face of a man watching a body burn.

"No," Tam said, seeing her face. "No, Ada, the bridge held, we bought the tide—"

"We bought the tide," she said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears, calm and terrible. "And we're spending it. Get down to the boats. Wake everyone. We leave on the next water or we don't leave at all."

Tam stared at her. "Leave? The channel's blocked both ways now. You trapped us *with* them—"

"I know." She was already moving, taking the stairs two at a time, the glass abandoned, the map of the flooded cut already unrolling behind her eyes — the narrows, the drowned rail bed, the channel her father had drawn for her when she was nine years old. *If you ever need to stop a river, this is how. If you ever need to leave one, this is where.*

Half the truth was ash on the wind and half was in Hale's pockets, and the man himself was climbing out of a sinking barge onto her father's wrecked bridge, alive, furious, and between her and the only way out.

"One tide," Ada said to the river, to her father, to no one. "Make it be enough."
