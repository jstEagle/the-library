## 1. Fuel, Rain, and Rumors

The rain at Sable Creek came down the way it always did in the interior of Cape Reckoning—not falling so much as arriving, all at once, like something that had been waiting for her.

Ines Vane brought the Cessna in low over the tree line, one wheel kissing the mud before the other, and let the strip do what it always did: grab the tires, slurp them sideways, try to flip her into the treeline for the crime of landing on it. She corrected with a hand that didn't shake because it had done this four hundred and eleven times. She knew the number. She knew most of her numbers.

Four hundred and eleven landings since she'd come north. Two hundred and six of those in rain. Ninety-one strips on her chart, thirty-eight still flyable this time of year, eleven that existed mostly as rumor and a man's insistence. She'd counted the miles too—somewhere past sixty thousand now, though she'd stopped updating that figure last spring, when the odometer in her head had started to feel less like a record and more like a sentence being served.

She taxied to the fuel drums under the tarpaulin and cut the engine, and the silence after was enormous, the way it always was, the propeller's absence ringing in her teeth.

"Vane!" Hollis came out of the shed with a drum cart and his collar up, a big soft-bellied man who moved like the mud owed him money. "You're late."

"I'm early," Ines said, climbing down into ankle-deep water. "The strip's just further away than it used to be."

"The whole coast is further away than it used to be." He wrestled the drum alongside her wing. "Monsoon's three weeks early. River at Halfway Bend is up two meters since Tuesday. You hear that?"

"I hear everything. That's my job."

"That and hauling penicillin to people who won't thank you."

"They thank me fine," Ines said. "They just don't mean it."

She worked while they talked, because work was the point. Fuel cap off, hose in, pump handle ticking its small metronome count—one, two, three, four—while Hollis told her what he always told her: who was sick, who was dead, who was fighting with whom, who had a new boat, who had lost theirs. The interior ran on two currencies up here, diesel and talk, and Ines Vane was the mint for both of them. She carried amoxicillin and gossip in equal weight, and both were perishable, and both kept better if you never stayed anywhere long enough to be affected by them.

That was the trick of it. That was the whole trick, five years running.

"You look thin," Hollis said.

"I look efficient."

"You look like a woman who eats standing up in the rain."

"I eat sitting down," Ines said. "In the plane. Out of the rain. It's called planning."

He laughed and shook his head, and she let him have it. Hollis had been trying to feed her for three years. He'd stop eventually. Everyone did. People got tired of offering things to someone who wouldn't take them; it was practically physics. You could set your watch by how long a person would keep reaching toward Ines Vane before deciding the reach wasn't worth it. Eighteen months, average. She'd run the numbers on that too, once, drunk on cheap rum in Halfway Bend, and then never let herself think about it again except at night, which was most nights.

* * *

The manifest said Sable Creek needed: forty kilos of flour, antibiotics for the Ferreira kid's ear infection, carburetor parts for the sawmill, batteries, and whatever news there was. The manifest did not say why the Ferreira kid's grandmother cried when Ines handed her the bottle, or that the kid was seven and had been deaf in the left ear since March and would probably stay that way because the infection had gone too deep, but Ines knew it anyway, because knowing was also part of the job, the part nobody paid for.

"He'll be all right?" Señora Ferreira asked, holding the bottle like it might hatch.

"He'll be loud," Ines said. "That's usually what all right sounds like at that age."

The old woman pressed her hand, wet and papery, against Ines's sleeve, and held it there a beat too long, and Ines performed the thing she performed: a smile with exactly enough warmth in it to be polite and not one degree more. Then she was back in the rain, back in the plane, back in the air, and the whole settlement shrank beneath her to a scatter of tin roofs and woodsmoke, a place she could hold in one eye.

From up here the interior made sense. From up here it was a map, and maps were honest—they showed you distance, and distance was the only clean measurement there was. A river was a line. A settlement was a dot. A person was nothing at all, unless you let them become something, and Ines had learned at cost what letting them cost.

Sixty-one nautical miles to Halfway Bend. She set the number in her head like a stone in a wall and flew it.

* * *

Halfway Bend sat where the Reckoning River gave up pretending to be tame and started braiding itself into a dozen brown arguments, and in monsoon season the town floated as much as stood. Ines came in over the water tower, dropped through a hole in the cloud the size of a church door, and put down on the strip—which was really a levee road—with the particular satisfaction of a pilot who has landed somewhere everyone said couldn't be landed today.

Three kids were waiting by the fuel cache, because there were always kids at Halfway Bend, and they always had news, and their news was always better than the adults' because it hadn't been edited yet.

"Pilot Vane!" The tallest one, a girl called Cricket with a permanent scrape across one shin, ran alongside the wing as she rolled out. "Did you bring the mail? Did you bring the—"

"Mail's in the box. Don't dig through it this time."

"That was Pip. Pip dug through it."

"Pip dug through it," agreed a smaller shape behind her, unrepentant, already elbow-deep in the mail sack Ines hadn't even tied shut yet. "Hey, there's a letter here with a real stamp. A real stamp! Who uses stamps?"

"People who want their letters to arrive," Ines said, and took it away from him, and he grinned at her with the total fearlessness of a child who has decided you belong to him, which was the most dangerous thing a person could decide about Ines Vane, and these dock kids decided it constantly, casually, the way you'd claim a stray dog.

She paid them in chocolate from Ketchikan—real chocolate, hoarded, rationed—and in exchange they paid her in news, and the news was this:

"The Magpie sank again," Cricket said. "Well, half-sank. At the east dock. There's an engineer working on it, some riverman, been here a week. He sleeps on the boat even though it's full of water. Mama says he's either very stupid or hiding from something."

"Could be both," Ines said.

"And Doc Amos is out at Marsh's cabin," Pip added, mouth full. "The old prospector. He's bad sick. Fever. Doc went up yesterday and hasn't come back and Mama says—" he lowered his voice with relish— "Mama says he won't last the week."

Something turned over in Ines's chest, small and cold, and she ignored it the way she ignored weather she didn't want to fly in. Elias Marsh. She knew the name the way you know a name on a gravestone you pass often—she'd flown supplies to his cabin twice, traded him coffee for garnet samples, exchanged maybe forty words total across five years. A hard old man who lived alone above the escarpment country and paid in ore and never asked her anything personal, which had made him, in her private accounting, one of her favorite people on the coast.

"Bad sick how?" she heard herself ask.

"Lung fever," Cricket said. "Coughs blood, Junie's brother says. He carried the message down."

Ines looked at the sky. It was going dark early, the monsoon stacking itself up over the escarpment like a debt coming due. She had a schedule. She had a routine, and the routine was the load-bearing wall of her entire life: deliver, refuel, count, leave. Never overnight anywhere you could avoid it. Never sleep twice under the same roof in a month. Never—

"Doc Amos will want me to fly medicine up," she said. "Penicillin, sulfa. I've got both."

"Mama says the river road's flooded and it's four hours by foot and Doc's too old for it," Pip said helpfully. "And he said, if Pilot Vane comes through—"

"He said that?" Something in her voice must have changed, because Cricket looked at her funny.

"He said, tell Vane the strip by Marsh's is rough but she's flown worse. He said—" Pip squinted, retrieving it. "He said she's the only one who'd go."

Ines stood in the rain on the levee with the mail sack dripping and the chocolate gone and the numbers in her head rearranging themselves without her permission. Forty minutes to Marsh's cabin, each way. Weather window: maybe three hours before the front closed the valley. Cost: her evening, her dry clothes, her schedule.

Benefit: none that she could name. An old man she barely knew, dying anyway, probably beyond what sulfa could do. Every rational column in her ledger said no.

But she thought of the señora's hand on her sleeve. She thought of the boy who might spend his life deaf because the medicine arrived a month late. She thought of Elias Marsh paying in garnets and asking nothing, and how few people left in the world asked nothing.

"One trip," she said, to herself as much as to the kids. "Up and back before dark."

Cricket grinned. Pip saluted with a chocolate-smeared hand. And Ines Vane walked back to her plane in the rising rain, telling herself—as she had told herself ten thousand times, on ten thousand strips—that this meant nothing, changed nothing, attached her to nothing.

It was just a delivery.

It was always just a delivery.

She loaded the sulfa, checked the sky, and lifted off into the gray toward the escarpment, toward a dying man she didn't yet know was waiting for her specifically—had watched her land at Sable Creek that morning through binoculars, had sent word ahead, had chosen her the way a drowning man chooses a rope—toward a promise she could not yet see, shaped exactly like a trap, closing gently around her heart at ninety knots.
