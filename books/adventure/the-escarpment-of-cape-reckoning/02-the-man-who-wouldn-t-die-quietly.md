## 2. The Man Who Wouldn't Die Quietly

The strip at Sable Creek was forty meters of mud pretending to be an airstrip, and Ines Vane landed on it the way she did everything—fast, precise, and without looking back.

She flared the Cessna a hair late to keep the wheels out of the deepest ruts, touched down with a chirp of tires on wet clay, and rolled out through standing water that threw brown rooster tails behind her. The rain came sideways off the escarpment, the way it had for six days, and she counted it without meaning to: sixth day of rain, third stop of the morning, eleventh delivery since Kobo Flats. Penicillin for the Sable Creek clinic. Amoxicillin for the Hendersons' boy, who'd been coughing since the last moon. A sack of mail that smelled like mildew and somebody's cigarettes.

Forty-one minutes from Halfway Bend. She knew because she always knew. Minutes were a currency she could bank against something worse.

She cut the engine and sat for a moment in the drumming quiet, watching water bead and run down the windshield in ropes. The cabin smelled of avgas and old coffee and herself, which was to say of work. That was fine. Work didn't ask questions.

"You're late," said Marta Okonkwo, the Sable Creek nurse, appearing at the wing with an umbrella she wasn't bothering to use.

"I'm early," Ines said. "You just don't know what I'm early for."

"Antibiotics. The Hendersons have been asking every day." Marta took the crate Ines handed down, hefted it, frowned at the weight. "This is light."

"That's everything they sent me. You want to argue with the depot, take it up with them. I just fly the stuff."

Marta's frown deepened into something else—not anger, more like the look of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like. "There's a man upriver," she said. "Trapper's cabin, past the second bend. Old prospector named Marsh. Somebody brought him down two days ago with a fever nobody could break, and now he's gone back up to his cabin because he says he won't die in a clinic."

"People say a lot of things when they're scared."

"He's not scared. That's the strange part. He's *waiting*." Marta shifted the crate to her hip. "I told him I'd flag down any pilot heading north. He asked if I knew Vane."

Ines's hands went still on the cargo strap. Rain ticked against the aluminum above her like fingers testing a lock.

"What did you tell him?"

"That you fly everywhere and owe everybody nothing. He laughed. Said that sounded about right." Marta squinted at her through the rain. "He wants you to come up. Wouldn't say why. Just kept saying your name like it was a word in a language he used to speak."

Ines looked north, where the river bent twice around a shoulder of black rock and disappeared into cloud. Forty minutes past the strip, maybe fifty in this weather. Fuel enough. Weather marginal but flyable, which in Cape Reckoning was as good as weather got in monsoon season.

Every sensible instinct she owned said no. Sensible instincts had kept her alive five years; she trusted them the way other people trusted God.

But there was a name in her mouth now, Marsh, sitting there like a stone she couldn't spit out, and names had a way of turning into errands, and errands into hours, and hours—

"Tell him I'll be by tomorrow," she heard herself say, and hated how easily the sentence left her, like it had been waiting in line.

* * *

Elias Marsh heard the plane before he saw it, which was how he'd known for three days that she would come.

Sound carried strange up the gorge. It came around the bends ahead of itself, arriving before its source, so that the world up here was full of rumors. He lay in his bunk under two blankets and one dog and listened to the engine grow from a whisper to a statement, and he thought: *there she is.*

He'd watched her land at Sable Creek yesterday—or rather he'd listened to it, lying flat on the cabin floor because standing made the world tilt, tracking the sound the way he'd once tracked game. A clean approach. No wasted maneuvering. A pilot who landed like she was doing arithmetic, touching down exactly where the numbers told her to and not one meter further.

That was why he'd asked for Vane. Not because of the name—though the name had stopped his heart the first time he heard it, two years ago in a trading post, spoken by a freight clerk complaining about a woman who wouldn't carry passengers. Because of the flying. Because a person who flew like that was either running from something or guarding something, and in Elias's long experience the two conditions looked identical from the outside.

The engine note changed overhead, circled once—the pilot checking his clearing, cautious, professional—and settled into a descent.

"She came," he said to the dog.

The dog, whose name was Biscuit and whose loyalty was entirely caloric, thumped her tail against the bunk frame.

Getting to the door took him eleven minutes, and he counted those too, because counting was the only thing keeping him on the near side of the fever. Six steps to the chair. Sit. Rest until the black spots stopped swimming. Four steps to the door. Rest again. By the time he got the latch open his lungs were full of broken glass and his heart was going like a stamp mill, but he was upright, mostly, leaning on the frame with the rain blowing in past him onto the floorboards.

The Cessna came out of the murk low over the trees, gray on gray, and flared onto his meadow strip—a meadow, not even a real strip, just the flattest ground within walking distance of a dying man's door—with a precision that made something in his chest ache that had nothing to do with the infection.

*Yes,* he thought. *Her.*

The propeller spun down. The door opened. A woman dropped to the mud without waiting for a ladder, tall and lean in a oilskin jacket, dark hair plastered flat by rain, moving toward the cabin with the quick economy of someone who had learned never to linger anywhere long enough to unpack.

Up close she was younger than her reputation and older than her face. Thirty-something carrying fifty years of mileage in the set of her shoulders. Her eyes did a sweep of the cabin, the woodpile, the smoke hole, the dog—cataloguing, assessing, already halfway to leaving—and then found him in the doorway and stopped.

Whatever she saw made her jaw tighten.

"You're Marsh," she said. Not a question. Marta had told her, then.

"And you're the woman who owes everybody nothing." He tried a grin. It cost him. "Come in out of the rain or stand there judging my housekeeping. Either way, shut the door. The heat's all I've got and I'm spending it fast."

* * *

Inside, Ines took stock the way she took stock of everything, in seconds, without appearing to look.

One room. Iron stove burning green wood because he'd run out of dry. Bed unmade in a way that wasn't laziness—he'd been getting up, or trying to. A rifle cleaned and oiled on the wall rack, cared for better than the man himself. Shelves of canned goods arranged with a soldier's neatness. And paper. Paper everywhere. Notebooks, loose sheets, maps weighted down with stones, covering the table like snowfall.

And the man himself: sixty, maybe seventy, impossible to tell, because fever had burned the fat off him and left a frame like driftwood. Yellowed skin. The sweetish smell underneath the woodsmoke that she recognized from a hundred remote strips, the smell of a body losing an argument with itself.

"You've got an abscess," she said. "Abdominal, by the smell and the way you're holding yourself. You need a hospital. Real one, not Marta's clinic."

"Need's a strong word."

"I can have you in Halfway Bend in under an hour. There's a doctor comes through on the fortnight boat."

"There's a doctor comes through on the fortnight boat," Elias agreed pleasantly, "and the fortnight boat is nine days out, and I am not going to make nine days, and we both know it, so let's not spend my remaining dignity pretending otherwise." He lowered himself into the chair by the stove with the care of a man handling explosives. "Sit down, Miss Vane. Or stand. But stop hovering by the door like the room might bite you."

She didn't sit. Sitting was staying, and staying was a habit with roots.

"How do you know my name?"

"Because I asked after it. Two years ago. Freight clerk at Kobo Flats, fellow with the lazy eye—you probably know him, you probably know everyone, that's the trouble with people who go everywhere." He nodded at the kettle hanging over the stove. "Tea's in the blue tin. I'd pour it myself but I'm saving my strength for the important part."

"The important part being?"

"Being stubborn for another hour or so." His eyes were bright and terrible, fever-lit, and behind the brightness was something patient and vast, like water under ice. "Miss Vane, I'm going to tell you a thing, and you're going to want to leave, and I'd take it kindly if you'd wait until I finish before you do. Most people don't let dying men finish. They get busy. They find reasons. It's kinder that way, they think. It isn't."

The rain hammered the roof. The dog sighed. Somewhere in the walls, water found a channel and ran.

Ines looked at the door. Measured the distance to it, the way she measured everything—forty-one minutes to Sable Creek, ninety to Halfway Bend, five years to anywhere that didn't know her name.

Then she pulled out the second chair and sat down, and told herself it was only because standing made her legs tired, and knew even as she thought it that she'd stopped telling herself true things somewhere around the time she'd started counting.

"Talk," she said.

* * *

Elias had rehearsed this speech for three days, and now that the moment came, the speech dissolved like sugar in rain, and what was left was just a man talking.

"I had a daughter," he said. "Ruthie. Lives at Kestrel's Landing, up under the escarpment, or lived—last letter was four months old and the flood's been sitting on that country since. She has a girl. Mara. Seven years old, stubborn as a mule with a head cold, gets it from both sides." He paused, steadied himself on the arm of the chair. "I've been up here twelve years. Came up after Ruthie's mother died, because grief and I don't share a house well, and I told myself I'd come down every season. And I did, for a while. And then the seasons got longer and the trips got shorter, and I told myself next year, next year, and you know how that song goes. Everybody knows how that song goes."

Ines said nothing. Her stillness had a quality he recognized—the stillness of a person holding a door shut against something on the other side.

"I got sick in the spring. Slow, then fast. And I sat here making lists of excuses, and then one morning I realized the list was finished. There weren't any left. So I wrote to Ruthie, and the letters—" his hand moved toward the table, the papers under their stones, "—the letters are all here. Twelve years of letters I wrote and never sent, because sending them meant admitting I'd stayed away, and every year it got harder to admit, until admitting it was harder than dying. Which is a trick, Miss Vane. Grief plays that trick on everyone. It makes the debt grow faster the longer you don't pay it."

"You want me to fly your letters to Kestrel's Landing," Ines said. Flat. Already calculating fuel, weather, the closing passes. "I don't carry passengers and I don't run charity runs. Mail goes through the depot like everyone else's."

"The mail takes six weeks and the passes close in ten days." He smiled, and there was iron in it. "And I'm not asking you to fly anything yet. I'm asking you to listen for five more minutes, and then I'm going to show you something, and then you can decide. That's all. Five minutes. Even you can afford five minutes."

Something in the phrase—even you—snagged on her like a fishhook. He watched it catch. Watched her decide not to ask what he meant by it, which was its own kind of answer.

"There's a cache," he said. "Above the Landing, high on the escarpment shelf, in a place I found thirty years ago and told no one, because telling people about good things is how you lose them. Medicine. Tools. Powder, salt, blankets. Enough to keep a settlement alive through a bad winter, and this is shaping into the worst winter in forty years. And other things. Things Ruthie will understand when she sees them." He leaned forward, and the effort whitened his knuckles on the chair. "I drew the route. Every bend, every bar, every place the river lies to you. Thirty years of knowing, on one sheet of paper. It's worth more than this whole cabin. It's worth more than *me*, and I know roughly what I'm worth."

"And what do you want for it?" Ines asked, and her voice had gone hard, trader-hard, because a price was something she understood, a price was safe, a price ended conversations instead of starting them.

"Your word," Elias said. "That you'll deliver it. To Ruthie, or to whoever's left of Ruthie's household. Before the monsoon seals the passes."

"No."

The word came out fast, and he'd expected it, had built the whole conversation to survive it. "No?"

"I don't take promises. Promises are debts, and debts mean coming back, and coming back means—" She stopped. Recalibrated. "I deliver cargo. Cash on the strip. That's the whole arrangement. Find another fool."

"Another fool," Elias repeated softly. He looked at her for a long moment, and the fever fell away from his eyes for a second, and what was underneath was clear and kind and unbearably sad. "You know what I see, Miss Vane? I see a woman who's spent five years flying away from something so fast she's convinced herself it's a career." He raised a hand before she could speak. "Don't. I'm not prying. Whatever it is, it's yours, and I wouldn't trade mine for yours on any market in the world. I'm just saying—I know the look. I wore it twelve years. 'Next season,' I said. 'Next year.' And the thing about running is that it works right up until the moment it doesn't, and then you're lying in a cabin listening to the rain, adding up everything you didn't do, and the sum is so big you can't look at it straight on."

The stove popped. The dog lifted her head, then put it down again.

Ines stood. The chair scraped. "I came to deliver antibiotics to Sable Creek," she said. "Done. I've got weather closing in from the west and a schedule that doesn't include deathbed negotiations. I'm sorry about your daughter. Truly. But sorry doesn't fly, and neither do I—not for strangers, not for promises, not for—"

"Mara's seven years old," Elias said quietly.

The words went into the room and lay there.

"—and she hasn't heard from her grandfather in a year, and her mother thinks he's dead, and maybe by now he is, and the only thing standing between that child and a winter that kills grown men is a piece of paper in a tin box under my bed." He didn't raise his voice. He never raised his voice; it was the loudest thing about him. "I'm not asking you to stay, Miss Vane. Lord knows I wouldn't wish staying on anybody. I'm asking you to *go*. One trip. In and out. You're the best pilot on this coast and the only one who'll fly in this, and I picked you two years ago and I haven't changed my mind."

Ines stood with her hand on the door latch, rain streaming down the window beside it, and felt the trap close around her—not with teeth, but gently, the way water closes over a stone. He hadn't mentioned the granddaughter until the end. He'd saved her like the last card in a hand, and it was a card she had no defense against, because children were the one cargo she'd never figured out how to refuse.

"I'll look at the map," she said finally, and heard in her own voice the exact moment the decision was made, however much she'd pretend otherwise later. "I'll look. That's all."

"Looking's all anybody does," Elias said, and grinned, fever-bright, triumphant, a dying man winning. "Biscuit—off the bed. We've got business."

He reached under the bunk and drew out a tin box, dented, older than she was, and inside it, wrapped in oilcloth, a single sheet of paper covered edge to edge in a draftsman's fine hand—rivers, ridgelines, notes in margins crowded so tight they read like scripture.

Ines looked down at it, and the map looked up at her, and somewhere far to the north, under all that rain, a river was already rising.
