## 3. A Map for a Promise

The fever had its own weather system. Ines could see it working in Elias Marsh's face—the sweat breaking and drying in cycles, the eyes going glassy, then sharp, then glassy again, like a man surfacing from deep water only to be dragged back under. She'd seen it before. Fever was the interior's oldest tax collector, and it always came to collect.

She'd done what she could with what she had. Cool cloth on his forehead. Water, sipped slow, because he'd tried to gulp it and coughed so hard she'd thought for one bad moment that this would be the end of it, right here, her plane still warm on the strip outside and a dead man's cabin around her ears. The wound in his leg was ugly—infected, angry, the skin around it hot as a stove lid—but it wasn't the wound that scared her. It was the smell of him, sweetish and wrong, the smell of a body starting to negotiate terms.

"You should go," he said. His voice came out gravelly, worn down to the nub. "Weather's turning. I can hear it."

"I can hear it too." She wrung out the cloth over a basin that had once held engine oil, judging by the rainbow sheen on the water. "Doesn't mean you get rid of me that easy."

"I'm not trying to get rid of you." He laughed, or something adjacent to laughing, and it turned into a cough that bent him double. When it passed he lay back against the rolled blanket she'd wedged behind his shoulders, breathing like a man who'd run a long way. "I'm trying to give you something first."

"Nothing to give. You're flat broke, Marsh. Everybody knows prospectors die rich in stories and broke everywhere else."

"That so." His hand went under the edge of his sleeping roll, moving with the deliberate slowness of a man who knew any sudden motion might cost him consciousness, and came out with a bundle wrapped in oilcloth and tied with fishing line. He set it on the cot between them like a man laying down cards. "Open it."

She didn't move. Outside, the wind had picked up, dragging its fingers through the pines above the cabin, and somewhere in that sound was the first cold taste of the monsoon proper—not the daily afternoon soak but the real thing, the weeks-long drowning that turned rivers into roads and roads into rumors. She had maybe two days of flying left before the passes started closing. Maybe less.

"Elias."

"Open it."

She opened it.

Inside was a map. Hand-drawn, ink on waxed paper gone soft at the folds, the kind of document that had been handled so many times it had become part skin, part paper. It showed the river country north and east of Halfway Bend in fine obsessive detail—every bend, every shoal marked with little notations in a cramped, patient hand. *Snag at low water. Gravel bar shifts after rain. Good anchorage, willow bank.* And there, up where the river climbed toward the escarpment, a place called Kestrel's Landing, drawn not as a dot but as a small careful sketch of buildings, a dock, a church steeple, rendered with the tenderness of a man drawing the face of someone he loved.

Above the Landing, in the folded hills, an X. Not a pirate's X—a surveyor's X, precise, annotated with elevations and bearings. *Cache,* said the label underneath, in letters pressed so hard they'd nearly torn the paper. *Medicine. Tools. Letters.*

"Letters," Ines said.

"My daughter's name is Ruth." He said it the way other men said prayers. "Was. Is. I don't know anymore. She went up to the Landing eight years back with her husband, took my granddaughter with her. I meant to follow. I always meant to follow." His mouth did something complicated. "Prospecting's a disease, Ines Vane. You keep thinking the next claim is the one that lets you stop. Nobody ever stops."

Ines looked at the map and felt the trap before she could name it. That was the thing about traps built out of kindness—they closed so gently you were inside them before you heard the click.

* * *

Here is what Ines Vane knew about promises: they were debts, and debts meant staying, and staying meant counting years instead of miles, and she had spent five years teaching herself to count anything but years.

She counted hours in the air. She counted deliveries—forty-one pounds of penicillin to the mission at Two Rivers, twelve gallons of kerosene to the lighthouse keeper at Cape Reckoning proper, a crate of schoolbooks to Halfway Bend, gossip everywhere, gossip being the only cargo that never spoiled. She counted landings, takeoffs, fuel loads, engine hours. Numbers were clean. Numbers ended. A mile was a mile whether you flew it over grief or over nothing at all, and when you landed at the far end of it, nobody asked you what you'd left behind at the near end.

A promise was different. A promise had no far end. A promise followed you into the next valley and the one after that, sat down beside you at every fire, weighed exactly as much as the person who'd asked for it, which is to say: everything.

"I can't take your map," she said.

"It's not a gift. It's a trade."

"A trade for what? I've already given you the water and the cloth. That's charity. Charity doesn't bind anybody."

"For your word." Elias Marsh looked at her then with a clarity that had nothing feverish in it at all, and she understood that some part of him had been waiting behind the sickness, patient as a spider, for exactly this moment. "Your word that you'll carry it up to Kestrel's Landing before the monsoon seals the passes. Medicine to the town. The rest—" his hand trembled toward the map, toward the X, toward the word *letters* — "to Ruth Marsh. Or whatever's left of her household. There's a girl. Mara. She'd be nine now."

Nine. Ines felt that number land somewhere below her ribs and stay there.

"No," she said.

Elias didn't flinch. "You haven't heard the price yet."

"The price is my word. I just told you no."

"The price," he said, "is that if you say no, you'll spend the rest of your life knowing a dying man asked you for one simple thing and you flew away. And you'll tell yourself it was practical. Weather windows, fuel loads, schedules. You're good at telling yourself things, Ines. I can see it. It's written all over you, the way a man's handwriting is written all over everything he signs." He paused, gathering breath. "I watched you land yesterday. Most pilots come in fast and shallow, afraid of the trees. You came in high and slow, read the whole strip twice before you committed, set it down like you were apologizing to it. That's not a coward's landing. That's somebody who's crashed before and decided never again. Somebody carrying weight."

"Everybody's carrying weight out here. It's why they come out here."

"Some carry suitcases. Some carry coffins." He closed his eyes, opened them. "Mine's got a girl's name on it. Nine years old. I'm asking you to make sure it doesn't have two."

* * *

The storm arrived while they argued, the way storms do—as a change in the light first, the greenish bruise-color sliding across the window, then the pressure dropping until Ines's ears popped, then the rain, sudden and total, hammering the cedar shakes like applause from an enormous indifferent crowd.

She stood at the window and watched her plane rock on its tiedowns and thought about the arithmetic. Fuel: enough for the Landing and back, barely, if the winds cooperated. Time: the pass at Widow's Tooth would funnel weather for three more days minimum. Cargo capacity: she had antibiotics aboard already, half a case bound for Halfway Bend that could be rerouted without anyone dying of the rerouting. And the map—

The map wanted a boat, really. The last stretch above the Landing was water country now; even in dry years the charts showed channels that shifted seasonally. But boats needed crews, and crews needed paying, and paying meant stopping, and stopping—

"You're doing it," Elias said from the cot. "The counting."

She turned. "What?"

"Your eyes go sideways when you do it. Like you're reading a ledger only you can see." He smiled, and it transformed his ruined face into something almost young. "My wife used to do that. Counting. Right up until the day she stopped counting and started living, and then we had eleven good years, and then she died anyway, and you know what she said at the end?" He drew a breath that cost him visibly. "She said, *I wish I'd stopped counting sooner.*"

"Easy for a dying man to quote the dead."

"Best source there is. They've got no reason to lie."

Ines came back to the cot and sat down on the stool, and for a long moment neither of them spoke, and the rain filled the silence the way water fills a hull—steadily, from below, where you don't notice until the deck tilts.

"Why me?" she said finally. "There are forty pilots on this coast. Half of them are better men than me."

"That's why not them." He fumbled for the map, and she put it in his hands, and he smoothed it against his chest like something alive. "Good men deliver packages. I need somebody who'll argue with me first. Arguing means she's thought about it. Thinking means she'll finish it." His eyes found hers and held. "And because you landed like a woman apologizing, and I've been waiting five days to die for somebody who understands apology."

The words went through her armor through a gap she hadn't known was there. She thought of a runway five years ago and a sister's voice on the radio, bright and certain, saying *watch this* —and she shut the thought the way you slam a door on wind.

"If I say yes," Ines said slowly, "it's delivery. Nothing else. I fly it in, I hand it off, I fly out. No vigil. No deathbed promise to look after anybody. No letters read aloud over anybody's grave."

"Delivery," Elias agreed. "Nothing else."

"And if the passes close first—if the weather beats me—that's it. Nature wins. I don't die trying."

"Nature wins," he said. "Though between us, that woman's beaten me every year of my life and I've kept showing up. Stubbornness counts for something with her."

Despite herself—in spite of everything, the closed heart, the locked ledger, the five years of practiced departure—Ines felt her mouth twitch. "You're a terrible negotiator, Marsh."

"I'm a dying man. Terrible's the best I've got." He held out the map. "Well?"

And here was the trap closing, gentle as snowfall, and she walked into it with her eyes open because the alternative was walking out of it, and she had been walking out of things for five years and her legs were tired, though she'd have cut out her tongue before admitting it.

She took the map.

"I'll deliver it," she said. "Before the passes seal. Medicine to the Landing, the rest to Ruth Marsh or her girl. My word on it."

Elias Marsh exhaled—a long, shuddering sound, like a knot coming untied—and lay back, and for a moment she thought the effort had taken him under. Then his hand crept out and gripped her wrist, weak as a bird's foot but insistent.

"One more thing," he whispered. "The cache—it's not just supplies. Under the floor of it, there's a strongbox. Survey documents. Don't let anyone else know it's there. Not anyone. There are people who'd kill for what's in that box, and I mean that literal, Ines. People are already looking."

The rain chose that moment to redouble, and beneath its drumming, faint but unmistakable, carried up the muddy track from the direction of the river: the sound of an outboard motor, running rough, getting closer.

Elias's grip tightened. "Too late," he breathed. "They found me after all."
