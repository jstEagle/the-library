## 4. Grounded

The storm came off the escarpment like something with a grudge.

Ines saw it building for forty minutes—she always saw them, that was the job, the whole point of her—but she'd misjudged its speed. That was the thing about the interior of Cape Reckoning. It didn't move at the pace of instruments or forecasts or hope. It moved at its own pace, and it was faster than you.

She had the map folded in her breast pocket, against her heart, which was a place she'd never put anything living.

*Get it to Kestrel's Landing before the monsoon seals the passes.* Elias Marsh's voice still rattled around in her skull, feverish and certain, a man trading the only thing he owned left worth having. She'd said yes. God help her, she'd said yes, and now the word sat inside her like a swallowed stone, and every mile she flew it got heavier.

The strip at Halfway Bend was a brown smear along the river, mud the color of old tea, water standing in the wheel ruts. She came in low over the tree line with the rain already ticking against the windshield like fingers asking to be let in, and she set the Cessna down hard because there was no other way to set it down—the mud took the wheels and held them, sucking, and she rode the bounce out with her teeth clenched and her hands doing what hands do when they've done this ten thousand times.

She taxied to the lean-to at the end of the strip, shut down, and sat for a moment in the drumming quiet.

Halfway Bend. Named by some optimist a century dead, because it wasn't halfway to anywhere. A river town of maybe two hundred souls when the water was low, fewer when it was high, a fuel dump, a mission store, a church that doubled as a schoolhouse, and a dock where the riverboats sulked at their moorings like old dogs left out in weather they were too tired to complain about.

She'd landed here maybe sixty times. She knew the strip the way you know your own handwriting.

Which is why what happened next was nobody's fault but hers.

---

She should have waited out the front. Any sane pilot would have. The barometer had been falling all afternoon, the clouds stacking up over the escarpment in towers the color of a bruise going bad, and the strip was soup, and the wind was quartering across the runway at angles that made her squint. Every one of those facts stood in line and introduced itself to her, and she nodded politely at each one and ignored them all, because Elias Marsh was dying on a trapper's cot forty miles back the way she'd come, and his daughter was somewhere ahead of her behind rising water, and promises, she was learning, did not wait politely for weather.

So she fueled fast. She ran the preflight fast. She skipped walking the full length of the strip because she'd walked it sixty times and knew it, and she didn't look at the left main gear the way she would have looked at it if she'd been thinking instead of counting—hours to the pass closure, gallons remaining, miles remaining, the arithmetic she ran instead of feeling things.

The takeoff roll was fine for four hundred feet.

At five hundred feet, running through standing water that had pooled where the strip dipped—a dip she *knew* about, a dip she could have drawn from memory—the left main gear hit the water, hydroplaned, caught the submerged lip of the drainage culvert Elias Marsh himself had probably walked over thirty years ago, and tore loose with a sound Ines felt through her spine before she heard it: a crack, deep and structural, the sound of a promise breaking.

The tail swung. The wingtip kissed the mud. And Ines Vane, who had landed on glaciers, on river gravel, on a road once with cattle on it, sat with her engine at idle and her heart going like a trapped bird while the Cessna settled onto its belly in the rain with one leg folded under it like a sleeping crane.

She sat there for a long time.

Then she shut everything down, methodical, and pulled the map out of her breast pocket, and looked at it, dry and intact, and laughed—one short, ugly bark of a laugh with nothing in it—and put it back.

"Well," she said to the empty cockpit. "That's that."

---

The rain kept coming. It came all night and into the next morning, and Halfway Bend turned into a place of gray sound—rain on tin roofs, rain on the river, rain on the world—with the air between drops so wet it felt like breathing through cloth.

Ines got the plane dragged to the lean-to by noon of the first day, paying three men in fuel money and cigarettes to haul it on rollers, and then she knelt in the mud under the fuselage and looked at what she'd done.

It was bad. Not unfixable—nothing was ever unfixable, that was the one mercy of machines—but bad. The left main gear leg was bent past true, the attach fitting cracked clean through, and worse, the spar carry-through had taken a knock that needed an inspection she couldn't give it without tools she didn't own. There was no parts supplier within five hundred miles. There was no machinist. There was a man named Ferro who ran the fuel dump and could weld, sort of, if you didn't ask too many questions about what he welded.

She lay on her back in the mud with rain dripping off the fuselage above her and did the math, because math was safe, math was the wall she'd built her whole life out of.

Parts: none available. Fabrication time: days, if Ferro was sober, longer if he wasn't. Inspection: impossible here. Weather window to Kestrel's Landing before the passes closed: shrinking by the hour, and the monsoon didn't negotiate.

Conclusion: she was not flying anywhere.

She lay there a moment longer than the math required, staring up at the wet gray underside of her own airplane, and let the fury come. It came the way it always came—not loud, not thrown, but cold and precise and turned inward, the way she'd learned to aim it years ago, after the last time her carelessness had cost more than a landing gear.

*You knew about the dip. You knew about the water. You flew anyway.*

Because a dying man had asked her nicely. Because he'd looked at her with those sunken eyes and seen something she hadn't agreed to show him, and she'd said yes just to make him stop looking, and now the universe—the great indifferent bookkeeper of Cape Reckoning—was collecting on the debt. Promises cost. That was the exchange rate here. You gave your word and the world reached into your pocket and took whatever it found, and right now what it had found was her wings.

She crawled out, stood, and wiped her hands on her trousers, which accomplished nothing, since everything including her thoughts was mud now.

"Bad?" asked Ferro, who had wandered over with the unhurried gait of a man whose opinion of airplanes was low.

"Bent gear, cracked fitting, possible spar damage."

Ferro sucked air through his teeth, a long professional performance. "I can straighten the leg. The fitting I can build, maybe, if I find steel the right grade." He looked at her. "You're grounded, missus. Two weeks, minimum. Three if the rain keeps."

Three weeks. The passes would be sealed in nine days. Maybe eight.

"Thanks," Ines said, and meant it as an insult, and Ferro, being local, took it as thanks.

---

She spent the afternoon at the mission store buying things she didn't need—batteries, a tin of coffee, rope—because moving through a shop was better than sitting still, and sitting still was the one thing Ines Vane had never learned to do. Stillness was where the counting stopped working. Stillness was where the years got in.

Five years. Five years since the crash. Five years of hours logged and miles flown and deliveries counted, a ledger so long and so clean it could have been mistaken for a life. She'd gotten good at it. She could go weeks without thinking about her sister's name. She could go months without hearing the sound the radio made when a voice stopped answering.

But a broken airplane was stillness whether she liked it or not. A broken airplane was a cage with her own signature on the repair invoice.

She stood at the counter while the storekeeper wrapped her useless purchases, and through the window she watched the river.

It was high. Higher than she remembered it being this early in the season, brown and muscular, carrying whole trees downstream like matchsticks, and along the near bank, tied up in front of a boatyard that smelled of tar and diesel even from here, sat a riverboat with its stern half underwater.

Not sunk. Half-sunk. Sitting at an angle of stubborn refusal, like a man leaning back from a table he intends to stay at.

There was a figure moving on it. Small at this distance, dark against the gray water, doing something methodical with a winch. Even from across the street, even through rain, there was something about the way the figure moved—unhurried, exact, a person talking to a machine in a language both understood—that snagged on Ines like a burr.

She looked away. She had a map in her pocket and no way to deliver it and a fury to feed, and the last thing she needed was to stand around admiring strangers.

She paid the storekeeper. She went back out into the rain.

And that was when she heard it, from the direction of the dock, carried on the wet air clear as a bell:

"You're the pilot." A man's voice, level, unhurried. "The one who just broke her airplane on the flattest strip in the territory."

Ines stopped.

She turned, slowly, rain running off her hood, and looked down the muddy street toward the boatyard, where the figure from the half-sunk boat had come to the rail and was watching her with an expression she couldn't read at this distance but didn't end up liking any better up close.

He was tall, broad through the shoulders, with dark skin gone ash-gray at the temples and forearms scarred in the particular way of a man who had spent years reaching into machinery that bit. He didn't smile. He didn't frown. He just watched her come, the way you watch weather.

"I don't recall introducing myself," Ines said.

"You didn't have to." He nodded at the lean-to, at the Cessna sitting on its belly like a shot bird. "Vane. That's a blue-and-white Cessna 180. Registration ends in seven. I'd know it anywhere." Something moved behind his eyes then, quick and unreadable as a fish turning under water. "I've been expecting this plane for five years."

The rain filled the silence between them.

"I think," Ines said carefully, "you've mistaken me for someone else."

"No," Tam Oduya said. "I really don't."
