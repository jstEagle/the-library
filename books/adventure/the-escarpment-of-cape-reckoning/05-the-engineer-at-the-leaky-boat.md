## 5. The Engineer at the Leaky Boat

Halfway Bend was the kind of town that existed because the river couldn't decide what it wanted to be. It widened there, slowed, dropped its silt in lazy brown bars, and a settlement had grown up on the indecision like mold on bread. Ines counted eleven buildings above waterline, three docks of varying honesty, and one saloon that called itself a hotel. She'd slept there twice in five years and remembered both nights as arguments with the mattress.

She came down the main street with Elias Marsh's map rolled inside her jacket, against her sternum, where she could feel it with every step like a second heartbeat. Rain fell in the fine, persistent way it did here—not falling so much as hanging, waiting for her to walk through it.

The plane sat on the mud flat below the last dock, one wheel buried to the axle, the landing gear bent at an angle that made her jaw tighten every time she looked at it. She'd walked away from the takeoff run with nothing worse than a bruised hip and a cracked pride, but the Cessna's strut had folded like a bad hand of cards, and now the plane that was her whole life—her house, her job, her escape route—sat listing in the muck like a drunk at closing time.

*Grounded.* The word kept surfacing and she kept pushing it down.

She'd spent two days trying to rig repairs with what Halfway Bend had to offer, which was a blacksmith who worked mostly in horseshoes and optimism. It wasn't enough. She needed parts, or a machine shop, or a miracle, and the monsoon didn't care which.

So she did what she always did when the sky closed: she went looking for work, because work kept the hands busy and the hands kept the head quiet.

That was how she found the Magpie.

* * *

The boat lay half-sunk at the far end of the third dock, settled into the river up to its gunwales, looking less like a vessel than a suggestion of one. Forty feet of riveted steel, a stubby wheelhouse, a cargo deck stacked under rotting tarps. Water sloshed inside her hull when the river breathed. A hand-painted name on the bow had been weathered down to *MAG IE*, and someone had recently scratched a fresh *P* into the gap, so the boat read like a stutter.

A man was working on her. He stood waist-deep in the flooded bilge wearing rubber waders and no shirt, dark skin slicked with rain and sweat, welding something with a portable rig that spat blue light across the water. He worked with the unhurried precision of someone who had done this ten thousand times and expected to do it ten thousand more.

Ines stood on the dock and watched him for a while. There was a rightness to his movements she recognized—the same economy she brought to a preflight check. This was a man who knew exactly what he was doing and exactly why nobody else could do it.

"You're going to electrocute yourself," she said.

He lifted his mask. Underneath he was maybe forty, with a short beard going gray at the edges and eyes the color of the river after a storm—brown shot through with something lighter. He looked at her without surprise, as if he'd been aware of her on the dock for some time.

"Water's grounded," he said. "Mostly." His voice was low and even, an accent from somewhere downriver, or upriver, or nowhere. "You're the pilot."

It wasn't a question. Ines felt the small automatic tightening in her chest, the one that arrived whenever a stranger opened a conversation with a fact about her. "Word travels fast in a town with eleven buildings."

"Eleven buildings, one crash." He nodded past her, toward the mud flat. "Heard you come in. Sounded wrong on final. I was going to come look, but—" he gestured at the drowned boat around him, "—I had my own patient."

"Is she going to live?"

"That depends." He set the torch down carefully on a dry shelf he'd rigged from crates, and climbed the ladder out of the hull with a smoothness that suggested the waders were old friends. Up close he was leaner than he'd looked, all wire and tendon, with forearms mapped by old scars—one long white line from wrist to elbow that hadn't come from any engine. "Depends mostly on whether I can find a replacement shaft bearing before the river finishes making up its mind about the flood."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then she stays where she is until the water goes down, and I stay with her, and we both get very good at fishing." He wrung out a rag and looked at her properly then, and Ines had the distinct sensation of being read—cover to cover, quickly, like a man skimming for the good parts. "Ines Vane."

Not a question either. Her chest tightened again. "Do I know you?"

"No." He said it easily enough, but there was a half-second delay, a hair's breadth of calculation, and Ines had spent five years learning to hear that delay in people's voices. It was the sound of a man deciding how much truth a moment could hold. "Tam Oduya. I own the boat, such as she is. You're looking for work—I've watched you ask at the dock office, the chandlery, and the mission kitchen. You didn't find any."

"I found some," Ines said. "Just not the kind that pays."

"What kind is that?"

"The kind where they want me to fly their grievances upriver to people who already know them."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile—a recognition. "And what do you want? Besides pay."

"A machine shop. A lathe. Someone who can turn a strut fitting out of solid stock, because the smith here can't, and my gear won't hold another takeoff on hope."

Tam looked at her for a long moment. Then he looked at the sky, doing some private arithmetic involving clouds and hours, and when he looked back there was something decided in his face.

"I can't fix your plane," he said. "But I can get you upriver. And I need hands."

"I don't need to go upriver."

"Everybody needs to go somewhere." He said it lightly, but his eyes didn't. "Here's my situation. The Magpie runs supplies to the settlements along the escarpment base—Kestrel's Landing, the mission at Two Rivers, the survey stations. Or she did, until she took a rock in the shallows at Widow's Tooth and started drinking the river. I've got a contract to deliver before the monsoon seals the passes, a hull with a hole in it, and a list of parts longer than my arm. What I don't have is a second pair of hands that knows the difference between a wrench and a wish."

"So hire the smith."

"The smith welds horseshoes." He wiped his hands slowly, watching her. "You grew up around engines. Bush strips, by your boots. You fly a taildragger into strips that don't have names, which means you've done your own maintenance or died young, and since you're standing here arguing with me, I'll assume maintenance."

Ines said nothing. The rain ticked on the tarps. Somewhere downriver thunder cleared its throat.

"My offer is this," Tam went on. "You help me finish her—caulking, plating, the shaft, the fuel system, three days' work if the hands are good. When she floats, you ride upriver as far as Kestrel's Landing. Free passage, plus I'll put you to work in my shop afterward if you want it—there's a lathe, a drill press, tools worth more than the boat. You could have that strut fitting turned in an afternoon."

"And what does it cost me besides labor?"

He tilted his head. "Why would it cost you anything?"

"Everything costs something up here. Passage, favors, information. Especially information." She held his gaze. "You knew my name before I gave it. That's not free either."

For a moment—just a moment—something flickered behind his eyes. Not guilt exactly. Weight. The look of a man holding a door shut against a draft.

"Small river," he said. "Names travel."

"Names don't travel. People carry them." She let that sit. Then, because she needed him more than she needed the upper hand, she stuck out her hand. "Three days. If the boat floats in three days."

"If the boat floats in three days," he agreed, and shook. His grip was careful, precise, the grip of a man used to handling things that broke.

* * *

That night, in the wheelhouse of the Magpie, wrapped in a blanket against the damp, Tam Oduya listened to the river push against the hull he was trying to save and thought about the name Vane.

He'd heard it first eight years ago, before it meant anything. A supply pilot named Vane who flew the eastern escarpment, back when Tam still ran engines for the mining company and life had a schedule. Then five years ago the name had come back to him the way bad news comes back—carried on other men's voices in a bar in Corvus Bend, told and retold with the garnish each telling required. A Vane aircraft, down in the pass country. Weather, they said. Weather and a heavy load and a young copilot who never got out.

They'd said the pilot walked away.

They'd said it the way men say things in bars, and Tam had believed it the way men believe things in bars, which is to say completely and without checking. It wasn't his tragedy. He'd had his own by then, freshly minted, and he'd carried it upriver and set it down in a boat hull and tried to build a new life on top of it, the way you build a fire on wet wood—lots of smoke, not much heat.

Then yesterday he'd heard a sick-sounding engine on final, gone out onto the dock, and watched a taildragger settle onto the mud flat with the particular controlled violence of a pilot who refuses to give the ground the satisfaction of a crash. And when the woman climbed out, cussing at the gear with a fluency that only comes from love, he'd seen the name stenciled under the wing.

*VANE.*

And underneath it, smaller, older, half painted over: the ghost of another name. Another Vane. A sister's name, maybe. Or the same name, repainted over grief.

He'd checked the registry at the dock office that afternoon, casual as asking about mail. One aircraft registered to I. Vane, sole owner, five years current. No co-pilot listed. No history. As if the paper itself had agreed to forget.

Tam pulled the blanket tighter and looked out through the salt-fogged glass at the black water sliding past the hull. He knew what it was to be a name attached to a thing that had happened. He knew the specific loneliness of it—how you could outrun a place, a company, a whole coastline, and still be traveling with yourself.

He also knew he'd lied today. Not big—just a half-second's delay, a door held shut. *No, I don't know you.* True, in the way that mattered. False in every way that didn't.

He wasn't ready to tell her. He wasn't sure he ever would be. But he'd watched her on the dock today, arms crossed against the rain, sizing him up with those gray eyes that counted everything and trusted nothing, and he'd thought: *there she is. The one who walked away.* And then he'd thought something else, something that surprised him:

*I wonder what she's running from. Because nobody flies like that unless they're fleeing the scene of something.*

Tomorrow she'd show up with her sleeves tied back and her hands out, and she'd work like a woman paying off a debt, and he'd teach her the fuel lines and the shaft packing and the trick to seating a patch plate on a wet hull. And for three days they'd be two liars building a boat together, each pretending the other's silence was ordinary.

Tam reached over and turned down the lamp. The river breathed against the hull. Somewhere out in the dark, the rain kept up its patient work on everything unfinished—including, though he didn't yet know it, on both of them.

* * *

Ines was at the boat before dawn.

She told herself it was because the light was better, which was a lie; there was no light at that hour, only a gray suggestion of it. She told herself it was because she wanted first crack at the tool inventory, which was also a lie; she just couldn't stand the mud flat anymore, couldn't stand one more hour of looking at her broken plane and doing the arithmetic of days. So she came down to the third dock in the dark and found Tam already in the bilge, and neither of them remarked on it, and that unremarked thing became the first honest agreement between them.

The work was brutal and exact. They pumped her out, and the Magpie rose in the water by inches like a creature remembering its own shape. They chased the seam crack from bow to midships with hammer and cold chisel, Ines holding the light while Tam drove the plate home, and when he said *hit it again, harder,* she hit it again, harder, and felt something in her shoulders unknot that had been knotted for years. Work with weight behind it. Work you could measure in blisters instead of miles.

By the second day they'd found a rhythm that frightened her a little. He'd say a measurement and she'd already be cutting. She'd spot a weep in a seam and he'd already have the caulking iron heated. Once, working the fuel lines, they reached for the same wrench and both withdrew and both laughed—short, startled sounds, like strangers discovering they spoke the same language—and Ines caught herself thinking *this is easy,* and immediately corrected it, the way you correct a compass error. *Nothing is easy. Nothing stays. You're passing through.*

On the second evening Bess from the cookhouse sent down a pot of stew nobody had ordered, and Tam ate his share sitting on the gunwale with his boots dangling over the water, and asked, casually, conversationally, in the tone of a man asking about the weather:

"What's upriver for you? Really."

Ines kept her eyes on the stew. "Delivery."

"For who?"

"A dying man."

Tam was quiet a moment. "That's a heavy fare."

"He paid in advance." She shrugged, and hated how light she made it sound, and made it sound light anyway. "A map for a promise. I'm delivering the promise."

"To Kestrel's Landing."

She looked up sharply. "How do you know where—"

"Contract route," he said mildly. Too mildly. "Kestrel's Landing is the end of my run too. Small river." But his eyes stayed on her a beat too long, and Ines filed it away with the other half-seconds, the delays, the doors held shut. This man knew something. Maybe several somethings. Well—so did she. Two liars, one boat. It might almost be fair.

"Your turn," she said. "What are you running from?"

The question landed harder than she'd intended, and she watched it land. Tam looked out at the river for a long time, at the brown water carrying sticks and leaves and one entire floating fence post down toward the sea, going wherever the water decided.

"Same as everyone," he said finally. "A mistake with my name on it."

"And you're hoping the river washes it off."

"No." He smiled then, really smiled, and it changed his whole face, and Ines understood suddenly that he was dangerous in a way she hadn't accounted for—not because he lied, but because he saw through them. "I'm hoping the river teaches me to carry it better. There's a difference."

Ines looked down at her hands, blistered and black with caulking compound, and thought about a map pressed against her sternum, and a promise she'd made to a stranger because saying no had cost more than yes.

"There's a difference," she agreed.

* * *

On the morning of the third day, the Magpie floated.

Not well—she wallowed like a fat woman in a shallow bath, and the port list would need watching—but she floated, and when Tam fired the engine she caught with a cough and a roar and settled into a rumble that Ines felt in her teeth and, absurdly, in her chest. Tam stood in the engine compartment grinning like a fool, grease to the elbows, and Ines leaned on the rail and let herself feel it for exactly four seconds before correcting course.

"Passage as agreed," Tam said, climbing out. "We load tomorrow, run the day after. River's rising fast—if we don't go soon, we don't go at all."

"Good." Ines shouldered her pack. "The sooner I deliver, the sooner—"

She stopped. The sentence had been going to end with *the sooner I'm done,* and she heard, with uncomfortable clarity, how that sounded. How it had always sounded.

Tam heard it too. He didn't smile this time. He just looked at her, that reading-cover-to-cover look, and said, "The sooner you're done. Sure."

Behind him, at the head of the dock, two kids had appeared—river rats, nine and twelve maybe, barefoot on the wet planks, watching the revived boat with the open hunger of children who had already claimed her in their hearts. The older one, a girl with a fierce braid, cupped her hands and shouted down the dock:

"When do you leave? We're coming!"

"We're not taking passengers," Tam called back.

"Yes we are!" the girl shouted, with total confidence, and the little one beside her laughed like this was the funniest thing ever said.

Ines looked at Tam. Tam looked at Ines. And down at the dock office, a man was hurrying toward them through the rain, waving a telegram, and even before he reached the gangplank Ines felt the day tilt beneath her feet, the way the air tilts before a storm cell builds.

"Boat's yours?" the man panted. "Magpie? Message came up on the relay—fella at the trading post says there's strangers buying up every seat and berth on every boat heading for Kestrel's Landing. Paying triple. Asking questions." He glanced at Ines, apologetic. "Asking about a map."
