## 6. Three Days of Caulking

The Magpie sat in the shallows like a drowned animal that hadn't gotten the news yet—half her hull below the waterline, her deck awash, her name flaking off the stern in curls of green paint. Tam Oduya worked on her the way a surgeon works on a patient he's already told the family to worry about: fast, unsentimental, and with his hands moving like they knew things his mouth wouldn't admit.

Ines arrived at dawn with two buckets, a bag of oakum, and no intention of staying past noon.

"Where'd you get the oakum?" Tam asked, not looking up from the seam he was picking clean.

"Traded for it."

"Traded what?"

"None of your business."

He grunted. That seemed to satisfy him. She was learning that about him—he didn't ask twice, which she appreciated more than she could say, because most people asked twice, and three times, and then wanted to know why you wouldn't answer.

They fell into a rhythm by midmorning. He caulked; she held the plank steady and drove the seams home with the mallet and iron while river water slopped against their knees. It was miserable work. Her hands were black with pitch inside an hour, her back ached from bending over the bilge, and the rain came down in that fine, patient monsoon way that wasn't dramatic so much as relentless, like it had all the time in the world and intended to use it.

"You've done this before," Tam said around noon, watching her set a seam.

"Boats?"

"Work. With your hands. You fly, but you're not soft."

"My father ran a sawmill." It came out before she could stop it. A true thing, small enough to be safe. "I grew up caulking things. Just different kinds."

"Planes."

"Planes." She drove another seam. The oakum bit into the gap and stayed there, swelling already as the water found it. "A plane is just a boat that made some bad decisions."

Tam laughed—a short, surprised sound, like it had escaped custody. Ines felt something loosen in her chest and immediately tightened it back down. Careful, she told herself. That's how it starts.

* * *

Bess appeared the first evening without being asked, which Ines would later understand was simply how Bess did everything.

She came down the dock road carrying a covered pot balanced on one hip and a look on her face that suggested she'd been feeding people since before either of them was born and considered it less a kindness than a law of nature. She was broad, gray-haired, wearing a man's oilskin coat two sizes too big, and she planted herself at the top of the Magpie's gangplank like a customs official.

"You two are going to fall over dead if you don't eat," she said.

"We're fine," Ines said.

"I can see your ribs through that shirt, girl." Bess thrust the pot at her. "Fish stew. There's bread under the cloth. Bring the pot back tomorrow or don't bother bringing it back at all."

And she turned and walked away before either of them could construct a refusal, which Ines suspected was also part of the method.

"She does this for everyone?" Ines asked, lifting the lid. Steam rose off the stew, rich with pepper and something smoky underneath.

"Everyone who looks like they need it," Tam said. "Which is everyone. She lost her husband to the river eleven years ago. Started feeding the whole dock after that. I think she's trying to keep the town fed so the river can't have anyone else."

Ines looked down the dock road where Bess had gone, then at the pot in her hands. It was warm. That was the problem with Halfway Bend—it kept handing you warm things.

* * *

The second day, the kids showed up.

They materialized out of the rain like weather—two of them, barefoot, a boy of maybe ten and a girl a few years older, both soaked to the skin and entirely unbothered by it. They stood at the edge of the shallows watching the work with the frank, unblinking interest of children who had never been taught that staring was rude.

"You're fixing the boat," the boy said.

"No," Ines said. "We're drowning it slower."

The girl snorted. The boy grinned like Ines had just performed magic.

"I'm Pip," the boy said. "That's Junie. Are you the pilot?"

"How do you know I'm a pilot?"

"Everybody knows you're a pilot. Your plane's got a bent leg now. Junie says you landed it like a drunk flamingo."

"Pip," the girl said—not a correction so much as a punctuation mark.

"It's true."

Ines looked at them. Mud to the knees, hair plastered flat, eyes bright and unafraid. Dock kids. Halfway Bend was full of them, children whose parents worked the river or had been taken by it, who raised each other in loose confederations along the pilings and knew every boat, every barge, every stranger worth knowing about.

"Why aren't you in school?" she asked.

"School flooded in March," Junie said. "Teacher left in April."

"So we're in advanced studies," Pip said. "Today's lesson is boats."

Tam, without looking up from his work, said: "If you're going to study boats, study from there." He pointed at dry dock. "River takes people who stand in the wrong places."

The kids moved to dry dock. They did not leave. They watched all day, and when Bess brought food at dusk there were suddenly four bowls instead of two, and nobody had discussed it, and nobody needed to.

* * *

It was on the third morning, hauling the last rotten plank off the starboard side, that Tam started talking.

He didn't do it the way most people did—there was no warming up, no throat-clearing. He just picked up a story the way he'd pick up a tool, used it, and put it down again.

"I worked the ferries out of Port Meridian," he said, prying nails. "Six years. Good runs, bad runs. Then I took a job running cargo up the Serevan coast, and the boat I was on went down with forty tons of timber and a crew of nine."

"What happened?"

"Bad loading. Bad weather. Bad decisions, mostly mine—I signed off on the manifest when I shouldn't have." He set the nails in a tin. "Three men didn't come up. I did. After that I stopped working for other people. Now I work for boats that are already dead." He nodded at the Magpie. "Nobody expects much of a dead boat. It's restful."

Ines kept her eyes on the plank. He was giving her something—a true thing, shaped carefully, edges sanded down but still true. And she understood, with the particular dread of someone fluent in evasion, that he was waiting to see if she'd give one back.

"My family's from the hill country east of here," she said. "Sawmill country. I learned to fly because I hated the mill. First job was medicine runs. Five years now." A pause. "I like the work because you land, you deliver, you leave. Nobody expects much of a pilot who's always leaving. It's restful."

Tam looked at her for a long moment. Rain dripped off the brim of his hat between them.

"That's the most honest lie anybody's told me in years," he said.

She almost smiled. "You should hear my good ones."

* * *

By the third afternoon the Magpie floated. Not well—she wallowed like a fat woman in a hot spring, and Tam said she'd handle like a shopping cart—but she floated, and when the bilge pump finally beat the seepage, Tam sat back on the deck planks, dripping sweat and pitch, and let out a breath that seemed to come up from his boots.

"There," he said. "She'll float. Whether she'll go anywhere is a different question."

"You said you'd take me upriver."

"I said I'd consider it."

"You said passage for work. That was the deal."

"The deal was repairs. Repairs are done." But he was already looking at the engine housing, and Ines recognized the look of a man negotiating with himself and losing. "Engine needs a full day yet. Fuel line's suspect. And the river—" he nodded toward the water, which had climbed another hand's width up the pilings since morning, brown and fast and carrying whole trees downstream like matchsticks, "—the river isn't getting any kinder."

Ines followed his gaze. The current had changed overnight. You could see it in the way debris moved—faster now, angled hard downstream, the water pulling with intent instead of idly. Somewhere upriver, the monsoon was doing its work. Somewhere upriver, Kestrel's Landing was counting down days it didn't know it had.

Elias Marsh was counting too. She thought of him in the trapper's cabin, fever-bright, pressing the map into her hands like it was the last coin of a currency only he believed in. *You swear it. On whatever you've got left.*

She had sworn. She was starting to suspect that swearing was the trap and she'd walked into it with her eyes open, which was somehow worse than walking in blind.

"Ines." Tam was watching her. "What's upriver? Really. Nobody hauls a dead boat through monsoon water for medicine."

She weighed it. Three days of work beside a man bought her exactly this much truth, she decided—enough to keep him rowing, not enough to hang her with.

"A dying man's cache," she said. "Medicine, supplies, letters. For a settlement called Kestrel's Landing. They're flood-stranded and short on everything."

"And the map?"

"Is worth more than medicine." She heard herself say it and realized it was true—the way Elias had talked about the cache, the survey marks on the margins, the old government stamps in the corner of the paper. "Maybe a lot more."

Tam absorbed that. Down on the dry dock, Pip was teaching Junie to skip stones across the flooded lot, badly, both of them shrieking every time the stone sank on the first bounce.

"And if I say no?" Tam asked.

"You won't."

"Why not?"

"Because you fix dead boats," Ines said, standing, wiping her hands on her thighs, "and then you have to find out if they'll run."

* * *

That night she slept aboard the Magpie, in the tiny aft cabin that smelled of tar and mildew, and woke once in the dark to the sound of rain on the deck above and, beneath it, voices—low, from the dock road. She lay still and listened without meaning to.

Bess's voice, unmistakable even muffled. And Tam's. And between them, thin and clear through the hull, the sound of laughter—Junie's, high and bright, and Pip's crack-edged copy of it—and the clink of dishes being passed around, a meal happening without her, a small warm economy of people who had apparently decided, in three days, without consulting her, that she belonged to something.

She lay in the dark and did the arithmetic she always did. Distance to Kestrel's Landing: ninety miles by water, call it four days if the river behaved, six if it didn't. Days until the passes closed: unknown, but fewer than comfortable. Fuel: insufficient. Engine: questionable.

It was a good list. Numbers were solid ground. She built the flight plan out of them the way she'd built five years of them, mile by mile, delivery by delivery, and felt the familiar calm settle over her like a tarp.

Except that tonight the numbers wouldn't stay clean. Tonight the ledger kept adding lines she hadn't entered—one for a cook who fed strangers, one for a boy who called lessons out of floodwater, one for a girl who watched her the way Junie watched her, steady and unblinking, like Ines was a fixed point worth navigating by. One for an engineer who lied beautifully and saw through her anyway.

Belonging, she thought, was a compass error. You didn't feel it happen. You just looked up one day and realized you were miles off course, pointed somewhere you never meant to go, and the worst part—the truly unforgivable part—was that some cold small room inside you didn't want to correct it.

She rolled over and shut her eyes hard.

Tomorrow they'd load the boat. Tomorrow they'd argue about fuel and routes and whether the kids were coming, and she'd win that argument, because she always won arguments about leaving people behind.

Down the dock, faint through the hull, Junie laughed again.

Ines lay awake a long time, listening to it, hating how much she wasn't listening to anything else.

* * *

Morning came gray and rising. The river had swallowed the bottom step of the dock stairs overnight, and the debris ran thicker—whole sections of fencing now, a drowned canoe spinning slowly end over end. Tam stood at the Magpie's rail with binoculars, studying the channel markers, and his face had gone careful in a way Ines had learned to read.

"What?" she said, coming up the gangplank with two cups of Bess's coffee.

"Fuel barge came through at first light." He lowered the glasses. "Didn't stop. Headed upriver, and it wasn't ours." He passed her the binoculars and pointed. "See the crane rig on the stern? Salvage outfit. That's Corliss Vance's crew."

Through the glass, already shrinking in the rain-haze, Ines watched the barge grind north against the current, its crane arm folded like a sleeping insect. On its deck, figures in yellow slickers moved with purpose.

"They don't run medicine," Tam said quietly.

"No," Ines said. Her grip tightened on the binoculars until the casing creaked. "They don't."
