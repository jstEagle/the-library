## 7. The Salvage Crew

The trouble with Halfway Bend, Ines had decided by her third day there, was that it was exactly the kind of place where news traveled faster than boats.

She was under the Magpie's stern with a scraper in one hand and a fistful of old caulking cotton in the other, working a seam that Tam swore would hold and she privately suspected wouldn't, when Junie came down the dock at a dead run. The girl skidded on wet planks, caught herself on a piling, and leaned over the edge of the boat, breathing hard.

"There's people at Bess's," she said. "Strangers. Buying things."

"Buying things is what people do at a trading post."

"Not like this." Junie's eyes were wide, and she glanced over her shoulder toward the shore as if someone might be following her. "They're buying *passage*. Every boat going upriver. They're paying triple what it's worth. Old Man Ferro just sold them his fuel barge slot and he's been saving that for two years."

Ines sat back on her heels. Her hands were gray to the wrist with oakum and pitch, and somewhere below her ribs, a cold little voice she hadn't heard in years began to speak.

*Who pays triple?*

People who expected to be paid back triple. People on a schedule. People who wanted to be upriver before anyone else.

People who'd heard about a map.

"How many?" she asked.

"Six. Maybe seven. Big man doing the talking, but there's a woman—she's not from here. Boots too clean." Junie wrinkled her nose. "Pip's watching them. He's good at watching."

"Pip is nine years old."

"Pip is invisible," Junie said, with the flat certainty of an older sister stating physics. "Nobody looks at Pip. Come on."

Ines looked down at the seam she'd been caulking, then at the river, brown and swollen and moving fast, carrying whole trees past the dock like it was late for something. Three days of work left on the Magpie, maybe four if the rain kept up. She climbed out from under the stern, wiped her hands uselessly on her trousers, and followed the girl up the boardwalk.

* * *

Bess's trading post smelled of woodsmoke, dried fish, and lamp oil, and it was crowded in a way Ines had never seen it. Half the town had turned out not to buy anything but simply to watch the strangers spend money, which in Halfway Bend was better entertainment than a fight.

And there they were. Six of them, hard-looking, gear stacked by the door in expedition cases—aluminum, new, the kind of kit that cost more than most boats on this river. The big man was doing exactly what Junie had said: talking to Ferro about fuel, sliding bills across the counter with the bored generosity of a man paying for silence along with everything else.

But it was the woman Ines couldn't stop looking at.

She stood apart, studying a wall map of the river system that Bess kept behind the counter—faded, hand-corrected, twenty years out of date—and she studied it the way Ines read weather. Not casually. Professionally. Her finger traced the escarpment passes, moved up the Kestrel branch, stopped, tapped twice, and lifted away.

Corliss Vance.

Ines had never met her. But Elias Marsh, in his fever, between coughs, had muttered names the way dying men mutter prayers, and Vance's had come up more than once—not with fear, exactly. With something worse. Regret. *I showed her a corner of it once,* he'd said. *Years ago. Thought she was a friend. She never forgot a corner.*

Tam appeared at Ines's shoulder, and she felt rather than saw him go still.

"That's her," he said quietly. "Vance."

"I know."

"She's bought Ferro's slot. That puts her on the water tomorrow morning. We're three days out if the caulking holds, four if it doesn't."

"Then we caulk faster."

"Ines." He turned her gently by the elbow so they faced each other, and his voice dropped to almost nothing. "Look at what they're carrying. Look at the cases. That's not a medicine run. That's a salvage operation. Somebody told them something worth money is up that river."

The cold voice under her ribs spoke again, louder now. *Somebody. Or some map. Who did Elias talk to before you got to him? Who came through the trapper's cabin country in the last month?*

"I need to see that map," she said.

"It's in your jacket."

"It's in my jacket because I haven't let it out of arm's reach since Halfway Bend, and I'm starting to think that wasn't paranoia. I think it was arithmetic." She pulled him toward the back of the store, past the counter, into the shadow of the dry-goods shelves where Bess kept the flour and nobody lingered. "Elias wasn't a fool, Tam. Dying, yes. Feverish, yes. But he drew that map over years—he told me that himself. And a man doesn't spend years drawing a map to a cache of canned peaches and spare parts."

"So what do you think it is?"

"I think we've been reading it wrong." She dug the map out, unfolded it carefully against a flour barrel, and weighted the corners with her elbows. "Look at these marks. I thought they were survey notes—elevations, distances. Elias was a prospector; I figured it was habit."

Tam leaned over it. Rain drummed on the tin roof, and lamplight from the front of the store reached them only in a thin yellow wedge, but the map's ink lines were dark enough to read.

"What am I looking at?"

"These numbers here." She touched a column running up the eastern margin. "I took them for elevations. But elevations don't repeat. These repeat—same figure, different stations, all along the escarpment base. You know what repeats like that?"

He shook his head.

"Benchmarks. Datum points. That's not a prospector's elevation log—that's a *survey grid*. Pre-flood. Before the government maps went into the water, somebody ran a full cadastral survey up this valley, and Elias found the vault where they stored the plates. The original records. Title records, Tam. Boundaries. Ownership."

Tam straightened slowly. Somewhere in the front of the store, Corliss Vance laughed at something, a short, controlled sound, and half the room laughed with her because it seemed safer than not.

"The Landing," Tam said.

"The Landing. All those families sitting on land they've farmed for sixty years with paper nobody's ever been able to produce—the flood took the registry office in '09, everybody knows that. And everybody also knows that whoever shows up holding original title documents owns every argument from here to the coast." Ines folded the map along its old creases, precisely, pressing each fold flat. "It's not a cache. It never was. The cache is real—medicine, tools, whatever Elias stockpiled—but it's the *key* to the vault, or the vault's next to it, or the letters tell you where to dig. Either way, Vance isn't racing us to a dying man's pantry. She's racing us to the deed to half a valley."

"And she's ahead."

"She's ahead," Ines agreed. "But she's buying passage, which means she doesn't have a boat worth taking upriver in flood conditions, which means she's going to lose time and equipment at Widow's Tooth no matter how much money she spends. Money doesn't read water." She looked up at him. "We do."

Something moved through Tam's face then—not quite a smile, but its foundation. "You're talking about racing a salvage crew through monsoon water in a half-sunk boat we fixed ourselves, with two stowaway children aboard."

"I'm talking about keeping a promise," Ines said, and heard, with a strange distant surprise, that she meant it completely. "The rest is just the route."

* * *

They didn't make it out of the store unnoticed. Of course they didn't. Halfway Bend noticed everything.

Corliss Vance crossed the floor toward them with the unhurried gait of a woman who had already paid for the room and knew it. Up close she was younger than her voice, mid-forties maybe, sun-lined, with the forearms of someone who'd worked boats or winches for a living before she'd graduated to paying other people to work them. Her eyes went to Ines's jacket—to the shape of the folded map beneath the canvas—and stayed there a beat too long.

"You're the pilot," she said. "Marsh's girl. Word came downriver you took a delivery off him."

"Word travels fast."

"Word travels where I pay for it to travel." Vance smiled without warmth. "Elias Marsh and I did business once. Long time ago. I'd like to finish it. Whatever he gave you—I'll buy it. Name a figure. I'm told pilots are practical people."

For a moment—one clean, cold moment—Ines let herself imagine it. A number. Enough to fix the plane properly, enough to vanish north for a decade, no promises, no passengers, no children on docks watching her with eyes like open doors. The old arithmetic, miles versus debts, and it balanced so easily, the way it always had.

Then she thought of Elias's hand shaking as he'd drawn the last line of the map. *Not for me,* he'd said. *I'll be done either way. It's for the ones still breathing.*

"No," Ines said.

Vance's smile didn't move, but something behind it recalculated. "You haven't heard a figure."

"Don't need to. It's not mine to sell. It's a delivery."

"A delivery." Vance tasted the word like bad coffee. "To Kestrel's Landing. In that—" her chin tipped toward the window, toward the dock, toward the Magpie sitting low and lopsided in the gray water, "—that thing Oduya's resurrecting. Through the Tooth. In peak water." She shook her head slowly, almost pitying. "You'll drown, and I'll have the papers anyway, and the only difference will be that I won't have wasted a week being noble."

"Then why offer to buy it?"

"Because I prefer cheap to drowned." Vance turned, then paused, and looked back over her shoulder. "Last chance, pilot. Tomorrow morning I'm gone, and after me the river closes. Everything after that is weather and luck, and I don't lose to either."

"You might this time," Ines said. "You're bringing six men and aluminum cases up a river that eats barges. I'm bringing a promise. Promises float."

Vance held her eyes for a long moment, and Ines understood suddenly that this was the real transaction, the one underneath the money—the two of them measuring each other, each deciding how much the other would be willing to lose. Whatever Vance concluded, she kept it behind her face.

"We'll see," she said, and walked out into the rain.

* * *

That night the Magpie's cabin was crowded and loud and, Ines realized somewhere around midnight, the first place in five years she hadn't wanted to leave.

Bess had declared herself coming along before anyone voted, on the grounds that nobody eats right on a salvage run and she'd cooked for loggers, which made river pirates look like choirboys. Junie and Pip had been formally discovered as stowaways an hour earlier—a fiction everyone maintained with a straight face, since both children had been sleeping aboard openly for two days—and Tam had laid out the route on the galley table while rain hammered the deck above like applause.

Now the lamp was low, the kids were asleep in a nest of nets in the corner, and Ines sat at the table with the map spread flat, copying it. Twice. One copy for her jacket, one for Tam's, in case the river decided otherwise.

Tam watched her work. "You could still fly it," he said quietly. "Weather breaks tomorrow morning, maybe an hour's window. You could be over the escarpment by noon."

"With a cracked landing gear and no strip to land on at the other end? I'd be delivering Elias's map to the bottom of a ravine." She didn't look up. "Besides."

"Besides?"

She set down the pencil. Outside, the river ran loud and high, and somewhere upstream, hours north, a woman with clean boots was buying her way toward a dead man's life's work.

"Besides, I used to leave," Ines said. "Every time it started mattering. Five years of wheels-up before anything could take root. And it turns out the trick isn't outrunning the losing—it's finding something you won't trade." She tapped the map, the letters, the marginal notes in Elias's cramped hand that she still hadn't finished reading aloud. "He trusted a stranger with the only thing he had left. Cheapest thing I can do is be worth it."

Tam said nothing for a while. Then: "Ferro says Vance's barge loads at first light."

"Then we load tonight."

They worked until the small hours—fuel drums lashed amidships, the repaired skiff secured, Bess's crates of food distributed like ballast, the kids' small hoard of treasures stowed in a biscuit tin. And as Ines coiled the last line and stepped back onto the deck into the warm hammering rain, she looked upriver into the dark, toward the escarpment she couldn't yet see, and felt the strange new weight settle over her—not fear, exactly, and not dread.

Anticipation. Terrible, unpracticed, alive.

Behind her, forward of the engine, Tam was already turning the flywheel, and the Magpie coughed, caught, and settled into a rough, stubborn heartbeat.

First light was four hours away. Corliss Vance would push off at dawn believing she had a day's lead.

Ines smiled into the rain.

She was going to give her three.
