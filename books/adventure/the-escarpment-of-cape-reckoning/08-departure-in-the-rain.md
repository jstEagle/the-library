## 8. Departure in the Rain

The rain came down like a verdict.

Ines stood on the Magpie's stern deck at first light, watching the river climb the pilings of Halfway Bend's dock, one gray inch at a time. The water had swallowed the third step of the ladder overnight. By noon it would take the second. She had watched water do this her whole life—take things slowly, patiently, the way debt collectors work—and she had never once gotten used to it.

Cargo that could drown. That was the thought that kept circling her skull like a bird of prey. She'd hauled medicine, mail, engine parts, live chickens, once a wedding cake balanced on her knees for two hundred miles of turbulence. All of it cargo she could lose without losing herself. But this morning the Magpie's manifest included four human beings who could not swim worth a damn and two children who thought the whole enterprise was a carnival.

*You're not their mother,* she told herself. *You're not anyone's anything. You're a pilot with a broken airplane and a map you never wanted.*

The lie didn't even hold its shape long enough to be comforting.

Behind her, Tam Oduya was doing something violent and rhythmic to the engine housing with a wrench, muttering to it in three languages, none of them kind. Bess had come aboard an hour ago with a crate of food that could feed a mining camp and had begun rearranging the galley as if she'd owned it for years, which, knowing Bess, she probably intended to.

And Junie and Pip were supposed to be ashore.

That was the part Ines couldn't stop chewing on. She'd found them at dawn, curled together under the tarp over the spare fuel drums like a pair of kittens in a hayloft, Pip's head on Junie's shoulder, both of them soaked through and grinning about it when they woke. Stowaways. On a boat going upriver into monsoon country, toward rapids called Widow's Tooth and a gorge the trappers wouldn't fish anymore.

"You can't come," Ines had said, and meant it with everything she had.

Junie had looked up at her with those grave dark eyes—twelve years old and already carrying more grief than most adults Ines knew—and said, "There's nothing here. Dockmaster says the flood office is evacuating the low quarter Thursday. Pip's got nobody. I've got nobody." A pause, precise as a rivet gun. "You've got us."

*I don't,* Ines had almost said. *I've got a plane on a busted skid and a promise to a dead man walking and five years of practice leaving before anyone gets attached.*

But what came out was: "Life jackets. Always. Even asleep."

Which was how, apparently, you acquired children. Nobody warned you. There was no paperwork.

* * *

Tam surfaced from the engine compartment wiping his hands on a rag that had given up being a rag sometime last week. He came to stand beside her at the rail, close enough that she caught engine grease and woodsmoke off him, and followed her gaze downriver.

"Water's up another foot since yesterday," he said. "Widow's Tooth will be running mean by the time we reach it. Maybe too mean."

"Then we go around."

"There's no around. There's only through or back." He glanced at her sideways. "You want to say back? Now's the time. I'll pretend I suggested it so you don't have to owe me anything."

Ines almost laughed. Almost. It was the closest thing to kindness anyone had offered her in weeks—offering her an exit dressed as cowardice—and she hated how well he'd learned to read her in six days of caulking seams and hauling planks.

"We're going through," she said.

"Figured." He leaned on the rail. "Your sister flew, didn't she. That's where the Vane name comes from."

The words landed like a hook behind her ribs. She kept her eyes on the water. "Everyone flies, up here. It's not a name, it's a job description."

"Sure," Tam said, in the tone of a man filing something away for later, and went back to his engine.

She should have been angrier than she was. Instead there was just that dull pressure in her chest, the one that lived there permanently now, like ballast she couldn't jettison. Five years since the crash. Five years since she'd made the call—the wrong call, her call—in weather she'd been taught since childhood to respect, and Nell had died in the right-hand seat because Ines was the one with the hours, the authority, the arrogance. *Pilot in command.* That was what they wrote on the report. Pilot in command. As if command were a thing you could have instead of a thing that failed you.

She hadn't flown in real weather since. She'd built a whole life out of fair-weather hops and dry-season schedules, a lattice of small safe miles, and told herself it was freedom.

Now she was about to take a boat full of people she'd known less than a week into rising floodwater, because a dying prospector had handed her a piece of paper and a child had decided she was family, and because—this was the part she couldn't look at directly—some buried part of her believed that if she could carry this cargo safely, it might balance some older ledger.

Guilt rode shotgun. It always did. It just usually had the decency to stay quiet until she was alone.

* * *

They cast off at 0900, in rain that had stopped pretending to be weather and committed fully to being a condition.

Bess took the wheel with the serene authority of a woman who had steered worse boats through worse rivers, though when Ines asked her about it she said only, "I've run supplies up the Kestrel arm twice, dear, and drowned zero times, which on this river counts as expertise." Pip stationed himself at the bow rail in a life jacket two sizes too big, arms spread like a figurehead, daring the river to be impressed. Junie sat near Ines with Elias's map open between them, tracing the route with one finger, careful not to touch the ink.

"He drew this for her," Junie said. "His daughter. Look—it says *for Wren, if I'm slow.*"

"I know."

"Do you think she's still waiting?"

Ines looked at the marginal notes she'd been avoiding for days. *Third channel past the burned snag, Wren. Take the left even if it looks wrong. You always doubt the left.* A man writing instructions to a daughter across a hundred miles of flood, hoping the paper would arrive where he couldn't.

"I think," Ines said, "we're going to find out."

The dock fell away behind them. Halfway Bend shrank to a smear of tin roofs and gray smoke, and then the river bent, and it was gone, and there was nothing ahead but brown water, black timber, and rain moving in sheets across the current like curtains being drawn room by room.

Ines felt the old reflex fire—the itch in her hands, the calculation of distance. *Halfway Bend to Widow's Tooth, forty-one miles. Widow's Tooth to the escarpment cut, sixty. Sixty to the Landing.* Miles were how she kept the world manageable. Miles had edges. Miles ended.

But the boat didn't move in miles. It moved in hours, and hours moved in weather, and the weather answered to no arithmetic she trusted. For the first time in five years she was traveling at the speed of other people, and every one of them was cargo that could drown.

Pip whooped as the bow took the first swell. Bess corrected the wheel without looking up. Tam's engine turned over below decks, steady as a heartbeat someone else was lending them.

Ines gripped the rail and did not let herself count anything at all.

* * *

They made the burned snag by midafternoon, and Ines understood immediately why Elias had drawn it.

It rose out of the channel like a ribcage—a dead tree the size of a church steeple, stripped and blackened, its branches reaching downstream as if waving them toward the shortcut. The main channel swung wide around it, calm and obvious. The third channel split away to the left, narrow, fast, choked with debris riding the current.

"Map says left," Junie said.

"Map also says the left looks wrong," Ines said. "Elias wasn't wrong about much."

Bess eased the throttle back. Tam came up from the engine bay, dripping, and studied the water with the expression of a man reading a difficult letter. "Current's pushing hard into that entrance," he said. "We commit crooked or we don't commit. If we clip that snag coming in, we're done."

"Then we don't clip it," Ines said, and heard her own voice come out flat and certain, the voice she used to use in cockpits, the one she'd thought was retired. "Come in wide, straighten late. Trust the boat."

Bess looked at her for a long second. Then she grinned, pushed the throttle forward, and said, "Trust the boat. Lord help us all."

The Magpie swung left.

For eleven seconds—Ines counted, she couldn't help it—they were pointed straight at the snag's black roots while the current grabbed the hull and tried to spin them, and then Bess fed in power, Tam shouted something encouraging in Swahili that was probably not encouraging, and the bow broke sideways into the narrow channel with a sound like tearing cloth.

Then they were through. The channel narrowed to half a boat's width, walls of flooded cane closing overhead, water running green-black and fast beneath them, and the Magpie ran it clean for two miles before the channel opened onto a drowned forest and the rain slackened to a steady drum.

Nobody spoke for a while. Pip turned around at the bow, eyes enormous.

"Again," he said.

"No," said everyone else, in unison.

But Ines felt something loosen in her chest, some strap she'd been wearing so long she'd forgotten it was there. She had read the water. She had called the line. And the cargo—all of it, every drowning-capable pound of it—was intact.

*Don't get comfortable,* said the old voice, the guilt voice, the one that sounded like a crash investigator reading her name off a report. *Comfortable is how it happened last time.*

She looked at Junie, who was carefully folding the map along its original creases, and at Pip, wringing out his oversized jacket, and at the rain marching across the drowned trees, and she thought: *This time you know exactly what you're carrying. That's the difference. That has to be the difference.*

* * *

They tied up at dusk against a stand of ironwood, hull ticking against the trunks, and Bess produced a stew that tasted like forgiveness. The kids fell asleep mid-chew, more or less, Pip sliding down the bench until Junie propped him upright with one hand without looking away from the fire ring Tam had rigged on a sand patch.

Ines washed the pot at the waterline, and that was where Tam found her, crouched in the shallows, sleeves rolled, staring at nothing.

"The map," he said. "Show me tomorrow's stretch again. I want to check the fuel burn against the current."

She dried her hands and unrolled the paper on a flat rock, weighting the corners with stones. Tam leaned over it, and then went very still.

"Ines." His voice had changed. "Where did you get this?"

"Elias Marsh. Dying prospector, cabin past the Saddle country. Why?"

Tam's finger hovered over the lower margin—not the route notes, but a smaller annotation in different ink, faded, older. She'd never paid attention to it. She'd never once looked at it closely.

*Survey vault confirmed. Coordinates filed. Tell Vane.*

Tam looked up at her, and the firelight did something complicated to his face.

"Tell Vane," he repeated quietly. "Five years ago, a survey team went into the escarpment and never came out. One of them was named Vane." He met her eyes. "I think we need to talk about your surname. And I think we need to talk tonight, before we run the gorge tomorrow—because whatever's in that vault, Corliss Vance isn't the only one who wants it. And neither, apparently, are you."

The river slid past in the dark, patient as ever, carrying them toward morning.
