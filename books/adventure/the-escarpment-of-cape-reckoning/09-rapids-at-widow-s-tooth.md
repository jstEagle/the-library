## 9. Rapids at Widow's Tooth

The river told them a day early.

Ines heard it first as a change in the sound against the hull—not the lazy slap of floodwater but a lower, hungrier note, like something chewing. She was on deck at dawn with coffee she'd already forgotten she was holding, and by the time Tam came up to check the intake screens, she'd been listening for an hour.

"Feel that?" she said.

He cocked his head, and she watched him do what he always did—translate water into machinery. "Vibration's changed," he said. "Paddlewheel's working harder for less speed."

"That's because the river's going faster than you are." She set down the mug. "We're coming up on the gorge, aren't we. The one Elias marked."

Tam didn't answer right away. He went to the chart table under the awning, where Elias Marsh's map lay weighted at the corners with four engine bolts, and looked at the place where the ink turned agitated—a scribbled gorge between two bluffs, and beside it, in the dead man's cramped hand: *Widow's Tooth. Do not run it high. Do not run it drunk. I did both.*

"He wrote that like a joke," Junie said from the hatchway. She'd been sleeping up top since the swamps, claiming the cabin smelled like Bess's cooking, which it did, which everyone secretly liked.

"It's not a joke," Ines said.

"No," Tam agreed. "It's a confession."

* * *

They tied up below the gorge through the worst of the morning rain and walked the bank to look at what they'd have to run. It was the kind of errand Ines had done a hundred times in the air—walking a strip before taking a plane into it—but never on water, and the difference sat in her stomach like a stone.

Widow's Tooth was a basalt spur jutting from the eastern wall of the gorge, splitting the current into two channels. The left channel looked smooth—glassy, even, deceptively calm—and that was exactly what made her skin crawl, because she knew what glassy water over a submerged shelf meant. The right channel was ugly: white water standing in waves, a hole below the Tooth where the whole river folded back on itself and ate its own foam.

"The right side," Tam said.

"You see it too."

"The left is a lie. That flat water's moving twice as fast underneath. Boat goes in there, the current pins us against the wall and there's no power made that'll pull us off." He crouched at the edge, watching the boil lines. "The right side wants to flip us. But it flips you honestly."

Ines almost laughed. Almost. "You want the honest flip."

"I want the channel where the river tells you what it's doing." He glanced at her sideways. "You read air. Can you read this?"

She could. That was the problem. She stood at the edge of the gorge and let herself see it the way she saw weather—the V of a wave train pointing downstream, the dark seam where two currents braided, the tongue of smoother water threading between the holes like a runway centerline only she could see. There was a line through the right channel. A narrow one. Hit it wrong by ten feet and the Magpie would broach, roll, and feed all five of them plus Bess's stove to the gorge.

Hit it right and they lived.

"There," she said, and pointed. "Between the second wave and the hole. The river pushes left about halfway down—you can't fight it, you let it push and then straighten out late. If you straighten early, you're in the hole."

Tam studied the line for a long time. Then he said, "I can't hold that line with the wheel alone. Not with the wheel dragging in water like this. I need power on your call—hard ahead, hard astern, whatever you shout, I give it. You're my throttle."

"I'm your what?"

"You've flown boats with wings your whole life, Vane. This is just a slower crash." He was already walking back toward the Magpie, and over his shoulder: "Get everyone lashed in. Anything not tied down becomes a weapon."

* * *

Bess took the news the way she took everything—by feeding people. She made them eat eggs and rice before they cast off, standing over Junie and Pip until their bowls were empty, saying nothing about the gorge at all, which was its own kind of sermon. Pip ate fast and kept looking downstream, and Ines recognized the look. It was the look of a boy who thought rapids sounded like fun.

"Pip." He looked up at her, mouth full. "When I tell you to hold something, you hold it like the world depends on it. Because today it might."

"Yes, miss."

"And if you go in the water—" she stopped, because the sentence had a shape she didn't want to finish. "Don't go in the water."

Junie, eleven years old and older than the whole boat, took his hand without being asked. Ines looked away from that quickly.

They cast off at noon into rain so heavy the gorge walls blurred. Tam had the wheel; Ines stood at his shoulder at the rail, one hand on the gunwale, reading the water the way she'd once read a storm front—looking for the seam, the tell, the place where chaos admitted it had a pattern. The Magpie's old engine hammered below decks, and Tam had done something to it overnight that made it hammer louder, and when she'd asked him what, he'd said, "More," which was not an explanation but was somehow reassuring anyway.

The gorge swallowed them. Walls rose on either side, black stone streaked white with runoff, close enough some places that the paddlewheel's wash slapped back at them. The sound built—not loud all at once but layering, the way a storm builds, until Ines felt it in her teeth.

"There." She pointed. "The tongue. Starboard of the big wave, port of the foam."

"I see it."

"You don't, not yet. You will." The current grabbed the bow and swung it, casual as a hand on a shoulder, and Tam fought it level. "Let it take you. Don't fight the entry."

"I'm not fighting it."

"You are. You're steering like a man who thinks he knows where the boat should be instead of asking where the river wants it." She heard her own voice come out flat and certain, pilot-voice, the voice she used in weather that killed people, and realized she wasn't afraid. That was the strange part. Standing above a drowning machine, she felt clear as glass.

The first wave hit them broadside and the Magpie climbed it like a living thing, groaning, and came down the far side hard enough that Bess's whole pot rack let go and crashed somewhere below. Pip whooped. Junie elbowed him silent.

"Hold the line," Ines said. "Hold—hold—now starboard, hard, the river's pushing—"

The bow swung. The hole opened off their port side, a churning pit the color of wet slate, deep enough and hungry enough that she could see logs turning in it, whole trees going around like toys in a drain. Ten feet closer and they'd have been in it. Twenty feet past it, the channel widened, the waves laid down, and the gorge spat them out into long brown water that ran fast but honest.

For three seconds nobody moved.

Then Tam exhaled like a man surfacing and said, "Again?"

"Not today." But she was grinning, and so was he, and for a moment it was just the two of them laughing in the rain like idiots who'd beaten something that beat everybody.

Which was when Pip went over the side.

* * *

He'd been leaning on the rail. Later, Ines would replay it a thousand times—the rail, the wash, the boy's weight—and every version ended the same way: the river took him between one heartbeat and the next, the way rivers do, without malice or effort, the way you'd pick a coin off a table.

The sound Junie made was not a scream. It was smaller than a scream, and worse.

Ines was moving before her mind caught up. No calculation. No arithmetic of current speed versus swimmer drift versus the odds of a throw rope reaching. Five years of measuring everything—miles, hours, risk, cost—and none of it happened. What happened was: her boots were off, then they weren't, and she was in the water.

It hit her like a wall of cold fists. It pulled her down and rolled her and she came up gasping with the gorge walls spinning overhead, and she found him—there, ten feet downstream, small and thrashing, going under and up and under. She swam the seam the way she'd have flown it, angling across the current instead of chasing directly, letting the river carry her to him instead of fighting for every yard, and when she got a fist in his collar she hauled him against her chest and shouted the only thing worth shouting:

"Breathe when I say! Not before!"

The Magpie came thundering after them. She heard Tam's voice below everything, bellowing orders, heard the engine surge and die and surge again as he worked the wheel and throttle together, swinging the stern around, and then the boat was alongside and Bess was flat on her stomach on the deck with a coil of rope, and Ines caught it one-handed with Pip crushed against her, and for one bad moment the rope went taut and the river argued—

—and then hands. Many hands. Tam hauling the rope hand over hand, Bess hauling, even Junie hauling with her whole slight weight thrown backward, and the two of them came up the side like drowned cargo and collapsed onto the deck in a spreading pool of river.

Pip was coughing. That was the sound Ines fixed on—coughing meant breathing, breathing meant alive—and she held him while he coughed up half the gorge, patting his back too hard, saying "I've got you, I've got you, I've got you" in a voice she didn't recognize as her own.

Bess wrapped them both in blankets. Junie knelt in the puddle and put her forehead against Pip's wet hair and stayed there, saying nothing, which from Junie was a speech.

Tam crouched in front of Ines. Water ran off his chin. He looked at her for a long moment, and whatever he saw in her face made him say, quietly, "You didn't think first."

"No."

"You jumped."

"Yes."

Something passed over his face—not surprise, exactly. Recalculation. As if a number he'd been carrying for years had come back different than expected. "Okay," he said finally. "Okay, Vane."

* * *

That night they tied up in a bend above the gorge, and Bess fed everyone twice, and Pip fell asleep sitting up with rice still in his bowl, and nobody had the heart to move him except Junie, who arranged him against her shoulder with the competence of someone who'd been doing it a long time.

Ines sat on the cargo hatch with a blanket around her shoulders, shivering now that it was allowed, and Tam came and sat beside her without asking. For a while neither of them spoke. Downriver, faint under the rain, the gorge went on chewing.

"I counted," she said at last. "For five years. Miles, hours, deliveries. Numbers don't grieve, so I stayed with numbers." She turned her hands over, looked at the rope burn across her palm. "Today there weren't any numbers. He went in and I went in. That's all. I didn't decide it."

"No," Tam said. "You didn't."

"Is that what it's like? Just—going?"

He was quiet long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer. Then: "That's what it's like when it stops being a debt and starts being yours."

She looked at him. Rain dripped off the awning between them. "What did you carry, Tam? All those years at Halfway Bend with your leaky boat. What were you paying off?"

He didn't flinch, but he didn't answer either, and she understood suddenly that she'd asked the question he'd been waiting for her to ask, and that he wasn't ready, and that the not-ready was itself an answer she'd have to wait for.

"Tomorrow," he said instead, and nodded downriver, where the map said the channel narrowed again before the escarpment country began. "There's a trading post at the next bend. And Vane—" he paused, jaw working, "—there's smoke on the far bank. Has been since noon. Somebody's running a fire big enough to see in this rain."

Ines followed his gaze. Through the gray curtain, far off, a smudge of orange pulsed against the treeline. Too big for a campfire. Too organized for an accident.

"Somebody's buying fuel," she said slowly, remembering Corliss Vance's crew and their head start, "or burning evidence."

Tam stood, offering her a hand up. "Either way," he said, "we're not the only ones awake tonight."
