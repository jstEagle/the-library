## 10. What Tam Knows

The rain stopped an hour after midnight, and the silence it left behind was worse.

Ines lay in her bunk on the Magpie listening to the river work at the hull, that low patient grinding of water against wood, and she thought: this is what it sounds like when something is being worn down. She'd been thinking a lot about wearing down lately. About how rivers didn't rush, not really, not the way people said—they just never stopped, and never stopping was enough to move mountains given a few thousand years and no one watching.

She hadn't slept. Three days of caulking had put a fine ache in every joint she owned, and beneath the ache sat something older and less nameable, the way a tooth sits under a jawache. She knew what it was. She'd known since Halfway Bend, since the moment Tam Oduya had looked up from his engine with grease to his elbows and said her surname like he was reading it off a document.

*Vane.*

Nobody said that name out here. She'd made sure of it. Five years of being Ines, just Ines, the pilot, the woman who flew medicine and gossip and asked nothing back. She'd buried Vane so deep she sometimes went whole weeks without tripping over it.

But Tam had said it once, easy as breathing, and she'd told herself he'd picked it up from the plane's registration papers, which were old, which predated everything. That was a lie she could live with. She was good at those.

Boots on the deck above her. Heavy, unhurried, stopping directly over her bunk.

She was already sitting up when he knocked.

"Ines." Tam's voice through the deck planking, pitched low so it wouldn't wake Junie and Pip curled in the forward cabin like a pair of cats who'd decided the boat belonged to them now. "You awake?"

"Nobody sleeps on this boat," she said. "It leaks in six languages."

He came down the ladder anyway. He filled the little cabin the way he filled every space—not large, exactly, but *present*, the way an engine is present even when it's off. He didn't sit. He stood there holding two mugs of Bess's terrible coffee, and after a moment he handed her one, and she took it because refusing would have been a kind of answer, and she wasn't ready to hear whatever question came next.

"You checked the fuel lines again," she said. "Third time today."

"I check things when I can't sleep."

"That's a lot of checking."

"It is." He drank. Looked at the bulkhead, the rivets, the small brass lamp turning the cabin the color of weak tea. Anywhere but at her. And Ines understood, with the cold sliding certainty of a stall warning, that he was working up to something, and that she had perhaps thirty seconds to decide whether to let him.

She should have gone up on deck. She should have invented a job—there were always jobs, a boat like this grew jobs overnight like mold—and climbed out into the wet dark and been too busy for whatever he carried in those careful hands.

Instead she said, "Say it."

Tam set his mug down on the engine housing. The sound it made was very small and very final.

"The Vane Air crash," he said. "Five years ago. March. Out of Corvane Field, heading east over the escarpment in weather nobody should have flown."

The cabin got smaller. Or she did. It was hard to tell the difference.

"I don't know what you're talking about," Ines said, and heard her own voice do the thing it did—flat, pleasant, the voice of a woman discussing freight rates.

"Yeah," Tam said quietly. "You do."

* * *

Here is what Ines knew about Tam Oduya, three days in: he kept the Magpie's engine running on stubbornness and salvaged parts; he ate whatever Bess cooked without complaint; he let Pip follow him around the engine room asking questions and answered every single one, even the stupid ones, especially the stupid ones. He had hands that moved like they'd done every job twice. He did not talk about himself, and she had respected that, because she didn't either, and between them they'd built a comfortable architecture of silence—the kind two liars can share without either one feeling lied to.

Now the architecture was coming down and she saw it had been load-bearing.

"How," she said. Her voice cracked on the second word and she hated it, hated him, hated the mug warming her palms like it was complicit. "How do you know."

"Because I read the report." He finally looked at her, and his eyes in the lamplight were steady and sorry, and sorry was worse than accusing. Sorry meant he already knew the ending. "I worked salvage out of Corvane, before all this. Wreck recovery, mostly. When a plane goes down in our territory, we get the file." He paused. "Vane Air, twin-engine, registration CV-4471. Pilot Ingrid Vane. Copilot—"

"Don't."

"—copilot listed as her sister."

The mug was shaking. Ines set it down next to his, carefully, the way you set down something you might otherwise throw.

"Her name was Odile," she said.

The word came out of her like a tooth coming out—bloodless, shocking, leaving a hole where pressure used to be. Five years. Five years of saying nothing, of letting the name go unspoken until it had gone strange in her mouth, and now it fell out in a stranger's cabin on a leaking boat in the middle of a flooded country, and she couldn't take it back and found, to her horror, that she didn't want to.

"Odile," Tam repeated softly. Like he was receiving it. Like it mattered that someone else's mouth held it now too.

"She was twenty-six." Ines's laugh came out wrong, jagged. "Twenty-six and she flew like she'd invented it. I taught her short-field work when she was nineteen and by twenty-three she was better than me and we both pretended she wasn't. We flew together four years. Four years, and then—" Her throat closed. She made herself finish, because if she didn't finish she'd have to start again tomorrow, and she could not survive starting again tomorrow. "Then forty seconds of bad decision over the escarpment and she was gone, and everyone got very kind to me, and kindness felt like sentencing."

Tam didn't move. Didn't offer comfort, didn't reach for her, and she was grateful, because a hand on her shoulder right then would have shattered something she needed intact to keep talking.

"What happened," he said. Not a question. An invitation.

And here was the thing—the thing she'd flown five years and ten thousand miles to outrun, the thing that lived under the counting, under the miles and hours and deliveries, under the whole arithmetic of a life spent in motion:

"You want the report version?" she said. "Report version: weather deteriorated faster than forecast. Pilot elected to continue. Aircraft encountered severe downdraft on the lee side of the escarpment. Loss of control. Both crew recovered." She recited it flat, the way you recite a prayer you no longer believe in. "That's true. Every word. And it's also a lie, because reports don't say whose idea the flight was."

The lamp flickered. Somewhere above them, the river kept grinding.

"It was my route," Ines said. "My schedule. We were carrying vaccine, and the window was closing, and I stood in that hangar and looked at the sky and I *knew*. I want you to understand that. I looked at that sky and every instrument in my body said turn around, and I did the math instead. Miles versus margin. Time versus weather. And I ran the numbers and the numbers said go, because the numbers always say go when you want them to, and Odile—" Her breath hitched. "Odile looked at me and said, 'You sure?' Just like that. One word. She gave me the out. All I had to do was say no."

"And you said go."

"I said go." The words tasted like ash and river water. "Forty seconds later she was dead and I was alive, and I've been trying to figure out why ever since. Why the numbers. Why I listened to the numbers instead of my sister asking me one question with her whole face." She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes until the dark went red. "I didn't run from grief, Tam. Grief I could have carried. Grief is just love with nowhere to land. What I ran from was the other thing. The thing where you stand at the funeral and everyone says 'these things happen' and 'the weather turned' and you know—*you know*—that it didn't happen to you. You did it. You flew your sister into a mountain because you didn't want to lose a day."

The confession hung in the cabin, huge and ugly and finally, finally outside her body.

She waited for him to flinch. To do the kind-face thing, the *these things happen* thing, the thing that had driven her out of Corvane and across half a country. She braced for it like a hard landing.

What Tam said was: "I know."

Ines lowered her hands.

"What?"

"I know what blame does." His voice was rough now, sanded down. He lowered himself onto the bench across from her, knees nearly touching hers, and for the first time in three days he looked tired—all the way down tired, the kind that lives in a person for years. "I told you I read the report. I didn't tell you why I remembered it. Why I remembered *you*."

The river ground against the hull. She waited.

"There was a ferry," he said. "Eight years ago. Up north, the Kessume crossing. I was the engineer. Night run, storm coming, and the company man radioed and said make the crossing, there's a full manifest, we'll lose the contract otherwise." His jaw worked. "I knew the weather. I'd grown up on that water. And I was young, and I needed the job, and I told the captain we'd make it. My call. My numbers." He looked at his hands, which had gone still in a way that seemed effortful. "Eleven people, Ines. Eleven, including two kids. The ferry went over in the middle channel and I wasn't even hurt. Not a scratch. I stood on the bank afterward and watched them drag the channel for three days and I kept waiting for somebody to arrest me, and nobody did, because officially it was the weather. Officially these things happen."

"Oh," Ines said. It was all she had.

"So when I saw that registration on your plane at Halfway Bend—CV-4471—I knew the name before I knew the face. And I've been sitting on it for three days trying to decide whether telling you would be cruelty or mercy." He met her eyes. "I still don't know. But I watched you count everything on this boat. Fuel, rivets, caulking seams, the kids' meals. You count like a woman paying off a debt. And I recognized it, because I did the same thing for six years. Worked every rotten job on the coast. Slept in engine rooms. Never stayed anywhere long enough for anyone to ask me a real question." A ghost of a smile, joyless. "You can't outrun a number, though. Believe me. I tried eleven times harder than you."

Something broke open in Ines's chest—not painfully, or not only painfully. More like a window opening in a room sealed for years, letting in air that smelled of rain and diesel and someone else's grief, and finding that the air was breathable.

"Why are you on this boat, Tam?" she asked. "Really. Running south with a leaky hull and no destination."

"Same reason you're on it." He shrugged, and the shrug cost him visibly. "A man I couldn't save asked me to carry something, once, and I said no. Said it was too far, too risky, wrong season. He found another way and died doing it." He shook his head slowly. "When Elias Marsh grabbed my arm at the dock at Halfway Bend and asked me to help you get his map upriver—when he said, 'Son, I'm trading a dying man's last favor'—I couldn't say no twice. So here I am. Paying an eight-year-old debt with a stranger's promise."

"Elias asked *you*?"

"He asked whoever looked like they'd say yes. He was good at reading people." Tam almost smiled. "He read you in about four minutes, from what I hear."

"He read me in four minutes," Ines agreed, and heard her voice wobble, and let it.

* * *

They sat there while the coffee went cold, and the strangest thing happened, which was nothing. No thunderclap. No absolution. The river kept grinding, the lamp kept flickering, and Ines Vane sat in a leaky cabin having said the unsayable, and the world declined to end.

She kept waiting for the shame to come back and fill the space. It circled, sure—it would always circle, she understood that now, some debts you service rather than retire—but it couldn't land. Because shame needs secrecy the way fire needs oxygen, and she'd just thrown open every door.

"Odile would have liked this boat," she said at last, surprising herself.

"Yeah?"

"She'd have named it something ridiculous. She named everything. Our first plane she called the Teaspoon." Ines laughed—a real laugh, wrecked at the edges. "God. Five years and that's what comes back first. The Teaspoon."

"Tell me about her sometime," Tam said. "Not tonight. Sometime. The stories are the part that survives, I think. Everything else is just weather."

Ines looked at him—this careful, damaged man who had recognized her wreckage because it matched his own—and felt the compass needle in her chest swing off the heading she'd held for five years and settle somewhere new, somewhere that didn't have a mileage figure attached.

"Tam," she said. "The night at Widow's Tooth. When Pip went in the water. I didn't calculate anything. Did you notice? First time in five years I just—moved."

"I noticed."

"I think I'm getting worse at running."

"Good," he said. "It's terrible exercise."

She laughed again, and it turned into something else halfway through, and he had the grace to look at the bulkhead while she wiped her face, and neither of them called it crying, and both of them knew.

Above them, boots crossed the deck—Bess, doing her midnight rounds—and then a small voice, Junie's, calling sleepily from the forward cabin, and Pip's answering murmur, and the ordinary music of a boat full of people who were, by no plan anyone had signed, becoming each other's.

"We should sleep," Tam said, standing. "Long day tomorrow. We make the trading post at Sallow Creek by noon if the current holds."

Ines nodded. Then, as he reached the ladder:

"Tam. Thank you. For saying it out loud instead of letting me keep pretending you didn't know."

He paused, one hand on the rail, and something moved across his face—regret, or its cousin.

"That's the other thing I have to tell you," he said. "And you're not going to like it."

Ines went cold. "What."

"At Sallow Creek. Yesterday, while you were re-packing the medical crate." He met her eyes steadily. "There was a man buying passage upriver. Big party, cash money, asking questions about water levels above the gorge. Asking about the escarpment passes." He hesitated. "He wore a Vance Salvage jacket, Ines. And he asked one question twice. He asked whether anybody aboard was headed for Marsh's cache."

The lamp flickered. Downriver, faint under the river-sound, came the distant throb of a big engine running hard against the current—too fast, too late at night, and gaining.

"They know about the map," Tam said.
