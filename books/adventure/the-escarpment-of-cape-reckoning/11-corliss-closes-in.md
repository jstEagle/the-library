## 11. Corliss Closes In

The trading post at Sallow Creek announced itself by smell before sight—woodsmoke, wet hides, and the sweet rot of fruit nobody could sell because the road was gone. Ines brought the Magpie in against the current, Tam feathering the throttle while she read the eddies off the pilings, and they tied up under a roof of corrugated tin that drummed like applause for a show nobody wanted.

Sallow Creek was three buildings and a lie. The post itself, squat and gray, its veranda sagging under crates of tinned peaches that had been there so long the labels had bleached to ghosts. A fuel shed. And the Sallow Rest, which called itself a hotel the way a dugout called itself a ferry. The river had climbed the bank overnight and stood six inches deep on the lowest step of everything, brown and patient, waiting to be invited further in.

"Two hours," Tam said, coiling the line. "We take on diesel, take on food, we don't take on trouble."

"When have we ever managed that?" Bess said, already climbing onto the dock with her skirt hem knotted up around her knees.

Junie and Pip came up from below deck where they'd been hiding since Widow's Tooth, officially because Bess said the cabin needed sweeping and unofficially because neither of them had stopped talking about the rapids since it happened. Pip walked with a new swagger, one hand fisted in his shirt where Ines had hauled him out of the water, as if he were carrying a medal there. Junie walked close to Ines—not touching, never touching, but near enough that Ines could feel her like a second shadow, and found she didn't mind.

The post's owner was a narrow man named Ferro with a beard trimmed like he'd lost an argument with it. He took their list without comment, wrote nothing down, and said, "You're the third boat today."

Ines felt it before she understood it—a cold drop through the middle of her, like stepping off a stair that wasn't there. "Third boat."

"Fellow came through yesterday evening. Woman leading them. Bought all my diesel spare, paid cash, didn't haggle." Ferro shrugged. "Salvage crowd. Had that look."

"What look?" Tam asked.

"The look of people who aren't coming back this way."

* * *

They ate at a plank table on the veranda because the interior of the post smelled of kerosene and mouse, and it was there, over stewed something and hard bread, that Ines laid out what she'd been carrying since Ferro's words.

"She bought the diesel," Ines said. "All of it. That's not passage, that's denial. She wants us stuck here or walking."

"She doesn't know about us," Tam said. "She knows about the map. There's a difference."

"There's no difference if she's willing to buy every liter of fuel between here and the escarpment."

Bess set down her spoon. She did that sometimes—the small precise movements of a woman who had raised children through wars she never mentioned—and when she spoke, the table went quiet the way water goes quiet before it freezes. "Then we don't fight her for fuel. We find out what she knows, and we decide whether it changes anything."

"And how do we do that?" Tam asked.

Bess looked downriver, toward the moored boats at the far end of the dock, where a crew was working under tarps in the rain. Six figures, maybe seven. Winches. Salvage gear. A crane arm folded flat along a barge hull painted the color of old blood.

"One of them has been watching Junie since we tied up," Bess said. "Not the way men watch. The way someone watches a door they're thinking of leaving through."

* * *

The defector's name was Ansel Roque, and he came to them at dusk, alone, hands visible, walking the length of the dock like a man approaching a dog he wasn't sure was friendly. He was maybe forty, built like rope, with a face that had been handsome once and had since been used for other purposes. He stopped ten feet away and said, to Bess, oddly, not to Ines: "I need to talk to whoever makes decisions."

"You're looking at her," Bess said.

Roque glanced at Ines. Ines said nothing. It was becoming a habit, letting people assume the wrong hierarchy and then correcting it only when it mattered.

"I know what you're carrying," Roque said. "Marsh's map. I know where it goes. I've known since Halfway Bend."

"How?"

"Because I drew half of it." He said it flatly, without pride. "Ten years ago I ran survey lines up that escarpment for the government. Marsh hired me after, private work, marking his cache route. I know every switchback on that trail better than my own signature."

Tam had come up silently behind him, and Ines saw the big wrench in his hand, held loose, conversational. She shook her head once. Tam leaned it against a piling but didn't move away.

"So talk," Bess said.

Roque talked. He told them Corliss Vance had bought his contract out from under the survey company eight months ago, that she'd been hunting Marsh's cache since before Marsh was dying, that the map Elias traded Ines was the last piece she needed. He told them about the fuel. He told them Vance had two boats and eleven people and a winch barge rated for forty tons.

And then he told them the thing that made the rain sound suddenly loud.

"She's not going to the Landing," he said. "I want that clear. Whatever you think this is—salvage, relief, some kind of rescue run—it isn't. The cache sits above Kestrel's Landing, but the vault inside it isn't Marsh's. It predates him by sixty years. Pre-flood survey archive. Title records, land grants, the original escarpment plats. Everything the government lost when the first flood took the registry office." He wiped rain off his face with the back of his wrist. "Vance doesn't care about medicine. Medicine is heavy and perishable and worth nothing. Paper is worth everything. She's going to strip the vault, run it downriver, and sell it to the land company that's been trying to buy out Kestrel's Landing for three years. The same company that's been telling those people their homes are worthless because nobody can prove they own them."

Nobody spoke. On the far dock, the salvage crew's lanterns swung in the wind, yellow smears in the gray.

"The Landing," Ines said finally. Her voice came out strange, sanded flat. "There are families up there. Kids. They've been flooded out twice and told it's their own fault for building in a floodplain."

"I know," Roque said. "I surveyed their boundaries. I know exactly how many of them there are and what their deeds are worth, which is nothing, until the vault says otherwise."

"Why are you telling us this?" Tam asked. "What does it cost you?"

Roque was quiet long enough that the river filled the silence. When he answered, he didn't look at any of them.

"Because I drew those boundary lines," he said. "And I've spent eight months watching a woman plan to erase them. And I told myself it was just work. Same as I always tell myself." He finally met Ines's eyes, and what was in them was so familiar it made her chest hurt. "You spend enough years telling yourself it's just work, you either stop believing it or you stop being somebody. I haven't stopped yet. Barely."

* * *

Bess took him aside to the galley, alone, and Ines didn't ask what passed between them—only heard, later, through the bulkhead, the low murmur of Bess's voice doing whatever Bess's voice did, that patient relentless tide that had worn down harder things than Ansel Roque. What Ines knew was this: by full dark, Roque had a bunk aboard, a bowl of Bess's rice in front of him, and a truce that everyone involved understood was provisional, the way a ceasefire is provisional.

He earned part of it before midnight. Sitting cross-legged on the engine room floor with Tam's charts spread around him, he traced the escarpment approaches with a pencil stub and showed them what Vance's purchase of the fuel meant in practical terms: she hadn't just denied them diesel, she'd committed herself to a schedule. Two boats, eleven mouths, a barge loaded for stripping work—she couldn't wait out weather. If the monsoon closed the passes on schedule, she had a window of four days, maybe five, to reach the cache, gut it, and run.

"We have less," Tam said. "One boat, no reserve fuel, and a portage."

"We have something she doesn't," Ines said.

Everyone looked at her. She realized she'd said it without deciding to, the way you say things when you've stopped guarding the door.

"Which is?" Roque asked.

"Elias Marsh walked that route twice a year for twenty years and wrote notes on the map like he was writing letters. He marked water that runs in dry season. He marked a cut through the marsh country that isn't on any survey." She tapped the map, the marginal script, the tight patient handwriting of a man who'd had nothing but time and regret. "Corliss Vance has your survey lines. Clean, official, ten years old. We have a dead man's obsession. I'd bet on the dead man."

Roque studied the map for a long moment. Then he laughed—one short, unamused breath. "So would I," he said. "That's why I'm here."

* * *

It was past midnight when Ines gave up on sleep and went up on deck. The rain had thinned to a drifting mist, and the river breathed against the hull, and the lanterns on the salvage barge across the way had gone dark except one.

Junie was there. Sitting on the cargo hatch with her knees pulled up, awake, watching the dark boat.

"You should be asleep," Ines said.

"You too."

Fair. Ines sat down beside her. For a while neither of them said anything, which had become, Ines noticed with something like alarm, comfortable.

"That woman," Junie said. "On the other boat. Corliss Vance."

"What about her?"

"Pip asked Bess if she was bad. Like a story bad. Bess said she wasn't a story, she was just a person who decided something a long time ago and never looked at it again." Junie turned the words over carefully, the way she turned over everything. "Is that true? Can a person just... decide something, and then it's too late to look at it again?"

Ines watched the dark barge. Watched the single lantern, and the shape moving behind it—someone standing watch, alone, on a boat full of people who followed a woman who had decided something a long time ago.

"Yeah," Ines said. "It's true."

"Have you ever done that? Decided something and not looked at it again?"

The question landed the way Junie's questions always landed—no force at all, and total penetration, like water finding the crack in anything. Ines thought about saying something safe. Something edited. Five years of edits sat right there, ready.

"Not yet," she said.

Junie nodded slowly, accepting this, and put her head briefly against Ines's shoulder—a touch lasting two seconds, weightless, devastating—and then went below to bed.

Ines stayed on the hatch a while longer. Across the water, the watch-light on the salvage barge went out. Somewhere in the black, almost too faint to hear, came the sound of an engine turning over—Vance's lead boat, casting off in the dark, running the river at night, buying hours the honest way and the dishonest way both.

"They're moving," Tam said quietly, from the wheelhouse door. He'd been there longer than she'd known. He was getting good at that, or she was getting bad at noticing.

"Then so are we," Ines said. "Wake everyone. We run the marsh route at first light—Elias's cut, straight through the swamp country." She looked at the map in her hands, at the dead man's handwriting, at the note in the margin of the flooded channel that read, in his careful script: *Mara, if you're reading this, mind the third channel marker. It lies.*

Ines folded the map along its worn creases and felt the promise shift its weight in her arms—no longer just medicine now, no longer just a delivery, but a race against a woman who had decided something a long time ago and never looked back.

"First light," she said again, and went to wake the others.
