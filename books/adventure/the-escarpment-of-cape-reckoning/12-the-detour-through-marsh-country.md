## 12. The Detour Through Marsh Country

The channel was gone.

Not flooded—*gone*, swallowed whole, the trading post at its mouth reduced to a roofline and a flagpole standing in brown water like a man up to his chin. Ines stood on the Magpie's bow with Elias Marsh's map open against her chest, the paper gone soft as cloth from handling, and watched the river where a river shouldn't be.

"That's not on the map," Pip said behind her.

"Everything's on the map," Tam said from the engine hatch, wiping his hands on a rag that had given up being a rag sometime yesterday. "The map just doesn't agree with the water about it."

Ines traced the main channel with her finger. It ran straight through what was now a lake two miles wide. Elias had drawn it five years ago, maybe six, back when the monsoon still behaved like weather instead of appetite. But there—bottom left corner, cramped into the margin in pencil gone silver with age—a line broke away westward through a tangle of hatched marks he'd labeled simply: *marsh country. slow but true.*

And beneath it, smaller, pressed so hard the pencil had nearly torn the paper:

*Don't take the fast way when it's lying to you.*

"He knew," Ines said quietly.

"He knew this country," Tam corrected. "Knowing it and knowing it would do *this* are different animals." He came up beside her, squinting at the drowned channel, then at the marsh route, then at the sky, which was doing its monsoon impression of a lid closing. "It'll cost us two days. Maybe three if the reed beds are thick."

"We don't have three days."

"We have less than that on the main channel, because the main channel doesn't exist anymore." He said it without heat, the way he said everything, and Ines hated how much she'd come to rely on that steadiness—hated it because reliance was a debt, and debts were how people got kept.

She looked at Junie and Pip, who were supposed to be bailing the cockpit and were instead conducting a war against a frog that had boarded at the last stop. Bess was below, arguing with the stove. The Magpie sat low in the water, heavy with fuel drums and donated rice and a crate of letters that had nothing to do with any of them and everything to do with all of them.

"Marsh country," Ines said, and folded the map along Elias's crease.

* * *

The marsh did not want them there. That was the first thing. The second thing was that it didn't especially care—they were beneath its notice, a steel boat nosing through corridors of reed taller than the wheelhouse, water the color of steeped tea, the sky cut into ribbons above them. Tam ran the engine at half throttle and steered by compass and by Elias's notes, which Ines read aloud from the bow because she was the only one who could decipher the handwriting.

*"Third bend after the dead kapok, bear south of the bird rookery or you'll scrape your hull on something the map has no business knowing about."*

"There's no dead kapok," Pip called up.

There was a dead kapok. It rose out of the reeds like the ribcage of something enormous, white and branchless, and Ines felt the small cold thrill of it—that a dead man's pencil marks could still steer the living. She didn't say so. She'd learned already that saying things like that out loud made Junie look at her with an expression too old for her face.

They made nine miles the first day. Nine miles, in water that should have carried them thirty. The Magpie's propeller fouled twice on submerged fence wire—fence wire, out here, from farms drowned so long ago nobody living remembered their names—and both times Tam went over the side in his waders and cleared it by feel, coming up swearing softly and methodically, a man who rationed his anger like drinking water.

Bess cooked rice and dried fish and canned peaches, and it tasted like a feast because everything did when you were tired enough, and Junie ate sitting as close to Ines as she could get without actually leaning on her, which Ines permitted and pretended not to notice.

That night they tied off to a strangler fig and let the boat rock. The rain came down soft instead of violent, almost polite, drumming on the canopy Bess had rigged. And Junie—Junie, who never asked for anything, who had stowed away on a stranger's boat the way children stow away from grief rather than toward anything—said:

"Read some more of them. The notes."

Ines looked at Tam. Tam looked at the stove. Traitor, both of them.

"It's getting late," Ines said.

"You read them yesterday," Junie said. "You stopped at the one about the heron."

Ines had stopped at the one about the heron because it had done something to her throat she hadn't authorized. She found the page now by feel, in the lantern light, Elias's cramped pencil marching across the margin of his own map like a man talking to keep himself company.

*"'Day forty-one, I think. Saw a grey heron stand in the shallows for near an hour without moving, waiting on one fish. I thought, that's patience, that is. Then I thought, no—it's not patience, it's certainty. The bird knows the fish is coming. I used to be certain like that. About Ruth. That she'd wait. Certainty's just another word for pride when you're the one who left.'"*

Silence, except the rain.

"Who's Ruth?" Pip asked.

"His daughter," Junie said, before anyone else could. "Mara's mother." She said it the way other children recited facts they'd decided mattered. "He left to find the cache so he could come back with enough to matter, and she died before he got there, and now Mara's alone at the Landing, and we're bringing him home even though he's dead."

Nobody corrected her. There was nothing to correct.

"Read another," Junie said.

Ines read another.

* * *

By the third day Ines had stopped pretending the readings were for the child's benefit.

It happened gradually, the way most surrenders do. The first night it was Junie asking. The second night it was Pip too, and Bess drying her hands and staying in the cabin "just till that one's done," and Tam appearing in the doorway with engine oil still on his knuckles, not entering, not leaving. By the third night Ines opened the map before anyone asked, and the whole crew arranged itself around the lantern like a congregation, and she understood that she had become something she had spent five years refusing to be: the voice of a family's dead man.

The notes had changed. She'd noticed it the way you notice a change in someone's breathing. The early marginalia were navigation—bearings, depths, warnings about snags and sandbars, the terse shorthand of a man surveying his own salvation. But somewhere around day thirty of whatever journey Elias had made through this same marsh, years ago, the navigation had started carrying passengers. A note about water depth would end with *Ruth hated deep water; she'd have laughed at me poling through this.* A warning about a false channel would close with *I took the false channel once in my life and it cost me eleven years. Don't.* A sketch of a campsite came annotated with *slept badly, dreamed the house on Alder Street, dreamed her at the window. I am going home. I am going home. I am going—*

The sentence never finished. It just repeated, three times, and then the pencil had dug hard enough to tear through to the next page.

Ines read that one and had to stop, and Bess put a hand on her shoulder, and Ines let it stay there for exactly four seconds, which was three seconds longer than she'd allowed anyone to touch her since before she could remember allowing it.

"Why'd he write them on the map?" Pip asked once. "Why not a letter?"

"Because a letter can be lost," Tam said from the doorway. "A map gets used. He wanted whoever came after him to have to carry him along."

Ines looked at him. He held her gaze, mild and unblinking, and she understood that he wasn't talking about Elias anymore, or not only, and that Tam Oduya had been carrying something along for years himself, and that sooner or later—sooner, probably, because the river was rising and men like Tam paid their debts before the water did—he was going to set it down in front of her and make her look at it.

She read the next note instead.

* * *

On the fourth day the marsh tried to kill them, in its unhurried way.

It came as a reed bed that wasn't on the map—or was on the map, drawn as open water, six years stale—and the Magpie plowed into it at speed and stopped as though the river had put out a hand. The impact threw Pip against the rail and cracked the portside rubbing strake and killed the engine with a sound like a held breath released.

They were stuck. Properly stuck, hull gripped by a matting of root and silt strong as concrete, in a corridor of reeds with no sightline farther than fifty feet in any direction, and the light going amber and then bruised with evening.

Tam stripped to the waist and went in with the boat hook and a machete, cutting the strake free while Ines poled from the bow, and Bess ran the bilge pump, and Junie and Pip bailed with buckets because there is always water coming in somewhere, always, that's the deal boats make with rivers. For two hours nobody spoke except in commands. It was, Ines realized somewhere in the middle of it, the least lonely she had ever been in her life, hauling on a pole beside strangers in a drowning swamp, and the realization frightened her more than the reed bed had.

They freed her at full dark and backed out and found open channel half a mile south, exactly where a note written in silver pencil said it would be:

*"'If the reed bed takes you—and it will, it took me—you'll find the true channel south by the twin lightning-struck pines. I named this stretch Forgiveness Run, because it lets you back in after you've been a fool. Ruth would have liked that name. She forgave easy. Too easy. I'm counting on it.'"*

"He counted on her forgiving him," Junie said quietly, "and she died first."

"Yes," Ines said.

"And now he's dead too, and Mara's the only one left to forgive him, and she doesn't even know he was sorry."

Ines closed the map carefully, along its creases, the way you'd fold a letter you intended to answer.

"No," she said. "She'll know. We're carrying it to her."

Junie looked at her across the lantern, and something passed between them that had no name Ines was willing to give it—the child looking at her the way she'd listened to the notes all week, like scripture, like proof that the dead could still speak if somebody agreed to be their mouth. And Ines understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that she had already agreed. Somewhere between Halfway Bend and here, between the heron note and the torn page, she had stopped being a courier and become a keeper.

She lay awake that night listening to the rain and the river and Tam's engine ticking as it cooled, and she did not count miles. She caught herself counting heartbeats instead, hers and the ones sleeping around her in the cabin, and she let the number go uncounted rather than spoil it.

* * *

They cleared the marsh on the sixth morning, into a country of drowned forest and long grey light, and the map's main channel resumed ahead of them, wide and swollen and running fast toward Kestrel's Landing. Two days, if the water behaved. Less than that, if it didn't.

Ines was folding the map away when her thumb caught on something at the spine of the fold—a thickness, a wrongness, a place where the paper was doubled. She frowned. Turned it to the light.

Pinned inside the final crease, hidden in a fold so precise it had survived six years of handling, was a second piece of paper. Smaller. Different stock. Folded in thirds, sealed with a smear of wax gone the color of old blood.

Not a note. Not navigation. An envelope, with a single word on the front in Elias Marsh's hand:

*Ruth.*

Ines stared at it. Around her the Magpie pushed into the current, the engine climbing to working pitch, the Landing drawing nearer with every mile. A letter to a dead woman, carried by a dead man's map, addressed to a daughter who would never read it—and a granddaughter waiting at the end of the water who might.

"Ines?" Tam's voice, from the wheel. "What is it?"

She turned the envelope over once in her fingers. It weighed nothing. It weighed everything.

"His last letter," she said. "It's addressed to Ruth." She looked up at Tam, and her voice came out lower than she meant it to. "But she's not at the Landing, Tam. Her daughter is."

Behind them, unnoticed, Junie had gone very still, watching the envelope the way you watch a door you're afraid might open.
