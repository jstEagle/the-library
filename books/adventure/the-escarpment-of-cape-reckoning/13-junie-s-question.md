## 13. Junie's Question

The rain stopped an hour after dark, and the silence it left behind was so complete that Ines could hear the river thinking.

They had tied off the Magpie against a stand of flooded casuarina trees, half a mile short of the marsh channel Elias's map called the Long Throat—a name he'd underlined twice, with a marginal note Ines hadn't yet read aloud. The water here moved slow and brown and patient, carrying whole trees past them like it had somewhere better to be. Mosquito coils burned on the rail. Bess had gone below to sleep off her watch. Pip was out before his head hit the bunk, still holding a biscuit.

Junie wasn't asleep.

Ines found her sitting on the cargo hatch at the stern, knees pulled up, watching the water slide by. She was nine years old and built out of elbows and questions, and she had the particular stillness of children who had learned early that adults tell you true things when they think you aren't listening.

Ines should have gone below. There was fuel to calculate, a chart to study, a hundred small tasks she used the way other people used walls. Instead she sat down on the hatch beside the girl, close enough to share warmth, far enough not to presume.

For a long time neither of them said anything. That was the thing about Junie nobody expected—she could be quiet like a held breath, not like an empty room.

"You flew over our house once," Junie said finally. "Last year. Before the flood."

"I fly over a lot of houses."

"You dipped your wings." Junie turned to look at her, and in the low green glow of the running light her eyes were very serious. "Pip says you do it for luck. Bess says you do it so people know someone's coming. Which is it?"

"Neither," Ines said. "I do it because it costs nothing and somebody down there might need to see it."

Junie considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then she asked the question.

"Why don't you ever stay anywhere?"

* * *

There it was. No wind-up, no softening. Children asked questions like dropping stones down wells—they didn't wait to hear how deep it went.

Ines felt every one of her defenses present itself, orderly as a checklist. *Pre-flight inspection of the heart.* She could give the standard answer—the work moves, the weather moves, a pilot goes where the flying is. She'd given it to settlement heads and mission nurses and one persistent customs officer in Port Meridian who'd written it down like it meant something.

She opened her mouth to give it to Junie.

And found she couldn't.

Maybe it was the hour. Maybe it was the river, which made everything feel provisional, unmoored, easy to say. Maybe it was five days of caulking seams with Tam Oduya while dock kids ran supplies and Bess fed them unasked, five days of belonging creeping up on her like water into a hull, and her correcting it, correcting it, correcting it—and being tired, suddenly, bone-tired, of correcting.

Or maybe it was that Junie had lost her mother to the flood season, and knew exactly what it cost to ask a hard question, and deserved better than a wall.

"Because staying is how you lose things," Ines said.

The words came out rougher than she intended. Junie didn't flinch. She just waited, the way she waited for stories, arms wrapped around her knees.

"I had a sister," Ines said.

She hadn't said it aloud in five years. Not to doctors, not to investigators, not to the priest at the memorial service who'd kept looking at her like she was a door that wouldn't open. Saying it now felt like pulling a splinter that had grown a root.

"Her name was Wren. She was my copilot. We flew supply runs together all across the interior—same routes I fly now, mostly. She was better at landings than me. Worse at arguing. She'd apologize to the plane when we bounced a touchdown." Ines heard her own voice do something strange on that last part, and let it. "Two years ago—no. Five. Five years ago we got caught above the escarpment in weather I should have turned around in."

Junie said nothing. Downriver, something big moved through the shallows and settled.

"There was a gap in the ridge," Ines went on. "A notch. I'd been through it eleven times. I knew it like my own kitchen. The storm was building fast but I did the arithmetic—you always do the arithmetic, hours versus miles versus fuel versus weather—and the arithmetic said we'd make it through before it closed. And the arithmetic was wrong, because the arithmetic doesn't know anything. It doesn't know the wind will shear off the escarpment face at two hundred feet. It doesn't know your sister is sitting next to you saying *go around, Ines, go around,* and you're already committed because turning back feels like losing."

Her hands had closed on the edge of the hatch without her noticing. Her knuckles were white.

"She died in the crash," she said. "I lived. That's the whole story. That's everything anyone needs to know about why I don't stay."

Junie frowned—not at the death, Ines realized, but at the logic. Children take death straight on; it's the evasions that confuse them.

"But staying isn't crashing," Junie said. "Staying is just staying."

"Yes."

"So if you never stay anywhere, then—" Junie worked it through, lips moving slightly, doing the arithmetic she'd inherited from no one, arriving where children always arrive, at the terrible clean center of things. "Then nobody can ever be your sister again. Because you leave before they get that close."

The mosquito coil between them guttered and flared.

Ines looked out at the black water and understood that she had just been seen more clearly than at any moment in five years. Not by a doctor, not by a lover, not by God. By a nine-year-old on a cargo hatch, who had taken the entire architecture of her survival apart in four sentences.

"That's right," Ines said quietly. "That's exactly right."

"Does it work?"

"What?"

"Not letting anybody get close." Junie's voice wasn't cruel. It was genuinely curious, the way you'd ask if a medicine worked. "Does it stop the hurting?"

And there it was—the question under the question, the one Ines had spent five years refusing to run the numbers on, because she already knew the answer and knowing it would change everything.

"No," Ines said. "It doesn't work. It just makes the hurting quieter. Like flying above the storm instead of through it. Everything underneath keeps happening. You just can't see it anymore."

Junie nodded slowly, satisfied, as though Ines had finally passed some test she'd been failing since Halfway Bend. Then she leaned sideways until her shoulder rested against Ines's arm—light as a bird landing, ready to take off again at any twitch—and stayed there.

"My mum used to count us at night," Junie said. "Me and Pip and Dad. She'd touch each of our heads and say the number. One, two, three. Every night, even when nothing happened. I thought it was silly." A pause. "I do it now. For Pip. Even when nothing happens."

Ines's throat closed. She put her arm around the girl's shoulders, carefully, the way you handle something you're afraid of breaking—or of being broken by.

"Count him every night," Ines managed. "Even when nothing happens. Especially then."

* * *

Tam heard most of it from the wheelhouse doorway, and would deny for years afterward that he'd been standing there long enough to hear any of it.

He'd come up to check the tie-lines—he'd told himself that, and almost believed it—and had stopped when he saw the two figures on the stern hatch, silhouetted against the running light. He'd meant to turn around. He hadn't.

He heard Ines Vane say her sister's name.

He stood very still in the shadow of the wheelhouse, one hand on the frame, and listened to the woman he'd recognized on a dock in Halfway Bend—the pilot of the Vane aircraft, the survivor of the escarpment crash, the name that had been a cold stone in his pocket for five years—say out loud what the accident report had only ever implied. *I was already committed because turning back felt like losing.*

He'd carried his own version of that sentence a long way. He knew its weight exactly.

What undid him wasn't the confession. It was the ending—Junie leaning into her shoulder, and Ines Vane, the woman who measured life in departures, putting her arm around the girl and holding on like the hatch might pitch her into the river if she didn't.

*She's not running anymore,* Tam thought, and the realization landed in him with a strange double weight: relief, and fear. Because he'd built his own plans around the assumption that everyone left eventually. It was the one law of the river he'd trusted. And watching her sit still in the dark, keeping watch over a sleeping child without being asked, he understood that the law had just been repealed, and he had no idea what he was going to do about that.

He withdrew down the companionway without a sound, and lay in his bunk staring at the overhead, listening to the river think.

* * *

Ines stayed on the hatch long after Junie fell asleep against her shoulder. She counted the girl's breaths without meaning to—one, two, three—and understood, with something between horror and homecoming, that she was counting people now instead of miles.

Eventually she carried Junie below, laid her beside Pip, and stood a moment in the lamplight looking at the two of them tangled together like puppies. Bess stirred in her bunk and said nothing, which was its own kind of blessing.

On deck, the clouds had torn open along the eastern escarpment, and moonlight came down the valley in a single wide bar, laying silver across the flooded plain. Ines stood at the rail and looked at it—the wall she'd flown alongside for five years, never once looking at it straight—and let herself look.

Then she saw the light.

It came from upstream, around the bend below the Long Throat: a white flare, rising, hanging, dying. Then another. Deliberate. Spaced. Someone signaling in the dark, three miles ahead of them, in country where nobody had any business being.

Not distress. Distress fired flares in threes, fast. This was two, slow, patient—a mark, a message, a flag planted.

Corliss Vance was already in the Throat.

Ines woke Tam with a hand on his shoulder, and by the way his eyes opened—no confusion, no groggy blink, just immediate and total attention—she knew he'd seen the flares too, or heard them, or simply known, the way engineers know when a machine is running wrong.

"How much draft do we have under us?" she asked.

"Four feet, maybe less. The Throat won't take the Magpie at night even if the channel's open." He was already moving, pulling the chart, striking a match. "But Elias drew another way. Look here—" His finger stopped on the map, on the tangle of contour lines west of the channel, where Elias's handwriting climbed the margin in tight, urgent script. "That's not a river route. That's a ridge trail. Twelve miles overland."

Ines stared at it. Twelve miles of portage, hauling a boat over a mountain spine, in monsoon country, with two children aboard.

"He marked it *for Mara, if she's stubborn like me,*" Tam said.

Ines looked at the sleeping kids below, at the flare-smoke still ghosting the air upstream, at the map drawn by a dying man for a daughter he'd never see again.

"Wake Bess," she said. "We go at first light."
