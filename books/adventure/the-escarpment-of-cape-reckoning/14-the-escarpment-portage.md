## 14. The Escarpment Portage

The rain stopped at dawn, which was worse. Ines had learned by now that the monsoon's pauses weren't mercy—they were reloads. The sky above the escarpment hung low and bruised, and the ridge trail Elias had marked on his map climbed out of the marsh country like a scar someone had tried to stitch shut and failed.

"Two miles," Tam said, studying the map by the gray light. He'd spread it on the Magpie's deck, weighted at the corners with four washers and a tin of Bess's tobacco. "Maybe three, with the switchbacks. Elias's notes say there's a slide about halfway up."

"His notes also say the trail's passable," Bess said. She stood at the rail with her arms crossed, looking up at the ridge the way a woman looks at a bill she can't argue with. "Passable for a mule, maybe. Not for a boat."

"Not for a whole boat," Tam said. "We take her apart."

The word *apart* sat in the air. Ines looked at the Magpie—her hull patched twice since Widow's Tooth, her rail scarred, her engine room smelling of Tam's sweat and diesel—and felt something she refused to name. It was ridiculous. It was a boat. It was a half-sunk wreck Tam had been nursing back to life when she'd met him, and now the idea of unbolting it made her chest tighten like someone was asking her to disassemble a person.

"How long?" she asked.

"Two days to strip her down to sections we can drag," Tam said. "A day up the ridge if the trail's kind. It won't be. Two days to put her back together on the far side."

"Five days," Ines said. "The pass closes in six."

"Then we don't sleep much."

They didn't.

* * *

Taking the Magpie apart was a kind of surgery, and Tam was the only surgeon. Ines had expected to feel useless—she could read water and weather and the lie in a man's voice, but she couldn't tell a kingpin from a cotter pin—and for the first morning she was, mostly, a pair of hands that held things. But Tam taught the way Elias's marginal notes read: no wasted words, everything in the order you'd need it. *Hold here. That bolt's the one that fights. Don't force it; ask it.*

By the second morning she was pulling the engine's fuel lines herself, and when she got the last one free without kinking it, Tam looked at her over the manifold and said, "You'd have made a decent engineer."

"You'd have made a decent pilot."

"No, I wouldn't. I get airsick reading about it."

Junie and Pip had claimed the job of inventory. Everything that came off the boat got logged in Pip's careful block letters on a sheet of tarpaper: *bolts (34), rail sections (6), engine (1, heavy), hope (unmeasured).* Junie had added that last one. Nobody crossed it out.

The dock kids from Halfway Bend were a hundred miles behind them now, but these two had made themselves into something new on the water—deckhands, lookouts, and in Pip's case, a self-appointed morale officer who narrated their progress like a radio announcer. *"Day three of the great dismantling,"* he announced to the marsh at large. *"The crew grows weary but unbowed. The cook has made rice again. The cook always makes rice."*

"The cook," Bess said, without turning from her fire, "has made rice because rice is what there is."

* * *

They hauled the first section up the trail that afternoon, and Ines learned what the ridge intended to charge them.

The trail was Elias's handwriting in dirt: switchbacks cut into the escarpment's shoulder, washed to ribbons in places, in others choked with deadfall that the storm had stacked like kindling for a giant's fire. They'd built a sledge from two rail sections and a door Tam had pried off the wheelhouse—*she'll forgive me,* he'd said, and patted the hull like an apology—and they took turns on the tow ropes, four abreast, leaning into the grade.

The first switchback was fine. The second was fine. The third had a slide across it, exactly where Elias's note said it would be, a fan of red clay and shattered rock as slick as a licked thumb. Tam went first, testing each foothold, and called back the line. Ines came second with the tow rope over her shoulder, and halfway across the slide her right boot punched through the crust into clay the consistency of cold porridge, and the rope went taut, and the world tilted.

She didn't fall. She did the thing her body had learned five years ago in a cockpit that was coming apart around her: she stopped being a person and became a set of correct responses. Weight low. Rope braced. Foot out slow, don't yank, the clay will keep you longer than you want to stay. Pip, behind her, made a small sound, and she heard herself say, "I'm fine. Watch where I step and don't step there," and her voice came out level as a spirit gauge, and she hated how easy it was. How practiced. Some part of her had stayed in that crash and never left, and it did all her hardest work for her.

They got the sledge across. On the far side, Tam looked at her a beat too long—the look of a man who'd watched her go somewhere and come back—and said nothing, which was its own kind of conversation now. They'd had the big one, back at the trading post, the night she'd told him about the Vane crash and her sister and the blame she'd been hauling like ballast. Since then they'd developed this: he noticed, and he let her have it, and she noticed him noticing, and somehow that was more intimate than talking.

The second section went up in the last light, and that was when Junie slipped.

Not badly. A root, a skid, six feet of slope, a catch on a sapling. By any honest accounting it was nothing—Ines had seen men die falling out of chairs. But Junie came to a stop against the sapling and stayed there, breathing, and Ines was down the slope before she'd decided to move, hands on the girl's shoulders, checking her over with a thoroughness that would have embarrassed a field medic.

"I'm okay," Junie said. "Ines. I'm okay."

"You're muddy."

"I'm muddy," Junie agreed, and grinned, and there was clay on her teeth and her braid had come half undone and she was thirteen years old and alive and laughing, and something in Ines's chest went off like a flare.

She helped the girl up the rest of the slope with a grip she couldn't make herself loosen. At the top, Bess took one look at her and said, "You went white as a gull's egg," and Ines said, "No, I didn't," and Bess said, "You did," and that was the whole argument.

* * *

They camped that night on a shelf of rock halfway up the ridge, the Magpie's sections lashed under tarps below them, the marsh country spread out behind like a drowned world. The monsoon came back around midnight and they sat under the tarps and listened to it work.

Pip had taken a rope burn across his palm on the second haul. Bess's shoulder had seized up—she wouldn't say from what, but Ines had seen her catch the sledge when it slid, one-armed, a save that would have cost a younger woman. Tam had a gash over his eyebrow from a recoil of the tow rope, and Junie had scraped both knees raw, and Ines's hands were blistered in four places and her right shoulder had a deep bruise blooming where the rope had caught her.

"We're a wreck," Bess observed, passing around the rice.

"We're a crew," Tam said.

"There's a difference?"

"A wreck can't be fixed."

Pip held up his bandaged hand. "I'm collecting injuries," he announced. "By the time we get to Kestrel's Landing I'm going to have a scar for every day. I'm going to be the most scarred man on the river."

"You're the most *talkative* man on the river," Bess said. "The scars have to catch up."

Ines sat at the edge of the tarp with her shoulder against a boulder and let the talk wash over her. This was the thing she kept discovering and kept being surprised by: the noise didn't cost her anything. For five years, other people's voices had been weather to be flown around—pleasant at a distance, dangerous up close, always a reminder of what she'd lost and what she'd walked away from. But Pip's voice listing his hypothetical scars, and Junie's laugh, and Bess's grumbling, and Tam's low answers—it landed on her like rain on a roof. It didn't get in. It just made the inside feel like inside.

She caught herself doing the arithmetic she'd sworn off. Five days to the Landing. Six until the pass closed. And then, unbidden, the other column: Junie would be fourteen in the spring. Pip would outgrow his boots by Christmas. Bess made rice on Sundays. Tam hummed when he worked and denied it.

*Stop,* she told herself. *You know what this is. You know what it costs.*

Because she did know. She'd done this once before, in a way—with her sister, with a crew, with a life where people knew what she took with her sugar and what she was afraid of. And she'd learned what the bill came to. It came to a cockpit and a mountain and a name she couldn't say in her own head. Every person you let aboard was a person the sky could take from you. She'd paid that fare once. She'd sworn she'd never—

"Ines." Junie had come over and sat down next to her, close, the way kids do when they've decided you're theirs. "Your shoulder's hurt."

"It's a bruise."

"It's a big bruise." Junie dug in the pocket of her coat and produced a rag and a smear of something foul-smelling and green. "Bess's liniment. She gave it to me for my knees but my knees are fine now, so."

"I don't need—"

"You do." Junie took Ines's arm with the imperiousness of a girl who had lost a mother and had decided, somewhere on the river, who she was going to practice being fierce on. She worked the liniment into the bruise with both thumbs, frowning, concentrating, and it hurt like sin and smelled like a swamp's armpit, and Ines sat perfectly still and let a thirteen-year-old take care of her.

"There," Junie said, satisfied. "Now it'll heal."

"It smells like it'll kill me first."

"That's how you know it's working. Bess says so."

And Ines looked at the girl's face in the tarp-light—mud still in the creases of her ears, the rope burn on her own palms, the absolute unguarded certainty that Ines Vane was a person who could be counted on—and felt the truth arrive the way weather arrives, all at once and from everywhere: *I would die for this child.* For this child, and for the boy narrating his scars, and for the cook, and for the engineer. She would stand between them and the escarpment itself and she knew, with the same flat certainty she read water with, that she would not calculate the odds first.

It should have felt like strength. It felt like a debt.

That was the part she hated—the cost. Not the dying; dying was easy, she'd been half-ready for it for five years, had flown like a woman with nothing in her pockets. It was the *keeping*. To die for someone was a single decision. To stay for them was a thousand, made daily, in weather, in boredom, in the long flat stretches where love was just showing up. She had spent five years making sure no one could collect on her. And here she was, halfway up a mountain, holding a boat in pieces on her back, already collected.

"You're doing the face," Junie said.

"What face?"

"The face where you're somewhere else. Tam does it too." Junie packed the liniment away. "Pip says it's because grown-ups are thinking about money. I think it's because you're thinking about the crash."

Ines went still. "Tam told you about—"

"Nobody told me. I listen." Junie shrugged, matter-of-fact, devastating. "You fly like somebody who already crashed. I've watched you. You fly like you're checking every cloud for the one that got you." She stood, brushing off her knees. "Anyway. Your shoulder's fixed. Come eat before Pip tells the story about the eel again."

She went. And Ines sat alone under the tarp with the rain hammering the escarpment and let herself do the one thing she'd refused to do since the mountain, since her sister's name, since the last page of the accident report: she let herself want to arrive. Not to deliver. Not to keep a promise to a dead man. To *arrive*—with these people, at that place, and then to still be there the day after, and the day after that.

It felt like stepping off a cliff. It felt, also, exactly like flying.

* * *

They reached the top of the ridge at noon the next day, and the monsoon, in a fit of theatrical mercy, opened a seam of light over the far side.

The view stopped them all. Below, the country fell away in folds of green to a river they could barely see, silver-threaded, running high and fast toward Kestrel's Landing. The escarpment's face was striped with waterfalls that hadn't existed a week ago—dozens of them, white ropes thrown down the rock. The air after the rain was so clean it hurt to breathe.

"Look at it," Pip breathed. "It's like the world's brand new."

"It's not," Tam said. "It's just washed. There's a difference." He said it lightly, but Ines heard the other thing in it, the thing they'd all learned on this ridge: washed wasn't fixed. The flood down there was still taking houses. The Landing was still drowning. Elias was still dying at Halfway Bend, if he wasn't dead already, and the radio at the last relay post had carried nothing for two days.

They reassembled the Magpie in the river shallows below the trail, working through the afternoon and into the dark, Tam's lantern swinging, everyone's hands cut and shaking. The engine caught on the third try and the sound went through Ines like a heartbeat through a wall. Junie cheered. Pip announced their arrival in the new world. Bess, already thinking about dinner, said, "Somebody catch that rope before it takes Pip's other hand."

And it was as they pushed off—wet, bleeding, laughing, the whole battered crew of them aboard a boat they had carried over a mountain—that Ines saw it.

Downriver, where the silver thread of the water bent around the last shoulder of the escarpment, there was a light. Not a campfire's orange. Not lightning's white. A work light, cold and steady, moving along the bank in the deliberate way of people searching for something.

Tam saw it too. His hand went still on the tiller.

"That's the cache trail," he said quietly. "That's the approach to Elias's cache."

The light moved. Then a second one joined it.

"Vance," Ines said.

The river carried them toward it, and there was nothing on the water fast enough to change that.
