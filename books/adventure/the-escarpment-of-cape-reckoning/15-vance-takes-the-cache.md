## 15. Vance Takes the Cache

Ines smelled the camp before she saw it—diesel and cut timber and woodsmoke burning too wet to hide. She held up a fist and the Magpie's engine dropped to an idle, Tam easing the throttle back without being told. They'd been reading each other like that for days now, two people speaking a language made mostly of hands.

The cache sat in a fold of the escarpment where Elias's map said it would: a shelf of granite above a drowned creek, screened by ironwood, with a ramp of split logs running down to the water. Except the ramp wasn't empty. A barge was nosed against it, riding low, and men were moving between the treeline and the deck in a steady file, carrying crates.

"Vance," Bess said, stating it the way you'd state weather.

"How long have they been at it?" Ines asked.

Tam squinted through the drizzle. "Judging by how much is already on that barge? Since yesterday. Maybe longer."

Junie appeared at Ines's elbow, Pip behind her, both of them wearing the particular silence of children who understood that adults had stopped performing safety. "Is that them," Junie said. Not a question.

"That's them." Ines crouched to their level. "You two stay below. Hatch shut. This isn't a watching kind of thing."

"It's always a watching kind of thing," Pip said, but he went, dragging Junie with him, and Ines heard the hatch dog down behind them.

She counted what she could see from the water. Eight figures, maybe nine. Two rifles slung but not shouldered—Corliss Vance didn't waste posture on threats she hadn't decided to make. The crates coming out of the treeline were Elias's, no question; she recognized the stenciling from his marginal notes, the way he'd labeled everything twice, once for himself and once for Mara. *For when she asks why.* That was the note on half these boxes, if the man had been consistent, and Ines was beginning to understand he had been consistent about exactly one thing his whole life.

"He wrote those letters to her," she said quietly. "Every crate. He labeled them."

Bess looked at her. "And Vance's people are throwing them on a barge like scrap."

"We don't know that yet."

"Ines."

"I know." She turned to Tam. "How much fuel?"

"Enough to get there. Not enough to get there and back and then run the last pass hard. Pick one."

"Then we pick getting there." She stood. "Bring us alongside slow. I'll talk first."

"You always talk first," Tam said.

"Because I'm better at it."

"That's not true," Bess said. "But go on."

* * *

The ramp was slick with rain and the barge's wake slapped against the pilings in a tired rhythm as the Magpie came alongside. Ines stepped across the gap alone, hands visible, and let the nearest man—a big one with a rope scar across his knuckles—see her do it.

"I'm looking for Corliss Vance."

"She's looking for you too," the man said, and there was something in his voice Ines couldn't place until later, when she'd had time to turn it over: not menace. Relief.

They walked her up the ramp past the file of carriers. One of them stumbled under a crate and a woman behind him caught the corner without breaking stride, and Ines saw the stencil through the rain-blurred slats: *FOR WHEN SHE ASKS WHY.* Her chest did something complicated and she put it away where she kept things.

Corliss Vance stood at the top of the ramp under a tarp strung between two ironwoods, and up close she was smaller than her reputation, which Ines suspected was by design. Gray-shot hair cut short enough to be practical, hands folded, eyes doing arithmetic. Ines knew that look. It was her own look, five years ago, before she'd learned there were sums that never came out even.

"Ines Vane," Vance said.

"You have me at a disadvantage."

"No, I don't. Halfway Bend talks. A bush pilot, a river engineer, a cook, and two dock kids walk onto a boat. Everyone on this river knows your manifest better than you do." She unfolded her hands. "You've come about the cache."

"I've come about a promise."

"To a dying man you met once."

"Once is enough for some things."

Vance studied her, and Ines watched her decide something—not whether to fight, but whether fighting was worth the fuel. "You think I'm robbing graves," Vance said. "I'm not. There's nothing in this cache worth what it cost to reach except one thing, and it happens to be worth more than every settlement on this river combined. Marsh found a survey vault. Pre-flood. Title records, boundary plats, mineral claims—the legal spine of this whole escarpment, sitting in a tin box while the water rises. Whoever holds those papers holds the Landing's future. Or its throat." She paused. "I'm not going to hold anyone's throat, Ms. Vane. But I'm not leaving those documents to rot or to flood or to whatever sentimental use you've imagined. Some things are bigger than a promise to a dead prospector."

"Elias isn't dead yet."

Something moved behind Vance's eyes. "No," she said carefully. "I suppose he isn't."

And Ines heard it—the small wrongness, the half-second too long—and filed it next to the rope-scarred man's relief, and understood that she was standing in front of a crew that was coming apart at the seams.

"The medicine," Ines said. "The crates marked for Kestrel's Landing. Those aren't documents. Those are penicillin and sutures and a child's fever kit, and they're going on my boat."

"They're going wherever they're needed most efficiently."

"There's nothing efficient about you," Ines said. "You've spent three days hauling paper up a mountain in the rain."

Vance almost smiled. Almost. "Here's my offer, since you crossed a flooded delta to make one. Take the medicine. Take the letters, take the whole sentimental inventory—I don't care. The vault contents come with me. You'll never open that box anyway; it needs cutting equipment you don't have and time you have less of. Everybody walks away. Nobody drowns over this."

It was a good offer. That was the trap of it. Ines stood in the rain and felt the old arithmetic start up—miles against hours, fuel against current, the cold clean math that had kept her alive and alone for five years—and for the first time she noticed how much the math sounded like Corliss Vance.

"Who else knows about the vault?" Ines asked.

"Why?"

"Because someone on your crew has been talking. Someone left Halfway Bend the day after we did, and they didn't learn about the vault from me."

Vance's stillness changed quality. It went from composure to vigilance, and Ines knew she'd guessed right.

"That would be an internal matter," Vance said.

"Your internal matter just offered me a truce. Mine is offering you a warning. Somebody sold you out, Ms. Vance, and whoever buys information sells it. Ask yourself who else has been shopping."

For a moment neither of them moved, and the rain filled the silence, drumming the tarp, ticking off seconds none of them could spare.

Then the shot cracked from the treeline.

* * *

It went high—deliberately high, Ines realized a heartbeat later, a warning round, which meant the shooter wanted attention more than blood. The carriers scattered. Rope-scar grabbed Ines's arm and hauled her behind a stack of crates, and she let him because arguing took longer than ducking.

A second figure came down out of the trees with a rifle held loose and wrong, the way civilians hold rifles, and Ines recognized him before Vance did: the defector. Hollis. The man Bess had bought a truce from at the trading post with coffee and patience and the simple observation that a crew that strips a flood town and leaves it to drown will eventually leave him too.

"Hollis," Vance said, stepping out from cover with a calm that Ines recognized as expensive. "Put it down."

"You first," Hollis said. His voice shook. "You tell them—" he gestured at the carriers, at the barge, at all of it— "you tell them we're taking the papers and going south. Selling to the land company. You told me it was salvage. Salvage doesn't have a buyer waiting in Port Meridian."

"Lower the rifle, son."

"I'm forty-two years old."

"Lower it anyway." Vance took a step forward, unhurried, and Ines saw the crew's eyes tracking her, saw how much authority lived in that unhurried walk. "You want to know what happens after? Fine. After, the land company pays out, and everyone on this boat gets a share that keeps them fed for ten years. Nobody drowns. Nobody starves. The Landing was dying before any of us ever touched it—that's not ours to carry."

"That's a lie you tell so you can sleep," Hollis said. "I've been sleeping fine. That's the problem."

He lowered the rifle anyway. Ines watched him do it—watched him set it down in the mud, deliberate, like laying down an argument—and then he walked past Vance, past the crates, and stood beside Ines behind the stack.

"I'm done," he said, to no one in particular. "I'm going with them."

Vance looked at Ines over the rain. And here was the thing Ines remembered afterward, the thing she'd turn over on the portage and at the pass and for a year after: Vance didn't look angry. She looked like a woman checking a ledger and finding, at the bottom, a number she'd known was coming.

"Take the medicine," Vance said. "Take the letters. Keep the girl's fever kit dry—it's the only thing in this whole cache I'd have fought you for." She turned to her crew. "Load out. We're done here."

"You can't take the vault," Ines said.

"I can and I will, and you know why?" Vance was already walking. "Because you need the ramp more than I need to be liked. Because every hour you spend arguing is an hour the last pass closes a little tighter. And because somewhere in that tin box is a plat that says who owns the ground your friends are standing on, and I haven't decided yet what a person like you does with a thing like that." She paused at the treeline, half-turned. "You measure everything, Ms. Vane. Miles, hours, promises. So measure this: I'm twenty minutes ahead of you and going downhill with the current. Do the sum."

Then she was gone into the ironwoods, and her crew went with her, all but Hollis, who stood dripping in the mud like a man just set down from a long carry.

* * *

They loaded fast. Medicine first, then the letters—forty-odd crates of them, Elias's careful double-stenciled handwriting, *for when she asks why*, stacked in the Magpie's hold under tarps with Bess directing traffic like a harbor master born late to the job. Junie and Pip emerged the moment the shooting was safely historical and carried light boxes with the gravity of acolytes. Hollis worked silently among them, and nobody thanked him, and nobody told him to leave, and Ines understood that both of those silences were Bess's doing.

Tam found her at the ramp, staring downriver.

"Talk to me," he said.

"She's right about the math." Ines's voice came out flat. "Twenty minutes' head start, downstream, and we still have to run Widow's Tooth again at higher water. Even if we catch her, we can't take the vault back. We don't have the guns and I wouldn't use them if we did."

"So we lose."

"We lose the vault." She turned. "We don't lose the Landing. She thinks the papers are the prize because she thinks power is the prize. But Elias labeled forty crates *for when she asks why* and not one of them says *for when she wants leverage*. Whatever's in that vault, the way to use it isn't the way she thinks."

Tam looked at her a moment too long. "That's the first hopeful thing you've said since Halfway Bend."

"Don't get used to it."

Too late. She heard the explosion before she finished turning.

It wasn't loud the way thunder is loud. It was a flat, wet crack, and the ramp jumped—actually jumped, pilings and split-log decking leaping a foot and settling crooked, and then the whole structure groaned and slid sideways into the drowned creek with a sound like a jaw coming unhinged. The shockwave shoved the Magpie against the bank; Tam caught the rail and swore with real feeling.

Behind the treeline, small and receding, an engine turned over and pulled away.

"She blew the ramp," Ines said. Stating it, the way you state weather.

"Charges must've been set last night," Tam said grimly, already moving along the rail, assessing. "While we were slogging through the marsh. She planned the retreat before we ever arrived." He looked back at her, and the rain ran off his chin. "Ines. That ramp was our road home. There's no other launch point above the gorge. If we can't rebuild it—"

"How long to rebuild it?"

"With eight hands and salvaged pilings? A day. Maybe two."

"And the last pass closes in three."

Neither of them said anything. Downriver, gray water moved with terrible patience toward the escarpment, carrying away another minute, another mile, another piece of the margin they'd been spending like it was endless. Ines thought of Elias's marginal notes, his patient hand, *the river takes the interest, always plan for the interest*—and understood, finally, what the old man had been trying to teach her from beyond the reach of teaching.

Time wasn't fuel. Fuel you could buy. Time you could only give.

"Bess!" she called. "How are you with carpentry?"

"I'm from a family of preachers," Bess said, rolling up her sleeves. "I'm excellent at raising things."

Ines almost laughed. Then she picked up the first piling, and the work began.
