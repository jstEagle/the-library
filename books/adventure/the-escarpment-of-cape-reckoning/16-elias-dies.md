## 16. Elias Dies

The rain had a rhythm that night, and Ines had learned to sleep inside it—let it drum the Magpie's cabin roof like fingers on a door she didn't have to answer. She was dreaming of nothing in particular, which for her was a luxury, when Tam's hand closed on her shoulder.

"Radio," he said. "Relay station at Two Rivers is holding a message."

She came awake the way she always did—all at once, no soft edges—and followed him through the boat's narrow corridor to the wheelhouse, where the radio hissed and spat under Bess's careful hand. The signal was bad. It came through in pieces, a voice from Halfway Bend relayed twice over, stripped of everything but its bones.

"—Marsh, Elias, prospector, passed this evening—"

Ines stood very still.

"—asked before the end—" static swallowed a sentence whole. "—whether his promise was kept. Whether the girl made it upriver. That's what he wanted to know. The nurse says he asked three times in the last hour."

Bess turned down the gain and looked at Ines with an expression Ines couldn't bear to look at directly, so she looked at the rain on the wheelhouse glass instead, running in long silver threads.

"Say again the last part," Ines said into the handset. Her voice sounded like someone else's.

"Message ends: *Tell her he wasn't afraid. Tell her he went easy.* Over."

"Copy," Ines said, because there was nothing else to say. "Vane out."

She set the handset down in its cradle with exaggerated care, the way you set down something breakable when your hands aren't steady, and then she walked out of the wheelhouse into the rain without deciding to.

* * *

She stood on the aft deck and let the monsoon work her over. It wasn't cold—the interior never really got cold—but it was thorough, the way grief is thorough, finding every seam. She thought about how stupid it was. She'd known the man for one afternoon. One feverish afternoon in a trapper's cabin that smelled of kerosene and wet fur, where he'd lain propped against a roll of bedding with his lungs rattling like gravel in a pan, and traded her a map drawn in pencil on the back of a flour sack for a promise she hadn't wanted to make.

One afternoon. And here she was, soaked to the skin, crying like the man had been hers.

*He wasn't yours,* she told herself. *You don't get to do this.*

But the tears kept coming, and eventually she stopped arguing with them, because she understood—with the flat clarity that sometimes arrives in the middle of storms—that the argument itself was the old habit. The old arithmetic. Grief has to be earned, Ines. You have to have put in the years. You have to have the receipts. Five years of blood kin, or a decade of shared meals, or some minimum balance of history before you're allowed to stand in the rain and weep for a man.

Elias Marsh had spent maybe four hours conscious in her company. He had looked at her across a dying fire of a life and seen exactly what she was—a woman running—and instead of judging her, or pitying her, or trying to fix her, he had simply handed her the thing he loved most in the world and said: *I trust you with it. Go.*

Nobody had trusted her with anything since the crash. Since the inquiry. Since her sister's name got carved into a hillside and everyone who used to call her friend started looking at her like she was made of the same weather that killed Mara's—no. Not Mara. Her sister. *Ruth.* Ruth Vane, copilot, thirty-one years old, who died with her hands on the yoke beside her sister's hands, and whose death Ines had been carrying ever since like cargo she couldn't log, couldn't declare, couldn't set down anywhere legal.

And now Elias was dead too, and the strange truth of it was that his dying felt less like a loss than like a hand being withdrawn from hers—a hand she hadn't known was holding on.

"You'll catch your death," Bess said from the doorway, which was a thing Bess said, and which meant none of those words.

"He knew," Ines said. Rain ran off her chin. "At the end. He knew I hadn't come yet, and he still—he asked if the promise was kept. Not whether I'd failed. Whether it was kept." She wiped her face with a palm that did nothing. "Like he already knew the difference between me and my promise. Like the promise was a separate living thing and I was just the courier."

Bess came out onto the deck, unhurried, and stood beside her in the rain without an umbrella, because Bess had never once in Ines's presence acknowledged that rain was an inconvenience rather than a fact.

"My husband died eleven years back," Bess said. "Heart, quick, in the middle of mending a net. And you know what I learned? Grief doesn't check papers. It doesn't ask how long you knew somebody. It just asks whether they saw you. Whether anybody, anywhere, ever saw you plain and sent you off with something to carry." She nodded at the dark water sliding past the hull. "That man saw you, Ines. Four hours or forty years, it's the same arithmetic. You were seen. Now you've got weight to carry. That's all grief is. Weight from being seen."

Ines laughed—a broken, surprised sound. "You make it sound almost worth it."

"It isn't," Bess said comfortably. "But it's not nothing, either. Come inside before you rattle apart. There's tea."

* * *

They held the wake the only way a riverboat holds a wake: by working.

Tam took the news hardest after Ines, though he tried to hide it behind the engine, and hid it badly, because Tam Oduya could rebuild a carburetor blindfolded but could not lie about his face. He'd met Elias once, years ago, buying survey brass at a trading post—had spent an afternoon arguing topography with him over warm beer—and had liked him instantly, the way you like men who are exactly what they appear to be, which in the interior was rarer than gold.

"He drew that map knowing he wouldn't live to see it used," Tam said, elbow-deep in the engine housing, grease to the shoulder. "You understand that? He wasn't leaving instructions. He was leaving a letter. A letter shaped like a route."

"I know," Ines said.

"And we're going to deliver it."

"We're going to deliver it."

Junie appeared in the engine room doorway with Pip behind her, both of them damp and solemn in the way children are solemn when adults are sad and they haven't been given permission to be anything else. Junie had taken to carrying Elias's marginal notes folded in her shirt pocket, against her chest, like a talisman. She'd appointed herself their keeper somewhere in the marsh country, and nobody had argued.

"Is he really gone?" Pip asked.

"Yes," Ines said. She crouched so she was eye level with him. "He is."

Pip considered this with the terrible directness of eight-year-olds. "Did it hurt?"

"No," Ines said, and found that she believed it, because the nurse had said he went easy, and because she needed it to be true. "He was tired, and then he wasn't anymore. That's how it goes, sometimes, when it goes kind."

Junie didn't ask anything. She just crossed the engine room and put her arms around Ines's neck and held on, and Ines—who had spent five years making sure no one touched her without warning, who flinched from handshakes, who kept every human being at a wingspan's distance—held on back. Held on until the girl let go, which took a while, and which Ines did not rush.

*We're going to lose her,* Ines thought, with a clarity that felt like falling. *When this trip ends, we're going to dock at Kestrel's Landing and these kids will go back to whatever passes for their lives, and I'll fly out, and this will be over, and I will spend the rest of mine measuring the distance from it.*

She filed the thought away the way she filed all unbearable thoughts—in the compartment marked LATER—and went to make the tea herself, because Bess had fallen asleep sitting up in the galley, and someone had to.

* * *

Near midnight, unable to sleep, Ines sat in the wheelhouse with the flour-sack map spread on the chart table and read Elias's marginal notes by lamplight. She'd read them before. Everyone had; they'd become scripture on this boat, read aloud in the marsh country while the river rose around them. But tonight they read differently, the way a letter reads differently after the writer dies—every ordinary line suddenly freighted.

*Cache sits above the high-water mark of '09. If the flood's worse than '09, climb higher and curse my name.*

*The medicine is mostly penicillin and quinine. The quinine's for me, probably, if I'm honest. Take it anyway.*

*Mara will argue with you. Let her win one argument. Just one. It builds character, hers and yours.*

Ines stopped on that line. Read it again. *Mara will argue with you.* He'd written it as if he knew—knew that whoever carried his map would meet his granddaughter, would clash with her, would need permission to love her. As if he'd written the notes not for a courier but for a successor. For family he was recruiting by mail.

There was one more note, in the corner, smaller than the others, half worn away by folding:

*Ruth Vane flew me out of the Splits in '19 with a cracked manifold and no instruments. Best stick I ever rode behind. If her sister's reading this—I know whose hands are on this map. Fly gentle, girl. The sky forgives more than you think.*

Ines sat back. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, the river moved against the hull with the patience of something that intended to outlast everything.

He'd known. All along, from the first hour in the trapper's cabin—maybe before, maybe the moment her plane broke through the cloud cover over his clearing—he'd known whose sister she was. And he'd never said a word. Had simply handed her the map and the promise and the granddaughter and the whole impossible errand of his love, trusting her with all of it, the way Ruth had once trusted him with a failing engine and both their lives.

*Fly gentle, girl. The sky forgives more than you think.*

She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes until the colors bloomed, and let the words sit in her chest where they hurt the most, which was also, she was beginning to suspect, where they worked.

* * *

Dawn came gray and mean, the monsoon still working itself into a fury upstream. They gathered in the wheelhouse—not a ceremony, because none of them were churchgoing, but something adjacent to one. Bess poured four cups of coffee and set a fifth, full, on the chart table beside the map, and nobody explained why, and everybody understood.

Tam spoke first. "I met him once. Argued with him about the height of the escarpment passes for three hours. He was right. I've hated him moderately ever since." A pause. "Best man I ever argued with."

"He fed us," Pip said. "That time at the trading post. He gave us dried fish and told us lies about a giant catfish."

"The catfish was real," Junie said loyally.

"The catfish was real," Ines agreed, and heard her own voice crack on the second word, and let it.

Bess said grace in two sentences, most of which was about the weather. Then she looked at Ines, and everyone else looked at Ines, and Ines understood that the fifth cup of coffee was hers to account for.

"I barely knew him," she said. "Four hours, maybe five. He was dying and I was running and he caught me anyway—caught me with a promise, which is the one trap I can't fly out of." She looked at the steam rising off the cup she'd never pour out. "I keep waiting for the grief to feel wrong. To feel like it belongs to somebody else—to his daughter, dead now too, God help us, or to Mara, or to people who actually earned it. But it doesn't feel wrong. It feels like—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "It feels like he left it to me. On purpose. Like everything else."

Nobody spoke. The rain filled the silence, and the river filled the rain.

"So that's what I'm doing with it," Ines said. "Carrying it the rest of the way upriver. Him and the map and the medicine and the letters and all of it. Every mile."

*Not miles,* she corrected herself, and the correction didn't even sting anymore. *People. He's people now. He's ours.*

* * *

They cast off within the hour. There was no reason to linger and every reason not to—the last pass above Kestrel's Landing was due to close, and every report off the relay said the monsoon was peaking early and hard, the water coming down the escarpment like a verdict.

Tam had the wheel. Ines stood at the glass watching the brown water climb the banks, and Bess came and stood beside her, and for a while neither of them said anything, which was its own kind of prayer.

Then the radio crackled. Bess leaned in, tuned it, and her face changed.

"Ines. Relay at Two Rivers. It's Kestrel's Landing—they've got a transmitter working again, short range, bouncing off the repeater." She listened harder, and her knuckles whitened on the cabinet edge. "It's bad. The flood quarter's gone under since yesterday. And there's a girl missing."

Ines turned. "Name."

"They didn't say a name. They said—" Bess closed her eyes, listening through the static, lips moving. "They said *the prospector's granddaughter.* They said she went out looking for medicine two nights ago and hasn't come back. They said the water took the low quarter and she's out there somewhere in it."

Ines was already moving—already reaching for the chart, for the pencil, for the numbers, hours and current and distance, the old arithmetic rising in her like a tide. But halfway through the calculation she caught herself, and stopped, and put her hand flat on the map instead, on the flour sack, on the dead man's careful lines.

"Hold on, old man," she said, to no one, to him. "Hold on, Mara. We're coming."

"Tam!" she called down into the engine room, and her voice carried the kind of authority that doesn't come from rank. "Everything she's got. Full ahead. The last pass closes in two days, and we're going to be on the far side of it when it does."

Above them the rain thickened, and the river rose, and the Magpie drove north into the teeth of the monsoon like something with a promise to keep.
