## 17. The Last Pass

The radio crackled at dawn with the voice of a relay operator two hundred miles south, and the news was simple: the escarpment passes would seal within seventy-two hours. After that, nothing moved through—not boats, not planes, not prayers.

Ines stood at the Magpie's wheel with the message slip in her hand, watching the water change color ahead of them. It had gone the brown of strong tea, thick with runoff, carrying whole trees past them like matchsticks. The river was no longer a river. It was an argument the mountains were having with the lowlands, and they were caught in the middle of it.

"Seventy-two hours," she said.

Tam came up from the engine compartment wiping his hands on a rag that had been white once. He read the slip over her shoulder without touching her, which was its own kind of intimacy after three weeks on this boat. "That's if the forecast's honest," he said. "Forecasts out here are rumors wearing uniforms."

"Then we treat it like gospel anyway."

He nodded and went back below. That was the thing about Tam she'd come to trust most—he didn't waste breath on what couldn't be changed. He just went and put his hands on the problem until the problem gave up or he did.

Bess appeared from the galley with four mugs of something hot and terrible and handed them around. Junie took hers with both hands, hunched against the spray coming over the bow rail. Pip was already up in the crow's nest they'd rigged from a cargo boom, because Pip was eleven years old and made of rubber bands and nerve.

"How far?" Junie asked.

Ines did the arithmetic without wanting to. She'd spent five years doing this math—hours to fuel burn, miles to daylight, distance to the nearest strip—and the numbers came to her the way other people's mother tongues came to them. "Two days' running if the water cooperates. Three if it doesn't."

"And the pass closes in—"

"Less than that. If we're going to make it, we make it ugly."

Junie looked at her, and Ines saw the girl weighing that the way adults weighed things now, because the flood had aged all of them in dog years. "Ugly how?"

Ines drank the terrible coffee. "You'll know it when you see it."

* * *

The water turned against them by midmorning.

It came down from the escarpment in braided channels that hadn't existed on Elias Marsh's map, or maybe had existed but under different names, because a flood rewrites geography the way grief rewrites memory. Tam stood beside her at the wheel with his own copy of the map, Elias's marginal notes bleeding where rain had gotten at them, and called out the channel markers from memory of a run he'd made fifteen years ago in a boat half this size.

"Sandbar should be off the left bank," he said. "There." It wasn't a sandbar anymore; it was a brown smear just under the surface, moving. "Log jam past it. We thread between the second and third snag."

"You've done this before."

"I've done this before when I was young enough to think I was immortal." He glanced at her. "You're the one who reads water. I'm the one who keeps us floating. Division of labor."

She threaded it. The Magpie shuddered as the current grabbed her stern and tried to swing her broadside into the jam, and Ines gave her more throttle than she liked and felt the old boat answer—a groan deep in the hull, a stutter in the engine note that shouldn't have been there.

Tam heard it too. His head came up like a bird dog's.

"That's not the water," he said.

"No," Ines said. "It isn't."

* * *

The engine died for the first time at noon, forty minutes above Widow's Tooth, in a stretch of channel where the banks were close enough to touch from either side of the boat and the current ran like a slidewalk in reverse.

They drifted. That was the terror of it—the utter passivity, the land sliding backward while they went nowhere. Ines had spent her whole adult life in machines where forward motion was the proof of life, and drifting felt like dying in installments.

Below, Tam swore with a fluency that would have made Bess blush, and Bess, who was not easily impressed, said, "Language, dear," and kept bailing.

The restart took nine minutes. Ines counted every one of them, because counting was what she did instead of screaming. When the engine coughed back to life, the cheer from the deck was short-lived, because everyone aboard understood what a first failure meant: there would be a second, and a third, and eventually one that didn't answer.

Tam surfaced through the engine hatch, gray-faced, shaking his head. "Fuel line's picking up sediment. The river's in the tank somehow—bad gasket, bad luck, take your pick. I can keep filtering, but every hour we run, I'm buying us minutes with hours."

"Then spend them," Ines said.

"Ines." He wiped his face and left a black streak across it, and for a moment he looked like every engineer who had ever died politely in a machinery space while the passengers upstairs argued about turning back. "If we push through and she dies for good mid-channel, we're cargo. This river doesn't do rescues during monsoon. You know that better than anyone."

She knew it. She knew it in her teeth, in the scar tissue of five years ago, in the specific way she knew the sound a plane makes when it stops being a plane.

"There's a backwater two miles up," she said. "Left side, behind a gravel point. We could anchor, wait out the worst of the rise, run the last leg when the water drops."

"When the water drops," Tam repeated. "And when does it drop?"

"After the pass closes."

The word hung there. Behind them, downstream, everything they'd survived. Ahead, the escarpment, and Kestrel's Landing, and a promise made to a dying man who was probably already dead. And somewhere in between, a window narrowing by the hour.

Bess came up the companionway with her sleeves rolled and her forearms dripping. She'd been listening, obviously. Bess always listened; it was her superpower, along with feeding people who hadn't asked to be fed.

"Well," she said. "I've been on boats fifty years, and I'll tell you what I know. A boat is just a promise with a hull around it. Question is never whether the boat can make it. Question is whether the promise is worth drowning for."

"It's worth it," Junie said.

Everyone turned. The girl was standing at the rail with her mug forgotten, and her chin was up in a way that Ines recognized with a jolt—it was Elias Marsh's chin, from the photograph in his cabin, the stubborn set of it. Mara's mother's chin. Family wrote itself on bodies whether you signed for it or not.

"The Landing has people in it," Junie said. "People waiting. My grandmother waited her whole life for someone to come back through that pass, and nobody did, and she died thinking that was just how the world worked." Her voice cracked and she pushed through it. "So it's worth it. It's obviously worth it."

Nobody argued with her. There was nothing to argue with.

* * *

They anchored in the backwater for one hour, no more, long enough for Tam to strip the fuel system and rig a secondary filter from a coffee tin, a scrap of felt, and hose clamps he'd been hoarding since Halfway Bend like a dragon with a hardware addiction. Ines stood anchor watch in the rain and let herself do the thing she never let herself do: imagine turning back.

It would be easy. That was the obscene part. Downstream lay Halfway Bend, dry bunks, a machine shop, a telegraph office where she could wire for parts and wait out the season telling stories in Bess's cousin's kitchen. Nobody would call her a coward. They'd call her sensible. Sensible was the word people used for survivors, and she had been so tired, for so many years, of surviving.

Her sister had been sensible too, right up until the weather turned. Ines had built a whole religion around the idea that caution was love—that the way you proved you cared about people was by keeping them away from edges. And then she'd flown into weather she should have refused, because her sister was on the manifest and the manifest was a promise, and promises, she'd learned, were debts, and debts meant staying, and staying meant losing—

"Ines." Tam's voice, quiet, at her elbow. "Wherever you just went, come back. I need your eyes on the channel."

She blinked the rain off her lashes. "I'm here."

"You're here," he agreed. "But you were also somewhere else, and I've noticed you go there when the choice gets heavy." He didn't ask what the choice was. He'd known since the day he'd recognized her name on a freight manifest five years stale, and he'd carried that knowledge like ballast ever since, waiting for her to jettison it herself. "For what it's worth—I've run away from exactly one thing in my life, and I've regretted it every single day since, and the regret weighs more than the thing would have."

"What was it?"

"A person," he said. "Not a place. People are heavier than places. That's why running from them works so well and costs so much." He squeezed her shoulder once, engineer's grip, brief and certain, and went back below.

Ines stood at the rail and watched the escarpment rising ahead of them through the rain, gray-green and immense, wearing its waterfalls like medals. Somewhere up there was a pass that would close like a door. Somewhere beyond it was a girl named Mara, feverish, alone, waiting for the world to work the way it was supposed to.

Weighing more than a place, Ines thought. Fine. Then she'd carry it.

* * *

The engine failed for good at 4:17 in the afternoon, twelve miles below the pass, and Ines knew it was for good because of the sound—not the bang or the grind she'd braced for, but a soft mechanical sigh, like something exhaling after holding its breath too long. The Magpie lost headway and began to swing, and the current took her stern the way it always did, patient and enormous, a hand closing.

"Anchor!" Ines shouted, and Pip threw it, bless him, and the hook bit gravel and the boat swung head-on into the current and held, trembling like a living thing.

Tam was already below. Ines followed him down the ladder into the engine compartment, into heat and diesel stink and the sound of the river pressing against the hull from every direction at once. Water was coming in over the shaft log—a thin, insistent tongue of it, ankle-deep and rising.

"Gasket's blown," Tam said. He was already kneeling in the water, sleeves up, hands moving. "I can bypass it. I can keep her alive. But somebody has to stay down here and manage the flow while she runs, and that somebody is going to be standing in rising water next to a live engine for—" he checked his watch, an old habit of men who measured things—"four hours, minimum."

"So we bail," Ines said. "All of us. Buckets."

"Buckets don't fix a gasket. Buckets buy time. What fixes it is me, down here, clamping and re-clamping and jury-rigging while the river tries to unmake my work faster than I make it." He looked up at her, and the lantern light caught him from below, and she thought: he's already decided. He decided before he told me. "You have the wheel. That's not negotiable. Nobody else can read this water."

"Then I'll find another way through—"

"There is no other way through. There's this channel and there's the pass and there's seventy hours, and you know it, because you've already done the math twice. I've watched your lips move."

The water was at their shins now. Ines crouched down so they were eye to eye, and the closeness of the compartment made it feel like a confessional, which maybe it was.

"If you die down here," she said, "I will be so angry at you."

Something moved across his face—amusement, and under it, tenderness, and under that, the same fear she was carrying, held steady by sheer professional discipline. "Then I won't die," he said. "I hate making you angry. It's the only expression you own that scares me."

She laughed. It came out wrong, wet, halfway to the other thing, and she grabbed his forearm—engineer's forearm, scarred and sure—and said, "Four hours. You come up in four hours or I'm coming down after you, and I fly in weather, Tam. Do not test me on this."

"Go," he said. "Fly your boat."

* * *

The last twelve miles took four hours and eleven minutes, and Ines remembered every one of them the way she remembered the crash—frame by frame, sense by sense, the mind's ugly trick of preserving catastrophe in high definition.

She drove the Magpie the way she flew: reading the water three hundred yards ahead, feeling the boat's weight through the wheel like a yoke, listening past the engine noise to the deeper noise beneath it—Tam's muffled hammering, the bilge pump cycling, the river hissing along the hull. Bess bailed. Junie bailed. Pip, forbidden the engine compartment, stood at the bow calling out snags like a junior pilot, his voice cracking and steady both at once, and Ines thought absurdly: this is my crew. When did this become my crew?

At mile seven the water in the compartments reached Tam's waist, and he radioed up on the boat's ancient intercom, voice calm as a weather report: "Still with you. Flow's manageable. Don't slow down."

At mile nine the channel narrowed and the current doubled, and the Magpie's hull groaned like a ship in a story, and Ines gave her everything the dying engine had left and threaded the gap with six inches to spare on either side, and behind her she heard Bess say quietly, "Lord, look at her go," and realized Bess wasn't talking about the boat.

At mile eleven the pass opened before them.

It was less a pass than a wound in the escarpment—a slot of churning brown water between walls of black rock, half a mile of the worst water Ines had ever seen outside of a nightmare, and at its far end, visible through the rain, the wide drowned valley of the Landing, rooftops standing in gray water like gravestones.

"Tam," she said into the intercom. "Last mile. Talk to me."

A pause. Long enough to frighten. Then: "Flow's up. Pump's winning. Barely." Another pause, and when he spoke again his voice had something in it she'd never heard there—not fear, exactly. Certainty, maybe, or its twin. "Ines. Push her through."

She pushed her through.

The pass took twenty minutes and cost them the portside railing, one of the fuel drums, and several years of Ines's life. The Magpie surfed standing waves that lifted her stern and dropped her like a toy, and Ines rode the wheel with her whole body, flying a boat through a canyon of water, and somewhere in the middle of it she stopped being afraid—not because the danger passed, but because she finally understood, with the total clarity that only comes at the edge, that she was not going to turn back. Whatever happened next, she was already through. The woman who measured her life in miles had died somewhere back in that channel, and the woman at this wheel was measuring it in something else entirely.

Then the walls fell away, and the rain thinned, and the valley opened up before them—wide, flooded, silver-gray in the failing light—and the engine, faithful ruined thing, coughed twice and kept running, because Tam Oduya does not lose arguments with rivers.

They came into the drowned outskirts of Kestrel's Landing at dusk, past a church roof and a telephone pole with its wires trailing in the water, and the people were already on the rooftops and the levee crest, dozens of them, small and waving, and Junie was crying openly at the bow and pretending she wasn't, and Bess had her arm around her.

Ines keyed the intercom one last time.

"We're through," she said. "Come up and see it."

There was a long silence below. Then Tam's voice, rough with exhaustion and something warmer: "On my way. Somebody get the kettle on." A pause. "And Ines—next time we do this, I'm installing a bigger pump."

She laughed, really laughed, and brought the Magpie alongside the levee as the first lanterns came out along the crest, and that was when she saw it: a skiff pushing off from the shore, one figure rowing hard against the current, and standing in the shallows beyond it, half-supported by two neighbors, a girl of maybe sixteen, swaying, burning with fever even at this distance.

Mara Marsh. Alive. Barely.

Ines looked at the girl, then at her own wrecked plane waiting somewhere beyond the pass, then at the sky, where a seam of clearing air was opening over the escarpment like a door left unlatched.

"Hold her here," she said to Bess, and reached for the skiff line.
