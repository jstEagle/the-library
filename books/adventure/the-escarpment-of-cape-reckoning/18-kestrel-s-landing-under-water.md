## 18. Kestrel's Landing, Under Water

The escarpment opened like a book they'd been reading by feel for nine days.

One bend of the river, then another, and the walls fell away on both sides at once, and there it was: Kestrel's Landing, spread across the delta where the river gave up its argument with the mountains and went soft and wide toward the coast.

Half of it was gone under brown water.

Ines stood at the Magpie's bow rail with Elias's map in her hands and felt the promise rearrange itself in her chest. She had carried the thing for two thousand miles—folded, refolded, waterproofed in oilcloth, slept with it against her ribs like a second heartbeat—and she had imagined this moment a hundred ways. A town saved. A daughter found. Elias's fevered voice finally quiet in her head: *You'll know it when you see it.*

She saw it. Roofs poking out of the flood like drowned birds. The main street a canal. A church steeple standing in six feet of moving water, its bell green with old weather, ringing nothing. On the high ground east of town, a scatter of tents and tarpaper shacks clung to the slope where maybe two hundred people had dragged what they could carry and were now living on top of each other in the rain.

"Dear God," Bess said quietly. She had her good coat on, the one she'd been saving for arrival, and the rain was already ruining it, and she didn't seem to care.

Junie pressed against Ines's side. Pip stood on a crate to see over the rail, and for once he wasn't talking.

Tam brought them in slow, the Magpie's engine down to a mutter, feeling for the drowned fences and wells that lived just under the surface. He'd been below decks most of the last day keeping the engine alive, and he came up now with grease to his elbows and his shirt stuck to his back, and he looked at the flooded town the way an engineer looks at any failure—with his jaw set, already taking it apart to find out why.

"That's not monsoon water," he said. "That's backed up. Somebody dynamited the old weir upstream."

"The salvage crew?" Ines said.

"Or the land company." He cut the engine and let the current carry them. "Either way, somebody wanted this town underwater fast."

* * *

They tied up at the top of what used to be Front Street, where the water ended and the mud began, and the people came down to meet them before the lines were even made fast.

Ines had expected suspicion. What she got instead was a woman named Odile Ferrant—the settlement's postmistress, though there hadn't been a post in three weeks—who took one look at the oilcloth bundle under Ines's arm and put both hands over her mouth.

"You're Marsh's pilot," she said through her fingers. "Elias sent you."

"He traded me a map," Ines said. "For a promise."

"And you kept it." Odile's eyes went around the boat—to Tam, to Bess, to Junie and Pip dripping on the crate—and something in her face cracked open. "Nine days we've been watching the water come up and waiting to see who'd be fool enough to run it. And it's a bush pilot and a leaky riverboat and a cook." She laughed, and it turned into something else halfway up, and she pressed the back of her hand to her mouth until it passed. "Come up to the camp. There's tea, if you can call it that. And I'll tell you how bad it is."

It was worse than it looked, which Ines would not have believed possible.

The medicine cache—the reason for all of it, the thing Elias had drawn his map toward with a dying man's careful hand—was gone. Not flooded. Gone. Vance's crew had come through eleven days earlier, ahead of the worst water, and stripped the site clean: penicillin, quinine, the surgical kit, the whole of it, loaded onto their barge and hauled back downriver while the Landing's sick lay in the church hall burning with fever.

"They left the shelves," Odile said bitterly. They were standing at the edge of the high-ground camp, looking down at the roofs in the water. "Stacked them neat as a shop display. Like they were doing us a favor by leaving the furniture."

"How many sick?" Tam asked.

"Thirty-one. Two dead since the raid. A third won't see morning." Odile's voice was flat now, wrung out. "We sent a runner to Halfway Bend for help a week ago. Either he didn't make it or nobody cared."

Bess put her arm through Odile's and said nothing, which was, Ines was learning, Bess's particular genius.

Ines stood apart with the map in her hands and did the arithmetic she always did—hours, miles, fuel, distance to Halfway Bend, distance to anywhere—and for once the numbers came up empty. There was no flight to make. Her plane sat broken-backed at Halfway Bend. The passes would close within days. Vance had the medicine and a two-day head start and no intention of giving either back.

Then Junie touched her elbow.

"Ines. Look at the map again. The part Mr. Marsh wrote small."

* * *

They spread the map on a plank table under a tarp, weighted at the corners with stones, and the whole senior population of Kestrel's Landing crowded around it like it might speak.

Elias's route to the cache ran along the margin in his cramped, patient hand. But below the cache mark, below even the little X that had cost a man his life's savings and his last month of strength, there was more. Ines had read it on the river a dozen times and taken it for surveyor's notes. Now, with the drowned town in front of her, she read it aloud.

"*Cache sits above the old claim line. Vault beneath. 1911 survey. Everything the company says we never owned is in the ground under the cache shed. Deeds. Plats. The original filings. Corliss Vance's grandfather signed the denial himself—I've seen the letter.*"

Silence under the tarp. Rain on canvas.

"There's a vault," Odile said slowly. "Under the cache."

"A pre-flood survey vault," Tam said. "They built those into the bedrock back then. Flood-proof. Fireproof. Built to outlast everything except a man stubborn enough to dig for it." He was smiling, and Ines realized she'd never seen him smile like that—not the wary half-smile he rationed out, but the real thing, crooked and sudden. "Your prospector wasn't hiding medicine, Ines. Medicine was the bait. For us, maybe. Or for whoever loved him enough to come."

"The deeds," Odile whispered. She gripped the table edge. "If the original title filings are in that vault—if we can prove the company's grants were fraudulent—then the land grab dies. All of it. Every eviction notice, every 'abandoned property' claim. The whole scheme falls over the moment a judge sees paper older than their charter."

"Which is why they want the town underwater," Ines said. "Drowned towns don't file claims. Drowned towns get declared abandoned."

"Elias knew." Odile's voice broke and reassembled itself. "He knew for thirty years and he kept digging and nobody believed him, and now—" She stopped. Looked at Ines. "Where is the cache? Exactly."

Ines put her finger on the map. "Two miles up the escarpment trail. Above the flood line."

Above the flood line. The words landed in her own chest like a dropped anchor. Everything the water had taken, and Elias had built his vault above it. Of course he had. A man who spent his life reading rivers knew exactly what rivers did.

"When do we leave?" said Pip, from somewhere near her hip.

* * *

They left at first light, or what passed for first light, which was gray rain becoming slightly less gray.

Tam stayed with the Magpie—he wouldn't leave the boat, and someone had to hold the landing—but he stripped the vault tools off her himself: pry bar, sledge, two lanterns, the crowbar he'd been using on the engine. He handed them over wordlessly, then caught Ines's sleeve.

"She'll be there," he said quietly. "Vance stripped the medicine, but if she learned about the vault—"

"Then we're too late again."

"Then you're not." His grip tightened. "Listen to me. You've been measuring this whole trip in what you failed to deliver. That's not how promises work. Elias didn't trade you a delivery. He traded you a direction. Walk it."

She looked at him for a long moment. Five years of measuring life in miles, and here was a man telling her to measure it in steps.

"Keep the kids aboard," she said.

"Keep them alive," he answered, "and I'll consider it."

* * *

The escarpment trail was Elias's handwriting made physical: switchbacks cut into wet rock, cairns stacked by a patient hand, and every hundred yards or so a tin marker nailed to a tree with a few words punched into it. Ines read them as she climbed, Odile and six of the Landing's men behind her, Bess insisting on coming because, as she put it, "someone has to carry the tea."

*STEEP HERE. REST.*

*MY DAUGHTER CLIMBED THIS AT SEVEN. LAUGHED AT ME THE WHOLE WAY.*

*ALMOST THERE. ALWAYS ALMOST.*

At the top of the last rise, the cache shed stood against the sky—intact, door hanging open, empty shelves visible inside like ribs.

And beside it, the ground was torn open.

Not dug—blasted. Fresh rubble fanned down the slope, and a shaft gaped in the hillside where the vault should have been, and Ines's stomach dropped through the mountain before her boots even moved.

"No," Odile breathed. "No, no—"

But Ines was already running, sliding the last twenty feet on her heels, and she hit the edge of the shaft and looked down into it and started laughing—a wild, disbelieving sound that scared her more than the blast had.

The shaft was a decoy. It was four feet deep and full of loose rock and an old stove, and stenciled on the stove's flank in fading paint were the words PROPERTY OF E. MARSH — KEEP DIGGING, SUCKERS.

Below it, chiseled into the shaft floor in letters deep enough to survive a century of weather:

*WRONG HOLE. FOLLOW THE WATER.*

"Thirty years," Odile said faintly, beside her. "He had thirty years to think about Corliss Vance finding this."

"He had thirty years to think about *anyone* finding this," Ines said. "Follow the water."

* * *

The water was a spring, seeping out of the cliff face fifty yards east of the shed, and behind its curtain of runoff, behind a slab of rock that took four men and a sledge an hour to shift, the vault was exactly where a stubborn dead man had promised it would be.

Iron door. 1911 stamp. Lock corroded but honest, and the pry bar sang going in, and when the door swung open the lantern light fell across shelves of oiled paper, tin document boxes, leather deed books with gilt titles still legible, and a strongbox with KESTREL'S LANDING SURVEY DISTRICT painted on its lid.

Odile Ferrant knelt in the mud at the threshold and put her hands on the first deed book the way you'd touch a sleeping child, and she didn't say anything at all, and neither did anyone else, because there was nothing in any language equal to it.

Bess broke the silence eventually, gently, the way she did everything. "Tea's ready," she said. "Somebody find me a dry cup."

They worked by lantern light into the afternoon, cataloguing by Odile's shouted instructions, hauling boxes up the trail on a litter made from the decoy stove. Deeds. Plats. The original 1911 filings, countersigned by a government surveyor whose name meant nothing to Ines and everything to Odile. And at the bottom of the strongbox, wrapped in oilcloth like a relic, a bundle of letters addressed in Elias Marsh's hand to *My daughter Ruth, Kestrel's Landing*—every one of them returned unopened, stamped ADDRESS UNKNOWN, year after year after year.

Ines held the bundle and could not move.

Nine days of reading this man's marginal notes aloud to a child who'd lost her mother. Nine days of him becoming family to people who'd never met him. And here at the bottom of everything was the proof that he'd been writing to a daughter who never got a single page.

"We'll find her," Odile said quietly, watching her face. "Ruth Marsh. If she's alive, we'll find her."

"She has a girl," Ines heard herself say. "A granddaughter. Elias talked about her—Mara. He said—" And then she stopped, because Elias had said more than that, in the trapper's cabin with his fever climbing, and she had filed it away as a dying man's rambling and never let herself think it since.

*If anything happens to me, the girl will be alone. Ruth's gone two years now. The girl will be alone, and she'll be in the Landing, and nobody in the Landing will know to look for her because Ruth never told them where she settled. Find my map, find my vault, and find my granddaughter. Not in that order. The vault keeps. The girl doesn't.*

Ines turned slowly and looked down at the flooded town, at the brown water threading the streets, at the church hall on the high ground where thirty-one sick people lay and where, among the well ones, somewhere, a girl was alone and didn't know that a stranger had crossed two thousand miles carrying her grandfather's last words.

"Bess," Ines said. Her voice came out strange. "When we get down—first thing, before the deeds, before anything—we ask every soul in that camp about a girl called Mara Marsh."

Bess looked at her for a long moment, and whatever she saw in Ines's face made her nod without a single question.

"First thing," she agreed.

Down in the flooded streets, the bell in the drowned church tower shifted in the current and rang once—a single low note rolling across the water like something answering.
