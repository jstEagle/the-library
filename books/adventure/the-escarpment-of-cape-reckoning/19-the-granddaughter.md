## 19. The Granddaughter

The flooded quarter of Kestrel's Landing was a drowned alphabet. Rooftops spelled out streets that no longer existed; a church steeple stood in six feet of brown water like the last letter of a word nobody remembered how to pronounce. Ines poled the skiff through what had been Marsh Street—Elias's street, she realized, though nobody had told her that—past a floating door, past a child's tricycle hung in the branches of a submerged fig tree, its chrome handlebars catching the gray light like something signaling.

"Keep your hands inside," she said to Junie, who had insisted on coming and whom Ines had not had the heart to refuse. Pip stayed behind with Bess at the survey vault, guarding Elias's letters like a dragon on a hoard of paper.

"I know how boats work," Junie said. "I've lived on one for two weeks."

"You've lived on a boat for two weeks. That's different from knowing how they work."

"That's what my mother used to say."

Ines's pole caught on something soft and gave it up without investigating. The water here moved in slow spirals, carrying leaves, petals from somebody's drowned garden, a single rubber boot turning end over end like a small black fish. She had been in the Landing four hours and had already learned three things: the medicine was gone because Vance's crew had stripped the clinic first; the town's councilman wanted to argue about title deeds while his neighbors' houses floated away; and there was a girl missing.

Mara Marsh. Fifteen years old. Last seen yesterday morning, wading toward her grandmother's house on Cordova Lane to fetch insulin that no longer existed, because the clinic shelves were bare and the flood had come faster than anyone's arithmetic.

"She does this," the girl's aunt had said, wringing her hands in the doorway of the church shelter. "She goes off. Her grandfather always said she had river in her instead of blood." Then the aunt had looked at Ines—at the flight jacket, at the mud on her boots—and said, quieter: "You knew him? You knew Elias?"

"I met him once."

"He talked about you. At the end. He talked about the pilot who took his map." The aunt's eyes had gone bright and terrible. "Find her. Please. Everyone else is busy arguing about whose fault the levee was."

So now Ines stood in a skiff in a drowned street, calling a dead man's granddaughter's name into the rain.

"Mara! Mara Marsh!"

The name came back off the water flat and doubled, as if someone else were calling too.

* * *

They found her an hour later, and Ines would spend the rest of her life unable to explain how. Not logic. Logic said search the high ground, the shelters, the roofs where people tied themselves with clothesline. But Ines had spent five years reading weather the way other people read faces, and when the wind shifted and brought a smell of woodsmoke from the wrong direction—from the low quarter, the deep water—she turned the skiff without deciding to.

The old Marsh homestead sat on a rise that had become an island, maybe forty meters across, ringed by current where the street's drainage channeled through. Smoke curled from its chimney. On the porch roof, wrapped in a tarp that flapped like a broken wing, lay a shape.

"Mara!" Junie was standing before Ines could stop her, cupping her hands. "Mara Marsh!"

The shape moved. A hand came up, thin, waving once—the smallest gesture Ines had ever seen carry so much weight.

Getting to her was the kind of problem Ines's whole life had been training for. The current between them ran fast enough to sweep a swimmer into the open flood, so swimming was out. The skiff couldn't punch through it loaded. So Ines did what she'd done a hundred times in a hundred places nobody had names for: she read the water until it told her its secret, found the eddy line where the current curled back on itself behind a half-submerged truck, and rode it—a hard, ugly, sideways ferry-glide that put the skiff's nose against the porch posts with ten centimeters to spare and her heart going like a rivet gun.

Mara Marsh was fifteen years old and weighed nothing. She was burning with fever, lips cracked, eyes glassy, and she had dragged herself onto the porch roof because the house had taken water through the floorboards and she'd understood, with a dying prospector's instinct in her blood, that you go up. Beside her, wrapped in oilcloth, was a tin box. She wouldn't let go of it even when Ines pried at her arms.

"It's Grandpa's letters," she mumbled. "He wrote me. Every month. Even when Mama said he was dead." Her teeth chattered around the words. "I went back for them. I wasn't going to leave them."

Ines looked at the girl—the fever-bright eyes, the stubborn jaw, the same jaw she'd seen on a feverish old man in a trapper's cabin five weeks ago—and felt something crack open in her chest that she'd have sworn was load-bearing.

"We're taking you and the letters," she said. "Both. Hold on to me."

* * *

The shelter at the church had aspirin, boiled water, and prayers. None of those would do. Mara's fever was 104 and climbing, her breathing shallow and fast, and the town's one doctor had left on Corliss Vance's boat three days ago, conscripted to treat Vance's own injured crew. There was a hospital at Fort Meridian, four hours by air—if you could fly. The storm sat over the escarpment passes like a fist, and every pilot within two hundred kilometers was grounded, which meant every pilot but one, because Ines Vane had spent the last two days doing what Tam Oduya called "committing crimes against aviation" on the wrecked skid-plane Vance's men had left at the old mission strip.

It was a 1950s-era amphibian, fabric-skinned, one float crushed, the other patched with canvas and epoxy. Tam had rebuilt the landing gear struts from salvaged pipe while Ines re-rigged the control cables and Bess sewed the torn fuselage seam with waxed thread, complaining the entire time about sewing machines being better than needles. It was not a good airplane anymore. It was possibly not an airplane at all. But its engine turned over that morning with a sound like a giant clearing its throat, and Ines stood in the rain looking at it and understood that the universe had finally presented her with the bill she'd been dodging for five years.

Because the flight to Fort Meridian required crossing the Saddle—the notch in the escarpment where the wind funneled through with a violence that pilots called the Washerwoman, because it wrung you out. And crossing the Saddle in deteriorating weather required a maneuver every bush pilot in Cape Reckoning knew by its graveyard name: the Vane Turn. Dive through the gap on the backside of a downdraft, pull up hard in the rotor turbulence on the far side, and ride the curl of air over the ridge like a wave. Her sister Sylvie had invented it. Sylvie had died executing it—or dying trying—with Ines in the right-hand seat, screaming at her to climb, climb, climb, and the mountain coming up to meet them.

Five years Ines had flown around that maneuver the way you walk around a grave.

Now she stood in the rain looking at a dying girl and a bad airplane, and there was only one way through.

* * *

Tam found her at the plane, running her thumb along the patched strut.

"Bess says the girl has hours, not days."

"Bess is right."

"And you're going to fly the Saddle in this."

"In this break." Ines pointed east, where the cloud deck had split along a seam of dirty gold light—a window, maybe ninety minutes wide, maybe less. "The Washerwoman will be blowing itself out through the gap. If I hit it right, the rotor gives me the altitude I can't get any other way."

"If you hit it wrong—"

"I know what happens if I hit it wrong." Her voice came out flat and strange. "I've seen it. From the right seat. I watched it happen and I couldn't do anything, because I was twenty-three years old and I froze, Tam. Three seconds. Maybe four. I froze and my sister died knowing her little sister froze."

The rain drummed on the wing. Tam didn't say anything for a long moment. When he did, his voice was quiet, the voice he used on engines.

"You're not twenty-three."

"No."

"And you're not in the right seat."

Ines looked at him.

"You're in the left seat," he said. "You've been in the left seat for five years. Alone. Nobody freezes alone—they can't afford to." He wiped rain off his face. "I'm not going to tell you it'll be fine. I don't know that. What I know is you've spent five years flying away from that gap, and today you finally have a reason to fly through it that's bigger than your fear. So take it. And take the kid's letters with you, because if you don't come back, Pip's going to sulk for a decade."

Junie appeared at his elbow, soaked through, holding the oilcloth bundle. She pushed it into Ines's arms.

"Mara needs these," she said. "When she wakes up. Grandpa's letters. She reads them over and over." Junie's chin trembled, and she mastered it the way kids master things, brutally, all at once. "You come back too. Both of you. That's not a request. That's a promise, and promises are debts, and you told me debts mean staying."

Ines knelt in the mud and pulled the girl against her jacket, rain and all.

"Yes," she said. "That's exactly what they mean."

* * *

The skid-plane lifted off the mission strip at 14:20, light as a lie, Mara lashed into the cargo bay on a stretcher of canoe paddles and webbing, the tin box strapped beside her, cool wet cloths on her neck that Bess had prepared with field-medic efficiency and zero bedside optimism. The engine sounded angry. The airframe flexed in ways airframes shouldn't. Ines flew it anyway, climbing the eastern wall of the valley in a spiral, watching the cloud window ahead narrow and widen like breathing.

At three thousand feet she could see the Saddle: a notch in the escarpment two kilometers wide, streaming cloud off its lip like smoke from a wound. The wind through it was a visible thing, a horizontal river of air, and somewhere in that river was the downdraft that had killed Sylvie Vane, and somewhere above it was the rotor that could kill Ines Vane today.

She keyed the radio, more for herself than anyone. "Magpie, this is Skid-One. Crossing the Saddle. If you don't hear from me in twenty minutes, tell Junie—" She stopped. Started again. "Tell everyone I tried."

Tam's voice came back steady as a tachometer. "Skid-One, Magpie. We'll hear from you in twenty minutes. Fly it like you stole it."

"That's the plan," Ines said. "I stole it from myself."

She turned downwind, built speed to a number that made the patched fabric sing, and dove.

The world became noise. Rain hammered the windshield into a solid gray blur; the downdraft took the plane like a hand closing over it and shoved her down eight hundred feet in seconds, the altimeter unwinding, the ridgeline rising in the murk, and every nerve in her body lit up with the oldest memory she owned—Sylvie's voice on the intercom, calm even then, saying *I've got it, Ines, I've got it*, and the mountain, and the silence after.

*I don't have it,* the memory said. *You never had it.*

And Ines Vane, thirty-one years old, alone in the left seat with a dying girl behind her and her sister's maneuver ahead, answered the memory out loud, screaming into the roar:

"I have it NOW."

She hauled back on the stick.

The pull was brutal—four Gs, five, the patched airframe groaning, her vision graying at the edges—and then the rotor caught them. It was exactly as Sylvie had described it in the letter Ines had never finished reading, the one they'd found in the wreckage, the one describing the wave of air curling off the ridge: *like a hand under the hull, lifting*. The skid-plane surged upward, riding the curl, and Ines flew the edge of it—too much and she'd stall into the turbulence above, too little and she'd sink back into the downdraft—flying it by feel, by the seat of her pants, by five years of flying everything else in the world while refusing to fly this.

The ridge slid beneath her left wingtip, close enough to count the trees being bent sideways.

Then they were through.

The far side opened up like a held breath released: green valley, silver river, the storm behind her hammering the escarpment's back like a fist on a locked door. Ines leveled off at nine thousand feet, hands shaking so hard she had to grip the yoke with both wrists, and laughed—one raw, cracked sound, the first laugh she'd produced in five years that didn't have irony in it.

From the cargo bay, a voice, thin and feverish but unmistakably alive:

"Was that the Vane Turn?"

Ines twisted around. Mara Marsh's eyes were open, glassy but focused, fixed on her.

"Yeah," Ines said. "That was the Vane Turn."

"My grandpa said a woman named Vane invented it. He said she was the best pilot on the coast." The girl's mouth curved. "He said her sister was even better."

"He was right about both." Ines turned forward again, blinking hard, flying straight and level because straight and level was all she could manage. "Get some sleep, Mara. We'll be at Fort Meridian in forty minutes."

Behind her, the girl slept. Ahead, the sky was clearing. And in the pocket of Ines's jacket, against her heart, Elias Marsh's letters rode in their oilcloth like a second passenger—every one of them addressed to this girl, every one of them delivered at last.

* * *

Fort Meridian's hospital took Mara at 15:10. By 16:00 the fever had broken—not cured, the doctors said, but turned, the way a tide turns, and the antibiotics from the cache that Bess had carried the whole way upriver in a waterproof satchel did the rest. By 17:30 Ines was standing at a payphone in the hospital corridor, listening to the ring tone, holding the receiver so tightly her knuckles ached.

Tam picked up on the fourth ring.

"She's through," Ines said. "Fever broke. She's asleep and asking for her letters."

Silence on the line, and then Tam's voice, roughened: "And the Saddle?"

"Went through it like Sylvie drew it. Like she always said it could be done." Ines pressed her forehead against the cool glass of the phone booth, watching rain begin again outside, gentle now, almost apologetic. "Tam. I need to tell you something."

"Go ahead."

"When I come back tomorrow—I'm not just delivering the girl." She watched a drop of water trace down the glass, splitting, finding its way. "I'm coming back to stay a while. Fix the strip. Maybe longer than a while." A breath. "Don't make it weird."

A pause, and then Tam laughed, low and warm, and said the words that followed her out of the booth and into the wet evening:

"Too late. Bess already gave your bunk to Pip."
