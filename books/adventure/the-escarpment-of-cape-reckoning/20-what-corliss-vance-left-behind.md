## 20. What Corliss Vance Left Behind

They found Corliss Vance sitting on the flooded steps of the old survey station, alone, with a rifle across her knees and nothing left to guard.

The rain had thinned to a fine gray drizzle, the kind that didn't fall so much as hang in the air, waiting. Below her, the channel where the boat ramp had been was a brown torrent chewing at the pilings. Above her, the cache site stood stripped bare—crates pried open, canvas torn, Elias Marsh's careful handwriting still visible on labels nobody had bothered to remove. Her crew had taken everything worth carrying and most things that weren't.

Ines came up the trail first, machete in hand out of habit more than need, and stopped when she saw her.

Vance looked worse than the river. Three days since the defector—Okafor, his name was Okafor—had walked off with half her men and all of her leverage, and it showed in the hollows of her face, the way she sat like something that had been left out in weather too long. Her jacket was soaked through. She hadn't bothered with shelter. There was a quality to a person who had stopped bothering that Ines recognized from mirrors, from hospital waiting rooms, from the year after the crash.

"You're late," Vance said. Her voice was steady. That took effort; Ines could hear the effort.

"Ramp's gone," Ines said. "You saw to that."

"I did." Vance turned the rifle over in her hands, not aiming it anywhere, just holding it the way you hold a thing when your hands need a job. "It seemed like a good idea at the time."

"They usually do."

Vance almost smiled. It didn't survive the trip to her mouth. "Where's your engineer?"

"Below. With Bess. We're portaging around the ramp by morning if the rain holds off." Ines kept the machete low and loose. "Where are your people?"

"Gone." She said it simply, without drama, which somehow made it worse. "Okafor took the launch and four of them at dawn. The other two went into the bush an hour later. I told them to." A pause. "I told them to," she repeated, quieter, like she was checking whether she believed it.

"And you stayed."

"Somebody had to watch the mess." Vance finally looked up at her, and her eyes were dry and red-rimmed and very tired. "You want the rifle? Take it. I'm not going to make this complicated for you, Vane. I know how these stories go. I've read them."

Ines didn't move. "I don't want the rifle."

"Then what do you want?"

That was the question, wasn't it. Ines had flown five years on wanting very small, very portable things: fuel, clearances, weather windows, distance. She'd gotten good at it. And now she was standing over the woman who had gutted a dead man's legacy for salvage value, who had stranded a boat full of children behind a destroyed ramp, who had—Ines made herself think it—left a feverish girl named Mara in a drowning quarter of Kestrel's Landing because medicine was cargo and cargo was profit. Everything in her wanted to say *I want you to hurt*. It would have been easy. It would have been true, for about ten seconds.

What she actually wanted was to understand why mercy kept showing up in places she hadn't ordered it.

"The letters," Ines said instead. "Elias's letters. Did they take those too?"

Vance's jaw worked. "The survey vault was sealed. We got the title documents—that's what Corliss Vance Salvage was here for, whatever Okafor thinks he knows. The letters were in a separate tin." She reached beside her, slow, telegraphing every inch, and set a dented biscuit tin on the step between them. Rain spotted its lid. "I read one. Just one. Then I couldn't." Her voice cracked on the last word and she hated it, Ines could see her hating it. "He wrote to a daughter who never got them. Do you understand what that is? That's not salvage. That's—" She stopped. Started again, flatter. "Take them. They were never mine."

Ines picked up the tin. It was lighter than it should have been, the way important things always are.

* * *

Bess found them twenty minutes later, which meant Bess had been listening from the treeline for fifteen of them, because that was how Bess worked.

She came down the trail with her arms crossed against the damp and her chin doing the thing it did when she'd already decided something and was merely informing the world. She looked at Vance sitting wrecked on the steps. She looked at Ines holding the tin. She looked back at Vance.

"Well," Bess said. "Ain't this a picture."

"Bess—" Ines started.

"Hush. I ain't talking to you yet." Bess planted herself in front of Vance like a foreman at a hiring line. "Corliss Vance. Ran boats out of Port Meridian eight years back. Fed half the delta crews off her galley when the cannery strike hit, before any of this." She jerked her chin at the stripped cache, the ruined ramp, the whole gray ruin of the man's ambition. "I remember that woman. Wonder what happened to her."

"She drowned," Vance said.

"Mm. Looked like it." Bess crouched down, unhurried, until she was eye level with her. "Here's my thinking. You got two ways off this escarpment. One is that rifle and what's left of your pride, and we both know where that road ends—the bush don't take kindly to solo travelers in monsoon season, and neither does the river. Other way is the Magpie. She's ugly, she leaks where Tam hasn't gotten to yet, and she's crowded—we got two dock kids aboard who'll ask you forty questions an hour and a pilot who'd rather fly into a wall than forgive you. But she floats, and she's going downriver tomorrow, and there's room."

Silence. The river filled it, roaring over the missing ramp.

"You're offering me passage," Vance said. Not disbelief exactly. Something older than disbelief—the sound of a person testing a floor they expected to be gone.

"I'm offering you work," Bess corrected. "Passage is free. Work is what you owe. You're going to help haul that boat over the ridge tonight, and you're going to carry Junie when she tires, and you're going to eat what Bess cooks and say thank you, and somewhere downriver of here you're going to figure out what the hell you're for. That's the offer. It don't repeat."

"Bess." Ines stepped forward, and her voice came out harder than she intended. "This is the woman who raided the Landing's medicine. Who left Mara—"

"I know who she is." Bess didn't turn around. "I also know what happens to people the river spits out with nothing. I've buried three of them. I ain't burying a fourth so the rest of us can feel clean." Now she did turn, and her eyes were fierce and wet and absolutely certain. "Punishment's easy, Ines. Any fool can punish. Grace costs. That's why it's rare."

Ines stood there with Elias's tin under her arm and felt the words go into her like water into cracked ground. She thought about Elias trading a map for a promise from a stranger. She thought about herself, five years ago, being handed a second chance by nobody in particular and spending it badly. She thought about how every person on the Magpie was somebody's worst decision given one more day.

"Fine," she said. "But she sleeps on deck. Away from the kids."

"Fair," Bess said.

Vance hadn't moved. She was staring at Bess like a woman reading a language she used to speak. When she finally spoke, her voice came out rough. "Why?"

"Because somebody did it for me once," Bess said, standing, "and I asked the same question, and she said ask me again when you can answer it yourself. Took me six years." She held out a hand. "Come on. We got a boat to drag over a mountain and you're going to pull your weight or answer to Tam, and Tam's meaner than me."

Vance looked at the hand for a long moment. Then she took it, and let herself be pulled upright, and swayed, and steadied, and Ines watched the whole small resurrection happen in the space of three seconds and understood that she was seeing something she would never be able to explain in a logbook.

* * *

The portage began at dusk.

Tam had scouted the ridge trail that afternoon—a switchbacking track Elias had marked on the map's margin with the note *HAULING ROUTE. BRING ROPE AND BETTER FRIENDS THAN I HAD.* It climbed four hundred feet over the shoulder of the escarpment spur and dropped to a quiet eddy below the ruined ramp. The Magpie would have to come apart into pieces no heavier than six men could argue about, and then go back together on the far side, in the dark, in the rain, with the last pass closing in two days.

They worked by lantern light. Tam directed with the calm of a man who had already imagined every way this could fail and chosen not to mention them. Pip carried tools double his size and narrated his own heroism continuously. Junie, quiet since the Landing, hauled rope with a grim competence that broke Ines's heart a little each time she looked at it.

And Corliss Vance pulled.

She pulled without complaint, without speeches, without asking to be forgiven. She took the heaviest end of the engine crate when Tam hesitated about offering it. She waded chest-deep into the eddy to guide the hull section onto rollers and came out shivering and kept working. When Junie stumbled on the third switchback, it was Vance who caught her—not reaching for her, just being there, an arm offered sideways, and Junie took it without thinking, and both of them pretended afterward it hadn't happened.

Ines watched all of it from the rear with the tin under her jacket, keeping her hands busy, keeping her judgment busy too, because the alternative was letting something soften in her chest and she wasn't ready to name what.

Near midnight, at the top of the ridge, they called a rest. The clouds had torn open briefly and the moon came through, laying silver on the flooded valley below—the whole drowned country spread out like a map of someone else's mistakes. They sat in a row on the flat rock, seven of them, steaming gently, passing Bess's thermos down the line.

Vance sat at the end, apart by exactly the distance politeness required.

"You knew him," she said suddenly. Not to anyone. To the valley. "Marsh. Before."

"He hired me to fly him in twice a year," Ines said. "Five years' worth of trips. He talked the whole time and I never listened hard enough."

"What was he like?"

Ines considered the tin against her ribs. "Stubborn. Guilty. Writing letters he never sent to a girl who grew up thinking he didn't care." She glanced down the row. "Turns out he cared the whole time. He just did it in the wrong direction."

Vance nodded slowly, like a woman taking inventory. "My father ran salvage," she said. "Delta wrecks. He used to say there's two kinds of people on a river—rescuers and collectors—and the trick is knowing which one you woke up as." She laughed, short and joyless. "I woke up a collector. Been one so long I forgot it was a choice."

"It's always a choice," Tam said quietly, from the middle of the row. He said it without accusation, the way he said everything mechanical and true. "I chose wrong for eleven years. Ask me how."

Nobody asked. Nobody needed to. The Magpie's crew had become fluent in each other's silences, and this silence held all of them at once—the pilot, the engineer, the cook, the collector, the children learning what the adults were only beginning to learn themselves.

Junie broke it, because children always do. She scooted down the rock until she was next to Vance, close enough that their shoulders touched, and held out the thermos.

"You look cold," Junie said.

Vance stared at the cup like it might be rigged. Then she took it, and drank, and handed it back, and said, "Thank you," in a voice that suggested no one had said anything that simple to her in years.

Ines watched, and felt the last argument inside her go quiet—not won, just outlived. Mercy working where punishment wouldn't. Elias had known it. Bess had known it. Maybe it was the whole secret of the escarpment, written somewhere in the survey vault in a dead man's careful hand: *the flood doesn't care who deserves drying out.*

* * *

They reached the eddy at two in the morning and slept in shifts against the hull sections, and at dawn Tam began reassembling the Magpie with oil-stained hands and terrible humming, and by nine she was whole again, floating, ugly, alive.

Ines stood on the bank with the biscuit tin and opened it for the first time.

Inside: letters, bundled in oilcloth, dozens of them, addressed in Elias's cramped hand to *Mara Marsh, Kestrel's Landing.* And beneath them, wrapped separately, a single page newer than the rest, dated three weeks ago. Not a letter. Instructions.

*To whoever brings this down:*

*If you're reading this, I gambled right, and I'm sorry for the weight of it. The letters are for Mara. Give her time with them—all the time she needs, even if the passes close, even if it costs you. Some deliveries matter more than schedules.*

*The rest—the vault, the titles, the town—that's yours now, whoever you are. I watched you land at Halfway Bend and I saw a person counting. I counted too, for forty years. Miles, ounces, ounces, miles. It adds up to nothing, friend. It adds up to nothing.*

*Count something else.*

*—E.M.*

Ines read it twice. The rain had started again, soft on the oilcloth, soft on her shoulders. Downriver, past the bend, past Widow's Tooth, past the drowned quarter where a feverish girl waited with her mother's grave and her grandfather's name, the last pass was closing at a rate Tam had calculated twice and disliked both times.

She folded the page away and turned to the others, already moving, already loading.

"Magpie casts off in ten minutes," she called. "Everybody aboard—including you, Vance. You're on bilge duty."

And Corliss Vance, collector of wrecks, stood up from the bank, picked up a bucket, and reported for work.
