## 21. Miles Versus Years

The water went down the way a guest leaves a bad party—slowly, noisily, leaving things behind it didn't have permission to leave.

Ines watched it from the porch of the Marsh house, which was no longer the Marsh house in any way that mattered, because Mara had declared on her second fever-free morning that anyone who'd hauled her out of a drowned quarter got to call it whatever they liked, and Junie had immediately claimed the room with the good window. The flood had crested eleven days ago at the eave line and had been retreating ever since, peeling back off the streets of Kestrel's Landing like a bandage coming off a wound nobody wanted to look at underneath.

What it left was mud. Mud on the church steps, mud in the schoolhouse, mud six inches deep in the store where Corliss Vance's crew had stripped the medicine shelves bare. Mud in the airstrip—which had never been much of an airstrip to begin with, just a graded shelf of clay and gravel above the flood line, and was now a graded shelf of clay and gravel and silt, rutted like an old scar.

Ines stood there with a mug of Bess's coffee—it was terrible coffee, chicory and river water, and she had come to love it with an intensity that alarmed her—and did what she always did when she couldn't sleep, which was count.

She counted the ruts: forty-one that would break an axle if she tried to take the Cessna out today. She counted the days since Elias died: nineteen. She counted the days since she'd flown Mara out through the storm gap: twelve, and every one of them Mara had gotten stronger, color coming back into her face like ink into blotting paper. She counted the hours until the Cessna's engine needed its hundred-hour inspection, which she could do herself, which she would do herself, because that was the one thing in the world that had never lied to her—a machine, a checklist, a task with a beginning and an end.

Except.

Except that for twelve days now, every time she finished counting something, she caught herself not starting the next thing. Standing on porches. Holding mugs. Watching the river do its slow arithmetic of retreat. There was a word for what she was doing and she refused to think it, so instead she drank the terrible coffee and went down to the strip and started pulling silt out of ruts with her hands.

* * *

"You're doing it wrong," Tam said, on the third day of this.

He came down the slope from the Magpie with a shovel over his shoulder like a man who'd been looking for her and wasn't going to admit it. The boat sat high and dry now, beached on the new bank of the river like a whale that had decided, on reflection, to stay.

"Doing what wrong."

"Digging." He planted the shovel beside hers and leaned on it. "You're digging like you're trying to get away from the ground."

"I'm digging like someone who wants an airstrip by Thursday."

"Thursday." He said it the way he said most things, flat and warm at once, a man who kept his voice level the way other men kept their tempers. "And after Thursday?"

"After Thursday I take the mail run south. Antibiotics, seed stock, the Kestrel letters to the mission at Two Rivers. Then I'm back before—" She stopped. Heard herself. *I'm back.* As though it were a schedule. As though it were settled.

Tam let the silence sit there between them, patient as ballast. That was the thing about Tam Oduya: he never pried anything open. He just stood next to the closed door long enough that you opened it yourself to see why he was standing there.

"The strip needs more than digging," he said finally. "It needs grading, drainage ditches, gravel from the quarry cut two miles east, and a roller, which we don't have, so it'll need a log dragged behind the truck for about nine days straight. It's a two-person job. Maybe three." He looked up the slope, toward the house, where a small shape was already picking its way down through the mud with the absolute confidence of a ten-year-old who has decided the world is safe again. "Maybe four."

"Junie is not operating heavy machinery."

"Junie," said Junie, arriving, "heard everything and would like to register a complaint about being underestimated."

* * *

They worked nine days.

That was the strange part, later—the part Ines kept turning over like a stone in her pocket. Not the work itself; work she understood, work was the one language she'd been fluent in since she was sixteen and turning wrenches at a bush strip outside Fort Merrow. What was strange was that the work didn't end. Every day had a task and every task had a next task, and somewhere around day five she realized she had stopped counting the tasks, had stopped keeping the ledger at all, and the world had not collapsed. The sun came up without her auditing it.

Pip turned out to be a genius with the drainage ditch, or at least a genius at finding reasons to stand waist-deep in muddy water, which amounted to the same thing at that age. Bess sent food down the slope twice a day on a tray carried by whichever dock kid was least muddy, which meant the tray changed hands constantly and arrived half-eaten. Mara came down on the sixth day, still thin, still moving carefully, and sat on an overturned fuel drum and read Elias's marginal notes aloud while they worked—his voice drifting across the strip in fragments, *the clay holds water here, dig deep, M., if you ever read this, the escarpment looks different from above, I should have shown you.*

"He wrote like a man leaving instructions for a house he expected you to live in," Ines said once, quietly, to Tam, when Mara had gone up to nap.

"That's because he did," Tam said.

On the eighth day they dragged the log behind the Landing's one surviving truck, back and forth, back and forth, until the clay lay smooth as poured batter, and Junie rode on the log's towing frame the whole time in flagrant violation of every safety rule Ines had ever held sacred, and Ines found she didn't have the heart to make her stop. On the ninth day it rained, of course, because it was Cape Reckoning and the monsoon doesn't so much end as lose interest gradually, and they all stood under the Magpie's tarp awning eating Bess's stew and watching the rain hit the new strip and run off it in clean, orderly sheets, exactly the way drainage is supposed to work, and Pip said, "We built that," in a voice of such naked astonishment that nobody laughed, because everybody felt it too.

"We built that," Ines agreed.

Her plane sat at the north end of it, cleaned, inspected, fueled, ready. She had checked it that morning the way she checked it every morning—oil, control surfaces, tires, tie-downs—and it had occurred to her, standing there with her hand on the propeller, that she could load the mail and be gone in twenty minutes. South to Fort Merrow, west to Halfway Bend, the whole circuit, the whole familiar wheel of miles. Nobody would blame her. Everybody would understand. That was the trap of being competent: everyone gave you permission to leave, forever, because you were so good at it.

She put her hand flat on the cowling instead, feeling the metal cool under her palm, and thought about what it would mean to fly out tomorrow and what it would mean not to, and discovered—to her genuine surprise—that the two options weighed different amounts now. Five years ago they had weighed nothing at all. Leaving had been weightless. Leaving had been the absence of decision, gravity doing the choosing for her.

Now leaving had mass. Now it would cost something specific, itemized, named.

Junie. Pip. A girl reading her grandfather's margins aloud. An engineer who knew the worst thing about her and had stayed anyway. A cook whose coffee tasted like punishment and whose loyalty tasted like nothing else on earth.

She wiped her hand on her trousers and went back to the tarp and the stew, and didn't tell anyone yet, because some decisions you have to carry alone for a day first, to make sure they're yours.

* * *

The logbook lived in the Cessna's map case, and it had lived there for five years, and it was a particular kind of book: columns ruled in blue, HOBBS TIME, FUEL ADDED, ROUTE, REMARKS. Ines had filled thousands of pages like it. Her handwriting in those books was small and exact, a bookkeeper's hand, because numbers don't need flourish. Page after page. Fort Merrow–Halfway Bend, 1.4 hours. Halfway Bend–Two Rivers, 2.1. Remarks column mostly blank, because what was there to remark? Rain. Delivered. Returned.

On the evening of the tenth day, after the strip had dried enough to hold a tire, after she'd flown one careful circuit over the Landing just to prove the ground would carry her—Mara and Junie and Pip waving from below like the strip was a ship launching—she climbed back out of the cockpit and sat on the wing root with the logbook on her knees, and the pen wouldn't move.

Not because she didn't know the numbers. She knew them cold. 0.6 hours, local circuit, full stop. Fuel unchanged. Remarks: strip rebuilt, serviceable.

But her hand wanted to write something else, and it had wanted to for days, and she had been refusing it the way you refuse a sneeze, and now, sitting in the last gold light with the escarpment going purple behind the town and the smell of woodsmoke rising from forty chimneys that had lit their fires again because the town was drying out and living, she stopped refusing.

She drew a line under the flight entry. Below it, in the remarks column, in her small exact bookkeeper's hand, she wrote:

*Kestrel's Landing. Present company:*

And then she made a list.

*Tam Oduya — engineer. Knows my worst. Stayed.*
*Bess Amaro — cook. Feeds people unasked. Do not let her coffee near guests.*
*Mara Marsh — granddaughter of E.M. Recovering. Reads aloud.*
*Junie Okafor — navigator (self-appointed). Claims window seat permanently.*
*Pip Okafor — drainage consultant. Fearless. Watch him near water until further notice.*
*Elias Marsh — prospector. Gave me a map and a family in the same breath. Kept.*

She looked at it a long time. Nine lines. No hours, no miles, no fuel burned. The first entry in five years that measured nothing she could convert into distance.

There was a version of her, five years younger, who would have torn the page out. There was a version from last month who would have written it and then told herself it didn't count, that logs were for airplanes, that names weren't cargo. But the woman sitting on the wing had pulled a child out of the Widow's Tooth rapids and flown another child through a storm gap on purpose, and had learned—slowly, expensively, the way you learn anything true—that some cargo changes the aircraft.

She closed the book. Put it back in the map case. And found Junie standing at the wingtip, watching her with the frank, unnerving gaze of a child who has decided a question and is waiting for the adult to catch up.

"You're staying," Junie said. Not asking.

Ines considered lying, out of pure reflex, and found the reflex had atrophied. "I'm staying."

"Good." Junie nodded once, business concluded, then ruined it by grinning. "Because Pip already told everyone at the store you're ours now, and it's easier if it's official."

"Yours," Ines repeated.

"Mine and Pip's. We discussed it. You're the flying kind of ours. Like a dog, except useful."

"Thank you," Ines said gravely, "for the terms."

Junie shrugged and headed for the house, and Ines sat on the wing a moment longer, laughing at nothing, at everything, at the sheer outrageous luck of being claimed by children who had lost enough to know exactly what claiming meant.

Tam appeared at the nose of the plane, wiping his hands on a rag, having materialized the way he did when something mattered. "Heard the news," he said. "You've been acquired."

"It seems so."

"Any appeals process?"

"None. I asked."

He came around and sat on the wing beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched, and for a while neither of them said anything, because they were both people who had learned that silence shared is worth more than talk spent. Below them the lights of Kestrel's Landing came on one by one, windows filling with lamplight, the town reassembling itself out of the dark the way it had reassembled itself out of the flood—stubbornly, imperfectly, together.

"There's a survey vault under the church," Tam said eventually. "Elias's papers say as much. And the title documents are real—the land office will honor them, I checked the regulations twice. But somebody has to carry certified copies to the regional registrar at Two Rivers before the land company's lawyers file their claim, and the monsoon season ends in three weeks, and after that the passes close for four months." He paused. "Somebody with a plane."

Ines looked at him. "You're giving me a reason to stay busy."

"I'm giving you a reason to matter," Tam said. "Those aren't the same thing. Took me forty years to learn the difference."

She looked out at the escarpment, black against the last light, the wall she'd spent five years treating as the edge of the world. From the air that afternoon it hadn't looked like a wall at all. It had looked like a spine—something the country hung on, something that held it up.

"Two Rivers and back in a day," she said. "Certified copies, registrar's stamp, home by dark."

"Home," Tam repeated, very softly, testing the word the way you test ice.

"Home," Ines said, and found it bore her weight.

She slid off the wing, went around to the map case, and took the logbook out again. Opened it to the fresh page. Under the list of names, in the same small exact hand, she added one more line—not a measurement, not a route, just a heading for everything that came next:

*Next entry: arrivals.*

Then she closed the book, and the engine cowling ticked as it cooled, and somewhere up the slope Bess rang the supper bell, and the whole family started toward the sound.
