## 22. The Escarpment of Cape Reckoning

The medicine run left at first light, the way good runs always did, and Ines Vane was late for it.

Not catastrophically late. Not the old kind of late, where lateness was a philosophy and a fortress and a way of never having to say goodbye to anyone because you were always already halfway gone. Just ordinary late—the kind where a nine-year-old couldn't find her other boot and a six-year-old had hidden it, allegedly as a joke, and a riverboat engineer stood on the dock of Kestrel's Landing with his arms crossed and his face arranged into something that was trying very hard not to be a smile.

"You're going to miss your weather window," Tam Oduya said.

"I have four hours of weather window."

"You used to preflight at dawn."

"I used to sleep alone." She said it lightly, but she felt it land in her own chest like a stone dropped down a well. A year ago that sentence would have been a boast. Now it was just a fact, the way *the river rises in monsoon* was a fact—true, and no longer frightening.

Junie emerged from behind the fuel shed holding one boot aloft like a trophy. "Pip put it in the bilge of the *Magpie*. He says if you're going to fly away you can hop."

"Tell Pip," Ines said, pulling it on, "that stowaways don't get co-pilot privileges."

"He's not a stowaway anymore. Bess signed his papers. He's crew."

Everyone was crew now. That was the thing about Kestrel's Landing that still caught her off guard, a year on: the way the town kept absorbing people. The defector from Vance's crew—Ruthie, her name actually was Ruthie—ran the trading post now and gave credit to anyone who'd helped haul sandbags during the flood, which was everyone. Corliss Vance herself had lasted eleven days aboard the *Magpie* before she'd asked Bess, in a voice like someone reading a confession off a card, whether there was work for a woman who knew engines and nothing else worth knowing. There was. There always was, here. That was the whole trick of the place.

Ines did her walkaround with Junie shadowing her, calling out the checklist items she'd memorized without being taught, the way kids memorize anything they love. Control surfaces free. Fuel caps secure. The skid-plane's repaired gear—Tam's welds, tested through two hundred hours of strip landings—held true. On the tail she'd painted nothing new, but she'd let the old registration stay, the one that connected the plane to its history, to the crash, to Mara. Some numbers you don't paint over. You keep them so you remember what they cost.

Mara Marsh climbed into the back seat with the careful dignity of a girl who had died once already, more or less, and decided to be magnificent about the second chance. She was twelve now, tall for it, Elias's jaw and her mother's patience, and she carried a canvas satchel against her chest the way pilgrims carry relics. Inside it were letters. Elias's letters—the ones Ines had read aloud in the marsh country while Junie listened like scripture—copied out in Mara's own hand, one set for every settlement on the route. The interior had decided Elias Marsh was a saint, which he would have found hilarious, and the letters were treated accordingly: read aloud in mess halls, nailed to church doors, argued over by trappers who'd known him forty years and never once heard him say anything kind until after he was dead.

"Cache copies," Mara said, patting the satchel. "And the survey vault duplicates. Ruthie wants them filed at Halfway Bend before the wet season eats the originals."

"Copy work," Ines said gravely. "Very important cargo."

"The most important." Mara buckled in. "Aunt Ines?"

The name still did something to her, every time. Aunt. Conferred without ceremony, the way titles are given in families—by usage, by insistence, by a child deciding and an adult surrendering.

"Mm?"

"When we get to Widow's Tooth, can we do the high line? Pip says you did the high line last month."

"Pip wasn't there."

"Pip says everything."

The high line over Widow's Tooth gorge—the crossing that had nearly killed them all, the one where Pip had gone into the water and Ines had gone in after him without doing a single calculation first. She flew it now when the water allowed, a long climbing arc along the gorge wall, and every time she did it she thought of her sister, and every time it hurt differently than it used to. Less like a wound. More like a scar that had learned to be useful.

"We'll see what the air says," Ines said, which was what she always said, and Mara nodded, satisfied, because the air always said yes lately. Not because the weather had improved. Because Ines had gotten better at listening to it—and better at knowing the difference between brave and reckless, a distinction that had cost her five years and one sister to learn.

Bess came down the dock carrying a basket big enough to smuggle a child in, which, historically, it had. "Provisions," she announced. "And if you land anywhere near Corliss, tell her I sent jam and she owes me a letter."

"She owes you three letters."

"Then she owes me a fourth. That's how debt works, Ines. You'd know, if you'd ever stayed anywhere long enough to collect."

The dock laughed. Ines let herself be laughed at, which was another thing the year had taught her—that being known was not the same as being cornered.

She ran up the engine. The Landing fell away beneath her: the rebuilt stilt houses, the new school roofed with salvaged tin from Vance's abandoned salvage rig, the airstrip she and Tam had graded by hand, its windsock sewn by Junie from a dress that had belonged to nobody's mother and everybody's mother at once. The title papers from Elias's survey vault had held. Every family on the Landing held deed to their own ground now, and the land company's lawyers had withdrawn the way land company lawyers do—quietly, and with invoices.

The river was low and braided and bright with morning. Ines turned north, toward the escarpment.

* * *

She saw it whole for the first time at about four thousand feet.

That was the strange arithmetic of it. Five years of flying these routes, hundreds of passes, thousands of miles, and she had only ever seen the escarpment in pieces—a wall here, a notch there, a storm-swallowed rampart she threaded through with her teeth clenched and her mind on the far side. The escarpment had been, for five years, exactly what its name promised: an edge. Something you went over or around or through, never something you looked at.

But today the air was glass-clean after a week of rain, and the monsoon had pulled back south like a tide going out, and the whole escarpment stood revealed from the salt flats to the headwaters, two hundred miles of it, unbroken.

It didn't look like a wall.

It looked like a spine. Like the backbone of something enormous that had lain down across the cape a very long time ago and fallen asleep, and everything she loved had made its home in the hollows of it—Halfway Bend tucked in the shoulder, the trapper cabins along the ribs, Kestrel's Landing in the green country at the foot where the rivers spread their fingers. From up here she could see the smoke of the settlements rising straight in the still air, little threads of proof, one for each place she'd delivered medicine or gossip or letters or herself.

She keyed the mic, out of habit, then remembered the cabin was full and spoke anyway.

"There it is," she said. "Whole thing."

Junie pressed to the window. Pip, wedged between Bess's basket and Mara, said, "It looks like it's looking back."

"It is," Ines said. "It's been looking back the whole time. I just never stayed still long enough to meet its eyes."

Mara leaned forward. "Elias drew it once. In the margins. The whole escarpment, all squiggly, with a note that said—" she recited it the way children recite things they've decided are holy— "*'You spend your whole life thinking it's keeping you out. Then one day you're on the far side of it and you realize it was holding you in, like a hand.'*"

Ines flew in silence for a while after that. Below, the gorges opened and closed. Somewhere under that green water was the rock where she'd pulled Pip out, and somewhere beyond the next ridge was the swamp where a dying man's marginal notes had become a family's bedtime reading, and somewhere behind all of it was a runway in the flatlands where a woman named Vane had once walked away from a wreck and kept walking for five years, measuring her life in miles because miles were honest—they added up, they ended, they never asked you to stay.

She had stopped counting miles sometime in the past year. She'd noticed it first on the logbook: entries that weren't distances. *Junie lost first tooth. Magpie hull sound. Tam laughs in his sleep.* And then, this spring, in her own handwriting, a line she hadn't planned:

*Deliveries today: Mara, well. Letters, 14. Jam, 1 (for C.V.). Arrivals: all of us.*

Arrivals. That was the unit now. Who got there. Who came home. Who was waiting when you landed.

* * *

They made the run in the old order—Halfway Bend, the trapper strips, the mission at Two Rivers—and at every stop the ritual repeated itself: Mara delivering letters like a small solemn priest, Junie and Pip swarming whatever local children existed, Bess's basket emptying into grateful hands, Ines drinking terrible coffee and hearing news she would carry onward, gossip flowing upriver through her the way it always had, except now the gossip included her. *Vane's staying, they say. Built a house at the Landing. Got herself a family, more or less.*

More or less. She'd stopped correcting people.

At Two Rivers, the mission pilot—an earnest young man who reminded her painfully of herself at twenty-five, all fuel math and escape velocity—asked her how she stood it, the interior, the mud, the same faces year after year.

Ines looked at him for a long moment. She could see the runway behind his eyes, the next job, the next coast, the beautiful clean arithmetic of leaving.

"You count wrong," she said finally. "That's all. Everybody out here starts out counting miles, because miles are easy. But miles don't add up to anything. They just repeat." She tapped the armrest. "Count arrivals instead. Count who's there when you shut down the engine. One day you'll look up and realize the escarpment isn't a wall you crossed. It's a room you live in."

He wrote it down. They always wrote it down. Whether any of it took was between him and the sky.

* * *

They came home in the gold part of the evening, the light coming low across the footlands so that every river burned like wire. Tam was on the dock—he was always on the dock when she flew; he claimed it was engine curiosity, and everyone let him keep the fiction—and the *Magpie* sat beside him, caulked and repainted, her name regilt, taking on paying passengers now for the escarpment mail run that Bess had bullied into existence.

Ines brought the plane in over the water the way she liked best, low and slow, close enough to see the fish standing in the shallows, and touched down on the strip she'd built with her own hands, and rolled out, and shut down.

Four passengers climbed out. A basket came down. A satchel of copied letters. A boot that had spent the day in a bilge.

Tam walked up as she finished tying down. "Good flight?"

"Full flight," she said. "Every seat."

"That's the only kind worth logging."

They stood together while the light went out of the escarpment, ridge by ridge, west to east, like a lamp being dimmed room by room through a house. Junie and Pip were arguing about tomorrow. Mara was showing the letters to Ruthie. Somewhere Bess was already cooking for people who hadn't been invited, which was how everyone got invited.

Ines opened her logbook against the fuselage and clicked her pen. Tam watched her write, not pretending not to.

"What is it tonight?" he asked.

She read it back to him as she wrote it, slowly, the way you read something you want to hear twice:

*"Departed Kestrel's Landing 0640. Passengers: four. Cargo: letters, jam, one tooth (Junie's, loose, expected). Route flown complete. Weather: clear enough to see the whole escarpment, and I did, and it was not a wall."*

She paused. Then, underneath:

*"Arrivals: four. All accounted for. Home: same."*

Tam read it upside down, which was how he read everything of hers, and smiled the smile she'd first seen across a half-sunk boat, wary and crooked and now worn smooth as river stone.

"Any departures?" he asked.

Ines looked up at the escarpment, dark now, holding them in like a hand.

"Not one," she said. "Not one that counts."

And down at the far end of the strip, Junie lit the beacon they'd wired themselves—a single steady light at the edge of the dark, burning for whoever was still out there on the water, still coming home.
