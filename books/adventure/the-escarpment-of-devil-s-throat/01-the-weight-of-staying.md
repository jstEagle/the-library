## 1. The Weight of Staying

Puerto Maldonado has two seasons: the wet season, when the river tries to drown you, and the dry season, when everything else does.

I'd been in town eleven days when the fixer found me, which by my standards was practically a residency. Eleven days of sleeping in a room above a chicha bar where the walls were thin enough to conduct both music and gossip, eating one meal a day and drinking three, watching my boots come apart at the seams like something giving up. I had forty-one soles to my name and a blacklist that ran from Cusco to La Paz with my name stitched through it in red thread — *Hartwell, Z.* Do not certify. Do not insure. Do not, under any circumstances, let this woman near a client.

The blacklist was official enough that it had a number. Unofficially, it just said what it said: Hartwell's daughter. Like father, like daughter. The river keeps what it takes, and the Hartwells keep taking from the river.

So I sat on the balcony of the chicha bar with a warm beer I couldn't really afford and watched the Madre de Dios go brown and muscular past the fuel barges, and I told myself — for the eleventh day running — that I was only resting. That guides rest between expeditions. That this wasn't the same as stopping.

I was lying, of course. But I've found that if you say a lie often enough in a river town, it starts to sound like local dialect.

* * *

He came at four in the afternoon, when the heat lies down across the plaza like something dead and even the dogs stop pretending. I saw him before he saw me, or let me believe I did — a man who dresses for the jungle the way accountants dress for church: correctly, and without love. Khaki shirt pressed despite the humidity. Boots too clean. A canvas satchel worn at the shoulder with the careful newness of a man who bought it for this trip and would burn it after.

He ordered a chicha first, which was his first mistake. You don't order chicha unless you want the whole bar to know you're a tourist of something.

"Señora Hartwell?" He said it carefully, like a man stepping onto ice he'd been assured would hold.

"Depends who's asking."

"My name is Delgado." He set a card on the table between us. Heavy stock. Embossed. In Puerto Maldonado, heavy stock is either a government office or trouble, and the government offices here don't emboss. "I represent interests in the extractive sector. Mining concerns. We have an expedition upriver that has gone—"

"Silent. Yes." I turned the card over. Blank on the back. Even the trouble was cheap these days. "That's usually how it goes when you send men into country nobody's mapped since the rubber boom. Radios die. Satellites get shy. Men stop writing home." I took a pull of the beer. It had gone past warm into something geological. "What you actually want is someone to walk up there and count the bodies, so your lawyers know whether they're settling widows or writing obituaries."

Delgado smiled the way men smile when they've been told their own job description back to them and didn't enjoy the mirror. "We want someone discreet."

"Discreet costs more than competent."

"We can pay both."

I should have laughed and gone back to my beer. Every instinct I have — and my instincts are the only inheritance my father left me that anyone hasn't tried to repossess — said stand up, say no, walk out, find another town. There are always other towns. There are always other rivers, though fewer every year, and none of them mine.

But then he opened the satchel, and I stopped being a professional and became what I actually was, which was broke.

* * *

Here is what the blacklist doesn't put in the file: blacklisting a guide doesn't just take her work. It takes her credit, her references, her standing deposits, her ability to book a bus ticket without a cashier squinting at her name twice. It turns every transaction into a small interrogation. After two years of it, you develop a relationship with money that isn't romantic anymore. It's more like the way you feel about oxygen. You don't dream about it. You just notice, constantly, precisely, how much of it is left.

I owed the chicha bar's owner, Rosa, six weeks' rent. I owed a boatman in Atalaya for a run I'd made on credit last season, back when I still believed the season would pay for itself. I owed a clinic in Quillabamba for stitches I'd taken without anesthesia because anesthesia cost extra and I'd been feeling philosophical about pain that week. And underneath all of it, unspoken but compounding daily, I owed my father a debt I couldn't itemize, which is the worst kind, because it accrues interest in a currency nobody exchanges.

Delgado counted out bills on the table. Not all of it — half, he said, the other half on completion — but enough. Enough to clear Rosa. Enough for the boatman. Enough for boots that would hold together past the first set of rapids, which is not a luxury in my line; it is the difference between a story you tell and a story they tell about you.

"The expedition," he said, while I tried to look like a woman doing arithmetic instead of a woman drowning. "Led by a Dr. Voss. Survey team, eight personnel, contracted to assess mineral potential along the upper reaches. They were due to report twelve days ago. Their last transmission placed them near—" he paused, and I watched him decide whether to say it, and watched him decide wrong, or right, depending on how you price information, "—the Devil's Throat."

The beer went cold in my mouth, which is a trick, because it had never been cold.

"You know the name," he said. It wasn't a question. Everyone within two hundred kilometers knows the name. The escarpment stands over the gorge like a verdict — a wall of red stone split by a falls that sounds, from the ridges, like the earth clearing its throat before it speaks. The Ashaninka won't go there. The colonists won't either, mostly, and the ones who will come back wrong or don't come back. There are maps that mark it and maps that leave it blank, and the blank ones are the honest ones.

"I know the name," I said.

"And I understand your father—"

"Don't." I set the bottle down harder than I meant to. Somewhere behind me a chair scraped, and I felt the bar recalibrate around me, the way rooms do when a woman's voice finds an edge. "Whatever you read in whatever file you bought, keep it in the file."

Delgado raised both hands, palms out, a man demonstrating he carried nothing sharp. "Of course. Forgive me. My point is only that you know that stretch of water better than any guide currently... available." He let the word *available* sit there between us, polite as a knife laid flat on a cloth. "Eight men, Señora. If any of them are alive, they will be very glad to see you. If none of them are alive—" he slid the remaining half of the payment forward, an envelope this time, thick enough to have weight, "—then we will need to know that too. With certainty. And we will need anything Dr. Voss was carrying. Documents. Notes. His personal effects. Company property."

"Company property," I repeated.

"All survey instruments are company property."

Something moved in me then, down deep, below the arithmetic, below the debt — a fish turning over in dark water. I ignored it. I was good at that. Two years of practice.

"When do I leave?"

"Tomorrow, if possible. There's a boat chartered at the military dock. The pilot's name is Chávez. He knows you're coming." Delgado stood, buttoned his jacket against a heat that had already won, and offered his hand. I looked at it until he converted the gesture into adjusting his cuff, which was graceful of him, I'll grant that.

"One more thing," he said, at the rail, with the river going gold behind him. "The Throat itself. Our contract specifies the upper reaches. Nobody is asking you to enter the gorge."

"Nobody ever asks," I said. "That's how it always starts."

He smiled again, that accountant's smile, and went down the stairs into the plaza, and I sat there with an envelope full of other people's grief-money and finished a beer I now couldn't taste at all.

* * *

Rosa found me on the balcony at dusk, when the swifts came out over the water in their thousands, stitching the air shut above the river. She set down a fresh beer without being asked and stood with her hands on her hips, a broad woman built like the counter she leaned on, who had fed me on credit and listened to me talk in my sleep in two languages and never once asked what I was running from, because in Puerto Maldonado everyone is running from something and asking is bad manners.

"So," she said. "Work?"

"Work."

"Good work?"

I thought about it honestly, which is more than most people manage. "Paid work."

She grunted. Down on the river a barge went past loaded with fuel drums, its wake slapping the pilings, and the swifts wheeled and screamed and came apart and came back together. Twenty years ago — twenty-two, if I'm precise, and I am always precise about this, precision being the only prayer my father ever taught me — a survey team went up this same river with my father at the front of it, mapping territory for a company that swore the maps were for roads. He came back without two of his men and with a reputation that never fit him again, and then four years later he went back up alone, with his instruments and his journals and whatever it was he couldn't stop looking for, and the river kept him.

They found the raft. They found one boot. They did not find him, and they did not find the cache of surveys he'd supposedly been carrying, and after a decent interval the companies stopped calling it a search and started calling it a write-off, and after a less decent interval somebody started saying he'd been selling maps he had no right to sell, and that word — *forgery*, whispered, always whispered — is the word that grew up into my blacklist, the way a seed grows into a tree that shades out everything else.

And now a man in pressed khaki wanted me to go up the same water, toward the same wall of stone, to count the bodies of eight more men who'd been sent to look at what my father looked at.

"He died up there," I said to Rosa, because she deserved the truth even if I didn't deserve her credit. "My father. On that river. Above the Throat."

Rosa considered this with the gravity she brought to everything except gossip, where she was merciless. "And you're going anyway."

"I owe you rent."

"You owe me sixty soles. You're going for more than sixty soles."

"I'm going because I'm good at it," I said, "and because being good at something and not doing it is a slow way to die, and I've seen the fast way and it didn't tempt me." I turned the envelope over in my hands. It was heavier than money. Everything is heavier than money; that's the trap of it. "And because maybe whatever Voss found up there, my father found first. And maybe there's a piece of paper in a waterproof tube somewhere in that gorge with his handwriting on it, and maybe it says what everybody says it says, and maybe it says the opposite. And I have spent two years being the daughter of a word I never got to read."

Rosa drank some of my beer, which was her way of blessing things. "You could stay," she said. "I need help behind the bar. The pay is terrible but the hours are worse, so at least they're honest."

For one whole minute — I checked, because precision — I let myself want it. The bar. The steam off the rice pot in the morning. The swifts at dusk, every dusk, the same show, free. Staying. Just staying, in one place, with one view, until staying stopped being surrender and started being a life.

Then I heard the falls. Not the real ones — they were two hundred kilometers away — but the ones I carry, the sound under every quiet, the sound my father's river makes inside my head whenever I try to sit still. Some people inherit land. Some inherit debts. I inherited a current, and currents don't negotiate.

"No," I said, and stood, and my knees cracked like green wood, twenty-nine years old and sounding sixty, which is what rivers do to people ahead of schedule. "But I'll send you the rent from upriver. First money I touch."

"Liar," Rosa said fondly, and took the envelope from my hand and counted it, and kept out sixty soles on the spot, because she knew me better than any mapmaker ever had.

* * *

I packed that night by headlamp: rope, hammock, tarp, the medical kit I'd rebuilt piece by piece over two years like a man rebuilding an engine in exile, a machete with a handle worn to the shape of my father's grip because I'd bought it secondhand and never reshaped it, out of a superstition I'd deny under torture. Last, from the bottom of my bag, wrapped in oilcloth, I took out the thing I never travel without and never show anyone: a page of my father's journal, torn, water-stained, rescued from a burning of papers I arrived too late to stop. Four lines in his hand, tight as survey notes:

*River rises at the Throat. The stone remembers what the water forgets. If I do not come back, do not look for me. Look for what I found.*

I had read those lines perhaps ten thousand times. I read them once more, and heard beneath them the falls, the carried falls, the ones that never stop.

Outside, the swifts had gone to bed and the river ran on in the dark, brown and patient, going where it always went. Tomorrow I'd be on it, headed the wrong way — upstream, against the current, toward the wall of stone that had already eaten one Hartwell and was about to be handed eight more men's worth of reasons.

I clicked off the headlamp and lay in the hammock and did not sleep, which was fine. Sleep is for people whose fathers come back.

In the morning there would be a boat, and a pilot named Chávez, and a river that had been waiting for me with the patience of all things that win.

I almost didn't go. I want that on the record, somewhere, in case the record survives me: I stood at the window in the dark for a long time, and I almost stayed.

Almost is a word the river owns.
