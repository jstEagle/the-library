## 2. Dead Radio

The river told me before I saw it.

That's the thing about water that runs through a country where men have died—it carries the news upstream in its own language. A certain flatness to the current. A smell like wet iron and old leaves. The Ashaninka call it *yana yaku*, black water, though it wasn't literally black; it was just that the river had stopped bothering to pretend it was friendly.

I'd been poling the raft since dawn, six hours of the kind of silence that makes you invent conversations with dead people. My father was particularly chatty this morning. He had opinions about my grip on the pole, about the way I was reading the water wrong on the left channel, about how a Hartwell reads a river the way a priest reads scripture—front to back, then back to front, until you can recite it in the dark.

*You're drifting, mija,* he said, in the voice I'd built for him out of three surviving letters and one photograph. *Left channel. Watch the boil line.*

I watched the boil line. I hated that he was right. Twenty years dead and still correcting my technique—that was paternity for you.

The camp announced itself by smell first: woodsmoke gone cold and sour, the particular sweetness of food left rotting in canvas. Then the sound, or rather the absence of it. A working expedition camp has a heartbeat—generators, voices, the clatter of someone doing something badly and being told about it. This place had none. Just the river going about its business against the bank, indifferent as a bureaucrat.

I grounded the raft in the shallows below a cutbank and stood there a while, letting the midges find me, because walking into that camp was a door I couldn't un-walk. Behind me lay two days of river I could retrace. Ahead lay whatever had made eight grown men with satellite phones stop existing.

Debt, I reminded myself. Debt walks ahead of you. That's the whole trick of it.

* * *

The tents came first. Four of them, dome-style, company-issue, the kind of expensive nylon that's supposed to laugh at weather. Someone had laughed at them harder. Every one was slashed—not torn, not collapsed by wind, but opened with long deliberate strokes, the way you'd gut a fish. And not from outside. That took me a minute to understand, standing there with my hand on the nearest pole, but once I saw it I couldn't unsee it. The cuts ran outward. Whatever had happened here, it started inside the tents.

Or the men were asleep when it started, which amounts to the same thing and is worse.

The mess tarp was still rigged between two trees, sagging under a week of rain. Underneath it: plates with food fossilized onto them, a card game abandoned mid-hand—I counted the hands, seven players, someone had been winning and it hadn't saved him—and a crate of the good instant coffee that mining companies buy because their people complain otherwise. Nobody had packed out. Expeditions pack out. Even panicked expeditions pack out; panic is organized compared to this. This was a place where the leaving had happened all at once, sideways, without anyone deciding anything.

The radio sat on an ammunition box near the fire ring, and I'll give whoever did it credit for thoroughness. It wasn't smashed. It was *executed*—handset crushed under a boot heel, antenna bent double, the casing split so the circuit board showed like bone. I turned it over with my boot toe. Company asset tag, serial number, the works. They'd wanted no calls going out. Not interrupted calls. None, ever, from this spot.

That's when the dread stopped being atmospheric and became specific. You slash tents to terrorize. You smash a radio to isolate. Terror plus isolation isn't rage. It's procedure.

* * *

The grave was forty meters back from the bank, under a kapok tree whose roots would eventually do the job properly, if slowly. One grave. Eight men missing.

It was dug too shallow—I knew that before I got close, because the ground had settled unevenly, a shallow dish of earth instead of a smooth mound, and because the vultures had opinions. I won't describe what the tarp-weighted depression held beyond saying that the man had been buried in his clothes, face down, without ceremony, and that whoever dug the grave had done it fast and angry or fast and frightened, and either way hadn't finished the arithmetic on depth.

I crouched at the edge and looked for what I always look for, because looking is the only trade I have left: boots. Dead men's boots tell you their last hour. These said he'd run—the laces were snapped on the left one, tied hastily on the right, the kind of dressing you do while moving. He'd been caught from behind, and the angle of things suggested he'd known it was coming and had chosen to spend his last thirty seconds digging instead of running anyway.

Somebody in that crew had decided one of them deserved dirt. Or somebody had been ordered to provide it, and had provided the minimum. Either way, it meant the killers weren't animals. Animals don't bury. Men who bury are men managing appearances, and men managing appearances are men with employers, and employers leave paperwork.

I stood up too fast and the gorge-sickness hit me—the vertigo I get sometimes, the world tilting on its axis like a boat in a swell, my father's river tilting with it. I put my hand on the kapok bark and breathed until the trees agreed to stay where they were.

One grave. Eight men. Seven unaccounted for, minus whoever was in the tents, minus—

I stopped myself. Counting the dead was Rennick's department, or the fixer's, or whoever sat in an office somewhere with a ledger. My job was tracks.

* * *

The tracks led east, toward the escarpment, and they were the loudest thing in the forest.

Not subtle, not old. Two days, maybe less—the rain had blurred the edges but not erased them, and the leaf litter was compressed in a way that takes weeks to spring back. Boot prints, at least six distinct sets, some of them doubled up the way prints look when men are carrying weight. Drag marks between them, intermittent, the signature of someone being moved who could still mostly walk. And off to the side, lighter, quicker—a single set of smaller prints, barefoot or near it, that broke away from the group twice and was each time herded back by a heavier tread planted deliberately beside it.

Someone had run. Twice. And been caught, twice, and was still alive two days ago, because herded men are kept men.

I followed the trail to the point where it stopped making sense, which is also the point where it started making perfect sense. The boot prints converged into a column and marched straight at the Devil's Throat escarpment—the wall of stone and falling water that every map in Peru draws with a blank space and every guide in Peru treats like a cliff edge made of superstition. The trail didn't skirt it, didn't look for a crossing, didn't do any of the sensible things trails do. It walked directly into the blank space on the map like a sentence walking off the edge of its page.

I stood at the treeline and looked at the Throat for a long time.

You grow up hearing about it the way you grow up hearing about drowning—abstractly, then personally. No guide goes in. Not because of the falls themselves, though the falls would kill you cheerfully enough, but because of what the Ashaninka say lives behind the water, and because of what happened to the last three outsiders who ignored the saying. My father was one of those three. His name is on no marker because there is no marker, just a bend in the river and a story the boatmen still tell with their chins tucked down.

And now a column of armed men had walked into it dragging a survivor, and the company that paid me had sent me alone to a silent radio, and the arithmetic of that was starting to resolve into a shape I didn't like.

They hadn't hired me to rescue anyone. I'd known that since the border town, honestly—known it the way you know a bill is counterfeit, in the part of your brain that keeps the real knowledge and lets the rest of you carry on. But standing at the treeline with the spray reaching me across half a kilometer of air, cold as a hand on the back of the neck, the knowledge finished itself: they'd sent a disgraced guide with no colleagues, no radio contact schedule worth the name, and a reputation for going places she shouldn't, to walk into a place where witnesses go to stop being witnesses. If I found bodies, I confirmed deaths. If I found nothing, I reported nothing. Either answer served them. The only answer that didn't serve them was the one I was already thinking about giving.

I should have turned around. Any competent person would have turned around. I had two days of river behind me, a raft, and a debt that was large but not fatal—debts are negotiable, graves less so.

But the smaller set of prints had broken away a third time, twenty meters from the treeline, and this time the heavy tread beside it wasn't a herding print. It was a scuffle. A short one. And past the scuffle, at the very edge of where the leaf litter gave way to stone, there was a smear of blood on a root, still tacky at the center, and a single footprint pointing not toward the escarpment but along it—north, into the ravine country, the broken ground where a hurt man could hide and a search party would rather not follow.

Alive, then. Possibly. Probably. The math on "probably" was bad, but I've never needed good odds, only a direction.

My father went into that gorge chasing honest work and it ate him. The company that killed him—or the company that inherited whatever killed him, the corporate genealogy being unclear—had now sent his daughter to tidy up after the same appetite. There was a symmetry there that felt less like coincidence than like bait. And I resented it. I resented the neatness of it, the way grief gets recycled into labor, the way the dead keep getting drafted.

But the other thing I felt, standing there with the falls roaring like a crowd that never stops applauding something terrible, was not fear.

Well. Fear. Obviously fear. But underneath the fear, in the load-bearing part of me, something with edges. Something that had been waiting twenty years for a legitimate reason to walk at that wall of stone, and had finally been handed one by the worst possible people, and had decided—to my considerable annoyance—that it was going to take it.

"Fine," I told the escarpment, because talking to geography is a habit I picked up from a dead man and see no reason to drop. "You win. For now."

The escarpment declined to comment. It's very good at that. It's had practice.

I went back to the raft for the rope coil and the med kit, and I did not let myself think about the fact that I was choosing the blank space on the map. I thought instead about the barefoot prints heading north, and about how somewhere in the ravines a man was bleeding and hiding from the same people who'd buried his crewmate too shallow, and about how the last time anyone connected to my father had gone into the Devil's Throat, nobody had come back out to say what it was like.

Somebody was going to come back out this time. Even if it was only me.

Even if it was only to say: *it was exactly as bad as they told you, and worse, and here is the truth of it, written down at last.*

I cut the raft loose from its mooring and let the river take it downstream, empty. No sense leaving evidence of how many of us there were. Then I shouldered the pack, checked the bearing against the sun, and started north along the lip of the gorge, following a dying man's footprints into the one direction no guide in Peru would follow.

The falls roared behind me. Ahead, the ravine country opened its green mouth, and I stepped in.
