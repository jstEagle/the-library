## 3. The Survivor

The hollow tree announced itself before I saw it. A smell of rot and smoke and something underneath that I recognized from too many camps I'd walked into too late—the sweet, wrong smell of a body that has stopped taking care of itself.

I'd been following the river since dawn, keeping to the game trails on the left bank, moving the way my father taught me: never on the skyline, never twice through the same gap in the cane. The tracks I'd found at the dead camp had led me northeast, toward the escarpment, and they'd told me a story I didn't like. Three men walking in a line, unhurried, carrying weight. One set of prints smaller, lighter, weaving—someone running, then walking, then running again, then not running at all. That last stretch of prints had ended at the river's edge two days back, or so I'd judged from the mud, and I'd spent a bad hour imagining what the river did with people who stopped at its edge.

But the tracks had started again on the far side of the canebrake, and they'd led here, to a ceiba tree so old its heart had rotted out of it, leaving a black doorway in the trunk wide enough for a man to crawl into and disappear.

"Quispe?" I said. "Tomás Quispe?"

Nothing. The jungle went on with its own business overhead—the parrots arguing, the drip of last night's rain working its way down through the canopy. Somewhere behind me, the river kept up its low mutter, like a creditor who never leaves.

I crouched by the doorway and looked into the dark. My headlamp found a boot first. Then a leg in torn surveyor's khaki. Then the rest of him, folded up in the hollow like a letter someone had tried to cram into an envelope too small for it, and the brass gleam of an instrument case clutched against his chest with both arms, the way a child holds a stolen thing.

His eyes opened. They were the wrong color—too bright, glassy, fever cooking behind them—and they went straight past my face to something behind me, something that wasn't there.

"No," he said. "No, I already told you. I told you where the line runs. I told you—"

"Quispe. It's Zephyrine Hartwell. I'm looking for the Voss expedition."

That name did something to him. His whole body flinched, a full-body stutter, and he pressed himself deeper into the rot until his shoulders scraped the inside of the trunk. "You're one of them," he whispered. "You came with the rifles."

"I came alone. Look at me. Look at my hands." I spread them, empty, palms out, the way you'd calm a spooked animal or a drunk. "No rifle. No radio. Just me and a bad attitude."

He stared at my hands for a long time. Fever makes people honest, or it makes them incapable of lying, which comes to nearly the same thing. Whatever he saw in my empty palms—or maybe it was just the name Hartwell, said out loud in that voice, my mother's cadence and my father's stubbornness all at once—something in him unclenched by a single degree.

"You're Hartwell," he said. "The daughter."

"Depends who's asking."

"Voss had a file on you." He laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough that bent him double. "He had a file on everyone. He said you were blacklisted. He said you were the only guide who'd take the job, because you're broke, and because you'd want to know what happened to the survey cache." He wiped his mouth with the back of his wrist. "He said that like it was a joke. He said, *we'll hire the daughter, she'll work for free, she'll work for the name.*"

The words went into me like a fishhook. I stood very still and let them.

"And what did you say to that?"

"Nothing." His eyes were clearing a little, or focusing, at least. "I was the junior man. Juniors say nothing. Juniors carry the theodolite." He lifted the brass case a few centimeters, an offering. "I've been carrying it since the camp."

* * *

Getting him out of the tree took twenty minutes and cost me most of my patience. His left leg was a mess—a gash from knee to ankle, ugly but not the killer; the killer was the infection riding up the limb, hot and red and streaking toward the thigh. He'd bound it with a strip of shirt that had given up any pretense of being clean, and he'd been living in that hollow for two days, maybe three, drinking rainwater off the leaves and eating nothing, which is a way to die that takes longer than starvation but arrives at the same address.

I got antibiotics into him from my kit—two capsules, washed down with water from my filter bottle—and made him eat half a ration bar while I cleaned the wound, which he bore with his jaw set and his eyes fixed on the canopy, refusing to make a sound. I'll give him that. He was twenty-four, maybe twenty-five, and he had the specific stubbornness of men who have spent their careers being the youngest person in the room.

While I worked, he talked. Fever-talk at first, sliding in and out, and then, as the antibiotics began their slow argument with the infection, something more coherent.

"The causeway," he said. "Above the gorge. Voss found it on the third day. Stone, cut stone, fitted without mortar, running up the face of the escarpment like a stairway to nowhere. He was—" Tomás searched for the word. "Ecstatic. He'd been hired to survey for the road corridor, you understand? That was the cover. But he knew. He'd known from the start. He had maps that predated his maps. He had coordinates."

"What coordinates?"

"Points on the escarpment. Marked in an old hand." He looked at me. "An old *initials*. H.H. I thought it was a coincidence. I was the junior man. Juniors believe in coincidences."

H.H. Hernando Hartwell. My father's initials, stamped into every instrument he ever owned, every map he ever signed, every page of the journals I'd grown up reading like scripture. My hands went still on the bandage.

"Show me the theodolite."

He unclenched his arms—reluctantly, the way you'd hand over a child—and I opened the case. The brass instrument sat in its velvet grooves, green with tarnish at the joints, and there on the barrel, small and sure, the engraving: *H.H.*, and beneath it, in my father's cramped hand, a serial number I'd have known anywhere because I'd traced it with my finger a thousand times as a girl, sitting on his knee in a tent that smelled of kerosene and pipe smoke, asking him what the numbers meant.

This was his theodolite. The one that had gone into the river with him, according to every official account. The one that had never been recovered.

"Where," I said, and my voice came out flat and strange, "did you get this?"

"Voss had it. In his kit, from the first day. He said he bought it in a market in Lima." Tomás's eyes were on my face, reading me the way I'd read the tracks. "But it wasn't a market instrument. It was maintained. Oiled. Someone had kept it alive for twenty years." He paused. "Your father didn't lose this in the river, did he."

It wasn't a question. I closed the case.

"Tell me about the men with the rifles," I said.

And he told me. In pieces, in fever-order, out of sequence and back again—the story of how on the fourth night, after Voss had photographed the causeway and taken his readings and gone very quiet in the way of men who have just understood something that changes the price of everything, the security detail had stopped being a security detail. How they'd made the junior men dig. How the radio had been smashed first, so no one could call downriver. How Tomás had run, because he was the youngest and the fastest and because the man holding the rifle had hesitated half a second too long, and how he'd heard the shots behind him and hadn't stopped running until the river.

He told it all without drama, in the flat voice of a surveyor reading off measurements, and somehow that was worse.

"I buried what I could," he finished. "Before I ran. I buried the notes Voss kept in his tent—the real ones, not the ones he showed the company. I buried them by the big stone at the camp's edge. And I took the theodolite, because—" he hesitated, and for the first time he looked embarrassed— "because I thought it might matter. To someone. To the daughter, if there was a daughter."

I sat back on my heels in the rot and the rain-drip and looked at this boy who had crawled into a tree with my father's theodolite and waited there to die, and something in my chest that had been clenched for twenty years shifted by a single degree, like a joint remembering it could move.

"You're in no condition to travel," I said.

"Then don't travel." He gripped my wrist with a hand hot as a stove lid. "Listen to me. Listen. They'll send someone to confirm. That's how it works—Voss explained the whole architecture to me once, drunk, laughing at it. First the survey team. Then the confirmation team, to verify the deaths and collect the documents. Then the lawyers." His grip tightened. "You're the confirmation team. You have to be. Why else would they send a blacklisted guide up a river no one will guide?"

I'd been circling that suspicion for two days like a bird over a carcass. Hearing it said out loud, in a fever-roughened voice, by a boy who had nothing left to gain from lying—there was no more circling after that. The fishhook went all the way in.

"Then I won't take you downriver," I said.

His whole body sagged with relief, and then immediately tensed again, because he was smart enough to hear what I hadn't said yet.

"But I'm not taking you downriver *up*river either, if you take my meaning. The escarpment is a death sentence. No guide goes there. The Ashaninka don't allow it, and the river doesn't care about permissions. And you can't walk on that leg for three days, maybe four."

"Then what?"

I looked at the hollow tree, at the wound, at the brass case, at the river muttering its creditor's mutter beyond the cane. I thought about my father's initials in tarnished brass. I thought about the word *coincidence*, and how juniors believe in it, and how I'd stopped believing in it the day they'd pulled what was left of my father out of the water and told me it was an accident, and I'd been too young and too broke to argue.

"Then we do the stupid thing," I said. "We go up. Slow. You heal while we move, I carry you when you can't, and we find that causeway before whoever they send next finds us. Because Quispe—listen to me now—they sent me to confirm you were dead. When I don't report back, they'll know you're not. That gives us a head start measured in days, and I intend to spend every one of them climbing."

He looked at me for a long moment. The fever was still in his eyes but underneath it, something steadier had surfaced—the look of a man doing arithmetic and not liking the total but trusting the math.

"You could leave me," he said. "It would be easier. Faster. You know that."

"I know that."

"And you're not going to."

"I'm not going to." I stood and shouldered my pack, and held out my hand. "Not because I'm good. Because you've got my father's theodolite and my father's initials and a story that ends with my father's name on a forged claim, and I have spent twenty years being the daughter of a dead man who died by accident, and I am *done*, Quispe. Done with accidents. Done with downriver. Done with being hired to bury things."

He took my hand. His was burning, but his grip was a surveyor's grip—precise, deliberate, stronger than it looked.

"Upstream, then," he said.

"Upstream," I agreed, and turned to look at the escarpment's distant gray line above the canopy, where the river went to die and my father's secrets were waiting, patient as stone, for someone stupid enough to come and dig them up.

Behind us, far downriver, so faint I almost missed it, something echoed off the water that might have been an outboard motor.

Or might have been exactly what Tomás said was coming.
