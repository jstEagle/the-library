## 4. What Tomás Saw

The fever keeps its own calendar.

I know this because I have been living by it for — three days? Four? The sun comes up through the crack in the hollow tree and lies across my hands like a strip of yellow paper, and I try to write on it, and of course I can't, because it's sunlight, because I'm a surveyor and not a madman, though the distinction has been narrowing lately. Fever-time doesn't move in hours. It moves in returns. The same image comes back again and again, the way water comes back to a low place, and each time it arrives it has worn itself a little deeper into me.

Here is what returns most often: Voss's face when he saw the causeway.

I want to be precise about that face, because precision is the only prayer I know how to say. Marcus Voss was not a sentimental man. He was the kind of expedition leader who sharpened his own pencils with a knife rather than trust a sharpener, who ate the same ration every day at the same hour because variation invited chaos. When the porters joked about him behind his back — *el reloj*, they called him, the clock — he pretended not to hear, but I think he liked it. A clock is a thing you can set your life by. He had built himself into one.

And then the river took us around that bend above the gorge, where the water runs green and slow as if it's holding its breath, and the mist off the falls hung in the trees like gauze over furniture in a closed house, and Voss looked up at the cliff wall and stopped being a clock.

His mouth opened. Nothing came out. I remember thinking, absurdly, that I had finally found something the man couldn't schedule.

* * *

But I'm getting ahead of myself, and the fever hates it when I get ahead of myself. It wants everything out of order. It serves me the expedition like a shuffled deck, and I have to play the cards as they come or lie here listening to them laugh.

Fine. Shuffle again.

Card one: the radio dying.

Not smashed — I'll come to the smashing, God help me, I'll come to that — but dying first, the way things die before they're killed. For two nights the static had a shape in it, a rhythm underneath, like someone breathing on the other end of a very long room. Delgado said it was atmospheric interference from the storm front coming down off the cordillera. Delgado said a lot of reassuring things. That was his job; he was our fixer's man, the company liaison, and reassurance came out of him the way sweat comes out of a man in this heat, constantly and without his permission.

"It's the mountains," Delgado said, tapping the set. "They eat signals."

He was right about that, at least. The mountains do eat signals. Later I understood they were eating ours specifically, that somewhere downriver a man with a satellite phone was watching our little dot of telemetry blink out and feeling no more concern than a farmer feels when a leaf stops moving. But that night I just lay in my tent listening to the static breathe and told myself it was weather.

Weather. Yes. I was twenty-six years old and I believed in weather.

* * *

Card two: the porters leaving.

This happened on the fourth morning, and it happened wrong. Porters don't leave wrong. They negotiate, they complain, they finish their contract and walk out with their pay and their dignity intact, and if you've treated them well they tell the next crew you're worth working for. Our porters left at night, without their full pay, without a word to anyone except old Cusi, who woke me because he liked me — I was the junior man, the one who carried his own load and learned Quechua words badly and laughed at myself, and apparently that buys a kind of loyalty you can't requisition.

"They say the river is angry," Cusi told me, crouched at the edge of camp with his bundle already on his back. "Not the water. The river. The one under the water."

"What does that mean?"

He looked at me for a long moment, and I want to record honestly that there was pity in it, which should have frightened me more than anger would have. "It means we are not paid enough to meet what your maps don't show."

Then he was gone, and eight men with him, and the camp felt suddenly like a stage set after the audience has gone home — all the machinery visible, nothing left to make it real.

Voss recalculated. Of course he did. He redistributed the loads, cut two days from the schedule, and told us the survey would proceed. Delgado made a call on the satellite phone, walked a little way upriver to do it, and came back wearing the expression of a man who has been told the check is in the mail.

I should have left with Cusi. I want that written down somewhere, in some ledger that matters: *Tomás Quispe should have left with the porters.* I didn't, because I was twenty-six and the company was paying triple hazard rate and my mother needed the surgery and I had convinced myself that fear was a thing professionals managed, like weight limits and rope angles. Fear was data. You logged it and moved on.

I logged it. I moved on. Remember that I did both, when you decide what I am.

* * *

Card three. The good card. The one the fever plays gently, almost kindly, because even fever knows you need one beautiful thing to hold.

The causeway.

We'd spent five days working the tributary above the gorge, running transects through forest so dense the canopy closed overhead like a lid, taking elevations for a road alignment that existed only in a company office two thousand kilometers away. Miserable work. Beautiful country, miserable work — the leishmaniasis flies alone could drive a saint to blasphemy, and Voss was no saint. On the sixth day the lid opened. We came up onto a shelf of ground below the escarpment where the trees stood back as if respecting a boundary, and the light fell straight down for the first time in a week, and there it was.

Stone. Cut stone, fitted stone, running along the contour of the slope for — I paced it later, fever and all, I still have the number in my head — four hundred and twelve meters. Not a wall. A road. A causeway, wide enough for six abreast, its surface worn into shallow basins where centuries of rain had pooled, each basin holding a coin of sky. And at intervals, pillars, waist-high, carved — I knelt at the first one and put my hand on it and my hand was shaking and I told myself it was the climb, the altitude, anything, everything except the truth, which was that I was touching work older than any nation whose paperwork had sent me there.

The carving was water. That's what undid me. Not jaguars, not gods with fanged grins — water. Spirals and channels and fish rendered in a style so confident it made Inca masonry look like practice. Whoever cut those pillars had thought about rivers the way Voss thought about schedules: totally, devotionally, as the structure beneath everything.

"Quispe." Voss's voice. Strange. Stripped. "Come look at this."

At the causeway's upper end, where it climbed toward a fold in the escarpment, the stone changed. Newer — no, not newer, *repaired*. Repaired within living memory, by hands that knew the old technique, and set into the joint between two courses was a bronze plate gone green with age, and stamped into the plate, small and precise:

**E.H. 1971 — RUTA HONESTA**

Route honest. Honest route.

I knew the initials. Every guide on this river knew the initials. Hartwell. The Hartwell. The man whose maps the Ashaninka trusted when they trusted no one else's, who went up into the gorge in '71 and came back down alone and half-mad and wouldn't say what he'd seen, who died — drowned, they said, though the river never gave back a body to prove it — and whose name had become the kind of story boatmen tell at night until it stops being a story and starts being a fact of the landscape, like the depth of the channel or the location of the rapids.

"He found it," Voss said. He wasn't talking to me anymore. He was talking to the stone. "Twenty years ago he found it, and he mapped a way in, and he kept his mouth shut." A sound came out of him that I first took for a laugh. It wasn't. "Why would anyone keep his mouth shut about *this*?"

I could have answered him. Cusi could have answered him. Any porter on this river could have answered him. Some things are kept quiet the way a wound is kept clean — not from shame, but because exposure kills.

But Voss was already walking the causeway, pacing it, counting paces, and I was already opening my notebook, because we were professionals, and professionals log things and move on.

God forgive us. We logged it.

* * *

Now the deck turns ugly, and the fever knows it, and still it deals the cards, because fever is honest in the way that only sickness and small children are honest.

Card four: the rifles.

They came upriver the next morning. Nine men in a long black canoe with an outboard muffled so it sounded like a sewing machine, and they wore no uniforms, which was itself a uniform — I knew the cut of those clothes, the jungle boots, the way the slings of their rifles were run tight against the stock for fast access. Company contractors. I'd seen the type at the compound in Puerto Maldonado, drinking beer behind wire, laughing too loudly at nothing.

Delgado greeted them like family. That was the detail that turned my stomach cold before anything violent happened at all: the embrace, the back-slapping, the shared cigarettes. This was not a rescue arriving. Rescues don't know each other's nicknames.

Voss understood it maybe half a second before I did, or maybe he'd understood it for days and had been logging the data and moving on, right up until the data moved on him. The lead contractor — big man, gray at the temples, a voice like gravel poured slowly — asked to see the survey notes. Just the notes, señor, standard procedure, verify the alignment data. And Voss, who trusted procedure the way other men trust God, handed over his field book.

The contractor flipped through it. Stopped at the page with the causeway. Looked at the bronze plate sketch, the initials, the coordinates.

"You photographed the pillar carvings," he said.

"Some. Preliminary—"

"Give me the camera."

And Voss — *el reloj*, the clock, the man of procedure — did the one unscheduled thing of his entire adult life. He stepped back and put his body between the contractor and the instrument case, and he said, quietly, in the voice he used for correcting errors: "No. This site predates your claim by nine hundred years. Whatever paper you're carrying, it doesn't cover pre-Columbian remains. There are laws."

"There are," the contractor agreed pleasantly. "Laws need witnesses, though. And this far upriver—" he spread his hands, showing us the empty air, the green walls, the roar of the falls below like the breathing of something enormous asleep, "—witnesses are hard to keep alive."

I want to tell you that the violence came after a standoff, after choices, after some line was crossed that we might have refused to cross. It didn't. It came the way weather comes. One moment the morning was standing still with everyone breathing; the next, the man named Rennick — I learned his name later, from the way the others deferred to him, *jefe* said with a flatness that wasn't respect — nodded once, and the world became noise.

Delgado shot Voss. Our own liaison, our own company man, two rounds, close, while Voss was still looking at Rennick, still believing in procedure. I will see that forever: the clock stopping mid-second, Voss's hand still open toward the instrument case, offering the camera he'd already refused to give.

Then the camp came apart. Chávez ran for the treeline and they dropped him like a bird off a wire. Mateo got to the radio and started smashing it with the axe — not to call for help, I understand now, but so that no one ever could, so that our silence would be total and therefore deniable — and they shot him kneeling among the pieces, and the last thing the radio ever transmitted was the sound of its own destruction, a shriek of feedback that I heard from thirty meters away, facedown in the ferns, with a boot pressing my neck into the mud.

The boot lifted. Someone rolled me over.

Rennick looked down at me the way you look at a receipt you're deciding whether to keep. Junior surveyor. Twenty-six years old. Mother in Lima, surgery pending, triple hazard rate. All of it legible in my face, I'm sure; I've never been able to hide anything.

"The notes," he said. "Where are the duplicates?"

"No duplicates," I said, and it was even true, and it saved my life for exactly as long as it took him to decide a body in the open was a liability and a body in the river was a statistic.

They threw me in the shallows. I heard the shot go past — past, past, I've measured it since, it went past by the width of a prayer — and the current took me, and the river that eats signals decided not to eat me, and I came ashore two kilometers downstream with a bullet graze along my scalp leaking warmth into the water, and I crawled, and I climbed, and I found the hollow tree the way a wounded animal finds any dark place, which is to say: by dying toward it.

* * *

Card five. Last card. The fever plays it slow.

The grave.

On the second day — or the third; the tree's crack of light has lied to me more than once — I heard them come back. Voices, low. The scrape of a shovel. Through the gap in the bark I watched them bury Voss and the others in a pit beside the ruined camp, and I watched them do it badly, hastily, the mound barely raised, no markers, because a proper grave invites questions and a shallow one invites only rot and then silence.

Rennick stood over it longest. He held Voss's field book — the real one, the one with the causeway in it — and after a while he tore out the relevant pages, folded them into a waterproof pouch, and dropped the rest of the book into the pit before they filled it.

So the record exists. Half of it. Torn, buried, deniable — but existing, in the one place no one would ever dig: under the bodies of the men who made it.

Except one body declined to stay buried. Except one junior surveyor with a graze on his scalp and a fever climbing his spine like a vine lay in a hollow tree reciting coordinates to keep himself conscious, and among those coordinates, worn smooth by repetition, are four numbers that locate a bronze plate reading **RUTA HONESTA**, and a route, honest, cut into the escarpment by a dead man whose daughter, the river gossip says, has come upstream asking questions.

I heard the boat before I saw her. Everyone hears boats on this river; the water carries engine noise like a rumor carries a name.

She's coming. The fever tells me she's coming, and the fever has been wrong about many things — about the shapes in the dark, about the voices, about how long I've been here — but it is not wrong about this, because I can hear the outboard now with the part of me that isn't burning, the surveyor part, the part that logs data and moves on.

Listen. Listen to me, whoever finds this, whoever she is, whoever Hartwell's daughter turns out to be when she steps out of that boat with her father's stubbornness written all over her:

Don't go up to the camp. There's nothing there but a shallow grave and a smashed radio, and the men who made them will come back to check their work.

Come to the hollow tree. Come to the fever. I have four hundred and twelve meters of causeway in my head, and a route marked *honest*, and a story that ends with nine men in a black canoe who believe the river has finished its accounting.

It hasn't. I'm still here. And now, so are you.
