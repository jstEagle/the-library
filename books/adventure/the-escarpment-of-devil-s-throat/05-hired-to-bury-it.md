## 5. Hired to Bury It

I sat with Voss's field case open on my knees for a long time before I touched anything inside it.

That's not reverence. I want to be clear about that. Reverence is what you feel at a grave. What I felt was the particular stillness of a woman doing arithmetic she doesn't want to finish. The case was waterproof aluminum, scuffed green, the kind mining companies hand out to men they intend to spend. It had been wedged under the collapsed corner of the mess tent, and if Tomás hadn't shown me where to dig — feverish, half-lucid, drawing shapes in the dirt with a stick like a child playing houses — I'd have walked right past it.

Tomás had gone back to sleep in the hollow tree, or I'd put him there, which amounts to the same thing. Fever like his doesn't negotiate. I'd dosed him with the last of my antibiotics and left him muttering coordinates to nobody, and then I'd come back down to the dead camp alone, because that's the kind of decision-making that's kept me employed in this business, such as it is.

The lock was broken. Of course it was. Everything at this camp was broken in the way things get broken when people are in a hurry and angry.

Inside: a ledger with a rubber band around it. A leather document wallet, company-stamped. A satellite phone, dead as the radio. And a manila envelope with VOSS, R. — PERSONNEL / CONFIDENTIAL typed across the flap in the tidy bureaucratic font that always looks to me like a threat wearing a necktie.

I started with the ledger, because ledgers lie last.

* * *

Here's what I knew going in, and let me lay it out honestly because the dishonest version is the one I'd been telling myself since Puerto Maldonado:

A mining concern — Consorcio Vertiente, registered in Lima, beneficial ownership somewhere offshore where ownership goes to die — had hired me through a fixer named Salcedo to locate an expedition that went silent six weeks ago upriver of the Devil's Throat escarpment. Nine men. Survey crew, geological team, security detail. Their last transmission was garbled weather chatter. The company wanted them found, wanted an assessment of "site conditions," wanted any documentation recovered and returned intact.

Standard language. I've read a hundred contracts with that shape. Find our people. Tell us what happened. Bring back the paper.

What I told myself: they're worried about their men. Companies do occasionally worry about their men, in the way that a man worries about a tool he dropped down a well — but worry is worry, and it pays the same.

What I didn't tell myself: my father died on this river. That the last legitimate survey he ever ran traced these same banks. That when Salcedo slid the envelope across the bar in Puerto Maldonado — cash, no contract, no paper trail, *off the books, señorita, for someone with your discretion* — I looked at the money and thought about the clinic in La Paz that still had my mother's unpaid bill pinned to a corkboard like a trophy, and I said yes before the better part of me finished raising its hand.

Debt decides fast. That's its whole advantage over conscience.

So. The ledger.

Voss kept good books, I'll give him that. Daily entries, positions, sample counts, personnel notes. The handwriting starts confident and gets smaller as the weeks go on, which is what handwriting does when a man is writing less because he believes less. I read by the flat gray light coming through the slashed tent fabric, and the river talking to itself below the bank, and somewhere upslope a bird making a sound like a hinge that needs oiling.

The first third was geology. Core samples, assay requests sent downriver, gravel bars flagged for testing. Then, eleven days in, the entries change character.

*Causeway stone above gorge, cut jointing, not natural. Photos taken. R. wants samples held back from shipment.*

*R. questioned re: purpose of survey extension. Told him scope comes from Lima. He did not accept this.*

*Found marker cairn, old, deliberate. Hartwell-style placement.* 

I stopped reading.

I want to tell you that my hands were steady. They weren't. There's a specific tremor that starts in the chest and works outward, and every guide who's ever lost someone to a mountain knows it — it's the body's way of saying *we have been here before and it ended badly.* Hartwell-style placement. My father built his cairns a particular way, three stones base, one capstone offset to the northeast, a habit so ingrained that I once caught myself stacking firewood that way in a hostel in Cusco and had to sit down on the bunk and breathe for a while.

Voss had found my father's markers. Twenty years after my father stopped making them.

I kept reading, because that's the other thing about me. I keep reading.

*Met with Ashaninka hunters at secondary camp. They know we are here. R. gave them tobacco; they did not take it. R. says the valley above the falls shows old work — terracing, water channels. Says it matches nothing in the published record. Says Lima will want this handled quietly.*

There it was. *Handled quietly.* Three words that in my trade mean: whatever is up there, the plan for it does not include witnesses.

And then the entries got shorter, and the last one was four days before the radio died, and it said only:

*Men unhappy. R. has made calls. If anything happens to me, the full survey is in the green case, duplicate in the cache. V.*

Duplicate in the cache. Which cache? Voss hadn't known my father, couldn't have known his routes — unless he'd found the markers, followed them, and learned what my father had left up there twenty years ago. Unless the dead man's map and the missing man's insurance pointed at the same place, which would be either an enormous coincidence or no coincidence at all, and I have never once in my life been paid enough to believe in enormous coincidences.

I set the ledger down and opened the manila envelope, and that's when the arithmetic finished.

* * *

The personnel file wasn't Voss's. It was mine.

Not mine personally — though give it time — but a Hartwell file. My father's. Company paper, Consorcio Vertiente letterhead, dated nineteen years back. A proposed joint venture: exploration rights above the Devil's Throat escarpment, contingent on a certified boundary survey establishing the concession limits. The surveyor of record: E. Hartwell, licensed, recommended, *previously engaged in the region with satisfactory results.*

Attached: a boundary claim document. A beautiful thing, typeset, stamped, with a surveyor's certification block at the bottom and a signature line.

The signature line was empty.

Beside it, in the margin, in pencil, in handwriting I would know blindfolded, upside down, underwater, dead — the way you know your own name — someone had written: *Boundaries as drawn exclude all occupied land. Will not certify. E.H.*

My father refused to sign. Nineteen years ago, some version of this same company drew lines on a map that turned Ashaninka territory into vacant wilderness on paper, and they needed a licensed surveyor to make the lie legal, and my father — drunk half the time, broke most of it, blacklisted within two years anyway — looked at the money and wrote *will not certify* in the margin like a man initialing his own death warrant.

Two years later he was dead on this river. Officially: capsized at the Throat rapids, body never recovered, gear scattered, tragedy, moving on. Unofficially — and I had spent twenty years refusing to think the unofficial thought — a man who won't sign can become more expensive alive than dead, especially when he knows the country better than anyone who might be sent after him.

And now, nineteen years later, another expedition comes up the same river, finds the same valley, and its leader starts writing *handled quietly* in his log. And the company hires the dead surveyor's daughter — not despite who she is, understand, but *because* of it. Because if anyone could find the old markers, follow the old routes, reach whatever her father reached, it was the girl who grew up on his shoulders learning the water.

They hadn't hired a rescue. Rescues are for people you want back.

I sat in the ruined tent with my father's unsigned refusal in my lap and understood the job at last, the whole shape of it, top to bottom. I was hired to confirm nine deaths. To walk the ground, count the graves, verify that the problem had solved itself. And to recover Voss's documents — not to preserve them, not to deliver them to grieving families or honest officials, but to collect them, the way you collect shell casings after an execution. Every page Voss wrote was a page that could contradict the official story. The official story, I'd have bet the contents of my empty pockets, was already written: expedition lost to the river. Flood, maybe. The Throat takes people; everyone knows the Throat takes people. Ask the disgraced Hartwell woman — she'll confirm it, she knows that water better than anyone, her own father drowned there, poor creature, it's practically hereditary.

And if I found survivors? Well. Survivors complicate narratives. I thought of Tomás in the hollow tree, burning with fever, begging me not to take him downriver — and I had read that as delirium, trauma, a man afraid of the water that killed his crew. Maybe it was. Or maybe he'd seen exactly who killed them, and knew that downriver meant back into those hands.

Nine men. One shallow grave. Slashed tents, smashed radio — no flood does that. Floods don't slash canvas. Floods don't execute a radio. Men with rifles do, men whose paychecks say Consorcio Vertiente on them even if the paychecks themselves are burned monthly, and among those men, according to Voss's own declining handwriting, a security chief named Rennick who asked questions about why the survey was extended and did not accept the answer.

Rennick. I filed the name where I file names I expect to need later, in the drawer next to grudges and exit routes.

* * *

The satellite phone rang.

It sat there in the open case, gray and ugly, and it buzzed against the aluminum like something trapped, and the screen lit up with no caller ID because satellite phones don't bother with courtesy. I looked at it for three full rings. Long enough to know who it was. Who else calls a dead man's phone six weeks after the camp went silent, except the person waiting to hear whether the erasure was complete?

Salcedo. Or whoever Salcedo reported to, which was probably nobody I'd ever see, sitting in an office I'd never find, above Salcedo the way Salcedo was above me, the whole apparatus stacked like the terraces Voss described above the falls — each level holding up the one above it, all of it built by hands whose names nobody recorded.

Ring four. Ring five.

Here's what I should have done, by any professional logic: answered it. Said *camp located, all deceased, documents recovered, returning downriver.* Collected the second payment. Gone home to La Paz, paid the clinic, bought a bottle, buried the whole thing in the cellar of myself alongside everything else from this river. That was the job. That was what the money was for. Confirm the deaths. Recover the paper. Be the last pair of eyes and then close them.

Ring seven.

But here's what I actually heard, under the buzzing, under the river and the oil-can bird and Tomás's fever-muttering drifting faintly down the slope: my father's pencil. *Will not certify.* Four words, nineteen years old, in a margin, costing him everything, buying him nothing except the one thing I'd spent my whole career failing to understand was worth anything — which is that he knew what the paper said, and he would not make his hand agree with it.

He hadn't stopped running either, mind. He drank, he drifted, he took bad jobs from worse men, he died on a river he knew better than his own heartbeat. He was no saint. I inherited his stubbornness without his excuses and I've made a fine mess of both. But he drew one line, one time, in graphite, and the line held longer than he did.

Ring ten. The voicemail chime, soft and idiotic.

I picked up the phone, and I did not listen to the message. Some part of me — the part that keeps reading, keeps walking, keeps taking the job — wanted to. Intelligence is intelligence, and knowing what Salcedo expected might keep me alive. But listening meant the phone would show as accessed, timestamps logged somewhere, a small electronic confession that Zephyrine Hartwell was here, curious, reachable. And I was done being reachable.

I powered it off instead. Then I took the battery out, and the SIM, and dropped both into the river below the bank, where they sank with the efficiency of all things that trust gravity. The handset itself I wrapped in the tent scrap and stowed in my pack — dead weight, literally, but plastic and aluminum leave no fingerprints and might, in a pinch, convince someone at a distance that the recovery was proceeding on schedule. I'm not above theater. Theater has fed me for years.

The ledger and the file I kept. Voss's duplicate survey, wherever it was, was presumably upriver toward the cache — toward my father's markers, toward the causeway, toward whatever the company needed handled quietly. Which meant the documents everyone wanted buried and the truth everyone wanted dead were both in the same direction, and I was going to walk toward them carrying the only copies that mattered.

Which raised the question of what, precisely, I intended to do with them. Deliver them to Lima and watch them vanish into the same machine that forged the claim? Keep them and spend the rest of my life as a woman in possession of paper worth killing for, which is a life expectancy measurable in weather? Burn them and let the valley keep its secret, and my father keep his silence, and Salcedo keep his money, and every erased man stay erased?

I didn't know. I sat in the dead man's tent holding the dead man's ledger and I did not know, and for the first time in years that didn't feel like failure. It felt like honesty. The plan — *find them, confirm it, bring back the paper* — was dead here with the rest of the camp. What was left was upstream, and upstream was the Throat, and past the Throat was a valley that had killed my father for knowing too much and would not hesitate to do the same for his daughter.

Above me, on the slope, a stick cracked.

I was on my feet with the machete out before the sound finished, which is the one reliable inheritance my father left me: reflexes. But it was only Tomás, come stumbling down through the trees with the blanket around his shoulders like a ghost who'd lost his haunting, the brass theodolite clutched to his chest, his eyes too bright with fever and fixed on me with terrible clarity.

"You read it," he said. Not a question. His voice was wrecked, sanded down to the grain. "The ledger. You read what he wrote."

"Sit down before you fall down," I said.

"They're not done." He let me steer him onto a supply crate, shaking, gripping my wrist hard enough to bruise. "Señorita — Zephyrine — they're not done up there. Rennick kept men at the narrows. Whatever Voss found, they're still guarding it, and when you don't report in—" His eyes went to my pack, to the shape of the dead phone under the canvas, and understanding moved across his face like weather. "You didn't answer."

"No," I said. "I didn't."

He nodded slowly, and something in him settled, the way a rope settles when the load finally shifts to a point that will hold. "Then we go up," he said. "Before they come down."

The river ran north behind us, toward the world, toward payment and safety and the story the company had already written. Ahead it ran into the throat of the escarpment, into the noise of the falls, into everything my father had refused to sign away.

I shouldered the pack with the ledger in it, and we started climbing.
