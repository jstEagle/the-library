## 6. Upstream

The trail died at a bend in the river where the jungle closed like a fist, and I stood there looking at the water and thinking about all the sensible things I could still do.

Go back downriver. Report the camp, the slashed tents, the shallow grave. Collect whatever the fixer was paying for confirmation of deaths — because that's what it was, wasn't it? Nobody hires a blacklisted guide to rescue people. They hire her to count bodies and bring back paper. I'd known that since the satellite phone rang in my pocket at the last camp and I'd let it ring out like a church bell tolling for someone else.

Tomás stood beside me, swaying on his feet, fever-bright eyes fixed on the current. "They'll come up the trail," he said. "Voss's radio check-ins stopped four days ago. The company will have noticed."

"The company noticed before Voss did," I said. "The question is how fast they move."

"Rennick moves fast."

I looked at him. He was holding the brass theodolite against his chest like an infant, both arms wrapped around it, and he had the particular gray-green color of a man whose body has decided to keep going out of spite rather than health. "You know Rennick?"

"I've seen him twice. Once in Lima, once at the airstrip when we flew in." He swallowed. "He didn't introduce himself. He didn't need to. Men like him don't introduce themselves to surveyors."

Men like him hadn't introduced themselves to me either, back when I was still employable and still stupid enough to think a signature on a contract meant anything. I crouched at the water's edge and tested the current with two fingers, cold and fast and carrying more volume than it had been carrying yesterday, which meant rain somewhere upstream, which meant the rapids above the bend were going to be worse than the ones we'd run coming in.

"We can't go back down," Tomás said. It wasn't a question. He'd begged me not to take him downriver, back in the hollow tree, and I'd told him I wasn't taking him anywhere, which had been true then and was becoming less true by the hour.

"No," I said. "We can't."

"So we go up."

"Up is Devil's Throat." I said it flatly, the way you'd say *the water is wet*, because saying it any other way would let the fear have a shape. "No guide goes up there. No map goes up there. The Ashaninka will stop us if we're lucky and the river will stop us if we're not."

"My father fished this river," Tomás said quietly. "Before the companies came. He said the water remembers where it's been."

"That's very poetic for a man who's about to be sick over the side of a raft."

He almost smiled. It was the first time I'd seen his face do anything but beg or burn, and I noted it the way you note a bridge that might hold your weight: with interest, without trust.

* * *

There were three balsa logs lashed together with liana at the old logging camp half a kilometer downstream — a camp the company had abandoned years ago and never bothered to erase, because companies don't erase things, they just stop looking at them. The raft was rotten in one corner and buoyant everywhere else, which is more than you can say for most expeditions I've worked.

I spent an hour re-lashing the bindings while Tomás sat against a stump and talked, because talking seemed to keep the fever from climbing. He told me about Voss finding the causeway. He told me about the men with company rifles turning around on the trail. He told it in fragments, the way fever tells things, and every fragment made the water ahead look colder.

"They shot Esteban first," he said. "Because Esteban argued. Voss told everyone to drop their packs and walk back down, and Rennick's men just... started. Like it was already decided. Like it was paperwork."

"Paperwork," I repeated.

"There were forms. In Voss's case. Land claim documents with a signature block already printed. Hartwell, E., senior surveyor." His eyes came up to mine, careful now. "That's your father's name."

I tied off a knot harder than the knot needed. "My father's been dead twenty years."

"Yes."

"And his name is on a land claim for territory he never surveyed."

"That's what the forms said."

I sat back on my heels and looked at the river. Somewhere up there, past the rapids and the gorge and the escarpment no map would admit to, was a cache of papers with my father's initials burned into them, and somewhere behind me was a man named Rennick coming up a trail to make sure those papers stayed lost. And in between, apparently, was me — hired to confirm the deaths, retrieve the documents, and close the file on a forgery wearing my family's name.

"You could still walk away," Tomás said. "Downriver. Take the money."

"The money's blood money and the man paying it wants me dead once I'm done counting." I stood and brushed off my hands. "Besides, I'm a terrible walker. I'm much better on water."

* * *

We launched at first light, which is to say we launched into fog so thick the river disappeared three meters from the bow and the only sound in the world was water and our own breathing.

I took the stern paddle because I'm better at it and because steering means you get to pretend you're in control. Tomás took the bow, too weak to do much but too proud to say so, and for the first hour we made decent time — the river running wide and brown-green through country that got steeper on both banks, the walls closing in by degrees until we were paddling through a corridor of stone and green with the sky reduced to a bright ribbon overhead.

The first rapid announced itself as a sound before it was a sight: a low continuous roar, like something enormous breathing under the rocks. I backed water, read the line — left of center, past a drowned tree, then hard right where the current folded against the cliff — and shouted it to Tomás, who turned and looked at me with the expression of a man being asked to translate poetry during an earthquake.

"Just paddle when I say paddle!" I yelled.

"I don't know what I'm doing!"

"Nobody does! That's the whole secret!"

Then the river took us, and the secret stopped mattering. The raft dropped, rose, slammed sideways off a wave that hit like a shoulder-check, and for ten seconds there was nothing in the universe except white water and the animal problem of staying aboard. Tomás screamed something in Quechua that I chose to interpret as encouragement. We shot through the gap past the drowned tree with a hand's width to spare on either side, wallowed in the eddy below, and spun slowly in a circle while the fog closed back around us.

Tomás was soaked, gasping, gripping the gunwale with both hands. "You've done this before."

"Once or twice."

"You're laughing."

"I'm not laughing."

"You're absolutely laughing."

I checked my own face and found he was right, which annoyed me. "Adrenaline," I said. "It presents differently in some people. Paddle."

* * *

We ran six rapids that day, and by the fourth we'd stopped being two strangers strapped to the same bad idea and started being something else — not a team, exactly, nothing so flattering, but a pair of organisms that had discovered they functioned better in coordination than alone. Tomás learned to read my weight shifts before I called the lines. I learned that when he went quiet and gripped the theodolite case between his feet, it meant he'd spotted something on the bank worth worrying about.

It was Tomás who spotted the smoke.

Late afternoon, the fog long burned off, the sun cutting low and orange down the corridor of the gorge. He raised one hand — not pointing, just lifting it, the way you signal *stop* when shouting would cost too much — and I back-paddled into the shadow of an overhang while he nodded toward the far bank.

Smoke. Thin, deliberate, too clean to be a hunter's fire. Someone burning green wood to make themselves visible, or to cook for more than two.

"Company?" I breathed.

"Could be Ashaninka."

"Ashaninka don't burn green wood. They don't advertise." I studied the bank, the way the jungle came down to the water, the single narrow break in the vegetation that might be a trail or might be nothing. "That's a signal fire. Somebody wants to be found."

"Or wants us to think they want to be found."

"You're getting good at this."

"I had a good teacher for about four days before she tried to kill him." He said it without heat, and I let it stand, because it was true and because an apology would have cost us both more than we had.

We portaged around the next bend and made camp on a gravel bar in the lee of a fallen cedar, cold food and no fire, the raft hauled up and flipped. I set no watch because neither of us would sleep anyway; instead we lay on our backs watching the ribbon of sky go purple, and Tomás talked again, quieter now, the fever breaking into something more like clarity.

"When they turned on us," he said, "Voss grabbed me and shoved me into the brush. He said *run upriver, find the Hartwell marks, follow the Hartwell marks.* I thought he was delirious. But he kept saying it. *Hartwell knew. Hartwell always knew.*"

"Hartwell always knew," I repeated. My father's favorite phrase, delivered at the dinner table with coffee and certainty while my mother rolled her eyes. *Hartwell always knew, mija. The trick is knowing what he knew.*

"Do you know what he meant?"

"I think," I said slowly, "that my father found whatever's up there before any of us did. And I think he refused to sign his name to it. And I think that refusal killed him." I turned my head to look at Tomás. "And I think somebody has spent twenty years making sure nobody ever reads what he actually wrote."

Tomás was quiet for a long moment. Then: "So we read it."

"So we read it."

* * *

They came at dawn, or I should say their sound came at dawn — outboard motors, two of them, grinding upriver against the current with the patience of machinery, and I woke with my hand already on the paddle and my heart going like a bird in a box.

Through the trees I could see them: a long aluminum boat, camo canvas, four men, and at the bow a figure standing braced against the wake with the stillness of a man who owns whatever he's looking at. Even at distance I knew the type. Broad, deliberate, binoculars up, sweeping the banks section by section like a man reading a ledger.

Rennick.

"They're slow against the current," Tomás whispered beside me. "Maybe two kilometers an hour. We're faster on the water."

"On flat water. Not through what's ahead." I looked upriver, where the gorge walls were pinching together and the sound of the water had changed key — deeper, angrier, the voice of a river being squeezed. "There's a gorge section coming. Gorge sections mean portages. Portages mean we're on foot, and on foot we're slower than they are."

"So we don't portage."

"We run it."

He looked at me. Fever-flushed, exhausted, terrified, and underneath all of it something new — a surveyor's arithmetic, calculating odds the way he'd calculate coordinates. "What are the odds?"

"Of surviving the gorge?" I shouldered my pack and pushed the raft off the gravel bar. "Ask me after."

Behind us, faint over the water, came the grind of the outboards, patient as paperwork. Ahead, the walls closed and the river roared, and I dug the paddle in and thought, absurdly, of my father's voice across a dinner table twenty years gone:

*Hartwell always knew, mija.*

Time to find out what.
