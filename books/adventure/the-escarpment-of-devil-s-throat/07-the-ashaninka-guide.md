## 7. The Ashaninka Guide

The river told us we were being watched long before anyone stepped out of the trees.

It started with the birds. For two days the banks had been loud with them—parrots arguing, a heron standing on its one leg of judgment at every bend—but that morning the silence came down like a lid. Tomás noticed it first. He'd been dozing against the raft's cargo frame, fever-sweaty but mending, and his eyes snapped open with the particular alertness of a man whose body had learned to expect bad news.

"Zephyrine," he said quietly. "Listen."

I listened. Water on rock, water on wood, my own paddle dripping. Nothing else. In jungle terms, nothing else is the loudest sound there is.

"They've been following us since the narrows," I said. "I know."

"You *know*?"

"I counted four canoes yesterday, mostly hidden. This morning there were six." I kept paddling, easy strokes, no sudden movements. "They let us pass the first village because we were going away from it. Now we're not."

Tomás sat up too fast and winced, hand pressed to the healing wound in his side. "And you didn't think to mention this?"

"You were sleeping. You needed it more than you needed to panic."

"I wasn't going to panic."

"Everyone says that right before they panic."

He gave me a look—the look of a man recalibrating exactly how much trouble he'd tied himself to—and then, because he was Tomás, he did something useful instead of something dramatic. He checked the theodolite case was lashed down, checked our one dry bag, and checked me, head to toe, the way surveyors check instruments: for damage, for drift.

"We could put ashore," he offered. "Talk before they decide talking is over."

"Running ashore is how you get an arrow you never hear coming."

"So what's the plan?"

"The plan," I said, "is that I'm going to be very polite in terrible Quechua, and you're going to keep your hands where everyone can see them, and if any of this goes wrong, you're going to remember that I told you it might."

"That's not a plan, that's a eulogy."

"It's worked so far."

* * *

They took us at the bend below the black cliffs, where the river pinches and the current does half your steering whether you like it or not. Six canoes slid out of the shadowed bank in perfect silence—not a paddle creak, not a cough—and suddenly the water around us was full of men, and two boys who couldn't have been older than fifteen holding bows with the bored competence of teenagers handling familiar tools.

I shipped my paddle and raised both hands. "Tayta," I called, to the nearest elder—a broad man with a scarred jaw and eyes that went through me like a fisherman sizing up a hook. "*Allillanchu.* We come in peace. We are passing through."

My Quechua is, generously, functional. My father spoke it like a second mother tongue; I speak it like a tourist who studied hard on the plane. The words came out in the wrong order and half of them were probably Cusco forms that would mark me as an outsider even if everything else about me didn't.

The scarred man answered in a stream I caught maybe a third of. What I got: Who are you. Where from. Why here. Not questions so much as a net being drawn.

"Zephyrine Hartwell," I said, and tapped my own chest. "Guide. From—" I gestured downstream, toward Peru, toward the blacklist, toward everything. "We go upriver. To the escarpment. We ask only passage."

At the word *escarpment*—at the word *Devil's Throat*, which I said carefully, the way you say a name that belongs to someone dead—the whole flotilla went still. Even the current seemed to hush between the cliffs. One of the young guards shifted his bow, and the scarred man lifted two fingers without looking, and the boy froze.

Then came a rapid exchange among the elders, low and clipped, and I caught one word repeated twice, three times, with weight behind it: *Hartwell. Hartwell.*

Not my name. My father's.

My stomach dropped somewhere below the raft.

"They know him," Tomás whispered.

"Everyone on this river knows him," I whispered back. "That's either very good or very bad, and I genuinely don't know which."

The scarred man pointed upstream, then at us, then made a flat chopping gesture that needed no translation. Then he said something longer, and one word in it I understood perfectly, because it's the same in every language on every river I've ever worked:

*No.*

* * *

They didn't kill us. They beached us.

Our raft was towed—towed, with a competence that stung—to a village set back from the bank under old fig trees: perhaps forty houses, thatched, clean-swept paths, children who watched us with open fascination and dogs who watched us with none at all. Our gear was carried off with the same unhurried thoroughness, including, over Tomás's objection, the theodolite case.

"They're not stealing it," I murmured. "They're inventorying it."

"How can you tell?"

"Because if they were stealing it, they'd have taken the dry bag first. That's where anything worth money is."

We were given a bench outside the largest house, and cassava drink in a gourd, and left alone for the better part of an hour while the village conducted its deliberations at a volume calibrated to make sure we heard every disagreement. I sipped the drink—it was good, faintly sweet—and tried to run through my Quechua vocabulary like a deck of cards, discarding the ones I wasn't sure of.

Tomás fidgeted. "What are they deciding?"

"Whether we're liars, spies, or just lost. Possibly all three."

"And if spies?"

"Then we'll find out how far from help we actually are."

A boy of maybe ten wandered over during all this, emboldened by the universal courage of children, and stared at Tomás's fever-cracked lips, then at me, then asked a question I caught roughly half of. Something about my hair. I answered in Spanish, he answered in Quechua, and we had a perfectly pleasant conversation in which neither of us understood the other, which is more than I managed with most adults.

Then the crowd by the big house parted, and she came out.

She was small—that registered first, small the way river stones are small, worn dense by decades—and she walked without hurry past men a foot taller who made room for her the way water makes room for the keel. Grey braid to her waist. A red woven band at her wrist. She stopped in front of us and looked at me for a long moment, and I want to say I felt weighed, but it was less than that and more: I felt *read*. Like a page someone had already formed an opinion about.

She spoke in Quechua, slow, deliberate, each word placed like a footing stone. I caught fragments. *You come upriver. Toward the Throat. With a sick man and a dead man's tools.*

I looked at the theodolite case, sitting apart with the other confiscated gear. "It's my father's," I said, in Spanish, then dragged the Quechua after it as best I could. "*Taytaypa.* My father's."

"*Piru?*" she said. Whose father?

"Hartwell," I said. "Elias Hartwell. *Taytay.*"

The name went through the courtyard like a stone through still water.

I don't mean people gasped. I mean the quality of the listening changed. Every conversation within earshot bent toward us without appearing to move. The scarred guard's jaw tightened. And Naira—the elder, though I didn't yet know her name—went very still, in the way of someone hearing a voice they'd buried years ago and assumed was gone.

She said something short to the scarred man. He objected. She said it again, with a finality that ended careers, and he subsided. Then she turned back to me and switched to Spanish—fluent, accented, better than mine would ever be in Quechua—and said:

"Elias Hartwell had a daughter?"

"Yes."

"He said he had a daughter. Twenty years ago, he said it. He showed us a photograph, a girl on a horse, making a face." Her mouth did something that was almost a smile and decided against it. "Is that you?"

"It's me."

She looked at me a moment longer, and whatever she found there, she didn't share it. "Sit," she said. "Drink. Your friend looks like a drowned cat. Then you will answer questions, and I will decide if you are your father's daughter or merely his blood."

* * *

Her house was cool and dim and smelled of smoke and dried fish. She was Naira; she said it plainly, offering her hand, and her grip was stronger than mine. Tomás sat propped in a corner looking like he wanted to take notes and knew better. She poured three gourds of the cassava drink herself, which I gathered from the way the scarred man's eyebrows climbed was either an honor or a reprimand, possibly both.

"Now," she said. "Tell me why Elias Hartwell's daughter comes upriver with a stolen company raft, a sick surveyor, and a theodolite twenty years old, toward a place no one is permitted to go."

So I told her. Most of it. The silent expedition, the slashed tents, the shallow grave, Tomás in the hollow tree, Voss finding something above the gorge, the rifles turning inward, the satellite phone ringing out unanswered in my pocket. I gave her the shape of it without the coordinates—I wasn't a fool—and watched her face while I talked. She didn't interrupt once. When I finished she was quiet for a long time, turning her gourd slowly in her hands.

"Voss," she said finally. "The leader. He came downriver last year. Asked questions about the old terraces above the falls. We told him nothing. He smiled too much." She looked up. "Your father also smiled too much. But when he asked questions, he wrote down our answers correctly."

"I don't understand."

"No," she agreed. "You wouldn't. Sit. Listen instead."

She told it plainly, the way old people tell things they've decided not to be bitter about anymore, which is its own kind of bitterness. Twenty-two years ago, a stream of men had come mapping the upper river—company men, government men, always with papers, always with lines to draw. They drew the Ashaninka lands as empty. Uninhabited. *Available.* Their maps went to Lima, and Lima's maps went to desks, and desks issued concessions, and concessions brought chainsaws. It was, she said, a kind of weather. You learned to live under it or you moved.

"Then came a gringo who camped three months and asked to be taught the river properly. Not shown—*taught*. He paid attention. He marked our villages on his maps. Our fields, our fishing camps, our burial places. He drew the people onto the land, which no mapmaker had done in living memory." Her eyes came up to mine. "When the others' maps erased us, his maps named us. Do you understand what that is worth? A line on paper is a wall. His walls protected us for twenty years."

I thought of my father's survey kit in my memory: the brass, the canvas, the smell of pencil shavings and river mud. I thought of the legend I'd built my whole disreputable life on top of—the great guide, the honest mapper, the man the river killed. I had never once thought of him as a wall. Walls hold things up. Nobody thanks them; they just lean.

"He died up there," I said. "In the gorge."

"He died up there," she said, "because he refused to sign what they wanted signed. We have known this a long time. We knew it before your companies knew it, before your governments bothered to know it. An honest map is a dangerous document, Zephyrine Hartwell. Men have killed for less accurate paper."

The words landed in me with a weight I hadn't braced for. All my life I'd carried his name like luggage—sometimes proudly, sometimes as the thing that got me blacklisted, sometimes as the reason I couldn't stop moving. I had never once considered that other people had been carrying it too. That somewhere upriver, a woman now old had spent two decades leaning on a wall my father built, and that wall was about to be tested again, and here came his daughter drifting into range like loose cargo.

"There's a claim," I said. "Forged. It has his signature on it—or a copy of it. It says he surveyed the gorge and found nothing sacred, nothing living, and recommended it for extraction. If that paper reaches the right desks—"

"—the wall comes down," Naira said. "Yes."

"And I was hired by the people holding it. Or their cousins. I think—" I stopped. Said it plainly, because she'd earned plainness. "I think I was hired to confirm the bodies and bury the truth. I think I'm a loose end that doesn't know it's frayed yet."

Naira nodded, unsurprised, unsurprisable. "And now?"

"Now I'd like to see what my father saw. And read what he wrote. And then decide who I burn and who I save."

Something flickered across her face at that—not warmth exactly, but the recognition of a familiar animal. "You sound like him," she said. "He also talked about burning things. Papers, mostly. He was very disappointed by how often he couldn't."

Despite everything, I laughed. It came out cracked and surprised, and Tomás looked at me like I'd spoken in tongues.

Naira set down her gourd. "There is a price," she said. "There is always a price. I will take you to the escarpment. I will walk you to the place your father carved his stair, if it still stands. But you will swear something first, and I will not bargain on it, and neither will you."

"What thing?"

"That whatever his papers say—whatever treasure, whatever city, whatever proof of his innocence sleeps up there—the valley stays unclaimed. By companies. By governments. By you." She leaned forward, and her eyes were the exact grey-brown of the river in flood. "Swear it on his name, since that is the only thing you seem to own."

And there it was. The whole trap of my life laid out on a woven mat between two gourds of cassava drink: my father's name, which was the only currency I'd ever had, and which I'd have to spend to buy the truth about my father's name. Swear the valley away, and I might learn he was innocent. Refuse, and I'd stay loyal to a legend that might be a forgery wearing his handwriting.

Tomás shifted in his corner. "Zeph," he said quietly. "Whatever you answer—answer it true. She'll know."

I looked at Naira. She waited the way rivers wait, which is to say completely, and forever if necessary.

"My father mapped your people honestly," I said slowly, "when every other map lied. That's the inheritance. Not the legend. The honesty." I swallowed. "If I swear, I swear it all the way. The valley stays yours. Whatever the papers say. Whoever asks. Including me."

For the first time, Naira smiled fully, and it rearranged her whole face into someone I suspected had once been terrifyingly beautiful and remained terrifying in every other respect.

"Good," she said. "Now the harder part." She rose, joints cracking like green wood, and nodded toward the door, toward the river, toward the black cliffs waiting upstream. "Tomorrow we leave at first light. Tonight, you eat, your friend sleeps, and I teach you the real names of the water—because the maps, daughter of Elias, only ever tell you half of what a river is."

She paused at the doorway, silhouetted against the evening gold.

"And Zephyrine. There is something else you should know before we climb. Your father did not die alone up there." Her voice dropped, gentle and grim. "Someone was with him. Someone who is still alive. And she has been waiting twenty years to meet his daughter."
