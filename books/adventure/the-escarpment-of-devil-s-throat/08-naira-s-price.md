## 8. Naira's Price

The river gives and the river takes, and in sixty-eight years I have learned that the taking is always more honest than the giving. What a man carries to the water tells you everything. The dead ones come down floating with their arms open, having finally surrendered everything at once. The living ones come down clutching, and you can read their whole lives in what they refuse to let go of.

The Hartwell woman came down our river clutching a stranger.

We had watched them from the bank for half a day before we took them — my grandsons and I, from the shadows under the ceiba roots where the current slows and the water goes green as old bottle glass. Strangers on this stretch of the Ene do not pass unwatched. They never have. The gorge upstream has swallowed enough of them that we keep count the way a farmer keeps count of his lost sheep: not out of love, but because the arithmetic matters.

She had a raft lashed together from driftwood and stolen rope, which told me she was either desperate or competent, and by the way she read the water — leaning her weight before the rapids came, not after — I decided she was both. Desperate competence. It runs in certain families like bad eyesight.

And the man she carried was feverish, shivering under a plastic sheet, mumbling about causeways. Tomás Quispe. We knew his name before he gave it, because his uncle had come through two seasons ago asking after him, and we had told the uncle nothing, because the uncle had come with a company man wearing clean boots, and clean boots on the Ene mean one thing only: someone upstream wants something that isn't theirs.

So when my grandson Ruwi stepped onto the bank with his blowgun held casually, the way you hold a thing you have used and would use again, the woman did not run. That interested me. Most of them run. Running is what they come here to do, most of them — running from debts, from husbands, from governments, from their own names. She put her hands up instead and said, in Quechua that had been chewed by Spanish and spat back out, "We're not looking for trouble."

"Nobody is," I said. "Trouble finds its own way. You are looking for the escarpment."

I did not ask it as a question. Questions invite lies, and lies take time, and I am an old woman with a limited supply of both patience and afternoon.

* * *

They sat in the shade of the cooking shelter while my daughters fed them — fish, plantain, the bitter tea we brew against fever. The Quispe boy ate like a man who had forgotten food existed and was rediscovering it with religious feeling. The woman ate less, watching me over her bowl the way a cat watches a dog it hasn't yet decided about.

"You knew my father," she said. Not asked. Said, the way you set a stone on a table and wait to see if it rolls off.

"I knew of him," I said. "Hartwell. The mapper." I let the word sit. "There were many men with instruments who came through here. Some drew our land as empty, so that paper could be laid over it like a sheet over a face. Some drew it rich, so that men downstream would come and make us poor properly, with documents. Your father drew it true."

Her jaw tightened. "You say that like there's a 'but' coming."

"There is always a 'but' coming. That is what a river is — a 'but' between two mountains." I drank my tea. "He mapped honestly. He also mapped completely. Do you understand the difference? An honest map can be a gift. A complete map is a weapon waiting for someone to pick it up. He made us a gift and left a weapon in the same house, and then he went downriver and died, and we have spent twenty years wondering which one the river kept."

"He didn't die on the river," she said quietly. "Not the way people think."

"No," I agreed. "The way people think is usually wrong. That is why we don't use it for anything important."

Ruwi snorted from across the fire. My grandson laughs at everything I say, which is either respect or insolence, and after twenty years I have stopped investigating which.

* * *

That night I went down to the water alone, as I do, and I thought about what the woman wanted, because wanting is the most dangerous cargo anyone brings up this river, heavier than rifles, harder to unload.

She wanted the escarpment. Of course she did. They all want the escarpment eventually — the ones who come asking after gold, after ruins, after their fathers, after themselves. The gorge is a door and everyone believes they are the key. But behind this particular door is not treasure. Behind it is a promise, and promises are the only wealth my people have ever successfully defended, mostly because no one else wants them.

Behind the door is also the city. I will say the word plainly here, in my own head, where it is safe: the city. The terraces above the falls, the roads that go nowhere because everywhere they went is still here, the reservoirs cut into stone by hands that were dust a thousand years before Pizarro learned to write his name. Our elders called it the place the river keeps. Not hidden — kept. There is a difference. Hidden things are waiting to be found. Kept things are being cared for.

The company men who came last season — Voss's men, though I did not know the name then, only the boots and the rifles and the way they looked at our women the way you look at furniture you intend to buy cheap — those men found the door because a boy showed them, a boy who had been paid in a transistor radio. The radio lasted eleven days. What followed has not stopped lasting.

So now there was a survivor sitting by my fire, and a daughter walking my banks, and somewhere downstream a machine of men grinding slowly toward the throat of the world, and every path led through my decision, because that is what age is: the point at which other people's emergencies become your errands.

I thought about Hartwell senior. Twenty years ago, a tall man with sunburned forearms and a notebook he guarded like a child, who sat where the daughter was sitting and ate fish with his fingers because he said forks were a rumor. He asked me once, in his terrible Quechua, whether I believed maps could lie. I told him maps cannot lie; only mappers can, and only when someone is paying. He laughed until he coughed. Then he said, "Nobody's paying me for this one," and I believed him, and I have been paying for believing him ever since, in small installments of worry.

Because honest or not, he drew the door. And a drawn door is ajar forever.

* * *

In the morning I found her at the bank, checking the raft's lashings, doing the work of three men badly rather than the work of one man well, which is its own kind of honesty. The Quispe boy slept on. Fever burns a body like a field burn — some of it must be allowed, or nothing grows back.

"You'll go without me if I say no," I said.

It was not a question either. She didn't insult me by pretending otherwise. "Yes."

"And you'll die doing it, probably, and the boy with you, and the papers your father left will rot in the gorge with your bones, and the company will send better-prepared men next year, and the year after, until someone comes who is both lucky and wicked, and then the valley will be a mine with a museum attached, and our grandchildren will sell tickets to the door their grandmothers kept." I turned and looked at her fully. "This is what happens when guides stop guiding. Everything downstream floats up."

"What do you want from me?" she asked. No anger in it. Tiredness. The tiredness of a woman who has been negotiating since birth and knows the opening position when she hears it.

Good. Anger I could have handled. Tiredness meant she was listening.

"An oath," I said.

"Name it."

"Not so fast. First understand what you'd be swearing." I sat down on a stone, because this part deserved gravity and my knees supply it naturally. "Your father left papers up there. I know it the way I know the river bends — not because I've seen them, but because everything about him pointed upward and nothing ever came back down. Survey notes. Maps. Perhaps a claim, perhaps a refusal of a claim, perhaps a letter. Whatever they are, they are worth killing for — men have already died proving the price. So hear the oath: whatever those papers say, whoever they name, however they flatter your father or indict him — the valley stays unclaimed. Unowned. Unwritten. Not yours, not mine, not the company's, not Peru's. If his papers give the city to his daughter, you burn them. If they give it to the Ashaninka, we bury them deeper. If they prove he was a saint, the saint stays secret. If they prove he was a liar—" I paused, because this was the blade of it, and I am old enough to enjoy placing a blade gently rather than swinging it, "—then you carry that truth home alone, and the valley still stays secret, and you live with both."

The silence lasted long enough for the river to say several things.

"That's not an oath," she said finally. "That's a trap with a ribbon on it."

"It is a price," I said. "Traps catch the unwilling. Prices are paid by people who want something. Which are you?"

She looked upstream. I have watched many faces look upstream at that gorge — greed looks like hunger, fear looks like arithmetic, madness looks like joy. Hers looked like grief trying to remember which pocket it had put its keys in.

"My whole life," she said, "is built on him. On what he was. Every job I ever got, every trail I ever walked, every time someone said 'Hartwell's daughter' and meant *the real thing*, the honest guide, the last of a dying breed — I was spending money he earned. And you're telling me I have to hand it all over blind. Sign away the inheritance before I open the envelope."

"Yes."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I feed you, mend your raft, and point you at the river, and you go and die honestly, and I will mourn you properly, because you'll have died for love, which is stupid but respectable." I stood, joints announcing themselves. "Or you swear, and you go and maybe live, and the valley stays a valley. I am offering you the worse bargain for yourself and the better one for the world. This is the only kind of bargain I know how to offer. It is why I am not rich."

A heron took off from the shallows, all elbows. Somewhere behind us the Quispe boy cried out in his fever — a word, half a word, *causeway* — and I saw her flinch toward the sound and master herself, and I thought: there it is. There is the hinge. Not the father. The boy. Love makes liars of us all, but it makes oath-keepers of a few, and the few are recognizable by exactly this — the flinch, mastered.

"If his papers name me," she said slowly, "if he left the city to me, to his blood — and I burn that—"

"Then you will be the richest poor woman on this river," I said, "and the only one whose wealth no one can steal, because it consists entirely of a thing that continues to exist."

She laughed. It surprised her; I could see that. A short, cracked laugh, the kind that comes out of people when a weight shifts rather than lifts.

"You're enjoying this," she accused.

"Immensely. Do you know how rarely anyone comes up this river offering me a chance to be right? Usually I am merely old. Today I may be *correct*. It is nearly Christmas."

"It's June."

"Then it is nearly June. Even better."

* * *

But I will confess what I did not confess to her, since a narrator who confesses nothing is just a liar with stamina.

I hesitated too.

That night, before I named my price, I had gone to the water and asked my mother's mother's question, the one we ask the river when the choice is too large for a head: *is this mine to decide?* And the river, as usual, answered with the answer to every question, which is *yes, everything is yours to decide, that is the terror of it.*

Because here is what I did not say: I remembered her father's hands. When he showed me his survey sheets, twenty years ago, careful as a man showing photographs of children, there had been a page he turned past quickly. Too quickly. I was younger then and let it go, and I have wondered about that page for two decades — whether it held the city's heart, its name, its exact coordinates, whether the weapon was already built and he was already carrying it, honest man, complete map, gift and grenade in the same satchel.

If I make her swear, and the papers damn him, I take from her the only god she has. I know what that costs. I buried my own gods young; the digging never quite heals over.

And if I let her go unsworn, and the papers crown him, she will come down that mountain with proof in her teeth and righteousness in her blood, and righteousness is the most flammable substance known to our species, and the valley will burn for it — politely, with documents, but burned all the same.

Either way I spend something I cannot replace. This is the whole of leadership, whatever the men downstream think it is: choosing which irreplaceable thing to spend, and then standing in the smoke saying you meant to.

So I chose the oath. Because a living granddaughter who has given up her legend is safer than a dead one who kept it, and because — I will admit this much into the water where no one hears — I liked her. She flinched and mastered it. She ate what she was given. She checked the lashings twice. These are the three tests, and she passed them without knowing she was being tested, which is the only way tests count.

* * *

Morning came gray and dripping. The Quispe boy could sit up, which improved his conversation from scripture to gossip. Ruwi brought the woman a fresh bandage for the gash on her arm and pretended not to notice when she winced, which is the Ashaninka manner of bedside care: total attention, total denial of it.

I stood before her at the bank, where the water could witness, because water remembers oaths longer than stone does. Stone erodes. Water just keeps the minutes.

"Say it after me," I said. "Whatever the papers say."

"Whatever the papers say."

"Whoever they name."

"Whoever they name."

"The valley stays unclaimed."

"The valley stays unclaimed." She paused. Her eyes went upstream once, to the white thread of the falls visible between the ridges, thin as a scar. Then back to me. "Unowned. Unwritten. Until the end of me and after it."

I had not included that last part. She had added it herself. I felt something move in my chest that I had assumed was finished moving years ago, the way you assume a room is empty and then hear breathing.

"So be it," I said. "Witnessed by the water and by Ruwi, who will tell everyone, because he tells everyone everything."

"Grandmother," Ruwi protested.

"You will. It is your gift." I picked up my staff. "Now listen, Hartwell's daughter, because the oath buys you passage, not safety. Above the third bend the river stops being a river and becomes an argument, and I am the only guide alive who knows the argument's full vocabulary. We leave when the boy can walk. Two days. You will carry rope, and you will carry silence — from here to the escarpment, your mouth stays shut about what you seek, even to me, especially to me. Walls have ears and jungles have walls."

She nodded. Then, being who she was, she pushed once more, because people who have spent their lives bargaining cannot help testing the counter one last time.

"And if his papers clear him? Prove everything? That he refused them, that he died protecting the valley — I still can't tell anyone?"

"You can tell the river," I said. "The river already knows. That is the terrible comfort of honesty: it needs no audience, only a witness, and the oldest one is free."

She almost smiled. Almost. With this woman, I suspected, *almost* was the whole landscape, and full smiles were distant mountains you heard about from travelers.

She turned to go check the raft again — of course she did, the lashings, always the lashings — and I watched her go, and I thought about doors, and keys, and the difference between keeping a thing and hiding it, and how a woman who swears away her inheritance might be the first person in twenty years to actually inherit something.

Then Ruwi touched my elbow, gentle, which he only does when the news is bad.

"Grandmother," he said. "Men on the lower bank. Boots. Rifles. Moving fast." He swallowed. "And they're not following the river."

"They're following *her*," I said, and the water carried the words upstream ahead of us, the way it carries everything, whether we swear it to or not.
