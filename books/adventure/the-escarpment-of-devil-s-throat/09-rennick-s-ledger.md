## 9. Rennick's Ledger

There are two kinds of men who keep ledgers. The first kind keeps them because they're honest and want proof of it. The second kind keeps them because paper is the only witness that never panics, never runs, and never has to be paid twice.

I am the second kind.

I sat in the company's forward camp below the escarpment with a kerosene lamp turned low and the file open on my knees, and I read it the way I read everything: twice, slowly, and looking for the sentence that will eventually hang somebody. There is always a sentence like that. Most men write their own nooses without ever feeling the rope go around their necks. They think they're signing contracts.

The file was thin for what it cost. That was the first thing I noticed, back in the Lima office, when Delgado handed it to me across a desk so polished I could see my own face looking back at me like a warning. Six pages of survey notes, photocopies of photocopies, a map annotated in a dead man's hand, and a single sheet of letterhead that was supposed to be the whole point of the operation. Twenty years of history compressed into a manila folder. You'd think an empire would keep better records. Empires don't. Empires keep *leverage*, and leverage doesn't need to be complete. It only needs to be enough.

I poured myself the last of the pisco and started at the beginning, because beginnings are where men lie least. By the end of a story everyone has an angle. At the beginning they're still just tired.

* * *

Elias Hartwell had been a good surveyor. That's not sentiment; that's arithmetic. I've read a hundred field books from a hundred men who came up this river thinking they owned it, and most of their measurements are garbage padded with optimism. Hartwell's numbers were clean. His triangulations closed. His elevations matched across seasons, which means he went back and checked himself, which means he cared about being right more than being fast. In my experience that kind of precision is either honesty or obsession, and in Hartwell's case the file suggested it was both.

He'd come up the river in '03 under contract to a consortium that no longer existed on paper — absorbed, renamed, dissolved, the usual shell game. His job was simple: establish whether the gorge above Devil's Throat contained mineral deposits worth the cost of building roads into country where roads go to die. Standard work. Men had been trying to put a value on that valley since the rubber barons, and the river had eaten every one of them, figuratively or otherwise.

Except Hartwell didn't find minerals. He found the causeway.

The field notes got strange after that entry. His handwriting changed — not deteriorated, exactly, but tightened, the way a man writes when he knows someone might read it someday and he wants to be understood. He described a stair cut into the limestone above the falls. Terraces. Reservoir walls fitted without mortar. A road running along the gorge rim toward country no satellite image had ever bothered to resolve, because satellites look for what they're told to look for and nobody had ever told them to look here.

And then, three pages later, the sentence that had kept me employed for the last four months:

*I have been asked to certify boundaries that do not exist. The claim as drafted places the entire escarpment within a concession line drawn in an office in Lima. This land belongs to no concession. It belongs to the people who never left it. I will not sign.*

He'd written it out twice, actually. Once in the field book, once as a formal refusal appended to the survey, witnessed by his own signature and nothing else. A man refusing to commit fraud, alone, upriver, twenty days from anywhere, with a company's money already spent on the assumption that he wouldn't refuse. I've seen that movie before. It always ends the same way. The only variable is how long the credits take to roll.

Hartwell died on the descent. Drowned, officially — swept at the lower rapids, body recovered downstream by Ashaninka fishermen, personal effects returned to a daughter in a border town. The report was thorough. Almost too thorough. Reports about accidents are usually sloppy, because nobody cares enough to be careful about a dead guide. This one had every date cross-referenced, every witness statement notarized. Somebody had built it. And you don't build a wall unless there's something behind it you don't want looked at.

I turned to the last document in the folder. The claim itself. Concession application, land description, boundary coordinates — and there, at the bottom, a certification of survey bearing Elias Hartwell's signature.

Dated eleven days after his death.

I set down my glass and looked at that forgery for a long time. Not because it surprised me. Because of how *good* it was. Whoever drew it had worked from Hartwell's actual hand — had practiced, probably, for weeks. The slant was right. The pressure was right. Even the little hitch in his terminal strokes, the way his letters leaned into each other like tired men leaning on a fence, was there. A lab would call it authentic. A lab might even believe it.

But a signature is a small thing to counterfeit compared to a life. Someone had taken a dead man's integrity and spent it, posthumously, to steal a valley from the people living in it. And then they'd needed the file to disappear, except they couldn't make it disappear entirely, because the original refusal existed in enough hands that destroying it outright would raise the exact questions they were trying to bury. So instead they'd done something cleverer. They'd let it survive — misfiled, forgotten, buried in a Lima archive under a consortium name that no longer existed — until such time as it became useful again.

Which was now. Which was why I was sitting under a kerosene lamp with wet boots and a rifle that had killed two men whose names I'd made a point of never learning.

* * *

Here is what the file did not contain, and what I had assembled myself, piece by piece, the way you assemble anything true in this business: patiently, and from things people said when they thought they were saying something else.

Voss knew. That was the part that had taken me longest to accept, and I pride myself on accepting ugly facts quickly. Marcus Voss hadn't stumbled onto the city the way the official narrative wanted — the brave expedition leader discovering what fate intended him to discover. Voss had been sent. The expedition's real orders weren't in any folder; they were in a conversation I'd had with Delgado over lunch in Miraflores, where the ceviche was excellent and the discretion was better. Confirm the site. Confirm the claim's legal foundation. Recover Hartwell's original refusal if it could be found, and destroy it. If Voss located the old man's cache — and Delgado believed, with the unshakable faith of a man who has never been upriver, that he would — the company would finally own the one document that could unravel twenty years of quiet acquisition.

Voss found the causeway inside nine days. Faster than expected. And then, according to the survivor — Quispe, junior surveyor, currently a fever in a hollow tree somewhere above me — Voss made a decision that wasn't in anyone's orders. Or maybe it was. Maybe the orders were always going to end where they ended, and Voss simply read them correctly while the rest of his crew read them as employment.

Six rifles turned on six colleagues at a stone causeway above the loudest waterfall in the Andes. Five dead. One gone into the green. And a radio smashed so thoroughly that whoever did it wasn't hiding a location. They were hiding the fact that there was nothing left to hide.

Then the company did what companies do. It waited. Let the silence ripen. And when the silence had aged properly, it hired a disgraced guide — blacklisted in two countries, broke, and carrying the same surname as the dead man whose signature anchored their forgery — to go upriver and confirm the deaths.

On paper, Zephyrine Hartwell was a search-and-recovery contractor. A pair of experienced eyes to locate remains, recover effects, close the file with a professional's credibility. Nobody questions a mountain guide's report from bad country. Guides are assumed to be honest the way priests are assumed to be celibate — the assumption does all the work.

In practice, she was three things, and I'd catalogued them in order of usefulness.

She was bait. If Tomás Quispe was alive upriver — and every instinct I had said he was, because frightened young men run uphill toward water and Quispe had grown up on this river — he would come to the name Hartwell the way a drowning man comes to a light. She was the one person in Peru he might trust faster than he'd run. We hadn't needed to tell her that. We hadn't needed to tell her anything. Her reputation did our recruiting for us.

She was leverage. This was the elegant part, and I'll admit I admired it even as I resented being handed its execution. If she confirmed the deaths and signed off on the recovery, the company gained a Hartwell's tacit endorsement of the official record — the daughter certifying the circumstances of the father's accident, twenty years later, same river, same rapids, same terrible luck. History rhyming on command. And if she refused, if she started asking questions, well. A disgraced guide trespassing in forbidden territory, armed, in the company of a deserter from a murdered expedition. Every word she spoke afterward would sound like exactly what it would be: the testimony of a woman with everything to gain from a different story. We wouldn't even need to discredit her. She arrived pre-discredited. Her father's blacklist had done that work a decade before she was born into its shadow.

And she was a loose end. Because Zephyrine Hartwell had one thing in this world that money couldn't buy and threats couldn't confiscate: she was the person Elias Hartwell's letter was addressed to. *My daughter, if she ever stops moving.* Delgado didn't know about the letter. I did — or I suspected it, the way you suspect a load-bearing wall behind bad plaster. The old man had been meticulous. Meticulous men leave instructions. And instructions addressed to family have a way of surfacing at funerals, in attics, in the bottom of seabags, at precisely the moment they become maximally inconvenient.

So: leverage and loose end. Both. The two conditions are not contradictory. They're sequential. Leverage first, while she's useful. Loose end after, when she isn't. I had watched that sequence play out enough times that I could almost set a watch by it, and the watch usually said *soon*.

* * *

The lamp guttered and I trimmed the wick, and outside the tent the river went on saying the only thing it has ever said, which is that it was here first and will be here last.

I want to be clear about something, since nobody else will ever be clear about it on my behalf. I don't enjoy this work. People assume men like me enjoy it — that we're monsters in boots, that we go home and sharpen our knives by candlelight. The truth is duller. I'm good at it, and being good at something is a trap that closes slowly enough that you mistake it for a career. I keep ledgers because ledgers are how I sleep. Every name, every date, every order received and executed, entered in a waterproof notebook in my own hand. If it's ever my turn to be the loose end, I intend for there to be a record. Not justice. Just a record. In this country those are different things, but the second one is at least possible.

Hartwell senior was in the ledger. Page one, in a sense — the refusal, the drowning, the forged signature, dated and cross-referenced. I hadn't killed him. I'd inherited him, the way I'd inherited everything else in this file: other men's decisions, aging in the dark, accruing interest.

His daughter would go in the ledger too, one way or another. I found I minded that less than I expected, and I examined why, because a man should know his own weather. The answer was simple. She'd taken the contract. Whatever happened next, she'd signed the same implicit agreement every one of us signs when we take a company's money up this river: that the river keeps what it takes, and the company keeps what it pays for, and neither of them gives change.

Still. I turned back to the field notes one more time, to the page where the handwriting changed, and I read the old man's words again — *this land belongs to the people who never left it* — and I felt the small cold draft that comes through a ledger when a figure refuses to balance. Because there it was, plain as print: the one document that could undo everything, and the one person alive with a blood claim to receive it, walking up my river right now with a defrocked archaeologist and a stolen theodolite, getting closer every hour.

Delgado wanted her managed. Rennick's method, per the standing instruction: confirm, contain, conclude. Three words that had meant the same thing in every operation I'd ever run, and everyone involved had agreed not to notice.

I closed the file. I took out my notebook and wrote the date, the camp position, and one line: *Subject proceeding upstream. Assets adequate. Window closing.* Then, after a moment, because a ledger is only as honest as its keeper:

*Note: she is her father's daughter. Plan accordingly.*

Outside, far up the gorge, thunder rolled — or the falls, or blasting, or a helicopter too distant to see. In this country you learn not to guess. You learn to wait, and to write down what actually happens, and to understand that sooner or later every account comes due.

I banked the lamp and checked my rifle, and began drafting the order I hoped I wouldn't have to give.

By morning, I suspected, I'd have to give it anyway.
