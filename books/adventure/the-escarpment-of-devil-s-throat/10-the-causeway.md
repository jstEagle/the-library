## 10. The Causeway

The rain stopped an hour before dawn, and in the silence that followed I heard the river change its mind.

We'd been climbing since first light—Naira ahead of us moving through the cloud forest like a rumor, Tomás behind me breathing in wet rags, and me somewhere in the middle doing what I'd done my whole life: counting steps, reading water, pretending the weight on my back was just gear. But it wasn't just gear. It hadn't been since the hollow tree. Somewhere above us, cut into rock no map admitted existed, was a stairway with my father's initials on it, and every switchback brought me closer to a conversation twenty years too late.

"You're walking like a woman going to a funeral," Tomás said.

"I am."

"Whose?"

I didn't answer. He didn't push. That was the thing about fevered surveyors—they ration their curiosity like a man rationing matches.

Naira stopped at a shelf of granite where the trees gave out and the mist came through sideways. She said something in Ashaninka, then translated into the Spanish we shared, which she spoke the way you'd handle someone else's tools—carefully, without affection. "The gorge begins below. The stair is above. My father's father said a white man made marks here that did not fade." She looked at me. "He said the man asked permission before he drew anything. That was new to us."

"Sounds like him," I said, and hated how much it sounded like hope.

* * *

We found the mark at midday, and I'll tell you honestly: I walked past it twice.

It wasn't hidden, exactly. It was *disguised as geology*—a seam of quartz veining across a boulder face, and threaded through the quartz, so fine you'd swear it was mineral inclusion, a line of chiseled ticks. Three long, two short. A rhythm. A signature.

My father used to tap it out on the kitchen table when he was thinking. Three long, two short, like a heartbeat that had decided to be stubborn. I was eight years old and I thought it was just a habit, the way some men drum their fingers. It wasn't a habit. It was a trailhead.

"Zephyrine." Tomás had gone very still beside the boulder. "Come look at this."

The ticks framed a hairline crack in the rock—not a crack. A joint. Someone had found the fault line in the stone and widened it by millimeters over who knows how many weeks, until a slab sat there looking like every other slab, waiting for a hand that knew to ask.

I put my palm flat against the granite. Cold came through it, and under the cold, faintly, the vibration of the falls somewhere below—the Devil's Throat clearing its throat.

"Ask," Naira said.

I looked at her.

"You heard me. Ask the mountain. Your father did."

So I stood there like a fool with my hand on a rock and said, out loud, in English because it was the only language I had left that wasn't broken, "Papá. It's Zeph. I'm coming up whether you like it or not."

Then I pulled. The slab swung inward on a pivot I felt more than saw, smooth as a drawer, and cold air breathed out of the dark smelling of stone and water and time.

Tomás made a sound that wasn't quite a word. Naira said nothing at all, but she touched the threshold with two fingers before stepping in, and I understood that this, too, was asking permission.

* * *

The stair went up inside the mountain.

Not a tunnel—a shaft, open in places to slivers of sky, so that we climbed by veins of gray light and the sound of our own breathing. And the steps themselves—God, the steps. Each one cut from living rock, each one faced, and on the face of nearly every tread, small and precise as a jeweler's work:

**E.H.**

Elias Hartwell. Chiseled into stone that would outlast every government that ever blacklisted him.

"He did this himself?" Tomás whispered. His voice bounced up the shaft and came back changed.

"With a star drill and a hammer, if I know him." I ran my thumb along one set of initials. The edges were sharp. Twenty years of jungle humidity should have softened them into suggestion. "These cuts are fresh."

"That's impossible."

"Nothing about my father was possible. That was his whole problem." I said it lightly. It landed like a blow anyway, and I felt it land, and kept climbing.

Here's what nobody tells you about grief with a long fuse: it doesn't detonate. It *excavates*. Every step up that stair was another meter down into me, past the blacklisting and the border towns and the cheap whiskey, down to a girl on a porch in Huaraz watching a man pack a survey kit and knowing—he knew, I think—that he wasn't coming back, and neither of us saying it because saying it would have made it real and we were both better liars than that.

Three long, two short. On every landing. Like a pulse. Like he'd known someone would come looking someday and wanted to leave a door open and a light on.

At the fourth landing the shaft opened onto a ledge, and the ledge opened onto the world.

* * *

Below us, the gorge.

I've guided the Cordillera Blanca, the Apurímac, the Tambopata in flood. I have stood on summits that make grown men cry. None of it prepared me for the Devil's Throat seen from above, from the side the maps refuse.

The river we'd rafted—the one that had tried to kill us twice—dropped into a slot so deep and narrow it looked poured rather than carved, a green-black ribbon vanishing into a throat of red rock. Mist rose out of it in columns, slow as smoke off a sacrifice. And rising out of the mist, catching the afternoon light—

Terraces. Walls. The corner of a structure that had no business existing, built of fitted stone so large and so seamless that my eyes kept trying to reclassify it as cliff face and failing.

"There," Tomás breathed. He had the brass theodolite clutched to his chest like a child. "That's the causeway. Voss found it from the far bank—I told you—and they made him stop looking. They made him—" His voice snagged. He swallowed. "They made all of us stop."

"We're not stopping," I said.

"No," he agreed, and there was something new in his voice, something that hadn't been in the hollow tree. Not courage exactly. Its cheaper cousin, the kind that shows up when running costs more than standing. "No, we're not."

Naira stood apart, facing the city, and she was singing. Low, half-spoken, a melody with no beginning I could find. When she finished she turned to me and her face was unreadable as weather.

"My people say the ones who built it went into the earth when the sicknesses came. That the river closed its mouth over them so no one could steal their bones." She pointed down. "Your father found the mouth of the river's secret. Now you must decide what a daughter does with a stolen thing."

"It's not stolen. He mapped it honestly."

"Honestly for whom?" she asked, and started walking.

* * *

The causeway itself was a mile of engineered miracle: a raised road of fitted stone running along the gorge rim, drainage channels still shedding water after five hundred abandoned years, stairs worn into shallow cups by feet that had been dust for centuries. We walked it in single file, and I kept thinking about the last survey crew that had walked it. Voss in front, excited, alive. Then men with company rifles turning around. Tomás running. The radio smashed. The grave dug too shallow.

And before them, twenty years earlier, my father, walking this same stone, tapping three-long-two-short, asking permission of a mountain.

Tomás stopped at a flat slab near the causeway's midpoint and knelt. "Here. This is where Voss's notes said the benchmark cluster was." He dug the survey notes from his jacket—water-stained, fever-warped, but legible, because surveyors write for the apocalypse—and spread them out.

I watched him work and understood, suddenly, why companies hire defrocked archaeologists and junior surveyors and disgraced guides. Because we're the ones who read the fine print. Because the men who sign the claims never learn to.

"Coordinates match," he murmured. "Benchmark E.H.-1 through E.H.-7. Elias Hartwell, original survey, dated—" He looked up. "Dated nineteen years ago. He surveyed the whole terrace system. Boundaries, reservoirs, the works. These aren't exploration notes, Zephyrine. This is a *cadastre*. A legal-grade property survey."

"So the company's claim—"

"Is built on his." Tomás's finger moved down the page, and I watched the color leave his face. "Except. Except, except, except. Look at the boundary description in the mining concession filing Rennick's people carried. I memorized it—it was my job to check the datum references." He recited it flatly, the way you recite something that has been sitting in your stomach for days: "'Commencing at the confluence of the eastern tributary, thence north-northwest along the divide—'"

"That's not this divide."

"That's not even this *watershed*. The claim describes ground forty kilometers south. Fertile river terraces. Ashaninka gardens." He looked up at me. "Zephyrine, the claim isn't for the city. The city can't be claimed—it's pre-Columbian, it's patrimony, it's untouchable. The claim is a *shell*. They used your father's discovery as cover to file title over indigenous farmland. The city was never the prize. The city was the *alibi*."

The wind came up the gorge and moved through the ruins below, and I swear the whole valley exhaled.

"They killed Voss's whole team," I said slowly, "to protect a lie about bean fields."

"They killed them because Voss figured out the coordinates didn't match. Same as I just did." Tomás folded the notes with hands that weren't quite steady. "And your father—"

"My father refused to sign." I said it before he could. I don't know how I knew it; I knew it the way you know a load-bearing wall is cracked. "Twenty years ago they handed him the same forged claim and asked him to put his stamp on it, and he wouldn't, and so they buried him in the paperwork instead of the ground. Blacklisted. Erased. 'Difficult.' And then they waited for him to die quietly, and when he did, they came back for his survey and dressed it in his credibility." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "They've been forging my father's signature for twenty years. The one honest man on that river."

Tomás touched the theodolite. Stamped into the brass: **E.H.** "This was his?"

"Theirs now. They gave it to the expedition. Probably thought it was poetic." I took it from him, weighed it. Warm brass, cold history. "Or careless. Careless is more their speed."

Naira had come back silently—she moved like that—and stood listening, and whatever she heard in our voices made her nod once, decision made.

"Tonight we sleep in the high houses," she said. "Tomorrow you see what your father protected. And then, Zephyrine Hartwell, you decide whose name it carries forward. His. Or theirs."

* * *

We made camp in the lee of a ruined gateway, and while Tomás slept—fever finally breaking, sweat cooling—I sat up with the survey notes and a headlamp and read my father's marginalia by the hour.

He wrote in pencil, small, in the margins, the way he talked: dry, exact, and only occasionally letting the knife show. Next to benchmark E.H.-4: *"Ashaninka planting grounds. Do NOT include in any concession perimeter. Told R. this to his face. He smiled."*

Next to the reservoir measurements: *"Whoever built this understood water better than Lima does."*

And on the final page, alone, no context, just four words that I read maybe thirty times:

*"Tell Zeph to stop."*

Stop running. Stop guiding. Stop paying his debts. I didn't know. Sitting on a causeway above a drowned civilization, with his initials fresh-cut in the stair behind me and his handwriting warm in my hands, I still didn't know. But I knew he'd written it to me, specifically, across twenty years and one grave too shallow, and that the mountain had held it for me the way mountains hold everything—without opinion, until someone asks.

Down in the gorge, the falls roared on, patient as a creditor.

And far downstream—so far I couldn't hear it, but I felt it the way you feel weather coming in a healed fracture—something else was moving upriver toward us. Men with rifles and a ledger. Men who believed the dead stay bought.

I closed the notes. Tomorrow, the city.

Tonight, my father's handwriting, and the growing certainty that whatever waited for us in those terraces, we were no longer the only ones climbing.
