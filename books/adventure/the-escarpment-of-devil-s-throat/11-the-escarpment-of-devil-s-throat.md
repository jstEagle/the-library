## 11. The Escarpment of Devil's Throat

The falls announced themselves a full hour before we saw them—a sound like the sky tearing along its seam, felt in the sternum before it reached the ears. Naira stopped at the head of the trail and put her hand flat against the rock, the way you'd quiet a horse, and I understood that we were being asked to enter something that had been listening for a very long time.

"Behind the water," she said in Quechua, and Tomás translated with his hands as much as his voice, still clumsy from fever and captivity. "The stair goes behind the falls."

Of course it did. My father had never once taken the direct route to anything in his life. I'd spent my childhood watching him refuse shortcuts on principle—*the long way teaches you what the short way hides*, he'd say, infuriatingly serene while I starved on some ridge he'd chosen for pedagogical reasons—and here was his masterpiece of indirection: a city hidden behind a curtain of falling river, entered through a passage carved where no sane person would look.

The spray hit us first, cold enough to hurt after days of sweat and river. Then the noise took everything else. It was impossible to think inside it, which I suspect was rather the point. You could not carry fear or scheming through that sound; it shook them loose like coins from a pocket. Naira went first, small and certain on wet stone, her lantern a smudge of gold against all that white violence. Tomás gripped my sleeve. I gripped back. Neither of us said anything, because nothing could be heard, and there is a particular intimacy in being unable to speak to someone—you have to decide, wordlessly, whether you trust them.

I decided.

The passage behind the falls was narrow, cut into living rock, and the walls were grooved with channels that had been engineered by someone who understood water better than any hydrologist I'd ever met. Not drainage. *Music*, maybe—or prayer, or simply the pleasure of making a river sing through stone. The channels hummed under the roar, a subsonic chord I felt in my teeth. Twenty years of guiding the worst rivers on two continents, and I had never been inside anything like this. I had never been inside anything that was built to be entered reverently.

Then the passage turned, the roar fell away behind us like a door closing, and the escarpment opened.

I want to tell it honestly, which means admitting that my first reaction was not awe. It was arithmetic. Terraces meant agriculture; reservoirs meant population; roads meant trade, administration, permanence. A civilization does not build aqueducts into a cliff face unless thousands of people intend to stay. Which meant everything Voss had found, everything Rennick wanted, everything my father had died refusing to sign—it was all real, and it was all worth killing for, and I stood in the middle of it doing surveyor's math while my heart caught up.

It caught up eventually.

The terraces descended the gorge wall in great green steps, each one wide as a football pitch, held by retaining walls fitted so precisely that four centuries of jungle had failed to pry them apart. Water moved through the whole system without pumps or pipes—channeled from the falls above, spilled terrace to terrace in silver threads, collected in reservoirs that lay flat and mirror-still in the evening light. Roads ran between them, paved with stone worn smooth by feet that had stopped walking them five hundred years ago, and still the roads held their line, patient as handwriting.

Tomás sank down onto the first terrace and just breathed. Naira walked out onto the paving and stood with her arms slightly away from her body, palms open, and I understood she was greeting it. Not discovering. Greeting. Her people had known this place was here the way you know a relative is alive in another city—not visited, not forgotten, simply *kept*.

"The river kept it," she said, when I came to stand beside her. "Not hidden. Kept. There is a difference."

There is. Hidden things are lost things waiting to be found. Kept things belong to someone.

We made camp on a terrace halfway up, in the lee of a wall that still carried niches where someone had once set lamps or offerings. Naira showed us how the old hearths were placed so the smoke rose through a flue in the masonry, and we cooked without a fire visible from more than ten meters, and I thought: *my father ate here.* The thought arrived whole and physical, like a hand on the shoulder. His marks were on the stair below—his knife-cuts, his initials, fresh as last season—and somewhere in these terraces was the cache he'd sealed with his own hands, addressed to a daughter who'd spent twenty years running in exactly the opposite direction.

I didn't sleep so much as drift, and in the drifting I dreamed the way I hadn't since I was a child: not images but routes. Switchbacks descending a wall. A ledge behind a second, smaller fall. A chamber dry as paper. I woke before dawn with my father's voice in my head reading elevation figures, and I lay there in the dark listening to the reservoirs spill their silver threads, and for the first time since the radio went dead at Voss's camp, I wasn't afraid.

That should have warned me.

* * *

Morning came up gold through the mist off the falls, and the ruins turned from gray to honey, and I made the mistake of feeling happy.

Naira led us along the topmost road, which ran along the escarpment lip toward a plaza I could see in the distance—an open square of paving with a single standing stone at its center. On the way she taught without teaching, pointing out the reservoir gates, explaining in her unhurried way how the whole system could be closed in an afternoon if the people needed to disappear it. "The water remembers how to leave," she said. "They only had to ask it."

Tomás had the survey notes out whenever we stopped, working through my father's figures with the focused joy of a man doing the one thing he was born for. He'd gone quiet in a new way overnight—the fever-burnt terror of the hollow tree replaced by something steadier. Purpose will do that. So will a night spent inside a miracle.

"The numbers don't lie," he told me, tapping a page. "Your father surveyed the boundaries to the meter. The company claim overlaps Ashaninka territory by—" he turned the page, checked, and his jaw tightened "—by eleven thousand hectares. And the signature authorizing the resurvey isn't his. It's traced. He must have copied it to prove the forgery."

"He kept proof," I said.

"He kept *everything*. Look at the notation system—he cross-referenced every measurement to a field book we haven't found yet. The cache." He looked up at me, and there was something in his face I recognized from mirrors: the expression of a person about to hand someone bad news wrapped in good news. "Zephyrine. If we find the cache, we don't just clear your father's name. We break the claim entirely. Legally. Permanently."

And there it was, the other side of the ledger, the one I'd been not-reading since chapter one of this disaster. Break the claim, and the world comes up this river. Surveyors, lawyers, tourists, men with satellite phones and permits. Eleven thousand hectares of kept land becomes eleven thousand hectares of disputed land, and disputed land gets decided by people who have never heard the water sing through stone.

Naira was watching me. She'd been watching me since the falls, I realized—with kindness, and with the patience of someone who has watched outsiders arrive at this exact moment of understanding many times across many generations.

"You see it now," she said.

"I see it."

"Your father saw it too. That is why he is dead, and why he is honored. Both." She touched the standing stone as we passed it, lightly, the way you'd touch an elder's hand. "Tonight you rest. Tomorrow, the hard question."

"I already know the hard question."

"No," she said, not unkindly. "You know the easy version of it. The hard one waits until you are holding the answer in your hands."

* * *

We spent the day doing what guides do, because guides are what we were, whatever else the week had made us. Tomás and I walked the terraces mapping water gates and stair alignments, partly because the knowledge might keep us alive, mostly because our hands needed work. Naira moved ahead of us like weather, appearing and reappearing, and twice I caught her standing still in places where the paving was worn deepest, listening to something under the ground.

In the late afternoon we climbed to a ridge below the escarpment lip, and I saw the whole valley at once—roads converging from four directions, reservoirs stacked like coins, terraces stepping down to a river that had agreed, for five hundred years, to keep a secret. Somewhere down there was a chamber dry as paper with my father's handwriting in it. Somewhere beyond the lip was Rennick, and beyond Rennick was a fixer with a helicopter, and beyond him a boardroom full of people who would burn this valley for a line item.

But standing there, with the gold going out of the stone and the first bats stitching the air over the reservoirs, none of that felt real. What felt real was the engineering. The care. Somebody had loved this place enough to make the water sing through its walls, and for one evening I let myself believe that love was a form of protection—that anything tended this well couldn't truly be lost.

My father believed that too, probably. Right up until the river.

We came down to camp as the light failed. Naira had built the fire small, in the old hearth, and the smoke rose straight up through the flue and vanished. Tomás was asleep almost before he finished eating, the survey notes zipped into his jacket against his chest like a second heartbeat. I sat up with the first watch, which was less about vigilance than about wanting one more hour of the sound—the falls behind us, the water spilling terrace to terrace, the whole valley breathing.

I remember thinking: *one night. Let me have this one night.*

The valley granted it. That was the last generous thing it had time to do, because near midnight, from far downstream—too far to be real, too distinct to be dismissed—I heard the thing I'd been hearing in nightmares since Voss's camp.

A generator. Faint, steady, obscene, chewing gasoline somewhere below the falls.

Rennick had found the stair.
