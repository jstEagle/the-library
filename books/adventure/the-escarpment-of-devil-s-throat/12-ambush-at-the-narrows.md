## 12. Ambush at the Narrows

The fever never really left me after the hollow tree. It went into remission the way a bad debt does — quiet, patient, waiting for the worst possible moment to come collecting. On the fourth morning above the gorge it came collecting, and I woke with my teeth chattering in ninety-degree heat and Naira's hand pressed flat against my forehead like she was testing a cooking pot.

"He burns," she said to Zephyrine, in Spanish, as if I weren't there. As if I were luggage.

"He's been burning since I found him," Zephyrine said. "He'll keep burning. He's too stubborn to do anything else."

I wanted to object that I was right there and could hear them, but my tongue had gone thick as a strip of jerky, and what came out was something between a cough and a prayer. Zephyrine crouched beside me and put a strip of dried fish in my hand, folding my fingers over it the way you'd fold a letter you didn't want blown away.

"Eat. Today we make the narrows. After that, the walls come together and nobody can flank us, and you can be as sick as you like in peace."

That was Zephyrine's whole philosophy of comfort, I was learning. She didn't lie to you. She just arranged the truth so it sounded survivable.

I ate the fish. It tasted like bark and salt, which is to say it tasted like the best meal of my life, because for six days it had been the only meal of my life, and because Naira had shown us which lichen to scrape and which river stones held grubs if you knew how to look, and because Zephyrine had shot a bird on the second day with a slingshot she'd made from a sandal strap, and had said nothing about it, just handed me half, still warm, and looked away while I cried a little, pretending to study the water.

The survey notes rode against my chest in an oilcloth packet, tied with the same cord that had held my father's rosary, which I had lost in the river on the first night of the massacre and had never told anyone I'd lost. The notes were Voss's copies — pages torn from a field book, plus Zephyrine's father's annotations in a hand so small and precise it looked typeset. I had read them so many times by firelight that I could recite the coordinates in my sleep, which was fortunate, because I was doing most of my sleeping in a state that could generously be called sleep.

The city was real. That was the thing I kept returning to, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. Terraces. Reservoirs. A causeway road running along the escarpment's shoulder like a sentence someone had started writing four hundred years ago and never finished. And the claim — the claim Voss had carried, the one with Hartwell's signature on it — was drawn over coordinates that matched nothing, that had been shifted, that covered Ashaninka ground the way a blanket thrown over a sleeping child covers the child and half the floor besides. A forgery. A beautiful, legal-looking forgery with a dead man's name on it.

And I was the man carrying the proof. Which meant I was not a person anymore. I was a document with a fever.

* * *

The narrows announced themselves an hour before we reached them. The river, which had been running wide and brown and lazy, began to grumble, and the canyon walls leaned in like conspirators, and the light that had been falling generously through the canopy suddenly had to queue and filter and come down in single file. Naira stopped at the edge of a gravel bar and looked at the water for a long time, and I watched her look, because I had learned that Naira's silences were more informative than most people's speeches.

"Bad?" Zephyrine asked.

"Fast here," Naira said. "Narrow. One way through, on the water or on the stone. The old people say the river swallows the careless and spits out the bones for the vultures to argue over."

"Cheerful," Zephyrine said. "Any way around?"

"Over. Two hours, high trail. But the high trail can be seen from above." Naira's chin lifted toward the canyon rim, where the forest stood in a dark unbroken line. "And below."

Zephyrine stood with her hands on her hips and did the arithmetic. I could see her doing it — the little crease between her brows, the way her thumb tapped her thigh twice and stopped, twice and stopped. She'd been a guide for fifteen years; she did sums with her body the way I did them with a slide rule.

"We go through," she said finally. "Fast and low. If Rennick's watching the rim, we're a smear on a gravel bar he can't reach in time. If he's watching the water, we're already past before his glasses find us. Either way, standing here deciding is the one option that kills us."

Naira looked at her for a moment, and something passed between them — some old, dry acknowledgment, the look of two women who had both survived things that should have killed them and had decided to be unimpressed about it.

"The boy goes in the middle," Naira said.

"I can paddle," I managed.

"You can boil," Naira said. "You go in the middle and you hold on and you leave the paddling to women who have not spent a week cooking from the inside."

I have been insulted by better men than Naira, but never more accurately.

We had one raft — the one we'd stolen from Rennick's lower camp, patched twice with rubber cement and once, memorably, with a strip of Voss's tent. We loaded it, and Zephyrine checked the lashings with a violence that was really tenderness wearing work gloves, and then we pushed off into the throat of the narrows, and the river took us the way a hand takes a card it intends to play.

* * *

I want to describe the rapids properly, because for about four minutes they were the last good thing that happened to any of us.

The walls came down to thirty meters, then twenty, then ten, black stone slicked with spray, and the river stood up in places — actual standing waves, green and glassy, taller than my head — and Zephyrine read them like a page. She'd lean, and Naira would lean, and the raft would swing around some fanged rock with a hand's width to spare, and I would be in the middle, clutching the line, doing my job, which was boiling quietly and holding on. Spray hit us in cold sheets. The noise was total. It was the first time since the massacre that the noise in my head and the noise outside my head matched, and I remember thinking, with the fever's strange clarity: *this is what quiet feels like, when you do it right.*

Then we came around the last bend, where the walls pinched to their tightest, and Zephyrine stopped paddling so fast it was like a door slamming.

Smoke. A thread of it, rising from the gravel beach on the far side, thin and deliberate. Not a campfire. A signal.

"Off the water," Zephyrine said, low. "Now. Left eddy."

We weren't going to make the eddy. I understood that before she did, because I was facing backward, and because the man standing on the rim of the canyon, two hundred feet up, had already raised his arm, and because I had spent one terrible night watching men with company rifles arrange themselves around a survey camp, and my body remembered the geometry before my mind did.

The first shot hit the water a meter off our bow. The second hit the raft.

Not us — the raft. Rennick's men were good, and good men don't waste bullets on a moving target when a stationary one will do. The round went through the forward tube with a sound like a knuckle cracking, and the raft began, with tremendous dignity, to die.

"Beach it," Zephyrine said. "Right side. Now."

We hit the gravel at an angle that would have made her weep in any other context, and we came off that raft at a dead run, and I learned something about fever in that moment, which is that it burns off. For about ninety seconds I was not sick. I was a surveyor's apprentice from Cusco running through boulders with a packet of papers against his chest and a lifetime of being the slowest person in the room finally, finally corrected.

Naira ran behind me. I heard her, not saw her — the slap of her feet on stone, the rasp of her breath, steady as a metronome. She had a machete and a blowgun and a face that had looked at worse than this, and I believed, with the whole of my burning heart, that we were going to make the high trail, that we were going to climb out of the throat of this canyon and disappear into the green and take the truth to Lima or to God or to whoever would finally listen.

Then the men came down from the rim on ropes.

* * *

There were six of them. Later, I would learn there were six, because Rennick liked six — three to fix you, three to make sure the fixing held. They came down the canyon wall on climbing ropes like they were descending into their own pockets, and they hit the gravel bar between us and the high trail, and the geometry of the narrows closed like a book.

Zephyrine took stock the way she took stock of rapids — fast, flat-eyed, no wasted motion. I watched her do it. Gravel bar, two exits, six men, one machete, one blowgun, one dying raft, one feverish document. I watched her arrive at the same sum I did.

"Run," she said to me.

"I'm not leaving—"

"Tomás." She didn't raise her voice. The canyon did it for her; the narrows took her word and threw it around the stone until it was everywhere. "Naira knows a way. A crack in the wall, an old path. You carry the notes. You are the mission. *Run.*"

And Naira — this is the part I will carry until I am carried, this is the stone in my shoe for the rest of my life — Naira stepped forward.

Not away. *Forward.* She stepped in front of Zephyrine, machete low, blowgun already at her lips, and she put her body into the gap between us and the six descending men the way you'd put a hand over a candle flame, and she fired.

The dart took the lead man in the shoulder and he sat down hard on the gravel, screaming about his arm, and for three full seconds — three seconds that I have spent every night since trying to spend differently — the whole arrangement of the world hung balanced on an old woman with a blowgun.

Then one of them shot her.

Not a warning shot. Not a wild one. The man with the rifle knelt, aimed with the patience of a man who had done this in other canyons for other companies, and fired, and Naira turned as if someone had called her name, and the bullet went into her side, and she folded down onto the gravel like a garment coming off a hook.

I did not run. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger this whole nightmare is being kept in: I did not run. I ran *toward* her. I got maybe four steps. I got four steps and then a hand closed on my collar from behind — one of them had come down the wall on our side, silent, patient, a man built out of the same stone as the canyon — and the packet was torn out of my jacket with a jerk that lifted me onto my toes, and someone said, in careful Spanish, "The papers. Just the papers," as if that were a kindness.

Zephyrine was at the waterline. I saw her — the last thing I saw clearly for a long time. She stood at the edge of the gravel with the river roaring behind her and the six men between her and me, and Naira bleeding on the stones, and me held up by my collar like a kitten, and she looked at me across all of it with an expression I had never seen on her face. Not guilt. Not yet. Something before guilt, rawer — the face of a woman watching a second group of people die on a river she had sworn off rivers for.

"Go," I said. My voice came out in pieces. "Zephyrine. *Go.*"

She went.

I have replayed it ten thousand ways. She could have charged. She could have died with us, uselessly, heroically, and the notes would be in Rennick's hands and the claim would stand and her father would stay buried under a lie forever. She went, up the left wall, into a crack in the stone that I would never have seen, that only a Hartwell would have seen, because her father had cut the first handholds there twenty years ago with a cold chisel and a stubbornness the family kept like a watch.

The men fired twice at the crack and hit stone. Rennick — I saw him then for the first time, coming down the last rope, a big deliberate man with the face of an accountant at a funeral — Rennick raised his hand and the firing stopped.

"Let her run," he said. "The gorge does our work for us. One woman, no supplies, no map." He turned the oilcloth packet over in his hands, and I watched him weigh it, and I watched the small satisfied shift in his shoulders when he felt the pages inside, and something in my chest tore along a seam I hadn't known was there. "And we have the little clerk. He'll tell us everything."

He looked at me. Up close his eyes were the color of the river in flood, and about as interested in what they carried.

"Quispe, isn't it? Junior man. The one who hid in a tree." He smiled without any of his face joining in. "You hid well, Quispe. You'll tell me where they were going, and what Hartwell left up there, and you'll do it because the alternative is that I leave you for the vultures to argue over — the old woman's phrase, I believe. Rivers carry words a long way."

They bound my wrists with the cord from my own pack and marched me up the gravel bar, and the last thing I saw of that day was Naira. They left her where she fell. One of them, the youngest, looked back at her twice, and I understood that even among Rennick's six there was a man who would not sleep well tonight, and I hated him only slightly less than the others for it.

She was breathing. I saw the shallow lift of her side as they pulled me past — barely, but there. The old people say the river swallows the careless. Naira was not careless. Naira had put herself in the river's mouth on purpose, and if there was any justice in this canyon — and I had stopped believing there was, but the fever makes a man superstitious — the river would give her back.

They took me up the ropes. The canyon walls rose around me, and somewhere above, in a crack in the stone, a woman with her father's hands was climbing into the dark with nothing but a machete and a grudge, and I thought: *good.* I thought: *run, Hartwell. You were always running. Run all the way up.*

And then I thought about the pages in Rennick's hands, and about the coordinates I could recite in my sleep, and about the fact that a forged claim is only as strong as the man who can't prove it's forged — and that I was, at this moment, the only living person who could.

Rennick thought he'd taken the mission off me.

He'd actually just handed me a new one: stay alive, stay useless, and lie to an accountant until a Hartwell comes down out of the walls.

I'd had worse fevers. I intended to survive this one too.
