## 13. Interrogation

They tied me to a folding chair, which I thought was almost funny. After everything—the causeway, the narrows, Naira going down with an arrow through her shoulder so I could run, and then not running fast enough—after all of that, the company's idea of menace was office furniture. A folding chair. A camp table with a lantern on it. A man named Rennick sitting across from me with a ledger open like we were about to reconcile accounts.

The rope at my wrists was real enough, though. And the jungle behind him was full of his men, and the jungle beyond that was full of everything else.

"You're Quispe," he said. "Junior surveyor. Tomás." He said my name the way you'd read it off a shipping manifest. "You've had a bad month."

"I've had a bad year," I said. My voice came out steadier than I felt, which surprised me. Fever will do that sometimes. It burns off everything except whatever's left at the bottom of you. "My whole crew is dead. So yes."

"Your crew," Rennick repeated, and made a small note in the ledger. Actually wrote something down. I have spent my life around men with instruments and men with guns, and I can tell you the second kind loves paperwork more than the first ever did. Every bullet gets a receipt eventually. "Let's talk about your crew."

* * *

Here is what I understood about my situation, sitting in that chair with my wrists going numb: I was alive because I was useful, and I would stay alive exactly as long as I stayed useful, and the moment the usefulness ran out there was a very specific kind of quiet waiting for me upriver near where Voss had found the stone. I had watched these men work. I knew their arithmetic.

So when Rennick opened with small talk—water, fever, whether I'd eaten—I let him do it. A man who wants something talks first. A man who wants something explains himself. Every minute he explained himself was a minute Zephyrine Hartwell was out in the dark getting further away, or getting closer, or doing whatever it is she does instead of dying, which so far appeared to be a great deal.

"Voss was reckless," Rennick said. He turned the ledger toward me. There were photographs clipped inside. Our camp. The radio. The shallow grave they'd let us dig for Esteban before they stopped letting us dig anything. "He went past the marked line. He took a company expedition into restricted territory without authorization, provoked local hostiles, and got his people killed. That's the record as it stands."

"That's not what happened."

"No," Rennick agreed mildly. "It isn't. But records are cooperative things, Quispe. They become true if enough people sign them."

He slid a paper across the table. Typed. Clean. The company letterhead at the top looked official enough to bank with.

I read it twice, because the first time I thought the fever was playing tricks on me.

*Statement of Tomás Quispe, junior surveyor:* — and then three paragraphs in careful bureaucratic Spanish explaining that the expedition's guide, one **Zephyrine Hartwell**, daughter of the disgraced surveyor Emilio Hartwell, had led the team into the gorge under false pretenses; that she had conspired with river traffickers; that when the leader Voss objected to her route, she and armed accomplices had murdered the crew and burned the camp. My signature at the bottom would make all of it true. My signature would hang the deaths of nine men around the neck of a woman who hadn't even been on that river when they died.

"She wasn't there," I said.

"She's there now."

"She didn't kill anyone."

"No," Rennick said again, patient, like a teacher whose student is almost keeping up. "But she's inconvenient, and dead men need someone to blame who can still stand trial. You're the survivor. Survivors are credible. That's the whole tragedy of surviving—you become evidence."

I looked at the paper for a long time. The lantern hissed. Somewhere out in the dark a bird made a sound like a saw going through wet wood, and one of Rennick's men shifted his weight, boots grinding in the mud.

"If I sign," I said, "you let me go."

"I put you on a boat to Pucallpa with money in your pocket and a story about how you walked out alone. Grief-mad, half-starved. Nobody questions a survivor. That's rather the point."

"And if I don't sign?"

Rennick closed the ledger. He did it gently, which was worse than slamming it.

"Then I'm afraid the hostile territory claim becomes accurate in a different way. Accidents are common on this river. You've seen how common."

* * *

Here is the thing nobody tells you about fear: after a certain point it stops being a wall and becomes a door. You walk through it because staying on this side is worse. I had already walked through mine back at the narrows, when I heard Naira shout and saw the arrows and understood that running meant living and living meant testifying, someday, to somebody, about all of it. I'd been caught anyway. Fine. But the part of me that fear used to own—the part that kept junior surveyors polite and quiet and signing things—had drowned somewhere between the causeway and this chair.

What was left was a man with a fever and a talent for stalling.

Surveyors stall professionally. You cannot lay out a baseline without patience; you spend half your life waiting for cloud cover to lift, for water levels to drop, for the instrument to settle. Waiting is the job. So I waited.

"Before I sign anything," I said, "I want to understand what I'm signing. If I say Hartwell killed the crew, people will ask how. One woman, nine men? People will ask why. What was her motive? People will ask where she got armed accomplices. I'm a surveyor, señor. Precision matters. If my statement has holes, it's worthless to you—and worse than worthless to me, because I'll have signed my name to something that falls apart the moment anyone competent reads it."

Something moved behind Rennick's eyes. Not anger. Recognition. The look of a man discovering the merchandise is more complicated than advertised.

"You want me to help you write it better."

"I want it to survive contact with a prosecutor," I said. "You don't want me to sign a confession. You want me to sign a *testimony*. Those are different documents. A confession is what a guilty man gives you. Testimony is what a frightened witness gives you—and frightened witnesses contradict themselves unless someone helps them keep the story straight. Help me keep it straight."

He studied me for a while. Then he took out a pen, uncapped it, and pulled the paper back toward himself.

"All right, Quispe. Let's keep it straight."

And that's how I spent the next two hours of my life: collaborating with the man who'd murdered my crew on the composition of a lie, asking questions, raising objections, requesting clarifications. *How did she know the gorge? Through her father's maps—good, good, that ties the father in, the blacklist, the grudge.* *Where did she recruit the Ashaninka? She didn't recruit them, they captured her—no wait, better if she recruited them, it shows premeditation.* He wanted dates. I gave him dates, wrong ones, off by days, and watched to see if he caught them. He didn't. He wasn't checking against anything, because there was nothing to check against. The truth of this operation lived entirely inside his ledger and nowhere else.

But here is what I actually learned, hour by hour, question by question, while my wrists burned and the fever climbed: Rennick kept glancing at the sky.

Not the jungle. Not the river. The *sky*. Every time the helicopter sound came over the ridge—distant, mechanical, wrong for this place—he stopped writing. His pen lifted. His shoulders changed. Once, when the sound faded without coming closer, he exhaled through his nose, slow, controlled, the way you breathe when you're making yourself not react.

Men afraid of the jungle check the treeline. Men afraid of each other check the sky.

"You have a superior coming," I said, conversationally, testing.

"The company has interests in the region."

"The fixer," I said. "The one who hired Hartwell. The one whose voice was on the satellite phone at the last camp, giving Voss his orders." I had heard that call. I had been crouched behind the radio tent with a theodolite case hugged to my chest, listening to a pleasant urban accent tell Voss to proceed past the marked line, and I had said nothing, because I was twenty-four years old and the accent sounded like money and money sounded like authority. That silence had cost nine lives. I had decided, somewhere in a hollow tree, never to be silent again—but I could still use silence tactically. "Does he know you've got me?"

Rennick's pen stopped moving.

"Because if he doesn't," I went on, mild as milk, "then this statement is yours, not the company's. And when his version of events arrives—and it will arrive, señor, men like that always send their version—it won't match yours, and then there are two documents, and two documents mean two stories, and two stories mean somebody has to decide which one is the forgery. And I don't think it'll be you deciding."

The lantern hissed. Rennick looked at me for a long, long moment, and I understood I had just done something either very brave or very stupid, and that from the outside they look identical.

"You're smarter than your file says," he said finally.

"My file says I'm a junior surveyor."

"It does." He capped the pen. "Get some sleep. We'll finish tomorrow."

They cut me down from the chair and marched me to a tent and tied my wrists to the center pole, high enough that I couldn't lie flat, low enough that I couldn't stand. Comfortable, by their standards. Before he left, Rennick paused at the tent flap.

"For what it's worth," he said, not turning around, "the statement was well constructed. Your corrections improved it."

"I know," I said. "That's how you'll lose."

He glanced back at that—just a flicker—and then the flap fell and I was alone with the pole and the fever and the enormous breathing dark.

* * *

I didn't sleep. Sleep is for men whose plans are finished. Instead I worked.

A surveyor tied to a tent pole still has instruments: ears, memory, arithmetic. So I surveyed the camp. Two sentries walking overlapping circuits, meeting every eleven minutes at the north end—a gap of maybe ninety seconds on the south side where the lantern light didn't reach. Generator, because the lights flickered at intervals, which meant fuel, which meant a fuel cache, which meant fire was available to anyone desperate enough. The helicopter pad was a cleared stretch downstream; I counted four landings' worth of churned mud, which meant traffic, which meant the fixer was coming soon, and soon was a word with a clock inside it.

And the rope holding my wrists—I tested it the way you test everything on a river, patiently, with small loads, feeling for the weak strand. There was one. Of course there was one. Nothing in this camp had been maintained properly since the crew died, because maintenance is a habit of men who expect a future.

Somewhere out there, Zephyrine Hartwell was following her father's markers through the gorge, wounded probably, starving certainly, and completely unaware that the document that would destroy her was being drafted with her name in it. She'd hate that. She'd hate it more than the ropes, more than the hunger. A woman like that doesn't fear dying; she fears being *defined* by other people's paperwork. Her whole life was a file someone else had written—blacklisted, disgraced, daughter-of—and she had come up this river to take the pen back.

Well. I had held the pen all evening. And every correction I'd insisted on, every date I'd fudged, every hole I'd demanded be patched—I'd built them deliberately, badly, load-bearing flaws dressed up as thoroughness. When the statement finally met the truth, it wouldn't just fail. It would fail *informatively*. It would show its seams. A forged survey and a forged testimony fail the same way: along the lines someone was too hurried to check.

Naira had taken an arrow so I could carry the notes out. The notes were gone—taken from me at the narrows, added to Rennick's ledger, another exhibit in his collection. But notes were only paper. What Naira's people had, what Zephyrine had, what the valley itself had, was older than paper. Paper burns. Seams show.

Near dawn, the helicopter sound came again—closer this time, committed, descending. I heard Rennick's boots move fast across the camp, faster than he'd moved at any point since they'd caught me, and I heard him shouting orders to clear the landing zone, and his voice had a crack in it that hadn't been there before.

The fixer was arriving. Which meant, according to every rule of the world these men had built, that someone else was about to become expendable.

I sat in the grey light with my raw wrists and my burning head and I smiled, because I couldn't help it. Rennick feared the sky. The fixer feared exposure. And somewhere above the falls, a woman who feared nothing except other people's versions of her was climbing toward all of them.

They should have checked their arithmetic too.

The tent flap opened, and the light that came through it wasn't lantern light.
