## 14. The Fixer Arrives

I'd been living in the cliff face for two days, and the cliff had opinions about it.

The overhang Naira's people had shown me—before the ambush scattered us, before Tomás was taken, before I learned what my own lungs sounded like when they panicked—was a seam of granite above the narrows, wide enough to lie in if you didn't mind lying like a corpse. Which, given the circumstances, felt thematically appropriate. Below me the river ran white through Devil's Throat, loud enough to swallow a gunshot and stupid enough to swallow a body too, which I suspected was why everyone kept choosing this gorge for their crimes.

My left side had stopped hurting sometime during the second night. This was not good news. Pain is information, and silence from a wound usually means either healing or sepsis, and I'd been drinking river water boiled in a tin cup I'd stolen from Rennick's own camp, so the odds were running about even.

Below me, maybe sixty meters down and across the water, sat the company camp: four green tents, a generator humming its small arrogant hum, a tarp strung between trees over crates. And Tomás, when they let him sit outside, wrists zip-tied to a tent pole, looking smaller every day in a way that had nothing to do with food. They were feeding him. That was the part that scared me. You don't feed a man you plan to shoot; you feed a man whose signature you still need.

Rennick moved through the camp like a man doing arithmetic he hated. I'd watched him for two days through a gap in the rock, close enough with the binoculars I'd taken off a dead man that I could count the gray coming into his beard. He checked the perimeter at intervals so regular you could set a watch by them, and regularity, in my experience, is what men do when they believe the structure around them is holding. Rennick believed the structure was holding.

He was wrong, but he didn't know it yet, and neither did I—not until the third morning, when the sky changed.

* * *

You hear a helicopter in the gorge before you see it, because the walls throw the sound around like a rumor in a small town. It came as a stutter first, then a flattening roar, and the river birds went up off the water in a single gray sheet. I pressed myself deeper into the seam of granite and watched the machine come up the throat of the valley sideways, careful and slow, the way you drive a truck on ice. Not a military bird. Corporate. White and blue, no markings, the kind of clean that costs money.

It circled the camp once. Rennick came out of his tent already walking fast, and even from this distance I could read his body the way you read weather: the set shoulders of a man who has just realized the meeting he asked for is not the meeting he's getting. He stood in the open with his hands visible, which told me everything. Men who feel safe don't make themselves visible to aircraft.

The helicopter set down on the gravel bar below the camp, downwind of the tents, rotors idling. Two men got out. One was heavyset, in a field jacket too new to have ever been rained on, and he walked like a man arriving rather than visiting—the difference matters; arrivals expect the ground to have been prepared. The other stayed by the aircraft with a rifle held low and casual, the way you hold something you've used often enough that it's boring.

I knew the heavyset man. Not personally—I'd never had the pleasure—but I knew the type the way you know a species. Salcedo. The fixer. The man who'd paid me in cash in a border-town bar and told me to find out what happened to Voss's expedition, and whose satellite phone I'd let ring out in a dead man's camp while understanding, slowly and completely, that I hadn't been hired to rescue anyone. I'd thought I understood the shape of it then. I'd thought the pyramid went: Salcedo, then Rennick, then the rifles, and me somewhere near the bottom being useful.

Watching him cross the gravel bar toward Rennick, I began to suspect the pyramid had more floors than that, and that everyone on every floor was standing on someone.

* * *

Sound carries strangely in the gorge. The river eats most of it, but voices thrown off the rock walls come back doubled, ghosted, and if the wind runs right—down-valley in the mornings, always down-valley, my father taught me that before he taught me anything else—you can catch fragments across water that should be private. I lay in my granite seam with the wind in my favor and listened to two men conduct a murder at conversational volume.

"—told you to contain it," Salcedo was saying. His voice arrived flat and unhurried, stripped of accent, the voice of a man who had paid to have his edges sanded off. "Containment. That was the assignment. Instead I get a surveyor in a cage, an indigenous community arming itself, and a Hartwell loose in the gorge."

"The Hartwell woman is handled," Rennick said. "She's alone. Wounded. She can't—" 

"She found the causeway, Rennick." A pause, and in the pause something shifted in Salcedo's tone, some temperature dropping. "We know she found the causeway because your own radio traffic mentioned it. In the clear. Unencrypted. Do you understand what I'm telling you? There are people monitoring that band who are not ours."

I watched Rennick's back go rigid. From where I lay I couldn't see his face, only the geometry of a man learning the floor beneath him isn't there.

"Sir, the claim documentation—"

"There is no claim documentation." Salcedo said it almost gently, the way you tell a child there's no tooth fairy. "There never was. The paper your principals waved at investors was a device. It was never sanctioned, never filed, never meant to exist past the quarter. It exists to establish narrative presence—a story we can point to, or disown, depending on how the wind sits. What it is not is a legal instrument, and what it is definitely not is worth a war with the Ashaninka, a dead expedition, and now a federal interest in unencrypted radio traffic originating from a concession that does not officially exist."

The wind gusted. I lost ten seconds of it, and in those ten seconds Rennick must have said something desperate, because when the words came back Salcedo was answering a question I hadn't heard.

"—because you were convenient," he said. "That's all anyone ever is. Convenient. You contained the problem cheaply and locally, and I was going to let you retire on that. But you took hostages. Hostages are witnesses with a delay timer. And you did it after the moment the calculus changed, which means you're not reading the situation anymore, which means you're a variable." He turned slightly, and I saw his profile against the white noise of the falls, and there was nothing in it at all. "Variables get removed."

"I built this operation."

"You built a liability with tents."

Somewhere below, one of Rennick's men came around the corner of the mess tent, saw the helicopter, and froze with the particular stillness of a man doing math of his own. Salcedo's rifleman didn't even turn his head. That was somehow worse—if your security chief is being fired mid-gorge, you'd think the staff would react. Unless the staff had already been briefed. Unless the staff had gotten the call last night on a frequency Rennick doesn't monitor, and the only person in this camp who didn't know the meeting was an execution was the man standing in the middle of it.

I lay very still in the rock and felt the vertigo come, the real kind, the kind that has nothing to do with heights. Because here was the thing I finally understood, lying sixty meters above two men deciding a life like a line item: there was no top to this thing. I had spent weeks assuming there was a head of the snake, a room somewhere, a desk, a name—find the name, end the nightmare. But Salcedo answered to principals who'd disowned a claim that had never existed, and those principals answered to capital that had never heard of Devil's Throat, and the capital answered to nothing at all except its own appetite. Every man in this valley was expendable, including the ones spending the others. Including, probably, Salcedo. Especially Salcedo. Somewhere above him there was another phone call he hadn't gotten yet, scheduled for a morning he wouldn't see.

And me? I wasn't even a floor. I was debris. A loose end with a famous surname, hired to confirm deaths and retrieve papers, meant to be tidied away the moment I confirmed them. The only reason I was still breathing was that I'd stopped being convenient and started being difficult, and difficulty buys time, and time is the only currency the jungle pays.

Salcedo raised his voice just enough to carry to the tents. "Pack it. Everything burns except the surveyor—he flies out with us. Federal people will walk this valley inside the week, and what they find will be an abandoned illegal mining camp and nothing else. No documents. No bodies in company gear. Nothing with a logo."

"And Quispe?" Rennick's voice had gone thin. "He signs the statement?"

"He signs whatever keeps him cooperative. If he doesn't, the river's right there." A shrug I could actually see from sixty meters. "He was always going to be the statement. That's what junior surveyors are for."

Something in my chest went cold and specific. Not rage—rage is hot and general, and this was neither. This was the cold clarity of a woman watching a man price her friend at exactly zero and finding, to her lack of surprise, that the number matched her own.

They were taking Tomás out by air. Out of the gorge, out of the equation, into whatever room Salcedo's principals used to convert witnesses into signatures. Once that helicopter lifted, I would never find him again. Not in this valley, not in this lifetime. Lima would swallow him the way it swallowed everything—slowly, legally, with paperwork.

The rotors were still turning. Idle. Waiting.

* * *

Here is what I knew, lying in the granite with my side throbbing again as if the wound objected to decisions made without it:

One: the camp would burn within the hour, and burning camps draw eyes up the gorge, which meant movement, which meant confusion, and confusion is the only ally who never betrays you.

Two: Salcedo's rifleman was bored, and bored men watch the horizon, not the cliffs. The cliffs were mine.

Three: the descent route on the north wall—my father's route, the one with the fixed line he'd rigged twenty years ago and that Naira's people, God bless their thoroughness, had quietly maintained ever since—dropped into the boulder field two hundred meters upstream of the gravel bar, behind a screen of fallen granite that no bored rifleman was watching.

Four: I could not fight four men. I could not fight one man, honestly, in my current condition. What I could do was steal.

Because here's the beautiful thing about organizations that erase people: they generate paperwork. Even Salcedo—especially Salcedo—flew with the evidence of his erasures in a case, because his principals demanded proof of work, proof of containment, proof that the variable had been removed. Somewhere in that helicopter was a folder with my name in it. With Tomás's. With Voss's. With my father's, probably, posthumously defamed for the second time in twenty years. And a folder is a thing that can be taken, and a thing taken becomes leverage, and leverage is the only language spoken above the floor I'd been standing on.

Or—and the thought arrived quietly, the way true things do—I could simply take Tomás. While they burned the camp and watched each other and waited for the betrayal they could all feel coming like weather. Four men, one helicopter, a gravel bar, and a surveyor tied to a tent pole who had spent three days convincing his captors he was broken enough to be safe.

I looked at the camp through the binoculars. Tomás sat where they always put him, head down, wrists bound, radiating the studied harmlessness of a man who has decided to be underestimated. As I watched, he coughed—long, wet, theatrical—and sagged against the pole, and the guard nearest him glanced over, assessed, and looked away with the contempt of a man who'd already filed the prisoner under *handled*.

Tomás Quispe, I thought, you magnificent liar.

The wind shifted. Down-valley to up, the afternoon turn, and Salcedo's voice came to me one last time, ghosted off the rock, giving orders I was never meant to hear: burn it now, wheels up in twenty minutes, and if the surveyor gives you trouble—

He didn't finish. He didn't need to. The river finished it for him, roaring through the Throat below, patient as arithmetic, ready to do what it had always done for the people who used it.

Twenty minutes. I was already moving along the seam of rock toward the north wall, toward my father's fixed line, toward the only plan I had, which was a bad plan wearing the costume of a desperate one. Behind me the first smoke went up from the camp, black and oily against the green, and the gorge filled with the smell of burning canvas and diesel, and somewhere in that smoke a man named Rennick was discovering, far too late, that the structure he'd trusted had been scaffolding all along.

I clipped into the line and stepped off the edge into open air, and the vertigo finally left me—not because the drop was gone, but because I'd finally understood the view. There was no top. There was no one to appeal to. There was only the climb down, the camp burning, and twenty minutes to steal a man back from people who priced human lives at zero.

My father had mapped this wall honestly. It seemed fitting to use his rope to rob a liar.
