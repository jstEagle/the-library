## 15. Alone in the Gorge

The first thing I learned about being shot is that it's mostly bookkeeping.

You lie in the dark doing arithmetic. How much blood on the shirt—too much. How deep—through the meat of my shoulder, not the bone, thank whatever god keeps sloppy ledgers for disgraced guides. How many hours until infection turns a wound into a death sentence at this latitude—two days if I'm lucky, less if I'm stupid, and my track record suggests I should plan around stupid.

So: two days to get out of the gorge, or die in it with my father's ghost for company. Which, knowing him, would be insufferable. He'd have opinions about my knots.

I'd crawled maybe four hundred meters from the narrows before I let myself stop, wedged into a crease of limestone behind a curtain of hanging roots, and listened to Rennick's men crash around above me like drunk tourists looking for the bathroom. They had Tomás. They had the survey notes. They had Naira bleeding somewhere in the green, and no way of knowing if she was alive, and every time I tried to think that sentence through to its end my shoulder sent up a flare and reminded me that grief, like everything else in this gorge, was going to cost extra.

I tied off the wound with a strip of shirt and a length of cord from my pack, using the one-handed knot Papa called the widow's hitch, because he taught me nothing gently. Then I did what Hartwells do when the world ends.

I kept moving.

* * *

Here's the thing nobody tells you about memory: it doesn't live in your head. It lives in your hands.

I didn't decide to follow his route. There was no moment where I sat down, consulted some internal map, and plotted a course. What happened was dumber and stranger than that. Somewhere past the second bend of the gorge, starving, feverish, half-blind with pain, I found myself scrambling up a shelf of rock I had no business climbing—and halfway up, my hand went left. Not right, where the route continued. Left, to a knob of stone worn smooth as a doorknob.

A handhold. Deliberately polished. Twenty years of rain hadn't erased it, because it sat under an overhang, sheltered, exactly the way a man who thought in decades would place it.

I hung there by one arm, blood ticking out of me in slow time, and stared at that smooth stone like it had spoken.

He'd marked this route. Not for himself—for whoever came after. For me, though neither of us knew it yet, or maybe he knew and never said, because saying things was the one cliff face Elias Hartwell could never climb.

"Left at the white scar," I said out loud, and then heard myself say it, and felt the bottom drop out of the day.

Because I knew that phrase. Not remembered—knew, the way you know a song you haven't heard since childhood, the way your mouth knows a prayer it stopped believing in. *Left at the white scar, three fingers above the waterline.* It was from his journal. The journal I'd read so many times as a girl that the pages went soft as cloth, the journal I'd burned through twice and memorized without meaning to, back when memorizing my father's words was the closest thing I had to being held by him.

Three fingers above the waterline. I looked down. The flood stain on the rock ran in a dark band, and above it, three finger-widths, a white mineral scar slashed across the black stone like a signature.

I climbed toward it.

* * *

By full dark I'd made perhaps two kilometers, which on flat ground would have been pathetic and in the Devil's Throat gorge, alone, shot, and running on zero food since the morning before the ambush, was something close to heroic. I allowed myself the word. Nobody was around to mock me for it.

The gorge at night is a different country. The river drops away below you—you can't see it, only hear it, a vast breathing thing somewhere under the black, and the walls go up and up until the stars are a jagged zipper overhead. Every sound is amplified and assigned a threat. Water dripping is footsteps. Wind in the bamboo is whispering. Your own heartbeat, once the fever gets going, is somebody following you.

I talked to Papa to keep the panic down. This is either madness or method; in my family the line was always negotiable.

"You'd hate this," I told him, hauling myself over a lip of rock while my shoulder screamed its one long note. "You'd say I was rushing. You'd make me set the anchor twice." A pause, and then, because the fever makes a man honest: "I'm sorry I said your maps were a cage. I was nineteen and an idiot and I've been apologizing ever since, just never to your face, because your face was underground."

The journal passages kept surfacing. That was the strange part—not remembering them but being *reminded*, as if the gorge itself were reading aloud. I'd round a bend and the words would arrive whole:

*Where the fig leans over the channel, cross high and trust the root, not the branch.*

And there, leaning over the channel, a fig tree, its lower branches rotted to lace, its roots still gripping the bank like knuckles. I crossed high, on the root. The branch I would have chosen snapped off under a test of my boot heel and went into the dark, and the river swallowed it without comment.

"Show-off," I muttered, shaking, and meant both of us.

* * *

Dawn came gray and thin through the mist, and with it came the arithmetic again, updated. Fever: yes. Wound: angry, hot-edged, weeping. Food: I'd eaten a handful of something I chose not to identify and two grubs that tasted like regret. Water: plentiful, at least. The gorge was generous with water and miserly with everything else, which struck me as a fair portrait of my life.

I was resting against a boulder, letting ten minutes become thirty, when I saw the mark.

It was small. A chiseled arrow, no longer than my thumb, pointing up and left into a crack in the wall—a crack I'd have sworn was impassable, a crack I'd have walked past, a crack that was in fact a chimney, hidden behind a screen of ferns, leading up through the gorge wall itself.

*When the walls pinch, go up. The river lies; the stone doesn't.*

I put my hand on the arrow and laughed, a short ugly sound that echoed embarrassingly. Because I finally understood what he'd done. He hadn't just mapped the gorge. He'd written a letter into it, twenty years long, addressed to anyone stubborn enough to read stone—and I was the only correspondent he'd ever counted on. Every marker was a sentence. Every polished hold was punctuation. He'd known the company men would come for him someday—he'd refused to sign their beautiful forgery, and Elias Hartwell did not survive refusing things—and so he'd built his escape route into the escarpment itself, patient as erosion, waiting.

For his daughter, if she ever stopped moving.

I wasn't stopping. For once in my life, that was the correct answer.

* * *

The chimney nearly killed me anyway. Let's keep the record honest; he'd have wanted it honest, it was practically his religion.

Halfway up, with the fern screen far below and daylight a bright coin overhead, my bad arm simply quit. No drama, no warning—the muscle just resigned its post, and suddenly I was hanging by three fingers of my good hand on wet limestone with a hundred feet of nothing underneath and my boots skating on stone the texture of wet soap.

I remember thinking, with perfect clarity: *This is how he died. Not on the rope. On the moment after, when the body says no.*

Then the fever-memory did something it had never done before. It gave me his voice, close, the way it used to be at my ear on the practice crag behind our house in Huaraz, when I was nine and furious and crying and he wouldn't let me come down.

*"Breathe low, Zeph. Fear sits high in the chest. Put it in your belly where it can work."*

I breathed low. I put the fear in my belly. And my boot found a nub of stone that I swear was not there a second before—or was there all along, placed by a man who'd climbed this exact chimney with a full pack and a guilty conscience, and who had kicked loose every treacherous hold within reach of the line, clearing the route the way you'd clear a path to your own front door.

I came out of the top of the chimney gasping, filthy, bleeding fresh through the old bandage, and alive, and I lay on the sun-warmed slab and laughed until it turned into something else, and I let it, because there was no one to see and even if there were, Hartwells don't apologize for weather.

* * *

That afternoon I ate properly for the first time in two days, if you count palm hearts and a crayfish I caught barehanded in a side pool "properly," which after the grubs I absolutely did. The protein steadied me. The fever broke around midafternoon, breaking like a storm, sweat pouring, shivering, then a clean cold clarity on the other side of it, the world sharpening as if someone had wiped the lens.

And in that clarity I took stock, the way he taught me. Not of my body—I'd inventoried that enough—but of what I actually had.

Rennick had Tomás and the notes. But Tomás was smarter than Rennick believed—junior surveyors survive by being underestimated—and those notes were copies of copies; the originals, the true survey, the honest maps, were wherever Papa had sealed them, and the sealed place would be marked the same way everything else was marked. Patiently. In stone.

Rennick had guns and men. I had the gorge, and the gorge was on my side now, or rather on his, and I was the last speaker of his language.

And Rennick had made the mistake that every man who ever underestimated a Hartwell had made before him: he thought I'd run. Downriver, out of the country, gone, another name scratched off another ledger. He was holding Tomás as bait for exactly that—a trap laid for a fleeing animal.

But I wasn't fleeing anymore. I understood that somewhere in the dark of the chimney, hanging by three fingers. Running had been my whole adult life—out of Huaraz, out of Cusco, out of La Paz with the blacklist growing behind me like a shadow at noon—and I'd told myself it was freedom, motion, momentum, all the pretty words guides use for not having a home.

It wasn't. It was just fear sitting high in the chest.

I put it in my belly where it could work.

* * *

Nightfall found me at the base of the final wall below the terraces, where the gorge pinched one last time and the air changed—that cool mineral breath of deep stone, the sound of the falls arriving through the rock before the light does. And there, at eye level, shielded under a ledge where no flood would ever reach, the last marker.

Not an arrow this time.

An initial. E.H., cut small and precise, and beneath it, so faint I had to touch it to be sure, a single added line in a different hand, shallower, hurried:

*Z.*

He'd cut my initial next to his. Twenty years ago, in this stone, in the throat of a gorge everyone called cursed, my father had signed us both.

I stood there a long time with my fingertips on the letters, and the river thundered its one note below, and I said the thing I'd been carrying since the narrows, since before the narrows, since I was nineteen and too proud to say it to a living man:

"I'm here, Papá. Show me the rest."

Above me, faint in the starlight, the stair began—steps cut into the living rock, ascending into the dark toward the terraces, toward the city, toward the cache, toward whatever truth he'd died rather than surrender.

And below me, far down the black throat of the gorge, a light flickered. Then a second. Lanterns, moving fast, coming up my father's route.

They'd found the markers too.
