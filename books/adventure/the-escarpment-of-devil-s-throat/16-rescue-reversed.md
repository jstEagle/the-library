## 16. Rescue Reversed

The camp sat in a bowl of the gorge like something that had fallen and never bothered to get up. Three tents, a generator humming on a plastic tarp, a cook fire banked low, and four men—one awake, the rest pretending in the way sleeping men pretend when they've been told there's a woman in the gorge who kills people.

I don't kill people. But I appreciated the rumor. It was the only good press I'd gotten in years.

I'd been watching the camp since dusk from a shelf of granite above it, close enough to smell the burnt rice and diesel, far enough that the generator's drone swallowed the small sounds a person makes—breath, the click of a knee joint, the private negotiations of a body that hadn't eaten properly in four days. Below me, Tomás sat tied to a tree at the edge of the firelight, hands bound in front of him, head lolling in a way that was either exhaustion or performance. With Tomás it was hard to tell. The fever had burned off days ago, but the theatrical instinct the fever had installed seemed permanent.

Rennick had taken the survey notes. That was the part that sat in my chest like a swallowed stone. Everything my father had measured, everything Tomás had deciphered on the causeway—the honest maps, the coordinates, the arithmetic of a forgery—was folded in a waterproof pouch that Rennick treated like a rosary, touching it, checking it, counting on it. Without it, Tomás and I had testimony and grief. Rennick had leverage.

So I'd done what my father would have done, which is to say I'd done something stupid with excellent technique.

* * *

The awake guard was the youngest, a wiry man who chewed coca and stared at the fire like it owed him an explanation. The others slept in a line, rifles stacked under a tarp that would take him eleven seconds to reach if he heard something. I counted it twice. Eleven seconds. In my old life, before the blacklist, before the border towns and the fixers with satellite phones, eleven seconds was the margin I'd been paid to live inside.

I came down the back of the bowl through a seep of water my father had once described in his journal as "a gutter the mountain weeps into," and I heard his voice saying it, flat and dry, the way he'd said everything, as though the mountain weeping were a scheduling inconvenience. The memory arrived word for word these days. I had stopped fighting it. Grief, it turns out, is just inheritance with the volume turned up.

The guard heard me at four meters, which was three meters later than he should have. He turned, and I put the machete flat against his throat—not the edge, the flat, because I meant it as a conversation—and pressed my other hand over his mouth.

"Your rifle is under the tarp," I whispered. "Eleven seconds. I need four."

His eyes did arithmetic. I let him finish it. He nodded.

I took his knife, his radio, and his boots—boots because a man without boots in this gorge doesn't chase anyone, and I'm not a monster, whatever the blacklist says. I pointed him toward the trail downriver and watched him go at the careful, dignity-preserving walk of a man who has decided that whatever happens next, he was on break.

Then I crossed the firelight to the tree where Tomás sat, and his head came up, and for a second I saw the whole disaster of the last weeks in his face—the hollowed cheeks, the scab at his hairline, the eyes that had seen men with company rifles turn on their own crew and had not yet found a place to put that.

"You're late," he whispered.

"I had to find boots."

"Whose?"

"A man who's taking some personal time."

Tomás worked his bound hands against my knife, and I sawed through the cord, and the moment his hands were free he did something I hadn't planned for. He grabbed my wrist.

"The notes," he said. "Zephyrine. He sleeps with them inside his jacket. I've watched him. He checks them like—" He stopped, hunting for the word, and found it with a fever-bright certainty. "Like a man checks whether his heart is still beating."

"I know."

"You're going for them."

"I'm going for him," I said. "The notes come along as ballast."

He stared at me, and I watched him do the thing he always did, which was to calculate every exit and then ask, politely, why we weren't using any of them. It should have annoyed me. Somewhere in the last two hundred kilometers of river it had stopped annoying me and become the sound of the only other sane person in the gorge.

"Rennick's tent is the one with the generator cord," he said. "He sleeps on his left side. He wakes at—" A pause. "I don't know when he wakes. He never seemed to sleep at all."

"Then we'll wake him gently."

"Zephyrine." His grip tightened. "He asked me to sign a statement. Saying you killed the expedition. That you led Voss's men into an ambush for the gold in the city. He has it written. He just needs my name."

The gorge roared somewhere below us, the Devil's Throat clearing its throat, patient as geology.

"And?" I said.

"And I told him I'd think about it," Tomás said, and there it was—the guilt, the thing he'd been carrying since the hollow tree, since the theodolite with my father's initials. "I told him I'd think about it because a man who thinks about it is a man they keep alive. I bought us three days. Maybe four. I'm sorry. I'm not— I'm not brave like you."

I looked at him for a long moment. The firelight moved on his face.

"Tomás," I said, "brave is just scared with a deadline. You've been brave for a week. Come on."

* * *

Rennick's tent smelled of canvas and gun oil and, faintly, of the fixer's cologne—the smell of men who worked for other men and knew it. Rennick himself lay on his left side, exactly as advertised, and the waterproof pouch was inside his jacket, exactly as Tomás had promised, rising and falling with his breathing like a second, smaller heart.

I crouched over him with the machete and considered the geometry of the next thirty seconds. Wake him, and he'd go for the pistol under the groundsheet—Tomás had told me about the pistol too, my junior surveyor had turned out to be an excellent intelligence asset, mostly because he was too frightened to sleep and too observant to be any use at all as a prisoner. Don't wake him, and I'd have to slide the pouch out of a sleeping man's jacket, which is a trick that works in stories and ends in screaming in gorges.

I chose the screaming. I just chose it quietly.

I pressed the machete's flat to his neck and my knee to his sternum and his eyes opened the way a trap opens—no surprise in them at all, only a kind of professional disappointment, as if I were a line item that had come in over budget.

"Hartwell," he said.

"Rennick."

"You're supposed to be half-dead in the gorge."

"I was. I got better. It's the water here."

His hands started to move and I shifted the machete a centimeter and they stopped. Below us, the generator hummed. Somewhere in the dark, a bird made a sound like a hinge that needed oil, and I thought absurdly of my father's survey markers, his little carved signs of home, and I hated myself for the timing of my own sentiment.

"The notes," I said. "Slowly. Left hand. Inside jacket."

"You understand what happens if you take them," Rennick said. His voice was level, almost friendly, the voice of a man reading terms and conditions aloud. "The fixer doesn't need the notes. He needs the absence of them. You take them, you become the only copy of the truth, and the only copy of the truth is the one thing men like him hunt."

"I've been hunted before. I have references."

"You're your father's daughter," he said, and for the first time something moved under the level voice, something that might have been respect in a better man or just recognition. "He said almost exactly that. Word for word, nearly. Right before—"

"Before your company's rifles turned around," I finished. "I know how the sentence ends, Rennick. I've spent my whole life ending it differently."

I took the pouch. He didn't fight me, which frightened me more than a fight would have, because men like Rennick only surrender an object when the object has stopped being the plan. I backed out of the tent with the notes against my chest and the machete between us and the whole camp holding its breath around me like a held chord.

Tomás was waiting at the treeline, vibrating.

"You have them," he breathed.

"I have them. Run."

He ran. And then—this is the part I will be turning over for the rest of my life, the part I'll tell no one and think about always—he stopped. Ten meters into the dark, in the direction of the river, in the direction of freedom and rafts and the whole forgiving downstream world, Tomás Quispe stopped running, turned around, and came back.

"Your rope," he whispered, shoving something into my hands. "You dropped your rope coil at the seep. I went back for it while you were in the tent. You'd have left without it. You always leave without things. It's your whole personality."

I stood there holding forty meters of rope I had, in fact, forgotten, and felt something crack open in my chest that had nothing to do with fear.

"You went back," I said. "Into the camp."

"You went in for the notes. Someone had to go in for you." He shrugged, fever-bright, terrified, grinning. "It's just arithmetic."

It was not arithmetic. I knew arithmetic. What it was, was the mirror of a hollow tree—me finding him, him finding me, the same choice made twice in opposite directions, and each time the chooser staying when every sane calculation said go. My father had mapped this gorge honestly and died for it, and I had spent twenty years running from his example, and here was a defrocked archaeologist who couldn't hold a compass straight handing it back to me in the form of a rope coil.

"Come on," I said, and my voice did something embarrassing, and I let it. "Before the man without boots raises the alarm."

* * *

We went up. Not down—up, along the goat-track my father had cut into the gorge's eastern wall, because Rennick would expect flight downriver and had men posted for it, and because upriver, above the narrows, was Naira.

Naira's people found us before we found them, which is the way with people who belong to a place. We'd climbed an hour, the camp's alarm finally shrilling behind us like a wounded insect, the pouch of notes a dead weight against my ribs, when a stone clicked against stone in a rhythm too regular for accident. Then they were simply there—six of them in the moon-shadow of the cliff, with blowguns and machetes and, on the eldest, a shotgun older than the republic, and the sight of them did something to my throat I wasn't prepared for.

Because Naira had told me, three nights ago at the fire: *We do not carry weapons into the valley. It is not our way.* And here they were, armed, in the gorge, on our account.

"You brought them out of the valley," I said to the shadow that was Naira's grandson.

"You brought the reason in," he said, not unkindly. "It balances."

Tomás made a sound beside me that was either a laugh or a sob. With Tomás it was hard to tell.

Naira herself had stayed below, at the terraces, guarding the city with her body and her stubbornness, and it was her price I carried now along with the notes—the promise I'd made on the causeway, that the valley would stay unclaimed, whatever my father's papers said. The papers in the pouch. My father's honest maps. The letter I hadn't opened yet, addressed to *my daughter, if she ever stops moving,* which I had carried for three days like a coal in my pocket and not dared to touch, because some things you can only read once, and I wasn't sure I was finished becoming the person who could survive reading them.

We moved along the cliff in single file, armed men ahead and behind, Tomás's hand occasionally finding my elbow in the dark—not because he needed it, but because I did, though neither of us said so. Below us the camp's searchlights swung uselessly at the gorge walls. Somewhere behind us, Rennick was discovering that his notes were gone and recalculating, and the recalculation would not be calm. Men like Rennick, when the arithmetic fails, stop doing math and start doing violence.

And somewhere beyond even him, past the gorge, past the escarpment, past the whole stacked hierarchy of men working for men, the fixer was out there with a satellite phone and a helicopter and no reason at all to leave witnesses—Rennick's, mine, or anyone's.

The eldest guard stopped at a notch in the cliff where the stars showed through, and pointed up.

"The stair," he said. "Your father's stair. It goes to the high terraces. Naira waits there." A pause, and then, in the tone of a man delivering a message he'd been given verbatim: "She says to tell you—the letter will keep until morning. But you will not."

I put my hand against the pouch, against the maps and the letter and the whole buried weight of my father's twenty years, and above us the stair climbed into the dark toward the roaring of the falls, and I began to climb.
