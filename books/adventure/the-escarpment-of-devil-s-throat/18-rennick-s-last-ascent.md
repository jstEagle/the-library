## 18. Rennick's Last Ascent

I have been in the employ of men who lie for a living, and I have learned to read a lie the way a tracker reads spoor—by what it avoids, not by what it says. So when the fixer, Mr. Aldous Crane himself, stepped out of the helicopter at the narrows camp with his polished shoes and his handshake like a cold fish being offered as tribute, I knew within four minutes that I was dead.

He didn't say it. Men like Crane never say it. He said, "Rennick. Good work up here. Messy, but good work." He said, "The claim is nearly secured." He said, "We'll want your final report tonight, and then you can take the bird back down with me."

That last one was the tell. Nobody flies me anywhere. I fly myself, or I walk, or I stay put until the walking improves. A man who offers you a ride out of the jungle is a man who has already decided where you're going to be buried.

"Report's on the sat-link," I said. "Encrypted, per protocol."

"Of course." He smiled with the bottom half of his face only. "Protocol. That's why we hired you."

Behind him, two of his own security detail were unloading crates from the helicopter—crates I hadn't ordered, stamped with a logistics company I'd never heard of, which is how you know a company is real: when it exists solely to move things nobody wants traced. Rifles, probably. Or worse. Or money. With Crane it was always hard to say which was worse.

Twenty-two years running security for mining interests across three countries. Twenty-two years of knowing exactly where I stood in every room. And here I was, fifty-one years old, standing in mud that smelled like rot and old stone, realizing that the ledger had turned a page and my name had moved from the asset column to the liability column without so much as an entry to mark the transition.

I smiled back. Bottom half of the face. We understood each other perfectly.

* * *

Let me be honest about something, since honesty is in short supply this far upriver: I never wanted any of this to go the way it went.

When Voss's expedition went silent, my instructions were clean. Confirm deaths, recover documents, contain the situation. Contain is a beautiful word. It means everything and commits you to nothing. I've contained situations in Bolivia that would curdle your blood, and I slept fine afterward, because containment is just another word for finishing what other people started.

But then the Hartwell woman showed up, and everything about her was wrong. She wasn't supposed to be there. She wasn't on anyone's schedule. And she moved through that gorge like she'd been born in it—which, I came to understand, in some sense she had—and every hour she stayed alive was an hour the story got less containable.

And then there was the file. The file I read on the flight up, the file I'm going to tell you about now because it matters, because it explains why a man like me does what he does next.

Hartwell senior. The father. Twenty years ago he'd surveyed this whole valley under contract with a predecessor company—the same money, different letterhead, which in this business means the same company wearing a new shirt. He found the terraces. He found the causeway, the reservoirs, the roads cut into living rock above the falls. And then, according to the internal memo that someone had been careless enough to leave in my briefing packet—or careful enough, depending on whose carelessness you believe—he refused to sign.

Refused. One word in a margin, in his own handwriting, photographed and archived by a man who thought he was protecting himself and was actually building the noose for everyone downstream. *Will not certify. Boundaries falsified. Territory belongs to its people.*

The company solved the problem the way companies solve problems. Hartwell senior died on the river—drowned, they said, though the report had the specific texture of a report written before the death occurred. The survey vanished into a sealed chamber somewhere above the escarpment, because the man was sentimental enough to hide his work and stubborn enough to make finding it a pilgrimage. And the forged claim sat in a Lima archive for two decades like a loaded gun in a drawer, waiting for someone greedy enough and stupid enough to pick it up.

Crane was that someone. And I was the hand he'd picked to hold the gun.

Except now the daughter had found the cache—I was nearly certain of it, from the movement patterns, from Naira's people arming themselves, from the way Tomás Quispe had stopped flinching when I mentioned her name—and a signed statement pinning forgery on a dead man's disgraced daughter was no longer going to cover anything. You can forge a signature. You can't forge a map drawn in a dead surveyor's own hand that contradicts your claim down to the contour line.

Which meant the plan was dead. Which meant the plan's witnesses were next. Which meant I was somewhere on that list, probably between the archaeologist and the guide, filed under *loose ends, miscellaneous.*

I've spent my whole career being the man who closes lists. I did not intend to die as an item on one.

* * *

The camp at the narrows held nine of Crane's men, my remaining four, the prisoner Quispe, and Naira—who they'd brought in off the ridge two days ago with a bullet through her shoulder, because apparently even Crane's people had figured out that the old woman mattered more than any of us. She sat tied to a supply crate near the fire, refusing food, watching everything with those eyes that made my most experienced men look away first.

Quispe they kept in the equipment tent. He'd given them nothing—not the route to the cache, not the girl's position, not so much as a curse in proper Spanish. Feverish, half-starved, hands still shaking from the rope burns, and he gave them nothing. I found myself respecting him, which in my profession is a symptom. Respect is the first stage of sympathy, and sympathy is the first stage of getting yourself killed for somebody else's principles.

I checked on him that evening, after Crane's helicopter lifted off toward some forward base, leaving behind his two minders and a deadline I could feel ticking in my teeth.

"You look terrible," I told him.

"You look," he said, "like a man reading his own obituary."

Sharp. Fever had burned the softness out of him and left the wit. I crouched outside the tent flap, keeping my voice low.

"They're going to kill you regardless of what you sign," I said. "You know that."

"I assumed."

"And they're going to kill me right after, because I know too much and cost too much and Crane doesn't pay for problems he can erase."

Quispe studied me through the dimness. "So what do you want from me?"

"I want to know if the cache is real. The father's true survey. Honest maps."

"Why does that matter to you? You're not the sentimental type."

No, I'm not. But here's the thing about reading ledgers your whole life: eventually you start asking who wrote the entries, and in whose interest, and whether the whole book might be balanced against you. I'd spent twenty-two years enforcing other men's versions of events. Somewhere in the last week—maybe it was the gorge, maybe it was the old woman's eyes, maybe it was just age eating through the calluses—I'd started wanting there to be one version that was true.

"It matters," I said, "because if it's real, then everything I've done up here was for a lie, and I'd like the chance to do one thing that isn't."

He almost laughed. "You want absolution from a surveyor."

"I want leverage," I said, which was truer, or at least more useful. "Crane wants the cache destroyed and the claim enforced. If I control the cache, I control the only thing Crane can't afford to lose. That's not sentiment. That's arithmetic."

It was both. I've learned not to examine which one is driving.

Quispe was quiet a long moment. Then: "She'll never trust you."

"She doesn't have to trust me. She just has to hate Crane more than she hates me. People manage that every day."

* * *

The deadline arrived at 0400, the way deadlines do—delivered by satellite phone, in a voice so calm it could have been reading a shipping manifest.

"Rennick. Status."

"The site's secure. Extraction pending."

"There's been a change." A pause, the particular pause of a man consulting notes written by someone else. "Your contract concludes today. Mr. Crane asks that you remain at the narrows for debriefing. Our team will handle the upper gorge."

Our team. Not yours. Not ours. *Our.* The pronoun of the condemned.

"Debriefing," I repeated.

"A thorough one."

I looked at the phone. I looked at the dark mouth of the gorge beyond the fires, where the river roared up out of the earth like something being swallowed. I did the arithmetic I'd been doing all night, and it came out the same way every time: zero exits, negative margin, account closed.

"Understood," I said, and hung up, and stood there in the cold listening to my own heartbeat count down like a clock in a locked room.

Here is what I decided in the next ninety seconds, and I'll defend every piece of it:

If I was going to die on this river, I was not going to die as a footnote in Crane's filing system. If the story ended here, it was going to end with my handwriting in the margin, the way Hartwell senior's had—one line, refusal, on the record. And if the only currency left to me was the thing everyone wanted, then I was going to pick it up and hold it with both hands and make every man on this mountain negotiate.

I woke my four men quietly. Two of them—Vásquez and the boy, Ochoa—had followed me long enough to follow me into this. The other two I sent to sleep with a lie, because loyalty is rationed and I didn't have enough for five.

Then I walked to the equipment tent, put a knife through the tent pole ropes, and put a pistol against Quispe's ear loud enough for him to hear the safety click.

"Change of management," I said. "Get up. You're coming with me."

* * *

Naira was harder. Not physically—she was sixty-some years old with a ruined shoulder, and the ropes were rope. What was hard was the looking. She watched me cut her free with an expression I couldn't file anywhere, and when I pulled her to her feet she said, in English better than mine, "You are the one who shot at my grandchildren on the ridge."

"One of them," I admitted.

"And now you carry us to safety like a good man."

"No. Now I carry you because you're worth more alive than dead, and so am I, and together we're worth more than either of us alone. That's the whole speech. Don't dress it up."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile, more like the memory of one. "Good," she said. "A man who lies to himself cannot be bargained with. A man who tells the truth badly—" she shrugged with her good shoulder "—we can work with that."

Vásquez covered the east trail. Ochoa took the girl's probable approach along the waterline—we'd tracked her movements well enough to know she'd come back for the surveyor; it was the only variable in her behavior I'd ever been able to predict with confidence. And I took the cache.

Because that was the pivot of the whole night, the hinge everything swung on: I knew where it was. Three days of interrogating the terrain, cross-referencing the old expedition's route notes against the father's survey markers, and I'd narrowed it to a sealed chamber below the second terrace—a fissure in the rock face behind a curtain of roots, marked with a chiseled symbol I'd seen once before, twenty years ago, on a dead man's theodolite case.

Getting there meant climbing the goat track above the narrows in the dark, with a wounded old woman I was supposedly rescuing and a feverish surveyor I was definitely kidnapping, while Crane's men slept ninety meters below and my own employers' replacement team was already moving upriver from the south.

Desperation, they say, is a fine clarifier. They're wrong. Desperation is a fog. But sometimes in a fog you stop seeing the horizon, and that's its own kind of clarity—you navigate by what's directly in front of your boots, one step, then the next, and you don't ask where the cliff edge is because the answer changes nothing.

Halfway up the track, Naira stopped, leaned against the rock, and said, "They will come for us before dawn. Crane's men will find the empty tent and they will come angry, and angry men climb badly but shoot well."

"I know."

"And the girl will come too. Zephyrine Hartwell. She will come for the surveyor, and she will find you holding us both, and she will think you are the monster from her story."

"I am the monster from her story."

"No." The old woman's voice was quiet and absolutely certain, the way only people who have outlived everyone who argued with them can be certain. "You are a man in a story that has no good men in it. That is different. There may still be time to choose which one you are when it ends."

I didn't answer. The cache chamber was forty meters above us, its entrance breathing cold air through the roots, and below us—already, God help me, already—a shout went up from the camp. A lantern. Then three. Then the crackle of the sat-phone being answered, and Crane's voice, thin with distance and thick with fury, saying words I couldn't make out and didn't need to.

The clock had run out. All that was left was the ascent.

I put my shoulder under Naira's good arm, nodded Quispe upward, and started to climb—toward the sealed door, toward the honest maps, toward whatever version of myself was going to be standing on that escarpment when the storm broke and everyone came to collect.

Behind us, the first shots rang out against the rock, and somewhere below, faint through the roar of the falls, I heard a whistle. Three notes, rising.

Her father's route marker signal. She was already on the mountain.
