## 19. Devil's Throat

The storm came up the gorge like something with a grudge.

I'd been watching it build all afternoon—clouds stacking themselves against the Andes in gray towers, the air going thick and electric, the river below turning the color of old pewter. By the time Rennick's radio crackled through the trees with his terms, the first fat drops were already hitting the stone terraces, hissing where they struck sun-warmed rock.

"Zephyrine Hartwell." His voice came through the static flattened and strange, a man speaking from inside a tin can. "You have one hour. The escarpment lip. Come alone, or I put a bullet in the old woman and drop your surveyor off the edge in pieces small enough to fit in my report."

He'd taken Naira at dawn.

I'd been asleep—or close to it—in the hollow beneath the causeway stair when Tomás shook me awake, his face gray in the pre-dawn dark. "They're gone," he said. "Both of them. Naira went out to check her snares and Rennick was waiting."

Rennick had gone rogue two days before. We'd watched it happen from the high terraces: the helicopter coming down through the cloud cover, not to extract him but to erase him, the fixer's men fanning out along the rim with orders that had nothing to do with rescue. Rennick had understood it faster than I did, maybe because he'd spent his whole career being the expendable part of other people's plans. He'd seized the cache—the sealed chamber, my father's true survey, the letter addressed to *my daughter, if she ever stops moving*—and he'd taken Naira as his bargaining chip, and now he stood on the lip of Devil's Throat with a hostage, a ledger full of forged paper, and nowhere left to run except down.

Which is exactly where he wanted me.

"He needs you alive," Tomás said, pacing the terrace while the storm built. He'd been free for less than a day and his wrists still bore the rope marks, but his mind had come back sharp. "The claim doesn't hold without a Hartwell signature. That's been true since the beginning—that's why they hired you, why they hunted you, why he hasn't just shot you from a distance. You're not leverage, Zephyrine. You're the missing line on the document."

"I know." I checked the pistol I'd taken off one of Rennick's men in the narrows ambush. Three rounds. I'd counted them so many times the number felt like a heartbeat. "That's why I'm going."

"That's why you shouldn't—"

"Tomás." I made myself look at him. Rain was starting in earnest now, running down his face, and behind him the falls roared somewhere below us in the gorge, a sound like the world clearing its throat. "They have Naira. She took a bullet covering our escape and she still walked into their camp every day for a week to bring us food, and now she's standing on a cliff edge with a gun at her back because she kept faith with a stranger who carries a dead man's name. I don't get to do the math on this one. There's no version where I stay up here safe and live with it."

He opened his mouth. Closed it. Then he did the thing that made me trust him more than anything else in this whole rotten expedition—he stopped arguing and started thinking.

"The rope routes," he said.

"What about them?"

"Your father's routes. The ones you've been following by memory you didn't know you had." He wiped rain from his eyes. "Rennick knows the escarpment lip. He chose it because it's open ground, one approach, nowhere for you to maneuver. But he doesn't know what's underneath it. Your father mapped every anchor point on this gorge twenty years ago. If there's a way onto that ledge that isn't the trail—" 

"There is." It surfaced as I said it, whole and certain, the way everything of my father's had been surfacing for days now. Journal passages word for word. Handholds remembered before seen. *The gorge keeps its doors on the underside,* he'd written once. *A man watching the path never watches the wall.* "There's a traverse. A rope line pinned under the lip itself, running from the falls' eastern shoulder to the ledge. He rigged it for a survey crew that never came. Twenty years of weather, though. It'll be rotten."

"So don't trust it. Trust yourself." Tomás gripped my shoulders, and for a second the feverish junior surveyor from the hollow tree was gone entirely, and what looked back at me was someone who had decided, somewhere in the dark, what kind of man he was going to be. "You traded your name for this valley once already. Trade the rest if you have to. Just make sure Rennick pays retail."

* * *

Getting to the traverse meant crossing the falls' shoulder, which meant getting soaked to the bone in spray while lightning walked the ridgelines above me like a sentry pacing its post. My father's anchors were where his journal said they'd be—a bolt driven into living rock beneath an overhang, camouflaged by twenty years of lichen, and beside it, chiseled into the stone in letters gone shallow but still legible:

**E.H. — 1974 — FOR THE ONE WHO READS THIS**

My initials weren't E.H. They didn't need to be. He'd carved it the year I was born, on a route he never expected anyone to use, and somehow that hit harder than anything Rennick had done to me in three weeks of hunting. He hadn't known if he'd have a daughter who climbed. He'd just left the door open anyway.

I clipped in. Tested the first anchor with everything I had. It held.

The traverse itself was forty meters of vertical nothing, a handline strung along a wall that leaned out over the gorge, the river a white roar six hundred feet below. The old rope was frayed to threads in places; twice I hung my full weight on anchors I'd placed myself, hammering new pitons into cracks my father's notes described, and each time I thought about how he'd done this same work alone, with no one above, no one below, no one believing him. The rain turned to proper rain then, a deluge, and the gorge filled with thunder that wasn't thunder.

Halfway across, I found his second message, scratched into a flake of quartzite no bigger than a dinner plate:

**DON'T LOOK DOWN. LOOK ACROSS.**

So I did. Across at the escarpment lip, where three figures stood hunched against the storm—Rennick tall and wet and wrong, Naira kneeling between two of his men with her hands bound, her gray braid plastered to her skull, her chin up the way she always held it, as if the weather were a personal insult she intended to survive out of spite. And beside them, on the ground, a waterproof case. The cache. My father's honest maps, his letter, twenty years of truth packed into a box a strong man could carry and a desperate man couldn't put down.

I reached the ledge below the lip with four minutes to spare and climbed the last ten feet into plain view, hands empty, pistol left in its holster where Rennick could see it.

"Alone," I called over the wind. "Like you asked."

His men flanked me before I finished the sentence—one of them taking my knife, the other patting me down with rough efficiency and coming away with the pistol. Rennick watched all of it without expression. Up close he looked worse than the last time I'd seen him: the rogue chief's confidence had burned off him like fog, and what remained was a man doing arithmetic he kept losing. The fixer's helicopter had come for him two days ago and it hadn't landed. He knew what that meant. Everyone above him had signed off on his erasure, and the only asset he had left was a forged land claim and the woman whose signature could legitimize it.

"You came," he said.

"You knew I would."

"I hoped." He almost smiled. "Hoping's cheaper than knowing. Cheaper than planning. That's the difference between us, Hartwell—you plan, and it costs you everything. I hope, and it costs everyone else." He nodded at Naira. "Your signature on the transfer documents. Then the old woman walks."

"Liar," Naira said, conversationally, like she was commenting on the weather.

"She's right," I told him. "We both know she walks either way or she doesn't, and the signature only changes what happens to me. So here's my counteroffer." I stepped forward, into the rain, close enough that he had to decide whether to step back. "Me for her. Now. Unbound, unshot, and walking. You get your hostage exchange, I get mine, and nobody does arithmetic on a cliff edge in a lightning storm."

"And why would I take that trade?"

"Because I'm worth more to you unbound than she is bound. Because every minute you stand here, your own employers are closer to finding this ledge. And because—" I let him see me look at the case, at the cache, at the thing he thought was his last card, "—because you haven't read the letter yet. Have you?"

Something flickered in his face. First doubt I'd seen there.

"The letter," I repeated. "In the cache. Addressed to me. From my father. The man who found this city, mapped it honestly, and refused to sign your precious claim—which means the forgery has a hole in it the size of a dead man's conscience, and the only person alive who can testify to what's actually in that chamber is standing right in front of you getting rained on. Kill her, keep me bound, and you're holding a box you can't open and a witness who won't talk. Let her go, and maybe I will."

It was a bluff stacked on a bluff. The letter was real—I'd held it, in the chamber, three nights ago—but everything else was improvisation, and Rennick knew it, and for five long seconds the only sound was the falls and the storm arguing with each other about who got to be louder.

Then he jerked his chin at his men. "Cut her loose. Walk her to the trailhead. If Hartwell moves before she's clear, shoot them both."

They cut Naira's bindings. She got to her feet slowly, joints cracking, and paused beside me just long enough to press something into my palm—small, wet, warm from her skin. Her voice was a breath under the roar: *"The old route. Below. Your father's door."*

Then she was walking, slow and proud, toward the treeline, and I was alone on the lip with Rennick and his arithmetic.

"Now," he said, pulling the case toward him, "the signature."

"Open the case first."

"Why?"

"Because if you're going to threaten me with what's inside, you should know what you're threatening me with." I nodded at it. "Go on. It's not locked. It never was. My father figured anyone determined enough to find it deserved to look."

Rennick hesitated—and that hesitation was the whole trap, the hinge everything turned on, because a careful man would have put a gun on me and opened it with his boot between us. But Rennick wasn't careful anymore. Careful men don't go rogue. Desperate men go rogue, and desperate men, when offered the thing they want most, reach for it with both hands.

He crouched. Flipped the latches. Lifted the lid—and the wind, which had been building all evening, chose that exact moment to slam into the escarpment lip with the full weight of the storm behind it, tearing the papers out of the case in a great white flurry, maps and pages and the letter itself lifting into the air like a flock of startled birds.

Rennick lunged. I was already moving.

I went low and left, the direction his weight was committed against, and drove my shoulder into the case itself—not him, the case, because the case was what he couldn't let go of. He caught it. Of course he caught it. And for one suspended moment we were both anchored to that box while the papers streamed past us into the gorge, and his man raised his rifle—

—and the lightning hit the ridge behind us, close enough that the world went white and the rifleman flinched, and in that flinch I pulled the pin on the last trick my father had left me.

The escarpment lip was not natural ground. My father's journal had been specific about this: *the overhang is shelf stone resting on clay, a hundred tons of it balanced on a hand's width of erosion.* He'd flagged it in his survey. He'd even, God help him, drilled a weep hole through the clay seam to monitor it, and plugged the hole with a wooden dowel that twenty years of rain had been quietly rotting.

I'd knocked the dowel out an hour earlier, on my way in along the traverse.

The shelf groaned. Just once, deep, like a whale sounding. Rennick heard it—everybody heard it—and in the half-second of pure animal confusion before understanding arrived, I let go of the case and threw myself backward off the lip.

Free fall. One breath of it. Then the traverse line snapped taut and I swung in a great arc under the escarpment, out over the open throat of the gorge, the storm screaming past me, the river a mile of white noise below—and above me the shelf stone let go.

A hundred tons of rock and one desperate man went over the edge together. Rennick got one hand onto the lip, one arm wrapped around the case, and hung there while the cliff crumbled around his grip, and his men shouted and ran for the trail, and Naira, bless her spiteful heart, had apparently doubled back along the treeline because a thrown machete suddenly buried itself in the wrist of the man raising his rifle at my swinging body.

I hauled myself along the traverse, hand over hand, toward the place where Rennick hung.

He saw me coming. Even now, even hanging by one hand over Devil's Throat with his evidence dissolving in the river mist below, he saw me coming and understood, and his face did something complicated—hate and arithmetic and, briefly, something almost like respect.

"Hartwell," he shouted over the roar. "The claim! Help me up and the claim burns! I'll swear it was never sanctioned!"

"It was never sanctioned!" I shouted back. "That's the whole point!"

"I have the letter! Your father's letter!"

"Read it first!"

And here is the thing I will carry to my grave, the thing I'll never say out loud in any testimony in any Lima courtroom: I could have saved him. The traverse passed within six feet of his handhold. I had a sling, I had carabiners, I had thirty seconds and the strength to use them. Some cold and competent version of me ran that calculation in real time, the way I'd run a hundred rescues, and found the answer *yes*, and then I looked at his other hand—the one wrapped around the case, the one he would not, even now, even dangling over the throat of the devil himself, let go of—and I understood that he had already chosen. He was holding the forgery instead of the rock. He'd been making that choice his entire life.

I reached him anyway. I got a sling around his wrist. I said, "Let go of the case."

He looked at me. Rain in his eyes. The shelf groaning again above him, the rest of it going.

"Zephyrine," he said, and used my first name for the first time, and there was something in his voice I hadn't expected, something almost young. "What was in the letter?"

And because he was going to die anyway, and because some debts should be paid even to bad men, I told him the truth: "It said *stop running.*"

His fingers opened.

Whether he let go or the rock decided for him, I genuinely don't know. The case went first, cartwheeling down six hundred feet of roaring air, and then Rennick followed it, and the gorge took them both the way the gorge takes everything—completely, indifferently, without echo. The shelf settled with a long grinding sigh. The storm broke overhead like a dam giving way.

I hung on my father's rope in the rain, sixty feet below a cliff that had nearly eaten me, and laughed until it turned into something else.

* * *

Naira was waiting at the top of the traverse, soaked through, machete recovered, expression suggesting she had opinions about my plan and intended to deliver all of them at length once I'd stopped shaking.

"The dowel," she said. "You planned that."

"My father planned that. In 1974. For a survey crew that never showed up." I sat down hard on the wet stone. "I just knocked out the plug."

"You dropped a man into the falls."

"He was holding on to the wrong things."

She considered this. Somewhere in the gorge below, the river kept whatever it kept. Lightning walked away north along the cordillera, and the rain softened, and through a tear in the clouds the late light came down the valley sideways, gold on wet stone, the whole escarpment shining like something newly made.

"Naira," I said. "The letter's gone. Into the river. His words, his handwriting—all of it."

She lowered herself beside me, old joints folding with the ease of long practice. "You read it?"

"In the chamber. Three times."

"Then it isn't gone." She tapped my chest with one knuckle, hard, the way she did when she was making a point she expected to outlive us both. "Paper is just the river's first draft. What your father wrote, you already carry. And what you carry—" she nodded down the valley, toward the hidden city sleeping under its terraces, toward the people who had kept it secret for five hundred years, "—you will spend the rest of your life deciding what to do with. Starting tomorrow. Starting with the officials who are coming up this river asking questions."

I looked at my hands. Rope-burned, bleeding, steady.

"They'll ask where the city is," I said.

"Yes."

"And Tomás will want to tell them. He's a scientist. It's the find of a century."

"Yes," Naira said again, and smiled, and it was not entirely a comforting smile. "So you had better decide, Zephyrine Hartwell, daughter of Emilio, what kind of mapmaker you intend to be. Because they arrive at the confluence in two days."

Below us, far down in the gorge, the river roared on—taking what it takes, keeping what it keeps—and for the first time in my life, I noticed that I wasn't running from the sound.

I was listening to it.
