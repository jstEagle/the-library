## 21. Testimony

They gave me a tie to wear. A government man, some functionary from the Ministerio Público with sweat rings under his arms, held it out on a plastic hanger like it was a medal, and I stood there in the doorway of the witness room thinking: this is what it comes to. Twenty-two days on a river, a bullet through my shoulder, four months of statements and depositions and a lawyer who kept calling me "the surviving witness" as though survival were my profession—and the state's final gesture is a polyester tie with a faint curry stain near the knot.

I put it on. I'm not proud of that, but I put it on.

"Deep breaths," the functionary said, patting my arm. "The commission is not hostile. They just want the truth."

The truth. I had learned on the river that the truth is not a thing you hand over. It's a thing you carry, and it changes weight depending on the altitude.

* * *

The hearing room in Lima was colder than any place I had been in a year, including the night we spent above the Devil's Throat with the wind coming off the glacier like a debt collector. Long table, seven commissioners, microphones on little flexible necks like patient cobras. A stenographer. A woman with a camcorder on a tripod who kept adjusting the zoom on my face as if I might bolt.

They seated me facing the room, which meant that when I looked up from my notes I was looking directly into the public gallery, and that is how I saw her.

Third row, against the wall, under the window with the cracked blind. Grey cardigan. Hair pulled back in a way that made her look like a schoolteacher, which Zephyrine Hartwell is about as much as a condor is a pigeon. She had done something to her face, too—not makeup, exactly, but an arrangement of stillness, the kind of expression you wear when you want to be furniture. She'd told me on the phone from Cusco: *I'm not coming to testify, Tomás. I'll be there, but I'm not a witness. Witnesses get names.*

So there she was, a nameless schoolteacher in the third row, and when our eyes met she gave me nothing at all, which from Zephyrine is a full sentence. It means: *Get on with it. Tell it straight. Don't perform for me.*

I had spent four months deciding how to tell it. There were versions. There was the short version, which was a lie by subtraction: *the expedition was murdered, the company did it, here are the names.* There was the long version, which was a lie by drowning: every rapid, every fever, every hour of Rennick's interrogation rendered in such loving detail that the commissioners would drown in it and sign whatever was put in front of them just to make the water stop.

And there was the true version, which had no shape at all, because the truth about the Devil's Throat was that it had been true for five hundred years before any of us arrived, and would go on being true after we were all footnotes.

I took a breath and started with the radio.

* * *

"The radio was smashed on the ninth of March," I said. "I know because I was the one who maintained it. Voss—Expedition Leader Voss—had a ritual about it. Every evening, six o'clock, he'd key the transmitter twice, just a click, no voice. A heartbeat to base camp. On the ninth, the heartbeat stopped."

Commissioner Arana, the chairwoman, a small woman with reading glasses on a chain, leaned forward. "And you concluded this was deliberate."

"I concluded the radio had been struck with a rifle butt approximately four times, ma'am. The conclusion came later."

There was a small sound from the gallery. I didn't look up. I knew that sound. It was Zephyrine not laughing, which she does with tremendous discipline.

I told it in order, because order was the only dignity I had left. The causeway above the gorge. The stone stair, old beyond old, and Voss going quiet the way men go quiet when they see something that rearranges the furniture of their ambition. The city—no, I didn't call it a city at first, I called it "an extensive pre-Columbian settlement," because the lawyers had coached me, and then I stopped, because the lawyers were not the ones who had stood on those terraces.

"It was a city," I said. "Roads. Reservoirs still holding water. Agricultural terraces cut so precisely that the joints haven't shifted in—Naira would know how long. Longer than anything. It was a city, and it was not lost. It was *kept*. There's a difference. Lost things are accidents. Kept things are decisions."

I watched Arana write that down.

Then the hard part. The part I had rehearsed in a hundred hotel rooms and never once gotten through without my hands shaking. The men with company rifles turning on the crew at the narrows. Delgado, who had taught me to read contour lines, going into the water. The others. Voss's face in the last second—not afraid, I told them, and this was the detail I could never leave out, the one that kept me up at night—Voss's face *ashamed*. He had signed things, you see. Early on, before the expedition, he had signed things for the company, and the city above the gorge was the bill coming due.

"Rennick interrogated me for three days," I said. "He wanted a statement. Not a confession—a statement. Pinning the deaths on a rival surveyor. Pinning the forgery on a dead man named Hartwell, who had been dead twenty years and could not object, and whose daughter was to be named as an accomplice, on the theory that a blacklisted guide would do anything for a claim."

I looked up then. I couldn't help it. Third row, grey cardigan, still as furniture.

"She wasn't an accomplice," I said. "She was the only person in this entire affair who behaved as though other people were real. I'd like that entered into the record."

"Entered," said Arana, dry as the coast. "Though the commission prefers evidence to character references, Mr. Quispe."

"Then I have four months of evidence," I said, "and one character reference, and I'll tell you which one kept me alive."

* * *

They broke for lunch. A court officer walked me to a sandwich and a glass of chicha morada in an anteroom, and through the window I watched Lima do its grey, magnificent, indifferent thing—traffic like a river that had given up on banks, gulls arguing over the Plaza.

Zephyrine found me there. Of course she did. She came in with two more sandwiches like she owned the anteroom, sat down across from me, and said, "You said *city*."

"The lawyers wanted *settlement*."

"The lawyers wanted a lot of things. Rennick wanted things. Voss wanted things." She unwrapped a sandwich, inspected it, ate it anyway. "Everyone in this story wanted something. You're the first one who just *said* something."

"Is that approval? Because it sounded like a eulogy."

"It sounded like lunch." But her mouth did the thing it does, the almost-smile she rations like water on a dry traverse. "Naira watched the proceedings on the radio in the valley. The community has a transmitter now. Your testimony is being translated into Ashaninka as we speak, sentence by sentence. An old man is doing it who refuses to sit down between sentences. You're apparently very dramatic in Ashaninka."

"Am I?"

"You called the reservoirs *still holding water*. He made everyone wait while he found a way to say it that kept the water in it." She brushed crumbs from her cardigan. "That's the part that matters, Tomás. Not the commission. The water."

I wanted to ask her the question I'd been carrying since the cache, since the letter addressed to *my daughter, if she ever stops moving*. Whether she'd stopped. Whether she could. But Zephyrine answers questions a beat before you ask them or three weeks after, and never on schedule, so instead I said, "They're going to ask me about the maps tomorrow. The ones we gave to Naira's people. They're going to ask where the valley is, formally, on the record."

"I know."

"And I'm going to refuse."

"I know that too." She looked at the window, at the gulls. "You understand what that costs. Contempt, possibly. The lawyers will call it obstruction. The company's ghosts will call it worse."

"The company's ghosts," I said, "are currently testifying against each other in three separate rooms of this building. The fixer is blaming Rennick, Rennick's lawyers are blaming the fixer, and the board is blaming a subsidiary that apparently existed only on paper, which is the most honest thing the company ever created." I leaned forward. "Zeph. They don't need the coordinates. They need to hear someone say *no* in a room with microphones. That's the whole testimony. Everything else is geography."

She was quiet for a while. Then: "Her father used to say that a map is a promise you make to strangers." She stood, gathered the wrappers. "Make the promise to the right strangers."

And she was gone before I could ask which strangers she meant, which was also very her.

* * *

The afternoon session was the company. Not the men—the *company*, a legal person, represented by three actual persons in excellent suits, who had the particular smoothness of people being paid by the hour to apologize without apologizing. Their lead counsel, a silver-haired man named Orbegoso, spent two hours establishing that Andes Condor Mining S.A.C. deeply regretted the "unauthorized actions of former personnel," that the land claim in question was "the product of a rogue subsidiary," and that the corporation "welcomed the commission's scrutiny."

Welcomed. I wrote the word in my notebook and underlined it twice. Rennick had welcomed things too, right up until the helicopter came for him.

Then Orbegoso made his one mistake. He was cross-examining me about the theodolite—the brass one, stamped *E.H.*, the one I'd carried out of the gorge clutched to my chest like a child, the one that had started everything—and he said, with a small, humane smile, "Mr. Quispe, isn't it true that this instrument was recovered from the expedition's stores, and that its presence in your possession proves nothing except sentiment?"

And I said, "It proves the surveyor who owned it mapped this territory honestly in nineteen seventy-one, when every other surveyor on the company's payroll was drawing lines through land that didn't belong to the company. It proves that when he was asked to sign the forgery, he refused. It proves that the last thing he did on this earth was the right thing, and it took twenty years and seven dead men for anyone to notice. So yes, señor. Sentiment."

The stenographer's keys clattered. Arana took her glasses off, which I would later learn is what she does when she's about to end someone's afternoon.

"Mr. Orbegoso," she said, "does your client maintain that the nineteen seventy-one survey by Esteban Hartwell was commissioned by Andes Condor?"

A pause you could have rappelled into. "The commission is aware that the company's archives from that period are... incomplete."

"Incomplete," said Arana. "Or *erased*? Because this commission has heard from an archivist, Mr. Orbegoso, who testified that the Hartwell file was pulled in March of last year, eleven days before Expedition 7 departed, and has not been seen since. The commission is aware of a great many things. What the commission is still deciding is who to be patient with."

I have never heard a courtroom go so quiet. Somewhere in the third row, a grey cardigan shifted, and I swear the whole room heard it.

* * *

It took three more days. Testimony is not a waterfall; it's a glacier, moving a centimeter a day, grinding everything under it flat. The fixer—Serrano, the man who'd hired Zephyrine off the books—turned state's evidence with the enthusiasm of a man jumping from a burning building. Rennick's men, caught in Bolivia with forged papers, sang in harmony. The forged claim was read aloud in the hearing room, all its elegant lies, the false signatures including one purporting to be Esteban Hartwell's, dated four years after his death, which even the company's lawyers conceded was "an administrative error."

An administrative error. The man's corpse, conscripted. I thought of Zephyrine burning the original claim in the gorge, the pages going up like startled birds, and how she'd said afterward, *Now it's only true. Now it can't be used.*

On the last day, Arana read the findings. The company's concessions in the corridor of the Río Alto were void. Criminal referrals: Serrano, Rennick, two directors, a notary whose name made Orbegoso close his eyes. And then, near the end, in the flat administrative voice governments use for the things that matter most:

"Regarding the surveyor Esteban Hartwell: the commission finds that his nineteen seventy-one survey was accurate, honest, and conducted with the cooperation of the Ashaninka communities of the upper valley; that his refusal to certify the fraudulent claim was the proximate cause of his professional ruin; and that the subsequent blacklisting of his name, extended after his death to his daughter, Zephyrine Hartwell, was without legal basis and sustained only by the commercial interests this commission has today referred for prosecution." A pause. The glasses came off. "The commission notes, with regret, that this correction arrives twenty years late, and to a woman who, the record shows, never once asked for it."

That was it. No trumpet. The blacklist didn't shatter; it just quietly stopped existing, the way a fever breaks in the night—you wake and the weight is gone and you can't point to the moment.

An apology no one quite said. I looked for her in the gallery.

She was already standing, already at the door, grey cardigan, anonymous by choice, and just before she slipped out she did the thing I will keep longer than any verdict: she took off the schoolteacher stillness for exactly one second and looked at me the way she'd looked at me from the raft in the rapids, the day we stopped running—*not gratitude, not triumph, just recognition, one survivor to another*—and then she was gone through the door, into the corridor, into the rest of her life.

The commissioners were gathering papers. A clerk touched my elbow: "Mr. Quispe, the press is asking for a statement."

The press. Cameras. My name in the papers next to hers, finally spelled right.

I thought about the valley, the transmitter, the old man translating sentence by sentence, refusing to sit down. I thought about a brass theodolite in a duffel bag under my hotel bed, and a marked tree on a river I knew the way you know your own heartbeat, and a promise about strangers and maps.

"Tell them," I said, "that the witness has one more trip to make—and that this time, nobody's paying him."

I was on the night bus to Cusco before the verdict hit the papers. Some things you don't testify to. Some things you just go and do.
