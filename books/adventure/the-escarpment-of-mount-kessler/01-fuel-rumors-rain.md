## 1. Fuel, Rumors, Rain

The rain came up the valley like a rumor — first as a smell on the wind, then as a gray smudge over the escarpment, and only at the end as the thing itself, falling.

Rafael Nakamura watched it build from the open door of the Cessna Caravan while the fuel man dragged the hose across the apron at Kessler Landing. She'd been flying this route long enough to read weather the way other people read faces. The monsoon wasn't here yet. But it was coming, and it was coming early, and everyone in the valley knew it except the men in the concession office who kept signing schedules written for a drier world.

"Forty minutes to full tanks," the fuel man shouted over the idling engine. He was a boy of maybe nineteen with sunburned ears and a concession polo two sizes too big. "You want the drums too?"

"All six." She'd already counted them off the manifest in her head: diesel for the dredge camp, chain oil, rice, salt, medical stores, and one crate marked FRAGILE that she'd been told not to ask about and hadn't. Seven years of flying for Kessler Consolidated had taught her which questions cost money. "Load them aft. I want the CG right."

"You always want the CG right."

"That's why I'm still alive."

He grinned and went to work, and Rafael turned back to the sky. The escarpment stood across the northern horizon like a wall someone had built and then abandoned — four hundred meters of gray limestone, forested to the crown, with the river falling off it in three white steps visible even from here, forty kilometers downstream. Above it, the clouds were stacking themselves into something with intent. Not the scattered afternoon thunderheads she could thread around. This was a ceiling, a lid being lowered onto the whole upper valley.

She'd been born under different skies, but she'd learned this one. That was the arrangement she'd made: this valley got her labor, her caution, her silence, and in exchange it never asked where she'd come from. The river didn't care about names. The river cared whether you respected its current, its fog, its gravel bars that moved every flood season like furniture in a house you couldn't quite memorize. She trusted the river more than any person she'd met since the accident, and the river had never once betrayed her. People were another matter. People had memories, and memories had teeth.

The radio crackled behind her in the cockpit. "Caravan Seven-Three, Landing Traffic, you still planning the upstream run today?"

Rafael leaned into the cabin and keyed the mic. "That's the plan."

"Ceiling's dropping fast above the falls," said the voice — Delia Marsh, the concession's radio operator, a woman who treated every transmission as an invitation to conversation. "Survey team hasn't checked in since Tuesday, either. You hear anything on the way up?"

"Radio silence works both ways, Delia. I don't carry gossip."

"You carry everything else." A pause, static filling it like water fills a hull. "Brandt's been asking about them too. He sent a boat up yesterday. Came back empty."

Rafael looked at the escarpment. The survey team — five geologists and their botanist, flown up three weeks ago to core samples out of the strata above the falls — had gone quiet the way equipment goes quiet, not the way people do. Or so the concession claimed. She'd hauled their gear herself: drills, sample cases, a radio set worth more than her annual salary. They'd waved from the gravel bar like children going off to school.

"Maybe they're busy," she said.

"Five days?" Delia's voice did something careful. "In monsoon season, with a storm building? Rafa, come on. You've flown up there a hundred times. You know what that plateau does when the weather closes."

"I know what it does. Which is why I'll be back down before it closes."

Another pause. When Delia spoke again, the friendliness had thinned, and underneath it Rafael heard the thing she always heard in Kessler Landing eventually: the sound of someone who wanted to know her and couldn't be allowed to. "You never talk about yourself, you know that? Three years on this route. I don't know where you learned to fly, or why a woman who flies like you do is hauling diesel drums for a mining company."

"Because they pay me."

"Nobody flies like that for the pay."

Rafael let the static answer for her. Outside, the fuel boy was wrestling the last drum onto the cargo ramp, and beyond him the town went about its business: the dredge crews' laundry strung between stilt houses, the company store shuttering early against the rain, two concession security men standing under the awning of the office with the particular stillness of men whose job was watching. One of them raised a hand to her — not friendly, exactly. Confirmatory. As if checking her off a list.

She raised two fingers back and turned away.

* * *

Full tanks, six drums lashed, crate secured. Rafael ran the preflight twice because the weather deserved it, then swung the Caravan onto the strip and took off into the rising wind, climbing south first to gain altitude before turning north toward the escarpment. The river unspooled beneath her, brown and swollen already, carrying whole islands of torn-up forest toward the delta. In another week it would take the low landing stages too. In another week nobody would fly this valley but fools and the dead.

The concession's grip showed itself in small ways from the air: the new fence around the fuel depot, the second patrol boat moored at the company dock, the fresh survey stakes marching up the tributary valleys where no survey had any business being. Kessler Consolidated had arrived seven years ago, the same season she had, and in that time it had grown from a camp to a town to a fact of geography, the way a tumor grows — patiently, fed by permits signed in offices she'd never see. The miners called it progress. The river people called it nothing at all, because calling it anything got your dredge license reviewed.

Ahead, the escarpment had disappeared entirely behind the cloud deck. She climbed toward it anyway, watching the altimeter, watching the gray wall thicken, feeling the air go rough the way it did when a storm system was feeding on the heat below. At nine thousand feet she leveled off into scud and flew on instruments and memory both, counting minutes to the plateau.

The survey camp sat on a bench of flat ground above the falls, a cluster of dome tents and drill rigs she could usually pick out through a gap in the clouds. Today there was no gap. There was only the ceiling, lowering, and the radio, silent, and the growing certainty in her chest that had nothing to do with meteorology.

"Seven-Three, Landing Traffic," Delia's voice said, faint through the static. "Be advised, Brandt wants a visual confirmation on the camp. If you can't get in, you are to return and he'll send the boat again."

"He already sent the boat," Rafael said.

"The boat came back empty," Delia repeated, and this time there was something else under it. Something that sounded, in the crackle and hiss, almost like fear. "Rafa. Nobody came back."

Rafael flew north into the gray.

* * *

The plateau appeared beneath her all at once, the way land does when you punch through cloud — sudden, close, and wrong. She saw the camp from half a kilometer out and knew before she circled that the rumors were true, though she couldn't yet have said how she knew. The tents were down. Not collapsed by wind — wind leaves patterns — but flattened, cut, dragged. The drill rig stood untouched, absurdly neat, its boom folded like a sleeping insect. And beside the camp, in the trampled mud, was a rectangle of darker earth the size of a grave, mounded shallowly at one end, as if whoever dug it had run out of time or interest halfway through.

No people. No smoke. No movement at all.

Rafael banked, throttled back, and dropped low along the bench, looking for signs of life, for a flare, for anything. The wind hit her from the west without warning — the storm's leading edge shoving over the escarpment lip — and shoved the Caravan sideways like a hand on her shoulder. She corrected, corrected again, felt the plane wallow heavy with its drums, and made the decision she would spend the rest of this story paying for: one more pass, lower, to look for survivors.

The squall took the wing on the turn.

It didn't break it. It lifted it, slammed it, dropped fifty meters of altitude out from under her in a single sickening breath, and suddenly the trees were coming up off the plateau edge toward her windshield and the river was a white braid far below and there was nowhere to go but forward and down. She fought it — rudder, trim, full throttle, the drums groaning in their lashings — and cleared the escarpment lip by a margin she would later measure in meters, and then the downdraft on the lee side caught her like a trapdoor opening.

The gravel bar came up fast. She flared into it the way you're taught to put a dying plane into water — wings level, nose high, take the impact on the belly — and the Caravan hit, skidded, dug in, and stopped with a violence that threw her against the harness and filled the world with the sound of tearing aluminum and then, after, with rain.

* * *

For a while there was only the rain, hammering the broken fuselage, and Rafael's own breathing, loud inside her headset, and the taste of blood where she'd bitten her lip.

Then, through the shattered side window, she heard footsteps in the gravel. Fast ones. Coming toward her.

Someone was shouting her name — not her name, her callsign, Seven-Three, Seven-Three — in a woman's voice cracked raw with running and fear, and Rafael Nakamura, who trusted the wilderness more than any person alive, reached for her door handle with a shaking hand and prepared to meet whatever the escarpment had decided to send her.
