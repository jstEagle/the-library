## 2. The Silence Above the Falls

Brandt was waiting at the fuel drums when Rafael came off the dock, and that alone told her the day had gone wrong. Managers of the Kessler Concession did not wait on docks. They sat in the air-conditioned office above the warehouse and sent runners, and you came to them like a supplicant with your manifest and your complaints about spare parts.

He stood with his hands in his pockets, watching the river as if it owed him something. Rain beaded on his shoulders and he didn't seem to notice it. Behind him, the concession's compound climbed the low bluff — the corrugated roofs, the diesel smell, the flag of a company she'd never bothered to learn flying limp over the gatehouse.

"Nakamura." He didn't turn. "You're current on the Cessna."

It wasn't a question, but she answered it anyway. "Current enough."

"The survey team's gone quiet." Now he turned, and his face had the flat careful look of a man who had already decided what he wanted to hear. "Three days. Last transmission was routine. Then nothing. I've tried every band, every hour."

"Radios die up there," Rafael said. "Solar panels get fouled, batteries crack in the cold at night. You know this. You've been running camps on this plateau for six years."

"I know this." He said it the way a man repeats an insult he's decided to tolerate. "I also know the weather window is closing. The monsoon line comes over the escarpment tomorrow, maybe the day after, and after that nobody goes up or down for two weeks. So you're going to fly up there today, land at their strip, look around, and come back and tell me my geologists are sitting around a stove complaining about the coffee."

Rafael looked past him at the sky. It was wrong and getting wronger — a bruised green-gray stacking up over the western rim, the kind of light that made her teeth ache. She'd flown into weather before. Everyone who flew the Kessler had; the valley didn't offer clean options, only degrees of bad.

"Alone?" she said.

"You're the only pilot I've got who knows the plateau approaches." He said *only pilot* the way other men said *expendable*, but there was something else under it, something she couldn't name yet. "Take the 206. Full tanks. Be back by dark."

"And if they're not sitting around a stove?"

"Then you'll have done your job and I'll have mine." He finally smiled, a small transactional thing. "Radio check at the top of the hour. If I don't hear from you—"

"You'll send someone worse prepared than me," Rafael finished. "I know how it works."

She walked to the hangar without hurrying, because hurrying would have meant admitting the dread that had settled in her chest somewhere between Brandt's second sentence and his fourth. Seven years in this valley had taught her to read men like weather, and Brandt's front had a pressure drop in it she didn't like. He wasn't worried about his geologists. He was worried about what they'd found, or what they'd say, or who they'd say it to. And he wanted her gone up the mountain before the storm so that whatever happened next could happen without witnesses.

Or she was paranoid. Seven years of building a new name out of old fear did that to a person. She preflighted the Cessna with more care than it needed, checking the fuel samples twice, wiggling surfaces, staring into the cowling at an engine that had never given her a reason to doubt it.

The radio operator, a boy named Tusi with a face full of acne and admiration, helped her pull the chocks. "You want me to keep a listening watch for you, Miss Nakamura?"

"Keep one for everybody," she said. "That's the job."

"My cousin says the survey team found gold up there." He said it lightly, testing. "Big strike. That's why the company's been flying all those extra loads."

"Your cousin says a lot of things."

"He says the team stopped reporting because they didn't want the company to know how big it was. That they were going to—" He caught himself, glanced toward the office windows, lowered his voice. "Going to make their own deal."

Rafael swung up into the pilot's seat and latched the door. "Tell your cousin gold makes people stupid," she said. "And tell him the rain's coming either way."

She taxied out onto the strip with the wind already gusting crossways, took the runway, and lifted into air that felt like a held breath.

* * *

The climb up the Kessler gorge was forty minutes of white-knuckle work even in good weather, and this wasn't good weather. The cloud base sagged lower with every mile, ragged and fast-moving, and the escarpment itself — that great limestone wall the whole valley was named for — kept appearing and disappearing through the murk like something surfacing to watch her.

She flew the route from memory: the bend at Two Sisters Rapids, the drowned forest where the river spread wide and slow, then the long climb along the falls where the water dropped four hundred feet in a single silver stair. The survey strip sat on the plateau above, a scraped gravel scar in the forest canopy, and she'd landed there a dozen times hauling core boxes and diesel and once, memorably, a very unhappy surveyor's dog.

At the top of the hour she keyed the radio. "Kessler Base, Nakamura. Approaching the plateau. Weather is marginal, repeat, marginal."

Static answered her. Then, faintly, Tusi's voice, thin and broken: "—copy, Nakamura. Base acknowledges. Be advised—" and the signal dissolved into the mountain's own noise.

She pressed on, because turning back now meant landing at the concession with no answer and doing this again tomorrow in worse weather, and because some part of her — the part she didn't examine — needed to see the camp with her own eyes.

She broke through the last cloud layer two hundred feet above the trees and the plateau opened beneath her, green and vast and streaming with rain. The strip appeared where it always was, and she saw the problem before she saw anything else.

No windsock. No movement. And parked crooked at the edge of the gravel, the survey team's quad bike, tipped on its side like a dead insect.

Rafael brought the Cessna in short and slow, fighting a gusting quartering wind, and set down harder than she liked. She rolled to the far end and shut down, and the silence that rushed in when the engine died was the loudest thing she'd heard in seven years.

The camp stood a hundred meters off the strip, a cluster of dome tents and a cook shelter, and it had been wrecked. Not burned, not blown apart by weather — wrecked, deliberately, the way a man wrecks a thing when he has time and permission. Tents slashed open, gear strewn across the mud, the cook shelter's table snapped in half. A solar panel hung from its mount by one wire, swinging.

The radio shack was worse. Someone had taken a machete or an axe to the equipment — both sets smashed, the antennas torn down, the batteries cracked open and left to bleed acid into the dirt. Whoever did it hadn't wanted the team silenced temporarily. They'd wanted them unreachable forever.

Rafael walked the perimeter with her heart going hard and her hands wanting a weapon she didn't carry. Boot prints everywhere, churned to mush by rain. At least three sets, maybe four, heavy-treaded, the kind issued by companies that outfitted their security contractors well.

Near the fire pit she found the grave.

It was dug too shallow. Anyone who'd spent a season in the tropics knew you went deep — deep past the dogs, deep past the rain, deep enough to give a body its dignity against the worms. This hole was barely two feet down, hastily refilled, the mound already slumping, and no marker, no name, nothing. Just a disturbed rectangle of darker earth the size of a man curled on his side.

She didn't dig. Her hands actually shook with the not-digging of it, but she made herself step back, because whatever was in that hole, she couldn't help it, and digging it up would change nothing except what she carried out of here.

She counted tents. Four. The survey team had been five people — she'd flown three of them in herself over the spring, and the manifests lived in her head the way manifests always did. Dr. Anselm, the team lead, loud and bearded. The two young techs, brothers, inseparable. The geochemist, a quiet woman whose name Rafael had never learned. And the botanist — Vale, Imogen Vale, sharp-eyed, sharp-tongued, the one who'd argued with Brandt at the landing strip about sampling sites until Brandt's jaw went white.

Four tents. One shallow grave. Four missing geologists, unaccounted for, in country that ate the careless alive.

The wind was rising. She could feel the squall line coming off the western rim like a freight train, could see it now, a gray wall swallowing the far end of the plateau. Every instinct she owned screamed at her to be airborne, to be below the cloud, to be anywhere but standing in an open field holding a dead man's silence.

She ran for the plane.

* * *

The takeoff nearly killed her, and she would spend the rest of her life knowing exactly which decision killed her.

She should have waited. She should have tied the aircraft down, gotten under the cook shelter's collapsed roof, ridden out the squall on the ground like every rule she'd ever taught younger pilots. But the grave was behind her and the storm was ahead of her and the thought of spending the night on this plateau — near that shallow hole, near those boot prints, within reach of whoever had made them — was worse than any weather.

She lined up on the strip with the wall of rain fifty meters away and closing, pushed the throttle to the stop, and flew into it.

The world went white. Rain hit the windshield like thrown gravel, visibility dropped to nothing, and the wind took the tail and shoved it sideways. She fought it down into the valley, instruments and instinct, descending blind along the escarpment face where she knew — she *thought* she knew — the terrain. The downdrafts off that cliff face in a squall were legendary. She'd warned a hundred passengers about them.

One picked the Cessna up and threw it.

The aircraft rolled hard left, and she hauled it level with both hands and both arms screaming, and then the trees were there, closer than they should have been, taller, and she clipped the crown of a emergent hardwood with her left wingtip and the world became noise and rotation and green.

She got the nose down. Some deep animal part of her got the nose down and killed the power and steered the falling machine toward the gray ribbon she could barely see through the shattered windshield — the river, the wide slow water above the drowned forest — and then gravel, white water, impact, and blackness.

* * *

Sound came back first. Rain, steady and enormous, drumming on metal. Then pain, a hot wire pulled tight from her temple down her neck. Then cold — river water, ankle-deep, flooding through the footwell.

The Cessna sat nose-down on a gravel bar, half in the shallows, its left wing folded like a broken arm, the propeller bent into a sculpture of its former self. Through the spiderwebbed glass Rafael could see the escarpment rising behind her, invisible now in the rain, and the river sliding past, brown and swollen and fast.

Alive. She was alive. She checked herself with shaking hands — scalp bleeding, ribs on the right side singing when she breathed, left ankle swelling inside her boot. Nothing bent that shouldn't be. Nothing numb that shouldn't tingle.

She tried the radio. Dead, along with everything else electrical. She tried the door, and it opened, and the rain came in and washed the blood off her face and she laughed — one short, ugly bark of a laugh — because of course. Of course the storm, the crash, the wrecked camp, the shallow grave, all of it arriving together like it had been scheduled.

She dragged herself out onto the gravel and stood swaying in the downpour, looking at the wreck that had just ended her livelihood and very nearly her life, and the dread that had been riding her since Brandt's dock hardened into something else. Something colder and more useful.

Nobody knew where she was. Nobody was coming. The concession would assume the storm took her, and Brandt — Brandt might even prefer it that way, a lost pilot making a tidy footnote under a lost survey team. The radios up there hadn't died. They'd been murdered. And somewhere between that plateau and this gravel bar were four missing geologists and whoever had dug that hole too shallow.

Rafael Nakamura had spent seven years being no one, moving supplies for people she didn't trust, keeping her head down and her history buried. Standing in the rain on a gravel bar at the bottom of the world, she understood that the burial was over. Whatever was happening in this valley had reached out and put its hand on her, and she could either drown quietly or find out what she'd stumbled into.

She salvaged what she could — a first aid kit, a poncho, a flare pistol with three flares, a dented pot — and hauled it above the waterline under an overhang of roots. Then she sat with her back against a fallen log, splinted her ankle as best she could, and watched the river run.

Which was when she saw the smoke. Thin, gray, deliberate — a signal fire, someone feeding it wet wood to make it visible, upriver along the bank perhaps two kilometers, tucked against the escarpment wall.

Someone else had survived. Someone who wanted to be found.

Rafael looked at the fire for a long time, the rain streaming off her chin, her ribs aching with every breath. Every sensible instinct said stay hidden, stay still, stay no one.

She got up, and started walking toward it.
