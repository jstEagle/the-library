## 3. The Survivor

The plane came apart the way a promise does — all at once, and then in pieces for a long time after.

Imogen Vale heard it before she saw it: the engine note climbing into something wrong, a shriek of aluminum through wet canopy, and then the crack and settle of trees giving way along the river's edge. She had been walking downstream from the mission track with nothing but a machete, a water bottle, and three days of not sleeping, so at first she thought the sound was the escarpment calving again. It did that now, in the rain. The whole valley had learned to make noises like dying.

Then she came around the bend of the gravel bar and saw the tail.

It stood up out of the shallows at an angle that made her stomach drop, painted in the concession's colors — white and rust-orange, KESSLER RIVER SUPPLY stenciled along the fuselage — and the rest of the plane was scattered upstream like a dropped toolbox. She ran. She didn't decide to run; her legs simply went, and the machete swung wild at her hip, and the rain drove sideways into her face hard enough to sting.

The cockpit was half-submerged, nose-down against a drift log. Inside, a woman hung in the harness, blood sheeting down one side of her face, her left arm bent at an angle arms don't go.

"Hey." Imogen clawed at the door frame. "Hey — can you hear me?"

The woman's eyes opened. Brown, unfocused, then focusing with a speed that startled her — snapping onto Imogen's face like a camera locking on. Even concussed, even bleeding, there was something in that look that was taking inventory. Counting Imogen's hands, her pockets, the exits.

"You're one of mine," the woman said. Her voice was rough, slurred at the edges. "Survey."

"I'm Vale. Botany." Imogen got her fingers on the harness buckles. "What's your name?"

A pause. Long enough to be interesting.

"Nakamura," the woman said. "Rafael Nakamura."

Later, Imogen would think about that pause. Later she would have time to think about a great many things. But just then the river was rising around the drift log and Rafael Nakamura was bleeding into the floodwater, and there was no later, only now.

"Your arm's broken," Imogen said, working the buckle over the good shoulder. "I'm going to pull you out and it's going to hurt and I need you to not fight me."

"Do it."

She did it. Nakamura made no sound at all, which frightened Imogen more than screaming would have. She hauled her across the gravel bar on a tarp she'd cut from the wrecked fuselage, above the high-water line of dead grass, and then she knelt in the rain and did what she could — pressure, splint, the last of the codeine from the survey kit she'd carried out of hell three days ago. Nakamura watched her work with those inventory eyes and said, once, quietly: "You've done this before."

"Fieldwork." Imogen tied off the bandage. "Things break."

* * *

Nakamura slept in fits, waking sharp each time, checking the sky, checking Imogen, checking the treeline. Between sleeps she asked questions in a flat, economical voice: How long since the crash? An hour. Where are we? Eleven kilometers below the falls, give or take. Is the plane flyable?

Imogen looked at the fuselage folded around the drift log like paper around a fist and said, "No."

Nakamura absorbed that without expression. Then: "How did you get here?"

"I walked."

"From where?"

"Upstream."

Another pause, longer this time. Rain ticked on the tarp Imogen had rigged between two saplings. Downriver, the water was coming up brown and fast, carrying branches.

"There were six of you," Nakamura said. Not a question. "Brandt told me six. I was supposed to check on the camp." Her jaw tightened; Imogen watched her decide how much to say. "I found the grave. One grave, dug shallow. Radios smashed. No sign of anyone else."

Imogen said nothing. She looked out at the river instead, at the gray wall of rain moving down the escarpment, and felt the old nausea rise — the same nausea she'd had standing in that ruined camp with the smell of kerosene and turned earth, doing arithmetic about bodies.

"Dr. Vale." Nakamura's voice had changed. Still flat, but underneath it something pressed. "I flew supplies to your team for seven months. I know what Brandt is. So when I ask you what happened up there, understand that I already believe you're in danger, and I'm asking because I'd like to keep both of us alive."

"The strata," Imogen said.

"What?"

"You want to know what happened. It started with the strata." She pulled her pack over, took out the oilcloth bundle she had not let go of in four days, not while swimming the side channel, not while hiding under a deadfall listening to men with flashlights pass within ten meters. She unwrapped it halfway. Cores, sealed in wax and foil, labeled in her own handwriting. "We were coring the escarpment face for the baseline study. Standard work. Except the sequence doesn't match the filings. At all."

"Meaning what?"

"Meaning the concession's environmental assessment describes a formation that isn't there. The mineralogy is wrong. The contamination profile is wrong. Either they sampled somewhere else and lied about it, or—" Imogen stopped. Her hands were steady; she'd trained them to be. "Or they knew exactly what was down there and built the filing to hide it. What's buried in that rock would void every permit they hold downstream. Every single one. That's not an oversight. That's the whole company."

Nakamura looked at the cores for a long moment. Then at Imogen. "And Brandt found out."

"The night we voted to go public." The words came out of Imogen like teeth being pulled, one at a time. "There was a vote. Four to two. We were going to transmit the data on the weekly flight." She wrapped the oilcloth back over the samples, slowly, precisely, because if her hands started shaking she wasn't sure they'd stop. "The men came that same night. Concession security — I knew two of their faces from the landing. They came up the switchback road in the dark with no lights, which nobody does unless they mean it."

"And?"

"And I'm here," Imogen said.

Silence. Rain. The river climbing its banks with a sound like breathing.

"That's not an answer," Nakamura said.

"No," Imogen agreed. "It isn't."

She expected pushing. She'd been expecting pushing for four days, rehearsing lies in the dark, and some part of her that never stopped cataloguing — botanist's habit, everything sorted, everything keyed — noted that Nakamura's face did something complicated and then settled into a decision not to press. Yet.

"Get some sleep," Nakamura said instead, and shifted herself upright against a log with her broken arm cradled, and took first watch, and Imogen lay down on the tarp and did not sleep at all, staring at the underside of the plastic, seeing the camp again, hearing it.

* * *

They stayed on the gravel bar through the worst of the light, because Nakamura, pale and sweating, insisted on assessing the wreck before anything else, and Imogen went with her because leaving her alone seemed unwise in every direction.

Nakamura worked one-handed, fast and certain, reading the wreckage the way Imogen read leaf damage — symptom by symptom back to cause. "Compression fracture in the spar. Gear's gone. She's not flying again, not without a hangar." She sat back on her heels and looked downriver, where the road should have been and wasn't — just a brown smear where the washout had taken it. "Road's out. Radio's dead. And the monsoon proper arrives in—" she squinted at the sky, at the bruise-colored mass stacking up over the escarpment, "—a day. Maybe less."

"So we walk."

"With this?" Nakamura lifted her splinted arm an inch. "Eleven kilometers of flooded ground on a broken arm, carrying you and whatever that is?" She nodded at the oilcloth bundle. "No. We float. Your team had a boat."

Imogen went still. "How do you know that?"

"I delivered it. Aluminum skiff, fifteen-foot, two drums of fuel, in April." Nakamura's eyes came around to her, and there it was again, that inventory look, except now Imogen understood it was pointed inward too — the woman was calculating, always calculating, odds and distances and people. "Where is it?"

"The cache below the second bend. We cached it when the portage trail washed out." Imogen hesitated. "If it's still there."

"If it's still there," Nakamura echoed. She looked at the sky again, and Imogen watched her do the same arithmetic everyone in the valley did this time of year, the arithmetic that ended with the word *monsoon*, and then Nakamura said, quietly, almost to herself: "Brandt will report the plane stolen. By tonight. He'll say I took it."

Imogen stared at her. "Why would he—"

"Because I found his grave and his smashed radios and then crashed my plane in his valley, and because it's cheaper to bury a thief than a witness." She said it without heat, the way you'd read off an instrument panel. "He'll have patrols on the river inside two days. Which means we move tonight or not at all."

"You sound like you've run from that man before."

Something crossed Nakamura's face — quick, submerged, gone. Like a fish turning under water.

"I sound like a pilot who knows her employer," she said. "Get your kit. We'll walk the bank while there's light."

* * *

They reached the cache at dusk, and the boat was there — dented, half-full of leaves, but whole — and Imogen felt something loosen in her chest that she refused to name as hope. Nakamura inspected the hull with her good hand, found the hairline seam near the stern, and grunted. "Sealant. Tomorrow. Tonight we get downstream past the first rapids while the water's high but not suicidal."

They loaded in silence: fuel drum, the salvageable crate from the wreck, Imogen's pack with the oilcloth bundle riding against her spine where it had ridden for four days. Nakamura steered from the stern with one arm and gritted teeth, and Imogen bailed with a cut-down jerry can, and the river took them smooth and brown between black walls of forest, and for twenty minutes neither of them spoke.

It was Imogen who broke it, because she couldn't help herself, because the question had been sitting in her since the gravel bar like a stone in a boot.

"Nakamura. Before — at the crash. When I asked your name." She kept her eyes on the bailer, on the water streaming out. "You thought about it. Before you answered."

The pole knocked twice against rock. Ahead, the first rapids showed as a long seam of white in the failing light.

"Pilot habit," Nakamura said. "You check everything twice."

"That wasn't checking. That was choosing."

The boat slid toward the white water. Nakamura's jaw was set, her eyes fixed downstream, and when she finally answered her voice was level and final as a locked door.

"Row, Doctor."

So Imogen rowed. But she had spent six years keying out species in the field, learning to read the small tells that separate one thing from another thing that looks almost identical — and she knew, with the certainty of her own training, that the woman steering them into the rapids was not entirely named Rafael Nakamura.

And that whatever she was hiding, it fit — somehow, in a way Imogen couldn't yet see — into the same night the men came up the switchback road with their lights off.
