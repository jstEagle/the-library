## 4. Leaking Boat

**Rafael**

The plane was dead. Rafael knew it the way she knew weather — not from any single sign but from the whole of it. The propeller blade bent like a thumbprint in clay, the cowling crumpled where the gravel bar had caught her, and when she'd pulled the panels she found the oil line split and the port-side spar cracked clean through. No fixing that with sealant and stubbornness.

The road, if you could call the concession's logging track a road, had washed out two seasons back above the falls. Brandt knew it. Everyone knew it. Which meant nobody was coming for them, at least not soon, and not by accident.

So: the river.

The survey camp's aluminum boat sat half-buried in brush a hundred meters from the wreck, where Imogen said the geologists had cached it. Twelve feet of riveted hull, an outboard with a fuel tank maybe three-quarters full, and a crack along one seam near the transom that wept a thin silver thread of water whenever they loaded it.

"Sealant," Imogen said, crouched over the seam with her sleeves pushed up. She'd found a tube of marine epoxy in the survey stores, half-hardened but workable, and she worked it into the crack with a spatula she'd sterilized over flame. Her hands were steady. Her eyes weren't. "It'll hold until it doesn't."

"That's the spirit."

"I'm serious. I want to know what 'doesn't' looks like so I can plan around it."

Rafael almost smiled. Almost. "It looks like swimming."

They patched it twice, let it cure through the afternoon while rain came and went in gray rags, and launched at first light with the monsoon still building somewhere over the escarpment like a held breath. Rafael took the tiller. Imogen sat amidships with the maps spread across her knees under a sheet of plastic, because the botanist had turned out to be the only one who'd thought to keep the survey charts dry.

* * *

**Imogen**

The system assembled itself out of necessity, the way systems do.

Bailing shifts: twenty minutes on, twenty off, the cut-down fuel can passing between them without either woman having to ask. Rationed fuel: Rafael ran the engine at a throttle setting she called "half and prayer," which Imogen calculated would get them to the mission station by dark if the river cooperated. Navigation: a negotiation conducted mostly in short sentences.

"The chart says the channel bends left after the second island."

"The chart is four years old. The channel bends right. Islands move; rivers don't read surveys."

Imogen marked the correction in pencil and didn't argue further, which surprised her more than it should have. She was used to being the one who knew things. On the plateau, among the team, her word on the flora had been final. Here her expertise ended at the waterline, and everything below it belonged to the woman at the tiller.

She watched Rafael the way she watched unfamiliar species — cataloguing, cautious, aware that anything capable of surviving this long alone was also capable of something else. The pilot flew a supply route for a company whose permits Imogen had spent six months proving fraudulent. That fact sat between them in the boat like ballast neither would name.

And yet. When the first rapids came — nothing serious, just a chute of standing waves between boulders — Rafael read the water aloud, low and continuous, a running commentary of *left side, avoid the boil, hug the rock, now ease right*, and Imogen understood she was hearing seven years of this river spoken fluently. Nobody knew a river like that by reading charts. You learned it by living on it, or hiding on it.

By midday the rain had settled in for good. They ran the boat into the lee of a gravel point to refuel from the reserve cans, and Imogen ate hardtack and dried fish and studied the woman across the bow.

Rafael Nakamura. The name was on the flight manifest Brandt kept posted at Kessler Landing. It fit her the way a borrowed coat fits — correctly enough at a distance, wrong at the shoulders. Imogen had spent years learning to read people the way she read strata: by what was missing. There was a layer absent in Rafael Nakamura, a disconformity, something eroded away before the present surface formed.

"You fly for them," Imogen said. Not a question.

"Somebody has to move the freight."

"And you never asked what the freight was for?"

Rafael's jaw tightened, a small motion, quickly controlled. "I fly. I don't audit."

"No," Imogen agreed. "You don't." She folded the map along its worn creases and said nothing else, and the silence they traveled in afterward was a different kind than before — not empty, but load-bearing.

* * *

**Rafael**

They made the mission station as the light failed, a roofless shell of stone above a bend where the river widened and went quiet. The chapel walls still stood; someone had hung a tarp where the altar used to be, decades ago, and the tarp had rotted to lace. Rafael beached the boat, bailed it, and got a fire going under the broken arch while Imogen rigged shelter from the survey tarp and two paddles.

It was during the fire-tending that she caught her.

Imogen sat against the far wall with the maps, ostensibly studying tomorrow's water. But she wasn't looking at the maps. She was looking at Rafael — at her hands, specifically, then her face, then her hands again, with the fixed attention of someone comparing a specimen against a description in a key.

"What?" Rafael said.

"Nothing."

"You've been doing it all day. What?"

Imogen held her gaze without flinching, which Rafael respected and resented in equal measure. "Your callsign," Imogen said. "Okafor mentioned it once, on the plateau, before—" She stopped. Started again. "He said the concession's pilot flew under a handle nobody used. Something old. He couldn't remember it."

"Callsigns are decoration."

"This one wasn't. He said the older pilots still used it when you weren't around. Like it meant something."

The fire popped. Rain ticked on the tarp. Rafael fed a stick into the flames and heard herself say, easily, smoothly, "Kestrel. It was Kestrel. Pilots collect stupid names."

It was a lie, and it was badly built, and she knew it the moment it left her mouth because Imogen tilted her head exactly the way she had when examining the falsified core samples — noting the discrepancy, filing it, saying nothing yet.

"Kestrel," Imogen repeated.

"Small falcon. Hovers before it strikes. Somebody thought it was funny."

"Mm." Imogen rolled the maps closed. "Get some sleep, Kestrel. First watch is yours."

Rafael lay back against her pack and stared at the broken vault of sky, where the clouds had torn open just enough to show stars, cold and indifferent and very far away. She had lied about her name for seven years and gotten good at it — good enough that customs officers, radio operators, Brandt himself had all swallowed it whole. One day with a botanist and the lie had come out crooked.

Because Imogen Vale hadn't asked what her name was. She'd asked what it *meant*. And Rafael had answered the wrong question, which was how you always betrayed yourself.

Above the escarpment, thunder rolled — long, low, patient, like something turning over in its sleep. Tomorrow the storm would arrive for real. Tomorrow the river would rise.

And somewhere downstream, in the dark, a patrol engine coughed once and caught, carrying on the water the way sound carries before rain.

Rafael sat up, listening, hand already finding the tiller bar beside her.

Imogen opened her eyes. "You hear it too."

"Yeah," Rafael said. "I hear it."
