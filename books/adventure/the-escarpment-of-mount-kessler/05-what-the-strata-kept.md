## 5. What the Strata Kept

The mission station appeared out of the rain like something surfacing from a drowning. A chapel with its roof half-caved, a long dormitory building gone silver with rot, and a bell tower leaning at an angle that suggested it had been considering falling for decades and hadn't committed. The sign above the gate had lost its letters to weather and lichen, but Imogen could read the ghost of them: *San Miguel de la Ribera, 1911.*

"Nobody's been here since the mission pulled out," Rafael said, cutting the engine and letting the boat drift the last fifty meters to a stone landing cracked apart by flood roots. "River traffic goes wide around this stretch. Superstition."

"Of what?"

"Of places that used to mean something." Rafael stepped onto the landing, testing it with her weight before committing, and Imogen watched her do it — that constant, animal assessment of every surface, every sound. "It'll hold. Get the case."

Imogen got the case. She'd slept with it strapped to her wrist for six nights now, and her forearm bore the bruise of the buckle to prove it. The aluminum was cold and dented and it weighed almost nothing, and it was the heaviest thing she had ever carried.

Inside the dormitory, the rain found its own way through the roof, a dozen small percussion sections ticking on the warped floorboards. Rafael walked the length of the building with a flashlight, checking corners, checking the back door, checking the tree line through the empty window frames, and only then did she set her pack down. She built a small stove fire under the one section of roof that still held, and hung their wet clothes above it, and said nothing while Imogen spread a tarp and tried to stop her hands shaking.

It wasn't cold. It wasn't fear, exactly. It was that she had been carrying the truth alone through the jungle for six days, and she was about to set it down in front of a stranger, and there was no way to know what the stranger would do with it.

"Your wrist," Rafael said. "Let me look at it."

"It's fine."

"It's the size of a grapefruit. Let me look at it."

Imogen let her look at it. Rafael splinted it with a tent stake and a strip of tarp, quick and impersonal and competent, her fingers cool and certain, and Imogen thought: *she has done this before, or something like it, somewhere she won't talk about.* Everyone in this valley was running from something. She was beginning to understand that the river collected them the way an eddy collects leaves.

When it was done, when the fire was fed and the rain had settled into its long evening rhythm, Imogen unlatched the case.

"I need to show you something," she said. "And then I need you to decide something. And I need you to understand that I've been deciding whether to tell you since the gravel bar, and the only reason I'm telling you now is that we're past the point where you can drop me on a bank and fly away. There is no away. Not for either of us. Not anymore."

Rafael sat down across the fire. She didn't say *get on with it.* She just waited, and that steadiness was the thing that made Imogen's throat close. She had spent three years with the survey team and she could count on one hand the people who listened like that — like the answer mattered more than their own next sentence.

She opened the case.

Inside, in foam cut to fit, sat rows of sample vials and sealed bags, each one labeled in her own handwriting, each one logged, each one catalogued against the survey's master records. And beneath them, wrapped in a poncho, the field notebooks. Six of them. Three years of the escarpment's memory, kept in graphite and rainproof paper.

"The concession's filings," Imogen said, "say the tailings from the upper mine are contained. Impervious substrate, no leaching into the watershed, no mobilization of heavy metals downstream. That's the basis for every operating permit they hold. Every one. Water rights, road rights, the whole concession — it all rests on that one claim."

She lifted a vial into the firelight. The sediment inside was a gray so fine it looked almost like smoke.

"This is from the seep line below the tailings pond. I sampled it in March, and again in June, and again three weeks ago. The substrate isn't impervious. It's fractured karst — the whole escarpment is riddled with channels, and the tailings are moving through them like water through a sponge. Arsenic. Cadmium. Mercury in the lower strata. It's already in the tributaries. In another two seasons it's in the Kessler mainstem, and then it's in every village from here to the delta, and by then it's in the people, and there is no filing on earth that fixes that."

Rafael's face didn't change. But her eyes went to the river — Imogen could see it, the flick toward the black water beyond the doorway, the recalculation. The river she flew supplies beside. The river she knew bend by bend.

"How bad?" Rafael said.

"Bad enough that if the numbers are honest, the permits are void. Not reviewable. Void. They were issued on a falsified environmental assessment, and the falsification is provable from the strata itself. The karst doesn't lie. It just keeps the receipts."

The fire popped. Somewhere in the chapel, a piece of rotten timber gave way and came down with a soft, exhausted sound.

"Say the rest of it," Rafael said quietly.

Imogen wrapped her arms around her knees. "Three weeks ago the team voted. Six to one. We were going public — the data, the logs, everything, to the provincial registrar before the concession's permit renewal came up in November. Dr. Achebe was going to carry the case out himself on the supply flight." She stopped. Started again. "The supply flight. Your flight."

Rafael went very still.

"He never got the chance. The night before, three men came up from the concession. No insignia. New boats, new rifles, and they knew exactly which tent was his. I heard the first shots from the botany tent and I — " Her voice caught, and she let it, because lying about the shape of that night would be worse than the ugliness of the truth. "I ran. I took the case and the notebooks and I went into the escarpment forest and I stayed there for four days while they searched. They dug one grave, Rafael. One. For six men. It wasn't even deep enough. I saw it."

She hadn't said any of this aloud before. Saying it made it real in a way the jungle hadn't; the jungle had let her keep it as something that might still be survivable, a bad night that would end. Here, in the firelight, in front of a witness, it became what it was.

"They didn't come back for you," Rafael said. It wasn't a question.

"They think the river took me. Or the forest. They reported the camp lost to a slope failure — a landslide, a tragic accident, six geologists, very sad, very final. The concession's statement is already drafted. I've seen the template they use; it's always the same." She looked at Rafael across the fire. "Which means every day I stay alive and unaccounted for, I'm a problem they haven't finished solving. And now you're a problem too, because you've seen me and you've heard this and you flew out of that camp after it happened."

"I was sent to find a missing team," Rafael said. "Not a massacre."

"You were sent to find out what happened to a team that was about to end the concession," Imogen said, "and I don't believe for a second that Brandt didn't know what he was sending you into. Or what he'd want done if you found it."

Rafael was quiet for a long time. The rain ticked. The fire settled. And then she said, "Let me see the filings. You said you have copies."

Imogen pulled the folder from the bottom of the case — the concession's own environmental assessment, obtained through one of the junior geologists who had a cousin in the registrar's office. She passed it across the fire.

She watched Rafael read. Watched her go page by page, fast at first, then slower, then stopping entirely on the signature page. Watched her thumb move once, slowly, across the ink at the bottom of the certification.

"I know this signature," Rafael said.

"You'd know it. It's on half the permits in the valley."

"No." Rafael looked up, and in the firelight her face had gone to something old and cold and very far away, the face of a woman looking at a country she'd emigrated from. "I mean I know *him.* Brandt. Before he was Brandt the concession manager. Before the valley. He signed my — " She stopped herself, jaw tightening, and Imogen watched her decide, in real time, how much of the sentence to keep. "He's the reason I came to this river. That's all you get tonight."

"That's not all I get," Imogen said. "You know that, right? You've just heard me say that men with rifles killed six people to bury a folder. And you've told me the man who signed the order is the man who drove you up this river in the first place. Whatever you're running from, it just caught up. It's standing in the same room."

Rafael fed a stick to the fire. Her hands were steady. Imogen noticed that they were always steady, and wondered what it cost.

"Then here's what matters," Rafael said. "The case. The notebooks. If they get those, you're a drowned botanist and I'm a pilot who crashed a stolen plane, and the filings stand and the river dies slowly and everyone downstream drinks it. If those samples reach the registrar — if *you* reach the registrar, alive and identifiable — the permits are void and Brandt is finished. So the mission isn't the boat anymore. It's not the river. It's the witness."

"Yes," Imogen said. "That's what I've been trying to tell you since the gravel bar."

"No." Rafael looked at her, and there was something in her face Imogen hadn't seen before — not the flat competence, not the guardedness, but something underneath it, lit for the first time. "What you've been trying to tell me is that you're afraid. What you just told me is that we're not running downstream anymore. We're carrying something."

Outside, the rain thickened, and beneath it, far off down the valley, Imogen heard it — or thought she heard it, or maybe Rafael heard it too, because Rafael's head came up like a deer's.

An engine. Low, steady, working its way upstream against the current.

Rafael was on her feet with the fire killed between one breath and the next, the flashlight dead in her hand, her mouth at Imogen's ear.

"Patrol boat," she breathed. "They run this stretch after dark when they're looking for something." A pause. "Stay down. And Imogen — whatever happens, the case stays with you."
