## 6. The Man from Downriver

The portage at Serrano Rapids announced itself the way bad news always did on the Kessler: first a sound, then a smell, then the whole truth laid out in front of you.

Rafael heard it before she saw it — a low, continuous thunder under the rain-hiss, the river dropping twelve feet through a slot of black granite. Then came the smell, wet stone and churned foam, and then the truth itself: three hundred meters of water no boat could survive, and no path around it except the one the survey crew had cut years ago with machetes and stubbornness.

She backed the aluminum boat into an eddy behind a boulder the size of a bus and killed the engine.

"Portage," she said.

Imogen looked up from her maps. Her face had settled into something over the past two days — not comfort, exactly, but a working arrangement with fear. "How far?"

"Half a kilometer along the left bank. The trail's overgrown but it's there." Rafael was already hauling the gear bags forward, dividing weight. "We carry everything twice. Boat first, then cargo."

"And if someone's waiting on the other side?"

Rafael paused. She hadn't said that out loud, but Imogen had been thinking it too — she'd seen it in the way the botanist kept checking the river behind them, reading the current for wakes that weren't waves.

"Then we'll know soon enough," Rafael said.

* * *

The trail was worse than she remembered, which meant it was worse than the survey crew had left it, which meant someone had let it grow back on purpose. The concession didn't like footpaths it didn't control. Every year Brandt's crews "maintained" the routes that served the mine and let the rest strangle in vine.

Imogen carried the sample case strapped across her chest like a child, both arms wrapped around it even though her shoulders were raw from two days of bailing. She climbed well — better than Rafael expected from a woman who lived among slides and specimens — placing her boots with a geologist's economy, testing each hold before trusting it.

At the halfway point, where the trail bent inland around a fallen cedar, Rafael stopped walking.

The engine sound reached her a half-second before she understood it. Not the river. A diesel, idling low and patient, somewhere below the bend ahead.

She put out a hand and Imogen froze mid-step, water running off her hood.

"Boat," Rafael breathed. "Downstream side of the rapids."

They stood in the rain and listened. The diesel didn't move. It just sat there at the bottom of the chute, holding position against the current the way only a boat with power to spare could hold it. A concession patrol hull. She knew the idle of those engines the way she knew her own heartbeat.

"They followed us," Imogen whispered.

"No." Rafael shook her head slowly. "They didn't follow us. They were coming upriver. They knew where we'd have to portage."

That was worse. Following meant reaction. This meant arithmetic — someone upstream doing the same math she'd done, standing at the same map, drawing the same circle around the only place on forty kilometers of river where two people in a leaking aluminum boat would have to get out and walk.

"We go around," Imogen said. "Inland. There has to be—"

"There isn't. Not with our fuel, not with the light we've got left." Rafael turned the situation over once, looking for another answer, and found none. "We walk down to them. We're supply runners. That's all we are. I've got a delivery manifest in my bag and a concession radio code in my head, and you're a hitchhiker I picked up after my plane went down in the storm."

"I'm a what?"

"You're whatever I say you are until we're past this." She met Imogen's eyes through the rain. "Can you do that?"

Imogen's jaw worked. Then she nodded once, sharp, and shifted the sample case higher on her chest.

They walked down toward the diesel.

* * *

He was standing on the gravel bar at the bottom of the rapids with his hands in his pockets, watching the water, and he didn't turn around when they came out of the trees. He just said, in a voice pitched to carry over the river:

"Callsign Kestrel used to run this stretch faster than anybody. Never portaged unless she absolutely had to."

Rafael stopped walking.

The rain filled the silence. Imogen looked between them, not understanding yet, and Rafael couldn't speak because the name had gone through her like a fishhook — *Kestrel*, seven years buried, a callsign she'd worn like skin back when she'd been someone else, back when the valley had known her by a different name and a different life.

Okafor turned around then.

He looked older. Grey coming into his beard, a scar through one eyebrow she didn't remember giving him time to earn. But the eyes were the same — steady, tired, appraising — and he watched her the way he used to watch weather fronts, with respect and no illusions.

"Hello, Kestrel," he said.

"That's not my name."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't. But it used to be, and I never forgot it, because you were the best stick-and-rudder pilot I ever flew with, and because when you disappeared I spent two years wondering if you were dead." He tilted his head toward the patrol boat riding at anchor behind him, two of Brandt's men visible in the cabin, watching through the glass. "I stopped wondering about a month ago, when a new hire named Nakamura started flying the supply route and banked out of Serrano Canyon exactly the way somebody taught me to."

"What do you want, Okafor?"

"To offer you a ride." He spread his hands, palms out, rain drumming on them. "Dry cabin, hot thermos, and a hull that doesn't leak. Downriver to the junction, no questions asked."

"And in exchange?"

"In exchange, nothing. That's the part you're not believing right now, so I'll say it again: nothing." His voice dropped. "But there's something else you need to hear, and I'd rather say it here than on my radio. Brandt filed a report yesterday. Your plane — the survey plane — is logged as stolen. And there's a woman named Rafael Nakamura listed as the suspect. Grand theft, trespassing on concession land, and—" his eyes flicked to Imogen, to the case strapped to her chest, "—suspected theft of proprietary geological materials."

"He can't do that," Imogen said. "The plane wasn't—"

"It doesn't matter what it was. It matters what the paper says." Okafor shrugged. "Brandt's good at paper. By tonight every patrol on this river will have your description, your boat, and your route. You've got maybe thirty-six hours before the net closes. My offer stands for about ten minutes of that."

Rafael's mind was moving fast now, cold and clear, the way it moved in bad weather. *Stolen plane. Suspect.* It was elegant, actually — turn the survivor and the pilot into criminals, and suddenly any patrol that found them wasn't rescuing witnesses, it was apprehending thieves. No bodies required. No questions about the survey camp. Just paperwork swallowing two women whole.

"And if we take your ride," she said, "where does the boat go?"

"Wherever you tell me it went."

"And the manifest? The radio logs? Every check-in I've made since Kessler Landing?"

"Gone. Paper burns, Kestrel. I've burned plenty." He held her gaze. "You knew me for four years. Ask yourself whether I'm lying now."

She wanted to. God, she wanted to believe him — the old hunger for a familiar face rising in her like floodwater, four years of shared cockpits and shared storms pressing against the wall she'd built. That was exactly why she couldn't trust it. Brandt would know that too. Brandt would know precisely which bait to set, and who better to set it than the one man on the river whose face made her want to stop running?

"We appreciate the offer," she heard herself say. "We'll take the river ourselves."

Something moved behind Okafor's eyes — disappointment, or maybe relief; she genuinely couldn't tell. "That's your answer."

"That's our answer."

He exhaled through his nose. Nodded slowly. "Then I'll do you one courtesy before you go. The junction station got new radios last week. Military-grade scanning. If you're on the water in thirty-six hours, they'll hear your engine before you see their lights." He glanced at the sky, at the bruised wall of cloud stacking up over the escarpment. "And that storm coming down off the plateau is going to hit tomorrow night. Full monsoon edge. Nobody should be on this river when it lands."

"Then we won't be," Rafael said. "We'll be through the junction before it."

"You won't." He said it flatly, without malice. "Nobody's through the junction in thirty-six hours. Not in that boat." He looked at Imogen then, really looked at her, at the case. "Dr. Vale. Whatever's in that case — Brandt wants it more than he wants either of you. Understand that. The women are a problem to be managed. The samples are a problem to be erased."

Imogen's arms tightened around the case.

"So here's how this ends," Okafor said, and his voice changed — regret flattening into procedure. "I can't let you carry it past me. If I do, and it surfaces, the first question anyone asks is why the patrol boat at Serrano Rapids let it through. I'm not dying for your evidence, Dr. Vale. I'm sorry."

One of his men stepped out of the cabin onto the deck, and the shape of the rifle came up with him, casual as a tool.

Rafael's hand found Imogen's arm. *Don't.* The case was aluminum and seals and six months of a dead team's work, and it was worth exactly as much as the lives standing next to it, and Okafor knew that she knew it, and that was why he didn't even raise his voice.

"The case," he said. "Set it on the gravel. Then take your boat and go, and by tonight I report that I searched a derelict vessel at the rapids and found nothing but camping gear. You'll be ghosts again. It's the best deal on this river today."

For a long moment nobody moved except the rain.

Then Imogen unstrapped the case. She did it slowly, deliberately, holding Rafael's eyes the whole time — and Rafael understood, with a lurch, that the botanist had decided before she'd ever walked down this bank. Decided that the data was worth carrying only while carrying it cost less than the lives holding it. She knelt, set the case on the wet stones, and stood back up empty-armed, and her face didn't crumple the way Rafael expected. It hardened.

"Duplicate sets exist," Imogen said quietly. To Okafor, but loud enough for Rafael. "My team wasn't careless. Check inside. You'll find the field notebooks missing, and you'll find the chain-of-custody log missing, and you'll spend the rest of this trip wondering what else we kept."

Okafor crouched, opened the case, and went still.

Whatever he saw in there, he looked at it for a long time. When he straightened, he wouldn't meet either of their eyes.

"Get off my beach," he said, not unkindly.

* * *

They ran the rapids' tail water in silence, Rafael working the tiller through the standing waves below the chute while the patrol boat sat at anchor behind them, getting smaller. It wasn't until the bend took it out of sight that Imogen spoke.

"You knew him."

"Four years. He flew co-pilot on the medevac runs." Rafael kept her eyes on the channel. "Good man. Careful man. The kind of careful that keeps everyone alive right up until the day it doesn't."

"Do you believe him? About the thirty-six hours?"

"Yes." Because that part cost Okafor nothing, and men told the truth cheapest when the truth worked in their favor. "The net's real. The storm's real. Which means we can't run the junction in the open, and we can't outrun the weather, and we can't stay on this river."

"So what's left?"

Rafael was quiet for a long moment, watching the escarpment rise ahead of them through the rain — that long grey wall of stone where the survey team had dug their samples out of the world and been buried for it. Somewhere up there was the old survey track, cut into the escarpment face decades ago, climbing toward the upper airstrip. She'd flown over it a hundred times. She'd never once thought about walking it.

"Up," she said. "There's a road above us that Brandt thinks is dead. We're going to bring it back to life."

Imogen followed her gaze up the wet stone wall, and Rafael saw her do the arithmetic — the climb, the wrist she'd been favoring since the wreck, the storm coming down off the plateau like a closing door.

"And if the road doesn't come back?" Imogen asked.

Rafael pushed the throttle forward and the leaking boat surged into the rain.

"Then we'll be the ones who find out first."
