## 7. Monsoon

**Rafael**

The sky had been promising it for two days, and on the third morning the escarpment finally made good.

She heard it before she saw it — a sound like a freight train running along the top of the valley, though there were no trains within four hundred kilometers. Then the wall of rain came down the river at them, gray and total, erasing the far bank, the near bank, the boat's bow.

"Get the tarp over the gear!" Imogen was already moving, hauling the fuel drum toward the center thwart. "Rafael — the seam — watch the seam!"

"I know where the seam is."

The patch had held through three days of flat water and one set of easy riffles. It had never been tested against anything like this. The Kessler in monsoon wasn't a river anymore; it was a landslide with a current. Water came off the escarpment walls in ropes, in waterfalls, in whole hillsides liquefying into the channel. The brown water stood up in places like it was trying to climb out of itself.

The seam split a kilometer below the second gorge.

It didn't tear so much as unzip — a slow, obscene peeling sound under the roar of the rain, and then the river was inside with them, cold and immediate, filling the bilge faster than any bailer could argue with it.

"Out!" Rafael shouted. "Beach her — beach her now!"

There was no beach. There was the escarpment wall on their left, sheer and black and streaming, and on their right a chaos of standing waves marching downstream toward a horizon of white. She jammed the throttle open and steered for the only thing that looked like land: a gravel ledge wedged between the wall and the flood, maybe six meters wide, half of it already underwater.

The boat grounded hard enough to throw them both forward. Imogen went over the side into thigh-deep water, dragging the sample case — the duplicate case, the one they'd recovered from the cache — up onto the stones while Rafael killed the engine and started throwing supplies after her. Fuel first. Food. The tarp. The first aid kit she'd been hoarding like a religion.

The river took everything else. The tent roll went first, then the spare fuel, then the box with most of their food, then — she watched it happen, unable to stop it — the tool kit, which meant the patch kit, which meant the boat was dead as far as going anywhere was concerned even if the river ever calmed down enough to try.

They got what was left above the waterline just as the ledge shrank again. The flood was rising. You could measure it against a stone the size of a coffin lid ten meters from the wall; an hour ago its crown had been dry, and now the water licked at it like something tasting.

"We can't stay here." Imogen's voice was steady, which meant she was scared. Rafael had learned that about her by now. The steadier she got, the worse it was.

"There's nowhere to go." Rafael looked up. The wall rose four hundred meters, wet black rock veined with white water. Somewhere above them, invisible, was the old survey track. "We climb or we drown. And we climb when the rain gives us a window, not before."

"When will it give us a window?"

Rafael didn't answer, because she didn't know, and because at that moment the ledge tilted under them — not tilting, she realized, *sliding*, the gravel bar itself being rearranged by the current — and her feet went out from under her on the slick stone, and the river took her.

* * *

**Imogen**

Later, Imogen would reconstruct it in fragments, the way she reconstructed all disasters: the flash of Rafael's orange jacket going under, the rope — God, thank God for the rope they'd tied to the bow cleat, still coiled on the stones — the burn of it across her palms as she threw herself flat and let the current's own pull take up the slack.

She couldn't have done it alone. That was the arithmetic she kept returning to afterward, lying awake under the tarp with her wrist screaming. If Rafael hadn't spent three days teaching her to read the river — *watch the V's, watch the holes, the river tells you where it doesn't want you* — she'd have misjudged the eddy, thrown the rope wrong, and lost her.

Instead she'd read the water the way Rafael read it, seen the seam behind the drowned stone where the current folded back on itself, and led the throw into it. Rafael's hand came out of the brown water and closed on the line like it had been waiting for it.

Hauling her in was the part that broke the wrist. Not dramatically — no crack she could hear, just a wrongness that arrived all at once, a white flare from her left forearm to her elbow when Rafael's weight came against the rope and Imogen braced to take it. She didn't let go. That was the thing she kept coming back to. It hurt beyond anything she'd imagined and she did not let go, and thirty seconds later they were both on the gravel, coughing, alive, the river having decided, for today, to keep nothing more.

"You idiot," Rafael said, when she could talk. "You absolute — you should've cleated it off—"

"And watched you die." Imogen sat down hard, cradling her arm. "No."

Rafael looked at the arm. Looked at her. Something moved behind her eyes that Imogen had spent a week cataloguing without naming — the flinch people got when someone chose them without doing the math first.

"That needs splinting," Rafael said quietly.

"Yes," said Imogen, and discovered she was shaking, and let it be true.

* * *

**Rafael**

Two days trapped on a ledge teaches you things.

It taught Rafael that Imogen Vale set a broken wrist with the same methodical calm she applied to plant specimens, biting down on a rolled sleeve while Rafael pulled the bones straight, and that she described the pain afterward in taxonomic terms — *sharp, proximal, radiating* — as if naming it reduced it. It taught Rafael that hunger made Imogen funny, a dry, surprising humor that surfaced like fish rising. It taught Rafael that the sample case, wrapped in the tarp, dry, intact, mattered more to Imogen than her own arm had.

And it taught her that she was going to run out of silence.

They sat under the tarp on the second night, the storm still working the valley like a man shaking a locked door, sharing the last of the rice. Imogen's splinted wrist lay in her lap. The fire was a joke — wet wood, wet everything — but the spirit stove gave them heat and light enough to see each other's faces, and faces, Rafael knew, were the danger.

Imogen was watching her. Not the casual watching of the past week. This was deliberate. A scientist who had finished her observations and was preparing to publish.

"You're not Rafael Nakamura," Imogen said.

The rain filled the pause.

"No," Rafael said.

"I've known since the airstrip. Since you checked the magneto with your left hand and your right hand did the rest automatically — a pilot who favors one hand is a pilot retraining herself. And your callsign on the concession roster has a gap in it. Seven years, no history, no emergency contact, next of kin listed as *none*. Nobody's nobody, Rafael. Somebody taught you to fly like that."

Rafael fed another tablet to the stove. Watched it catch. There was a version of this conversation where she lied better, and she found she didn't want it. Maybe it was the rope. Maybe it was the wrist. Maybe it was seven years of talking to radios.

"My name is Rafael," she said. "That part I kept. The surname was my mother's. My father's name was Okonkwo-Díaz. Chidi Okonkwo-Díaz. I flew medevac out of Port Meridian under that name."

Imogen waited. She was good at waiting. It was probably why she was alive.

"There was a fire," Rafael said. "Not the kind that burns. The kind that spreads through paperwork. A charter company I flew for was moving cargo it shouldn't have been — mine equipment, conflict minerals, once a hold full of people who paid to be cargo and didn't all survive the altitude. I reported it. I had evidence. I was twenty-six and certain the system worked."

"What happened?"

"The evidence disappeared. The company's lawyers appeared. And then the crash happened — my aircraft, an engine failure I still can't prove wasn't manufactured, down on the coast road. My copilot died. Twenty-three years old. Amara. She was training for her medical." Rafael heard her own voice go flat and distant, the voice she used for weather reports. "The inquiry blamed me. Fatigue. Poor judgment. The company's insurers settled fast and quiet, and I understood that the quiet was the point, that the settlement was bought with my name attached to the blame, and that if I stayed, I would spend my life being the person who killed Amara Sesay instead of the person who tried to stop them."

"So you buried yourself."

"I buried the name. The name was the crime scene." She looked up. "I figured a clean name was a safe name. Fly supply runs, touch nobody, owe nobody. Seven years of touching nobody."

"And how's that worked out?"

Rafael laughed — a real one, surprised out of her, echoing off the escarpment wall. "Terribly. I'm stranded on a rock with a fugitive botanist and a case full of evidence, and I've never felt less alone in my life, which frankly concerns me."

Imogen smiled, then sobered. She shifted, wincing, and looked into the stove flame for a moment before speaking.

"My turn, then. You've earned the part I haven't told anyone." Her voice changed — lower, careful. "The night the men came to camp, I wasn't there. I'd gone down to the river to check the gauge station. When I came back, the tents were burning and Okafor's boat was pulling away from the bank, and I hid in the rocks and listened to them make sure." She swallowed. "Dr. Adeyemi was still calling out from inside the wreck of the radio tent. They didn't go down to help him. They poured fuel on the bank and left, and the fire did the rest, and I lay in the mud twenty meters away with my hand over my own mouth."

The rain hammered the tarp. Rafael reached over and put her hand on Imogen's good arm, and Imogen turned her hand over and gripped back, hard, the grip of someone holding a rope.

"They voted," Imogen said. "The week before. All five of us. We knew what the strata showed and we voted to publish anyway. Adeyemi said — he said, *if we're right, they'll come for us, and if we're wrong, we'll have embarrassed ourselves in front of the world, and I can live with that trade.*" Her breath caught. "He couldn't live with it. None of them could. I'm the only one living with it, and some nights I think the wrong person survived."

"The right person survived," Rafael said. "Because you're the one who didn't let go of the rope."

Imogen looked at her for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, and wiped her face with her good hand, briskly, like closing a ledger.

* * *

**Imogen**

On the third morning the rain thinned to a drizzle and the river dropped a meter overnight, uncovering the upper half of their ledge and, upstream, a fold in the escarpment wall that might have been a gully, might have been a route.

Imogen stood at the edge of the tarp, testing her weight on the splinted arm, and studied the gully the way she'd study a cliff face of strata — reading upward, layer by layer.

"Above us," she said, "somewhere along that face, is the survey track. It switchbacks up to the plateau and runs east to the upper airstrip. Brandt's airstrip."

Rafael came to stand beside her. Followed her gaze up the wet black rock.

"You want to climb that. With that wrist."

"I want to get off this ledge before the river changes its mind. And I want—" Imogen stopped. Started again, quieter. "Adeyemi told me once that evidence isn't evidence until someone survives to carry it. We're carrying it. So we climb."

Rafael was silent long enough that Imogen turned to look at her. The pilot was watching the gully, but her jaw had the set Imogen had learned to recognize as decision forming.

"There's a cache," Rafael said slowly. "If the track's still flagged, there may be supply caches along it — the survey teams always cached fuel and food at the switchbacks. And if Adeyemi was half as thorough as you are..." She didn't finish it. She didn't need to.

Imogen felt it land in her chest, the same jolt she'd felt reading the strata: the sense of a pattern completing. Duplicate samples. An affidavit. A man who'd known they'd come for him.

"He hid copies," she breathed. "Before the attack. He must have — Rafael, if Adeyemi cached duplicates somewhere on that track—"

"Then we're not just survivors anymore." Rafael was already kicking dirt over the spirit stove, already moving, already becoming motion the way she did when staying still got too expensive. "Get the case. Get the rope. We climb."

Above them, the clouds were tearing open along the escarpment crest, and the first thin light of the day came down the wet rock face like something being handed to them.
