## 8. The Escarpment Road

The rapids below the ledge sounded like a crowd that had been waiting all week to say something cruel. Imogen stood at the water's edge with her casted wrist cradled against her chest and watched the river fold itself into white chaos and understood, finally and completely, that they were not going down it.

"We could portage," Rafael said behind her. She didn't sound like she believed it.

"Portage around what? Look at it." Imogen nodded at the gorge where the Kessler dropped through a slot no wider than the plane had been. "There's no bank. There's no trail. There's a hundred meters of vertical rock on both sides and a hydraulic at the bottom that eats boats for breakfast." She turned. "We go up."

Rafael followed her gaze to the escarpment wall rising behind their ledge — two hundred meters of grey limestone seamed with vegetation, and cut diagonally across its face, half-swallowed by the jungle, the old survey track. Imogen had walked it once in the dry season with Okonkwo and Lindqvist, back when the team was whole and the worst thing in the valley was the humidity. A switchbacking shelf of packed earth and rotting corduroy logs, built by the concession's own road crews years ago and abandoned when the upper airstrip made it obsolete. It climbed toward the plateau rim and the strip beyond.

"That track hasn't seen traffic in six years," Rafael said.

"It exists. That's more than the river can say."

Rafael looked at the rapids a moment longer, jaw working, doing the arithmetic pilots do — fuel, weight, margin, luck. Then she shouldered the dry bag with everything they had left: one tarp, the med kit, three days of food gone soft, Imogen's field notebook wrapped in plastic, and the flare pistol they'd salvaged from the boat. The sample case was gone; Okafor had taken it at gunpoint four days ago, and Imogen still felt the absence like a pulled tooth.

"All right," Rafael said. "Up."

* * *

The first hour nearly broke them both.

Imogen went first because she knew the route, calling holds over her shoulder — root, ledge, the fallen cedar you had to duck under — while Rafael climbed below her with the bag strapped high and one hand free to catch her every time the broken wrist cost Imogen her balance. Which was often. The splint Rafael had built from a tent pole and duct tape held the bones rigid, but the wrist was a dead weight at the end of her arm, useless for gripping, and by the third switchback Imogen's right hand was shaking so badly she had to stop and press her forehead against the wet stone.

"Breathe," Rafael said. Close. Steady. "Ten seconds. Then again."

"I'm slowing us down."

"You're navigating us up a cliff with one arm. You're allowed to be slow." A pause, and then, drier: "I'm the one carrying the boat anchor."

Imogen laughed despite herself — a short, cracked sound — and climbed.

They fell into a rhythm as the morning wore on. Imogen read the track from memory and from the terrain itself, finding where the old grade had slumped into the hillside, where a culvert had washed out and left a gap they crossed on a fallen trunk slick with moss. Rafael hauled. That was the word for what she did — Imogen had never watched someone move like that, economical and relentless, hauling the bag up ledges, then reaching back to haul Imogen, one-handed grips under her good arm, lifting her over obstacles like she weighed nothing. Once, when the track narrowed to a shelf barely wide enough for their boots with the drop falling away sheer beneath them, Rafael went first and simply held out her hand, palm up, and waited without hurry until Imogen took it.

"You did this before," Imogen said at the top of that stretch, breath ragged. "Whatever you were running from. You've done hard things alone."

Rafael didn't answer. But she didn't let go of the hand until the ground widened.

* * *

They reached the treeline bench near midday, where the track crested onto a shoulder of the escarpment and the jungle opened into scrub and wind-bent grass. Below them the river they'd escaped glinted through the canopy, small now, almost innocent. Above, the wall continued, but here the old road graded gently along the contour toward the saddle where the airstrip sat hidden in cloud.

Imogen called a rest because Rafael wouldn't. They ate cold rice and shared the last of the water, sitting against a boulder with the monsoon clouds dragging their bellies across the plateau above.

"I keep thinking about how they died," Imogen said. She hadn't meant to say it aloud. It came out anyway, the way things did now, in the exhaustion after climbing. "Not the men with guns. I mean — before. The night before, we were arguing about the affidavit. Lindqvist wanted more corroboration. Okonkwo wanted to send it out with the next supply flight immediately, and I voted with him, and we won, three to two. And then the flight got cancelled — weather, Brandt said — and I remember being *relieved*. Because I wasn't ready. Because I wanted one more week to be sure." Her voice thinned. "One more week is what killed them."

Rafael was quiet for a long time. Wind moved through the grass.

"Then don't waste it," she said finally. "The week they bought you."

Imogen looked at her. Rafael was watching the clouds, face closed, but her hands — scarred, competent, restless — were folded tight in her lap like something being held down.

"You know," Imogen said slowly, "for seven years you've been nobody from nowhere. And in five days on this river you've become the most specific person I've ever met."

"Don't."

"I'm not asking. I'm just saying I see you." She pushed herself up with her good arm before Rafael could decide whether to be angry. "Come on. The cache should be past the second creek crossing. If it's still there."

* * *

Okonkwo had shown her the spot eleven days ago, in the hour between the vote and the cancellation of the flight, walking her out along the track with a shovel over his shoulder like a man going fishing. *If anything happens to us,* he'd said, not dramatic, just factual, the way he said everything, *you come back here.* She'd thought he was being morbid. She'd thought there was time.

The second creek crossing was exactly as she remembered: the track dipping through a cut in the hillside, water running clear over gravel, a lightning-split fig tree leaning over the far bank. And at its base, where the roots gripped the earth —

"It's disturbed," Rafael said.

Imogen's stomach dropped. Someone had been here. The ferns around the fig's base were flattened, and a shallow depression in the soil had been refilled — hastily, badly — with a scatter of loose rock on top.

"No," she said, and knelt, and dug with her one good hand, tearing at the soil. Rafael crouched beside her and joined in without a word, and together they excavated the depression in about forty seconds of increasingly frantic scraping until Imogen's fingers hit metal.

A ration tin. Standard concession issue, olive drab, rust freckling the seams. Empty. Beside it, a scrap of waterproof paper, torn, weather-blurred — but the corner carried the survey's own field-code stamp, and across the visible fragment, in Okonkwo's block capitals:

**MOVED IT. THEY'LL CHECK THE OBVIOUS. — T.O.**

"He moved it," Imogen whispered. She sat back on her heels, mud to the elbow, laughing and crying in the same breath. "He *moved* it. He knew they'd come. He knew they'd tear this place apart looking, so he—" She pressed the paper scrap flat against her knee, careful as scripture. "Where would Tom hide something he needed a stranger to find?"

Rafael was already scanning the cut, the banks, the leaning fig. "Somewhere not obvious," she said. "But findable. By who?"

"By me." Imogen closed her eyes and put herself back in that walk, Okonkwo's voice, his habits. Tom Okonkwo, who labeled every sample twice. Who never trusted a single point of failure. Who had spent twenty years teaching students that redundancy was a form of love. *If anything happens to us, you come back here.*

Her eyes opened. "The cairn. At the saddle — there's a survey cairn at the top of the last switchback, before the airstrip. We built it as a benchmark. He'd have—"

She was already moving. Rafael caught her good arm, steadied her, then simply picked up the bag and went first, setting a pace Imogen could follow, and the last switchback passed in a blur of wet stone and driving mist and the two of them breathing hard together.

* * *

The cairn stood where they'd raised it, a waist-high stack of stones on the shoulder below the saddle, cloud streaming past it. Untouched. Of course untouched — why would anyone pull apart a pile of rocks that meant nothing?

Imogen couldn't lift the capstone one-handed. Rafael did it, easing it aside, and there in the hollow beneath, sealed in a garbage bag and wrapped in Okonkwo's spare shirt, lay a second case — smaller than the stolen one, an ammunition box — and a document sleeve, and on top of the sleeve, weighted by a stone, a folded page with her name on it.

*Imogen —*

*If you're reading this, we didn't make the flight. Duplicate set inside: strata cores 4 through 11, chain-of-custody signed by all five of us. Affidavit executed and witnessed at Kessler Landing, countersigned by the mission priest, who apparently has more spine than our lawyers. Everything the concession filed says the aquifer sits ninety meters below the ore body. It doesn't. It sits nine. Every permit downstream is built on that lie.*

*Don't grieve long. Grieve usefully.*

*— T.O., for all of us*

Imogen read it twice. The second time her vision blurred so badly she had to hold the page against the stone to keep it steady, and Rafael's hand came to rest on her shoulder — light, awkward, offered the way people offer things they aren't practiced at offering — and stayed there while the cloud swallowed them and the grief moved through her like weather.

Grieve usefully. She folded the letter into the sleeve, into the box, into the dry bag, into herself.

"They're going to destroy whatever's left at the airstrip," she said. Her voice came out level. Purpose does that; it flattens everything else. "Brandt's deadline is the permit renewal — day after tomorrow. If there are originals up there, any records, anything that ties the falsification to him personally, it burns tomorrow night."

Rafael straightened. Above them, through a tear in the cloud, the saddle showed for one clean second: the fence line, the fuel drums, the hangar roof of the concession's upper strip — and, faint but unmistakable on the wind, the sound of an engine turning over.

Someone was already there.

"Then we're too late," Rafael said, "or exactly on time," and started up the last slope.
