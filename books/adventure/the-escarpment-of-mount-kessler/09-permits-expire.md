## 9. Permits Expire

They smelled the fuel before they saw the lights.

Kerosene and avgas, carried on the wet wind off the escarpment, and beneath it something sharper — the chemical bite of accelerant. Rafael went flat behind the last stand of pandanus at the tree line and pulled Imogen down beside her. Below, the upper airstrip lay in a bowl of dying light: two hangars, the concession's yellow fuel drums ranked like soldiers, and a survey plane sitting on the apron with its cowling open, its guts exposed to the rain.

Four men. Five. She counted twice, counting by the way the torch beams moved. One was walking the perimeter of the cache shed — the little tin-roofed building where the geologists had stored their heavy equipment before the survey went quiet — and he was carrying a jerrican in each hand like a man doing chores.

"They're going to burn it," Imogen whispered. Her splinted wrist was cradled against her chest, her voice scraped down to almost nothing after two days of storm. "Rafael. The affidavit, the duplicates — if they burn the shed—"

"Then we make sure it doesn't matter what's in it." Rafael checked the pistol she'd taken off the patrol boat's dead radio, an ugly little thing she'd never fired at a person and hoped to keep it that way. "The plane first. A plane is a decision. A shed is just a shed."

She didn't say the rest of it: that they had climbed nine hundred meters of escarpment on one working arm and pure spite, that Imogen's wrist had swollen to the size of a grapefruit under the splint, that the permit deadline was dawn and Brandt's lawyers in the capital were already drafting the filings that would make the valley legally dead. That the only thing between all of that and the truth was a battered Cessna with a hail-dented wing and whatever Rafael had left in her hands.

They moved through the tree line while the men were inside the second hangar. Rafael knew the ground here — she'd flown into this strip a dozen times in her first year on the river, before she learned to avoid the escarpment, before she understood that the concession's upper airstrip was where inconvenient things went to be forgotten. She took them along the drainage ditch to the hangar's blind side, rain hammering the tin, and found the Cessna unlocked. Of course it was. Nobody stole from Brandt. Nobody was supposed to be able to.

She got the cowling closed and her hands into the engine with the speed of seven years' habit. Fuel — three-quarters. Oil — low but alive. The hail damage to the left wing was cosmetic; the storm damage to the aileron cable was not. She spliced it with safety wire from the hangar bench, fingers working blind in the rain shadow of the wing, and thought, absurdly, of her father teaching her to tie fishing knots in a kitchen in a city she no longer named.

"Rafael." Imogen's hand on her shoulder. Not her name — her warning. "Lights. Coming around the hangar."

* * *

The man in the lead stopped ten meters away and raised his torch, and Rafael's whole body went cold, because she knew the way he stood before she saw his face.

Okafor.

He looked older than the last time she'd seen him — everyone did, but him especially, as if the years since the river had been heavier on him than on her. He was holding a rifle the way he'd held it on the patrol boat: like a tool he was tired of. Behind him, the other three fanned out with their jerricans, and one of them — a big man with a concession cap — said something to Okafor that the rain ate.

"Brandt wants the shed burned and the plane's registration pulled," Okafor said, in the flat voice of a man reciting someone else's orders. "And he wants the pilot found." His torch found her face. Held there. "Found and brought downriver."

"Then you've found me," Rafael said.

The big man laughed. Okafor didn't. He walked forward, slowly, until the torch light was full on her, and Rafael stood still and let him look, because running was over. It had been over since the ledge below the escarpment wall, since she'd said her true name out loud to a stranger with a broken wrist and found the world didn't end.

"I read the file," Okafor said. "The one Brandt filed on the stolen plane. Registration, serials, pilot's license number." He paused. "The license number was yours. The name on it wasn't Rafael Nakamura."

The rain filled the silence.

"I flew with you for two years," he said. "I logged hours beside a woman who could put a floatplane on a gravel bar in fog and make it look easy. And then she died, officially, in an accident downriver, and a new pilot showed up with the same hands and a different name, and I did what everyone does in this valley. I kept my head down." He lowered the torch. "But I've been reading files all week, Rafael. Brandt's files. The survey team didn't have an accident. And the woman who's been hiding from her own name for seven years — she didn't steal a plane. She came back for the truth."

"Okafor." The big man with the cap. "What are you doing?"

Okafor turned, and when he turned, the rifle came up — not aimed at Rafael.

"Brandt reported this plane stolen," he said. "I've been looking for the thief. I found her. And I'm letting her go, because the thief is the only honest pilot this company ever hired." He jerked his head toward the hangar. "Kessler samples, the affidavit, the survey's field books — they're in the shed. Burn it and you're burning evidence in front of a federal witness. Or walk away, and in six months you tell your grandkids you were never here."

The big man's torch wavered. Rain drummed on the tin. Somewhere down the valley, thunder rolled along the escarpment like a door being dragged shut.

The big man looked at the shed. At the plane. At Okafor's rifle, steady as a surveyor's level.

He dropped his jerricans and walked into the dark.

* * *

They worked fast after that. Okafor hauled the cache boxes from the shed — waterproof crates, the geologists' careful handwriting still legible under the mud — and Rafael loaded them into the Cessna's cabin while Imogen strapped them down one-handed, swearing with a fluency that would have impressed the drill crews. The affidavit went inside Imogen's jacket, against her skin, where a jerrican could never reach it.

"Runway's flooded at the far end," Okafor said, walking the strip with his torch, reading the water the way Rafael read rivers. "You've got maybe four hundred meters of usable ground. Storm's easing but it's not gone — you'll have wind on the nose out of the valley, then shear off the escarpment wall at two hundred meters. And Brandt's night man at Kessler Landing will see you come downriver."

"Let him see," Rafael said. She was in the left seat now, doing the preflight by touch and memory, and her hands were not shaking. That was the thing that surprised her most. Seven years of running, and at the moment of leaving she was calm as still water. "Okafor. Why?"

He stood at the door a moment, rain running off his chin, and looked at her the way you look at something you thought was lost.

"Because I logged those hours," he said. "I know what you can do. And because somebody has to be on the ground here when the permits die, and it might as well be a man who's tired of keeping his head down." He stepped back and slapped the fuselage twice, the old river sign for *clear prop*. "Go home, Rafael. Your real one."

The engine caught on the third turn, coughed, caught again, and settled into the rough, honest roar of a machine that had no business flying but didn't know it.

She taxied to the end of the flooded strip with the rain silvering across the windshield and Imogen silent beside her, both hands — the good one and the splinted one — pressed flat against the sample crates as if she could hold the truth in place by force. Four hundred meters. A drenched runway. A storm with its last temper to spend.

"Rafael." Imogen's voice, very quiet. "If we don't make it—"

"We'll make it."

"That's not an answer."

Rafael looked down the strip, at the wall of rain where the valley began, and thought about the woman she had buried seven years ago, and the woman sitting beside her who had dug her back up.

"It's the only answer I've got," she said. "Full throttle."

She pushed it forward and the Cessna lurched through the standing water, spray sheeting off the tires, the airspeed needle climbing in stuttering increments while the far trees rushed up out of the dark. Fifty knots. Sixty. The tail came light. She eased it off the ground with maybe eighty meters to spare, dropped the nose to build speed, and flew straight into the rain wall.

The world went white. Then it went violent — the shear off the escarpment wall hit them like a hand, slamming the left wing down, and Rafael fought it with everything the old hours had taught her, trim and rudder and the rough engine snarling, until the turbulence spat them out above the valley and the Kessler River appeared below, a silver thread running home through the dark.

Behind them, the escarpment stood black against the last of the storm, and somewhere on its face a shed full of lies was burning for nothing.

Ahead, downriver, Kessler Landing's lights were coming up out of the rain — and among them, she knew, a radio already crackling with Brandt's voice, saying her name. The name on the file. The name she'd buried.

Rafael keyed the radio herself.

"Kessler Landing, survey two-seven-zero, inbound," she said, and gave the callsign Brandt had reported stolen, loud and clear, for the whole valley to hear.
