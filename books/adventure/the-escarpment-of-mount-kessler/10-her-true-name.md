## 10. Her True Name

**Rafael**

The pen was heavier than it should have been. A government-issue ballpoint, chipped blue plastic, the kind that lived in coffee mugs on a hundred desks across the province, and Rafael Nakamura held it over the affidavit like a man holding a live wire.

The hearing room in the provincial capital smelled of wet concrete and old paper. Rain ran down the tall windows in ropes — the monsoon's last stubborn weeks, still wringing themselves out over the city. Somewhere below, traffic hissed through flooded streets. Above, a ceiling fan turned with the lazy indifference of bureaucracy.

*Full legal name,* the form said. *As it appears on your certificate of birth.*

Seven years she had not written it. Seven years of callsigns and forged licenses and a name bought from a clerk in a river town for two months' wages. She had said it aloud exactly once since the escarpment ledge — to Imogen, in the dark, with the storm hammering the rock above them — and even then her voice had cracked on the second syllable like ice under a boot.

"You don't have to do this today," Okafor said quietly. He sat one row back, big hands folded, wearing the only shirt he owned without concession stitching on it. "They can take a statement under the other name. Vale's evidence stands either way."

"I know."

"That's not why you're doing it."

"No." She looked at the line on the form. "It isn't."

Imogen sat beside her, wrist still strapped, fingers of her good hand resting on the table between them — not touching, just there, the way she'd been there on the gravel bar, on the ledge, in the leaking boat, a fixed point Rafael could navigate by. She didn't say anything at all. She'd learned that about Imogen: she knew when silence was the better instrument.

Rafael thought about the grave dug too shallow above the falls. About three geologists who had voted to tell the truth and paid for it before they could. About Brandt's signature, neat and confident, on permits that were now suspended, filings unraveling thread by thread in offices like this one all across the province. The concession's lawyers had arrived in force two days ago. This morning, half of them had already flown home.

She wrote the name.

Her hand did not shake. That surprised her most — that after everything, the letters came out steady, ordinary, small. Just ink. Just a name. The sky outside went on raining as if nothing had happened, and maybe that was the point. Nothing had happened. And everything had.

The clerk took the page, stamped it without reading it, and slid the docket number across. That was all. No thunderclap. No door opening onto a new life. Just a woman standing up from a folding chair in a wet concrete room, lighter than she'd walked in, because she was no longer carrying something.

Outside on the steps, the rain had thinned to mist. Imogen lit a cigarette with cupped hands, then held it out unasked. Rafael took it.

"So," Imogen said. "That's you, then."

"That's me."

"Strange. You look exactly the same."

Rafael laughed — a real one, surprising her own chest. "I think I might not."

Okafor came down the steps behind them, rolling his shoulders against the damp. "For what it's worth," he said, "the file they built on your stolen plane? It never made sense. Wrong fuel logs. Wrong hours. I flew with you long enough to know how you treat an aircraft." He looked out at the drowned street. "I knew who the thief really was before I ever pointed that gun. I just needed you to make me sure."

"You could have taken the case and let us drown."

"I could have." He shrugged. "Instead I'm unemployed, subpoenaed, and drinking worse coffee than I've ever had in my life. So." He almost smiled. "Worth it."

* * *

**Imogen**

The provincial archive smelled of dust and diesel from the generator downstairs, and Imogen loved it with an intensity she found slightly embarrassing. Rows of filing cabinets. Ledgers going back sixty years. Rain on a tin roof. It was, she told Rafael, the closest thing to church she believed in.

They spent four days pulling records — Rafael reading numbers the way Imogen read strata, fast and total, spotting the falsified assay dates by their typography alone. The concession had been sloppy for years because no one had ever made them be careful. Now everyone was careful. Investigators. Journalists. Two of the downstream communities had filed injunctions of their own, citing the survey's findings, citing *her* samples, citing the affidavit signed in a cache above the escarpment by three people who would not live to see it read aloud.

On the fourth day, a junior minister met them in a corridor and confirmed what the radio had been saying all morning: every permit in the Kessler valley suspended pending review. The resettlement office would begin processing claims for the survey region within the month.

"There'll be hearings," the minister warned, in the tone of a man distributing umbrellas before a storm he couldn't stop. "Months of them. The company will fight every line."

"They can fight," Imogen said. "We kept duplicates."

In the evening, on the roof of their boarding house, the city spread out below them — generator light and cooking smoke and the river glinting far off where it widened toward the delta. Imogen's wrist ached in the cooling air. She'd been told it would. She'd decided not to mind.

"Kessler Landing will change," Rafael said. "When the concession pulls out. Half the town lives off that payroll."

"And the other half lives off the river those permits were poisoning." Imogen tapped ash into a tin lid. "Towns survive both ways. One of them survives honestly."

Rafael was quiet a while. Below them someone was playing a radio badly tuned, music dissolving into static and back again.

"I've been thinking about flying," Rafael said finally. "The resettlement office needs pilots. Supply runs, medevac, survey support. Same river. Same weather. Worse pay." She glanced sideways. "I put my name in this morning. My actual name. They actually checked it, and it was actually there, waiting seven years like it hadn't gone anywhere."

"What did they say?"

"They said I start when the strip dries out." Rafael looked at the clouds stacking up over the western hills, and Imogen watched her read them the way she always did — professionally, affectionately, like old enemies she trusted more than friends. "Two weeks, maybe less."

"You're staying." It wasn't quite a question.

"I'm staying." Rafael said it slowly, testing the weight of it, and Imogen heard the difference — the way the word *staying* had always sounded like a sentence before, and now sounded like a place. "Not fleeing through. Not hiding in. Staying."

* * *

**Rafael**

The airstrip outside the capital was a crushed-coral ribbon with one windsock and a hangar that leaned east, and on the morning of her first contract flight it stood ankle-deep in last night's rain, steaming in the early sun. Rafael walked the length of it out of habit, checking surface, drainage, the lie of the land — the same walk she'd done at Kessler Landing a thousand times, except this time nobody asked her where she'd come from, because everybody already knew, because she'd told them herself, in writing, under oath.

The plane was a battered survey aircraft the office had pulled from a boneyard — the same airframe type she'd wrecked on the gravel bar, which felt less like coincidence than like a joke she was expected to get. She spent the whole first day with her head inside the cowling, and Okafor showed up at noon with tools and no explanation, and stayed until dark, and came back the next day too.

"You're bored," she said.

"I'm unemployed and useful. Enjoy it while it lasts."

By the third day the engine ran clean. By the fifth, the strip had dried enough to bear weight. On the sixth morning, Imogen arrived in a truck loaded with sample crates and equipment manifests, her cast off, her wrist stiff but working, bound — she announced — for the resettled survey region as its consulting botanist, first flight of the new season.

"That's my cargo," Rafael said, looking at the manifest.

"That's my pilot," Imogen said. "Small valley. We were bound to run into each other."

They loaded the plane together in the rising heat, Okafor lashing crates while Rafael ran the preflight, and when the engine caught it roared off the coral and scattered birds from the treeline, and Rafael sat in the cockpit of her own name with the throttle in her hand and felt — not safe, exactly. Not certain. The monsoon wasn't finished; the mountains would still try to kill her; the hearings would drag; the concession's lawyers would circle for years.

But the river ran the right direction. The sky was open ahead. And in the seats behind her were the only two people alive who knew the whole story — the buried name, the shallow grave, the ledge in the storm — and had chosen, knowing all of it, to be here anyway.

She lifted off into the clear morning air and turned north, toward the escarpment, toward home.

Below her, far down the valley, a thin line of smoke rose from the landing at Kessler — signal fires, the resettlement teams lighting the strips dry. And beside it, on the old survey track along the escarpment face, she could just make out movement: figures, vehicles, a flag of some kind planted at the mouth of the mission station road.

Someone was already up there. Ahead of schedule. Ahead of anyone's paperwork.

Rafael keyed the radio. "Imogen. You seeing this?"

A pause, then static, then Imogen's voice, flat and sharp: "That's not the resettlement office. Nobody's due up there for weeks."

Rafael pushed the nose down and opened the throttle.
