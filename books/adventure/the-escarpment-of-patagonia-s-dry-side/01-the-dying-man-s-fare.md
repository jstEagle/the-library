## 1. The Dying Man's Fare

The dock at Estancia Los Aromos was held together by optimism and wire, and the man standing on it was bleeding through both.

Tamsin Delacroix saw him first as a smudge against the last of the light — a shape that resolved, as the *Cazadora* came alongside, into a tall, stooped figure with one hand pressed to his side and the other raised in a wave that was more plea than greeting. The river was doing its evening trick, turning the color of weak tea with honey stirred in, and the escarpment behind him stood up orange and indifferent, the way it always did, as if to say: *I was here before any of you, and I'll be here after.*

"Ah, no," Tamsin said to the wheelhouse at large. "No, no, no."

The wheelhouse, being empty except for a tin of biscuits and a chart she'd been meaning to correct since 2019, offered no opinion.

She brought the boat in anyway. That was the whole trouble with her, and she knew it. Forty years on the Deseado and its meaner cousins had taught her to read water, weather, and men, and the reading on all three tonight was the same: trouble, arriving at walking pace, expecting her to do something about it.

The gangplank was a plank. The man came down it like a man descending into cold water — deliberately, wincing, one boot at a time. Up close he was sixty-something, grey-stubbled, with a week of dust on him and a dark stain spreading low on his left side where his jacket had given up pretending. His eyes, though. His eyes were clear as a mountain creek and twice as stubborn.

"Captain Delacroix." His Spanish had a Chilean lilt to it, worn smooth. "They told me at Los Aromos you were the last one running this stretch."

"They told you right, and they should've told you the rest: I don't take passengers after dark, I don't take passengers who leak, and I don't take passengers who've been told about me by strangers, because strangers who know my name usually want something."

"Good," he said, and sat down abruptly on her fuel drum, which she suspected was less drama than gravity. "Then we understand each other. My name is Tomás Reyes. I want something."

Tamsin looked at him for a long moment. Then she went below, got the good brandy — the one she saved for lawyers, funerals, and other unavoidable disasters — and brought it up with a clean rag and her grandmother's sewing scissors.

"Jacket off," she said. "And before you argue, I've stitched up men on this deck in wind that would take the scissors out of your hand, so consider yourself lucky it's a calm evening and I'm in a reasonable mood."

"What makes it a reasonable mood?"

"The brandy."

* * *

The wound was a gash along the ribs, deep enough to be honest, shallow enough that he might have walked away from it if he'd walked anywhere but a riverboat. She cleaned it while he talked, which was clearly the arrangement he'd come prepared for: he talked, she worked, and neither of them pretended the talking was casual.

"I was surveying up past the Chacabuco narrows," he said. "Water work. Springs, seeps, where the dry side keeps its secrets." He hissed as she pulled a stitch tight. "A company has been buying estancias along there. Andes Sur. They pay fast and they ask no questions, and a man who asks no questions about money is a man who's already decided not to hear the answer."

"And you asked questions."

"I asked *all* the questions. It's my worst quality. My late wife said so, God rest her, and she married me anyway, which tells you either about her patience or my charm." He smiled, and it cost him something, and she pretended not to notice. "They sent two men to explain the error of my ways. I explained mine faster, on a horse I had no business riding at my age."

"Where are these two men now?"

"Behind me. Somewhere. Horses tire. Grudges don't." He looked at her sideways. "You have a first mate, I saw him on the dock at Deseado. Raul? Big fellow, quiet?"

"Raul is below, checking the bilge. Raul checks the bilge the way other men check their horoscopes — frequently and with great faith. He'll be up directly, and he'll want to know why I'm sewing a stranger to my fuel drum."

"Then I'll be quick." And something changed in his voice — the charm draining out of it, leaving just the creek-water clarity. He reached inside his jacket, moving carefully on the unstitched side, and brought out an oilcloth packet, tied with waxed string, flat and heavier than it looked.

Tamsin did not take it. She wiped her hands on the rag and folded her arms, which was her body's way of filing a formal objection.

"No."

"You don't know what it is."

"I know what it looks like. It looks like the kind of thing men bleed on docks over, and I have spent forty years building a life specifically designed to avoid those docks." She nodded at the packet. "Whatever that is, it belongs to the police in Puerto Deseado, or the land office, or a priest, or a fire. Not to me."

"It belongs to my daughter."

The river made its small noises against the hull. Somewhere upstream a night heron said something rude.

"She has two names," Tomás Reyes said. "Not a first name and a last name — everyone has those. I mean she lives under a name that isn't hers. She took it years ago, and I let her, because I was a coward about the reasons. She's in Deseado. She works with maps, they told me. A cartographer." He almost laughed, and the laugh cracked in the middle. "A cartographer. Her father spent his life drawing the country and she ended up drawing it too, and neither of us ever once drew the other."

Tamsin looked out at the water. It was doing nothing helpful, just moving along in the dark, minding its own business, being an example to them all.

"I'm a boat captain," she said. "I find channels. Sandbars. The occasional escaped horse. I don't find people, and I especially don't find people who don't want to be found, because people who don't want to be found are usually hiding from exactly the sort of men who chased you, and I don't volunteer for other people's chases."

"Then don't." He set the packet on the drum between them, on top of the brandy bottle, with the care of a man laying a stone on a grave. "Don't take it. Sit with it a minute. That's all I ask. Just sit with it."

"That's a ridiculous request."

"I'm a dying man. My requests are allowed to be ridiculous. It's in the contract."

"You're not dying. You've got a stitchable gash and a talent for melodrama."

"Captain." He said it gently, and she heard, under the gentleness, the thing she'd been refusing to hear since he'd come down the plank: he wasn't performing. He'd done his own arithmetic, alone, somewhere on a horse in the dark, and he knew the total. "I've seen this before. In the camps, in the war, on the survey crews. Sometimes the body just decides it's done negotiating. I'm asking you to deliver a packet. I'm asking you because there's no one else, and because I watched you bring this boat into a dock in the dark without a single wasted movement, and I thought: *there's a woman who keeps her promises, because she has to, because the river would eat her alive if she didn't.* Am I wrong?"

Tamsin said nothing, which he correctly interpreted as *no.*

"I'm not asking you to fight anyone. I'm asking you to carry something. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference. A courier can be honest. A courier just has to refuse to be stopped." He pushed the packet an inch toward her. "The season ends when the water drops. You know the date better than I do. Before then. That's all. Find the girl with two names. Tell her it's from her father, and that he was proud of her, and that the name she's hiding under was a good idea and she should keep it if she wants — it's hers now, she earned it. But the packet is hers too. Both things can be true."

"You've thought about this a lot."

"Since the horse."

She picked up the packet. It was warm from being carried against his body, and heavier than paper had any right to be, and it sat in her hand like a small, patient animal that had already decided to stay.

"I'm not promising," she said. "I'm accepting a delivery. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference."

"Captain Delacroix," Tomás Reyes said, and grinned at her with blood on his teeth and the escarpment going purple behind him, "you are going to be a terrible courier, because you're going to make it a promise before you're done. I can see it. It's all over your face."

"It is *not* all over my face."

"It is, a little."

"Drink your brandy."

* * *

He died an hour before dawn, in the deck chair she'd rigged for him under the wheelhouse overhang, with the blanket tucked to his chin and the river talking its low talk against the hull. She'd checked on him twice in the night — once professionally, once because she'd heard him murmuring and wanted to know if it was fever. It wasn't fever. He was talking to someone named Marta, softly, in the tone of a man catching up after a long trip, and Tamsin had gone back to her bunk and lain there staring at the ceiling and being angry at rivers, docks, strangers, and herself in no particular order.

In the morning she made coffee, which she did badly, and eggs, which she did well, and brought a plate up for him out of pure habit before remembering. She stood in the wheelhouse doorway holding the plate for a while. The escarpment was pink in the early light, looking pleased with itself.

Raul came up the companionway, took one look, and put a hand over his heart. "The eggs?"

"For me," Tamsin said. "He's gone."

Raul crossed himself with the efficiency of a man who believed in God but had a schedule. "The señor from the dock?"

"The señor from the dock." She set the plate down and looked at the oilcloth packet, which had spent the night on her chart table, radiating patience. "Raul. How would you feel about a detour through Puerto Deseado before we take on cargo?"

Raul considered this with the gravity he brought to all questions, including what to have for lunch. "The company launch was at the fuel dock yesterday," he said. "Asking about a surveyor. Asking about us."

Tamsin looked at him. "Asking about us."

"Asking about us," Raul confirmed, and helped himself to an egg, because grief is grief but breakfast is breakfast.

Tamsin untied the waxed string, unfolded the oilcloth, and found inside a hand-drawn map of the dry side — beautiful, obsessive, annotated in a small fierce hand — and beneath it, a letter addressed in the same hand: *Para mi hija, la muchacha con dos nombres.*

The girl with two names. Who worked with maps. In Puerto Deseado.

"Well," Tamsin said to the map, to the river, to the pink and self-satisfied escarpment. "Damn it all."

She rolled the map up, tucked it inside her coat, and rang for the engine.
