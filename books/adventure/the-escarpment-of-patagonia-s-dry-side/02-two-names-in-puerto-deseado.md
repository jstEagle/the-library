## 2. Two Names in Puerto Deseado

Puerto Deseado smelled the way all company towns smell: diesel, salt, and money changing hands too quickly. Tamsin Delacroix came down the *Cazadora*'s gangplank with the dead man's map folded against her ribs, inside her coat, where it had lived for six days now like a second heartbeat.

She had buried him at the mouth of the Chico, under a cairn of river stones because the ground was too hard for anything kinder. She had said no words over him, mostly because she couldn't think of any that weren't lies. *I didn't know you. I don't want this. I'll do it anyway.* All three true, and none of them a comfort to a dead man.

The map was drawn on waxed linen in ink gone brown as old blood. Rivers she knew, rivers she'd run, and one spring marked with a symbol she didn't recognize — a circle with three lines running out of it, like a wheel with broken spokes. And in the corner, in a hand shakier than the rest: *Elena. The girl with two names.*

Tamsin had spent thirty years on Patagonia's dry side learning one lesson above all others: nothing here gives itself up for free. Not water, not mercy, not information. So she did what she always did when the land refused to speak. She went looking for someone who listened to it.

* * *

The map office was on the third street back from the harbor, wedged between a chandlery and a bakery that had given up pretending to bake and now sold tinned peaches at criminal prices. A hand-lettered sign read **VASQUEZ — CARTOGRAFÍA Y LEVANTAMIENTOS**, and beneath it, smaller, almost apologetic: *Se aceptan encargos.*

Inside, it smelled of paper and glue and the particular dust that comes off old linen charts. Every wall was hung with them — the Deseado estuary, the Santa Cruz, the Chico in its various moods, surveyed in a fine, patient hand. Tamsin stood in the doorway a long moment, letting her eyes adjust, letting the woman at the drafting table finish her line before she looked up.

The woman was perhaps thirty, dark-haired, with ink on the side of her right hand the way left-handed people get it on the left. She wore a man's cardigan with the sleeves rolled twice. When she saw Tamsin's face — wind-scoured, gray-streaked, known along four hundred kilometers of coast as the last captain fool enough to run the escarpment — she went very still.

"Captain Delacroix." Her voice was quiet and careful, a voice that measured its words before spending them. "Everyone said you were finished. Retired."

"Everyone says a lot of things about me. Most of them are flattery." Tamsin crossed the room and studied the charts on the wall without touching them. Good work. Better than good — the kind of surveying that takes years of small boats and cold hands. "You drew the Chico reach yourself?"

"Two seasons ago. It shifts every flood year, so it's never finished."

"No chart is. That's how they stay honest." Tamsin turned. Up close, the resemblance was worse. Not in the features — the dead man had been weathered down to leather and gristle by forty years of prospecting — but in the eyes. Dark, set deep, with a watchfulness that seemed to be appraising the room even when the owner was looking elsewhere. The same eyes had looked up at Tamsin from a pool of blood on her deck and asked her, in a voice like gravel sliding, to keep a promise.

She said nothing about any of this. Instead she said: "I need a survey done. Confidential."

"I don't do confidential work for people I haven't met before breakfast."

"It's nine in the morning."

"Then come back tomorrow." But the woman didn't look away, and there was something in her stillness that wasn't rudeness. It was the stillness of an animal deciding whether the thing in the doorway was predator or weather.

"Tamsin Delacroix," Tamsin said, though the woman clearly knew it. "And you're Vasquez."

"Elena Vasquez." And there it was — the flinch. Small, fast, gone in half a blink, but Tamsin had spent a lifetime reading water and water taught you to see things that lasted only half a blink. People flinch at their own names for two reasons: shame or fear. This wasn't shame.

"Vasquez," Tamsin repeated, tasting it. "That's a Spanish name."

"It's a common name."

"Mm. My first mate is a Vasquez. Raul. From Coyhaique way."

"Then you know it's common." Elena turned back to her drafting table, which was either dismissal or armor, and began squaring a sheet of paper that was already square.

Tamsin stayed where she was. "I'm told the Chico runs dry-side through the escarpment country. Narrow channels, sandbars that move after every rain. You surveyed it. Tell me — where does a boat draw trouble between the old mission landing and the basalt shelf?"

Elena's pen stopped. "Why would a riverboat captain ask me that? You know that water better than any chart could tell you."

"Humor me. I like to hear how other people say it."

A long pause. Then, without turning around: "Trouble starts at the bend below the mission, where the current piles against the north bank and the channel pretends to be twice as wide as it is. Boats hug the south bank for safety and find the bar that builds there every spring from the wash of the cliffs. The safe-looking water is the trap. The ugly-looking channel, close under the north bank, is the truth." She paused. "My father taught me that. He ran supplies up the Chico before the company roads went in."

"Your father," Tamsin said. "What was his name?"

This time the pause went on long enough to have weather of its own. "He died," Elena said. "Some time ago. Drowned, they told me. In the Chico." Her voice stayed level, but her hand had closed around the pen hard enough that Tamsin could see the knuckles whiten. "Is your confidential survey about the Chico?"

"It might be."

"Then I can't take it." Elena finally turned, and her eyes — those eyes, God help her, those eyes — met Tamsin's straight on. "I'm sorry, Captain. I don't go upriver anymore. I don't take commissions that touch the escarpment country, and I don't discuss my family with strangers who arrive asking questions in the shape of answers. If that's all—"

"It isn't." Tamsin took off her cap, which was as close to a gesture of respect as her body knew how to make, and set it on the drafting table. "But it'll keep. I've been on the river six days without a proper meal, and I'm told the bakery next door has given up on bread entirely, which tells me everything I need to know about this town. Is there anywhere in Puerto Deseado that still cooks food it would admit to?"

Something shifted in Elena's face — not a smile, exactly, but the place where a smile might decide to live later. "There's a woman two streets over, Marta, who fries trout if you compliment her garden and insult her nephew. Not necessarily in that order."

"Then you'll introduce me."

"Why would I do that?"

"Because," Tamsin said, picking up her cap and settling it back on her head, "you're curious. You've been staring at my boat since I walked in — don't bother denying it, the window behind you faces the harbor and the *Cazadora* is the only thing moored there uglier than the customs launch. And people who are curious about my boat generally have a reason to want to know where it's going."

Elena held her gaze for three full seconds. Then she capped her pen, squared the sheet one final time, and reached for a coat that matched the cardigan in its air of borrowed masculinity.

"Marta's closes at two," she said. "And Captain Delacroix — if this is about my father, I should warn you that I've spent five years hoping he was dead. Hoping it very specifically. Because the alternative was worse."

"The alternative being?"

"That someone wanted him to be."

* * *

Marta's was a room with four tables, a wood stove, and a garden visible through a window that had been installed, judging by the view, purely so Marta could watch people admire it. Tamsin complimented the roses. Elena insulted the nephew. Trout arrived within ten minutes, whole, crisp-skinned, and glistening, and Marta refilled their glasses without being asked, which in Tamsin's experience was the universal signal that a hostess had decided two people were going to talk whether they liked it or not.

They ate mostly in silence for a while. Tamsin was hungry enough that silence felt like a fair price. Elena ate the way she drafted — precisely, completely, wasting nothing.

"You surveyed the Deseado estuary," Tamsin said at last. "The tidal channels above the second narrows. There's a spring-fed creek on the south shore that doesn't appear on any government chart. Fresh water, running year-round, into a salt estuary. Somebody found it and kept it quiet. Who?"

Elena set down her fork. "How do you know about that creek?"

"Same way I know about the sandbar under the south bank below the mission. I listen. Rivers talk; most people just talk louder." Tamsin leaned back. "Here's what I know, señorita, and you can spend it however you like. Six days ago a man boarded my boat at a dock on the upper Chico that hasn't seen traffic in a decade. He was bleeding from somewhere internal, though he'd done his best to hide it. He paid me for passage with a piece of waxed linen covered in his own handwriting, and he made me swear — swore me, a woman who has broken every promise she ever made to herself — to deliver it to a girl with two names before the season ends. He died before morning. I put him under stones at the river mouth because the frost was in the ground." She watched Elena's face across the table. "I'm not asking you anything yet. I'm telling you what I know, because I don't like carrying things alone, and because whoever that man was, he chose his courier badly and his daughter well."

The stove ticked. Somewhere in the kitchen Marta dropped a pan and swore with real artistry.

"My father's name," Elena said quietly, "was Tomás Vasquez. He was a surveyor. He worked the dry side for twenty-two years, and then he worked for himself, and then—" She stopped. Started again. "Eight months ago the letters stopped. Last month a lawyer in Comodoro sent word that he'd drowned. No body recovered. No inquest. A certificate signed by a doctor whose practice, I discovered, is registered to a holding company owned by Andes Sur Mining." Her eyes came up. "You said a girl with two names."

"I said that."

"Elena Vasquez is the name on my business license. It's also the name on a subpoena I received in March, requesting my testimony regarding certain land transactions I witnessed while surveying for the provincial court." She said it flatly, factually, the way she might describe a contour line. "The judge who issued it, Judge Ferrero, is the same judge who certified the Andes Sur concession boundaries. I declined to testify. I also declined to remain findable. So yes, Captain. Two names. The other one is mine, and I don't give it out over trout."

Tamsin sat with that for a moment. Outside the window, the harbor light was going silver and flat, the way it did before the afternoon wind came up off the Atlantic. Somewhere down the street a dog was losing an argument with a gull.

"The man on my boat," Tamsin said slowly, "had eyes like yours."

"I know." Elena's voice cracked, just once, and she pressed it flat again with visible effort. "When you walked in, I thought — for one stupid second — that he'd sent you. That he was alive, and angry with me, and coming to drag me home the way he used to when I wandered off surveying alone as a child." She laughed, a short, terrible sound. "Instead you're telling me he's under a pile of rocks."

"He asked me to find you. He didn't tell me why. He didn't have breath enough." Tamsin turned her glass on the table, watching the light move in it. "I've got the map. I haven't opened it past the corner where your name is written, because it struck me that a dying man's last request deserves better than a captain's curiosity in a bad mood. But I'll tell you what I've worked out from the little I've seen. Whatever's on that linen, it isn't treasure. Treasure doesn't need delivering before a season ends. Deadlines mean paperwork, and paperwork means somebody's claim runs out."

Elena had gone very pale, and very still, and her eyes — Tomás Vasquez's eyes, watching the room even now — had fixed on Tamsin with an intensity that made the hair on Tamsin's arms rise.

"Andes Sur's concession filings," Elena whispered, "expire at the end of the season. If there's a defect in the boundaries — if there's unregistered water inside them — the whole grant collapses. Everything they've bought. Everything they've bullied. Everything Judge Ferrero certified." Her hand found the edge of the table and gripped it. "Captain. Where is that map right now?"

Tamsin weighed the question, and the woman asking it, and the six days of dead man riding against her ribs.

"Close," she said. "Safer than a bank, closer than a church. But before I show it to anyone, I need to know one thing, and I need you to answer it the way you'd answer a client paying for honesty instead of comfort." She leaned forward. "If I put that map in your hands, and it turns out to be worth killing for — and men have already died getting it this far — what will you do with it? File it? Burn it? Bargain with it? Think carefully. I've promised a dead man, and I don't intend to break my promise just to watch it drown."

Elena looked at her for a long moment. Through the window, the afternoon wind arrived on schedule, flattening the roses Marta was so proud of, and rattling something loose in the street outside — footsteps, more than one set, moving toward the door of the little restaurant with the unhurried confidence of men who had never once needed to hurry.

"Captain Delacroix," Elena said, not taking her eyes off the window, "I think you're about to get your answer sooner than either of us planned."
