## 3. The Company's Kind Offer

Elena Vasquez had learned to read a room the way other people read tide tables: quickly, and with an eye for what could sink you.

The room in question was the back office of the Hotel Austral, where the landlady let her spread out her drafting work on the long table by the window because Elena paid in cash and never complained about the draft. She had been there three days, correcting harbor charts for a shipping agent who smelled of kerosene and gratitude, and she had spent those three days being nobody in particular — a quiet woman with ink-stained fingers and a name that fit her like a borrowed coat.

Which was why it unsettled her deeply when the man at the door said, "Señorita Vasquez," in a voice that made the borrowed coat feel suddenly two sizes too small.

She did not look up from her pen. "You have the wrong table. I'm nobody's Vasquez."

"Of course." He smiled the way a bank manager smiles when you've overdrawn. He was perhaps fifty, silver at the temples, wearing a coat too fine for Puerto Deseado and boots too clean for anywhere south of Buenos Aires. He set a leather folder on the corner of her table as if claiming territory. "Then I apologize to the nobody in question. My name is Ferrán. I represent Andes Sur Mining."

Elena kept her eyes on the coastline she was inking. Her heartbeat, traitorous thing, picked up its pace like a horse that smells home. "I don't know anything about mining."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "You know about water."

* * *

He pulled out the chair across from her without asking, which told her more than his introduction had. Men who asked permission were performing manners. Men who didn't had stopped believing they needed them.

"My employer has recently acquired certain estancias along the upper Chacabuco," Ferrán said. "Fine land. Poor land, actually — dry as a sermon — but the company is optimistic. We believe there may be unregistered water sources in the escarpment. Springs. Seeps. The kind of thing an old prospector might stumble across and, being old-fashioned, write down."

"I'm a cartographer," Elena said. "I draw coastlines. The sea doesn't keep secrets; there's no market for them."

"There's always a market for secrets, señorita. That's rather the whole economy." He opened the folder and slid a paper across her chart. It was a photograph of her father — taken years ago, before the gray came into his beard, standing on some scree slope with a grin like a boy who'd found something. Under the photograph was a figure written in neat figures.

"That's a great deal of money for a dead man's doodles," Ferrán said.

Elena looked at the photograph for exactly as long as it took to break her heart quietly, and then she looked up. "My father drowned. Two years ago. The river took him near the narrows. There was a notice."

"There was a notice," Ferrán agreed. "Notices are inexpensive."

The words went into her like a fishhook and sat there. She made herself stay still. Outside the window, a gull screamed at something on the dock, and the ordinary world went on being ordinary, which felt obscene.

"What do you mean by that?"

Ferrán folded his hands. He had the patience of a man being paid by the hour. "I mean, señorita, that Tomás Vasquez was not a careless man. Forty years on that river and he knew every rock between here and the cordillera by its nickname. A man like that does not drown in a stretch of water he could navigate blindfolded and drunk — and Tomás, whatever his virtues, was frequently both." He paused, letting it settle. "The company's information suggests he died with a boat hook mark on his temple. But you'd know better than we would. You received his effects, after all."

"I received a letter," Elena said carefully. "And his compass."

"And nothing else? No papers? No drawings?" His voice stayed gentle, which somehow made it worse. "A man who knew he was in danger sends things ahead. We believe he sent them to you."

* * *

Here is what Elena knew, and what she had known since the letter arrived six weeks ago in handwriting gone shaky:

Her father had written: *Mija — if this reaches you, do not come looking for me. Come looking for what I found. I've left it where only family would think to look. Trust no one who says my name softly.*

She had gone to the church in Comodoro where he'd been baptized, and behind the register, wrapped in oilcloth, she had found a map drawn in her father's own hand — the hand that had taught her hers. She had read it once, understood perhaps a tenth of it, and hidden it inside the false bottom of her drafting tube, where it had traveled with her ever since like a stone in her shoe.

She had not understood it was worth a man's life. She understood it now, sitting across from a man whose boots were too clean, while a photograph of her father lay on the table like bait.

"You're mistaken," she said. "There were no papers."

"Mm." Ferrán studied her the way she studied charts — patiently, looking for the discrepancy. Then, unexpectedly, he smiled, and for a moment it was almost warm. "You have his eyes, you know. Tomás had the same trick of going very still when he was deciding whether to lie. He was bad at lying. I suspect you're better."

"I'm excellent at it," Elena said. "But I don't need the practice today, because I'm telling you the truth. I have no papers. And even if I did, I wouldn't sell my father's last work to a company that buys dry land and prays for rain."

Something flickered behind Ferrán's pleasantness — respect, maybe, or recalculation. He closed the folder but did not take it back.

"Let me offer you a kindness instead, then, since money doesn't tempt you." He leaned forward. "The men the company employs to resolve these matters are not office men. They are gauchos with debts and tempers, and they have been given your description — though I confess they were given the wrong surname until this morning, which is the only reason you've had three quiet days. If the papers surface anywhere — a port, a land office, a riverboat — they will find them, and whoever is holding them will have an accident involving water. Rivers are convenient that way. Nobody investigates a drowning down here."

"Is that a threat?"

"Heavens, no. It's geography." He stood, straightened his immaculate coat, and left the folder on the table. "Keep the photograph. Keep the number. When you decide — and you will decide, señorita, everyone does — send word to the company office. No harm will come to you. That I can promise personally. What happens to anyone *helping* you, however..." He shrugged elegantly. "Geography again."

At the door he paused. "For what it's worth, your father loved you very much. He talked about you the way misers talk about gold. It's why he sent it to you and not to a lawyer. Family hides things best." And then he was gone, leaving behind a faint smell of good tobacco and a silence with teeth in it.

* * *

Elena sat for a long time. The light moved across her unfinished chart. The gull outside continued its one-sided argument with the dock.

She should be afraid. She was afraid — her hands had a fine tremor she controlled only by gripping the pen hard enough to whiten her knuckles. But underneath the fear, something else was rising, slow and hot, the way a spring comes up through rock: fury.

*Murdered.* Not drowned. Not careless. Murdered for a piece of paper, and the murderers had then had the gall to sit across from his daughter and call it geography.

She thought about her father teaching her to draw contour lines when she was eight, his big cracked hand over her small one: *"The land tells you where the water wants to go, mija. Your job is just to listen and write it down honestly. An honest map outlives every liar who reads it."*

An honest map outlives every liar.

Well. She had an honest map. And apparently she also had liars.

Elena opened the drafting tube, checked — as she had checked eleven times already — that the oilcloth packet was still there, still flat, still real. Then she closed it and began, deliberately, to finish her coastline, because a woman whose hands shook drew bad lines, and bad lines got people noticed.

It was while she was inking a headland that the plan arrived, fully formed, the way plans do when fear finally stops shouting and starts thinking.

She couldn't run. Running was what they expected, and running meant open country and gauchos. She couldn't hide in Puerto Deseado; Ferrán had already found her once and would find her twice. Which left the river — the one place her father had been king, the one place a woman with a strong back and no surname could disappear into honest work.

There was, she recalled, a riverboat captain in port. Delacroix, someone had said. The Cazadora. The last one fool enough to run the dry side. They said she was difficult, uncompromising, and short-tempered, which sounded to Elena exactly like the sort of person who hated mining companies on principle.

Tomorrow, Elena decided, she would go down to the dock and ask for work. Deckhand, cook, bilge-rat — she wasn't proud. She'd scrub decks until her hands bled if it put river water between her and men with clean boots.

She did not yet know that Tamsin Delacroix was carrying her father's map in the ballast, or that the captain had already looked into her face and seen a dead man staring back. She did not know that the boat she intended to hide aboard was already the most dangerous place in Patagonia.

She only knew that her father had trusted the river with his whole life, and the river had failed him — so it was past time somebody trusted it back.

Outside, the tide turned, and somewhere upriver, in the dark folds of the escarpment, water that belonged to nobody yet went on rising toward the light.
