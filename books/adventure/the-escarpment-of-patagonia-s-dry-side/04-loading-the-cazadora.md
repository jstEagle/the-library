## 4. Loading the Cazadora

The *Cazadora* had a way of telling Tamsin what she needed before Tamsin knew it herself. That morning she creaked in her moorings like an old woman complaining about the weather, and Tamsin took it as a verdict: they were leaving Puerto Deseado, and they were leaving soon, and they were not leaving alone.

She found Elena Vasquez at the harbor office again, third morning running, bent over the same chart table with the same pencil worn down to a stub. The girl drew rivers the way some people prayed — quietly, repeatedly, as if the lines might eventually answer.

"You're hired," Tamsin said, without preamble.

Elena's pencil stopped. "I haven't applied for anything."

"I know. That's the beauty of it. No paperwork, no references, no awkward questions about why a cartographer who knows the Deseado's sandbars better than the harbor master wants to spend a season hauling rope on a rust-bucket riverboat." Tamsin leaned against the doorframe. "Deckhand. Room, board, and wages I'll pay in fish when the fish are biting and apologies when they're not."

"I don't know anything about boats."

"Nobody does until they do. I didn't. My father threw me in the engine room at eleven and told me to find the leak. I found it. Eventually. After the engine room was mostly river." She shrugged. "You'll learn faster. You've got the look of someone who doesn't like drowning."

Elena studied her with those eyes — the dead man's eyes, patient and gray and full of rivers Tamsin hadn't mapped yet. "Why me? You have a crew."

"I have Raul, who is one man, and a boat that requires four. And I have a superstition." Tamsin let that hang. "The *Cazadora* runs better with a woman at the rail. Ask any fisherman in this port. They'll lie about it, but they know."

It wasn't entirely a lie. It also wasn't the truth, which was that Tamsin Delacroix had made a promise to a dying man on a lonely dock, and the fastest way to keep a promise was to keep the person it concerned where she could see her. Elena Vasquez — or whoever she was this week — was going to be delivered to her own inheritance if Tamsin had to drag her there by the collar, and a person could not be dragged toward something she was running from. She had to be led. Gently. With fish wages.

Elena closed her chart book. "When do we sail?"

"Tomorrow's tide. Wear boots you don't love."

* * *

The hiding of the map took Tamsin the better part of an afternoon and all of her patience.

The *Cazadora* carried ballast in her old hull — river stones and scrap iron packed into the bilge spaces below the cargo deck, a habit from her coal-barging days that no modern boat bothered with anymore. Tamsin had always liked the arrangement. It gave the boat a low, stubborn center of gravity, like a gaucho who'd had a bad night but refused to admit it.

She waited until Raul went ashore for provisions, then pried up the aft bilge plate, wrapped the prospector's oilcloth packet in three layers of tarred canvas, and wedged it between two stones where the condensation ran. It was not a clever hiding place. It was a *boring* one, which was better. Nobody searched for anything boring.

"There," she told the boat, pressing the plate back down. "Now behave."

The boat creaked, which Tamsin chose to interpret as agreement.

She was wiping her hands on her trousers when she heard the footsteps on the gangplank — Raul's particular rhythm, heavy on the left foot, the gait of a man who'd spent thirty years compensating for a knee the river had claimed in '09. He came down the ladder with a crate of onions and stopped.

He stopped the way Raul stopped when he'd counted something and the number was wrong.

"Capitana," he said. "We have fuel for the season."

"We do."

"We have fuel for the season *and a half*." He set down the onions. "Eight drums on the aft deck. I ordered six. The chandlery says the order came from this boat. I did not make this order. You did not make this order, because you are cheap in a way I have always admired."

Tamsin kept her face pleasant. "I'm feeling generous."

"You are feeling *something*." Raul's eyes went to the bilge plate — not obviously, not accusingly, just the way a man's eyes go to a place that has recently been disturbed, because thirty years on a boat teaches you where the dust lies and where it doesn't. "You came back from that dock with a dead man's face on you, capitana. I've known you eleven years. You wear your moods like weather. This one has a smell to it."

"Onions," Tamsin said. "The crate's leaking."

Raul looked at the crate, which was not leaking. He looked at her. He picked the crate back up with the sigh of a man who had decided, for the moment, to lose an argument he intended to win later. "The new deckhand. The cartographer. She starts tomorrow?"

"Tonight. She'll sleep in the fore cabin."

"The fore cabin leaks."

"Everything on this boat leaks. It's how we stay humble." Tamsin took the onions from him, which ended the conversation in the way taking a man's onions always does. "Raul. The drums stay. The questions can wait for the river."

"The river," Raul said darkly, "answers questions. That's the problem with it."

* * *

Elena came aboard at dusk with one canvas bag and a look of a woman who had left places before and gotten efficient at it. Tamsin showed her the fore cabin, the galley, the head with its bucket-and-hope arrangement, and the rail, which was the only part of the boat that worked as designed.

"You'll stand the morning watch," Tamsin said. "Sound the channel with the pole, call the sandbars, and wake me if the water turns the color of weak coffee, which means the wind's come around and we're in for it."

"Weak coffee," Elena repeated. "Not strong coffee?"

"Strong coffee means nothing. The river's always strong coffee somewhere. Weak coffee is a warning." Tamsin handed her the sounding pole, longer than Elena was tall. "You'll hate it by Friday."

"I've had worse jobs."

"I don't doubt it." Tamsin watched her test the pole's weight, watched her hands find the grip without being told. Cartographer's hands. Her father's hands. "You can ask, you know."

"Ask what?"

"Whatever it is you're not asking. Everyone who comes on this boat with a bag that small is not-asking something. It's practically a uniform."

Elena was quiet a moment. The harbor lights came on across the water, one by one, like the town deciding to admit it existed. "Why did you really hire me?"

Because your father paid me in a map and a promise. Because you have his eyes and his stubborn jaw and you flinch at your own name like it's a door someone kicked in. Because the men who killed him will come looking for what he carried, and I'd rather they find me standing between.

"Because you know rivers," Tamsin said, "and I need someone who knows rivers, and I don't trust anyone in this port who *says* they know rivers."

It was, at least, true.

Elena nodded slowly, and Tamsin saw — or thought she saw — the exact moment the girl decided not to believe her, and to sail anyway. There was a word for two people on a small boat each holding a secret and both pretending the boat was just a boat. Tamsin couldn't think of it. She suspected it was in a language she didn't speak, probably one with a lot of consonants and a long history of shipwrecks.

"Get some sleep," she said. "Weak coffee at dawn."

* * *

They cast off on the morning tide with the fuel drums lashed aft, the ballast hiding its secret, and Raul at the wheel wearing the expression of a man keeping a ledger in his head that would eventually balance against somebody.

Tamsin stood at the rail as Puerto Deseado slid astern — the land office, the chandlery, the harbor office where a quiet cartographer had spent three mornings drawing rivers that weren't hers yet. The *Cazadora* settled into the channel, low and stubborn, and the escarpment rose ahead of them along the dry side, red and patient as an old wound.

Elena took the sounding pole to the bow. Raul watched her do it, then watched Tamsin, then watched the fuel drums, cataloguing.

"Capitana," he said at last, too quietly for the bow to hear. "The girl with the bag. The drums. The bilge plate you think I didn't see you move." He turned the wheel a half-point, gentle, keeping them off the shoal without looking at it. "Whatever we're running from — I'd like to know if I should be running *toward* it or *ahead* of it."

Tamsin looked at the water. Weak coffee, just beginning.

"Neither," she said. "Just keep us off the sandbars, Raul. Everything else, I'll tell you when I can. And if two gauchos come asking about a dead man's effects—" she met his eyes, "—we've never heard of any dead man."

Raul was quiet for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, the way he did when the river made a decision for them both.

"Two gauchos came to the chandlery yesterday," he said. "Asking about this boat. I told them we were hauling salt to the estancias." He adjusted the wheel another half-point. "I also told them we'd be at the fuel stop at Cañadón Mocoso by Thursday."

Tamsin went cold. "Raul."

"Which," he said, "is why you should know that I have a brother in Bahía who owes me money, a knee that predicts storms, and a memory that is very good but not perfect." He looked at her steadily. "I have not yet decided what I remember about Thursday, capitana. It would help my memory a great deal if you told me what we're actually carrying."

Ahead, the channel bent toward the open river, and the escarpment leaned closer, and the weak-coffee water carried them all — the map, the secret, the girl with two names, and the first mate's arithmetic — toward whatever the dry side had decided to charge for passage this season.
