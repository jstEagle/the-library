## 5. Sandbar Justice

The fuel stop at Cañadón Miserere was less a settlement than an apology for one: a rusted pump, a tin-roofed shed that doubled as a store and a church depending on the day of the week, and a long gravel spit where the river ran shallow enough to ground a flat-bottomed boat without shame. Tamsin had been stopping there for eleven years. The pump worked about half the time, and the man who ran it, old Fermín, charged whatever he felt like and called it commerce.

She brought the *Cazadora* in slow, letting her kiss the gravel with that particular grinding sigh of a hull finding bottom. Elena stood at the rail with a coil of rope she'd been holding for twenty minutes without once needing it.

"You can put that down," Tamsin said. "We're not docking. We're beaching. There's a difference, and it's mostly dignity."

Elena set the rope down carefully, as though it might be offended. "How deep is it here?"

"Knee-deep at the spit, waist-deep if you step wrong. The river doesn't forgive carelessness, but it does appreciate a good sense of humor." Tamsin swung herself over the rail and dropped into the shallows, boots splashing. "Come on. We need two hundred liters and whatever Fermín is passing off as coffee this year."

Fermín's coffee was, as always, a crime against the bean, but it was hot, and the shed smelled of diesel and dried herbs and forty years of men arguing about rain. Tamsin paid for fuel and drank the coffee standing up, watching the river through the doorway out of habit. Habit had kept her alive longer than skill had.

It was Elena who saw them first. Tamsin noticed because the girl went still — not frightened-still, but the stillness of someone counting.

"Two horses," Elena said quietly. "Coming down the north bank."

Tamsin turned. Two riders, moving at that unhurried pace gauchos used when they wanted you to know they weren't hurrying because they didn't have to. Dust hung behind them in the dry air like a question mark.

"Fermín," Tamsin said pleasantly, "how much would you charge me to close that door and pretend we were never here?"

"More than you have," Fermín said, not looking up from his ledger. "And they'd see the smoke from your galley anyway."

"Fair." She drained the coffee. It tasted like punishment. "Elena. Listen carefully, because I'm only going to say this once, and then I'm going to lie to two men with very poor manners."

* * *

They came onto the spit at half past ten, dismounting with the loose-jointed ease of men who spent more hours in a saddle than in a bed. The taller one wore his hat low and his smile lower; the shorter one carried a rifle slung backward, which was either carelessness or theater, and Tamsin suspected theater.

"Captain Delacroix," the tall one said. His voice was soft, almost courteous, which is how she knew he'd been told to be frightening. "You had a passenger last week. Out of the north dock. An old man."

"I have passengers every week," Tamsin said. "Most of them alive when they board. I don't keep records of their sins."

"He paid you. For delivery of certain effects."

"Effects." She let the word sit in the sun a moment. "You mean papers."

The short one shifted. The rifle came around off his shoulder — slowly, casually, the way a man handles a tool he's decided he'll use. "The captain is being difficult, Osvaldo."

"The captain is being honest," Tamsin corrected. "Which looks similar from a distance. I gave the old man passage. He died aboard, poor soul, before noon the next day. I buried him where the law requires — marked, witnessed, filed with the port authority at Deseado. Whatever he carried went into the ground with him or into the magistrate's hands. You're welcome to write to the magistrate. He writes back very slowly."

Osvaldo smiled. It did not reach anywhere near his eyes. "My employers believe otherwise."

"Your employers believe a great many things. That's what money does — it buys belief the way cheap whiskey buys courage. Briefly, and at a markup."

The tall one stepped closer. Close enough that she could smell horse and sweat and the leather oil on his chaps. "Señora. I am going to ask you plainly, and then I am going to stop asking."

"Please do," Tamsin said. "I've been waiting all morning for the part where you stop asking. It's my favorite part."

Behind them, Elena had gone pale but hadn't moved. Tamsin registered that peripherally — the girl standing by the gangplank, hands loose at her sides, watching with the fixed attention of someone memorizing a lesson. Good. Let her learn what kind of boat she'd signed onto.

"Search the boat," Osvaldo said to his companion.

"No," Tamsin said.

The word came out flat and final, a door closing. The short one laughed and started up the plank anyway, and Tamsin let him get exactly three steps before she moved — not toward him, but past him, down the spit, wading to the *Cazadora*'s stern with the unhurried stride of a woman checking her mooring lines.

"Gentlemen," she called back, "I should mention something about my engine."

"What engine?" Osvaldo said.

"Exactly." She climbed aboard, disappeared into the engine room, and began, with great deliberation, to make a tremendous amount of noise.

* * *

What followed was, Tamsin would reflect later, among the finer performances of her career, and she had once talked a customs officer out of inspecting her hold using nothing but a borrowed wedding ring and a convincing cough.

She hammered. She swore in three languages. She produced a wrench and beat upon the fuel line housing with the rhythm of a woman losing a fight with machinery. Then she emerged onto the deck, wiping her hands on a rag already black with grease she'd applied from the bilge, and announced, tragically:

"She's dead. Fuel pump's seized. Hasn't run since yesterday — I've been drifting on the current and praying, and God and I have an arrangement that doesn't extend to engines."

Osvaldo looked at the boat. The boat sat on the gravel spit, knee-deep in water, looking innocent. "Then how did you arrive?"

"On a prayer and a favorable wind, señor. Ask any widow." She hopped down to the spit, rag over her shoulder. "I'm stranded until parts come up from Deseado. Three days, maybe four. You gentlemen are welcome to wait with me. There's coffee."

"We'll search the boat while we wait," the short one said.

"While she's broken down? While she's full of water in the bilge and my personal effects are scattered everywhere?" Tamsin shook her head with genuine sorrow. "No. No, I couldn't allow it. A captain's vessel is her home, señor. You wouldn't search a widow's house."

"I would," the short one said, "if I thought she was lying."

"Then it's fortunate I'm not." And here Tamsin delivered the masterstroke, the one she'd been building toward since the hammering started. She pointed downstream, brow furrowed with sudden concern. "Although — if you're waiting anyway, you should move your horses up. That shoal there—" she gestured at the gravel bar extending off the spit into the channel, the one every local pilot knew by its true name, *El Mentiroso*, the Liar, because it grew and shrank with the season and had grounded more boats than bad luck — "that shoal drowns at midday tide. Comes right up over the gravel. Your animals will be swimming."

Both men looked at the shoal. Both men looked at their horses, standing placidly on the low gravel bar, cropping at nothing. Neither man knew the river. That was the whole trouble with hired men, Tamsin thought — they knew money, and money didn't know water.

They led the horses up the spit, grumbling, and settled in to wait under the eave of Fermín's shed, rifles across their knees, watching the *Cazadora* with the patience of vultures who believed the carcass wasn't going anywhere.

Tamsin bought them lunch. It cost her almost nothing and confused them completely.

* * *

At eleven forty, the tide began to turn.

Tamsin felt it before she saw it — a change in the river's voice, a deepening note under the surface chatter. El Mentiroso began to drown. Water crept over the gravel bar in a slow silver sheet, so gradual that a man watching the boat instead of the bar would never mark it.

At noon, she made her move.

"Elena," she said quietly, at the rail. "Engine room. When I give the word, open the fuel valve and crank her — she'll start fine, she always starts fine, that's rather the point. Then get us off this spit and into the channel, hard to starboard, past the shed."

"And them?"

"They'll be busy."

Elena looked at her — really looked, the way she'd been doing for two days, assembling pieces. "You're going to leave them."

"I'm going to *strand* them. There's a difference, and it's mostly dignity." Tamsin smiled, and found, to her mild surprise, that the smile was entirely sincere. "Their horses are picketed upstream behind the shed. Their boat—"

"They came by horse."

"They did. Which means they'll walk to Fermín's cousin's estancia, six hours north, and borrow transport, and by the time they reach anyone with a radio, we'll be thirty miles downriver and the story they tell will sound exactly like what it is — two grown men fooled by a tide." She watched the water climb the gravel, silvering wider. "The law says I should report them to the police at Deseado. The police at Deseado take Andes Sur's money for their annual festival. So the law and I will be taking a brief holiday from one another."

"That's not legal," Elena said. Not accusing. Testing.

"No," Tamsin agreed cheerfully. "It isn't. It's justice, which is cheaper and more reliable, though harder to file paperwork on." She glanced sideways. "If you want off the boat, this is the moment. I'll put you ashore at the next crossing and think no worse of you. But if you stay, understand what you're staying on. This isn't a ferry anymore, girl. It hasn't been since an old man bled on my deck."

Elena held her gaze a long moment. Something passed between them — not trust, not yet, but the honest acknowledgment that neither of them trusted easily, which is sometimes the beginning of the same thing.

"I'm staying," Elena said.

"Good." Tamsin raised two fingers. "Now. On three. One—"

Down the spit, Osvaldo stood suddenly, frowning at the water around his boots. It was past his ankles. The horses whinnied somewhere behind the shed.

"—two," Tamsin said, savoring it, "three."

The *Cazadora*'s engine caught on the first turn — that big faithful diesel roaring to life like a dog pretending it hadn't been napping all morning. Elena threw her whole body into the helm, and the boat came off the gravel with a lurch and a spray, swinging hard to starboard, gathering speed down the channel.

Tamsin stood at the stern rail as the shore fell away. Osvaldo was shouting something — threats, probably, though the wind took most of it. The short one fired once, wildly, the bullet slapping the river a hundred meters astern, and Tamsin lifted her hand in a small, gracious wave.

The *Cazadora* rounded the bend, and Cañadón Miserere was gone.

Elena killed the throttle and leaned on the wheel, breathing hard, staring at her own hands as if they belonged to someone more interesting. "They'll come after us."

"Oh, certainly." Tamsin took the wheel from her, easing the boat into the current's rhythm. "But now they have to explain to their employers how two armed men lost a widow and a cartographer to a *tide*. Men who work for money hate telling stories where they look foolish. They'll hesitate. They'll second-guess. Hesitation is fuel, and theirs just ran dry."

Elena was quiet a moment. Then, slowly, she smiled — a small, private thing, like something remembered rather than invented. "You enjoyed that."

Tamsin considered denying it. Considered the version of herself that disapproved of stranding armed men on drowning gravel bars, that filed reports and trusted magistrates and slept soundly.

"I did," she admitted. "That's the part that worries me."

She said it lightly, but it wasn't light, and Elena heard that. The girl nodded once and went forward to check the lines, and Tamsin steered alone with the river talking under the hull.

Because here was the truth she'd been circling for two days: the old man's map was hidden in her ballast, and men had already died for it, and she had just discovered that some corner of her — a corner she'd kept locked since her husband's funeral — had *liked* the fight. Had liked being outside the law's reach, where the only rules were hers and the river's.

The river forgives nothing, her father used to say. Tonight it doesn't have to.

Ahead, the sky over the western meseta had gone the bruised purple that meant weather coming down off the cordillera — a real storm, the kind that forced boats ashore and opened hatches and shook loose everything a hull was hiding.

Tamsin looked at the clouds, and then at the ballast hatch, and did not like the arithmetic at all.
