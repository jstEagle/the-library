## 6. What the Map Shows

The storm came off the meseta like something with a grudge.

Elena felt it before she saw it — a pressure in her ears, a smell of dust and ozone rolling down the escarpment's dry face. Tamsin Delacroix stood at the wheel with her nose lifted like a hound's, and said one word: "Coyhole."

"Is that a place or a threat?" Elena asked.

"Both." Tamsin spun the wheel. "Old fuel stop. Roof leaks, but it leaks in only two places, and I know where to put the bunks."

They made the inlet with ten minutes to spare, the *Cazadora* nosing into a slot of brown water between walls of wind-scoured rock while the sky went the color of wet slate. Elena worked the bow line without being told twice now; three days on the river had taught her hands things her head hadn't approved of yet. The storm hit as she tied off, a fist of rain and grit that turned the world to static, and then they were inside the coyhole's corrugated shed, listening to it hammer the roof like an impatient creditor.

Tamsin lit the stove, put water on for mate, and announced that this was officially the part of the voyage where nobody did anything useful.

"I can mend lines," Elena offered.

"You can sit down is what you can do. You've been coiling rope like it insulted your mother."

"It coiled badly first."

Tamsin grinned — that quick, crooked grin that kept ambushing Elena when she wasn't braced for it — and settled onto a crate with the deep sigh of a woman who had been waiting all day for permission to stop moving.

* * *

It was Raul who left the map where she could find it. Or rather, it was Raul's carelessness, or his snooping, or whatever it was he thought he was doing in the forward hold at dusk with a lantern and a pry bar. Elena went looking for him because the bilge pump sounded wrong, and found instead the loose section of ballast Tamsin must have opened and not closed properly — and inside it, wrapped in oilcloth, the dead man's hand-drawn map.

She should have put it back. She knew that immediately, the way you know you're standing somewhere you shouldn't be. But her father's handwriting has a gravity all its own, and there it was, the cramped, patient capitals she'd last seen on postcards from places with no postal service: *RÍO CHICO — LADO SECO*, and beneath it, in ink gone brown, a survey.

Not a treasure map. She'd half expected a treasure map, if she was honest — everyone always imagines their missing parent died sitting on gold. It was worse than gold. It was a hydrological survey, done in her father's meticulous, maddening detail, and it recorded a spring.

An unregistered spring. A permanent seep rising out of the escarpment's flank, feeding an underground channel that surfaced in three places — and those three places were marked with names Elena knew, because she'd drawn the boundary maps herself, two years ago, working freelance for Andes Sur Mining. Estancia Alvear. Estancia Fuentes. Estancia Chacabuco Sur.

Three estancias the company had bought in the last eighteen months for prices that had made even the sellers uneasy. Three estancias sitting on top of the only reliable water on the dry side for forty kilometers.

And in the margin, in her father's smaller hand, a note that stopped her breath:

*Spring predates all registered claims. Survey filed nowhere. If I die before filing, whoever finds this — file it. Water belongs to whoever stands on it, not whoever buys the paper first.*

Elena sat down on a coil of anchor chain and read it four more times.

* * *

The grief came first, which surprised her. She'd spent years being angry at Aurelio Vasquez — angry for leaving, angrier for never coming back, angriest of all for dying in some anonymous way that let her keep imagining he might still walk through a door. But sitting in a leaking shed with his handwriting under her fingers, all that anger collapsed into something simpler and much heavier: he had been here. He had walked this exact ground, measured this exact water, and thought of her. Not in the note itself — the note was addressed to a stranger, to anyone, which hurt in its own particular way — but in the fact of it. He had believed someone would find it. He had believed in strangers doing right by each other, which was either faith or foolishness, and Elena had never learned which.

Then the fury arrived, and it brought arithmetic with it.

Because she remembered the purchase files. She'd catalogued them. Andes Sur hadn't just bought the three estancias — they'd bought them cheap, using a valuation report that described the land as "seasonally arid, no reliable surface water." She'd photocopied that report herself. And the spring, unregistered, unfiled, meant the valuation was built on a lie the company may or may not have known about — except her father had surveyed the spring, and her father had shown the survey to somebody, and then her father had drowned in a river so shallow that drowning required explanation.

The company representative in Puerto Deseado had asked for "her father's papers." Had known what papers existed. Had known enough to offer money for them.

Elena folded the map along its old creases, wrapped it back in the oilcloth, and sat in the dark hold for a long time, listening to the storm argue with the roof.

* * *

She climbed out to find Tamsin awake, alone, feeding the stove. Raul had turned in early, or pretended to. The captain looked up, and Elena watched her do the thing she apparently did with everything: assess, weigh, decide whether to say it out loud.

"You've been in the ballast," Tamsin said.

There was no point denying it. "You left it open."

"I left it open," Tamsin agreed, "because I'm getting too old to pretend a locked box keeps secrets on my own boat. Did you read it?"

"Yes."

"And?"

Elena sat down across the stove from her. The firelight moved on Tamsin's face, and Elena realized she was about to cross a line she couldn't uncross — that telling this woman the truth would make them partners in something, and partners in something was exactly what she'd sworn never to be again after Puerto Deseado, after the judge, after the whole slow disaster of trusting people.

But her father had believed in strangers doing right by each other.

"He found a spring," Elena said. "Unregistered. It feeds three estancias — Alvear, Fuentes, Chacabuco Sur. The ones Andes Sur bought last year."

Tamsin went very still, in the way of river people, who learn that stillness is sometimes the only safe response to news. "I ran fuel past Chacabuco Sur in March. Company men had fenced the whole estancia and posted signs in two languages. Signs are expensive. Nobody fences dirt they think is worthless."

"The valuation called it seasonally arid."

"Seasonally arid." Tamsin snorted. "That's a phrase invented by a man who's never been thirsty. So the old man mapped water the company didn't know was there — or didn't want anyone else to know was there — and then he drowned."

"In knee-deep water."

Tamsin looked at her for a long moment. "You've done your own arithmetic."

"I'm a cartographer. Arithmetic is most of the job." Elena fed a stick into the stove, mostly to have something to do with her hands. "Here's what I can't get past. He wrote the note to a stranger. 'Whoever finds this.' He didn't write it to me. He didn't even try to send it to me."

"Maybe he did try," Tamsin said quietly. "Maybe sending it to you is what got him killed, and the boat was his second choice."

The stick snapped in Elena's fingers. She stared at the pieces.

"He paid me with that map," Tamsin went on. "Bloodied, half-dead, and he bargained. Men who bargain are men with a plan B. Plan A was probably a letter that never arrived, or arrived and was intercepted, or—" She shrugged. "I don't know. But I know this: he chose the river. People who trust rivers have usually run out of people to trust."

"That's bleak."

"That's Patagonia." Tamsin poured two cups of mate and pushed one across. "Drink. Then tell me what we do with it."

"We?"

"You're holding the inheritance, girl. That makes it a conversation, not a decree."

Elena drank. The mate was bitter and perfect. "We file it. The survey, the note, everything — with the land office in Puerto Deseado, before the concession review closes. If the spring predates the company's claims, the water rights revert to the landholders. All three estancias. The whole fraud unravels, because the valuation collapses, and the financing collapses with it."

"And the company will do anything — anything — to keep that paper from reaching the land office."

"Yes."

Tamsin nodded slowly, and there was no fear in her face, which frightened Elena more than any amount of fear would have. "Good," the captain said. "I was worried you'd want to sell it to them."

"They murdered my father."

"Or they didn't, and they'll murder us anyway once they realize we know. Either way, same weather ahead." Tamsin leaned back against the crate, and for a moment she looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with the storm. "You understand what you're asking. This isn't a court case. There's no protection out here. Once we turn upriver toward Deseado with that map aboard, every hour is a negotiation with people who've already decided how it ends."

"I've been negotiating with them since Puerto Deseado," Elena said. "They just haven't noticed yet."

Something kindled behind Tamsin's eyes — respect, maybe, or appetite. "You know, when I hired you I figured deckhand. Rope-coiler. Maybe conversationalist, eventually."

"I coil adequately."

"You coil like a woman thinking about something else." Tamsin drained her cup. "Get some sleep. Storm breaks by dawn, if the barometer's honest. We make Chacabuco narrows tomorrow afternoon, and after that—" she waved a hand at the dark window, at the hammering rain, at the whole indifferent wilderness, "—after that we find out how much trouble a piece of paper can be."

Elena lay in her bunk later, the map's words printed on the inside of her eyelids, and listened to the storm spend itself against the escarpment. Her father had chosen the river. She was starting to understand why.

She was almost asleep when she heard it: the forward hatch easing open, soft as a lie, and Raul's boots moving quiet along the deck toward the wheelhouse radio.
