## 7. Raul's Price

Elena learned to read Raul the way she'd learned to read contour lines: by what was missing.

A good map lied by omission. A river showed you everything except the rock under the foam, and you learned to distrust the smooth places, the stretches that seemed too easy, too cooperative. Raul had become smooth. He laughed at Tamsin's jokes a half-beat too quickly. He stopped complaining about the coffee, which no living man stops doing voluntarily. And on the third night after the storm, when Elena came up on deck to take the middle watch, she found him crouched over the radio cabinet with the back panel off and a soldering iron glowing in his hand.

He didn't startle. That was the worst part. A guilty man startles. Raul just looked up at her with an expression she could only call *tired*, and said, "Condensator's shot. Been meaning to fix it."

"You're holding the new one," Elena said. It was sitting on the chart table beside him, still in its paper wrapping from the chandlery in Comandante Luis Piedrabuena, still bearing Tamsin's handwriting on the receipt: *R. — before we lose the weather.*

Raul looked at it for a long moment. Then he set the soldering iron down on the chart table, directly on top of Tamsin's copy of the Chacabuco narrows survey, and left a small brown scar there that Elena would think about later, though at the time she didn't know why.

"You're the cartographer's daughter," he said. Not a question.

The river went quiet around them. Somewhere upstream a night heron made its sound like a hinge that needed oil.

"My father is dead," Elena said carefully.

"Your father was a careful man. Careful men draw maps. Careful men hide springs." He wiped his hands on a rag that had once been white. "I owe four hundred and sixty pesos to a man in Río Gallegos who has brothers. The *Cazadora* is worth maybe three hundred, and Tamsin would let it sink before she'd let it be sold. So." He shrugged, a small, terrible shrug, the shrug of a man setting down something heavy. "So."

"You're telling me this," Elena said. "Why would you tell me this?"

"Because you have honest eyes, and I wanted one person on this boat to know I'm not proud of it." He picked up the soldering iron again. "Also because in about two days it won't matter what you know. Go to bed, muchacha. Take the morning watch instead. Mornings are prettier."

Elena did not go to bed. She sat in the wheelhouse until dawn with the map pressed against her sternum inside her jacket, watching Raul's silhouette in the engine room, and she did not wake Tamsin, and she has never entirely forgiven herself for that. She told herself it was because there was no proof, because Raul would deny it, because a captain who couldn't trust her own mate had nothing left. She told herself a great many things. The river, which forgives nothing, was not listening.

* * *

The Chacabuco narrows announce themselves a full hour early, if you know how to listen. The water changes its mind. It stops moving with the valley and starts moving against it, and the banks draw in like a man taking a breath, and the birds — this was the thing Elena noticed, the thing she would carry — the birds leave. They rise off the willows all at once, silently, the way people leave a room when the argument has already started downstairs.

Tamsin felt it too. Elena watched her come forward from the engine room with her head cocked, listening to the water the way other people listen for a name being called in another room.

"Raul," Tamsin said. "Radio check. Call Puerto Deseado, tell them we'll be through the narrows by dusk."

Raul turned the dial. The set hissed, crackled, produced nothing but the long empty ocean-sound of a dead frequency. "Condensator," he said. "Told you."

Tamsin stood very still. Elena saw her look at the radio, then at the wrapping paper — still on the chart table, she realized with a lurch, still *there*, evidence sitting in plain sight — and then at Raul's back, and Elena watched something move across Tamsin's face that she had no name for yet. It was not surprise. It was the look of a woman doing arithmetic she'd been avoiding.

"Then we run it blind," Tamsin said, and her voice was perfectly level, and that was how Elena knew how afraid she was. Tamsin Delacroix only sounded calm when she was terrified. "Elena, take the bow. Hand signals. You've read my chart marks — you'll call the water for me."

"You want me to—"

"I want your eyes where mine can't reach. Go."

The narrows took them at 6:14 in the evening, with the light going copper and long. Elena stood at the bow with one hand on the rail and one hand free, and the *Cazadora* leaned into the first chute like a tired horse that still remembered being young, and for eleven minutes it was the finest thing Elena had ever been part of. Tamsin drove her by memory and by Elena's hands — *left, left, hard left, hold, HOLD* — and the boat threaded rocks that the charts said were not there, because the charts were three years old and the river had redecorated, and Tamsin read the water's face and believed Elena's hands over her own eyes, which is a trust Elena had never been given by anyone, and which made what came next feel like a personal wound.

The launch came out of the eastern chute at full throttle.

Elena saw it before anyone — two men in the bow, and one of them raising something long and dark, and her hand was already moving, *right, RIGHT* — but the shot came first. It went through the wheelhouse glass and through Tamsin's left shoulder and out the other side of the man she trusted with her boat, and Elena heard the crack of it and then heard nothing at all for the space of three heartbeats, because the river had gone enormous and silent, the way it does.

Then Tamsin stood up.

Elena will swear to this until she dies: the woman took a bullet through the shoulder and *stood up*, put both hands on the wheel — the left one shaking, leaving a red smear on the varnish that was still there a year later, that Elena still touches sometimes when she thinks no one is looking — and drove the *Cazadora* into the rapids with blood running down her ribs, because the only way out of an ambush in the Chacabuco narrows is through, and through means down.

"Raul!" Tamsin shouted, and it wasn't a call for help. It was a sentence. "You sold us for *four hundred and sixty pesos*."

Raul was at the stern rail, and he had the engine hatch open, and whatever he meant to do with it, he didn't get to do it, because the river chose that moment to put a standing wave the size of a church through the port chute, and the *Cazadora* shipped half a ton of green water, and the hatch slammed on Raul's forearm, and the launch behind them — the company's beautiful fast launch, built for calm lakes and confident men — met the second chute wrong and began, with a slowness that felt almost polite, to come apart on the rocks.

Elena ran aft. She got Raul's arm out of the hatch — he was screaming, not from the arm, she understood later, but from the look on her face — and then the boat hit the last chute, the one the gauchos called *La Viuda*, the Widow, and the world went white and loud and upside down, and Elena's last coherent thought was that the map was inside her jacket and the jacket was inside the river.

* * *

They came through the bottom of the narrows at 6:39 in the evening, listing, half-drowned, and alive. The *Cazadora* sat low in the water with her engine coughing like a smoker, and Tamsin sat on the wheelhouse deck with her shoulder bound in a shirt and Elena's scarf, gray as old candle wax, still holding the wheel out of what could only be called stubbornness.

The launch was gone. One gaucho clung to a rock mid-channel, screaming about his horse, which was on the wrong side of the river and, in every sense that mattered, so was he.

Raul sat at the stern with his broken arm cradled against him, and nobody spoke to him. That was the sentence. Not banishment, not the police — there were no police out here, and everyone knew whose police the police would be. Just silence. Raul looked at Elena, and opened his mouth, and Elena turned away and went to help Tamsin, and Raul looked out at the water for the rest of the evening and did not ask for anything again.

"Map," Tamsin said. It was the first word she'd said since the narrows. Her voice sounded like gravel in a tin cup.

Elena patted her jacket. Soaked through, but the oilcloth had held. "Here. Dry."

"Good." Tamsin closed her eyes. "Because I can't outrun them in this state, and they can't outrun the dark, which means tomorrow we race." She opened her eyes and looked at Elena, and there was fever starting in them, a bright wet shine that had nothing to do with courage. "Puerto Deseado. Concession office closes Friday at noon. My shoulder says we make it by an hour. Maybe less."

"You need a doctor."

"I need Friday noon. Doctor's a luxury for people whose paperwork is already filed." She tried to smile and got about halfway there. "You still remember the survey marks I showed you?"

"Every one."

"Then tomorrow you're going to learn what my first mate never learned," Tamsin said, and her hand found Elena's wrist and gripped it, hard, red-smeared, and did not let go. "Which is that the river doesn't care who you paid. It only cares whether you're telling the truth about the water."

Downstream, the dark was coming up the valley to meet them, and somewhere behind it, the company was already counting what four hundred and sixty pesos had bought them — and what it hadn't.
