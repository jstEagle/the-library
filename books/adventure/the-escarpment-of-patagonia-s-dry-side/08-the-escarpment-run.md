## 8. The Escarpment Run

The fever came on like a passenger who'd paid no fare and intended to stay the whole voyage.

Tamsin felt it first in her teeth, a fine buzzing ache, then in the shoulder wound beneath Elena's clumsy bandaging, which had gone from hot to furnace to something that pulsed in time with her heartbeat. By the time the sun went down behind the escarpment — that long, rust-colored wall of basalt that made the dry side look like the edge of somebody's abandoned world — she was shivering in ninety-degree heat and seeing double.

"Two channels," she said.

"There's one channel," Elena said. She stood at Tamsin's elbow with the wheelhouse lamp turned low, one hand hovering near the wheel as if she might have to catch it, or catch Tamsin. "There's been one channel since Chacabuco."

"That's what they want you to think." Tamsin laughed, and the laugh cost her something, so she stopped. Behind them, far upriver where the narrows bent out of sight, a searchlight swept the water. The company's launch. It had found the Cazadora's wake an hour after dark and had been patient ever since, the way a cat is patient, the way debt is patient.

Elena killed the lamp entirely. In the sudden dark the river was a rumor — the sound of it moving over gravel bars, the smell of dust and cold stone coming off the escarpment walls. Somewhere below decks the engine knocked in a rhythm Tamsin did not like at all. Raul's sabotage had done more than kill the radio; he'd left her a parting gift in the fuel line too, some spiteful little obstruction she'd spent two hours clearing by lantern light while blood soaked through her shirt and Elena held the light and asked questions Tamsin couldn't answer yet.

"How bad is the shoulder?" Elena asked now.

"It's attached. That's the important part."

"You're burning up."

"I'm running warm. Boats do it all the time." Tamsin squinted into the dark. "Get me the charts."

A silence. Then, quietly: "Tamsin. The charts went into the river at the narrows. With Raul."

Ah. Yes. There it was, waiting for her every time she reached for it: the fact of Raul. Twenty-two years he'd stood beside her at this wheel, through floods and droughts and one memorable incident involving a horse. And he'd sold them for the price of his debts, and taken the chart case off its hook when nobody was watching because he knew — he'd always known — that without charts, the dry side's worst channel would chew the Cazadora into kindling before dawn.

"He took them because he knew I could still do it," Tamsin said. "That's the insult of it. He didn't even think the river would finish us. He thought I would."

"Can you?"

Tamsin didn't answer right away. She let her hands rest on the wheel, wood worn smooth by twenty-two years of her own palms, and listened. The river talked the way it always talked on the dry side — low, gravel-voiced, unimpressed by human arrangements. Concessions, permits, companies, judges. The water had been running this channel since before any of it, and it forgave nothing. Not the gauchos stranded on their shoal. Not Raul. Not her, if she gave it a reason.

But it kept accounts honestly. That was the thing nobody understood about rivers. They were the only honest creditor left.

"Elena," she said. "How much of my rambling have you written down?"

"All of it." A pause. "You talk when you're working. You probably don't know you do it."

"I know I do it. I'm checking whether you listen." She turned the wheel a hand's width. "Sandbar off the port bow in about four hundred meters. Gravel, not rock — you'll hear it change pitch before you see it. When you hear the water go quiet, tell me."

"Quiet how?"

"Like it's holding its breath. Like a man deciding whether to lie to you." Tamsin smiled into the dark. "You'll know."

* * *

They ran the first ten kilometers on sound alone.

It was, Tamsin would think later — in the fever-haze, in the hospital cot in Puerto Deseado, in all the years after — the purest work she'd ever done. No charts. No light, because light meant the launch behind them would find them, and the launch drew less water than the Cazadora and could go places she couldn't. Just the wheel under her hands, the engine's honest knock, and the river telling her everything, the way it had for thirty years, if you spoke its language.

Gravel sang. Rock went silent. Current around a hidden bar made a sound like tearing cloth, and current through a deep channel made a sound like nothing at all, like the river relaxing. Her body knew these things the way it knew her own name, and the fever, perversely, helped — it stripped away everything except the knowing. No fear. No arithmetic about odds. Just the next hundred meters, and the next.

"Quiet," Elena said, once, sharply, and Tamsin swung the wheel starboard and heard the gravel hiss past their port side close enough to touch, and then the deep water opened under them like a door.

"How did you—" Elena began.

"Hush. Listening."

They fell into a rhythm. Elena became her ears' second voice, calling the changes in the water's sound from the bow rail while Tamsin translated sound into rudder. Twice the searchlight swept close enough to silver the escarpment wall above them, and twice Tamsin put the Cazadora into the shadow of an overhang and let the engine idle down to a whisper until the light moved on. Once, drifting in that darkness, Elena said, very softly, "My father used to make sounds like descriptions. He'd say *the river goes quiet like a held breath* and I thought he was being poetic."

"He wasn't. He was being accurate. Where do you think I learned half my vocabulary?" Tamsin kept her eyes forward. "He ran this channel twice a year for fifteen years. He and I traded routes like other men trade lies."

Elena was quiet a moment. "You knew him."

"I knew him," Tamsin said. "And I'll tell you everything, I swear it on this wheel. But not tonight. Tonight we run."

Because ahead — she could feel it now, the way you feel weather in a scar — lay the Quebrada del Diablo. The Devil's Gorge. Eleven kilometers of channel where the escarpment pinched the river down to a third of its width, where the water dropped forty feet in a series of steps the surveyors had given up trying to map, where three boats had died in her lifetime and one of them had carried friends. Every pilot on the dry side ran it in daylight, slow, with an anchor ready. Nobody ran it at night.

Nobody ran it with a hole in their shoulder and a fever and a company launch closing the distance behind them.

But the launch would reach the gorge entrance within the hour, and if the Cazadora waited for daylight, the concession expired with her evidence still aboard, and everything — the prospector's death, the sandbars, the betrayal, the blood — would have been spent for nothing.

"The gorge," Elena said. She'd read the water ahead somehow, or read Tamsin's face. "We're going through it. At night."

"We're going through it," Tamsin agreed.

"Is there a version of this conversation where I argue?"

"Sure. Have it. I'll steer."

Elena laughed — a short, cracked, real laugh, the first one Tamsin had gotten out of her since Puerto Deseado — and went forward to tie down anything loose.

* * *

The gorge announced itself as a change in the air.

Cold came up out of it, the breath of deep water over deep stone, and the sound changed from the river's gravel murmur to something lower and vaster, a continuous drumming that Tamsin felt in the wheel more than heard. Above them the escarpment walls rose black against the stars, close enough that she could have thrown a bottle at either one.

She slowed the engine to bare steerage way and stood very still, letting the fever fall away from her the way you shake off a coat. This was the part that required everything she had, and she intended to spend all of it.

"Listen," she told Elena. "There are three drops. The first one you can take straight down the middle — it looks like suicide but it's the only line that doesn't put you on the rocks. After that there's a pool, maybe two hundred meters, and the current will try to shove you left toward the undercut. Fight it. The second drop has a tongue of smooth water just right of center — that's your door. Miss the door and the gorge keeps the boat. Third drop, hold hard left, then immediately hard right, because there's a reversal wave that will spin you if you let it. After that it opens out and it's just water again."

"And if I miss something?"

"Then you'll hear it, and you'll shout, and I'll trust you faster than I've trusted anyone in twenty years." Tamsin flexed her hands on the wheel. "Ready?"

"No," Elena said honestly. "Yes. Go."

Tamsin opened the throttle.

The Cazadora went into the dark like a thrown knife. The first drop came up faster than memory promised — it always did — and for three seconds the whole world was falling water and spray and the engine screaming, and then they were down, level, alive, in the pool, and the current seized them sideways exactly as advertised. Tamsin fought it, shoulder shrieking, wheel heavy as a church door, and Elena was shouting bearings from the bow — "smooth water! Right of center! THERE!" — and Tamsin put the nose into the door and the second drop took them gentler than the first, almost kindly, as if the river were acknowledging good manners.

The third drop tried to kill them. Of course it did. The reversal wave caught the stern and slewed them halfway around, and for one endless moment the Cazadora was broadside to the current in the worst place on the dry side, and Tamsin did the only thing left: full throttle, full opposite rudder, and a word in Spanish she'd learned from Raul and would never use in church. The boat dug in, groaned, and came around — and then they were through, out, spat into open water under open stars, the gorge receding behind them with its drumming unchanged, indifferent, magnificent.

Elena made it back to the wheelhouse on legs that didn't entirely cooperate. She looked at Tamsin. Tamsin looked at her. Both of them were soaked to the skin and shaking.

"The charts are gone," Elena said at last, wonderingly. "And we're through."

"The river doesn't need charts," Tamsin said. "It needs attention. Everything else is paperwork." She grinned, fever-bright. "Speaking of which — Puerto Deseado is four hours downstream, and the land office opens at eight."

Behind them, faint now, the searchlight swept the top of the gorge and stopped. The launch could follow them through if its captain had a death wish and a better boat. From the way the light wavered, hung back, and finally began to swing upstream, Tamsin guessed he had neither.

"They're turning around," Elena breathed.

"Company men don't die well in gorges. No insurance." Tamsin eased the throttle forward, and the Cazadora ran on through the dark, wounded and whole, carrying her cargo of paper and truth toward the sea. "Four hours, Elena. Sleep if you can. Because tomorrow morning, we don't outrun anybody."

Elena looked at her, question in her face.

"Tomorrow morning," Tamsin said, "we walk in the front door."
