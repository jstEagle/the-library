## 9. Concession Day

**Tamsin**

The Cazadora came around the last bend of the Deseado at four in the morning, running on a fuel mixture that would have made her engine's manufacturer weep, trailing smoke like a widow's veil. Tamsin stood at the wheel with her left arm strapped to her chest and her right hand doing the work of two, and she had never in thirty years on the water felt so much like singing.

"Port town," Elena said beside her, pointing at the smudge of lights. "I can see the land office chimney."

"You can see a chimney from here?"

"I can see anything if it means my father didn't die for nothing."

Tamsin decided not to argue with that. She'd learned over nine days that arguing with Elena Vasquez — or whatever her true surname was; the girl had finally told her, in the dark after the narrows, and Tamsin had promised to forget it until the right moment — was like arguing with the tide. Possible, but pointless.

The harbor was nearly empty at that hour. Nearly. One launch sat at the company dock with its running lights on, and standing on its deck, hands jammed in his pockets like a man who'd rather be anywhere else, was Raul.

"There he is," Tamsin said quietly. "My first mate. My traitor. My bad knee of a human being."

"What are you going to do?"

"What I always do when something's broken and I can't afford to replace it." She swung the wheel and brought the Cazadora in toward the public pier, the far one, the one with the rusted crane and the seagulls who considered it holy ground. "I'm going to stand between him and what he wants until he loses his nerve."

"And if he doesn't lose his nerve?"

"Then I'll bore him to death. I've done it before. Ask anyone from Comodoro."

Elena almost smiled. It was the first almost-smile since the shooting, and Tamsin counted it like coin.

She brought them alongside gently, considering the shoulder wound, the missing chart case, the bullet hole through the wheelhouse glass that let the cold river air whistle in like a paid informant. Elena jumped to the pier with the survey tube strapped across her back and the affidavit in an oilskin wallet against her heart.

"The land office opens at eight," Elena said. "The permits expire at noon."

"Then you have four hours to do the most important paperwork in Patagonia." Tamsin looked at her, this girl with two names and one spine. "Walk fast. Don't run. Running people look guilty, and you are the only innocent party in this entire estuary."

"What will you do?"

Tamsin settled herself on the pier bollard, which was cold enough to make her reconsider several life choices, and nodded toward the company launch, where Raul had begun to walk along the dock toward them, and behind him, two gauchos in coats too good for honest work.

"I," said Tamsin Delacroix, "am going to have a conversation."

* * *

**Elena**

The town of Puerto Deseado at half past five in the morning smelled of fish, diesel, and bread beginning somewhere out of sight, and Elena walked through it feeling every one of her father's years settle onto her shoulders like a coat that finally fit.

The land office was a squat stone building with a brass plaque and a door that wouldn't open for two more hours. So she did what her father had taught her: she found the back way. Every office has a back way, he used to say, because every clerk is a person, and every person drinks coffee somewhere.

The coffee place was a kitchen behind the office where an old woman named Doña Mercedes was feeding wood into a stove. Elena introduced herself as a surveyor with an urgent filing, and Doña Mercedes looked at her for a long moment — at the mud on her boots, the salt in her hair, the tube on her back — and said, "You're Tomás's girl."

Elena's breath went out of her. "You knew him?"

"He drew charts for me once. Of the spring road up to my sister's estancia, when the company men said there wasn't water there and I knew there was." The old woman poured a second cup without being asked. "He talked about a daughter who could read a river better than he could. Sit down. You look like your father when he was about to do something stupid and correct."

Elena sat. She drank. And when the clerk arrived at seven-forty, red-eyed and buttoning his vest wrong, Doña Mercedes met him at the door and said, "There's a young woman inside with a filing that can't wait until nine, Ignacio, and if you make her wait I'll tell your wife about the poker debts."

The clerk looked at Elena. Elena held up the oilskin wallet.

Ignacio sighed the sigh of a man who understood exactly which side of history his breakfast depended on, and unlocked the door early.

* * *

**Tamsin**

Raul stopped six feet away, which showed some sense. The gauchos stopped behind him, which showed they were following orders, and men following orders always stop where the first man stops.

"Tamsin." He said her name like an apology he hadn't finished writing. "The company just wants the papers. Nobody has to get hurt."

"Nobody has gotten hurt," Tamsin agreed pleasantly, "except me, shot through the shoulder by your new friends at the Chacabuco narrows, and my boat, holed twice, and my faith in humanity, which was already in rough shape."

"That wasn't supposed to happen. They said it would be a quiet pickup."

"They said that about the last three things they said." She shifted on the bollard, letting her coat fall open just enough to show the revolver at her belt — unloaded, as it happened, because she'd given her last two rounds to Elena with instructions involving the word 'only,' but Raul didn't need to know the arithmetic. "Here's how today goes, old friend. In about ninety minutes, a survey gets filed in that stone building behind you. A real one, stamped and sworn. When that happens, Andes Sur's concession dies on the vine, the estancia families get their water back, and everything you sold me out for becomes worth exactly nothing. Less than nothing. Worth a warrant."

Raul's jaw worked. "You don't know that."

"I know clerks. Clerks are the most dangerous animal in Patagonia. A puma only wants to eat you; a clerk wants it *on record*."

One of the gauchos muttered something. Raul raised a hand, and to his credit — the small, ruined credit he had left — the hand came down again.

"Tamsin, they'll ruin you too. Your boat, your license—"

"My boat is named Cazadora, Raul. Huntress. She's been run aground, shot at, seized twice by customs, and struck by a floating dead cow in 1974. Whatever they try, she'll outlive the company, the judge, and very possibly me." She stood up off the bollard then, slowly, and the movement cost her, and she let them see it cost her, because wounded women make men careless. "But here's the thing I actually came to say."

She stepped closer. Six feet became four.

"You were my first mate for eleven years. You taught my nephew to tie a bowline. You cried at my sister's funeral and pretended you had smoke in your eyes. I am not going to forgive you, because forgiveness is for priests and I've never trusted their bookkeeping either. But I am going to tell you that whatever debt you owed, whatever number they wrote on paper to buy you — it was never worth the man you were on my deck. And when this is over, you're going to have to live on that river knowing everyone knows it. That's your sentence. Not prison. *Memory.*"

Raul looked at her for a long time. His eyes were wet, and this time there was no smoke to blame.

"They're still going to come," he said finally, quietly. "The tall one doesn't take orders from me anymore. He takes them from Puerto Madryn."

"Then he'd best hurry," Tamsin said, and checked her watch with theatrical slowness. "It's ten past eight."

* * *

**Elena**

The clerk's pen scratched. Elena watched each stroke as though she could will the ink deeper into the page.

Survey of an unregistered spring, upper Deseado basin, coordinates verified. Feeding three estancias whose water rights had been extinguished by a concession granted on falsified flow data — data purchased, per the attached affidavit, by Andes Sur Mining S.A., sworn before witnesses, signed Tomás Vasquez, cartographer, in the month before his death.

"He signed it the week he died," Elena said. Her voice held. She was surprised by that.

"Clerks respect a dated signature," Ignacio murmured, stamping. "It shows intent. Intent is everything in this office. We are, frankly, drowning in intent."

The stamp came down. Once. Twice. A third time for the registry copy. Each thud went through Elena like a heartbeat she'd been waiting twenty-nine days to hear.

At eleven-twelve in the morning, four hours and forty-eight minutes before the concession expired, the filing entered the register of the Puerto Deseado land office, and the water of the dry side belonged — on paper, in ink, in the only language companies truly feared — to the families who had always drunk it.

Doña Mercedes appeared in the doorway with three cups of coffee and no explanation of how she'd known.

"To Tomás," the old woman said.

"To Tomás," Elena echoed, and drank, and outside the window she saw Tamsin Delacroix walking up the street from the docks with her arm in a sling and her chin up, flanked by absolutely no one, which meant she'd won.

* * *

**Tamsin**

They met halfway, in the plaza, under a poplar that had no business growing there and had clearly decided not to care.

"Well?" Tamsin asked.

"Filed. Stamped. Registered." Elena's voice cracked on the last word, and she laughed at herself for it. "It's done. It's actually done."

"Of course it's done. I stood on a freezing bollard for three hours threatening a man with an empty gun. Something had to justify that." Tamsin looked past her, down toward the docks, where the company launch was casting off in unseemly haste, and where Raul stood alone on the pier watching them go, not waving, not leaving either. Just standing. Serving his sentence. "Your father would be proud of you. And insufferable about it. He'd have had the survey framed and shown it to strangers at bus stops."

Elena wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand and laughed — really laughed, for the first time since the dying man had boarded at the lonely dock with blood on his shirt and a map in his fist.

"So," Tamsin said, and felt the question rise in her like water finding its level. "The money. There'll be damages, settlements, the families will want to thank you. What will you do with all of it?"

Elena looked at the river. Then at the Cazadora, scarred and smoking and magnificent at her mooring.

"Teach me the sandbars," she said. "All of them. Even the lying ones."

Tamsin Delacroix, last captain of the dry-side escarpment, discovered that her throat had gone tight, and blamed the cold air, and knew she was lying.

"First lesson starts tomorrow at dawn," she said gruffly. "And the first rule is: the river forgives nothing."

"Good," said Elena Vasquez, daughter of Tomás, pilot-to-be of the Cazadora. "Neither do I."

Down at the pier, the gulls wheeled and complained, the tide turned, and the water began, patiently, to remember everything.
