## 10. Water Remembering

The news came to the *Cazadora* the way news always came to Puerto Deseado — by a boy on a bicycle who shouted it at the dock before pedaling off to shout it everywhere else.

"The concession's dead! Andes Sur pulled out! The land office says the spring survey voided everything!"

Tamsin was sitting on her stern hatch with her arm in a sling and a cup of coffee going cold in her good hand, and she heard herself laugh — an actual laugh, rusty as an unused winch, startling a gull off the pilings. Across the dock, Elena stood very still, holding the telegram from the land office like it might dissolve.

"They did it," Elena said, not quite believing it. "The estancia families — the Marchettis, the Fuentes — they get the water rights. All of them."

"Your father's paperwork," Tamsin said. "His affidavit, his survey, filed by his daughter under her own name." She let that sit for a moment. "Both of your names."

Elena looked down at the paper. Vasquez written there in her own careful cartographer's hand, no hiding in it, no flinch. "It felt strange to write it," she admitted. "Like signing someone else's work. But then it didn't."

"It takes practice," Tamsin said. "I've been signing Delacroix for forty years and I'm still not sure I've grown into it."

* * *

The collapse took three days to finish falling, and it fell loudly. The company men left town on the same launch that had chased them down the escarpment, their faces arranged into expressions of men who had merely decided to pursue other opportunities. Raul did not leave with them. He sat in the harbormaster's office for two hours giving statements to a magistrate with a very patient notebook, and when he came out he walked past the *Cazadora* without looking at her, which Tamsin understood to be the closest thing to an apology he had in him.

She'd gone down to see him through the office window, sling and all, mostly to confirm what she already knew: that he looked smaller than the man who'd sabotaged her radio. Debt does that. It shrinks people until they can fit through whatever crack the money comes out of.

"You could have hated him more efficiently," Elena said beside her.

"I could have shot him," Tamsin agreed. "But bullets are expensive and paperwork is free, and look how well the paperwork worked this week."

Elena smiled — one of the real ones now, not the guarded harbor-light version Tamsin had first met in the port café. It changed her whole face. It made her look, Tamsin thought, exactly like a man bleeding out on a dock at midnight, laughing at some private joke about a river.

* * *

On the fourth day, the money arrived.

Not the company's money — that was frozen somewhere in Buenos Aires behind lawyers — but the other kind. A clerk from the land office came down to the boat with a folder and an apologetic cough and explained that the water rights settlement included compensation for the original claimant's heirs, and that as the person who had filed the affidavit, Señorita Vasquez was entitled to disbursements that made Tamsin's eyebrows climb. Enough to buy a small estancia. Enough to buy a large one, if you weren't fussy about the view.

Elena read the figure twice. Then she handed the folder back.

"I don't want it," she said.

The clerk blinked. People didn't say that in Puerto Deseado. "Señorita, it's a considerable—"

"I know what it is." She glanced at Tamsin, and something passed between them — the question Elena had clearly been turning over for days, finally surfacing. "Can it be directed elsewhere? To the families? The Marchetti place lost half its herd last summer waiting on water that was legally theirs."

"That can be arranged," the clerk said slowly, writing it down as though the sentence might bite him.

"And the rest—" Elena stopped. Started again. Tamsin watched her decide, watched the girl with two names choose, right there on the dock, which one she was going to be. "The rest I want to spend on something. But I need to ask the captain first."

Tamsin raised an eyebrow over her coffee. "Ask."

"Teach me the sandbars."

* * *

They talked about it that evening in the wheelhouse, the lamp turned low because fuel was still fuel even after miracles. Tamsin sat with her bad shoulder propped against the window frame and listened while Elena laid it out — not pleading, just laying it out, the way you'd chart a channel.

"I've spent six years being someone else in rooms where it was safer to be no one. Maps of places I'll never see, drawn for people who'll never thank me. My father died carrying proof that water remembers where it belongs, and everyone who touched that proof got hurt except the water." She turned her cup in her hands. "I don't want to draw rivers anymore, Tamsin. I want to be on one."

"The pay is terrible," Tamsin said.

"So I've heard."

"The hours are worse. The company is nonexistent — I am the company, and I'm a poor one. The retirement plan is drowning, eventually, somewhere beautiful."

"My father drowned somewhere beautiful," Elena said quietly. "I'd rather it ran in the family than ended with him."

Tamsin looked at her for a long moment. Outside, the harbor lights wobbled on the black water, and somewhere upriver the escarpment stood in the dark doing what it always did — forgiving nothing, promising nothing, waiting to see who came.

"There's a sandbar below the second bend of the Chacabuco," Tamsin said at last, "that isn't where any chart says it is. It moves every flood season. My father found it the hard way; I found it the hard way; my father's father found it the hard way, which suggests we are a slow-learning family." She took a sip of cold coffee and grimaced. "First lesson starts tomorrow. You'll take soundings until your arms fall off, and you'll log every reading, and in ten years — if the river likes you — I'll tell you where the bar actually sleeps."

"And if the river doesn't like me?"

"Then you'll have learned a great deal about humility, which is also inheritance." She stuck out her good hand. "Welcome aboard, pilot. God help us both."

Elena shook it. Her grip was stronger than it looked. Most things about her were.

* * *

They cast off on a morning so clear it felt like the sky had been washed for the occasion.

The *Cazadora* sat lighter without Raul, and Tamsin felt the difference in the way she answered the wheel — eager, almost, like a dog whose least favorite guest had finally gone home. Elena stood at the bow with the lead line, having insisted on taking soundings before they were even out of the harbor, and Tamsin let her, because a pilot who wanted to learn started learning immediately or never.

The estancia families came down to see them off. Old Marchetti, who had once refused to sell Tamsin firewood at anything less than robbery prices, pressed a sack of empanadas into Elena's hands and called her *la hija del río* — the river's daughter — and Elena went pink to her ears and pretended to check a mooring line. The Fuentes' youngest boy asked if they were pirates now, and Tamsin told him gravely that they were worse: they were properly documented.

Then the lines came in, and the engine took the strain, and Puerto Deseado began sliding away astern with its land office and its lawyers and its bicycle boys shouting the news, and the river opened ahead of them, brown and patient and full of everything it remembered.

Tamsin held the wheel and found, to her mild surprise, that she was talking.

"He wasn't a prospector, you know. Your father. Not really."

Elena came aft, coiling the line. "No?"

"He was a hydrologist. University-trained, though he hid the accent most of the time. He came up the dry side twenty years ago measuring flows nobody paid him to measure, because he said somebody ought to know where the water actually went before the companies drew lines on maps pretending it went wherever they wanted." She steered around a drifting branch, easy, automatic, her hands remembering so she didn't have to think. "He found the spring the year I wrecked my first boat. Pulled me out of the shallows myself, soaked to the ribs, and fed me lamb at his camp and talked all night about aquifers like other men talk about horses. Best conversation I ever had with a stranger."

Elena had gone very still. "You knew him."

"I knew him for one night, twenty years ago. He signed my repair bill 'Emiliano Vasquez, friend of the river,' and I thought it was a joke." Tamsin's throat tightened, and she let it, because some things deserved the room. "When he boarded at that dock, bleeding, I didn't recognize him. Twenty years is unkind to faces. But I recognized the voice. And when he gave me the map and made me swear, I swore partly because a dying man asked — and partly because I owed a night's hospitality to the only man I ever met who loved this desert for its water instead of its emptiness."

The wheel hummed under her hand. The first bend came up, green-willowed, unhurried.

"He said something that night," Tamsin went on. "I've carried it around like a stone in my boot ever since. He said: *the water always finds the lowest honest ground.* I thought it was geology talk. Now I think it might have been instructions."

Elena laughed — wet-eyed, but laughing. "For people?"

"For boats. For families. For judges and clerks and stubborn old captains." She nodded at the lead line. "Now soundings, pilot. Second bend coming up, and somewhere under that brown water is a sandbar that has fooled three generations of Delacroixes, and today, if the river's feeling generous, it starts fooling a Vasquez instead."

Elena swung the lead, sang out the depth, and the *Cazadora* leaned into the current, running light and true toward the escarpment — carrying nothing worth killing for, and everything worth living on.
