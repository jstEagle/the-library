## 1. The Map of Closed Lines

There are two kinds of people who keep notebooks about railroads. The first kind works for the railroad. The second kind used to.

I am the second kind, and I have been the second kind for eleven years, which is long enough that I've stopped calling it a wound and started calling it a hobby. Hobbies don't ask questions. Hobbies don't subpoena you.

The notebook itself is nothing to look at — a water-swollen thing with a cracked spine and a rubber band holding the whole enterprise together, bought in a stationery shop in a city whose name I've since given back. Inside, though, is my life's work: every defunct rail spur between the monsoon belt and the coral coast, catalogued by gauge, gradient, date of abandonment, and probable reason. Landslide. Flood. Bankruptcy. War. Once, memorably, *crocodiles*, though I suspect that entry was more folklore than engineering. The reasons are the interesting part. Rails don't die of old age. They die of decisions.

I was halfway through re-inking the entry for the Sembilan spur — abandoned 1974, washed-out culvert, never rebuilt because the tin ran out before the money did — when the *Meridian Star* rolled over a swell the size of a municipal building and reminded me who was in charge.

"Zhou!" Captain Ferreira's voice came down the companionway ahead of him, the way his voice always did, arriving like weather. He appeared at the top of the ladder gripping the rail with one hand and a mug of coffee with the other, managing both with the confidence of a man who had never once considered losing either. "You're still alive down here. Good. I owe you money."

"You owe me for the pump bearings," I said. "The money part was settled."

"Then I owe you gratitude, which costs less." He wedged himself onto the bench across from me, which in the *Star*'s aft cabin meant his knees were now involved in our conversation whether he liked it or not. He nodded at the notebook. "Still writing your book?"

"It's not a book."

"What is it, then?"

"A list."

He looked at me the way he always looked at me when I gave an answer that was technically true and spiritually useless — with the patient amusement of a man who'd spent thirty years on coastal freighters and had learned that everyone aboard is running from something, and the polite thing is to never check what's behind them. "A list of dead train tracks."

"Closed lines," I said. "They prefer that term."

"Do they."

"They would, if they could prefer anything." I turned the page so he could see the neat columns, the little annotations in three colors of ink. Red for structural failure. Blue for economic. Black for the ones where nobody wrote down why, which are the ones that keep me up at night. "Every line here carried something once. Tin, timber, tea, troops. Then one day it didn't, and somebody made a call, and the jungle got its territory back. That's all a closed line is. A decision the ground eventually agrees with."

Ferreira drank his coffee and studied me over the rim. He had a face like a dock cleat — all function, no ornament, salted white at the edges. "And you catalog them because..."

"Because someone should."

"That's not a reason, Zhou. That's a shrug with a pen."

I smiled the smile I keep for exactly this situation. It's a good smile. It has ended conversations on four continents. "Because it passes the time."

He let it go, the way he always did, and I noted — as I always did — the small mercy of it, and hated how much I needed small mercies, and hated noting that too. This is the tax on my particular way of living. Everything gets recorded twice: once in the notebook, once in the ledger of things I can't say out loud.

* * *

Here is the rule, so we're clear on it, because the rule matters later:

Never stay longer than a job.

Not longer than the contract. Not longer than the repairs. Not longer than it takes to fix what's broken, collect what's owed, and be standing on a different deck when anyone starts asking the kind of questions that begin with *so tell me about* and end with a name I buried under three aliases and a burned reference letter. Eleven years ago there was a bridge, and an inquiry, and a finding that fit the politics better than the physics, and I learned that the difference between an engineer and a scapegoat is mostly paperwork and timing. So now I do the paperwork fast and watch the timing closer than a lighthouse keeper watches weather.

It sounds lonely when I say it like that. In practice it's just efficient. Loneliness implies you're missing something, and I've done a thorough inventory, and what I'm missing is a liability.

Mostly I believe this. Some nights I believe it at about sixty percent.

The *Meridian Star* was carrying diesel drums, rice, roofing tin, and me — the last item listed nowhere on the manifest, which suited us both. I'd signed on in port as a deckhand with an engineer's hands, which is a category of person every working captain wants and no insurance company approves of. My actual job description, as negotiated over bad coffee in a harbor office, was *whatever breaks*. On a forty-year-old coastal freighter, that is a full-time position with overtime.

What broke that morning, three days out, was the bilge pump on the port side, and I spent the afternoon elbow-deep in the engine room's humid dark, talking to the machinery the way I talk to all machinery, which is to say with respect and mild threats. Impeller worn to lace. Coupling shimmed by some previous optimist with a beer can. I rebuilt it with parts from the spares locker and a gasket I cut myself from an old flip-flop, and when it came back to life it made the sound pumps make when they're happy, which is very close to silence.

Sef — no, not Sef, that comes later; this was Tomas, the *Star*'s one-man engine department, a nineteen-year-old with a burn-scarred forearm and the permanent expression of someone being personally victimized by thermodynamics — watched me work from the doorway.

"How'd you learn that?" he asked.

"Learn what?"

"*That.* You didn't even look at the coupling. You just knew it was wrong."

"I looked at it for eleven years' worth of other couplings," I said. "After a while they all start confessing."

"Where'd you study?"

Ah. There it was. The question with teeth in it, wearing a friendly collar.

"Here and there," I said, and handed him the impeller. "Feel that wear pattern? That's cavitation. Means the suction side's starved. Check your strainer before you blame the pump. Pumps get blamed for everything. They're the engines room's convenient widow."

He turned the impeller over, frowning at it like a puzzle, and forgot the question. This is the trick, if you want one: give people something interesting enough to hold, and their curiosity about you will drop it. Machinery is excellent for this. Machinery is honest, which makes it better company than most people, including me.

* * *

That evening the sea went strange.

Not storm-strange — not yet. Just wrong in the way sailors notice and landlubbers don't. The swell changed its rhythm, coming from somewhere the wind wasn't. The light went the color of old pewter. The birds, which had been trailing us all afternoon in a ragged white escort, peeled off toward the southwest all at once, like a crowd leaving a theater before the third act.

Tomas noticed the birds. Ferreira noticed the swell. I noticed both, plus the barometer in the wheelhouse, which had dropped so fast it looked like it was trying to escape the chart table.

"Reef weather," Ferreira said quietly, to no one, to the glass, to whatever gods mind coastal shipping. He altered course two points north, then two more, chewing the inside of his cheek. The chart showed open water for forty miles in every direction except below us, where the Coral Triangle began its real work: a drowned architecture of reefs and atolls and tidal channels that had been eating ships since before ships had names.

I stood at the rail while the light died and felt the change come on like a fever. There's a smell to a building storm — ozone and iodine and something older underneath, something the sea keeps in its basement. I'd smelled it before, on the night the river took the trestle at Kandar, though that memory lives in a locked drawer and I only opened it a crack, just to confirm the label, and shut it again.

"You should sleep," Ferreira said beside me. I hadn't heard him come up. For a big man he moved like ballast shifting.

"So should you."

"I'll sleep when the sea tells me I can." He looked south, where the horizon had gone the color of a bruise deciding. Then, casually, the way men ask dangerous questions when they think the danger is social rather than meteorological: "You ever been through this stretch before?"

"No."

"Where were you before this, then? Before the *Star*?"

"Up the coast."

"Doing what?"

"Rail work. A spur rehabilitation. It's in the notebook."

"The famous list." He smiled. "You know what I can't figure out about you, Zhou? You talk about those dead lines like you're writing their obituaries. But you read them like you're looking for somebody."

The wind chose that moment to arrive properly, shoving between us, and I gripped the rail and looked at the water instead of at him, because the water doesn't require an answer.

"I'm looking for why they closed," I said. Which was true. And also a door I could hold shut with one hand.

"And why's that?"

"Because a closed line means somebody decided the cost of keeping it open was too high." The first rain hit the deck then, fat and warm as spit. "And I've never met a decision like that that was as simple as the paperwork said."

Ferreira looked at me for a long moment. Rain ran off the brim of his cap. Whatever he saw — and he'd had six days to look, six days of me fixing things and saying little — he apparently decided it wasn't cargo he wanted to handle in weather like this.

"Get below," he said. "Lash everything. Tomorrow's going to be a conversation with God."

* * *

The storm found us at midnight and introduced itself without pleasantries.

I won't describe it in detail. Storms at sea are like funerals: everyone who was there remembers them differently, and the details matter less than the fact of having survived. What I remember is the sound — a sound like the whole world being unzipped — and the *Star* climbing swells that turned from hills into buildings into geography, and Ferreira at the wheel singing some fado in Portuguese, badly, defiantly, because that was how he prayed. I remember Tomas throwing up into a bucket with tremendous commitment. I remember doing arithmetic in my head against my will — wave height versus freeboard, wind load versus hull stress — because that's what my brain does instead of panicking. It calculates. It has always calculated. Sometimes I think it calculates specifically so I won't have to feel.

At some hour that might have been dawn or might have been the storm simply eating the morning, there was a sound beneath everything else — a long, grinding, geological shudder through the keel, the sound of a ship meeting a reef and losing the negotiation.

Then the world tilted, and the engines died mid-word, and Ferreira stopped singing.

We grounded hard, twice, the second time with a finality that rattled my teeth, and then the *Meridian Star* lay over at an angle no naval architect intended, wedged on a coral shelf with the sea still trying to take her apart piece by piece. Emergency lights. Shouting. The specific chaos of seven people discovering simultaneously that they are now, temporarily, islanders.

By true dawn the storm had moved on inland to ruin someone else's day, and the sky cleared to a blue so clean it felt like an apology, and the sea around us had begun — impossibly, obscenely — to calm.

I stood on the canted deck, soaked to the bone, hair full of salt, and looked at where we'd landed.

It was a reef island — a comma of sand and shattered coral maybe half a kilometer long, green at the center with scrub, ringed by flats the retreating tide was already uncovering. The wreck had missed the island proper by a ship's length, which on the scale of maritime luck was a rounding error away from catastrophe. Ferreira was already on the radio, burning through battery to raise anyone, and Tomas was counting the diesel drums like a man counting his own pulse, and I —

I was looking at the tidal flats.

Because the storm surge had drained them further than any ordinary ebb, way past the normal waterline, exposing a shelf of limestone and coral rubble that no chart would bother naming. And at the far end of that shelf, where the scrub gave way to black volcanic rock, there was a dark opening in the cliff face. A cave mouth. Sitting there in the drained flat like a held breath.

Local charts mark such things as hazards or ignore them entirely. Local legends, I would learn later, mark them differently.

I told myself I was going to stretch my legs. I told myself I was going to inspect the hull damage from outside, which was even technically true. I took the notebook, because I take the notebook everywhere, the way other people take cigarettes or grudges.

The flats were treacherous going — loose coral, sudden pools, starfish lying stranded like dropped toys. The air smelled of exposed reef, that raw mineral breath the ocean gives off when it shows you what's usually hidden. Halfway across, I passed a drowned anchor, encrusted to uselessness, and then a scatter of timbers that might once have been a boat, and I understood that this shelf was a graveyard that only opened for visitors twice a year, and that I was trespassing on an appointment.

The cave mouth grew as I approached until it was tall enough to walk into upright, its lip hung with dried weed and barnacles, the rock around it stained in bands — high-water marks written in mineral, a tide gauge kept by the stone itself for centuries.

Inside, the light lasted ten meters and then gave out. I clicked on my headlamp.

The beam swept across walls carved with figures — spirals, fish, human shapes with arms raised — old beyond guessing, the kind of carving that isn't decoration but argument. And scattered across the floor of the chamber, half-buried in coral sand, glinted metal.

Rusted instrument cases. A tripod, corroded to lace. A theodolite box with its brass fittings gone green. Survey equipment — professional survey equipment, decades old, left where it fell or left deliberately, I couldn't yet tell which.

And beside it, wedged in a crack in the rock as if placed there by a hand that wanted it found by someone patient, a brass document tube, its cap fused with corrosion, stamped with a survey company's mark.

I knelt there in the dripping dark with my heart doing something unprofessional, and scraped the corrosion away from the stamp with my thumbnail, and read the name.

I knew the name. Of course I knew the name. It was in my notebook — in the black ink, the entries with no reason recorded, the ones that keep me up at night. A firm that had surveyed these islands fifty years ago and then folded overnight, its files sealed, its crews scattered, its final project erased from every index I could find.

Eleven years of cataloguing other people's closed lines, and here was one that had never been on any map at all.

Behind me, far off across the flats, Ferreira's voice was shouting my name against the wind. The tide, I would realize much too late, had already turned.

The sea was coming to collect its admission.

* * *

But that's getting ahead of things. Let me say what matters about that first morning, kneeling in the dark with a dead man's brass tube in my hands, while the water crept back across the flats toward me like a debt collector.

I could have left it. That's the truth I'll have to live with later, so I may as well say it plainly now. I could have walked out, waded back, helped Ferreira rig pumps and wait for rescue, and been on another deck within the week, and none of what followed would have followed. The rule would have held. Never stay longer than a job. The tube was not my job. The cave was not my job. The village that sold its river, the source marked forbidden, the dam with its secret arithmetic of failure — none of it was my job.

But I opened the tube anyway.

Because here is the other thing about people who catalogue closed lines, the thing I leave out of the ledger: we're not really collecting reasons. We're collecting evidence. Evidence that somewhere, sometime, somebody looked at a dying thing — a railway, a river, a reputation — and wrote down honestly why it died. And if such a document exists, then maybe the world is the kind of place where the honest version survives the official one.

I have been waiting eleven years for proof of that.

The cap gave with a crack of breaking corrosion, and the smell of old paper rose out of the brass like a breath held for fifty years, and behind me, soft and patient and absolutely indifferent to my plans, the tide began to fill the cave.
