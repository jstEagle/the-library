## 2. What the Tide Gave Back

The sea decided my future at three in the morning, which is about the hour I'd expect a body of water to make any major decision — late, dramatic, and without consulting anyone who'd have to live with it.

I was awake anyway. I'm always awake in storms. There's a particular sound a ship makes when it's thinking about dying — a groan in the plates that's less like metal and more like an animal doing arithmetic it doesn't like — and I'd been listening to it for two hours through the bulkhead of my cabin on the *Meridian Star*, a coastal freighter with the structural confidence of a man who has never read his own inspection reports.

When the reef took us, it wasn't cinematic. No lurch, no scream of tearing steel. Just a shudder, a long grinding hesitation, and then stillness — the specific, insulting stillness of a ship that has stopped being a ship and started being a very expensive piece of driftwood. The engines cut out. Somewhere above me, cargo straps snapped loose and began their own percussion section.

I got dressed, collected my notebook from under the mattress where I keep it because I don't trust safes or people, and went up to find out how much of my life had just changed without my permission.

The answer, by dawn, was: all of it, probably.

* * *

Captain Reyes stood on his bridge wing looking at the horizon the way men look at wives who've left them for someone younger. The storm had moved off eastward, taking its tantrum with it, leaving behind a sky scrubbed pale and a sea gone strangely gentle, as if embarrassed by its behavior overnight.

"Reef island," he said, when he saw me. "Chart calls it nothing. Fishermen call it Pulau Karang, maybe. We're hard aground on the seaward shelf. Propeller's likely done. I'll need to signal for salvage."

"How long?"

He gave me the look captains give passengers who ask questions they already know are bad news. "Week. Maybe two, if the tow company is feeling righteous."

Two weeks. Two weeks of standing still on a rock in the middle of nowhere, being a person with a name and a face and a paper trail, while other people had time to look at me. My rule was simple and I'd kept it for six years: never stay longer than a job. A job has a shape — you arrive, you fix the thing, you leave before anyone starts asking what you do when you're not fixing things. Stranded was not a job. Stranded was the opposite of a job. Stranded was a waiting room.

"I can help with the assessment," I said, because idle hands ask questions too, and mine needed something to hold.

Reyes shrugged the shrug of a man whose insurance premiums were already writing themselves. "Suit yourself. Mind the tide. It drains way out here — goes strange around this island. Locals say the flats open twice a year." He said it the way sailors say local things: with a small contempt for superstition and a smaller, hidden respect for it.

I went down to the exposed deck and looked at the island properly for the first time.

It wasn't much. A green hump of volcanic rock maybe half a kilometer across, fringed with black basalt and a skirt of reef that the storm had just introduced us to at speed. But the tide, going out, was doing something I hadn't seen before. It wasn't retreating so much as *withdrawing* — pulling back from the island in a wide, smooth sheet, like a curtain being drawn off a stage, exposing flats of wet coral and sand that gleamed in the early light with the smugness of a secret finally told.

And there, on the northern shoulder of the island, where the basalt cliffs met the drained flats, was a hole in the world.

* * *

I want to be honest about what happened next, because honesty is a discipline I practice in private and lie about in public.

I should have stayed on the ship. That's the whole doctrine of my life, distilled: stay aboard, stay small, stay moving. Curiosity is a debt collector; it always finds you eventually and it always charges interest. I have watched curiosity ruin better engineers than me — men who pulled one more thread, asked one more question about a load calculation that didn't add up, and ended up either dead or testifying, and between those two options I know which one I'd pick.

But the cave mouth sat in the cliff face at the exact height of the drained tide, a black arch maybe four meters tall, and around its rim the rock was carved.

Not eroded. Carved. Deliberately, with tools, by hands that had wanted whoever came after to notice.

I told myself I was going down to survey the reef damage for Reyes. I even brought the surveyor's level from the ship's stores, to give the lie some structural integrity. Then I walked out onto the flats, and the lie stopped bothering to hold itself up.

The flats were a drowned city of coral. Branching colonies stood like bare trees; brain corals domed up out of the sand like the roofs of buried houses. Fish lay stranded in pools, furious and glittering. The light came low and gold across all of it, and every pool held its own small sky. I walked carefully — reef cuts infect fast in this climate — but I walked toward the arch, because apparently six years of discipline could be undone by a hole in a cliff with good masonry.

Up close, the carvings resolved into figures. Processions of them, worn shallow by salt but unmistakable: lines of people walking toward the arch, and above the arch itself, spirals — rivers coiling into circles, circles into knots. At the base, almost erased, a band of symbols I didn't recognize, except that they reminded me of nothing so much as the survey marks railroads used a century ago. Benchmarks. Elevations. Someone had measured this place, long before the consortium's brass-and-steel era, and recorded it in stone.

The air coming out of the cave was cool and smelled of stone and old salt — the breath of something patient.

"Twice a year," I said aloud, to no one, because saying it aloud made it a fact instead of a temptation. "The sea opens it twice a year."

Which meant the sea would close it again. Which meant whatever was inside had been sealed like a letter, addressed to whoever happened to be standing here on one of the two mornings a year when the water stepped aside.

I am not a superstitious woman. I am, however, an engineer, and engineers believe in loads, tolerances, and timing. The timing here was obscene.

I went in.

* * *

The tunnel ran back perhaps thirty meters, floor sloping gently down, walls slick with a film of moisture that caught my headlamp in dull crescents. My boots splashed through shallows that deepened to mid-calf, then ankle, then firmed onto old coral pavement. And then the passage opened into a chamber, and I stopped walking, and for a long moment I forgot to be careful, which for me is roughly equivalent to forgetting my own name.

The chamber was high enough that my lamp couldn't find its ceiling. The far wall rose in shelves of flowstone, and the carvings continued there — denser now, more urgent. Rows of figures again, but these were different: the same processions, but bent, kneeling, reaching toward a spiral at the center of the composition. Around the spiral, carved deeper and fresher-feeling than anything else, ran a repeated phrase in trade script, the old colonial lingua franca that predates the consortium's tidy corporate dialect:

**WE SOLD THE RIVER TO KEEP THE VILLAGE.**
**WE KEPT THE VILLAGE AND LOST THE RIVER.**
**THE RIVER REMEMBERS WHAT WE FORGOT.**

Three lines. Chiseled over and over, spiraling inward, until the final repetition was cut so deep and so fine it might have been done yesterday.

I stood there with cold water in my boots and read it four times. It read like a contract. It read like a confession. It read like the kind of thing you carve when there's no court that will hear you, and I know exactly what that kind of thing looks like, because I've spent six years drafting my own version of it in a notebook nobody will ever see.

That's when I saw the equipment.

It was tucked against the left wall, half-hidden behind a curtain of flowstone that had grown over part of it — which meant it had been there a long time, decades at least, long enough for stone to start claiming it. A transit level, its brass housing furred orange with rust. A canvas pack, rotted to lace. Three bamboo survey rods, collapsed and mildewed. And a wooden case, stenciled with faded letters, holding glass photographic plates packed in straw that had gone to dust.

A survey team. Somebody had surveyed this cave, and the island, and — if the carvings were any indication — whatever story the island was trying to tell. And they had never walked back out with their findings. Or they had, and this was the duplicate, the copy cached against exactly the possibility that they wouldn't.

Beneath the transit, wedged into a crack in the rock where someone had clearly wanted it found by hand rather than by flood, was a brass tube. The kind survey charts ship in — threaded cap, wax seal, built to survive everything except human curiosity.

The wax crumbled at my thumb. Inside, rolled tight, were five sheets of drafting linen, and I unrolled them with the care I'd give a live charge, because that's what they were, in a sense. Charts. Hand-drawn, beautifully drafted, in a style I recognized the way you recognize a voice through a wall.

Coastal hydrography of the surrounding islands. A plan view of the cave system itself, annotated in a cramped, confident hand. And then — my breath actually caught, which annoyed me — a route map running inland. A river, drawn in blue ink that had faded to the color of old bruises, traced from its delta up through gorges and terraces to a point in the highlands marked with a small, precise triangle and two words in block capitals:

**SOURCE — FORBIDDEN.**

Around the triangle, the draftsman had written notes in the margins. Water levels. Dates. Flow estimates. And one line, boxed for emphasis, that I read three times because the first two readings felt like wishful thinking:

*"Consortium dam at source is unsound. They know. Report filed 19 years ago. No response. Resurveying under pretext of coastal work. If anything happens to us, the truth is at the source."*

If anything happens to us.

Something had happened to them. Their equipment was rusting in a cave that opens twice a year, and their charts had waited in the dark for a stranger with a lamp.

I sat down on the coral pavement, right there in the cold water, because my legs had opinions about standing. And I did the thing I always do when the world hands me a document: I looked at the signature block.

Every engineer signs. It's vanity, it's liability, it's the whole religion of the profession — your name against your numbers. The signature at the bottom of the route chart was small and slanted and sure of itself:

**E. Vasquez, P.E., Chief Surveyor — Escarpment Survey Expedition.**

Escarpment. The word went through me like current through a ground wire.

Because I knew that name. Not the man — I'd never met him, or if I had, I'd been a child carrying his instrument cases. But I knew the name the way you know a family ghost. Elias Vasquez had trained under my mentor, Tomas Reyes-Ibarra — no relation to the captain, the universe isn't that neat — back in the years before the bridge, before the inquiry, before my name became a thing I kept in a notebook instead of on a door. Tomas had a photograph of his old survey crews, and Vasquez was in two of them, squinting into the sun with a transit on his shoulder, and Tomas used to say: *"Vasquez could read country like a sentence. Best field eye I ever taught. He went east and disappeared into the islands."*

Disappeared into the islands. And surfaced, nineteen years later, in a tube of brass, addressed — whether he meant it or not — to the one student of Tomas Reyes-Ibarra who would wash up on this particular reef, on this particular morning, with a headlamp and a habit of reading marginalia.

Coincidence. I wrote the word in my head and examined it and found it structurally unsound.

Here is what I believed for six years, and what I had built my whole method of survival on: that the past is a closed line. That you mark it on your map, you note the date of abandonment, and you route around it forever. That's what my notebook is — hundreds of defunct rail spurs, closed branches, bridges torn out, stations gone to weeds. A catalog of everywhere the world stopped going, kept by a woman determined to do the same.

But I sat in that chamber with a dead man's charts drying against my knee, and I understood something with the unwelcome clarity of a load path revealing itself: closed lines don't stay closed. Water finds them. Water always finds them. That's the first thing hydrology teaches and the last thing pride learns. The river they sold doesn't forget its old channel, and neither does a past, and somewhere upstream of this cave, behind a dam that a good surveyor died trying to condemn, a village was living downstream of both.

The tide would turn within the hour. Reyes wanted his reef assessment. The safe move — my move, the only move I've made for six years — was to roll the charts up, leave them, walk out, and spend my two stranded weeks fixing winches and memorizing timetables and becoming nobody again.

I looked at the margin note one more time. *They know.*

So did I, now. And knowing, for me, has always been the expensive part.

I rolled the linen along its original creases, slid it back into the tube, capped it, and stood. My boots were soaked through and my heart was doing something unprofessional.

On my way out, passing the last of the carvings, I put my palm flat against the deepest-cut line — *the river remembers what we forgot* — the way you touch a railing on a stair in the dark. Not for guidance. Just to confirm it's real.

Then I walked out into the gold light of the drained flats, a brass tube under my arm and my rule lying somewhere back in the dark behind me, cracked clean through.

Behind me, far out on the flats, the sea had begun, very quietly, to come back.
