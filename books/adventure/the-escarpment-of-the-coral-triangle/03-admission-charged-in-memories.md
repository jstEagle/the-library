## 3. Admission Charged in Memories

The tide came back for me at noon, and I let it.

I'd spent the morning on the flats with the brass tube under my shirt, telling myself I was waiting for the freighter's boats, that I was being sensible, that a woman who had spent eleven years perfecting the art of leaving did not stand on a reef island staring at a hole in the rock like it owed her money. But the cave mouth had opened at dawn and closed at mid-morning, and the sea had sealed it behind me like a door deciding my business was its own now. So I sat above the tideline with my back against basalt and waited for the water to give me another appointment.

I should explain about doors. I have a professional relationship with them. A door is just a decision you can see, and I have spent most of my adult life walking through them in the wrong direction — out of offices, off of trains, away from towns where someone had started to learn my name. The trick, I'd found, was never to look at a door too long. Looking is how staying starts.

The cave had made me look.

* * *

Here is what I knew when the sun was still low: the tube held six sheets, drawn on linen paper that had gone soft as cloth, and every one of them carried a stamp I recognized before I understood why my hands had gone cold holding it.

**KETTLE & VANCE SURVEY DIVISION — PROPERTY OF THE ENGINEERING CORPS.**

Kettle & Vance. My mentor's firm. The firm that trained me, certified me, and then — after the bridge, after the inquiry, after the verdict I still can't say aloud without tasting copper — quietly struck my name from its rolls as if I'd been a misprint. Eleven years and half an ocean away, and there was their stamp, in a drowned man's cave, on charts of a river I'd never heard of until yesterday.

Coincidence is a word engineers use for patterns they haven't finished measuring yet.

* * *

The journal was wedged in a niche behind the survey equipment, wrapped in oilcloth that had done its job grudgingly. The cover was swollen with salt, the ink faded to the color of weak tea, but the hand was confident — a surveyor's hand, all economy and certainty. The name inside the front cover read **E. Sandoval, Asst. Surveyor, Ketapang Expedition**.

He wrote like a man who expected to be read. That should have been my first warning.

> *Day 4. The village elders will not say the river's name above a whisper. Our guide, Tomas, crossed himself twice when we asked where the water comes from. "Upstream," he said, "where the company bought what couldn't be sold." I have surveyed rivers on three continents and I have never been told a source is rude to ask after.*

> *Day 9. Found the carvings today. The whole east wall of the cave, floor to ceiling, cut into the living stone. Pak Darma says they are older than any map. He would not come inside. He stood at the entrance in the rain and called this place "the toll house." When I asked what the toll was, he looked at me the way you look at a man who has asked the price of something he cannot afford.*

I ran my lamp along the wall he meant, and the carvings rose out of the dark in spirals, in terraces, in figures with arms raised and arms lowered. Centuries of hands had worked that stone. And near the bottom, where the oldest cuts had gone smooth with touch, was a scene I understood immediately, the way you understand a diagram: a village beside a wide river. Then the same village, and the river shown as a line — broken. Then the village again, smaller, the houses rendered as hollow squares, the people as outlines with no faces.

A story in three panels. Sold. Severed. Emptied.

Below it, newer cuts, cruder, maybe a century old, maybe less: a dam drawn as a bar across the river's throat. And beneath the bar, scratched deep enough to gouge, in a script I needed Sandoval's journal to translate:

> *Day 11. Pak Darma finally spoke of it. Two generations ago, the consortium came with contracts and rifles and one offer: sign over the headwaters, or watch your children starve in the drought they were happy to arrange. The village signed. They tell themselves they saved themselves. Darma's grandmother was among the signatories. She lived thirty more years and walked to this cave every season to add her grief to the wall. He showed me her marks — the small hollow squares. One for each grandchild born downstream of the dam, in water the village no longer owned. "We paid admission," he said, "and the price was memory. Now we remember only that we agreed."*

I sat down on cold stone and read that last line four times.

*Now we remember only that we agreed.*

There is a version of me that hears a line like that and moves on. There always has been. She's the woman who left the inquiry hall without looking back, who learned to sleep in freight cars, who keeps a notebook of dead rail lines because dead things don't ask questions. That woman knows exactly what a story about selling your river costs, and she knows better than to buy.

But I kept thinking about my own map. Sixty-one abandoned spurs, and I could tell you the story of every one — not from records, mostly, but from standing where they died and reading the ground. A rail line is a promise somebody stopped keeping, and I have made a career of visiting promises like that, the way some people visit graves.

And here was a whole river treated the same way. Bought, severed, forgotten on purpose. With my old firm's stamp on the paperwork.

You can call it curiosity. I've heard braver words used for worse decisions.

* * *

The tide returned at noon with the sound of a crowd arriving, and I went back in because the alternative was standing in the sun doing nothing, and doing nothing has never once saved me.

With water at my knees I could reach the upper galleries, ledges where the storm surge hadn't touched. That's where the rest of Sandoval was. His theodolite, fused to its tripod by rust. A lantern with a burn mark on its housing that I chose not to interpret. And tucked into a crack, dry as bone, a final sheaf — not charts this time but working notes, the messy arithmetic underneath the clean drawings.

The clean drawings were beautiful. Six sheets, the river mapped from its mouth to within a few kilometers of its headwaters, contour lines flowing like handwriting. Any engineer would admire them. I admired them. Then I noticed what a careful eye was meant to notice and a careless one was meant to miss: the route upstream was complete except at the very top, where the contours converged on a boxed notation, ink pressed hard enough to tear the linen.

**SOURCE — ACCESS PROHIBITED BY CONCESSION 7-A. FORBIDDEN.**

Forbidden. Not dangerous, not unstable, not subject to seasonal closure. Forbidden — the word you use when the truth needs a fence around it.

And here is the thing I have not told anyone, the thing I will probably not tell the crew of whatever boat eventually fishes me off this rock: when I saw that word, my first feeling was not fear. It was recognition. Somewhere in my chest, a woman who has spent eleven years treating every closed line as a personal insult felt a map look back at her and say *you too?*

Sandoval's notes told the rest. I'll set them down as he did, because his voice deserves the space mine usually takes up:

> *Concession 7-A is a fiction with a seal on it. We traced the headwaters against explicit instruction — Voss's instruction, relayed through the company, though he signs himself "site security" as if that were a profession. What we found is a valley the maps show as unimproved land. It is not unimproved. It is improved upon by forty meters of concrete.*

> *They built the dam across the original gorge and drowned the old village behind it. Not the signatory village — the first one. The mother village, the one the elders' grandparents abandoned when the deal was signed. The company flooded it to settle the question of whether anyone might go home. You cannot return to a place that is underwater. Elegant. I confess I admired the engineering before I understood what it was for.*

> *But here is what Voss does not know, or does not care to know: I have run the numbers on that dam for nine days now, and the numbers do not hold. The abutments sit on fractured karst. The spillway gates are corroded past their design tolerance. Every rainy season loads it further. This is not a structure. It is a debt, and the interest compounds annually, and the collateral is every village on the lower river.*

> *I am going to write the full report. If anything happens to me, it will be where the company will never send divers — behind their own wall, in the valley they drowned to bury the argument. Let them guard it. Guarding is not the same as keeping.*

Behind their own wall. In the valley they drowned.

I stood in waist-deep water in a cave that charged admission in memories, holding a dead man's arithmetic, and I did the thing I have trained myself never to do. I imagined staying. Not staying put — I'm not a fool — but staying *with* it. Following the river up. Finding the report. Finishing what a surveyor named E. Sandoval started and paid for, apparently, with the lantern that burned his housing and the silence that followed Day 19, where the journal simply stops, mid-sentence, in the middle of a sentence about load tolerances—

Mid-sentence. About load tolerances. They killed him between one calculation and the next, and the company filed him under *weather event*, and the sea kept his work for twenty years and handed it to the one person alive whose whole life is a catalog of things other people decided to abandon.

I laughed. Actually laughed, alone in the dark, which should worry both of us.

Because here's the pattern, and I'm good at patterns: Kettle & Vance trained the man whose methods I can read like a sibling's handwriting. The same conventions, the same abbreviations, the same stubborn habit of annotating margins with doubts. My mentor learned from someone in that lineage; I learned from my mentor; and somewhere upstream of us both, the same current runs through Sandoval's notes and my own. I didn't find this cave. The way my luck runs, I'm starting to suspect the cave found me — waited twenty years for a storm to crack the door and a runner to wash through it.

The carvings say the toll is memory. Fine. I have memories I'd happily pay out — the bridge, the hearing, the face of the man who lied with such calm precision while my career went into the water. Take them. Consider the debt settled.

What I won't pay is attention. Attention is the one currency I've hoarded for eleven years, spending it only on dead lines that can't follow me home.

And yet.

The tide was rising around my ribs now, patient as a creditor. Ahead, the cave mouth glowed green-gold with afternoon, opening onto a lagoon where — I could see through the entrance — a boat lay at anchor. Salvage rig, patched sails, a hull that had argued with reefs and won on points. Figures moved on her deck.

On any other day of my eleven years, I'd have noted the boat, noted the people, and planned my departure from their lives before they'd finished learning mine.

Instead I found myself checking Sandoval's charts one last time, tracing the river's line inland to that hard-pressed box of ink, and thinking — with the specific clarity of a woman about to make a mistake she already respects — that forbidden sources and closed lines are the same animal. Somebody seals them because somebody else might come looking. And I have never once, in sixty-one abandoned spurs and eleven years of leaving, met a sealed thing that didn't want finding.

I waded toward the light, journal inside my shirt next to the brass tube, and the water pushed at my back like a hand between my shoulder blades.

The boat's crew had seen me by now. One of them — a broad woman at the rail, arms crossed, evaluating me with the frank arithmetic of someone pricing salvage — raised a hand, not quite a wave, not quite a warning.

I raised mine back, and thought: *whatever this costs, I've paid more for less.*
