## 4. The Salvagers of Pulau Karang

**Mei-Lin**

I have fished three things out of the water off Pulau Karang in my years running the *Kestrel*: a drowned outboard motor, a crate of contraband cigarettes that turned out to be full of expired antibiotics, and now a woman.

The woman was the only one of the three who thanked me.

She came up over my transom like something the sea had decided it was done with — soaked to the bone, one boot missing, clutching a brass tube against her ribs the way other people clutch children or money. Sef hauled her aboard by the collar while Dara held the wheel and shouted opinions about both of them, and Pak Uda just looked at her for a long moment with those eyes that have seen too much ocean to be surprised by anything in it.

"Consortium scouts were chasing her," Dara said, jerking her chin toward the skiff. It was already falling away behind us, its two-man crew pretending very hard that they had never been interested in anyone at all. "Two of them. Armed."

"Armed with what?"

"Rifles they didn't fire."

"Then they weren't armed," I said. "They were *discouraged*. There's a difference, and the difference is paperwork." I looked down at the woman coughing seawater onto my deck. "You. What's your name?"

"Orsen Zhou." Her voice was rough but level. She got to her feet without asking permission, which I noted, and didn't thank me again, which I noted harder. People who don't grovel after being rescued are either proud or dangerous, and usually both.

"And what did you do to earn two men with unfired rifles, Orsen Zhou?"

"I walked on a beach."

"She walked on a beach," Dara repeated, delighted. "Captain, we should charge admission."

I should have put her ashore at Karang village and let the tide take responsibility for her. That is what twenty years of keeping this boat alive and this crew fed has taught me: every stray costs more than it promises. But she stood there dripping, holding that brass tube like it was the last true thing in the world, and when the scouts' skiff circled back at a cautious distance she turned to face it instead of away from it, and I thought — well. I thought nothing sensible at all.

"You'll work," I told her. "Nobody rides free on the *Kestrel*. Deckhand's wage, deckhand's chores, and you sleep where the rope smells worst."

"Fine," she said.

"That's it? No argument? No sob story?"

"Do you want one?"

"No," I said, and discovered I meant it. "Good. Sef, show her where the rope smells worst."

* * *

**Sef**

My sister says I fall in love too easily. She says it like it's a flaw and not a highly efficient system for identifying interesting people.

The new one — Zhou — fell asleep standing up in the engine room within an hour of coming aboard. Just leaned against the bulkhead next to the winch motor with her arms crossed and her chin down, out cold, while the whole boat rocked through the swell. And when I clanked a wrench on purpose to wake her, she didn't startle. She opened her eyes all the way, instantly, like a switch, and said:

"Your main bearing's dying."

"It's been dying since before you were born," I said. "It's tradition."

"It's been dying loudly. You can hear it in the hull. Another month and it takes the shaft with it."

Dara appeared in the doorway, because Dara appears whenever someone says something mechanical near me, drawn by some twin radar that has never once failed to ruin my day. "He knows," she said. "We know. We can't get parts for it. The consortium owns the only supplier in the archipelago and they don't sell to boats flagged independent."

Zhou crouched by the motor, still dripping, and put her hand flat on the casing like a doctor taking a pulse. Then she did something I have never seen anyone do: she listened to it with her palm instead of her ears.

"You don't need parts," she said. "You need shims. Half a millimeter, cut from sheet stock, and your bilge pump gasket is the right thickness if you sacrifice it. It'll buy you six months. Maybe eight."

"The bilge pump gasket," Dara said slowly, "is the only thing keeping us afloat in the wet season."

"So is that bearing, currently. Pick one."

There was a silence in which my sister and I communicated entirely through eyebrows, which is our most fluent language. The stranger waited through it patiently, hand still on the motor, like she was introducing herself to it.

"Who trained you?" I asked.

"Nobody worth mentioning."

"Everybody's nobody trained them somewhere."

She smiled for the first time since the water gave her back. It was a small smile, quick, gone before it could be accused of sincerity. "Railroads," she said. "A long time ago. In a country far away where nobody chases you on beaches."

Then she asked for tools, and I gave them to her, and by midnight the bearing sounded ten years younger and the bilge pump was wearing a gasket made from a cut-up fuel funnel, which is the kind of engineering that would make my old teacher weep, and I mean that as a compliment.

Dara found me watching her work. "You're doing the face," she said.

"I'm not doing any face."

"You're doing the face you did when you found the diving helmet."

"I'm going to do a face at *you* in a minute."

But it was true. I was doing the face. Because here is the thing about the *Kestrel*: everything on her is held together by habit and stubbornness, and everyone aboard has stopped believing she could be more than she is, including me, including maybe especially me. And then the sea delivers a woman who puts her hand on our dying engine and tells it to live, and it listens.

I fall in love easily. My sister can say what she wants.

* * *

**Dara**

Here is what I knew about Orsen Zhou by the end of her first day aboard, which is more than she told us and less than I intend to find out:

One. She lies about small things fluently and badly. Where she's from, what brought her south, whether she's eaten — all smooth, all wrong. But ask her about metal or load tolerances or why a river bends the way it does, and suddenly she can't lie at all. The truth comes out of her like water finding a crack.

Two. She sleeps in shifts of ninety minutes exactly, wakes without an alarm, and positions herself every time so that the door is in view. I know because I checked, and she caught me checking, and neither of us mentioned it, which is its own kind of conversation.

Three. She holds that brass tube like it owes her money. Or like she owes it. Hard to say which.

Four. When Pak Uda offered her tea — and Uda offers nobody tea, Uda's tea is a decoration reserved for state occasions — she took it with both hands and drank it like it might be taken back, and the old man watched her do it with an expression I have only ever seen him wear at graves.

So naturally I needled her.

It's not cruelty. It's triage. Anyone who comes aboard carrying that much locked cargo behind their face is either going to open it eventually or go over the side with it, and I'd rather know which kind of passenger we've got before we're three days upriver with no options. So at dinner — rice, dried fish, the good chili sauce Sef pretends he doesn't ration — I asked her, casual as weather:

"So. Beaches. Walking on them. Is that a profession or a hobby?"

"Dara," Sef said.

"What? I'm making conversation."

"You're conducting an interrogation with chopsticks."

"I'm being friendly. Some cultures interrogate as friendship. Ours, now, apparently."

Zhou ate through it, unhurried. Then she set down her bowl and looked at me straight on, no flinch, and I'll admit it raised the hair on my arms, because most people look away around the third second and she hadn't even started counting yet.

"You want to know if I'm trouble," she said. "Fair question. Here's my answer: I'm trouble the way weather is trouble. I arrive, things change, and I generally leave before anyone has to decide how they feel about it. If that's unacceptable, say so now and I'll row myself ashore."

"Row yourself ashore," Sef repeated. "In what."

"I'll build the boat tonight. Give me until dawn and a barrel stave."

And there it was — that flash of the real person under the smooth lies, dry as a struck match — and I laughed before I could stop myself, and I saw Mei-Lin at the head of the table trying very hard not to do the same, and failing, and pretending she'd been clearing her throat.

"Stay," I said. "But I'm going to keep asking questions."

"I'd expect nothing less from someone who rations chili sauce and calls it hospitality."

Sef choked. Uda poured her more tea. And I sat there thinking: *oh no.* Not because she was trouble. Because she was funny, and competent, and alone in a way I recognized from the inside, and I could feel the exact moment my brother's efficient system activated in me too.

* * *

**Pak Uda**

I am the oldest thing on this boat except the rust.

People think a diver's job is the water. It is not. A diver's job is the waiting, and the reading of what waits with him. Forty years I have gone down into the green dark, and forty years the dark has shown me what people carry when they think no one sees: the smuggler who prays before every descent, the salvage broker who counts his money underwater because he trusts fish more than partners, the young ones who dive angry and the older ones who dive sad.

The woman carries a map.

Not the paper one in the brass tube — though that too, always, close. I mean the one behind her eyes. I have met her kind before, in the years when the consortium first came buying rivers and silence both: engineers, surveyors, clerks, people who drew lines on the world for a living and then had to run from the lines they drew. They move constantly. They memorize exits. They are the loneliest swimmers in any current, because they are always swimming away from something, and a body cannot swim away forever; eventually it must swim toward, or drown.

She came to me on the second evening, when the others were arguing about fuel prices, and sat across from me without speaking, which is correct manners. I poured the tea. We drank. The sea did its sea business against the hull.

"You knew the cave," she said finally. "On the reef island. The one the storm opened."

"The sea opens that door twice a year," I said. "Admission charged in memories. That is what the old people say. Whether it is true—" I shrugged, an old man's shrug, which contains multitudes. "I have been inside. Twice. Both times I came out lighter. Once by wonder. Once by grief. The cave does not choose which."

"There were charts inside. Survey charts. Stamped with a name — Harrow & Vance Consortium, upstream division. And a journal. A dead man's handwriting." Her hands were steady around the cup. Her voice was not, quite. "The route goes upriver to a source marked forbidden. Do you know what's there?"

I looked at her for a long time. The dark behind her eyes was very busy that night.

"I know what the elders of the river villages know," I said. "That twenty years ago the consortium built a dam where no dam should be, and bought every mouth that might object, and the ones who wouldn't sell moved away or stopped being heard from. I know that the river above runs strange now, and the fish below grow thin, and last season a man from the delta showed me photographs of cracks in the dam face that the company says do not exist." I refilled her cup. "And I know that two men in an unfired-rifle skiff chased you off a beach for looking at old paper. Which means the paper is worth killing for. Which means the source is worth reaching."

"You think I should go."

"I think," I said, "that the cave charged you admission, and you have not yet paid. People who leave debts like that unpaid carry them into every room they enter. I have watched it happen. It is a heavy way to travel."

She was quiet a long moment. Out on the water, a night bird called twice and got no answer, which seemed rude of the night.

"And if paying costs more than I have?"

"Then you will be glad," I told her, "to discover you have more than you thought. It is always this way. The sea taught me that before anything else did."

She left soon after, cup washed and returned — correct manners, learned somewhere, by somebody, once. I stayed in the stern with my tea and my rust and my forty years, and I thought about the drowned surveyor whose handwriting she carried, and I thought: *the river is calling its people home. It does that, sometimes. Slowly. Through storms and strangers.*

* * *

**Mei-Lin**

Morning of the third day, I called a crew meeting, which on the *Kestrel* means I stood at the wheel and everyone else pretended to be busy nearby.

"We have a job," I said. "Salvage contract, two weeks, standard wreck recovery off the southern cape. After that, fuel is low and the pickings are thinner every year because the consortium buys wrecks before we reach them. So." I looked at each of them in turn. Sef, hopeful. Dara, suspicious. Uda, serene as a Buddha with salt water in his veins. "Anyone with objections to adding a passenger leg, speak now."

"A passenger leg," Dara said. "To where, exactly?"

"Upriver."

Silence. Even the engine seemed to lean in.

"The river," Sef said carefully, "where the consortium is."

"The same river."

"And the passenger," Dara said, "who walked on a beach and attracted armed men, wants to go up the consortium's river, past their checkpoints, toward the place their maps call forbidden. Have I summarized correctly?"

"You forgot the part where she fixed our winch," Sef said.

"I didn't forget it. I'm choosing not to be manipulated by it."

"You were manipulated by it yesterday when she also fixed the fresh-water pump."

"That was *also* manipulation."

I let them wear themselves out. This is captaincy: knowing when the argument needs you and when it just needs room. When they finally ran down, I spoke to the deck in general and the woman in particular, because she had come up from the engine room and stood at the rail with salt drying white on her sleeves, listening to herself discussed like weather.

"Orsen Zhou," I said. "State your business upriver. All of it, or none of it, and I'll decide based on which you choose."

She was quiet long enough that Dara shifted her weight. Then:

"Not all of it. Not yet. Some of it I don't understand myself yet. But I'll tell you what I know for certain." She set the brass tube on the chart table and did not open it, which took discipline, I could see. "There's a dam at the head of that river. It's failing. There's proof it was built to fail, and proof of everything else the consortium buried along with it, and there are villages downstream that will drown when it goes unless someone makes noise loud enough that even owned governments have to hear it. I intend to be that noise." Her jaw set. "I've spent nine years being no one, moving fast, leaving no trace. I'm done. If you take me upriver, I can't promise you safe. I can promise you useful."

Another silence, this one different in texture. Uda nodded slowly, once, like a man confirming a tide table.

"Work, not charity," I said at last. "That's the rule. You've paid some already — the winch, the pump, the bearing. The rest you pay in labor and in truth, delivered as earned. And Zhou—" I waited until her eyes met mine. "If Voss's people come for you, and they will, because men like that always come, you will not run from this boat. Running splits crews. Crews that split die separately. Are we understood?"

Something crossed her face then — quickly, but not quickly enough to hide from a woman who has watched crews form and break for twenty years. Relief, maybe. Or fear of relief. The expression of a person who has just been told *stay* and doesn't know yet whether staying is a gift or a trap.

"Understood," she said.

"Good. Dara, plot us a course to the river mouth. Sef, fuel what we have and pray to whatever god maintains small engines. Uda—" He was already pouring tea. "Yes, fine, everyone gets tea. Apparently it's a state occasion."

As they scattered to their tasks, I turned the wheel and brought the *Kestrel*'s nose around toward the south and the brown water of the great river, and I felt the old familiar weight settle onto my shoulders — the weight of every decision I'd ever made for other people's lives — and underneath it, unfamiliar and unwelcome, something lighter.

Hope, probably. Damn it.

Behind me I heard Dara say, "So, beach-walker. Ever run rapids?" and Zhou answer, "No. How hard can it be? It's just water with opinions," and Sef laugh the big unguarded laugh he thinks nobody notices, and I gripped the wheel and pointed us all at the current, and the current, as currents do, pointed back.
