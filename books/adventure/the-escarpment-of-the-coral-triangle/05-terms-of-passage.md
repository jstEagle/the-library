## 5. Terms of Passage

I have haggled over diesel in three currencies, salvaged a propeller shaft with a bicycle chain and prayer, and once talked a customs officer out of impounding the *Kestrel* by convincing him she was cursed. I am not a woman who frightens easily at a negotiation table.

But there was something about the way Orsen Zhou sat down across from me that morning — spine straight, hands flat on the table like she was presenting evidence at her own trial — that made me want to count my fingers after shaking on anything.

We used my cabin for it, because the deck was still littered with the aftermath of pulling her out of trouble. Sef had dragged two crates up to serve as a table and Dara had "generously" contributed a lantern that flickered like it had opinions. Pak Uda leaned in the doorway, which meant he'd already decided something and was waiting for the rest of us to catch up.

"You want passage upriver," I said.

"I want passage upriver," she agreed.

"The Kestrel doesn't go upriver. The Kestrel is a salvage boat. She works the coast, the reefs, the wrecks. Rivers are someone else's problem."

"Your winch is someone else's problem too," she said. "Has been for a while, by the sound of it."

The lantern chose that moment to gutter, as if taking her side. I made a note to have words with it later.

"Everyone who's ever offered to fix my boat for free has wanted something worth more than the fix," I said.

"Then it's good that I'm not offering it for free." She turned her notebook around and slid it across the crates. On the page was a diagram of our winch — our actual winch, drawn from memory after hearing it strain twice yesterday — with the fault circled. A cracked drum bearing, by her read. She was probably right, which annoyed me more than being wrong would have.

"Terms," she said. "Passage to the head of navigation on the Karang River, and my labor aboard until then. In exchange: food, a bunk, and no questions about where I'm going or why."

"No questions," I repeated.

"None."

"That's not how crews work, engineer. You eat at my table, you sleep under my deck, you will hear questions whether or not anyone asks them out loud. Sef alone is a walking interrogation with a wrench."

Something moved behind her eyes — not fear exactly. More like a door closing very quietly so no one would notice it had ever been open.

"Then I'll answer what I can," she said, "and the rest stays mine. That's the best price I've got."

I looked at Pak Uda. He looked back at me with the expression he uses when he's known the ending of a story since the second paragraph and sees no reason to spoil it.

"Here's my counter," I said. "You work, we feed you, you get the bunk by the engine room because nobody else wants it. But I don't take passengers into the green hell on faith alone. You'll prove your hands before I point this boat upstream. And when I ask a question, you'll tell me the truth or tell me plainly you won't. Lies rot a crew faster than salt water rots steel."

She considered that for longer than most people consider their own funerals. Then she nodded once, sharp, like closing a circuit.

"Truth or plain refusal," she said. "Agreed."

"One more thing." I tapped her notebook. "Upriver isn't a pleasure cruise. There are men on that river who work for people who own the law, and they don't like company. If we get shot at, you haul, you patch, you bleed with the rest of us. No standing aside because you're only paying for passage."

"I wouldn't stand aside anyway," she said, and the thing about it was — I believed her.

* * *

Of course, believing her hands was another matter entirely, and I'm an old enough captain to know that everyone can talk a good winch.

That evening I set the test without announcing it as one. The winch had been complaining for a month — a grinding shriek every time we lifted anything heavier than Dara's ego — and I'd been nursing it along with grease and denial because a new drum bearing meant a trip to a port where people asked questions about cargo manifests. I told Orsen the winch was temperamental, watched her nod, and went to bed.

What I didn't tell her was that the spare parts locker hadn't been properly inventoried since the monsoon, that half our shims were the wrong size, and that the last man who'd tried to fix it had quit halfway through and joined a monastery, or possibly a pirate crew; the distinction was unclear even to him.

I woke at dawn to a sound I hadn't heard in weeks: silence from the aft deck. No grinding. No shriek. Just the water against the hull and Sef's voice saying, in tones of genuine religious awe, "How did you *shim* that?"

I came up the companionway to find the winch stripped to its bones, cleaned, reassembled, and running smooth as a minister's promise. Orsen sat on the crate beside it, grease to both elbows, hair tied back with what appeared to be a length of packing twine, eating rice out of a bowl like she'd been part of the furniture for years. Her notebook lay open next to her, and I caught a glimpse of the page before she turned it — not just the winch. The whole aft deck, sketched in fine lines, with load ratings noted in a hand so precise it looked printed.

"She took it apart at midnight," Sef reported, vibrating slightly. "Found the crack. Machined a temporary bushing out of a bronze valve housing we weren't using. It'll hold until we can get a proper bearing cast. Maybe longer. Captain, she *improvised a lathe*. Out of the anchor windlass."

"It was mostly already a lathe," Orsen said. "It just didn't know it yet."

Dara emerged from below, took one look at the winch, and narrowed her eyes at Orsen with the particular suspicion of a twin whose role as the clever one has been threatened. "Show-off," she said.

"Efficient," said Orsen.

"Same thing," muttered Sef, "when it works."

Pak Uda came up last, slow as always, looked at the winch, looked at Orsen, and smiled his gap-toothed smile. "Good hands," he pronounced, which from him is a knighthood.

I drank my coffee and did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since the cave island. A stranger washes ashore after a storm, asks for passage up the exact river where a survey team drowned twenty years ago, carries charts she won't show anyone, and fixes machinery like she was born holding a torque wrench. Either she was the luckiest coincidence afloat, or she knew precisely what she was asking for and was lying to me by omission.

By our terms, omission wasn't quite a lie. By our terms, I could ask and she could refuse. So I asked.

"The river," I said. "Why up it? Truth or plain refusal."

She set down her bowl. For a moment I thought she'd refuse outright, and I was already adjusting my plans around the refusal — because a person who refuses cleanly you can sometimes trust more than a person who lies prettily.

Then she said, "There's a name stamped on some old charts I found. A survey company. It's on my map too — a rail spur that closed the same year your dam went in, same year the survey team drowned. I don't believe in that many coincidences. Something happened at the source of that river, and somebody spent good money making sure it stayed buried." She met my eyes. "That's the truth. The rest I'm still digging out myself."

It was more than I'd expected and less than everything, which is the exact shape most truths come in. I nodded slowly.

"And if it's dangerous?" Dara asked, arms crossed. "If whatever buried it comes looking?"

Orsen looked at her hands, still stained bronze-brown from the bushing. "Then I'd be bringing it to your boat," she said quietly. "I know. I've thought about that. Every hour since you pulled me out of the water." She looked up. "I'm not asking you to walk into it blind. I'm asking you to decide with your eyes open, same as I am."

Silence, except the winch turning over smooth and quiet, like a witness for the defense.

"Well," said Pak Uda, "she fixed the winch."

"That's not an argument," said Dara.

"It's the only argument that matters on a boat," said Pak Uda.

* * *

I gave her my answer that night, but not before doing one more thing captains do, which is checking what people leave unattended.

I'm not proud of it. I'm also not ashamed. Twenty years of salvage teaches you that the sea gives things back and takes things away and never explains itself, and people are the same, only wetter. So while Orsen was on deck watching the stars like they owed her money, I looked at the notebook she'd left in the galley — open, deliberately or carelessly, I still don't know which — at the pages of defunct rail lines catalogued in that same precise hand. Spur lines, branch lines, closed sidings, dates of abandonment. Decades of them. Islands I knew, ports I'd drunk in, all threaded together in ink by someone who'd spent her life mapping everything the world had decided to forget.

And down one margin, small as a confession: *never stay longer than the job.*

I closed the notebook and put it back exactly as it lay, and I stood in the galley feeling something I hadn't felt since I buried my husband: the ache of recognizing a fellow exile. I'd run too, once — different direction, different sea, same engine of grief driving the propeller. I knew what it cost. I knew the bunk by the engine room wasn't loud enough to drown it.

So when she came below, I was waiting with two cups of tea instead of one, and I pushed the second across the table before she could invent a reason not to sit.

"We sail on the morning tide," I said. "Up the Karang, as far as she'll float us. After that, your feet and the jungle's mercy."

"Thank you," she said, and reached for her notebook to pay, and I put my hand flat on the cover.

"Not tonight. Tonight you drink your tea." I hesitated, then gave her the only gift I had that wouldn't cost either of us too much. "Whoever taught you to draw maps like that — they'd be proud. Even if you never let them say so."

Her face did something complicated and quickly hidden, the way a lock does when the right key turns a half-circle and jams. She picked up the tea. She didn't thank me again, which was correct. Some thanks are too heavy to lift in company.

At dawn the tide turned fair and we turned with it. Sef took the helm, Dara ran the bow line, Pak Uda smoked on the roof of the wheelhouse like a chimney with opinions, and Orsen Zhou stood at the rail watching the river mouth open ahead of us — brown water braiding out to meet the green sea, the current pushing back against everything we were about to ask of it.

I came up beside her and handed her a coil of line to coil, because belonging on a boat is measured in chores, not speeches.

"One condition I forgot to mention," I said.

She raised an eyebrow.

"When this is over — whatever's at the source, whoever's waiting at the end of it — you don't slip off in the night with your notebook and your closed lines. You say goodbye. To the crew. Out loud. That's the fare."

She looked at me for a long moment, and I watched her weigh the promise the way she'd weighed the winch — testing for cracks, finding the load rating, deciding if it would hold.

"Out loud," she agreed. "That's a strange kind of contract, Captain."

"On this boat," I said, "it's the only kind that's ever held."

She was still coiling the line when Sef called down from the wheelhouse, voice pitched wrong — tight, too casual, the way he gets when he's trying not to alarm anyone and alarming everyone instead.

"Captain? Smoke. Two points off the starboard bow. And there's a boat sitting in the channel ahead of us flying colors I don't like."

I got the glasses up. Grey hull, gun mount under a tarp that fooled no one, and painted on the bow, neat as a signature: the consortium's crane-and-mountain mark.

Beside me, Orsen went very still, the way water goes still right before it finds the hole in the dam.

"Well," I said, handing her the line after all. "Looks like the river's charging admission already."
