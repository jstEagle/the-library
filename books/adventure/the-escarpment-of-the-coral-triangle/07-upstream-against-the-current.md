## 7. Upstream Against the Current

The Kestrel hated the river, and the river knew it.

I could tell by the way she wallowed in the current, her old diesel coughing like a smoker climbing stairs, the propeller chewing brown water that wanted nothing so much as to push us back to the sea. Rivers are honest that way. They tell you exactly how much they don't want you. Oceans lie — they'll cradle you for a week and then eat you without changing expression. But a river argues with you the whole way, and I'd spent enough of my life arguing to respect the technique.

"Third time she's stalled today," Sef said, wiping his hands on a rag that had given up being a rag sometime last year. He crouched beside the engine housing with his twin sister's wrench — Dara's wrench, technically, though possession among the twins was a fluid concept — and peered into the guts of the Kestrel like a doctor who suspected the patient was faking.

"Fuel line," I said.

He looked up. "You've been aboard four days."

"I've been listening to it for four days." I nodded at the housing. "It's not stalling. It's starving. The pickup's half-clogged with tank sediment, and every time we hit a rapid the slosh pulls the dregs through. She's not dying. She's begging."

Sef considered this with the gravity of a man deciding whether to be offended or delighted. He was maybe twenty-five, built like a question mark bent over engines since he could walk, with grease permanently tattooed into the creases of his knuckles. "Dara says you're a spy."

"Dara says a lot of things."

"She also says you're an engineer, which is worse. Engineers always think they know everything." He handed me the rag, which I took as either a peace offering or a test. "Do you?"

"No. That's the first thing they teach you." I knelt beside him and put my ear near the fuel line, where a faint flutter rode under the engine's growl. "The second thing they teach you is where to look when you don't."

* * *

We ran the river from dawn until the light went thick and gold, and the whole time the jungle pressed in on both banks like a crowd leaning over a fence to watch us fail. The Karang River didn't have rapids so much as opinions — long stretches of sullen flat water punctuated by convulsions of white rock where the current dropped its temper all at once. Mei-Lin handled the wheel with the economy of a woman who had made peace with the fact that the world was trying to kill her and had decided to find it tedious.

"Hard left!" she shouted, and the Kestrel heeled over, and I braced against the engine housing while Sef fed the throttle in exactly the rhythm I'd shown him that morning — a stutter of power before each drop, never during, letting the propeller bite clean water instead of foam.

It worked. It worked well enough that by midday he'd stopped watching my face when he did it, which among engineers is the equivalent of a medal ceremony.

"You were on the coastal boats," he said during a lull between rapids, not looking up from the carburetor he was rebuilding out of what appeared to be spite and spare parts. "Before. You have sea hands. River people hold things different."

"Do they."

"River people hold on like the thing might leave. Sea people hold on like they might." He seated a jet with a small precise tap. "Which are you?"

Neither, I almost said. I'm the kind that lets go first, professionally, before anyone can ask me to stay. But there was something about Sef — the same quality I'd noticed in Pak Uda, in even gruff Mei-Lin — a lack of appetite for my secrets. They weren't indifferent. They were patient. It was worse, somehow. Indifference you can ignore. Patience waits for you.

"Both," I said. "Long story."

"We've got a river's worth of days," Sef said, and smiled, and let it go, which is the twin trick: Dara pries, Sef leaves doors open. Between them they could get into anything, including, apparently, me.

As if summoned, Dara appeared at the rail above us, upside down in my field of vision, hanging off the pilothouse ladder with the boneless ease of someone who had never once worried about falling.

"Heard you fixed our winch," she said.

"Sef told you."

"Sef tells me everything. It's his most annoying feature." She dropped down to the deck, landing light as a cat with a grudge. "Mei-Lin says you're worth the food. Pak Uda says you've got drowned-man eyes. I say you talk like a woman reading from a script somebody else wrote."

"That's three opinions. You want mine?"

"I want the true one. The one under the script." She leaned against the rail, arms crossed, entirely comfortable with the silence she was creating. The river roared ahead; she ignored it. "Everybody running has one sentence they never say. I'm good at waiting for sentences."

I turned back to the fuel line. There was sediment in the filter cup again, fine gray grit that hadn't come from any tank — river silt, working its way in past seals that should have held. I noted it without noting it, the way you do, filing the observation somewhere automatic.

"My sentence," I said finally, "is that I'm from up north, I do contract work, and I'm going upstream because the pay is good."

Dara laughed — a real laugh, surprised out of her. "That's not a sentence, that's a receipt." She pushed off the rail and headed aft, calling back over her shoulder: "Keep the receipt. I'll collect the truth later. I always do."

Sef watched her go, then looked at me. "She will, too. Give up now, save yourself the trouble."

"I've been saving myself trouble my whole life," I said. "Doesn't seem to take."

* * *

The ambush came at the narrows below the escarpment, where the river pinched between walls of black stone and the current ran fast enough to make conversation impossible.

Pak Uda saw them first — he was up in the bow rigging, and he made a sound I'd learned to translate as *trouble*, low and flat, no drama in it. Two longboats slid out of a side channel, narrow and shallow-drafted, each carrying six men and outboard engines that had been modified by someone who understood them better than their makers intended. They fanned wide, cutting off the channel downstream, herding us toward a gravel bar where more men waited with rifles slung casual across their backs.

"Hired guns," Mei-Lin said at the wheel, voice level, eyes doing arithmetic. "Consortium's river tax collectors. They'll board, take the cargo, maybe take the boat."

"Maybe take us," Dara said cheerfully, from the rail, where she'd produced a machete from somewhere.

"Not today." Mei-Lin spun the wheel. "Orsen. The intake."

She didn't have to explain. I'd already seen it — the gray grit in the filter cup, twice now, silt where silt shouldn't be. And I'd seen something else, something I'd filed away without examining: the Kestrel's raw-water intake sat low on the hull, drawing straight from the river, and the river here carried the escarpment's own ground stone, fine as flour, sharp as glass.

"You want to foul their engines," I said.

"I want to survive the afternoon," Mei-Lin said. "Fouling theirs would be a bonus."

"It won't foul them." I was already moving, dragging the tool locker open, pulling the spare impeller, the roll of copper wire, the canvas patch kit. "It'll choke them. Slowly. These outboards pull water from right at the surface, where the silt runs thickest in the fast channel. We run the narrows at full throttle, dead center, where the current's fastest and carries the most suspended grit —"

"They'll follow," Sef said.

"They'll follow *exactly*. That's the point. Their intakes are shallow, ours is deep and screened. We can drink this water all day. They can't." I looked up at Mei-Lin. "But I need ten minutes and your bilge pump."

She didn't waste breath asking why. That, I was learning, was the crew's whole grammar: requests came pre-approved, questions cost time nobody had. She threw me a switch and the bilge pump shuddered alive, and I tore the discharge hose off its fitting and started building.

Here's the thing nobody tells you about engineering: it's mostly theft. Every solution is some other machine's trick, borrowed and bent to a new purpose. I spliced the bilge hose onto a length of copper pipe, wired a canvas sleeve around the end to spread the flow, and rigged the whole assembly over the stern so it would discharge just below the surface, directly into the wake. Then I opened the seacock full and let the Kestrel start pumping her own bilge water — gray, gritty, thick with the same escarpment flour — straight into the water behind us.

A cloud. A slow, spreading, engine-killing cloud of abrasive silt, laid down our wake like a trail of bait.

"They'll run right through it," Sef said, catching on, grin spreading. "Right down our throat."

"If they're smart they'll swing wide."

"They're not smart," Dara said. "They're paid. Paid men follow the money, and the money's following us."

Mei-Lin put the wheel hard over and drove the Kestrel straight at the narrows' throat, full throttle, and the old boat surged forward with a sound like a clearing throat, and behind us the bilge pump hammered away, laying its gray ribbon in the brown water.

The longboats took the bait. Of course they did — the wake is a road, and roads say *this way*, and men with rifles and rented engines follow roads the way rivers follow gravity. They swung in behind us, throttles wide, close enough that I could see the lead boat's driver squinting against our spray, close enough to count the rifle barrels, and then they hit the silt cloud.

For a moment, nothing. Then the lead engine's note climbed — a thin, rising whine, the sound of an impeller grinding stone — and the boat began to fall back, wallowing, its driver wrestling the throttle as if the problem were fuel instead of abrasion. The second boat swung wide, too late, too slow, and its engine died altogether with a clunk I heard over our own roar. Men began shouting. Rifles came up.

"Pak Uda!" Mei-Lin barked.

The old diver was already moving. He'd spent the ambush coiling rope with the unhurried calm of a man who had done this before other lifetimes, and now he stood at the stern rail and threw the coil in a single flat arc that dropped the line across the lead longboat's bow. The Kestrel surged. The line snapped taut. The longboat yawed sideways, engine screaming its dying whine, and slewed broadside into the current, and the men aboard had to choose between their rifles and staying upright. They chose upright. Rifles are heavy, and drowning men travel light.

The second boat drifted, dead in the water, its crew poling frantically toward the bank, and the gravel-bar men — seeing their boarding party converted into ballast — melted back into the trees with the professional discretion of people whose contract hadn't priced in hydrodynamics.

And then we were through, the narrows opening up ahead into a long golden reach of river, and the only sounds were the Kestrel's steady heartbeat and the bilge pump still hammering away, pumping nothing now but clean water, and Sef laughing — actually laughing, doubled over the engine housing — and Dara whooping from the rail with her machete raised like a trophy.

"THE INTAKE!" Sef shouted at me over the noise. "YOU KILLED THEM WITH THE INTAKE!"

"Bilge pump," I corrected. "Intake just kept us alive."

"Same war!" He grabbed my shoulder, shook it, let go — and the touch lasted half a second longer than it needed to, and meant more than it should have.

Mei-Lin cut the throttle to a cruise and looked back once at the drifting longboats, then forward, forever forward. "They'll report we came through the narrows," she said. "Voss will know we're past his first door."

"Then we'd better get past the second one," Dara said, "before he builds a third."

Pak Uda coiled his rope, sat down at the bow, and said quietly, to the river or to himself or possibly to me: "Water remembers who fouled it. Long memory, water."

I looked downstream, back the way we'd come, at the narrowing gap of black stone, and felt the thing I always felt after a fight — not triumph, not fear, but a strange hollow alertness, the sense of having used a part of myself I usually keep locked. I'd enjoyed it. That was the part I didn't say. I'd enjoyed building the trap, enjoyed the arithmetic of it, enjoyed watching it work — and enjoyment, in my experience, was the first payment life demanded before it sent the bill.

Behind us, far downstream, a gunboat's engine note carried faintly up the gorge — deeper, heavier, more patient than any longboat.

Voss wasn't sending hired men anymore.

He was coming himself.
