## 8. The Village That Sold Its River

I have been diving these waters since before the consortium had a name, and I will tell you what forty years underwater teaches you: everything sinks eventually, and most of it was never worth keeping. Boats sink. Engines sink. Promises sink. The trick of a long life is learning which things are worth the breath to bring back up.

So when the Kestrel rounded the last bend above the rapids and I saw the smoke of Kampung Selat rising through the afternoon haze — thin cooking smoke, honest smoke, not the black greasy columns the mining barges make — I felt something in my chest that I had not felt in years, and I did not like it, because at my age feelings are usually just indigestion wearing a costume.

"Smoke," said Sef from the engine housing, unnecessarily, because Sef announces everything he sees as though the rest of us are blind and he is doing us a kindness.

"I have eyes," said Dara.

"One eye."

"My other eye is resting."

Mei-Lin stood at the wheel with her arms folded, watching the village come down to meet us the way villages do here — stilt houses leaning over brown water, nets hung like laundry, children already running along the bank because a boat on this river is an event and children are the historians of events. She looked at me over her shoulder.

"You know this place," she said. It was not a question. Mei-Lin does not ask questions she has already answered; she asks questions so you know she knows.

"I knew it," I said. "Before."

"Before what?"

"Before they sold the river," I said, and went forward to ready the lines, because there is only so much remembering a man should do standing up.

* * *

The Kestrel slid against the jetty with a groan of old rubber fenders, and I tied us off with hands that remembered knots older than everyone aboard except me. The village had changed and had not changed, which is the way of places that have been paid not to change. The jetty itself was new timber — consortium timber, stamped with the company mark burned into every fourth plank like a brand on cattle. But behind it the houses were the same houses, patched and re-patched, and the mosque's green roof still caught the light the same way, and the old men still sat under the same banyan tree as if the tree were holding them up by agreement rather than gravity.

An elder came down the steps to meet us. Haji Rahman. He had been middle-aged when I was young, which made him now approximately geological. His eyes found me across the jetty and something moved in his face — recognition, then shame, then the careful blankness men build when they have spent twenty years practicing not seeing things.

"Pak Uda," he said. "They told me you were dead."

"They tell you many things," I said.

He looked past me at the Kestrel, at Mei-Lin's hard face, at the twins pretending not to listen while listening with their entire bodies, at Orsen Zhou standing at the rail with her notebook held against her chest like a shield or a Bible, I could not tell which and did not ask.

"You bring strangers," Rahman said quietly.

"I bring divers," I said. "Strangers is your department."

The old man almost smiled. Almost. Then his eyes went to the stamped planks beneath our feet, and whatever smile there had been went back into its hole.

"There is tea," he said. "And rice. And we will talk about nothing, loudly, where everyone can hear." He turned and climbed the steps. "Come. Before the barge comes through. They pass at four."

"What happens if they see us?" asked Orsen. Her voice was low, careful, the voice of a woman who has learned that questions cost.

Rahman paused at the top of the steps without turning around.

"Nothing," he said. "That is what happens. Nothing is what they pay for."

* * *

We drank tea in Rahman's house, which sat highest on the stilts and therefore had the best view of the river and the worst view of the escarpment, and I understood that this was deliberate. You do not want to look at what you sold. The tea was bitter and good. The twins ate enough rice for four grown men each, and Dara asked questions about fishing until Sef kicked her under the table, and Mei-Lin sat with her hands around her cup saying nothing in the way that makes other people say too much.

It was Orsen who broke the silence, and she broke it the way she seemed to break everything — precisely, once, without warning.

"The cave," she said. "On the reef island. The one the sea opens twice a year. Your elders would know it."

Rahman set down his cup very slowly, and I watched him decide, in real time, whether to be the man who remembered or the man who had been paid to forget. Twenty years is a long practice. But he was old, and I think at a certain age the forgetting starts to cost more than the memory.

"We call it the Mouth That Counts," he said. "My grandmother called it that. The sea opens it twice a year, at the lowest tides, and those who enter must leave something inside. Not gold. Not goods." He looked at me. "Memories. A story. A name. Something true. The cave keeps accounts, my grandmother said, and it charges admission, and the price is always the same: you must give up something you cannot get back."

"And people paid it?" Orsen asked.

"People paid it gladly, once. Because the river gave back tenfold. Fish, flood-farming, passage, power." He gestured vaguely upstream, toward the escarpment none of us could see but all of us could feel sitting at the end of the valley like a debt. "The river came down from a source in the high valley. Clean and cold and never failing. There was a village up there. Our mother-village. Kampung Asal, the First Place. My grandmother was born there."

"And then?" Mei-Lin said.

"And then the company came." Rahman's voice did not change, which was somehow worse than if it had broken. "Twenty-two years ago. They came with surveyors and lawyers and a man with a very soft voice who explained, patiently, the way you explain arithmetic to a child, that the source valley contained copper, and that copper was worth more than water, and that we would be compensated. They built the dam. They sealed the source. They flooded—" He stopped. He picked up his cup again and found it empty and put it down again. "They flooded the First Place."

Nobody spoke. Downriver, faintly, I heard the long low horn of a barge passing, right on schedule, four o'clock, punctual as grief.

"They bought silence," Rahman continued, quieter now. "Jobs, at first. Half the village went to work the mine road, the barges, the loading docks. Good wages. Then fewer jobs, and the same wages, and by then the men who remembered the river were old and the young men had never known anything else. And for those who would not forget—" He spread his hands, palms up, an old man's shrug that contains an entire history of fear. "There were accidents. A fire at the fish-drying racks. A boat lost in fair weather. A headman who fell down his own stairs twice. After a while, silence stopped being something they bought and became something we grew ourselves, like a crop. Cheaper for them. We harvest it ourselves every season."

Orsen had not touched her tea. She was looking at the floorboards, and I saw her lips move slightly, the way people move them when they are reading something written somewhere no one else can see. Her notebook. Her maps. I had watched her on the Kestrel for days now, this woman who fixed winches in her sleep and lied about her hometown with the smoothness of poured oil, and I had dived long enough to know salvage when I saw it. She was salvaging herself. God help me, I recognized the wreck.

"The drowned survey team," she said. "Eight years ago. Their records were in the cave."

Something crossed Rahman's face like a shadow of a bird.

"We were told their boat capsized in the delta," he said. "We were told to attend the memorial and we attended. We were told—" He stopped himself, and this time the stopping was visible, effortful, a man hauling on a rope. "Pak Uda. You are old. I am old. Tell me why you have really come."

I looked at him. I looked at the stamped planks outside the window, at the new jetty the company had given them in exchange for the old world, and I thought about what forty years underwater teaches you, and I decided that some things are worth the breath.

"We've come to open the accounts," I said.

* * *

Afterward — after the plans sketched in tea-stains on the table, after Rahman sent his grandson running to fetch two more elders whose memories reached further back, after the long slow business of old men comparing what they had been told never happened — I walked down to the jetty alone to check the Kestrel's lines, because checking lines is what I do instead of praying, and it is nearly the same thing.

That was when I saw the child.

She was maybe eight, small and quick, sitting cross-legged at the jetty's end with her feet dangling over the brown water, carving something with a knife far too big for her. I sat down beside her the way you sit down beside children, which is to say slowly and without looking directly at them, as though you are merely tired and they happen to be furniture.

"That knife will take your toes off," I said.

"It hasn't yet," she said. "Grandfather says the river takes toes, not knives."

"Your grandfather sounds wise."

"He says the river used to be ours." She didn't look up. Chips of pale wood fell between her feet and drifted away. "He says before I was born it sang at night and now it only talks."

I had no answer for that, so I gave her the only thing I had, which was silence, and she accepted it the way children accept silence, which is completely.

When she finished, she held the carving out to me first — an old courtesy, offering to elders — and I saw it was a fish, rough-made, beautiful the way only rough-made things can be, with a hole bored through it for a cord. But she took it back before my hand closed.

"Not for you," she said. "For her."

"For who?"

"The woman who doesn't sleep." She nodded toward the Kestrel, where Orsen sat on the foredeck with her charts spread around her knees in the last light, moving her finger along lines only she could see. "Grandfather says she came out of the Mouth That Counts. Grandfather says nobody comes out of the Mouth unless the river wants them." The child threaded a cord through the fish and stood up, quick as a kingfisher. "It's a passage token. Old law. Older than the company." She looked at me with the terrible directness of the young. "It means nobody in any village on this river can turn her away. Even if they're afraid. Especially if they're afraid."

She walked down the jetty toward the Kestrel, small feet loud on the company's stamped planks, and I stayed where I was because old men know when a moment is not theirs.

I watched her hold the token up to Orsen. I watched Orsen go still — that particular stillness of hers, total, like water going glass before wind — and take the little carved fish in both hands as though it were heavier than it was, as though it weighed exactly what it weighed, which was everything. The child said something short. Orsen bowed her head over it. And I sat on the jetty in the falling dark and grieved, quietly, professionally, the way I grieve — for a village that sold its river and called the price fair, for old men who practiced blindness until they believed their own eyes, for a child handing safe passage to a stranger because the child still believed passage could be safe.

Then I heard footsteps behind me, and Mei-Lin settled onto the planks beside me with the groan of a woman who pretends the boat's age is catching up to her and not her own.

"Well?" she said.

"She'll do it," I said. "Whatever you're asking. She'll do it."

"I wasn't asking."

"You were always asking."

Downriver, the barge lights crawled north toward the dam, patient as ants, and above the black line of the escarpment the first stars came out over a valley where a village lay drowned and a river ran silent and a report waited in the dark like a lung waiting for air.

"Pak Uda," Mei-Lin said at last. "Those elders. The ones who remember the source valley. How deep is the water up there?"

I looked at her. Twenty-two years since anyone had asked me that question, and my chest did that thing again, the feeling wearing its costume.

"Deep enough," I said, "to hide a village." I stood, joints announcing themselves. "And shallow enough, if a man is old and stubborn, to bring one back."
