## 9. Charts in a Dead Man's Hand

I've always believed that dead men keep better secrets than living ones. Living men want things — money, forgiveness, another drink. Dead men only want to be understood, and they're patient about it in a way the living never manage.

The brass tube had spent twenty years underwater and come out of it smelling like the inside of an old clock. I'd cleaned it as best I could with diesel and a rag Sef would have objected to me using, and now I sat cross-legged on the Kestrel's deck plates under a hurricane lamp while the river muttered against the hull below, unrolling the charts inside with the care you'd give a sleeping animal.

The paper was linen-backed. Good stock. Survey-grade, the kind of thing that costs more per sheet than most men make in a week, which told me before I read a single line that this hadn't been some government afterthought. Someone had funded this expedition properly. Someone had wanted answers.

The name stamped across the header case was HALLORAN & VOSS HYDROGRAPHIC SURVEYS, and beneath it, in smaller type: *Commissioned by Karang Consolidated Mining — Confidential.*

Voss.

The name went through me like a fishhook. Ravel Voss was the man the salvagers whispered about when they thought I wasn't listening — the consortium's enforcer, the reason Pak Uda checked the horizon twice as often as he needed to. I'd assumed the surname was coincidence. Names repeat. Rivers repeat. That's practically their whole personality.

But Halloran.

That name didn't repeat. That name *echoed*.

* * *

My mentor — the man who taught me to read water the way other people read faces — was named Elias Wren. He was old-school, trained in the days when survey work meant weeks in canvas tents and instruments you calibrated by candlelight, and he had one story he told exactly once, late at night, after too much of whatever he kept in the dented flask he pretended I didn't know about.

He'd been junior on a crew once, he said. A hydrographic survey in island country, far south. The lead surveyor was the best he ever worked under — a man who could look at a river and tell you its whole biography. And one morning the lead surveyor's boat came back empty, drifting, and the commission was quietly closed, and everyone got paid a bonus to stop asking questions, and Elias took the bonus because he was twenty-three and hungry, and he had hated himself for it for forty years.

He never said the man's name. He said, "You have his hands, girl. I saw them the first week. You hold a pencil like him."

At the time I'd taken it as a compliment about my penmanship. It took me until tonight, sitting over a dead man's charts by lamplight, to understand he'd been telling me something else entirely. He'd been telling me I was carrying a ghost's handwriting.

Because I knew these annotations. Not the words — the *hand*. The small, slanted numerals with the crossed sevens. The way elevation marks were written with a tiny hook on the terminal stroke, like the number was reaching for something just out of frame. I'd spent three years copying that style without knowing why, because Elias made me practice it until my field notes looked like his, until they looked like —

Until they looked like these.

"Damn it, Elias," I said quietly, to no one, to a man probably dead by now, to a mentor who had handed me a dead man's habits like an inheritance nobody had inventoried.

The lamp hissed. Somewhere aft, Dara was arguing with her brother about fuel lines, their voices doing the thing siblings' voices do where the argument is mostly affection wearing armor. I pulled my notebook out — my real notebook, the map of closed lines, the thing I'd guarded harder than my own name — and laid it beside the surveyor's sheets.

The parallels were worse than handwriting.

* * *

Here is what I do, or what I did, before a storm picked me up and threw me into this story: I map railways that don't exist anymore. Spurs abandoned, lines closed, stations gone to rust and jungle. People assume it's a hobby. A quirk. Nobody asks twice why a woman would spend her life cataloguing the bones of infrastructure, because the answer they imagine — nostalgia, eccentricity — is more comfortable than the truth.

The truth is that closed lines are where bodies get buried. Not always literally. But every defunct spur I've ever walked had a reason it died, and the reasons were never "the ore ran out." They were lawsuits settled quietly. Inspectors paid off. Routes rerouted because someone upstream found something in the rock they didn't want found, or downstream found something in the water. A closed line is a company's confession written in steel and then locked in a drawer. I learned to read them because I was running from one myself — from a bridge, from a collapse, from a report I signed that got rewritten above my head — and somewhere along the way running became method, method became obsession, and obsession became the only family I allowed myself.

And here, spread on the deck of a salvage boat in the middle of a river I had no business being on, was proof that I'd been following this man's trail my entire career without knowing it.

His annotations flagged anomalies the same way mine did. Where I wrote *closed 1911 — no economic justification*, he wrote *line terminated — no geological justification*. Where I traced spurs that ended in nothing, he traced survey transects that ended in nothing — deliberately truncated, the data amputated at exactly the points where it got interesting. We were both, in our separate decades and separate oceans, mapping the same phenomenon: the shape of what powerful people decided should not be known.

I turned to the last sheet, the one the tube had been sealed around, and felt the temperature of the evening drop even though the air hadn't moved.

It was the source survey. The river's headwaters, upstream of everything, upstream of the village, upstream of the dam the elders said didn't exist officially and existed absolutely in fact. And across the face of it, in that hooked, reaching hand, were structural calculations so dense they looked like weather.

Halloran — if it was Halloran; Elias had never given me the name but the dates fit, the region fit, the *hand* fit — had surveyed the dam site two years after its completion. And he had found what the consortium had paid him, presumably, to confirm was safe.

He had found that it was dying.

* * *

I'm an engineer. Let me translate the dead man's arithmetic into plain language, because plain language is the one thing the people who build these monuments never use.

Concrete dams don't fail all at once. They fail the way trust fails — slowly, invisibly, through channels nobody inspects because inspecting would mean admitting the channels exist. Water gets into the structure through hairline fractures. The water freezes, or dissolves minerals, or simply pushes, season after season, and the fracture becomes a vein, and the vein becomes a void, and the void becomes a decision the dam makes on its own schedule, usually at three in the morning, usually in the wet season, usually with a village asleep downstream.

Halloran's numbers showed uplift pressures at the dam's base running nearly double the design allowance. His sketches showed drainage galleries silted shut — never maintained, because maintenance doesn't photograph well in annual reports. His stress diagrams showed the spillway gates corroded past the point where they could relieve pressure in a flood year. And in the margin, pressed so hard the pencil had nearly torn the linen, he had written a single sentence:

*Failure within 20 years. Sooner if the monsoon exceeds design flood. This must go to the Ministry regardless of contract terms.*

Below that, in different ink — administrative ink, the tidy hand of a clerk doing his job — a stamp: *RECEIVED. UNDER REVIEW.*

Under review for twenty years. That's how long the review takes when the reviewer owns the dam, the ministry, the gunboats, and the law.

And then there was the last page. It wasn't engineering. It was a letter, half-finished, the ink feathered where water had gotten to it before the tube sealed, or after — I couldn't tell, and it mattered more than I wanted it to.

*To whoever finds this. If the sea has given you this tube, then the sea has better judgment than the men who sent me up this river. I have copies. I have hidden them where they told me there was nothing worth surveying, which is how I knew exactly where to hide them. If I do not come down from the valley, it will be because the current was strong. Remember that phrase. It is what they will say. The current was strong. The weather turned. These things happen. In this country, rivers are blamed for everything except the one thing men did to them.*

*I was told to confirm the dam was sound. I confirmed instead that it is a loaded weapon aimed at four thousand people, and I have discovered that finding a thing is not the same as being permitted to know it.*

The letter stopped there. No signature. Just the date, twenty years gone, and beneath the date — I had to hold it to the lamp, turn it, catch the light raking low across the fibers — a faint indentation. He'd written something underneath on another sheet and pressed hard enough to leave a ghost. Three characters. A location shorthand, surveyor's style, grid-abbreviated.

I knew the format. Of course I knew the format. I'd been using it since I was nineteen years old, because Elias drilled it into me, because *his* lead surveyor drilled it into him.

The ghost-read said: *VALLEY — SOURCE — B-7.*

The drowned village. The place behind the dam. The place the cave charts marked FORBIDDEN.

* * *

I sat with it for a long time. Long enough that the argument aft had resolved itself into laughter, long enough that the lamp needed trimming, long enough that Mei-Lin's shadow fell across my charts and stayed there.

"You haven't slept," she said. It wasn't a question. Mei-Lin didn't traffic much in questions that could be answered with comfort.

"I found something."

"You found something two nights ago in a hole in the world. You've been strange ever since." She crouched, joints cracking, and looked at the papers upside down, which she apparently understood anyway, because after a moment she said, "That's consortium stationery."

"It's a murder weapon," I said. "Or the autopsy report. Depends which end of it you're standing on."

She waited. She was good at waiting. It was, I suspected, how she'd gotten a boat and a crew and a reputation in waters where the consortium owned everything else — by being the person still standing there patiently when everyone else had been bought, frightened, or sunk.

So I gave it to her. All of it. The uplift pressures, the silted galleries, the letter, the ghost-indentation, and then — because lying to her had started to cost more than the truth, and I noticed I resented that, and I noticed even more that I resented it *less than I expected* — I gave her the rest. Elias. The training. The handwriting. The way my maps of dead railway lines matched this dead man's maps of a dead river like two halves of a torn photograph.

"So the man who taught you," Mei-Lin said slowly, "was taught by the man they killed."

"That appears to be the shape of it."

"And you've spent your career walking the kinds of places he walked. Closed lines. Buried routes." She tapped my notebook with one blunt finger. "Looking for what? Him?"

"I told myself I was looking for patterns."

"What were you actually looking for?"

The honest answer sat in my chest like swallowed ballast. What had I been looking for, all those years, all those rusted junctions and flooded cuttings? Absolution, maybe. Proof that the thing done to me — the rewritten report, the blame, the exile — was systemic rather than personal. That I hadn't failed; that the game was rigged at a depth no individual engineer could reach. Every closed line I documented was evidence for the defense in a trial nobody had scheduled.

"A reason," I said finally. "A reason it wasn't my fault. Which is a coward's errand, if you're wondering. You can walk ten thousand kilometers of dead track and the bridge still falls the same way."

Mei-Lin studied me with those harbor-dredging eyes of hers. "Or," she said, "you can walk ten thousand kilometers and find out the same men who broke you are breaking this river right now, upstream, with a dam full of dead man's arithmetic and four thousand people asleep below it." She stood, knees protesting. "Doesn't sound like a coward's errand anymore. Sounds like jurisdiction."

She left me with that. Mei-Lin had a genius for leaving people with things — conclusions, chores, emotional reckonings — and walking away before they could negotiate.

* * *

I didn't sleep either. Instead I did what engineers do with grief, which is convert it into load calculations.

By gray dawn I had built the failure model in my notebook, working from Halloran's twenty-year-old figures and adjusting for two decades of continued neglect, which was generous, because neglect compounds faster than interest. My numbers were worse than his. His said twenty years. Mine said the dam was living on borrowed seasons, and the monsoon building black over the western escarpment looked like a creditor with a short temper.

Four thousand people downstream. One village directly in the flood path, plus the fishing camps, plus the jetty the Kestrel's crew traded through, plus — I caught myself doing it — plus Dara and Sef's laughing argument, plus Pak Uda's careful horizons, plus Mei-Lin's patience. Somewhere between the cave and the oxbow, the casualty list had stopped being abstract, and I resented how easily that had happened, how little resistance I'd put up. Twenty years of never staying anywhere long enough to be responsible for anyone, undone by a winch repair and a shared fire.

Sef found me at first light, drawn by the smell of lamp oil and obsession the way his sister was drawn by trouble. He looked at the charts, then at my notebook, then at me, and did the twin thing where his face went carefully blank because Dara was going to want the story first.

"The numbers are bad?" he asked.

"The numbers are a confession," I said. "Written by a murdered man, in my teacher's teacher's handwriting, about a dam that's going to kill a valley unless we move first."

Sef absorbed this with the practical calm of a man whose job is keeping machinery alive past its design life. "So we need the originals he hid. Copies can be disputed. A sealed original with chain of custody—" He stopped. Looked at the ghost-indentation again. "B-7. That's behind the dam, isn't it. In the drowned valley."

"It's in the drowned valley."

"Then we should tell Mei-Lin to start the engine," Sef said, "and you should tell us why you look like someone just mailed you a letter from your own grave."

Because that was the thing, wasn't it. That was the thing I hadn't said out loud yet, even to myself, even to the lamp. Halloran had hidden his copies where the consortium said there was nothing worth surveying. I had spent my life walking where the powerful said nothing worth knowing lived. Same instinct. Same compass heading. The same hand, passed down through Elias like a bloodline, arriving in this river valley twenty years late and one woman short of a full survey crew.

The past I'd been fleeing hadn't been chasing me at all. It had been *leading* me — trail marker by trail marker, closed line by closed line — straight upstream to this.

Above the eastern ridge, the monsoon stacked itself higher, purple and patient. Downstream, four thousand people were waking up to a river that looked calm and wasn't. And somewhere south, I'd have bet my notebook on it, a gunboat was turning north with a man named Voss aboard, coming to close the last open line of all.

I rolled the dead man's charts, sealed the tube, and went to find Mei-Lin.

"We leave within the hour," I told her. "And Mei-Lin — whatever's ahead of us, we're not scouting it alone anymore. Whatever this costs, the crew stays together."

She raised an eyebrow at that, hearing exactly what I'd just admitted and choosing, mercifully, not to say it aloud.

"Engine's already warm," she said.
