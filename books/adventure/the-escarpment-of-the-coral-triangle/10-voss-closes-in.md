## 10. Voss Closes In

*Voss*

The supply post at Batu Enam burned the way I liked all my work to burn: thoroughly, and with paperwork.

Not that there would be paperwork this time. The consortium's ledgers recorded a fuel depot, two drums of diesel, a case of engine parts, and a radio set assigned to a man named Hario, who had been selling diesel to river salvagers at a markup since before I took this posting. Hario was not present when I arrived, which was either cowardice or wisdom, and I had stopped distinguishing between the two years ago. What remained was the depot, and the depot's contents, and the message their absence would send to anyone traveling upstream behind me.

I poured the diesel myself. I have never trusted a subordinate to do a thorough job of anything I could do better, and burning things is a craft like any other. You want the fuel to spread before the flame draws attention. You want the structure to fail inward, so the fire eats its own evidence. You want, above all, to be gone before anyone comes to stand in the warm ash and ask questions.

I was gone. I stood on the far bank, in the shade of a durian tree whose fruit would taste of smoke this season, and watched the roof timbers go up like a signal flare that read, in a language only one woman on this river would understand fluently: *I know where you are going, and I have already been there.*

Orsen Zhou. I had spent four days with her file, and I will say this for the dead surveyor's ghost — he had kept good company, or at least interesting company. A railroad engineer with a talent for disappearing. A name attached to a bridge inquiry in the north that had ended in the professional destruction of a woman who, according to the technical record, had done nothing wrong. The consortium's interest in her had begun as routine: a loose end from a drowned survey team, a stranger asking after charts in a tide cave. It had become something else when my contact in the port authority described her notebook.

A map of closed lines. Every abandoned rail spur in the archipelago, catalogued in a hand neat enough to be read at gunpoint. I had seen such maps before. I had burned two of them, in fact, in a previous life, along with the men who kept them. There is a lineage to that kind of obsession, a school of training that the consortium spent a decade buying up and burying, because a man who can read a dead railway can read a living one, and a man who can read a living railway can read what the railway was built to carry away.

Her mentor had trained under the same man I had. That was in the file too, buried in a personnel cross-reference nobody below director level was supposed to read. I read everything. It is the only advantage I have ever permitted myself.

The fire settled into its long work. Somewhere downstream — two days, perhaps three, if the Kestrel's engines were as tired as her hull — a salvage boat was fighting the current with a hold full of cargo that was about to become very inconvenient for everyone involved. I did not know yet what the drowned surveyor had hidden in the valley behind the dam. I knew only that the consortium wanted it found before anyone else did, and that "found" and "destroyed" were, in this case, the same word.

I turned upstream and began to walk.

There was a toll bridge at the Kerangan narrows. There was a man there who owed the consortium money and a rifle rack that owed the consortium nothing. By the time the Kestrel reached the narrows, I intended for the river to have exactly one door, and for me to be standing in it.

* * *

*Mei-Lin*

The smoke found us before the smell did — a gray smudge on the southern horizon that Sef spotted from the wheelhouse and Dara confirmed from the bow with the binoculars, and for a long moment nobody said anything, because we all knew what a smudge like that meant on a river with only three supply posts along it.

"Batu Enam," Dara said.

I took the binoculars. The smoke was too far to see detail, but I had watched enough fires on this river to read their posture. This one was burning fat and low, a fuel fire, a depot fire. Somebody had gotten there ahead of us and turned our next stop into a lesson.

"Voss," Orsen said.

She was standing at my shoulder, which I had not invited and had not forbidden, and she said the name the way you'd name a weather system. Calm. Already calculating.

"You know him?" I asked.

"I know the type." She handed the binoculars back. "He'll have burned the diesel and the parts. He'll have left the radio, if the radio's broken, and the ledger, if the ledger makes Hario look like the thief Hario is. He's not destroying supplies. He's destroying our *schedule*."

I looked at her. "You've met this man."

"I've read his work." Something crossed her face — that flicker she got whenever her past leaned against the door, the look of a woman checking that all her exits were still marked. "Consortia don't hire arsonists. They hire accountants who burn things."

Well. I had run the Kestrel up and down this river for nineteen years, and I had survived three owners, two governments, and one marriage, and in all that time I had learned exactly one management technique that never failed: when a passenger starts telling you the truth unprompted, shut up and listen.

"Options," I said, to the wheelhouse in general.

"Option one," Sef said, "we turn around."

"No," said Dara.

"I'm listing *options*, not holding a vote."

"Option one is still no."

"Option two," Orsen said, over both of them, "we run light. Dump everything in the hold that isn't fuel or food, run the engines hot, make the narrows in thirty hours instead of forty. We lose the salvage contract with the Tanjung people — they'll dock us for the weight shortfall, and the copper ingots are half our margin."

"And option three?" I asked, though I already knew. I had watched her look at the smoke for too long. People who stare at fires are either remembering one or planning one.

"Option three, someone goes ahead on foot along the portage trail and scouts the narrows before we commit. If the bridge is held, we know it before we're inside rifle range. If it's clear, we've saved six hours."

The wheelhouse went quiet in the particular way it goes quiet when everyone has arrived at the same bad idea and is waiting to see who says it first.

"You're volunteering," I said.

"I'm the fastest walker aboard. I'm the one who knows what a consortium forward post looks like. And I'm the reason he's on this river at all." She said it flat, no drama in it, which was somehow worse. "Every hour he spends looking for me is an hour he isn't spending on you."

I want it recorded, in whatever ledger the river keeps, that I considered it. I considered it seriously. There was a version of me — the version from ten years ago, before Pak Uda fished me out of the Tanjung channel with nothing but a grudge and a boat — who would have taken that offer, because that version of me understood arithmetic and nothing else. One life risked against five. A clean trade. The kind of trade the consortium itself would make, and had made, and would make again tomorrow, in offices with air conditioning.

But I had watched this woman for two weeks. I had watched her fix our winch without being asked and then pretend she hadn't. I had watched her teach Dara to read a pressure gauge by drawing diagrams in spilled tea. I had watched her stand watch when it wasn't her turn, and give away her coffee when she thought nobody saw, and sleep curled against the rail like a woman who had never once in her life expected a wall to hold.

She was doing the thing that people do when they have been alone too long. She was pricing herself separately from the rest of us.

"No," I said.

"Mei-Lin—"

"You don't get to scout alone. You don't get to walk off this boat and be brave in the jungle by yourself. That's not how this crew works." I turned to face her fully, because she deserved to be looked at while I said it. "You want to know why? Because I ran numbers for ten years, Orsen. I know every argument you're making. I made them better, and I made them alone, and I will tell you what it bought me: a boat with a broken winch and a crew of strangers who had no reason to come back. Then I stopped trading and started keeping, and now I have a crew that argues with me at six in the morning about turning around, which is the most valuable cargo I have ever carried."

"Sentimental," Sef said.

"Shut up, Sef."

"Sentimental and correct," Sef said.

Orsen was looking at me with an expression I couldn't read — or could read too well, maybe, the look of someone being handed something they'd stopped believing existed. "The narrows are a kill box," she said finally. "If Voss holds the bridge, running it blind gets people killed."

"So we don't run it blind." I turned to the twins. "Sef — the skiff. How fast can you get her running quiet?"

Sef grinned, which on Sef meant he'd already done the math. "If I strip her weight and Dara stops complaining about the carburetor long enough to let me tune it — quiet enough."

"I don't complain about the carburetor," Dara said. "I complain about the *philosophy* of the carburetor. It's a fundamentally dishonest machine."

"Two of you take the skiff up the channel after dark," I said. "Not alone — together. You scout the narrows, you count rifles, you come back. Nobody goes anywhere alone on this river again. That's the new law of the Kestrel, and I'll throw the first person overboard who breaks it."

"Even you?" Dara asked.

"*Especially* me."

Orsen hadn't moved. "You're trusting the twins with the approach."

"I'm trusting the twins with each other. It's the same thing." I looked at her, and I softened it as much as I ever soften anything, which is to say about four percent. "And I'm keeping you where I can see you. Not because I doubt you. Because you keep volunteering to be a loose end, and I have decided you're not one anymore. You're crew. Crew stays on the boat unless crew comes back."

For a moment she said nothing at all, and I thought she might argue — she had the jaw set for it, the engineer's jaw, the one that meets resistance and starts calculating load paths. But then something in her let go. It was small. It was the kind of thing you'd miss if you hadn't spent nineteen years watching people decide whether to trust you.

"Thirty hours to the narrows," she said. "If I'm running the engines hot, I'll need to re-pack the port shaft bearing by dawn. Sef, you'll help me."

"Will I?"

"You'll hold the light and complain about the philosophy of bearings."

"That's fair," Sef said.

* * *

*Voss*

The man at the toll bridge was called Berto, and he was afraid, which was useful. Fear is the cheapest material in the world and the only one that never runs out. I gave him a photograph of Orsen Zhou, a description of the Kestrel, and instructions that amounted to a single sentence: *no boat passes the narrows without my word.*

"And if they don't stop?" Berto asked. He had the look of a man who had held a rifle for twenty years without ever wanting to find out what it was for.

"They'll stop. The woman aboard is an engineer. Engineers don't run kill boxes. They calculate them." I set a second photograph on his table — this one of a woman and two children, taken outside a school in Tanjung, and I did not explain it, because explanations are for people who need them. "Fuel the lamp on the bridge. Keep it lit. When you see the boat, signal the far bank."

He looked at the photograph of his family for a long time. I have found that people always look longest at the thing they are being asked to trade, as if weighing it, as if the weighing has ever once gone any other way.

"And after?" he said. "After they pass — or don't pass. What then?"

"Then the consortium's account with you is settled," I said, "and you will never see me again."

It was not quite a lie. I have made a career of never quite lying. It is more efficient than the truth and far more durable.

I walked out onto the bridge as the light went down. The river ran brown and heavy below me, carrying the day's heat seaward, and somewhere up that current — thirty hours, thirty-five, the Kestrel's engines were old and her captain was proud — five people were deciding, right now, whether to come through the door I had built for them.

I thought about the file again. The bridge in the north. The inquiry. A woman blamed for a collapse she had warned them about, in a report they had buried, in an industry the consortium owned. I had read the original inspection record that afternoon in the port office — it was in the same archive as the surveyor's file, filed by the same clerk, and I read everything. She had flagged the scours. She had flagged them in writing, with calculations. They had buried her instead of the river's evidence, and when the river produced it anyway, they had needed someone to blame, and they had chosen the one person whose warnings proved the crime.

I understood her, is what I am trying to record. I understood her completely. We had both been made into instruments by the same machine, and the difference between us was only that she had run and I had stayed, and I had stayed because I had learned, early, that running is just a slower way of being caught.

The lamp on the bridge caught. Berto had done as he was told. Its light lay on the water like a coin at the bottom of a well.

Let her come. Let the whole strange little family come — the captain with her arithmetic of loyalty, the twins with their honest machine-hatred, the old diver, the engineer with her dead men's charts. I had spent my whole career closing doors, and I had grown very good at it, and I had never once, until this river, wondered what it was that I kept locking on the other side.

The lamp burned. The river ran. I went to wait.

* * *

*Mei-Lin*

We ran her hot through the night, and by dawn the port bearing was singing the high note that bearings sing just before they become shrapnel, and Orsen had her arms in the engine to the shoulder, and Sef was holding the light and complaining about the philosophy of gaskets, and I stood in the wheelhouse watching my boat become something I hadn't planned for.

Because that was the thing I couldn't put in the log. Nineteen years I had run the Kestrel as a business that occasionally loved me back. Cargo in, cargo out, crew paid, crew kept, crew lost to better offers and worse luck. And now here was a woman who'd been aboard three weeks, elbow-deep in my engine at five in the morning because the twins' skiff needed the port engine running quiet by dark, and Dara was teaching Pak Uda's grandson's card game to nobody, and Pak Uda himself was mending nets on the foredeck and humming, actually humming, and I stood at the wheel with the smoke of Batu Enam long behind us and understood that Voss had already taken something from us whether he knew it or not.

He'd made us hurry. And a boat that hurries stops being careful, and a crew that hurries stops looking after each other, and that is how enforcers win without ever firing a shot.

So I did the one thing I could do about it. I rang the engine down to cruising speed, opened the skiff's fuel line, and told the twins they'd go up the side channel at moonrise, together, and come back by the second bell, together, and that if either of them set foot on the far bank alone I would personally introduce them to the river's opinion of it.

Orsen came up from the engine room wiping her hands, and stood beside me at the wheel, and didn't say anything for a long time. That was new, too. The old Orsen filled silence with exits.

"They'll be all right," she said finally.

"They'll be all right because they're together," I said. "That's the whole trick. There's no other trick."

Ahead of us the river bent north into the narrows, and the water there ran fast and green over drowned trees, and I did not need binoculars to know that somewhere in that green a lamp was lit and a frightened man was watching for us. But the twins would count the rifles before the rifles counted us. And whatever door Voss had built at the Kerangan narrows, we would not walk through it like customers.

We'd come through it like crew.

Below, in the engine room, the port bearing turned over, quiet as a held breath, and the Kestrel ran on into the narrowing dark.
