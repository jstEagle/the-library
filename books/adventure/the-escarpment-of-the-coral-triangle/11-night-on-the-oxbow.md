## 11. Night on the Oxbow

My brother says I talk too much, which is rich coming from a man who once narrated an entire engine rebuild to a monkey. But even Sef would agree that tonight on the oxbow, the Kestrel sat so quiet in her mooring that talking felt like a kind of trespass.

The river had bent back on itself a few kilometers downstream — a great lazy loop where the current forgot which way it was going and the water went glass-still. Mei-Lin called it an oxbow. Pak Uda called it *the river thinking*. Either way, we'd tucked into it at dusk with our fuel gauge reading like a bad joke, and Mei-Lin had declared a rest day with the particular grimness of a woman who'd rather be moving but knows the boat needs sleep more than she does.

Which is how six people who had known each other for less than two weeks came to be sitting around a driftwood fire on a mudbank, passing a bottle of palm arrack that Pak Uda produced from somewhere in his gear with the smugness of a magician who never reveals the trick.

I should explain something about me and fires. I like them. I like them too much, maybe. Sef says it's because I'm a fire sign, whatever that means; he read my horoscope out of a magazine three years old and has treated it as diagnostic ever since. But really I think it's because a fire is the only conversation that doesn't demand anything of you. You feed it, it glows, nobody asks where you're from or what you're running from or why you flinch when someone says the word *consortium* too loudly.

And Orsen Zhou flinched at that word. I'd been watching her do it for days.

Don't get me wrong — watching her wasn't hard. She was the kind of person you watched without meaning to, the way you watch weather come in over water. She sat apart from the fire circle by exactly one meter, close enough to belong, far enough to deny it. She'd done that since the night she fixed our winch. Closer every day, that gap. A centimeter here, a hand's width there. If you charted it, you'd have gotten a nice smooth curve converging on the fire. I know, because I did chart it, in my head, because I'm the twin who notices things and Sef is the twin who fixes things, and between us we make one complete person.

Tonight the gap was smaller than usual. That's what made me brave. Or stupid. With me those are often the same setting.

* * *

It started, as most dangerous things on the Kestrel do, with Pak Uda telling a story.

"Twenty years I dove these rivers," he said, turning the arrack bottle slowly in his hands like he was reading it. "You know what I found down there, once? A wedding party."

Sef leaned forward. He always leaned forward for Uda's stories; my brother has the emotional range of a diesel injector but Uda unlocks him somehow. "A whole wedding?"

"Tables. Chairs. The bride's chair still standing. Some village upstream flooded when they built the dam — not *the* dam," he said, and glanced at Orsen, careful, "an older one, before your time. Whole valley drowned. And I'm down there in the green dark, and there's this table set for a feast that never got eaten. Plates still on it. I took one plate." He patted his chest pack, where he kept things he never showed anyone. "Felt like stealing from ghosts. Still got it. Can't throw it away either."

Nobody spoke for a while after that. The fire cracked. Somewhere across the loop a bird made a sound like a hinge that needed oiling.

Then Mei-Lin, who has the conversational subtlety of a barge horn, said: "Zhou. Your turn."

Orsen looked up. "My turn for what?"

"A story. Everyone pays admission around this fire. That's the rule." There was no such rule. Mei-Lin was inventing it in real time, and we all knew better than to contradict a captain mid-legislation. "Something true. Doesn't have to be big. Dara will go first if you need a demonstration of how low the bar goes."

"Hey," I said.

"It's true," said Sef, loyally.

So I told the story about the time Sef and I were sixteen and rebuilt a generator out of scrap on our uncle's dock, and it worked beautifully right up until it didn't, and the resulting fireball took the eyebrows off both of us and singed our uncle's prize rooster into temporary baldness. The rooster lived. Its dignity did not. It held a grudge against all machinery until the day it died, attacking anything with an engine, including, memorably, a parked and entirely innocent bicycle.

Orsen laughed. Actually laughed — a short, surprised sound, like she'd been ambushed by her own mouth. And I thought: *there she is.* Because you catch people in their laughs. A laugh is a door left open while the person inside thinks no one's looking.

Mei-Lin paid her toll next — a story about her first command, age twenty-four, running contraband refrigerators up a river in a boat she'd bought with money she refuses to explain to this day. "The buyer met me at the dock wearing a suit worth more than my boat," she said, "and he counted the cash out slow, like he wanted me to ask. I didn't ask. Best decision of my career. Second best was selling the refrigerators before anyone opened one."

"What was in them?" Sef asked.

"You don't want to be the kind of person who knows that," Mei-Lin said serenely, and drank.

Pak Uda told a long, winding thing about a crocodile he'd known named Tuah who used to let him ride the current alongside his dive line, and whether it was true or embellished by forty years of retelling, nobody cared, because Uda tells stories the way the river moves — you go along.

And then the circle turned, the way circles do, and everyone was looking at Orsen.

She felt it land. I watched her feel it land — the small stillness that came over her, the way her thumb found the edge of her notebook in her jacket pocket, worrying it like a talisman. That notebook. She carried it everywhere. I'd caught her cataloging dead rail lines in it on the third night, hunched over it by lamplight like a monk illuminating a manuscript of ghosts. Abandoned spurs. Closed lines. Stations with no trains. It was the loneliest document I'd ever seen, and I say that as someone who once found Sef's childhood diary and read exactly one entry before deciding some doors are load-bearing and must not be touched.

"I don't really have stories," Orsen said.

"Everyone has stories," said Pak Uda gently. "Some people just keep theirs locked in cargo holds."

"That's a very specific metaphor," I said.

"I've been alive a long time. Everything becomes specific."

The fire popped. Orsen looked at it instead of at us, and I could see her doing arithmetic — the same arithmetic I'd seen her do at every crossroads since she'd come aboard. Weighing exits. Counting costs. Deciding how much truth she could spend and still afford to run if she needed to.

And here's the thing about that arithmetic: I recognized it. Not because I've lived it, but because Sef and I grew up watching our mother do it, after our father's debts came due and the men came to the house and our mother learned to calculate, always, the distance to the nearest door. Some people learn math that way. It stays in the hands. You can see it in how they sit in rooms.

"Okay," Orsen said finally. "One story. And then we never speak of it again."

"Deal," said Mei-Lin instantly, in the tone of a woman who absolutely intended to speak of it again.

* * *

"There was a bridge," Orsen said.

Her voice had changed. Lower. Flatter. The voice of someone reading from a report about their own life.

"Ten years ago. A rail bridge over the Serpent River gorge, up in the northern territories. I was twenty-six. Junior structural engineer, second year on the job, and I'll tell you honestly — I was good. Not talented-good. *Careful*-good. The kind of engineer who checks everything twice and then checks the check. My mentor used to say I calculated like other people breathe."

The firelight moved on her face. Nobody interrupted. Even Mei-Lin, who interrupts professionally, kept still.

"The bridge was called the Kestrel Span. Funny, that. I didn't pick the name of your boat, before anyone asks." A ghost of a smile, gone fast. "It was a replacement span. The old bridge underneath was eighty years old and the consortium that owned the line — different consortium, same species — wanted the new one built fast and cheap. Standard story. The survey data came in wrong. I flagged it. I flagged it in writing, three times. Cracked footings on the north pier, settlement readings that didn't match the soil reports, and a load model that assumed freight tonnage half of what actually ran that line. I documented all of it. Sent it up the chain. Got back a memo saying the schedule was non-negotiable and my concerns had been 'noted.'"

"Noted," repeated Sef, with the flat disgust of an engineer hearing a dirty word.

"Noted," Orsen agreed. "They poured the concrete anyway. They hung the span anyway. And eleven months after opening — eleven months, during which I'd filed two more memos and been formally reprimanded for 'disruptive negativity' — a loaded ore train hit the north pier at speed and the whole thing came down into the gorge. Forty-one tons of locomotive. Three crew. Two survived."

The arrack bottle stopped circulating. I don't think anyone was breathing much.

"They needed someone to blame," Orsen said. "And the convenient someone was the junior engineer who'd signed the inspection reports — because yes, my signature was on them, because that's how it works, you sign what the chain above you has already decided. What nobody mentioned in the inquiry was my memos. Those got lost. Misfiled. Found later, altered. By the time the hearing happened, the record showed a careless engineer who'd missed obvious defects, and showed nothing at all about the pressure from above, the falsified surveys, the schedule that mattered more than the pier footings. The consortium settled with the families quietly. The executives got bonuses. I got struck off the register and a name that followed me through every port in the territories."

She was quiet a moment. The fire ate a knot of driftwood and spat.

"So I ran. First to another territory, then another coast. Took work under other names — surveying, salvage assessment, anything with numbers and steel and no humans attached. Started keeping the notebook." Her hand went to her pocket again, automatic. "Every closed rail line I could find. Every dead spur, every abandoned junction. Partly it was work — derelict infrastructure is good cover for a woman who doesn't want to be found, lots of places with no people. But mostly—" She stopped. Started again. "Mostly I think I was mapping my own exile. Drawing the shape of everywhere I couldn't stay. A bridge engineer who can't look at bridges. An engineer who can't sign her name. You end up keeping a map of all the ways out, and after a while the map is all you are."

"And the memos?" Pak Uda asked softly. "The real ones. Do you still have copies?"

That question landed harder than any of us expected. I saw it hit her — actually hit, like a wave against a hull. She stared at Uda for a long moment.

"No," she said. "No. The originals were confiscated in the inquiry. Any drafts I kept were in a storage unit I abandoned in Vellano port eight years ago when I changed names. Why—"

She stopped. I watched her stop, watched the thought arrive behind her eyes like weather coming over water, and I swear the whole riverbank held its breath with her.

"Why do you ask that way?" she said.

Pak Uda turned the arrack bottle in his hands. "Because the man whose charts you carry," he said, "the drowned surveyor — Aris Wibawa — trained under a mentor. And that mentor's name appears in the old engineering journals. In articles about a bridge. About a young engineer who tried to stop it being built wrong."

The fire cracked. Across the black loop of the oxbow, something slid into the water — a fish, a log, the past coming home.

"His name was Tomas Reyes," Uda said. "He wrote letters for years afterward. To journals, to boards, to anyone who'd print them. Defending a student of his who'd been blamed for the Kestrel Span. He never named her — protecting her, maybe, or maybe just because the consortium owned every court that could hear the case. But he described her work. The checking-twice. The calculating-like-breathing. He said the world had lost its most careful engineer to a lie, and that someday the lie would surface, because lies are heavy, and heavy things come up eventually. Like plates from a drowned table."

Orsen had gone very still. The stillness of someone standing on a pier watching a ship they thought long sunk come back over the horizon.

"He died last year," Uda said. "Out here. Surveying the river for the elders — mapping what the consortium had sealed. His apprentice finished the charts. His apprentice drowned too, in the storm season, with the brass tube aboard. The tube the tide gave you."

"I know his name," Orsen said quietly. "From the stamps on the charts. I thought—" She shook her head. "I thought it was coincidence. Another dead line on another map."

"Nothing on this river is coincidence," said Mei-Lin. "Coincidence is just a pattern you haven't finished reading yet."

Silence again, but a different silence now. Heavier and warmer both, the way a room gets warmer when a window that's been rattling for years finally seats itself shut.

Orsen looked around the fire — at each of us, one by one, and I watched her do it, and I understood she was counting us. Not exits this time. Something else. I don't think she knew what to call it yet. I don't think she'd ever had the word in inventory.

"My mentor's name was Tomas Reyes," she said. "He taught me everything. When the hearing came, he testified for me. They discredited him too. Last I heard, he'd taken work in the islands." Her voice caught, just barely, on the last word. "He came looking for the truth about the dam, and he died with it in his hands, and the sea handed it to me like it was addressed to me personally."

"It was," said Pak Uda simply.

* * *

Later — much later, when the arrack was gone and the fire had burned down to a red philosophy of embers — I found myself sitting next to Orsen at the edge of the bank, both of us looking at the stars doubled in the still water of the oxbow. Above and below, two identical skies, and us stuck in the middle like a seam.

"Sef snores," I said, by way of breaking the silence. "Like a failing compressor. I've slept next to him for twenty-six years. I'm telling you this as a warning and as a cry for help."

"Noted," she said. And then, hearing herself say it, she almost smiled.

We sat a while longer. Then she said, quietly, not looking at me: "Your mother. The one who calculated doors. How did she stop?"

I hadn't mentioned my mother tonight. I checked back through the evening in my head and realized I hadn't said a word about her out loud. I'd only thought it, sitting in the firelight, recognizing the arithmetic in Orsen's hands.

"She didn't," I said. "Not for a long time. Until we got a boat and a crew and she realized the door she kept calculating toward was one she'd already walked through, years before, without noticing. Belonging isn't a place you arrive, she said. It's a place you stop leaving."

Orsen was quiet for a long moment.

"I don't know how to stop," she said. It wasn't self-pity. It was worse than that — it was honest. The flat honesty of an engineer reporting a defect she can't yet fix. "Running is structural for me now. Load-bearing. You take it out and you don't know what falls."

"Then you reinforce around it," I said. "That's the job, isn't it? Nothing collapses all at once. You shore it up, member by member, until the structure carries weight it was never rated for." I shrugged, feeling my ears go hot, grateful for the dark. "I'm the twin who notices things. Sef's the one who fixes them. But sometimes noticing is the fix."

She looked at me then — really looked, firelight and starlight and the doubled sky in her eyes — and nodded once, slow, like an engineer accepting a reading she'd been resisting.

Down the bank, Mei-Lin's voice cut through the dark, captain-loud even at midnight: "Lights! Upstream bend — two of them, moving fast. Not fishing boats."

We were all on our feet before she finished the sentence. Behind us the fire hissed as Sef kicked earth over it, and the doubled sky shattered into ripples as the Kestrel's wake began to turn the still water of the oxbow.

Orsen's notebook was already open in her hands, pencil flying, mapping the bend, the current, the ways out — except this time, I noticed, she was mapping them for all of us.
