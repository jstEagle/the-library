## 12. The Toll Bridge Trap

My sister says I have a gift for knowing when things are about to go wrong. She says it like it's a compliment. It is not a compliment. It's the same as saying I have a gift for being awake during earthquakes.

I felt it that morning before we ever saw the bridge — a wrongness in the river's voice, if water can be said to have one. The Kestrel's hull told me too, little tremors through the deck plates under my bare feet, the engine laboring half a beat off its usual rhythm because Dara and I had been nursing her along on fuel we could not afford to burn and patience we did not have.

"Slower," Mei-Lin said from the wheelhouse, and her voice had that flatness it gets when she's already done the arithmetic and doesn't like the answer.

The river narrowed ahead into a gorge the color of wet slate, and across the throat of it stood the last crossing between us and the high valley: the old toll bridge at Batu Rentong, iron spans gone orange with rust, built by some colonial engineer who never imagined it would outlive the empire and get conscripted by a mining consortium instead.

And standing on it, small with distance but unmistakable in the way a knife is unmistakable, were men with guns.

* * *

Let me back up, because my sister always says I tell stories like I'm reading a wiring diagram, and she's right about that too.

We'd been running upstream for six days by then. Six days of rapids and portage lines and Orsen Zhou quietly becoming the person our crew didn't know it was missing. I want to be clear about something, since this is my chapter and I get to say what's true: I did not trust her at first. Nobody sensible would have. She came to us out of a storm with a dead man's charts and a face like a locked door, and she fixed our winch overnight without being asked, which is either generosity or an audition, and auditions make me nervous.

But somewhere around the third set of rapids, when she talked me through re-pitching the impeller while bullets — no, wait, that comes later. Somewhere around the second set of rapids, when she knelt next to me in the bilge for four hours with her sleeves rolled up and her hands black with grease, and she said, "Your port engine's compression is down ten percent. You've known for months," and I said, "It's a personality quirk," and she laughed — actually laughed, short and surprised, like the sound had escaped custody — somewhere around then, I started trusting her.

Dara trusted her sooner. My sister collects strays the way other people collect stamps, except she never puts them in a book, she just lets them follow her around until they're family. Pak Uda trusted her immediately, on some diver's instinct about people who don't waste breath. Even Mei-Lin, who trusts nobody and bills everybody, had started giving Orsen the good coffee, which aboard the Kestrel is roughly equivalent to a knighthood.

So there we were, six days upriver, running low on fuel and high on the dangerous feeling that we might actually pull this off, and then the river bent through the gorge and showed us the bridge, and the bridge showed us Voss.

* * *

Ravel Voss. I'd heard the name from Pak Uda, who hears everything the river hears. Consortium enforcer. The kind of man who burns supply posts and calls it inventory management. We'd found his work two days back — the smoking ruin of the fuel depot at Kuala Serat, still hot when we passed, our reserve cache inside it turned to slag. That was the arithmetic Mei-Lin had been doing ever since: how far can the Kestrel run on what's left in her tanks?

Not far enough to turn around. That was the answer. So forward it was, and forward brought us to Batu Rentong.

Through the glasses, I counted eight men on the span, a mounted gun on the near tower, and a chain stretched across the channel below the arches — low enough that a boat couldn't pass under, high enough that a boat couldn't pass over without introducing itself to the ironwork. Behind them, a toll station squatted on the bank, stone-built, solid, with a fuel drum or two stacked outside like props in a play about extortion.

"He's not trying to stop us," Orsen said. She was beside me at the rail, glasses up, and her voice had gone quiet and flat, the way it does when the past has reached out and tapped her on the shoulder. "He's trying to make us come to him. He wants the report, and he wants witnesses he can account for."

"You say that like you know him," Dara said.

"I know his type." A pause. "I've spent my whole life being chased by his type."

Mei-Lin killed the engine and let us drift, and for a long moment the only sounds were water worrying the hull and the twins' shared silence — mine and Dara's, the twin frequency, where whole arguments happen without words. Hers said: *this is bad.* Mine said: *yes.* Hers said: *but we're not turning around.* Mine said: *when have we ever.*

"We could portage again," Pak Uda offered, without conviction. None of us wanted to hear it. Three days through jungle with a wounded boat and no fuel to move her even if we carried her around the gorge — it wasn't a plan, it was a eulogy with extra steps.

"They'll have scouts on the ridges," Orsen said. "Portaging means carrying everything past eight rifles in open country."

"So we fight," Dara said, cracking her knuckles, because my sister believes every problem is a nail and she is a hammer with excellent aim.

"No," Orsen said. And then she looked at the river for a while, the way she does — like it's a document she's reading — and something changed in her face. I've watched engineers do this. The moment the problem stops being a wall and starts being a machine. "No. We ask the river to fight for us."

* * *

Here is what Orsen Zhou understood about Batu Rentong that the eight men on the bridge did not, because they were muscle, not engineers, and their boss was a man who thought rivers were scenery:

Upstream of the gorge, maybe two kilometers, the river made a long oxbow bend around a spur of rock. There was an old flood channel cutting across the neck of that spur — we'd seen it the day before, choked with deadfall, dry for years, but a channel nonetheless, cut deep into soft stone by some ancient season when the river ran angry. And above the oxbow, the river was running high with the mountain rains, bank-full, impatient.

A river that wants to be somewhere else, given a door, will take it.

Orsen's plan, laid out on the chart table with grease-pencil arrows and that terrifying calm of hers, went like this: land the shore party — her, me, Dara — above the oxbow, clear the deadfall from the flood channel, and drop a crude timber gate we'd build from Kestrel's spare planking. Let the river do the rest. The flood channel rejoined the main stem just below the toll station, on the downstream side of the bridge footings. Opened all at once, it wouldn't drown anybody — she was very precise about that, checked the elevations twice, made me check them a third time — but it would put a chest-deep torrent through the station yard, sweep the fuel drums, cut the road, and turn the bridge approach into an island.

"It buys us maybe twenty minutes of confusion," she said. "The chain across the channel is the real lock. Someone has to cut it while they're busy watching their boots float away."

"I'll cut it," Pak Uda said, already reaching for his diving knife, because of course he would.

"And the gun on the tower?" Mei-Lin asked.

"The gun will be pointed at whatever's loudest," Orsen said. "So the Kestrel is going to be very loud."

Mei-Lin looked at her for a long moment. Then at the rest of us. Then she said the sentence I think she'd been waiting days to say, the one that meant Orsen Zhou wasn't cargo anymore: "Nobody dies doing this. Not one of you. If it goes wrong, we run, and we live to be stupid again another day."

"That's going on the company letterhead," Dara said.

* * *

We built the gate through the afternoon, sweating in the green shade above the oxbow, sawing and pegging while the river muttered behind its deadfall like a debt collector outside a locked door. Orsen worked faster than both of us together. I noticed things, working next to her. The way she tested every joint twice but apologized to the wood when she split a plank. The way her hands knew knots her mouth didn't have names for. The way she kept glancing downstream toward the bridge, not afraid exactly — calculating, always calculating, the way a person calculates who has been hunted before and knows the value of a head start.

"You ever going to tell us who you really are?" Dara asked her, casual, driving a peg.

"After," Orsen said. Just that. *After.* Like after was a place she was allowing herself to imagine for the first time.

I liked her for that. I also worried about her for that, because I'm the twin who worries; it's in the contract.

At dusk we took position. Mei-Lin and Pak Uda dropped back downriver with the Kestrel, dark-running, no lights, to wait below the bridge. Orsen gave us the countdown by hand: three fingers, two, one.

She pulled the trip line.

I wish I had better words for what happened next than the ones I have, which are mostly mechanical, because I'm an engineer and awe makes me reach for hydraulics. But: the deadfall went first, a crack like the gorge itself clearing its throat, and then the river came through the door it had been waiting twenty years to open. Not a wave, exactly. A decision. Two thousand tons a minute changing its mind all at once, pouring down the old channel in a brown roaring sheet, hitting the main stem below the bridge and piling up against it, and the pile-up walked backward up the banks like something alive looking for room.

The toll station's yard became a lake in ninety seconds. The fuel drums went bobbing away like toys at bath time. Men were shouting, lights swinging, someone firing wildly at nothing, at the dark, at the river, which is the one opponent that has never once been impressed by gunfire.

"Now," Orsen said, and we ran for the boats.

* * *

I won't pretend the crossing was clean. The Kestrel came up through the gorge with her engine wide open and Mei-Lin standing at the wheel like a figurehead with opinions, and the mounted gun on the tower found us almost immediately — tracers stitching the water, close enough that I felt the heat of one pass my cheek and filed it under *later, feel this later, not now.* Pak Uda was already in the water, a shadow with a knife, and I saw him reach the chain and start sawing at the link bolts while bullets rained silver rings around him, and I remember thinking, with the strange clarity of terror: *he's sixty-eight years old and he swims better than I walk.*

Orsen was at the bow with the boathook, guiding Mei-Lin's line through the gap under the arches, shouting numbers — clearances, tide-less river be damned, she'd memorized the span dimensions off the drowned surveyor's charts and was calling them out like a metronome. "Three meters! Hold! Two point five — starboard, starboard, NOW—"

The chain parted with a groan I felt in my teeth.

And then we were through, under the arches, into the dark beyond the bridge, and the gorge walls swallowed the gunfire behind us, and for one full minute nobody on the Kestrel said anything at all. We just breathed. All five of us, breathing together in the wheelhouse light, like a single ridiculous organism.

Then Dara started laughing, and that was that. We were alive, and alive people laugh.

* * *

The cost arrived quietly, the way costs do.

It was Pak Uda who found it, checking the tanks at midnight anchorage. "We're at eleven percent," he said. "Maybe less. The run through the rapids tomorrow, and the climb to the valley pool after — she'll drink most of what's left."

Nobody said the obvious thing, so I said it, because that's my job in this family: "We can't make the source and come back. Not on fuel. Maybe not even make the source."

"We don't need to come back," Mei-Lin said slowly, and I watched the idea take shape on her face the way weather takes shape over water. "If we find what the dead man hid — if it's what Orsen thinks it is — we won't be running anywhere. We'll be broadcasting."

Silence. The kind that has weight.

It was broken by Orsen, who had been standing apart at the stern rail, looking back downriver toward the bridge, where the consortium's lanterns still swung in the flooded ruins of their toll station. When she turned, her face was different. Lighter and heavier at once, if you can picture that. Unlocked.

"There's something you should know," she said. "All of you. About who's chasing us, and why he won't stop now."

And then she told us. Her name — her whole name, spoken aloud like setting down luggage she'd carried so long her shoulder had grown around it. The bridge collapse, seven years ago, the one they pinned on her because she was young and foreign and convenient, while the inspection reports that would have cleared her sat in a consortium subsidiary's filing cabinet, bought and buried. The same consortium. The same signature on the paperwork, she was nearly certain, as the dam upstream. Ravel Voss hadn't been hunting a survey report at all, not originally. He'd been hunting *her*, the loose end, the witness who got away — and the drowned surveyor had died because his findings and her disgrace came from the same poisoned well.

"So when he pulled the file on the survey team," she finished, "and found my name connected to it — he knows exactly who I am now. Tonight proved it. He saw me at the bow. He knows the engineer who humiliated him at Batu Rentong is the engineer who ruined his career seven years ago." She almost smiled. It wasn't a happy smile. It was the smile of a woman watching a door close behind her and realizing she isn't sorry. "There's no more running after this. For any of us. Whatever's in that valley, we see it through."

I looked at my sister. My sister looked at Mei-Lin. Mei-Lin looked at the fuel gauge, then at each of us in turn, doing her terrible arithmetic one more time.

"Eleven percent," she said. "One shot at it. Then we're committed."

"Mei-Lin," Pak Uda said gently, "we were committed the day we fished her out of the surf."

Above us, far downstream, the last of the consortium lanterns went dark, and the river ran on through the broken gorge, carrying us north toward the source, toward the drowned village, toward whatever the dead man had hidden in the deep water — and toward the man who would follow us there, because now he had no choice either.

Voss knew her name.

Which meant, whether she knew it yet or not, that Orsen Zhou's running was finally, truly over.

The only question left was what she'd become instead.
