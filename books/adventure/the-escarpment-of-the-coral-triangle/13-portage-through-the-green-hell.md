## 13. Portage Through the Green Hell

The river gave up on us a little after dawn, the way rivers do when the mountains stop pretending to be hills. One bend too shallow, one gravel bar too long, and Mei-Lin ran the *Kestrel* aground with the gentleness of a woman who had done it before and expected to do it again.

"Portage," she said, as if announcing lunch.

I looked at the jungle. The jungle looked back. It was the color of a bruise left out in the rain — every shade of green that has ever been invented and several that hadn't been patented yet — and it went uphill without apology for two thousand feet.

"You want to carry the boat through *that*."

"I want to carry the boat over that," Mei-Lin corrected. "Through is what happens to people who argue with me about terminology." She was already uncoiling rope from the bow locker, her hands moving with the economy of someone who resented every wasted motion on principle. "There's a logging cut on the ridge. Old one. If it still exists, we can drag her along it to the headwaters of the next channel. Three days, maybe four."

"And if it doesn't exist?"

"Then we learn humility." She tossed me a coil. "You first. You're the lightest thing aboard that isn't cargo."

It was not, strictly speaking, true. Sef weighed less than I did, but Sef was already halfway up the bank with a machete, arguing with a fern the size of a dining table, so I let it stand.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about hauling a forty-foot steel-hulled salvage boat overland through primary rainforest: it is less like an adventure and more like being slowly digested by something that finds you mildly disappointing.

We built rollers from felled bamboo and greased them with whatever the galley offered — coconut oil, mostly, which meant the *Kestrel* traveled through the green hell smelling like a bakery that had lost a fight. We rigged A-frames from the mast sections and block-and-tackle from Pak Uda's diving gear bags, because Pak Uda had opinions about mechanical advantage the way other men have opinions about religion, and most of his were correct.

By noon of the first day we had moved the boat perhaps two hundred meters.

By evening we had moved it another hundred and fifty, and I had learned three things about myself: that I could keep a pace longer than I'd believed, that leeches regarded me as a kind of moving buffet, and that Dara — who had spent the entire morning needling me about my past with the cheerful persistence of a mosquito with a philosophy degree — had gone quiet.

Not tired-quiet. Wrong-quiet.

She was walking sweep at the rear of the line, and she'd stopped complaining, which for Dara was roughly equivalent to a lighthouse going dark. When I looked back she was pale under her tan in a way that had nothing to do with fear, and her jokes had dried up somewhere back at the second stream crossing.

"Dara." I said it quietly, so the others wouldn't turn. "Talk to me."

"Fine," she said, which is what people say when they are the opposite of fine.

I crossed back to her. Up close I could see the shine of sweat that wasn't effort-sweat, the slight wrongness of her pupils, the way she was holding her machete like it was a stranger's baby — carefully, and with no idea why.

"When did you last drink?"

She considered this with the gravity of a scholar. "The stream. Before the climb."

That had been hours ago. In this heat, carrying half her body weight in gear, she'd sweated out everything she had and replaced it with nothing, and now her blood was thickening in her veins like paint left open.

"Sef!" I called, and my voice came out flatter and harder than I intended — harder than I'd have used on any crew I'd worked with before, because crews were temporary and hard voices cost you nothing with temporary people. But these weren't temporary people anymore, and the voice came anyway, unbidden, like it had been waiting years for permission. "Get water boiling. Salt and sugar if we've got it. Now."

Sef looked at me, then at his sister, and whatever he saw in my face made him drop the machete and run.

* * *

We got Dara into the shade of the boat's hull, which we'd propped on its rollers like a beached whale with ambitions. Pak Uda mixed rehydration salts with the practiced hand of a man who had pulled too many divers out of water that didn't want them, and Mei-Lin crouched beside her twin-engine troublemaker and said nothing, which from Mei-Lin was a full-throated expression of terror.

"She'll live," Pak Uda said finally. "But she walks tomorrow or she doesn't walk for a week. Heat doesn't negotiate. It sends terms."

Dara, drifting at the edge of consciousness, managed a grin. "Told you... Orsen was... one of us..."

"Hush," I said, and discovered my own hands were shaking.

Because here was the thing I sat with while the jungle breathed around us and the fireflies started their slow inventory of the dark: I had spent eleven years perfecting the art of not being responsible for anyone. Every crew I'd joined, I'd joined with one foot out the door. Every skill I'd taught, I'd taught shallowly, so no one would lean on me and no one would fall when I left. The bridge collapse had taken my name and made it radioactive, and I'd responded by becoming a rumor — competent everywhere, accountable nowhere.

And today, without deciding to, I had taken command. Not asked. Taken. And the crew had *obeyed*, instantly, gratefully, as though some part of them had been waiting for me to stop auditioning and start belonging.

Mei-Lin sat down across the fire from me later, after Dara was sleeping under a mosquito net rigged from the trawl netting, and studied me with those flat harbor-pilot eyes.

"You gave orders today."

"Somebody had to."

"Somebody always has to. Most somebodies wait to be asked." She fed a stick to the flames. "You didn't wait."

"No," I admitted.

"Why?"

I watched the fire rather than answer immediately, which is a coward's trick but a comfortable one. "Because I've seen what heat does to people who don't get treated fast," I said finally. "On a survey crew, years ago. We lost a man to it because everyone assumed someone else was handling it." That much was even true. What I didn't say was that the man's name had been on the same list of closed lines I carried in my notebook, in a way, because everything in my life seemed to end up connected to things I'd rather forget. "I decided a long time ago I'd never be the somebody-else again."

Mei-Lin nodded slowly, accepting maybe a tenth of what I'd said and filing the rest away — she was good at that, Mei-Lin, taking deposits of truth without demanding the whole account. "Tomorrow you lead," she said. "Not because I'm promoting you. Because I'm tired, and Sef will want to stay with Dara, and Pak Uda's knees are older than the boat. So it's you and me, and I'd rather follow the person who moves first than the person who waits."

"That's not how chains of command usually work."

"Neither is hauling a boat through hell." She stood, joints cracking like green bamboo. "Sleep, engineer. Tomorrow we find out if your map knows something mine doesn't."

* * *

It did.

Or rather, I did — or the part of me I'd been running from did, the part that had spent a decade memorizing dead infrastructure the way other people memorize poetry.

We broke camp at first light, Dara riding atop the boat's cabin like a figurehead with a headache, protesting weakly that she could walk and being firmly informed by her brother that she could not. We moved slower with her aboard the hull, but the rollers took the weight, and by mid-morning we'd gained the shoulder of the ridge where the canopy opened enough to show sky for the first time in a day and a half.

And there it was.

A cutting through the trees, straight as a ruled line, running along the contour of the hillside. Not a trail — trails wander; animals make trails, and animals are honest about terrain. This was geometry. This was intent. The embankment still held its shape after decades of rain, the drainage culverts still visible where they passed beneath, choked with roots but structurally sound. Ballast stone showed through the leaf litter in places, gray and angular, imported from somewhere else because nobody quarries stone that size locally.

Rail bed.

My heart did something complicated that I chose not to examine. Because I knew this cut. Not this specific one — I'd never been within five hundred miles of it before last month — but I knew its *kind*. I'd spent years cataloguing them, walking them, mapping them in a notebook I kept like other people keep letters from home. Abandoned spurs, closed lines, the skeleton of ambition that every colonial administration and every mining consortium leaves behind when the ore runs out or the politics turn. I knew their gauge by the spacing of their culverts. I knew their age by the species of tree growing through their ballast.

This one was old. Older than the war roads. Older than the plantation lines. And it ran — I turned, reading the contour the way other people read faces — it ran exactly where we needed to go.

"Mei-Lin," I said, and my voice sounded strange to me, almost reverent, which embarrassed me immediately. "Come look at this."

She climbed up beside me, Dara's weight shifting above us, and followed my finger along the line of the cutting until it vanished around the shoulder of the hill.

"A road?"

"A railway. Dead one. Fifty, sixty years, maybe more." I was already walking it, boots finding the ballast automatically, my body remembering a language I'd sworn I'd forgotten. "See the culverts? Spaced for heavy loading. See how the cut follows the contour instead of climbing? They weren't hauling timber up here — timber goes downhill. This was a mainline once, or wanted to be. Somebody dreamed big and then somebody else shot the dream."

"The consortium?"

"Maybe. Maybe whoever came before them. Consortiums don't invent abandonment, they just inherit it and add lawyers." I crouched, brushed leaf mold off a rusted fishplate still bolted to a rail stub buried to its web in the earth. The steel underneath was pitted but whole. "The rails are gone — salvaged, probably, during some crisis or another — but the bed's intact. Graded, drained, contoured. We can run the *Kestrel* along this all the way to the headwaters. Two days' work instead of five. Maybe less."

Mei-Lin looked at the cutting, then at me, and something shifted behind her eyes — the recalculation of a captain reassessing her cargo.

"You knew this was here."

"I suspected terrain like this would hold one. I didn't know *this* one." Which was true, and also not the whole truth, because the whole truth was that I had spent eleven years learning to read dead lines the way Voss presumably read men — fluently, obsessively, as a survival skill — and that knowledge had just saved us days we might not have had. "It's what I do, Mei-Lin. It's the only thing I've ever done that nobody could take away from me. I find the lines everybody else forgot."

She was quiet a moment. Then: "Your notebook. The one you're always writing in. That's what's in it."

It wasn't a question, and I didn't treat it as one. "Closed lines. Abandoned spurs. Every one I've ever walked or heard reliable word of, from here to the delta and up into the high country." I hesitated, then heard myself say, before the caution could catch it: "When this is done — if we're all still breathing — I'll draw you the ones in these islands. Every approach, every grade, every place a gunboat can't follow and a salvage boat can."

Mei-Lin's eyebrows went up a fraction of a millimeter, which from her was a standing ovation. "That's worth more than the cargo we lost at the toll bridge."

"Don't tell Sef. He'll want royalties."

Above us, from her perch on the cabin roof, Dara's voice drifted down, thin but recovering its music: "He'll want *naming rights*. 'Zhou Spur.' He's been calling the winch that since Tuesday."

I laughed — actually laughed, out loud, in the middle of the green hell with leeches in my socks and a feverish stowaway on my roof and a dead railway humming under my boots like a plucked string. And the strange thing, the thing I'm setting down here honestly because this account deserves honesty: it didn't feel like breaking a rule. It felt like the rule had simply expired, quietly, somewhere between the oxbow fire and this ridgeline, and nobody had thought to inform me.

* * *

We made nine kilometers that afternoon along the old bed, which in portage arithmetic is a miracle dressed in mud. The cutting held its grade beautifully; whoever built it had known their business, and I found myself talking to them in my head as we worked — *good camber on that embankment, shame about your drainage ditch* — the way you talk to a colleague whose name you never learned.

Sef rigged a sail from the trawl netting and a spare tarp, and the afternoon breeze, funneled clean down the linear corridor of the cut, actually helped push the rollers. Pak Uda walked point with a machete and a song he claimed was a blessing for travelers and which Dara, from her rooftop sickbed, insisted was a fishing chantey with delusions of grandeur.

It was nearly dusk when I found the marker.

It was set into the embankment where the cutting bent around a knoll — a concrete post, waist-high, cracked and stained black with decades of rain, but legible. Surveyor's datum. Chainage number. And below that, stamped deep enough to survive anything short of dynamite:

**C.T.R. — LINE 7 — KILOMETER 41**

Coral Triangle Railways. Line 7.

I stopped walking.

Because I knew that name. Not from legend, not from the cave charts — from my own notebook, page forty-something, an entry made in a hostel in a port city I won't name, from a drunk Dutch engineer who'd once worked these islands and talked too much: *Line 7, the one that went to the source, the one they sealed.* I'd written it down with ten thousand other fragments and thought nothing of it, because that was the discipline — collect everything, connect nothing, connection is how you get found.

Except the drowned surveyor's charts had said the same thing. FORBIDDEN, inked in a dead man's hand, at the head of a river whose source had been dammed twenty years ago. And here was the rail line that had been built to serve that source, abandoned at kilometer 41, pointing up-valley like a compass needle with an opinion.

"Orsen?" Mei-Lin had come back for me. She read the marker over my shoulder, and I felt her go still. "That's the line to the dam, isn't it."

"It's the line to everything," I said. "They built it to haul ore out of the valley. Then the valley became a problem, so they killed the railway first, then the surveys, then the people who asked questions." I touched the cold concrete. Under my palm, faint under the moss, I could feel the ghost of painted lettering, worn to nothing. "Every closed line is a story about why somebody needed it to close."

"And this story ends at the dam."

"This story ends at the dam."

We stood there while the light failed, the crew gathering behind us with the quiet instinct of people who had learned to read my silences faster than my words. Somewhere upslope, the river we'd left was making its way toward the same destination by the honest route, and I thought about the drowned village behind that wall of concrete, and the report hidden in its ruins, and the enforcer burning supply posts somewhere behind us on our trail.

Dara, wrapped in a blanket on the cabin roof, looked down at the marker and then at me, and her fever-bright eyes had gone sharp and clear.

"Kilometer 41," she said. "How far does Line 7 go?"

I took out my notebook — the map of closed lines, the record of my running, the one thing I'd sworn I'd burn before I let anyone read it — and opened it against my knee right there in front of all of them, and began, for the first time in eleven years, to draw a line that pointed forward instead of away.

"Far enough," I said. "If the bed holds past the washout at the head of the valley, we reach the dam by tomorrow night." I looked up at them — Mei-Lin with her arms folded, Sef with his arm through his sister's, Pak Uda with his machete resting easy — and found that none of them were looking at the map.

They were looking at me.

"And after the dam?" Mei-Lin asked.

After the dam. After the evidence, after Voss, after the wall of water that a dead surveyor had seen coming twenty years before any of us. I closed the notebook.

"After the dam," I said, "we find out whether a river remembers being stolen."

Behind us, far downstream, so faint it might have been thunder, something echoed off the escarpment — a horn, maybe, or a gunshot, or the first small crack in a structure that had been dying for two decades.

Nobody asked which.

We picked up the ropes, and Line 7 carried us toward the dark.
