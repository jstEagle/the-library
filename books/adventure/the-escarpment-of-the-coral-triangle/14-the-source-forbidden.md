## 14. The Source Forbidden

I have been running boats through the Coral Triangle for thirty-one years, and in that time I have learned exactly three things worth teaching: never trust a river that runs too quiet, never trust a man who quotes scripture before breakfast, and never — *never* — let an engineer tell you a structure is "probably fine." Engineers who say "probably fine" are either lying or about to be surprised, and both varieties have cost me cargo.

So when Orsen Zhou stood at the bow of the Kestrel at dawn on the ninth day upstream and said, "We're close," in the voice of a woman reading her own death warrant, I did what any sensible captain does.

I checked my fuel gauge, my crew count, and my escape routes. In that order.

* * *

The river had been lying to us for two days.

That's the only way I can describe it. A healthy river talks to you — it grumbles over gravel bars, it hisses around deadfall, it shoves at your hull like a drunk relative who wants to tell you something important. This stretch of the Seraung did none of that. It slid past us flat and green and silent as pond water, deep enough to swallow our soundings without a burp, and cold in a way tropical rivers have no business being.

"Reservoir head," Orsen said when Dara dropped the lead line for the fourth time and came up frowning. "The dam backs water this far down. We're not sailing a river anymore. We're sailing the top of a lake that doesn't know it exists yet."

Sef didn't like it. My twins are identical down to the calluses, but they diverge beautifully under pressure: Sef goes quiet and starts counting things, Dara gets loud and starts arguing with them. By midday of the silent water, Sef had counted every eddy line and foam patch within sight and announced there weren't enough. "A river this size should be breathing," he said. "This one's holding its breath."

Pak Uda, who had been uncharacteristically silent since the village — and Pak Uda silent is like a monsoon silent, you spend all your time waiting for what comes after — finally spoke from his perch on the cargo hatch.

"My grandfather fished this water," he said. "Before the company came. He said the Seraung here ran so fast and so loud that you could not hear your own thoughts. The old people called this stretch the Shouting Water." He looked at the glass-flat surface with the expression of a man visiting a grave. "Now look at it. Twenty years drowned, and still it remembers."

Nobody said anything to that. There was nothing to say. We were sailing on top of somebody's memory, and everybody aboard knew it except maybe the fish.

The gorge narrowed through the afternoon, walls of black limestone rising on either side until we were running in our own shadow, the canopy closing overhead like a lid. Twice I thought I heard something echo off the cliffs — machinery, maybe, or water where water shouldn't be — and twice I told myself it was the gorge playing tricks. Gorges do that. They take one sound and sell it back to you in four currencies.

Then the walls opened, and I stopped telling myself anything, because there in front of us, filling the valley floor wall to wall like a scar across the throat of the world, was the dam.

* * *

I've seen dams. Big ones, ugly ones, ones built by governments with more concrete than sense. I once ran salvage on a hydro project in the north islands that could have held back the sea itself if the sea had bothered to ask permission.

This was not that.

This was a wound dressed up as infrastructure. Maybe eighty meters of poured face spanning the gorge, stained dark with twenty years of seep, streaked below the spillway gates like something bleeding slowly and being very polite about it. No maintenance road. No lights. No signage, which in my experience is the loudest warning sign there is — companies that are proud of their work put their name on it. Companies that are ashamed of it post guards and nothing else.

And posted guards there were, or had been. Orsen found the watchtower foundation within ten minutes of putting us ashore in a fold of the gorge the cliffs hid from the main channel — good instinct, that; she reads water like I read weather, and I made a note of it, the way I make notes of everything, because a captain who forgets who's useful doesn't stay a captain long. The tower was abandoned, its tin roof rusted through, a consortium flagpole standing bare with the flag long since taken down or rotted away. But the ground around it was swept clean, and there was a generator pad with fresh oil stains, and a coil of new razor wire in a crate stamped with a shipping date eight months old.

"They stopped maintaining the show," Orsen said, crouched over the oil stain, "but they never stopped maintaining the secret."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning nobody comes up here anymore because nobody's supposed to know it's here. The village downstream got bought off with jobs and fear. The survey team that mapped this valley drowned — officially by accident. And the company keeps a skeleton watch on the one road in because the thing they're guarding isn't the dam." She looked up at me, and I saw something in her face I hadn't seen before, and it took me a moment to name it because I'd mostly seen it in mirrors during bad years.

It was dread with a deadline attached.

"It's what the dam is hiding."

* * *

What the dam was hiding, we found an hour later, and I will carry the sight of it until they put me in the ground.

Orsen led us along the reservoir rim on a game trail that climbed through secondary jungle — Pak Uda moving ahead of us light as a boy despite knees that complain louder than my engine room, the twins hauling the survey gear, me bringing up the rear doing what captains do best, which is counting heads and hating the terrain. The trail crested a ridge of broken limestone, dropped through a stand of fig trees hung with white orchids, and then the jungle simply ended, cut in a perfect line, and beyond the cut the land fell away into the hidden valley.

The valley behind the dam.

Except it wasn't a valley anymore. It was a lake — a small, still, impossibly green lake held in the bowl of the mountains, ringed by drowned forest whose dead trees stood out of the water like the masts of a fleet that had gone down at anchor. And beneath that water, visible in the afternoon light with a clarity that felt almost indecent, was a village.

Roofs. Walls. A church — or what passed for one out here, a building with a bell tower, the bell still hanging, tilted. Streets laid out in a grid, stone-lined, leading down to a jetty that now served only the water above it. A football pitch, the goal frames still standing, silt drifting across it like snow. Fruit trees in what must have been gardens, their crowns just breaking the surface, dead and silvered, dropping leaves that had nowhere to go.

"Kampung Asal," Pak Uda said behind me. His voice had gone somewhere young. "The Original Village."

He told it while we stood there, and I'll set it down as he told it, because some things shouldn't be paraphrased.

Twenty-two years ago, the consortium — then a younger beast, hungry, buying concessions across three island groups — came to the elders of the villages along the upper Seraung with a proposal. A mine in the high country needed power and water security. A dam in the gorge would provide both. The price was the valley: the original settlement, the oldest kampung in the watershed, the place the ancestors were buried. In exchange — jobs, a new town downstream, a school, a clinic, and silence, written into contracts most of the signatories couldn't read.

Some elders refused. Those families were moved anyway, with less courtesy. Some signed. And the whole watershed has been paying interest on that signature ever since — in the fear the company farms downstream, in the fish that never came back, in the fact that no one spoke the valley's name aloud for two decades except in the tide-cave carvings, where the sea kept the record the men with contracts tried to drown.

"The elders sold the river to save themselves," Pak Uda finished quietly. "That is what the cave says. And the cave also says the river does not forget who signed."

Dara had her hand pressed flat against a dead fruit tree at the waterline, the way you'd touch a wall of a house you grew up in. She wasn't crying. She was angrier than crying, which on Dara looks like absolute stillness, and I have learned to fear her stillness considerably more than her noise.

"They flooded a graveyard," she said. "They flooded the *ancestors*, Uda-pak. And then they hired the grandchildren to run the pumps."

"And paid them just enough," Pak Uda said, "that grief became a luxury. That is how it is done. That is always how it is done."

I looked at Orsen, because throughout all of it she hadn't said a word. She had walked the shoreline of the drowned village slow, eyes working — the roofs, the bell tower, the depth markers her engineer's eye couldn't help reading even off a church spire — and then she turned and looked at the dam face across the water, and stayed looking at it for a long time.

When she finally spoke, it wasn't about the village at all.

"Mei-Lin. I need to get onto that dam face today. Before dark."

"Why today?"

"Because of the seep lines." She pointed, and I squinted, and after a moment I saw what she saw: dark stains on the lower third of the concrete, spreading outward from points rather than cracks, fanning like ink dropped in water. "Those aren't twenty years old. Those are months old. Weeks, maybe. That dam isn't leaking the way old dams leak. It's leaking the way dying dams leak." She turned back to me, and the dread-with-a-deadline had sharpened into something with numbers in it. "I need to measure it. Because if I'm right about what I'm seeing, we don't have a season to expose this. We might not have a month."

* * *

Getting onto the dam face was Orsen's idea, Orsen's rig, and — though she was too polite to say so — Orsen's risk, which is why I made Sef go with her, over her objection that she worked better alone and my counter-objection that dead engineers survey nothing.

She built the descent rig out of our spare winch cable, two carabiners, and a section of ladder frame cannibalized from the Kestrel's hold, and watching her work I understood again why I'd traded passage for her hands back in chapter one of this whole misadventure, so to speak. Some people use tools. Orsen negotiates with them, and wins.

They went down the abutment on the shaded side, out of sight of the watchtower, Sef anchoring the line above while Orsen walked the concrete. I watched through Pak Uda's old binoculars from the ridge, and I will admit freely that my stomach spent that hour doing things my stomach is too old and too proud for.

She measured. That was most of it, from where I sat — a woman in a harness pressing a tape measure to a wall, chalking marks, tapping concrete with a hammer and tilting her head at each report like a doctor listening to a chest. Tap. Listen. Move. Tap. Listen. Once she went very still for a long moment, laid her whole palm flat against the face, and held it there as if taking a pulse. Then she chalked a circle around the spot, a big one, and inside the circle she wrote something, and even through binoculars I could read the shape of her handwriting, which was the shape of someone writing fast because the pen can't keep up with the knowing.

They came up at last light. Orsen's face in the dusk told me everything before her mouth caught up.

"Talk," I said.

She talked. Standing there in the fig orchard with the drowned village glowing faintly green below us, she gave me the worst engineering briefing of my life, and I include in that count a man who once explained to me, mid-fire, why my engine room was flooding.

"The dam was built fast and cheap — the concrete mix is wrong for this climate, no question, you can see the aggregate sorting in the exposed faces. It was probably marginal from day one, and the company knew it, because the survey team's notes flagged it before anyone died. But here's the part that matters." She held up her chalk-stained fingers. "Twenty years of reservoir pressure plus silt loading plus zero maintenance has done what time does. There's a delamination zone in the lower face — the shell and the core have separated along maybe forty meters. The seep isn't coming through cracks. It's coming up *behind* the skin. The skin is all that's holding."

"How long?"

"I don't know. Nobody knows, that's the honest answer — it depends on the next big rain, on the silt pulse, on whether there's another seismic tremor like the one the region's had two of this year." She looked at the dam, black now against the last light, and her voice went flat and precise, which I've learned is how she says the truest things. "But I can tell you this. Every hour I spent on that face, I kept thinking: this is not a structure with a problem. This is a structure that has already decided to fail and is negotiating the date. Days. Weeks if we're lucky. Not months. And when it lets go, everything behind that face — forty meters of standing water, the silt, the whole reservoir — comes down the gorge into the Shouting Water, and the first thing it hits is Longai village. Four hundred people. The children who gave us safe-passage tokens."

Nobody moved. Down in the green water, the bell tower of Kampung Asal stood tilted over its own reflection, patient as a headstone.

"So the company's secret," Dara said slowly, "isn't just that they drowned a village. It's that the thing they drowned it *for* is going to kill the village they moved everyone to."

"That's why the surveyor was murdered," Sef said quietly. He'd been turning something over the whole climb, I could see it. "Not for finding the dam was doomed. For finding it was doomed *and foreseeable*. A collapse you can blame on God is an act of nature. A collapse with a paper trail is a massacre."

"A paper trail," I repeated, because my mouth had gone dry and repetition buys time.

"The full report," Orsen said. "The one he hid instead of filing. His field copy." She looked at Pak Uda. "You said the journal mentioned a cache. 'Where the ancestors watch the water.' You know these valleys. Does that mean anything to you?"

Pak Uda was silent for a long moment. Below us, wind moved across the reservoir and the dead silvered trees let out a sound like a fleet remembering how to creak.

"There is a shrine," he said at last. "On the point above the old jetty. The elders used to row out to it every planting season — before. If a man wanted to hide something from the company, from the law, from everyone living—" he looked down at the water, at the rooftops ghosting beneath it, "—he would hide it with the ancestors. Where the company would never dig, because digging there means admitting the village existed. And admitting the village existed costs them everything."

The wind came again. Somewhere out on the water, a dead tree shifted and settled.

"So we dive," Orsen said.

"We dive," Pak Uda agreed, and there was something in the old man's voice I hadn't heard in years — not the diver's calm, but the diver's hunger, the thing that had put him under a hundred wrecks before his knees filed their complaint. "Tomorrow. Early, before the light makes us visible. Mei-Lin—" he turned to me, and I braced, because Pak Uda asking permission is like the tide asking permission, it's going to happen regardless, "—I'll need the good air, and I'll need the girl on the line."

"You'll need a second diver," I said, "because you're seventy-one years old and I am not explaining to your granddaughter why I let you solo a flooded graveyard."

"Grandmother," said Dara.

"What?"

"She's my grandmother. Sef's too. You've known this for six years, Captain. Your memory for cargo manifests is flawless and your memory for family is a disgrace." But she said it soft, and she was already checking her dive mask seal, and that was that. My crew, deciding things by deciding them. Thirty-one years I've captained this boat and I have never once successfully prevented it, and tonight, watching them plan a dive into a drowned village to steal a dead man's truth out from under the company that killed him, I found I didn't want to.

* * *

I stood the midnight watch alone at the ridge shrine trailhead, because I sleep badly in sight of other people's graves, and because someone had to count heads and someone always has to be me.

Below, the reservoir lay black and still, holding its village. Across the water, the dam held its lie. Two structures, I thought, built by the same hands, loaded past their design lives, both of them failing — one visibly, one invisibly, and the difference between them was only that the dam had engineers' chalk on it now, and the village had us.

I thought about the file Ravel Voss carried, and the gunboat somewhere south of us eating river miles, and the guide in Longai with hungry eyes and a family to protect, and I did the arithmetic captains do, the cold one, the one you keep in a locked drawer: odds of getting the report, odds of getting out, odds of Voss arriving before either. The drawer-arithmetic said we should take the Kestrel and run, now, tonight, while the river was still ours.

The drawer-arithmetic has been wrong exactly as often as my heart, and my heart was looking down at a tilted bell tower in green water, at a crew already asleep in a circle around a cold fire like commas around a sentence, at a woman who had spent nine days learning to stop running and was tomorrow going to swim down into the proof of why.

Days, Orsen had said. Maybe weeks.

I banked the fire, woke Sef for his watch, and went to check the winch cable twice, because it would be carrying my whole family over open water in the morning, and because checking things is the only prayer I've ever trusted.

The river, somewhere below the dam, was shouting again. I could just barely hear it.
