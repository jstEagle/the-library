## 15. Evidence and Leverage

The drowned village had a name once. The charts called it Selat Asri, which the elders' dialect rendered as something like "the beautiful strait," and the consortium's paperwork called it Resettlement Zone Seven, which is the kind of language people use when they want a word to do the opposite of its job.

From the ridge above the valley, all you could see of it was the water itself — a lake the color of bottle glass, held back by the dam's gray shoulder to the south. But Pak Uda stood at the shoreline that first morning and pointed his chin at the surface like a man greeting old friends.

"Minaret was there," he said. "Schoolhouse there. My cousin's house had blue shutters. You could see them from the river."

The shutters were forty feet down now, along with everything else. I'd spent my career reading landscapes — grades, cuttings, the way water negotiates with stone — and this one read like a confession written in a language nobody downstream was allowed to speak.

"You knew this place," I said.

"I grew up in it." He said it the way you'd report the weather. "Then the company came with papers and promises, and my father signed because the alternative was men with clipboards and then men without clipboards. We moved downhill. The river moved nowhere. It just stopped being ours." He looked at me sideways. "You have a face like someone who understands paperwork doing violence."

I did, but that was a story for a different depth of water.

* * *

The dead surveyor — Aris Wibowo, according to the brass tube, though the journal only ever called himself "A," as if even on paper he couldn't quite believe he'd be worth a full name — had left us a riddle wrapped in a dive plan. His last annotations weren't charts at all, not really. They were instructions disguised as bathymetry: soundings that made no sense for navigation but made perfect sense if you read them as bearings from the minaret's stump to the schoolhouse foundation, depths corrected for twenty years of silt, and a final notation in the margin that I'd translated three times before I trusted myself:

*Not under the floor. Under the floor they would look for.*

Which meant the mosque. Which meant, in a village drowned whole and preserved by cold, still water, that the most intact structure at the bottom of the lake was also the place every searcher would assume had already been stripped.

"Aris was clever," Pak Uda said, when I laid it out for him on the Kestrel's deck that night. He turned the pages with hands that had spent fifty years reading water by touch. "Clever men drown too. But sometimes slower."

Mei-Lin stood at the rail with her arms crossed, watching the dam's silhouette against the stars. She hadn't liked any of this since we'd come through the gorge — not the valley, not the plan, not the particular quality of silence the consortium maintained up here, which felt less like absence and more like held breath.

"Dive today, out tomorrow," she said finally. "I don't care what's down there. I care that we're floating on their reservoir inside their fence line with no exit that doesn't pass their gatehouse. Sef says there are sensor buoys on the north arm. Dara counted three before the light went."

"Four," Dara said, not looking up from the winch she was re-greasing. "The fourth is camouflaged as a fishing float. Whoever rigged it thinks fishermen are stupid, which tells you the riggers have never met a fisherman."

"So we avoid the north arm," Sef said.

"So we avoid the north arm," Mei-Lin agreed, "and we're quick about it. Uda dives, Orsen dives, everyone else stays ready to leave ugly. Agreed?"

Nobody argued with Mei-Lin when she used that voice. It was the voice of a woman who had once talked a port authority out of impounding her boat by explaining, correctly, that the boat was technically a reef.

* * *

We went down at first light, when the water was calmest and the light came down in long green columns.

I'll be honest about what I expected: murk, rot, the usual indignities of drowned places. What I got instead was a town. Cold water holds things the way amber holds flies. A bicycle leaned against a wall that still had whitewash on it. A cooking pot sat on a stove as if its owner had stepped out to argue with a man holding clipboards and meant to come right back. Laundry lines sagged between houses, the cloth long since gone to lace, but the lines themselves — nylon, patient — still held.

Pak Uda swam ahead of me, and I watched him move through his childhood like a man walking through a house he'd sold decades ago: touching nothing, seeing everything. At the schoolhouse he paused over a child's desk, and even through the mask I could see him decide not to feel what he was feeling. Later, topside, he'd say only: "The desks were small. I forgot how small."

The minaret's stump rose out of the silt near the center, and the mosque beside it was exactly as Aris had promised — too obvious to be useful, therefore never searched properly. We went in through a window whose glass had become a soft-edged memory of itself.

Under the floor they would look for. Aris's joke, if it was a joke, was that the floor they'd look under was the main prayer hall. Instead we followed his soundings to the ablution room at the rear, where the flagstones were loose by design, and beneath them a cavity lined with sheet lead — soldered, waterproofed, an engineer's coffin for paper.

Inside: oilcloth bundles. A full report, hand-bound, its pages stiff but legible. Stress calculations. Core samples. Photographs of the dam's foundation showing seepage paths that had been flagged, re-flagged, and flagged again in red ink that had faded to the color of dried blood. And a cover letter addressed not to the consortium but to *whoever finds this*, because Aris had known, by the end, that his intended recipients were the ones who'd arrange for him to stop sending reports.

I read the first page underwater by torchlight, and my hands shook hard enough that I had to press the page flat against a flagstone to keep it still.

The dam wasn't failing. The dam had been failing for nineteen years, and the numbers proving it had been sitting forty feet beneath the reservoir it was going to destroy, waiting for someone with lungs and a reason.

Pak Uda touched my shoulder and pointed up. Time. Air. The discipline of old divers. I sealed the bundles into the dry bag he'd brought for exactly this, and we rose together through the green columns, past the bicycle, past the laundry lines, past a town that had been holding its breath for twenty years and was finally, finally going to exhale.

That's what I was thinking about, anyway. That's my excuse for what happened next.

* * *

The sensor buoy was camouflaged as a fishing float, and Dara had counted four of them, and there were five.

The fifth sat on the edge of the north arm precisely where a lazy swimmer surfacing off-course would blunder into its tether line — which is what I did, at a depth of six feet, with a bag of evidence against my chest and my mind already halfway to the surface and the argument I was going to win with Mei-Lin about staying another day.

The line sang. Not loudly — a thin vibration through the water, more felt than heard — but I'd spent enough years around signaling equipment to know the difference between a mooring line and a tripwire, and this was a tripwire with ambitions.

Pak Uda saw my face and understood instantly. He didn't panic. Old divers don't; panic costs air. He simply took the bag from me, pointed me toward the far shore, and began swimming a wide, unhurried arc that would make our position ambiguous — two divers, multiple headings, let the bastards triangulate *that*.

We surfaced behind a stand of dead trees on the western shallows, well away from the Kestrel, and lay in the water breathing through reeds like children in a war story while a consortium patrol boat puttered up the north arm with a spotlight playing across the surface at nine in the morning, which told me they took their tripwires seriously and their theater seriously too.

It took them forty minutes to satisfy themselves that a fish, or a log, or a careless bird had triggered the buoy. Forty minutes during which I floated in cold water watching my own breath ripple the surface and thought about Aris Wibowo, who had presumably also once believed he was being careful.

When the patrol boat finally withdrew, we swam the long way around to the Kestrel, hauled ourselves aboard, and stood dripping on the deck while the entire crew stared at the dry bag like it might go off.

"Well?" Mei-Lin said.

"It's all here," I said. "Every test, every warning, every signature of every manager who chose to let it stand. Nineteen years of documents proving the collapse isn't an accident. It's a schedule."

For a moment nobody spoke. Then Dara laughed — a short, sharp bark with no humor in it at all.

"A schedule," she repeated. "So we're carrying the thing that gets everyone who touches it killed, and we tripped their alarm getting it."

"We tripped *a* alarm," Sef corrected. "Possibly theirs. Possibly a fish."

"Possibly a fish," Dara agreed, "that swims in straight lines and weighs eighty kilos."

Mei-Lin held up a hand, and the twins subsided the way they always did — grumbling, synchronized, a two-part harmony of complaint. She looked at me, and behind the iron of her expression I saw her actually weighing it: the cargo versus the crew, the same arithmetic she'd been running since the toll bridge, except now the cargo was justice and the crew included a woman who'd just led a patrol boat on a tour of the reservoir.

"They'll sweep harder now," she said. "Checkpoints on the outflow. Maybe aircraft. Whatever we do next, we do knowing they know something moved down there."

"I know," I said.

"And you'd do it again."

I looked at the dry bag, and through the canvas I could almost see the red ink fading to brown, the neat hand of a dead man who had done the math on his own survival and kept writing anyway.

"Yes," I said. "Faster, next time. But yes."

Pak Uda, wrapped in a blanket with tea steaming in his hands, said quietly: "My cousin's house had blue shutters. Someone should know why the water took it." He drank his tea. "Now someone will."

That should have been the end of the day. A good day, even — evidence secured, crew intact, hope with a spine of documentation.

But Sef had been fiddling with the radio through all of it, scanning frequencies out of habit, and just as the sun came down behind the escarpment he went very still and turned up the volume.

A voice, clipped and official, reading coordinates. Coordinates that were ours. Then a second voice answering, lower and colder, and even distorted by static and distance I recognized the cadence from the supply post, from the toll station, from every place Voss had left his fingerprints on the landscape.

He wasn't searching the valley anymore.

He was waiting at the only exit.
