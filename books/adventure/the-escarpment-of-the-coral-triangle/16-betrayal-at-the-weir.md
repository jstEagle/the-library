## 16. Betrayal at the Weir

*Arif*

You should understand something about me before you decide what I am.

I have three children and a wife with a cough that has been getting worse since the dry season started, and I live in a village where the consortium pays the schoolteacher's salary, the clinic nurse's salary, and — though nobody says this out loud — the headman's silence. I have guided fishermen to the good channels for twenty years. I know every bend of the river between here and the escarpment the way you know the lines of your own palm.

So when the strangers came through — the salvage crew with their patched-up boat and their engineer woman who walked like someone counting exits — the headman asked me to keep an eye on them. And when the man in the gray shirt arrived two days behind them, asking quieter questions in a voice like a closed door, he asked me the same thing.

Both of them offered money. Only one of them offered it with a photograph of my daughter's school on the back of his hand, so to speak. A gesture toward the village. A suggestion of what happens to villages that choose wrong.

I told myself I was choosing my family over strangers. I told myself the strangers were adults who had made their own bed, floating it up a river that did not want them. I told myself a great many things while I sat across from the gray-shirted man and drew the Kestrel's route on his map with a finger dipped in rice wine, because I could not make my hand hold the pen steady.

Her name is Mei-Lin, the captain. She paid me fairly at the last landing, asked after my family, remembered my son's name from a conversation we'd had eleven days earlier. That is the kind of detail that should have stopped me. It didn't stop me. That is the kind of man I learned I was, somewhere around the second bend of the river, drawing lines for a stranger with dead eyes.

The weir is where they'll stop. They have to — the channel above it is too shallow for the Kestrel's draft, and there's only one place to portage, a shelf of black rock on the left bank. I told him that too. I told him everything. I am telling you everything now, which is the only penance I have left, and it isn't enough, and I know it isn't enough.

My wife coughed all night after I came home. I lay awake listening to it and thinking: this is what I bought. This cough, these children, one more season of them fed. The price was a woman who remembered my son's name.

Justice outside the law cuts both ways. I have been cut. So will she be. So, if there is any sense left in this river, will the man in the gray shirt, eventually, though I no longer believe in eventually.

I light a lamp in my window tonight. If anyone from the Kestrel comes back downriver alive, they'll see it, and maybe they'll understand, or maybe they'll come for me with a machete, and honestly either would be easier than this lamp.

* * *

*Voss*

The guide wept. They always do, eventually, and it never means anything except that their bodies have caught up to what their hands already did.

I let him finish. Tears are cheap; information is not, and he'd already given me the information before the tears started, which is the correct order of operations. Route to the weir. Portage point. Six souls aboard the Kestrel, plus the woman Zhou. Fuel stores low since the toll station. One diver, old. Twins who argue like machinery running out of tolerance. A captain who loves her crew more than her cargo, which is the single most exploitable fact about her.

Zhou herself was the surprise. When the drowned survey file crossed my desk, she was a loose end — an engineer with a talent for abandoned infrastructure and a history of being exactly where things go wrong. Loose ends get tidied. But the toll station changed the arithmetic. Nobody floods a fortified position with a diverted millrace on instinct. That was design. That was a mind that reads water the way I read files. The consortium had trained her once, indirectly, through the mentor whose methods appear all over those old charts, and then discarded her, and discarded things have a way of becoming evidence.

Evidence gets collected. Or destroyed. I prefer destroyed; collection creates paperwork, and paperwork creates witnesses.

I took eight men up the portage trail before dawn, moving on the guide's marks. The weir announced itself before we saw it — that flat, patient sound of water going over stone, like applause from an audience that never leaves. Below it, the Kestrel sat against the bank with her engine cover open and two figures arguing inside it, and above, on the rock shelf, the rest of them were rigging blocks and rollers for the portage.

Mei-Lin was below. Good. I sent four men down the near side and took the last four along the crest, and we came at her from both ends of the boat like a knot being pulled tight.

She heard us at the last moment — of course she did, she'd been hearing rivers her whole life and footsteps are just another current — and she turned with a pry bar in her hand and her weight already committed to fighting. I respect that. I gave her no opportunity to use it. Two of my men took her arms and a third took the bar, and I stood in front of her and watched her decide, visibly, in the space of about two seconds, whether to spend herself against four men or save the spending for later.

She saved it. Smart captain. I would have expected nothing less from the woman who chose cargo over speed when it mattered and speed over cargo when it mattered more.

"Orsen Zhou," I said. "And the surveyor's report. Both, within the hour, or your captain goes into the weir."

Below us, the water went over the stone in its long white sheet, patient as paperwork.

"I don't negotiate with employees," Mei-Lin said. Her voice was steady. There was blood at her hairline where one of my men had been less gentle than necessary, and she ignored it with the same discipline she ignored everything else that wasn't useful. "I negotiate with owners. Which are you?"

"I'm the part of the owner you don't put in brochures."

"Then I'm negotiating with a tool." Almost a smile. "Tools get used up."

"They do," I agreed. "That's rather the point of you, Captain. You're how I reach Zhou. She won't watch you die. People who build families always pay for them eventually — that's the invoice nobody reads until it arrives."

Something moved behind her eyes at *families*, some recalculation, but she banked it fast. "You're assuming she cares."

"I'm assuming everyone cares about something. Caring is just leverage you haven't located yet. I've located yours." I nodded at my men. "Bind her to the bollard. Leave her where the whole crew can see her from the rock shelf. Grief is most efficient when it's public."

One of the twins — the loud one, Dara, according to the guide's intelligence — was already shouting from above, voice cracking across the water, and I raised a hand and my men raised rifles, and the shouting stopped in a way that told me everything about the order of love on that boat. The loud one shut up because the quiet one put a hand on her arm. The old diver went very still, the stillness of a man doing depth calculations he doesn't like. And the engineer stood at the top of the shelf with the morning behind her, small against the sky, looking down at her captain tied to a bollard, and I watched her arrive at the same arithmetic I had already done for her.

There was no version of this where she kept the report and kept the woman.

"An hour," I called up to her, conversationally, because fear travels better without amplification. "The report, and yourself. Then we discuss terms. If you're not down here, I start feeding the river."

* * *

*Mei-Lin*

There is a particular humiliation in being tied to your own boat.

Not a consortium boat, not a hired hull — my boat, patched with my money, named for a bird that hunts by patience. The rope bit my wrists behind the bollard and the spray off the weir soaked my shoulders, and I spent the first ten minutes testing knots instead of watching Orsen, because watching Orsen would have meant admitting what Voss had already understood: that he'd found the load-bearing member of my life and put his shoulder against it.

Sef worked the winch cable loose-handed on deck, pretending to maintain something, positioning himself between me and the nearest rifle. Dara paced the rock shelf like a cat deciding which dog to bite. Pak Uda sat on the gunwale with his feet in the water, which looked like senility and was actually the old man telling everyone, wordlessly, that he could dive out of this situation whenever he decided it was time, and that the rest of us should take notes.

And Orsen stood at the top of the shelf, and I watched her do the thing I'd seen her do with the winch, with the rapids, with the toll station: take a problem apart in her head until it lay on the ground in pieces she could name.

"Don't," I called up to her. My voice carried better than I wanted it to. "Whatever you're building up there — don't trade it."

"She's right," Sef said quietly, not looking at me, coiling cable. "Captain, don't be noble. It doesn't suit you."

"It suits me fine."

"It really doesn't," Dara said. "You're a terrible noble person. You're a magnificent stubborn person. Be that instead."

But even as they said it, I could feel the shape of the trap from both sides, because Voss had built it well. Give him the report and the dam fails unnamed, the village drowns, and every death downstream lands on our ledger. Refuse, and Mei-Lin goes into the weir, and the report survives but the family doesn't, and what is the report *for*, if not the people?

Pak Uda spoke without turning his head. "The water under this boat is four meters. The current past the bollard runs two, maybe two and a half." He said it the way he says everything, like a tide table. "If I was tied to that thing, I would not be worried."

"You're seventy years old," I said.

"And the river has known me longer than you have, girl. Worry about the engineer. She has the look."

I turned my head as far as the rope allowed. Orsen had come down off the crest alone, hands empty, walking the portage trail with the deliberate calm of a woman carrying something invisible and heavy. Behind her, half-hidden in the green, I caught the glint of the brass tube slung across her back — and something else, a shape wrapped in oilcloth that she held the way you hold a sleeping child.

She stopped ten meters from Voss. Even at that distance I could read her posture: weight forward, weight balanced, a woman who had decided exactly how much this was going to cost and had paid it in advance.

"The report," Voss said.

"Here's my counteroffer," Orsen said. "You untie my captain, and I give you the report — and a demonstration of why killing me is the worst decision available to you."

Voss's head tilted, a fraction. Interest, in a man like him, was probably the closest thing he had to joy.

"Convince me," he said.

And Orsen Zhou — the woman who had spent a decade teaching herself to leave before anyone could ask her a question — smiled, and it was not a nice smile, and I felt the rope at my wrists go suddenly irrelevant.

"First," she said, "you should know that the report you want is a copy. The original is somewhere you'll never find it, held by someone who'll publish it if I don't send word by tomorrow's tide. Second—" she lifted the oilcloth bundle slightly, "—this is the dam's own monitoring log, and it says the collapse is measurable, dated, and *foreseen*. Which means the day it fails, this stops being an accident and becomes a decision. Your decision. You're not holding hostages anymore, Mr. Voss. You're holding evidence."

* * *

*Orsen*

Let the record show I bluffed approximately forty percent of that.

The copy claim: true, mostly — Pak Uda had sealed the original report into a weighted cache below the drowned village on our way up, and only he and I knew the mark. The tomorrow's-tide deadline: invented, but plausible, because tides are plausible and men like Voss distrust anything they can't schedule. The oilcloth bundle was real, though — the surveyor's monitoring log, pages of strain readings and seepage rates that read like a countdown, and every page of it signed, initialed, witnessed. Foreseeable. Documented. Damning.

What I hadn't planned for was the guide.

He stepped out from behind Voss's men like a man walking to his own execution, which, given the expression on his face, was roughly how it felt to him. Arif. The man who'd taken our money at the last landing, asked after Dara's fever, told Pak Uda where the fish ran. He wouldn't look at me. He looked at the river instead, at the weir going over its long white lip, and he said, in English, deliberately, so that everyone would understand:

"The sensor net on the valley road — I told them where you crossed. And the portage. I drew the portage."

Dara made a sound like a struck wire. Sef's hand found her shoulder again, harder this time.

"Why?" I asked him. Not cruelly. I genuinely wanted to know, because I had spent ten years meeting people at their worst and I had learned that the answer is almost never greed.

"My daughter's school," he said simply. "My wife's medicine. He showed me a list of what happens to villages that choose wrong." His jaw worked. "I chose wrong. I know it. I'm telling you I know it, so that whatever you do to me, you do it knowing I already did it to myself."

For a moment nobody moved. The weir applauded its endless empty audience.

It was Mei-Lin who broke it, tied to the bollard, blood in her hair, and what she said was: "Arif. Your family. Are they safe right now?"

He blinked. "...Yes."

"Then listen carefully, because I'm only going to say this once." She turned her head as far as the rope allowed, and her voice went flat and hard, the voice she used on cargoes and currents. "Voss needs this quiet. A firefight at the weir, a dead guide, a burned boat — that's noise, and noise reaches the coast, and noise is the one thing his employers can't buy off. Which means the moment he's finished with us, your family becomes his loose end. You sold us to buy them a week."

I watched the truth land on Arif like a dropped anchor. Watched him look at Voss, and Voss look back with that door-closed face that confirmed everything and promised nothing.

"So here's your way out," Mei-Lin went on. "You know these banks better than his soldiers ever will. You're going to help us, starting now, and you're going to be so useful that keeping you alive and talking becomes worth more to us than making you sorry. That's the deal. Take it or drown in what you chose."

Arif looked at me. I don't know what he was asking for — forgiveness, permission, a reason. I thought about a notebook full of closed lines, about every place I'd left before anyone could need me, about how easy it would be to hate him and how much cheaper hatred is than understanding.

"Your son's name is Budi," I said. "You told me he wants to be a ferry pilot. Help us, and maybe he grows up on a river that answers to something besides a mining company."

Something in his shoulders unlocked. He nodded once, and turned to Voss, and said, in the tone of a man reporting weather: "The water level's dropping. By afternoon there's a second crossing, two kilometers down, that your map doesn't show. If you want to move your men off this exposed bank before the crew's friends arrive—" he let that hang, generous with fiction, "—I'd move them now."

Voss studied him for a long moment. Studied me. Studied the oilcloth bundle, the brass tube, the bollard, the boat, the whole board of it, the way he must have studied the drowned surveyor once, deciding what a man was worth.

"An hour," he said finally, to me. "You'll give me the log, the copy's location, and the names of whoever holds it. In exchange, the captain lives, and I consider letting the rest of you float away." He smiled without any muscle above his mouth moving. "You have until the water drops. And Ms. Zhou — if you're lying about any part of it, I'll know, and I'll start with the loud twin."

Behind him, far upstream, a sound rolled down the valley — low, tectonic, wrong. A sound like the earth clearing its throat.

Pak Uda lifted his feet out of the water and stood up slowly, and the look on his old face was the look of a man reading a tide table that had no good news in it.

"That," he said, "was not thunder."
