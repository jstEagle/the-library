## 17. The Price of the River

The weir at dawn looked like something a child had built and a god had stepped on.

Concrete slabs tilted against each other like bad teeth, rebar sprouting from the fractures in gray whorls. Behind it, the reservoir sat flat and green and patient, holding back twenty years of rain with nothing but arithmetic and arrogance. I'd spent my whole career looking at structures and seeing how they'd fail. This one didn't require imagination. It required only time — and time, according to the surveyor's report zipped inside my jacket, was nearly up.

Mei-Lin stood beside me on the downstream apron, arms crossed, watching the far bank where Voss's men had set up their table. An actual table. A white cloth, weighted at the corners with stones. Two folding chairs. It would have been funny if it weren't so obviously a message: *we are civilized; you should be too; civilization is available for purchase.*

"He brought a table," Mei-Lin said.

"He wants us to think this is a transaction."

"Isn't it?"

"Transactions end with both parties walking away," I said. "This ends with one party walking away and the other party not needing to walk anywhere ever again."

She grunted. That was Mei-Lin's version of agreement, condolence, and breakfast all at once.

Behind us, the Kestrel sat low in the shallows where we'd grounded her, Sef and Dara pretending to work on the winch while actually working on watching the treeline. Pak Uda had insisted on coming to the parley — his river, his right — and stood now at my other shoulder like a wall that had decided to grow old but not soft. The village guide, Rian, whose betrayal had bought us all this morning light, was tied to a stilt on the boat under Dara's eye. He hadn't stopped crying since midnight. I found I couldn't hate him. That was the worst part of it. He'd done exactly what any of us might do if the consortium held his children's names on a list. Justice outside the law cuts both ways, Pak Uda had said, and then he'd said nothing else for an hour, which from Pak Uda was a sermon.

The sun came up over the escarpment and lit the reservoir's surface, and for a moment it was almost beautiful — that flat green mirror hiding a drowned village, a drowned history, and about forty million tons of water with an appointment to keep.

"Orsen." Mei-Lin's voice dropped. "If this goes wrong—"

"It'll go wrong."

"—then don't be clever. Be fast."

"I'm never both," I said. "It's my defining flaw."

Almost, she smiled. Then she squared her shoulders and we walked out onto the weir to meet the man who owned the law in these islands, or at least the part of it that carried a gun.

* * *

Ravel Voss was not what his file suggested.

The file — the dead surveyor's notes on him, gathered secondhand — described a brute, an enforcer, muscle with a badge. What sat down across the white cloth from me was a lean, gray-templed man with reading glasses folded in his breast pocket and the posture of someone who'd once been somebody's accountant. He poured coffee from a thermos into three cups. Three. He'd counted us.

"Ms. Zhou." He said my name like he was checking it off an inventory. "You've cost my employers a great deal of money. I want you to know I bear no personal animosity. Money is impersonal. That's what makes it reliable."

"You brought a table," I said.

"I find negotiations go better when people can put things down." He gestured at the report-shaped bulge under my jacket. "So. Put things down."

I didn't. Instead I sat, and Pak Uda lowered himself onto the concrete beside me with the unhurried gravity of a man who had decided that if death was coming, it could wait until he was comfortable. Mei-Lin stayed standing. Good. Someone should have a view of the treeline.

"The terms are simple," Voss said. "You give me the surveyor's report — all copies, all annotations, whatever you decoded from those charts. You give me your maps. In exchange, you and your crew board your boat and leave these islands unmolested. My employers will even reimburse your fuel losses. Generously." He sipped his coffee. "You strike me as a woman who appreciates generous exit terms."

"What happens to the dam?" I asked.

"Nothing you need to concern yourself with."

"A village of four hundred people is downstream of nothing I need to concern myself with."

His expression didn't change, which was itself an answer. "Ms. Zhou. Let me be honest with you, because I respect competence and you have been impressively competent. My employers' plan does not involve the village. Their plan involves the dam failing naturally — a tragic act of God, heavy rains, unforeseeable seepage. Insurance pays. The mine writes off the valley. The government holds a memorial service and grants new concessions as a gesture of healing. Four hundred people become a rounding error." He set down his cup. "Your report doesn't change that arithmetic. It only changes whether there are witnesses when it happens. So: the report, the maps, and everyone walks away. Or—" he spread his hands apologetically over the white cloth, "—everyone stays, permanently, and the story remains the same. Act of God. Unforeseeable."

"There's just one problem with unforeseeable," I said.

"And what's that?"

"I foresaw it."

For the first time, something moved behind his eyes. Not fear. Recalculation.

"Go on," he said carefully.

I reached into my jacket — slowly, hands visible, because Mei-Lin had told me to be fast and instead I was going to be precise — and pulled out not the report but a single sheet of my own paper, covered in my own cramped engineering hand. Stress figures. Seepage gradients. The uplift calculations along the dam's foundation that the dead surveyor had flagged in red twenty years ago and that the consortium had buried with him.

"This is my analysis. Independent of the surveyor's. Different methods, same conclusion. The dam fails within days — maybe hours, given the seepage I measured yesterday at the north abutment. And here's the part your employers haven't thought through." I laid the page flat on the white cloth between us and turned it to face him. "I dated it. I signed it. I'm a licensed engineer, Mr. Voss, from a jurisdiction your employers' lawyers can't scrub. If that dam breaks after this meeting — after I told you, in writing, with figures — then every court in the world will read this page and understand that the collapse wasn't an act of God. It was an act of negligence. Foreseeable, documented, and ignored by the men who owned the law." I leaned forward. "You wouldn't be their enforcer anymore. You'd be their accessory. There's a difference. Enforcers get pensions. Accessories get extradited."

The silence stretched. Somewhere upstream, water dripped through the dam's cracked face with a sound like a slow clock.

Voss picked up the page. He read it the way I imagine he'd once read ledgers — thoroughly, without sentiment — and I watched him arrive at the truth of it, which was that I wasn't bluffing. The figures were real. The signature was real. The trap was real, and I had built it out of nothing but honesty, which is the most dangerous construction material there is.

"You're stalling," he said finally.

"Obviously."

"Why?"

"Because the dam is going to fail before your employers finish their paperwork," I said, "and every hour you spend drinking coffee with me is an hour the river gets closer to making its own statement. You can't kill me fast enough to un-know what I know, Mr. Voss. The knowledge is already distributed. It's in my head, in Pak Uda's head, in Mei-Lin's, written in two hands on paper in two locations. Your employers' only clean exit died the moment I did the math. Now they have exactly one move left that doesn't end with them in a courtroom." I let that sit. "Want me to say it out loud?"

He didn't ask me to. He already knew: *drain the reservoir.* Controlled release. Spend the water before the water spent them. It would cost the mine its dry-season tailings plan, cost somebody upstairs their bonus, cost Voss his reputation for solving problems quietly. But it beat prison, and unlike loyalty, prison has no price ceiling.

Voss folded my page precisely in half, then in half again, and put it in his breast pocket next to his reading glasses.

"You're asking me to betray my employers," he said.

"I'm asking you to stop being their instrument long enough to save your own skin. It's the same thing, but you'll feel better about it."

Something almost like humor crossed his face. "My employers will send someone else."

"They'll send someone slower. I've met their hiring standards."

And that was when the clock ran out.

* * *

It started as a sound — a deep, subterranean groan from the dam's north abutment, the kind of sound concrete makes when it stops being a structure and starts being a rumor. I felt it through the soles of my boots before I heard it through the air, twenty years of pressure renegotiating its lease.

Then the seepage stream at the abutment — the thin thread of water I'd measured yesterday — went from a trickle to a torrent in the space of a breath, carrying gray sediment and, briefly, something that glittered. Old rebar. Pieces of the dam's own skeleton, coming home.

"Move," I said, and for once nobody needed telling twice.

We came off the weir at a run — Voss's men scattering toward the gunboat, Voss himself standing frozen for one full second with his coffee cup still in his hand, an accountant watching the ledger catch fire. Then he dropped the cup and moved too, faster than I'd have credited him, shouting orders in a voice stripped suddenly of all its civilization.

Mei-Lin grabbed my arm. "The village—"

"Fourteen kilometers. At full pool breach, the flood front takes—" I did the math at a sprint, which is the only way I do math anymore, "—forty minutes. Maybe less if the abutment goes all at once."

"The Kestrel can't outrun it loaded."

"She doesn't have to outrun it. She has to get ahead of it and warn them." I skidded down the bank toward the boat, where Sef and Dara were already casting off the lines, Dara screaming questions I couldn't answer yet. Behind us the groan came again, longer, deeper, and a crack walked up the dam face like lightning drawn in reverse.

Pak Uda caught up with me at the waterline. The old man's breathing was ragged but his eyes were clear, fixed on the reservoir, on the drowned village beneath it, on everything rising toward the surface at last.

"The gate," he said. "The old spillway gate. Your surveyor marked it. If it could be opened—"

"If it could be opened, we could drain the pool in a controlled way. Buy the village time. Maybe save it entirely." I looked at the gate housing on the dam's flank — rusted, seized, twenty years unloved, and reachable only by a catwalk that was already trembling. "It's not a question of whether it can open. It's a question of who turns the wheel."

"I know that gate," Pak Uda said. "I helped build it, before they sealed it. Before they sold us." He was already moving toward the catwalk, and I understood with terrible clarity that I was watching a man walk toward the last job of his life, and that no argument I owned would stop him.

"Pak Uda—"

"Orsen." He turned, and smiled, and it was the kindest smile I have ever been hit with. "You gave the river back its voice. Let an old diver give it one more door."

The dam groaned again. The crack reached the crest.

And somewhere below us, fourteen kilometers downstream, four hundred people were waking up to an ordinary morning, and I started to run.
