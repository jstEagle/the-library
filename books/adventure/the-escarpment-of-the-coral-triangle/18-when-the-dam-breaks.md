## 18. When the Dam Breaks

**Orsen**

The dam told us it was dying before any of us said it out loud.

I'd been on the survey platform since before dawn with the theodolite and a plumb line and the particular dread of an engineer who has done this arithmetic before. Concrete speaks in cracks, and this one had learned to shout. A seam across the downstream face that hadn't been there when we arrived two days ago now ran the width of the spillway like a scar on a knuckle. Water wept through it in threads. Threads become ropes. Ropes become rivers.

I marked the crack's end with chalk, climbed down, and walked back to camp doing the numbers in my head because writing them down would make them real.

They were real anyway.

"Three days," I told Mei-Lin at the fire. "Maybe four if the rain holds off."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then it's tomorrow."

Sef looked up from the winch he was re-greasing. Dara stopped mid-joke. Pak Uda set down his coffee with the unhurried care of a man who had spent sixty years learning what water does when you stop asking its opinion.

"The villages," Mei-Lin said.

"Karang Selatan has maybe nine hours of warning once it goes. The lower settlements, less. And the flood won't spread evenly." I crouched and drew in the ash with a stick: the valley, the dam, the river bending east below it toward the escarpment. "It'll go where the river wants to go. Straight down the main channel. Through everything."

"So we run," Dara said. "Warn who we can, get high, let it happen."

"That's option one." I turned the stick over. "Here's option two."

Mei-Lin's eyes narrowed. She'd known me long enough by now to read my face the way I read concrete — not for what it showed, but for what was about to fail underneath.

"There's an old emergency breach channel on the west abutment," I said. "Cut into the rock when they built the thing, then sealed with a plug wall when the consortium decided controlled failure planning was bad for shareholder confidence. It's designed for exactly this. If we blow or cut the plug, the dam fails *early* — but it fails sideways, into the old channel, into the dry gorge that runs north away from every village on this river."

"You want to break the dam on purpose," Sef said slowly.

"I want to choose how it breaks. There's a difference between a murder and a surgery, and the difference is mostly where you make the cut."

Pak Uda laughed — a low, gravel sound. "The sea taught me this. You cannot stop the wave. You can only tell it where to spend itself."

"It's forty feet of reinforced plug," Mei-Lin said. "And we have no explosives worth the name."

"We have the Kestrel's engine room, a boatload of salvaged blasting gelatin from the wreck site, and me." I met her eyes. "It's a terrible plan. It's also the only plan that ends with the same number of people alive tomorrow as tonight."

She looked at the ash drawing for a long moment. Then she stood, and the captain came up in her spine like a mast rising.

"Sef, Dara — gelatin and det cord, all of it, up to the abutment. Pak Uda, I need you on the spillway gate. If we're going to drop the reservoir, we drop it through your gate first, take the pressure off the face while Orsen works." She turned to me last. "How long?"

"If nothing goes wrong? Six hours."

"And something will go wrong."

"Something always goes wrong. That's why I budgeted six."

* * *

**Voss**

Ravel Voss had been awake for thirty-one hours, and he considered sleep a professional liability he could no longer afford.

From the ridge above the valley, through field glasses, he watched them move on the dam like ants on a carcass. He counted five. The woman Zhou was easy to pick out even at this distance — she moved like an engineer, which is to say she moved like someone who believed the world obeyed rules, and he had built his career on the discovery that most people did, right up until the moment you gave them a reason not to.

The file lay open on the rock beside him. Surveyor Aldous Wren, drowned, ruled accidental, eight years ago. His final report: missing. Until a tide cave on a reef island coughed it back up and put it into the hands of a woman whose fingerprints were all over three closed investigations on two continents.

The consortium's instruction had been elegant in its simplicity: the dam fails, nature is blamed, the insurance settles, the villages are relocated to company housing at company wages, and the report stays lost. A tragedy with paperwork. Voss had arranged dozens of tragedies with paperwork. They were the cleanest kind.

But Zhou was going to make the dam fail *loudly*. Deliberately. With witnesses and documentation and a breach channel that would point, like an arrow, at everything the company had buried upstream — including, if his intelligence was correct, the flooded ruins where Wren had hidden his full report.

He closed the glasses. Below him, four men waited with rifles and his standing order, which was simple: no witnesses leave the valley.

Voss checked his watch. Six hours, the engineers would need. He intended to give them two.

* * *

**Orsen**

The first hour went almost well, which should have frightened me more than it did.

Sef and Dara drilled the plug wall's charge pattern while I calculated the sequencing — the gelatin had to fire in a staggered wave, bottom to top, so the plug fell outward into the gorge instead of collapsing inward against the dam's own abutment. Get it wrong and we'd seal our own escape route under ten thousand tons of concrete. Get it right and we'd opened a door for a river.

Pak Uda worked the spillway gate above us, cranking it open inch by inch to bleed the reservoir down. The gate was older than he was and twice as stubborn; the mechanism shrieked like something being murdered, and every few minutes the crank handle kicked back hard enough to knock a lesser man off the catwalk. Pak Uda just grunted and leaned into it again, his bare feet finding purchase on wet steel the way other men's found barroom floors.

"Your gate's singing flat," Dara shouted up to him.

"Everything sings flat when it's honest!" he called back, and kept cranking.

At hour two, the rain started.

Not the polite kind. The kind that arrives already angry, hammering the reservoir into whitecaps, and I stood on the abutment watching the gauge climb and did the arithmetic again with colder numbers. Every centimeter of rise pressed harder on that crack. My six hours were evaporating.

"Mei-Lin!" I shouted over the din. "We fire early or we don't fire at all!"

"How early?"

"Now!"

* * *

**Mei-Lin**

Mei-Lin had captained the Kestrel through typhoons, customs seizures, and one memorable incident involving a rival salvage crew, a wedding, and a stolen generator. None of it had felt like this.

Because this time she wasn't deciding whether her crew lived. She was deciding whether nine hundred strangers she'd never met lived too, and the difference sat in her chest like ballast she couldn't jettison.

"FIRE IT!" she bellowed, and dropped behind the abutment wall.

The world went white, then deaf, then upside down.

The plug wall didn't explode so much as exhale — a rolling concussion she felt in her teeth and boots, and then the western face of the dam opened like a wound, and the river poured through the gap into the old channel with a sound Mei-Lin knew she would hear for the rest of her life. Not a roar. A groan. The sound of something enormous changing its mind.

For eleven seconds, it worked.

Then the main structure shuddered.

She felt it through the rock before she heard it — a deep structural complaint, the whole dam flexing, and Orsen was suddenly beside her at the edge, hair plastered to her face, staring at the crack across the spillway face.

"It's going," Orsen said. Not a question. A diagnosis. "The unloading shifted the load path. We bought the villages their channel but we pulled the trigger early. Downstream flood in minutes, not hours." She was already moving. "The spillway gate — if Uda can hold it wide, the main channel takes the surge gradual instead of all at once. It's the difference between a wave and a wall."

"He can't hold that gate alone against full flow—"

"He's not alone." Orsen grabbed her arm. "Get everyone to high ground. Now. That's an order I don't have the rank to give."

Mei-Lin looked at the old man on the catwalk, small against the sky, both hands locked on the wheel, water sheeting over him in silver curtains. She looked at Orsen, already climbing.

"Go," Mei-Lin said. "Both of you come back. That's *my* order, and mine outranks yours."

* * *

**Voss**

Voss came down through the rain with his men spread in a skirmish line, and the world had betrayed him in the most fundamental way: his tragedy was refusing to stay quiet.

The breach channel had changed everything. A natural collapse could be explained. A surgically engineered breach, documented by engineers, pointed directly at twenty years of suppressed reports. The woman had turned his clean catastrophe into evidence.

He found them at the spillway catwalk — Zhou and the old diver, hauling the gate wheel together, silhouetted against the falling reservoir.

"Zhou!" His voice carried over the water. He raised the rifle, not to shoot — not yet. Shooting the engineer stopped the gate work, and stopping the gate work meant a wall of water through Karang Selatan, and even Voss preferred his atrocities deniable. "Step away from the gate!"

She didn't step away. She didn't even turn around. She just shouted back, over her shoulder, in the tone of a woman correcting a junior colleague:

"Voss! If that gate closes, the surge hits the village as a wall instead of a wave! You want to explain to your employers why their relocation costs just tripled — help us hold it!"

For one absurd moment, he actually considered it.

Then his men reached the catwalk stairs, and the moment passed, and Voss raised his rifle to fire — not at her, at the wheel housing, to jam the mechanism—

—and Pak Uda saw him.

The old man did something Voss would think about for years afterward. He didn't duck. He didn't run. He stepped *between* the rifle and the gate wheel, spreading his arms across the mechanism like a man sheltering a candle flame, and he looked down the barrel with the calm of someone who had spent his life diving into places everyone else agreed were fatal.

"The river remembers who held the door," Pak Uda said.

Voss fired.

* * *

**Orsen**

I heard the shot and felt the world tilt.

Pak Uda slid down the wheel, and I caught him before the water could, and his blood went pink in the runoff and vanished, the way water takes everything eventually. His hands were still on the spokes. Even dying, even sliding, he'd locked them there, and the gate stayed open.

"Uda. Uda, look at me. Hold on. Hold the—"

"I am holding," he said, and smiled at me with blood in his teeth. "You hold too, engineer. The gate. Not me. The gate matters."

Above us the dam made its final argument — a crack like the sky splitting, and then the whole eastern face began to move, sloughing, the reservoir leaning through it in a gray cataract. The surge hit the open spillway and roared through, and because the gate was open — because an old man was dying on the wheel and holding it open — the flood went down the main channel as a rising, raging, survivable torrent instead of a killing wall.

Voss's men were scrambling for the ridge. Voss stood a moment longer, looking at the old man, at me, at the water taking everything — and then the catwalk lurched under the flood load and he was gone into the rain, and I did not have the luxury of hating him properly because I was hauling on the wheel beside a dead man's hands.

The gate held. The flood spent itself down the channel, past Karang Selatan's stilt houses, which shook and groaned and stood. Past the low fields, which drowned and would drain. Past the church and the school and the jetty, all still there, all still standing, because a diver from Pulau Karang had decided where a river got to spend its rage.

When it was over, Dara and Sef came down through the mud and took him from me gently, and Mei-Lin stood in the rain with her cap in her hands, and nobody said anything for a long time, because there is nothing you can say that weighs as much as a man.

I knelt in the wreckage of the catwalk and closed his eyes.

"The river remembers," I said. "I'll make sure of it."

Below us, far downstream, faint through the rain, a horn sounded — the Kestrel, riding out the surge at anchor, alive.

And upstream, half-buried in the new channel the breach had torn open, something pale glinted among the exposed roots of the old valley floor. Metal. Man-made. A sealed case, wedged where no flood should ever have reached it — except a flood had just moved a mountain.

Wren's hiding place. Laid bare by the very disaster he'd died trying to prevent.

I wiped the rain from my face and started climbing toward it.
