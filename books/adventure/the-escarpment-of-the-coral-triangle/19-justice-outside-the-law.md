## 19. Justice Outside the Law

*Orsen*

The river took three days to remember its manners.

I stood on the escarpment road above the valley on that third morning and watched it happen — the brown fury thinning to green, the drowned palms standing up out of the floodwater like men rising from a long meeting they hadn't wanted to attend. Downstream, where the water had gone instead of through the villages, people were already wading back into their houses with buckets, laughing in that ragged way people laugh when they've spent a week expecting to die and haven't.

Pak Uda was not among them. That arithmetic sat in my chest like a stone I'd swallowed whole and couldn't cough up.

"You're doing the thing again," Mei-Lin said beside me. She had a thermos of coffee she'd been guarding since dawn and a face that suggested sharing it would be an act of charity she hadn't yet decided to perform.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you stand very still and try to carry the whole world's weight by yourself because you've never trusted anyone else's back." She unscrewed the thermos, poured one cup, drank it herself. "It's inefficient. I'm telling you as a captain."

"I killed him," I said. Not the thing I'd meant to say. The other thing, the true one, which apparently had been waiting behind my teeth for three days for a gap in my defenses.

Mei-Lin looked at me for a long moment. The wind off the valley moved her gray-streaked hair around, and she didn't bother fixing it, which from her was practically a confession of grief.

"No," she said. "The consortium built a dam out of greed and lies and let it rot for twenty years because fixing it would have cost them a dividend. Voss came upriver to bury the evidence. Pak Uda held a gate so that four hundred people downstream would live. You were there. You didn't cause any of those things." She handed me the cup after all, which was how I knew she meant what came next. "But if you need someone to blame, blame accurately. It matters. Sloppy blame is how men like Voss get away with everything."

I drank the coffee. It was terrible — salvager coffee, brewed strong enough to strip paint — and it was the best cup of my life, mostly because she'd made two cups without saying so and was pretending otherwise.

"Voss," I said.

"Gone," she said. "Dara walked the whole wreck site at first light. No body. His boat's missing from the mooring below the weir."

"He'll surface."

"He always does. Men like that are buoyant. It's their one talent." She took the thermos back. "Which is why we're not done. Come on. We've got a broadcast to make before anyone in a clean uniform tells us what we're allowed to say."

* * *

The Kestrel's radio room had been built by Sef and Dara out of stubbornness and stolen parts, and it smelled like solder, mildew, and Dara's incense, which she claimed improved reception and which Sef claimed was a fire hazard. Both were right.

The surveyor's report lay open on the chart table between us — water-stained, mud-fingerprinted, wrapped now in three layers of plastic because none of us were taking chances with it again. Halim Wicaksono's name was on the cover page. Twenty years dead in a tide cave, and his handwriting still sharper than anything the consortium had ever produced. Every inspection he'd been ordered to falsify, he'd falsified twice: once for them, once for the record he hid in the flooded village where he'd grown up. Spalling concrete. Unreinforced spillway. A dam designed for thirty years, pushed past fifty, holding back a reservoir the size of a small sea.

"They'll call it an act of God," Dara said. She was at the transmitter, tuning by ear, her fingers moving over the dials the way I'd seen her move over engines — half technique, half conversation. "That's what they did with the ferry sinking off Selatan. Act of God. God gets blamed for a lot of things rich men sign off on."

"Then we make it expensive for God's lawyers," Mei-Lin said. "How many stations?"

"Eleven on the salvage net that'll run it unedited. Maybe twenty more if I beg." Dara glanced up. "And the coastal stations will pick it up once the fishing fleet starts repeating it. Fishermen love nothing more than being right about something the government denied."

Sef was in the doorway with a coil of antenna wire over his shoulder and the particular expression he got when he'd done math he didn't like. "There's a consortium cutter anchored off the delta mouth. Big one. They'll be jamming by noon if they're smart."

"Are they smart?" Mei-Lin asked.

"They're rich. Sometimes that looks the same from far away."

"Then we don't read it once," I said.

Everyone looked at me. I'd been quiet for three days — quieter than even my usual, which the crew had learned to read as either calculation or grief, and this time it had been both — but the plan had assembled itself in my head somewhere between the coffee and the walk down from the escarpment, and it was simple in the way all good engineering is simple.

"We read it until they can't stop us. Different frequencies, different times, different voices. Dara reads the technical sections — she can make concrete spalling sound interesting, I've heard her do it. I read the charts. Mei-Lin reads the cover letter, because her voice sounds like a woman who has never once been lied to successfully." I looked at the report. "Halim wrote this so it couldn't be buried quietly. So we don't bury it quietly. We make it loud enough that every official who wants to look away has to do it in public."

"And when the cutter comes for us?" Sef asked.

Mei-Lin smiled, and it was not a warm smile. "Then they come for the Kestrel, in front of forty fishing boats full of people who just heard exactly why their river died. Let them explain that to a national audience." She set the thermos down like a gavel. "We go on air at noon. Orsen — you're with me. We've got one more document to find."

* * *

The second document wasn't paper. It was Pak Uda.

Not literally — though Dara argued for it, fiercely, tearfully, wanting to read his name into the broadcast along with Halim's, wanting the dead man's voice present somehow. What we actually had was better and worse: his dive log, thirty years of entries, including the four dives he'd made into the flooded village in years past, when the consortium's divers weren't watching, checking on Halim's cache because old Uda had known it was there all along and had never told a living soul. He'd kept the secret the way he kept everything — patiently, underwater, waiting for the current to change.

The last entry was dated nine days ago. *Took the girl down. She swims angry but she swims true. If I don't come up, tell Mei-Lin the air line was fine and it was just my time. Don't let her argue with the sea about it.*

He'd written it before we went under. He'd known.

I read that entry aloud in the second broadcast, at dusk, while the cutter's jamming crackled across the lower bands and Dara hopped frequencies like a rabbit crossing a road under fire. My voice only broke once, on *my time*, and I let it break, because Mei-Lin had said something the night before that I kept turning over: *People trust a voice that costs something.* So I gave them the cost. Four hundred listeners on the salvage net, then the fishing fleet, then — by the third day, when the national radio service could no longer pretend it hadn't heard — the whole coast.

By the fourth day, the government that had spent twenty years looking elsewhere announced, with a straight face, that it had discovered corruption in the Coral Triangle Mining Consortium and would be conducting arrests. An embarrassed government is a wonderful engine. It moves fast precisely because it's trying to outrun its own reflection.

They came upriver in white boats with new paint and cameras. They arrested the consortium's regional director at his own dock, mid-lunch, and the photographs ran everywhere: a fat man in a linen shirt, hands cuffed, looking genuinely astonished, as though the law were weather that had never once rained on him before. They arrested the safety officer who'd signed Halim's falsified reports. They arrested two ministers whose names meant nothing to me and everything to everyone else.

Nobody arrested Ravel Voss, because Ravel Voss was nowhere to be found.

* * *

*Mei-Lin*

I have chased wrecks for thirty years, and I will tell you the truth about salvage that no insurance company admits: you never recover everything. There is always something the sea keeps, or something you leave behind because taking it would cost more than it's worth. Justice, it turns out, works the same way.

We got the directors. We got the signatures, the memos, the dividend schedules that proved they'd known for six years the dam was failing and had voted, in writing, to defer maintenance. We got a village that still stood and a river that ran free and a name — Halim Wicaksono — spoken on the national news by anchors who pronounced it carefully, having practiced.

We did not get Voss.

I knew it before Dara confirmed it. You spend enough years reading water and you learn to read men, and Voss was the kind who doesn't drown, doesn't burn, doesn't stay where the evidence is. When the white boats came upriver, he was already three days gone, south and east, into the ten thousand islands of the delta where a man with money and no name can disappear the way silt disappears into the sea — not gone, just distributed.

The government put out a warrant. I looked at it. It described a tall man, foreign, last seen wearing dark clothing. Half the enforcers on this coast match that description. The warrant was theater, and everyone signing it knew it. Somewhere in a ministry office, someone had decided that one escaped enforcer was an acceptable remainder — the part of the equation you round down and hope nobody checks.

Orsen checked. Of course she checked. She stood in my cabin with the warrant flimsy in her hand and her jaw set in the way I had learned, over one hard month, meant she was about to propose something reckless and well-engineered.

"The delta," she said.

"No," I said.

"You didn't ask what—"

"I know what. The delta. Track him. Find him. Finish it." I took the warrant out of her hand and set it on my chart table, weighting the corner with the brass shell casing I keep there for luck, which is a superstition I recommend to no one. "Sit down, engineer."

She sat. That was new. A month ago she'd have stood there burning until I gave in or threw her overboard.

"Here's what happens if you chase him," I said. "You go into the delta alone, because the crew won't split again — we settled that on the oxbow, and I don't revisit settled things. Alone, against a man who has money, contacts, and nothing left to lose, in ten thousand islands of mangrove where every local either fears him or works for him. Best case, you find him and one of you dies. Worst case, you find him and both of you do, and the consortium's lawyers use your corpse to argue the whole case was personal vendetta, and some minister uses that argument to quietly release the directors we just got locked up." I let that sit. "Is that justice?"

"It's unfinished," she said.

"Everything is unfinished. That's what makes it justice instead of revenge. Revenge finishes. It ends with somebody dead and everybody else exactly as broken as before." I leaned forward. "You want to know what finished looks like? Ask the elders. Ask the kid who gave you the carved token — what's his name?"

"Tarek."

"Tarek. Ask Tarek what he wants. He wants a jetty that doesn't flood. He wants a school with a roof. He wants the river running clean past his grandmother's house. Nobody in that village woke up this morning praying for Ravel Voss's head. They woke up praying for morning, and for the first time in twenty years, they got it." I tapped the warrant. "Let the government fail to catch him in public. That failure is a splinter in their foot. Every year he's out there, it festers. And someday — not today — some ambitious prosecutor with a career to build will pull it out and use it. Our job was the river. The river is done."

Orsen was quiet a long time. Outside, I could hear Sef and Dara arguing about the winch, which meant the world was returning to its proper orbit.

"And if he comes for us?" she said finally. "For you. For the crew. For—" She stopped, and I watched her decide whether to finish the sentence, and watched her finish it anyway, which was its own kind of progress. "For me."

"Then he comes to a boat full of people who will see him coming," I said. "Because that's the difference between you a month ago and you now. A month ago you'd have faced him alone, and alone is how he wins. Now?" I nodded toward the deck, toward the noise of my crew, my impossible, irreplaceable crew. "Now you'd face him with family. And family, Miss Zhou, is the one weapon they don't have a file on."

* * *

*Orsen*

On the fifth day, the elders came aboard.

They arrived in two longboats, solemn as a funeral and dressed like a wedding, which I've learned is simply how important days work here — grief and celebration sharing a wardrobe. Elder Sumarni came up the ladder last, refusing Sef's hand on principle, and stood on the Kestrel's deck looking around at the rust and rope and radio antennas with the expression of a woman inspecting a house she might buy.

"You are the ones," she said. It wasn't a question.

"We're some of the ones," Mei-Lin said. "Pak Uda was the main one."

Sumarni bowed her head briefly. Then she produced, from her woven bag, a bundle of cloth, and unwrapped it with the care of a surgeon. Inside was a brass tube — older than the one from the cave, dented, crusted with river mud — and a folded sheet of oilcloth.

"Halim's mother was my aunt," she said. "When he went to work for the mining company, he gave her this and said, *Keep it where the water can't reach.* She kept it under her floor for twenty years. After the flood, when we dug out the houses—" her voice caught, steadied; these people had practice at that too — "we found it. Water reached everywhere, in the end. But brass is brass." She held it out to me. "The radio people say there are papers inside. More names. We thought it should come to the ones who shout."

I took it. It was heavier than it looked, the way true things usually are.

Inside, later, in the radio room with the whole crew crowded around pretending not to crowd, we found eleven pages in Halim's hand — earlier pages, the beginning of the record, including the names of the three other surveyors on his original team and what had happened to each of them. One transferred and died in a car accident. One resigned and emigrated. One was reassigned to a site in the highlands and never heard from again.

And one page that wasn't Halim's at all. Different ink, different hand, smaller and more precise than mine will ever be. A training exercise, a bridge load calculation, corrected in red — and at the bottom, a signature I hadn't seen in fifteen years and had never once stopped hearing.

My mentor's signature. On Halim Wicaksono's paper. In this valley, decades ago, teaching the man whose death had pulled me up this river — the same man whose methods, whose notation, whose stubborn doubled records I had inherited without ever knowing from whom.

Dara noticed my face before I could arrange it into anything presentable. "What?"

"This page," I said. "This isn't his writing. This is—" I stopped. Started again. "Someone taught him. Someone I knew."

Mei-Lin looked at me over the lamplight, and I saw her doing the same arithmetic I was: the closed lines on my map, the drowned surveyor's mirrored routes, and now this — a thread running backward through my whole exile, through every bridge I'd fled and every job I'd abandoned, pulling tight toward something I couldn't see yet.

"Well," she said dryly, capping the thermos. "Sounds like the river's not done with you after all."

Below us, the freed water ran silver toward the sea, and somewhere in the delta, a patient man was counting on us never to follow.

I intended to disappoint him.
