## 20. What Orsen Buried

The fire pit behind the rebuilt jetty cookhouse was the same one the crew had used for the oxbow night, back when I still flinched at the word *crew*. The stones had been rearranged since — Sef's doing; he couldn't leave a circle of rocks alone any more than Dara could leave a locked box alone — and someone had dragged a split log over as a bench. The log was mine now, unofficially. Nobody said so. That's how belonging works when it's done right: nobody announces it.

I sat down after midnight with my notebook in my lap and a tin cup of Pak Uda's terrible coffee substitute warming my hands, and I did not open the notebook for a long time.

Twenty-two years I'd carried that thing. Longer than some marriages last, longer than most ships. It had started life as an ordinary surveyor's field book, the kind you could buy from any chandler between Singapore and Suva, and it had become something else entirely: a map of closed lines. Every defunct rail spur, every abandoned branch, every line that had once carried ore or timber or people and then stopped. I'd walked them. Measured them. Noted the gauge, the ballast condition, the year of abandonment where records allowed and educated guesses where they didn't. Four hundred and eleven entries. I know the number because I counted them once during a bad night in a Darwin hostel, counting being cheaper than sleeping.

It wasn't a hobby. Let's be honest about that, since honesty is apparently the theme of the evening. A hobby is stamps. A hobby is competitive topiary. What I had was a religion of departure — a way of loving places only after I'd left them, which is the safest form of love there is. A closed line can't ask you to stay. A closed line doesn't have opinions about your character. You can trace its route with your finger and feel useful, competent, even tender, without ever risking the one thing I'd sworn off risking, which was mattering somewhere long enough to be blamed for what happens next.

Because that was the wound under all of it, wasn't it. The Kestang Viaduct. Eleven years ago, a bridge I had inspected and signed off on, folded into the river with a morning train on it. Nine dead. My signature on the inspection report, my name in the inquiry transcript, my face on enough front pages that I learned to sleep in railway stations because they were the one place nobody looked at you twice. The board struck me off. The consortium that owned the line — yes, *that* consortium, the same tentacled creature I'd spent this whole miserable adventure fighting upstream — buried the maintenance records that would have shown the corrosion reports had been falsified two levels above me. I found out three years later, from a drunk former clerk in a Kota Kinabalu bar, and by then proving it meant going back, going public, going *findable*. So instead I went sideways. And kept going sideways for eleven years, collecting closed lines like other people collect regrets.

Except here's the thing about running sideways: eventually every direction is behind you.

I'd learned that at the source of the river, holding a dead surveyor's report while a dam came apart above me. I'd learned it again watching Pak Uda hold the spillway gate with his hands and his shoulders and finally just his will, buying us the ninety seconds that bought the village its future. He didn't live to see the water settle. None of us got to thank him properly, which is the specific cruelty of sacrifice — it spends itself before the receipt arrives.

So. The notebook.

I opened it. The paper had gone soft and tea-colored at the edges, swollen from humidity, stained with salt and diesel and one memorable incident involving squid ink that Dara still brought up at dinner parties we didn't have. Entry 411 was the spur above the drowned valley — the one I'd found during the portage, the old logging bed through the green hell that cut four days off our march. The last line I'd ever added to the map of things that end.

I turned back to entry 1. A narrow-gauge tramway outside Sandakan, abandoned 1943, rails salvaged, sleepers rotted. I'd been twenty-six. I remembered the light that day, the way the jungle had already taken the cutting back, ferns growing up through the ties like the earth filing its paperwork. I remembered thinking: *here is a thing that stopped, and the world continued.* I'd found that thought comforting then. Sitting here now, it just sounded lonely.

"You're going to do it."

Dara. Of course. She moved like a cat burglar who'd gone respectable, and she'd clearly been watching from the cookhouse doorway long enough to read the situation. She crossed the sand and dropped onto the log beside me without asking, which was also new, and also not.

"Do what," I said.

"Burn it." She nodded at the notebook. "You've been carrying it around for two days like a man deciding whether to propose or resign. Either way, everyone can tell."

"It's a survey record. There's historical value—"

"Orsen." She said my name the way Mei-Lin said *Kestrel* — with ownership, with the particular tenderness of someone who has decided a thing is theirs and intends to maintain it. "You memorized it years ago. Don't look shocked. You recite gauge widths in your sleep. Sef timed you once."

"He *what*?"

"Fourteen minutes, full circuit, entries one through sixty. Very soothing, he says. Like a metronome that knows about ballast." She plucked the tin cup from my hands and drank my coffee, which was punishment for both of us. "The book isn't the map. You're the map. Burning it just means the rest of us don't have to watch you check the exits anymore."

I wanted to argue. I want to report that I argued, that I mounted a defense of archival preservation and personal autonomy, that I won on points. What actually happened was that I sat there with a soft old book in my lap and felt the exact weight of it — four hundred grams, maybe less — and understood that I had been treating it like ballast when it was really anchor chain.

* * *

Mei-Lin found me at dawn, or rather I found her, standing at the rail of the *Kestrel* with her hands behind her back, watching the channel the way she always did, as if the sea might try something. The salvage season was restarting. There was a barge aground off the southern reef and a consortium receiver's office suddenly very eager to settle claims quietly, now that the national government had arrested three of its officers and the newspapers had gotten hold of the words *foreseeable collapse* and *twenty-year cover-up*. Justice outside the law, followed by justice inside it, arriving late and embarrassed but arriving. Voss, meanwhile, had vanished into the delta like oil into water. Unfinished business. I caught myself scanning gunboats' silhouettes more often than I admitted.

"Voss is still out there," Mei-Lin said, without turning. She had a gift for starting conversations in the middle.

"I know."

"And you're staying anyway." It wasn't quite a question. It was the verbal equivalent of her testing a winch cable — applying load, watching for stretch.

"I'm staying anyway."

"Hm." She turned then, and gave me the look she reserved for machinery that had surprised her by working. "The elders asked me to pass along a request. They want a survey. Rail spur, up from the coast road, to bring trade back to the village. They asked for you by name." A pause, freighted. "They asked for *Wendell Brightwater*, actually, which is the name you gave the harbormaster, which means either you've been lying to everyone or you've been lying to yourself, and I've decided I don't care which. But the elders mean it. They're asking *you*."

"They barely know me."

"They know you led them out of the green hell. They know you stood on a dam face and told them the truth about how long they had. In villages, that's a résumé." She pushed off the rail. "Survey's not till the dry season. You've got time to decide whether you're the person who takes jobs like that or the person who finishes them." She paused at the companionway. "For the record, I think you're the second one. I don't say that often. Enjoy it."

Then she was gone below, leaving me with the morning and the decision, which is Mei-Lin's whole management style: hand you the weight and walk away so you can't pretend anyone else is holding it.

* * *

I burned the notebook that evening.

Not dramatically. I want that on the record. No wind, no speech, no audience except Sef, who had materialized with a toolbox he didn't need and stayed leaning against the cookhouse wall with the studied casualness of a man pretending not to attend a funeral. The tide was going out. The fire pit held last night's coals, banked and patient.

I tore the pages out in sections and fed them in slowly, because a surveyor respects combustion the way she respects rivers: it does what it wants, and you work with it. Entry 1 went first, the Sandakan tramway, and I watched the ink lift and curl. Then the coastal spurs, the mountain branches, the lines through country I'd crossed in the rain with my collar up and my heart shut. Each page took the flame with a little sigh, the way old paper does, and each one I let go felt less like losing something than setting down luggage I'd mistaken for a spine.

Somewhere around entry two hundred I stopped reading the entries. That surprised me. I'd expected ceremony, grief, some grand internal reckoning. Instead what I felt was mostly arithmetic — the slow tallying of years spent walking away from one collapsed bridge, and the discovery that the walking hadn't been keeping me safe, it had been keeping me *small*, and smallness had never once saved anybody on the *Kestrel*. Smallness wouldn't have held the spillway gate. Smallness doesn't build jetties or fix winches overnight or stand in front of Ravel Voss and talk about accessory liability until a killer checks his watch. Everything good that had happened in the last months had happened because I'd stopped, and everything I'd protected by running had turned out to be worth approximately nothing.

The cover went last. Stamped inside it, faded, was the supplier's mark from the chandlery in Sandakan where I'd bought it at twenty-six. I held it a moment longer than the rest. Then in it went, and the fire took it, and I sat back on my heels and discovered that the space where the notebook usually lived — breast pocket, always, against the heartbeat, like a pacemaker for cowardice — felt strange and light and enormous.

Sef pushed off the wall and crouched beside me. He didn't say anything for a while, which is one of the ten thousand reasons I'd have died for him by then.

"Dara says you memorized it," I said finally.

"She's exaggerating." A beat. "Entries one through sixty."

"Unbelievable."

"There's a copy," he added, looking at the fire. "Of the important ones. The portage route, the spur above the valley, the old logging grades. Dara made it. Said you'd want it eventually and would never ask." He shrugged. "She was right, obviously. It's in the chart table. We figured if you're going to build a line that stays open, you'll need to know where the closed ones are. Can't connect to nothing."

I laughed — actually laughed, wet-eyed and undignified, at the sheer presumptuousness of these people, planning my future without consulting me, correctly. "You're all impossible."

"We're a crew," Sef said, as if that explained everything, and the infuriating thing is that it did.

* * *

The letter took me three days.

Not because it was long. Because every draft came out wrong. The first was a legal brief, all clause numbers and corrosion data, and it read like a man hiding behind engineering the way I'd hidden behind departures. The second was a confession, groveling and shapeless, and I burned that one in the cookstove out of self-respect. The third I wrote at the chart table under the lamp while Dara pretended to inventory spare parts six inches away, and it came out simple, which is the hardest register there is:

*I am the engineer who signed the Kestang Viaduct inspection. You struck me off for negligence. I have spent eleven years letting you believe it, because the truth belonged to men with lawyers and I had a notebook and a head start. Enclosed are copies of the maintenance falsifications — dates, signatures, the clerk's affidavit, and the original corrosion surveys your contractor altered. I am sending these to you and, simultaneously, to the regional press and the families of the nine. I no longer require exoneration to survive. But the record requires correction, and I find I am finally the sort of person who corrects records. Come and find me if you like. I'm easy to locate now. That's rather the point.*

I signed it with my real name. Orsen Zhou. First time in eleven years I'd written it anywhere but customs forms under protest. My hand shook on the final stroke, and then, absurdly, steadied, as if the name had been waiting all this time to be used and had gotten impatient.

There was a mail boat Thursday mornings. Dara rowed me out to it — insisted, actually, over my objection that I was capable of operating a dinghy, to which she replied that this wasn't about capability, it was about witnesses, and I've learned not to fight Dara on matters of ritual. The mail boat's skipper, a leathery woman named Auntie Ros who had been delivering the archipelago's secrets for forty years, took the envelope, read the address, raised one eyebrow at the engineers' board letterhead, and said, "Trouble?"

"No," I said, and found it was true. "Housekeeping."

She stamped it, dropped it in the canvas sack with the rest of the world's correspondence, and cast off. I watched the envelope go. Somewhere downstream of that little boat, in offices I'd spent a decade orbiting without entering, people were going to open it and discover that the woman they'd ruined had come back not for revenge and not for pardon, but for the boring, brutal, permanent work of making the record true. Some of them would panic. One or two might even be honest. The consortium's local officers were already in custody; the rot went higher; my letter was a match thrown into a structure that had, thanks to a dead surveyor and a dying diver and a crew of salvagers who refused to leave me alone, finally become flammable.

Dara watched the boat shrink. "Feel different?" she asked.

I checked. Honestly checked, the way I'd survey a bridge — load applied, observe for deflection.

"Lighter," I said. "Which is terrifying."

"Nah." She shipped the oars and let us drift. "That's just what it feels like when the ballast's right. Boat rides higher. Handles better. Takes some getting used to." She grinned at me, that needle-toothed grin that had needled me upriver and half back down again. "You'll manage. You're going to be busy. Mei-Lin wants the *Kestrel* re-plated before dry season, the elders want their jetty finished, and Sef found something in the engine room he refuses to describe, which means it's either brilliant or on fire."

Above us, on the escarpment, the morning sun was climbing toward the ridge where I'd soon stand and watch the river run unbroken to the sea. Below us, the village hammering had already started — the jetty rebuild, Pak Uda's name going into the pilings in the only monument that mattered, which is work that holds.

I put my hand flat on the bottom of the empty dinghy, feeling the water move underneath, carrying us somewhere specific, on purpose, together.

For the first time in eleven years, I wasn't checking the exits.
