## 21. A Line That Stays Open

### Mei-Lin

I have sunk three boats in my life and rebuilt two of them, so when the elders of the river village asked if we could also rebuild their jetty, I said yes before my mouth had finished consulting my back. The Kestrel's spare timber was green and crooked. The tide here ran contrary to every chart I owned. And the village's last jetty had been built by a consortium contractor who, judging by the pilings we pulled out, had been paid by the nail and delivered by the handful.

"Your people built this," I told the headman, holding up a piling that crumbled like old bread between my fingers.

He had the grace to look embarrassed. "They built it cheap," he said. "We paid full price."

That, in a sentence, was twenty years of the consortium in these islands, and it was why I found myself at dawn on a mudflat with a level in one hand and a hammer in the other, directing salvagers who were better at taking things apart than putting them together.

Sef and Dara worked the pile driver like they'd been born to it, which, knowing them, they nearly had. Orsen Zhou stood hip-deep in the shallows with her notebook open, calling corrections in that flat, certain voice of hers — *two degrees off, bring it back* — and the twins obeyed without argument, which for the twins was unprecedented. They argued with me about the correct way to breathe. They obeyed her about load angles.

I didn't mind. A captain learns to recognize the moment a crew stops being a collection of hired hands and becomes something with a single spine. It happened somewhere between the weir and the flood, and nobody marked the day. These things don't announce themselves. You just notice, weeks later, that you've stopped counting who owes whom.

The villagers came down to help. Children hauled pegs. Old men who remembered the original jetty — their fathers' work, before the sale of the river — stood at the waterline and told us where the deep channel used to run, and they were right more often than my charts, which humbled me in a way I decided to keep private.

On the fourth day, the elders came aboard the Kestrel and sat in my cabin drinking tea that tasted of tin, and the headman said the thing I suspect they'd been rehearsing since the water receded.

"The road ended when the rail line closed," he said. "Our fathers laid track from the coast to the high valley. The consortium pulled the rails and sold them, but the bed is still there, sleeping under the ferns. If trade is to come back — real trade, ours — we need an engineer to survey a spur. To see if the old line can live again."

I looked at Orsen, who was standing in the doorway pretending she hadn't been listening, which fooled no one, least of all me.

"She's the best engineer I've ever shipped with," I said. "And I include myself."

"That isn't what I asked," the headman said gently.

No, it wasn't. What he'd asked was whether she would stay. Whether any of us would. And I watched her stand there in the doorway with her notebook against her chest like a shield she'd forgotten how to put down, and I understood that whatever answer she gave would cost her something either way.

I poured her tea and said nothing. Some negotiations a captain should lose on purpose.

### Sef

My sister says I fall in love with machines because they can't disappoint me, which is unfair, because I have been disappointed by many machines. But there's truth in it the way there's truth in most things Dara says, which is that it stings precisely because it lands.

The winch on the Kestrel and I have an understanding. The pile driver and I are still negotiating. And the old rail bed — that was love at first sight, though I didn't know it yet.

Orsen took us up it on the fifth morning after the jetty's first pilings went true. She'd found it during the portage, back when we were hauling the boat through green hell and she was carrying more than her share because Dara was cooking from the inside out with fever. She'd walked that overgrown spine of crushed stone like a woman reading a letter from home.

Now she walked it again with the whole crew behind her, and the ferns parted, and there it was: the ballast, the cut through the hillside, the rusted bones of a culvert still doing its job forty years after anyone checked.

"See this grade?" she said, running her hand along the embankment. "One percent, maybe less. Whoever surveyed this line understood water. Understood it better than the consortium ever did. Water doesn't fight you if you give it somewhere honest to go."

"You talk about water like it's a person," Dara said.

"Water is the only honest thing on any project," Orsen said. "Everything else lies. The water just tells you the truth faster."

We walked the line for six kilometers that day, her measuring, me pacing distances, Dara marking trees for clearing, Mei-Lin arguing with the headman's nephew about labor costs in the cheerful way Mei-Lin argues about everything, which is to say like a woman who has already won. Pak Uda wasn't with us. Pak Uda was never going to be with us again. But when we came to the crossing where the old line met the river, where the pilings of a trestle still stood in the current like patient soldiers, nobody said his name, and everybody thought it. The silence had his shape.

Orsen stood on the near abutment a long time, looking upstream toward the escarpment, where the river now ran free and loud over the scar where the dam had been.

"They'll want a bridge here," she said finally.

"They'll want everything," Dara said. "Villages are greedy that way. They want futures."

Orsen laughed — actually laughed, short and surprised, like the sound had escaped custody — and wrote something in her notebook, and I caught a glimpse of it over her shoulder. Not measurements. A list. Materials, labor, seasons. A schedule that ran years into the future.

I have read a lot of Orsen Zhou's notebooks, mostly by accident, some by snooping. Every page I'd ever seen was a map of ways out. Exits. Closed lines. This was the first page I'd ever seen her write that pointed forward instead of away.

I didn't say anything. You don't spook a wild thing by admiring it too loudly. But walking back down that sleeping rail bed in the gold light, I felt something in my chest I hadn't felt since we were kids in our father's engine room: the feeling of building something that will outlast you, alongside people who might.

### Dara

Here is what nobody tells you about almost dying of heatstroke in the jungle: afterward, everyone treats you like glass. Sef checks my water bottles like I'm a toddler. Mei-Lin assigned me the shade jobs for a week. Even Orsen — Orsen, who once told me my snoring violated maritime law — kept glancing at me during the portage aftermath like I might evaporate.

So when the elders asked for volunteers to clear the first section of the old rail bed, I signed up twice, under both my name and a fake one, just to make the point.

The point was made. The fake name was discovered within the hour, because Sef has the loyalty of a dog and the discretion of a foghorn. But I cleared brush for three days straight and only fainted once, briefly, and with dignity.

It was on the third day, hauling ferns off a stretch of ballast near the village, that I found the milestone. A stone post, waist-high, half-swallowed by roots, with letters cut into it: the distance to the coast, and beneath it, older, deeper, a second carving — a name. A surveyor's mark. The drowned man's mark. The same initials Orsen had shown us in the brass tube's charts, back when none of us knew what those charts would cost or what they'd buy.

I called her over. She came, saw it, and went very still, the way she does — the whole world narrowing to a point.

"He walked this line," she said. "Before the dam. Before any of it. He surveyed this spur as a working route and then went upstream and found the truth, and they killed him for it, and his line just... waited."

"Lines are patient," I said. "It's people who aren't."

She crouched and brushed moss from the carving with her thumb, gentle, the way you'd touch a grave. Then she did something I'd never seen her do. She took out her knife and cut her own initials beside the dead man's, small, low on the stone where the weather would take its time finding them.

Dara, I told myself. Say nothing sentimental. She will bolt like a startled goat.

"I'm not going anywhere," she said, before I could say anything at all.

Well. So much for the goat.

That evening the whole village turned out for the first fire on the cleared bed — a proper feast, fish and taro and a fermented something that Mei-Lin drank like a professional and regretted like an amateur. The elders told the story of the cave, of the admission charged in memories, and this time they told it laughing, because the memories it had given back — through Orsen, through the charts, through the dead man's report — had bought them their river instead of costing it.

A child — the same child who'd pressed the carved safe-passage token into Orsen's hand a lifetime ago, back when Orsen was still a stranger we'd fished out of trouble — climbed into her lap and demanded to know if trains would really come.

"Someday," Orsen said. "If the grades hold and the money holds and nobody sells the river again."

"And will you drive it?"

Orsen looked at the fire. At Mei-Lin, arguing happily with three elders at once. At Sef, teaching other children knots badly. At me, watching her, not even pretending otherwise.

"No," she said. "I'll build it. Someone else can drive. I'm done leaving before the interesting part."

The child considered this, found it acceptable, and fell asleep on her. And Orsen Zhou — the woman who catalogued defunct rail lines the way other people catalogue regrets, who had a rule about never staying longer than a job — sat very still under the weight of that sleeping child and let the rule die quietly, without ceremony, the way good rules should.

### Orsen

I have signed my name to documents exactly four times in ten years. Once to a freighter's articles under a false surname. Once to a hotel register I abandoned at midnight. Once to a coroner's statement I shouldn't have survived to give. And once, long ago, to the inspection report that ruined me — a signature I didn't choose and a blame I didn't earn, though it took me a decade and a flooded valley to prove it.

Signing things, for me, has always been the moment the trap closes. So understand what it meant, on the seventh morning, when the headman unrolled a fresh sheet of paper on the Kestrel's chart table and said, "The council wants the spur surveyed properly. Contracted properly. With a named engineer of record."

Named. Of record. Everything I had spent ten years avoiding, written out plainly, like a dare.

Mei-Lin stood by the porthole with her arms crossed, watching me the way she watches weather. Sef and Dara had found urgent business examining the winch, which was fine, except that the winch was outside and they were inside, and neither of them was looking at it.

I picked up the pen.

The contract was simple — simpler than contracts usually are, because villages that have been robbed for twenty years draft plain language. Survey the old rail bed from the coast landing to the high valley junction. Assess the trestle at the river crossing. Recommend repairs, costs, seasons. Term: eighteen months, renewable.

Eighteen months. My body did the arithmetic it always did — supplies, exits, the next coast, the next closed line — and for the first time the arithmetic came back wrong. There was no next coast I wanted. There was no line on any map I'd rather be reading. There was this bed of sleeping stone going into the hills, and this crew pretending not to watch me, and a river outside running loud and free to the sea for the first time in twenty years, and a dead surveyor whose initials I'd carved beside on a milestone because the dead deserve company and so, it turns out, do I.

I thought about my notebook. The real one, the old one, the map of closed lines that had been my bible and my cage. It was ash now; I'd burned it on the escarpment the week before, and the strange thing was how little it weighed, burning, and how much lighter I'd felt without it. Every closed line on that map had been a place I'd gone to disappear. This line — this one, under the ferns, waiting forty years — was a place I'd be going to arrive.

I signed.

Orsen Zhou. My own name, my true one, in ink, on paper, of record, with no exit clause and no false surname and no plan for the far side of the term. The pen scratched louder than it should have. My hand shook slightly, and I let it.

"There," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I expected. "You're stuck with me for eighteen months."

"We'll survive it," Mei-Lin said dryly, but she crossed the cabin and gripped my shoulder once, hard, which from Mei-Lin was a sonnet.

Sef whooped. Dara said something about how now she'd have to stop calling me a fugitive and start calling me a colleague, and which was worse. Outside, the tide turned, and the new jetty's pilings — ours, true, two degrees corrected — stood in the current like they meant to stay.

That night I walked out to the end of the finished section of jetty, alone, as alone as I ever am now, which is to say not very. The river ran silver in the dark, unbroken, source to sea. Somewhere upstream, the escarpment held the moon. Somewhere downstream, the Kestrel rocked at anchor with my family asleep aboard.

I took out the new notebook — the first page still stiff, the first entries already made: grades, culverts, the trestle's span, the milestone with two sets of initials — and on the flyleaf, above the first line, I wrote the title I'd been afraid to give any project since the bridge:

*Coastal Spur — Orsen Zhou, Engineer of Record.*

No exit plan. No alternate route. Just a line, drawn open, running forward into country I couldn't map yet — and, for the first time in my life, no wish to.

Behind me, up the beach, someone struck a light for the evening fire, and called my name, and waited, certain I would come.
