## 22. The Escarpment at Dawn

I have climbed a lot of things in my life. Fire escapes in cities that didn't want me, embankments in the rain to check culverts nobody paid me to check, the side of a freighter once, in Batavia, when I'd overstayed a harbor by four days and the harbormaster's men came looking with questions I couldn't afford to answer.

But I had never climbed anything the way I climbed the escarpment that morning — slowly, on purpose, with nowhere else to be.

The path was Pak Uda's, or it had been, forty years ago, when he was a boy diving the pools below the falls and needed a way up to the lookout his grandfather used for reading the weather off the sea. Dara had found it three days after the flood, half-swallowed by green, and had spent two more days swearing at it with a machete until it agreed to be a path again. She'd refused help. She said it was her gift to me, and that gifts from the Brightwater family were to be earned through suffering, and she was going to suffer on my behalf whether I liked it or not.

The trail switchbacked up through the kind of jungle that doesn't forgive hesitation — vines thick as hawsers, roots like the ribs of drowned ships, birds screaming insults from the canopy. My legs knew the work. My lungs complained anyway. Six weeks since the dam, and my body was still keeping its own ledger of what the river had taken: the burn across my shoulder where the spillway cable had kissed me, the two fingers on my left hand that ached when it rained and would probably ache forever, the general sense of having been wrung out like a rag and hung back on the line slightly wrong.

Worth it, though. Every ache had an address now. That was the difference between pain before and pain after. Before, my hurts had been anonymous — a bruise from a fall down some stairs in a town whose name I'd already forgotten, a fever in a bunkhouse, a loneliness so old it had stopped registering as anything but weather. Now every scar had a story attached, and every story had people in it, and the people were alive, which was more than I could say for most of the people in my stories.

The trees broke open near the top, all at once, the way they do — one step you're in the green dark, the next you're standing in light.

And there it was.

The escarpment dropped away beneath my boots in a long red-gold sweep of rock, falling maybe two hundred meters to the valley floor, and beyond it — God, beyond it — the river.

I stood very still and let it happen to me.

* * *

Here is what I know about rivers, because I have spent my whole professional life fighting them or fleeing them or building things across them: a river is just gravity with a destination. All that water doing is falling, patiently, from high places to low ones, and everything we build — dams, levees, mills, bridges, entire empires — is an argument against gravity. An argument gravity always wins eventually. The consortium's dam had been their best sentence in that argument, twenty years of concrete and law and silence, and it had lasted exactly as long as it deserved to.

From up here, the river ran unbroken.

That was the thing. That was the thing I couldn't stop looking at. For twenty years this water had been a prisoner above the escarpment's cousin downstream — held, divided, made into somebody's asset. The village below had lived beside a river that wasn't allowed to be a river, a trickle rationed out by a company man with a ledger, while the real current sat behind concrete doing nothing but getting angrier.

Now it ran. Silver in the early light, then gold, then a burning white as the sun cleared the ridge to the east and hit the long reach below the falls. It went over the broken sill of the old dam — we'd left the stump standing, deliberately; Mei-Lin said a ruin tells the truth better than a memorial plaque — and then it ran, mile after mile, through the gorge, past the rebuilt jetty at Pulau Karang, past the oxbow where we'd told our stories around a fire, past the toll station ruins and the drowned valley and every place where any of us had bled, all the way to the sea. Unbroken. The first time in twenty years the water had been permitted to finish its own sentence.

I sat down on the warm rock, because my knees suggested it, and because some moments deserve to be met sitting down, like old friends.

* * *

I want to tell you about the notebook, because the notebook is where this story really ends, even if the dam is where it climaxed.

For eleven years I kept a map of closed lines. Every defunct rail spur, every abandoned branch line, every stretch of track pulled up or left to rust across four continents — I catalogued them in a cheap ruled notebook with a cracked spine, in pencil, in my own shorthand. People who saw it assumed it was a hobby. A quirk. The engineer's equivalent of collecting stamps.

It wasn't a hobby. It was a theology.

A closed line is a promise that was made and then unmade. Somebody surveyed it, somebody graded the bed, somebody drove the spikes, and for a while — sometimes only a few years — that line meant something. It carried ore or timber or mail or hope. And then the economics changed, or the mine played out, or the company decided the town at the end of it was no longer worth the cost of reaching, and the line closed, and the town began to die, and the rails rusted, and everyone agreed quietly not to talk about it anymore.

I collected them because I was one of them. A closed line. A route that used to go somewhere and now went nowhere, kept operational only by the fact that I never stopped moving along myself. You can't close a line that won't stay still.

Eleven years, four notebooks' worth. And then, six weeks ago, on the night after the flood, after we'd pulled Pak Uda's body from the slack water below the spillway and wrapped him in sailcloth and sat up all night because none of us could sleep and none of us would say so, I took the last notebook out of my bag, walked down to the beach, and burned it.

I fed it to a driftwood fire page by page, and I watched eleven years of exits go up in smoke, and I felt — this is the honest part, the part I'd never admit to anyone who wasn't there — I felt lighter. Not happy. Grief and relief arrived together, holding hands, the way they do. But lighter. Because every entry in that book had been a door I was keeping open behind me, and doors are heavy, and I had been carrying forty of them for a decade, and it turns out a person can only walk so far carrying that many doors before she forgets what walking is for.

Mei-Lin found me on the beach at dawn, sitting in the ashes. She didn't ask what I'd done. She looked at the fire, and then at me, and she said, "About time," and handed me a cup of coffee that tasted like engine coolant and salvation, and that was the whole eulogy. Some funerals don't need words.

* * *

The sun was fully up now, and the river below had gone from gold to a hard glittering blue, and I could see the Kestrel at anchor in the roadstead off the new jetty, small as a toy from this height, her deck cluttered with the salvage of a hundred dives and the cargo of a future nobody had planned yet.

They were loading her for the run south. Not fleeing — loading. There's a difference, and I've spent enough of my life on the wrong side of it to know. Fleeing is leaving because staying will kill you. Loading is leaving because somewhere else needs what you're carrying, and you'll be back.

They were taking the surveyor's report — the real one, the full one, the dead man's testament we'd pulled from the flooded valley with our own hands while the sensor net screamed and Voss's gunboat came down on us like judgment — copies of it, certified, to the capital. To the engineers' board. To the newspapers that the consortium didn't own. Sef had built a radio strong enough to shout across the whole archipelago, and Dara had taught three village kids to run it, and between them they'd made sure that what happened here would never again depend on one brave dead man's handwriting surviving in a brass tube.

Voss was gone. Vanished into the delta like oil into water, and I won't pretend I don't think about him. I do. Usually at night, usually when the rain comes and my shoulder aches. He knows my name now. Orsen Zhou. The woman who cost the consortium their river. Somewhere out there is a file with my face in it and a budget line for my eventual inconvenience, and I'd be lying if I said I never glanced at the horizon and did the old arithmetic — how far, how fast, which way.

But here's what the old arithmetic doesn't account for: I'm not running alone anymore. You can't outrun people who are coming with you. Voss wouldn't be chasing one engineer down a dark street. He'd be chasing the Kestrel, and the Kestrel has a winch I rebuilt, and twins who fight like a single organism with four hands, and a captain who once stared down a gunboat by refusing to alter course, and a radio network, and a village, and a river full of people who owe us nothing and would still hide us in a heartbeat. He wouldn't be hunting a loose end. He'd be hunting a family. And families, I have recently learned, are the one thing in this world that doesn't run.

* * *

Below me, faint on the wind, the Kestrel sounded her horn.

One long blast. Not the urgent double-blast of departure, not the warning blast of a boat in trouble. One long note, held, the way you'd call someone home to dinner. The way you'd tell someone standing on a hill that the boat is ready, the coffee is terrible, and nobody is going anywhere without her.

I stood up. My knees objected. I let them.

And I thought about the freighter captain, all those months ago, asking me where I was from — the question I'd answered with a lie so smooth it barely cost me anything, because the truth was that I was from nowhere, by design, and the design was working, and the working was killing me by inches so slowly I called it living.

If he asked me now, I'd still lie, probably. Old habits. But the lie would be different. It would be, "Upriver." And it would be true in every way that matters, because "from" isn't a point on a map. It's the direction the water comes from. It's the place that made you, whether you consented or not. And this river — this impossible, unbowed, unbroken river running silver and gold and free to the sea — this river made me. It charged admission in memories, the legend said, and it took mine, the bad ones, the bridge and the blame and the eleven years of doors, and it gave me back something I hadn't known I'd pawned.

I turned to go down the path. Down to the jetty, down to the noise and the arguing and Dara's terrible singing and Sef's worse jokes and Mei-Lin pretending not to smile, down to the work — because there's always work, a spur to survey, a jetty to rebuild, a world to argue with gravity about.

Halfway down, I stopped. Looked back once.

The river caught the morning and threw it, all the way to the sea.

"Coming," I said, to no one, to everyone, and started walking — not away, not toward, just forward, on a line that stayed open.
