## 1. The River Gives Up Its Dead

The storm had spent itself by dawn, and August Ashgrove drove into its wreckage the way other men drove toward a lover—too fast, with his heart going.

The Alder River had come up out of its banks in the night, brown and muscular, carrying fences, outhouses, half a barn roof, and at least one drowned heifer that turned slowly in an eddy below the old rail bridge like something considering whether to come back. August pulled the truck onto the gravel shoulder where the road climbed away from the floodplain and got out into air that tasted of iron and torn earth. His barometer hung from a lanyard around his neck; his anemometer was bolted to the truck bed beside the winch, the spare fuel cans, the tarp, the duffel, the toolbox, the mattress rolled tight against the cab. Everything he owned fit in a truck bed. He had made peace with that years ago, or told himself he had, which amounted to nearly the same thing on most days.

The instruments were why he'd come. Not the river—the sky above it. The front had broken apart over the escarpment in a way he'd never quite seen before, the pressure trace on his recorder showing a stutter, a hesitation, as if the whole weather system had paused mid-sentence to remember something. He wanted the data. Data was the only thing anyone had ever paid him for, and even then not much, and lately not at all.

He set up on the high bank, tripod legs biting into wet gravel, and let the wind meter spin while he watched the river do its work. There was a pleasure in it he couldn't explain to people who asked—Sela Voss's people, mostly, when they still called, back when they still called politely. The pleasure of standing at the edge of something enormous and being *counted*, of holding a small brass instrument while the world came apart, and knowing exactly what the numbers meant. Most men ran from storms. August had built a life out of running *toward* them, and if you asked him why—and nobody did anymore—he'd have said it was because a storm was honest. It took what it took and left what it left and never pretended afterward that nothing had happened.

People were the opposite of that. People could erase things.

The thought arrived uninvited, the way thoughts about money always did, and he pushed it down where he kept the others: the letters from the Kestrel Consortium's collections office, three of them now, each one thinner paper than the last, as if they were rationing even their threats. Fourteen months of payments missed. The rig—his rig, though legally it was theirs until the last invoice cleared—was collateral, and everyone knew what happened to collateral when a man stopped answering letters. They didn't send men like Sela Voss out for small debts. But fourteen months wasn't small anymore, and Corren's Ferry was only forty miles downstream, and Corren's Ferry was where the Consortium kept its regional office, and August had been telling himself for two weeks that he was chasing this storm system for science when really he was chasing it because every mile north put another river between him and the mail.

He checked the recorder. The pressure stutter again—a drop, a strange plateau, a drop. He wrote the numbers in his field book with numb fingers, and that was when he saw the willow snag.

It stood maybe sixty yards out in the flooded channel, a great grey fist of roots and trunk that must have fallen years ago and been claimed by the river ever since. Debris had woven itself through the branches: fence wire, plastic sheeting, a child's bicycle with its wheels gone. And wedged deep in the crotch of the largest limb, riding just above the current line, sat a chest.

Not a cooler. Not a fuel drum. A chest—dark wood, iron-banded, the kind of thing you saw in museums or in the houses of people whose grandparents had stolen properly. It sat in the willow with the patience of something that had decided to stop traveling, and the river broke white around it and moved on without it.

August stood very still.

There was a version of him, the sensible version, the one the creditors would have preferred, who went back to the truck and drove on. Flood debris was flood debris. Half the time it belonged to somebody downstream who'd already filed a claim, and the other half it belonged to nobody, and either way touching it was how a man with no fixed address ended up explaining himself to a sheriff. He knew all this. He said it to himself, clearly, twice.

Then he looked at the chest again, and the sensible version of August Ashgrove lost the argument, the way it had been losing arguments since he was a boy.

* * *

The water between the bank and the snag was shin-deep over the gravel bar, then knee-deep, then suddenly waist-deep where the channel scoured, and cold enough to make his breath go small. He crossed with the tripod held overhead like a man wading out of a burning building, which earned him nothing but the wind's laughter, and reached the willow with his jeans plastered to his legs and his teeth going. The chest was heavier than it looked. It was also not free—the current had packed silt and branches beneath it, and getting it loose meant working it up and out against the drag of the water, and for one long ugly moment August was certain the river meant to keep both of them.

Then it gave. All at once, the way rivers give things up—grudgingly, and then completely, as if embarrassed by the delay. He hauled it onto the gravel bar and knelt over it, dripping, chest heaving, and looked at what he'd taken.

The wood was black with age and water but sound underneath, dense as a church door. The iron bands were hand-forged, riveted, gone to rust only at the edges. And stamped into the lid, pressed deep enough to survive decades of travel, was a seal: a circle, and inside the circle a design he had to tilt into the flat grey light to read—a mountain range above a line of water, and beneath the water, faint but deliberate, a row of small figures, standing. Around the rim ran lettering in an alphabet he didn't know, angular and patient, like footprints in snow.

August knew seals. Storm chasing had taught him the geography of paperwork—county survey marks, hydrological district stamps, the Consortium's own crest, a kestrel rising off a ledger line, which he'd seen embossed on enough collection notices to recognize it in his sleep. This was none of those. This was older. This was the kind of mark that got cut into stone.

He should have felt like a thief. What he felt instead was a sensation he couldn't name at first, and it took him a moment, kneeling there in the shallows with the river talking behind him, to understand that it was recognition—not of the seal, he'd never seen the seal—but of the *weight*. Some objects feel found rather than discovered, the way some doors feel like they've been waiting. The chest lay under his hands with the gravity of a thing that had been looking for him longer than he'd been looking for it.

"That's stupid," he said aloud, to hear a human voice. The wind took it. He opened the chest anyway.

The lock was long gone; the hasp lifted at his touch. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, lay papers. Bundles of them. Hundreds of pages, tied with cord, the topmost sheets water-stained at the corners but legible—dense columns of handwriting in that same angular alphabet, and here and there, interleaved, documents in a script he could read: land descriptions, boundary notes, a registry of parcels with names attached, name after name after name, written in a careful clerk's hand and each one stamped, near the margin, with the mountain-and-water seal.

He turned one page. Then another. The names went on. Somewhere in the middle of the bundle, a single sheet in a different hand—looser, hurried, almost angry—had been folded around a smaller packet, and August's fingers stopped on it without his permission, the way a man's hand stops on a scar.

He didn't open the folded sheet. He didn't know why. Later he would tell himself it was caution, and later still he would know it was fear, and later than that he would understand it was neither—that some part of him had understood, even waist-deep in the Alder River with his creditors forty miles downstream, that once you read a thing you became responsible for it, and August Ashgrove had spent his whole adult life avoiding responsibility the way other men avoided taxes: diligently, creatively, and without ever quite succeeding.

He rewrapped the papers, closed the lid, and sat back on his heels.

The river ran on. Downstream, invisible beyond the bend, was Corren's Ferry, and the Consortium office with its plate-glass windows and its polite letters, and Sela Voss, whoever she was exactly, who signed her correspondence with a title instead of a personality. Upstream, invisible beyond the hills, was the escarpment, the long wall of the high country, and past it—everyone said—the empty quarter, the land nobody farmed, nobody mapped, nobody mentioned, the blank space on every atlas he'd ever owned, printed in that apologetic pale tint cartographers used for places where nothing was supposed to be.

He looked at the seal on the lid. Mountains above water. Figures beneath.

"Where did you come from?" he asked it.

The chest, being a chest, said nothing. But the question stayed in him after he'd hauled the thing across the gravel bar and wrestled it into the truck bed, wedging it against the toolbox with the winch strap, and it stayed in him as he dried off as best he could and got the heater going and sat in the cab watching the flood run brown and endless past the windows. Restless curiosity, he called it. That was the official story, the one he'd tell if anyone asked: a man who chased storms found a box in a tree, and a man like that opens boxes, it's practically a medical condition. Curiosity was safe. Curiosity was respectable. Curiosity didn't have creditors.

But underneath the curiosity, in the place where he kept the letters and the arithmetic of what he owed, dread sat doing its quiet work. Because he knew how the world worked, and the world worked like this: things that floated out of floods belonged to whoever was strong enough to claim them, and a man with everything he owned in a truck bed was not strong. If the chest was worth nothing, it was ballast. If the chest was worth something—

He looked at it in the mirror. Rain began to tick against the roof, fine and cold, the front's last courtesy.

If the chest was worth something, then somewhere downstream there were people who already knew it was missing, and people like that did not file claims. People like that sent letters on thin paper, and then, when the letters failed, they sent Sela Voss.

August put the truck in gear. The tires spun once on the wet gravel and caught. He drove south, toward Corren's Ferry, because the pawnshop there was the only one for eighty miles and because there was a gold ingot wrapped in the third bundle from the top, and because a man who owed fourteen months of payments needed liquidity more than he needed wisdom.

He told himself he'd sell one piece. Just one. Enough to buy time.

Behind him the Alder River ran on toward town, carrying its fences and its branches and its secrets, giving up its dead one piece at a time, and the chest rode in the truck bed under the tarp with the seal facing down, mountains above water, figures beneath, patient as debt.
