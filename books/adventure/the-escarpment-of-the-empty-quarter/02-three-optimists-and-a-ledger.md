## 2. Three Optimists and a Ledger

The chest had ridden in August's truck bed for two days, wedged behind the spare tire, and he had not once opened it past the first lid-crack that showed him gold and paper and a smell like a library left out in the rain. He told himself this was caution. He knew it was fear. The two felt identical in his hands.

Corren's Ferry announced itself the way border towns always did: by smell before sight—diesel, fry oil, river mud—and then by its one tall thing, the grain elevator, standing over the rooftops like a man craning to see who was coming down the road. The Alder ran wide and brown along the town's east edge, still carrying the storm's leavings: fence rails, a drowned ewe turning slowly against a piling, somebody's kitchen chair riding the current with its legs up, almost jaunty. August crossed the bridge at walking speed and watched the water take the chair under the pilings and give it back whole on the far side. Everything the river took, it kept or returned broken. He'd pulled the chest out of that arithmetic, which meant either the river had made an exception or the chest had never really belonged to the water at all.

He parked in the lot of the Ferryman's Rest, bought a room with the last cash he trusted himself to carry, and sat on the bed's edge for an hour with the chest between his knees, listening to the pipes knock in the walls. Then he did what any sensible man does with a problem too heavy to think about: he decided to sell a piece of it.

* * *

The pawnshop was called Halvard's Loans & Sundries, which was honest in the way that mattered—it admitted up front that everything inside was collateral for something else. August had been in a hundred shops like it across three provinces. They all smelled the same: gun oil, old leather, the sweet dust of instruments nobody played anymore. This one had a wall of wedding rings in velvet trays, each tagged with a number, and August always looked at those walls longer than he meant to. Somewhere in every tray was a ring pawned in anger and never reclaimed.

The man behind the counter was sixty-odd, built like a barrel stave, with reading glasses pushed up into white hair and a jeweler's loupe hanging from his neck on a braided cord. He looked up when the bell over the door rang, took in August's coat, the river silt still dried gray in the seams of his boots, and said, "Flood salvage?"

"Something like that."

"Flood salvage has a rate." The man—HALVARD, said the card taped to the register—came around the counter. "State law says found property gets held thirty days against claims. You got a bill of sale? A provenance? Anything with your name and the object's name on the same piece of paper?"

"I've got a truck bed full of instruments and a debt I could stack under them," August said. "Paper's not my strong suit."

Halvard grunted, which August chose to read as sympathy. He set the ingot on the glass counter. It was smaller than a brick, dense as sin, stamped on its face with the same crest he'd seen on the chest: a tower above three descending lines, like water falling from a height. Around the crest ran letters in an alphabet he didn't know—not quite Cyrillic, not quite anything else, the characters leaning as if into a wind.

Halvard picked it up and his eyebrows went up and stayed there. He turned it under the desk lamp. Weighed it in his palm twice, the way men do when they don't trust the first answer.

"This is good weight," he said. "Better than good. And this stamp—" He tilted it toward the light. "I haven't seen this mark since I was a boy. My father kept one of these in a drawer he didn't let my mother clean."

"What is it?"

"That's the question you should have asked before you carried it through my door." Halvard set it down but kept two fingers resting on it, like a man holding a dog's collar. "You know what a bullion assay costs? Nothing. You know what it costs when the wrong person learns you walked in here with bullion bearing a foreign state crest? Everything you've got, plus interest." He looked up over the glasses. "You're not from here."

"Nobody's from here. It's a border town."

"Border towns are *all* from somewhere." Halvard's eyes went to the window, to the street, doing the quiet arithmetic shopkeepers do. "Tell me what you want for it and I'll tell you if I can sleep after paying it."

August named a number that would clear his smallest creditor and buy diesel north. Halvard named a number half again smaller, and they were still haggling—genuinely, comfortably, the way two men haggle when neither is afraid yet—when the bell over the door rang a second time.

* * *

She came in out of the rain wearing a gray coat cut close enough to be armor, and she closed the door behind her with her hand instead of letting it swing, which was the first thing August noticed about her—the economy of it, no motion spent that wasn't spent on purpose. Dark hair, wet at the temples. Thirty-five, maybe younger, maybe older; some faces refuse the question. She looked at the ingot on the counter, then at August, and something moved through her expression like weather through a valley—there and gone, leaving the air changed.

"Mr. Ashgrove," she said.

Every muscle in August's back decided independently to lock. He kept his hands loose on the counter because locked hands were a confession. "Do I know you?"

"You bank with people I work for." She stepped forward and offered a hand, and he took it because refusing would have cost more than shaking. Her grip was dry and brief. "Sela Voss. Kestrel Consortium, Asset Recovery Division—which sounds sinister and mostly isn't. Mostly it's accounting with legs."

"Asset recovery," August repeated. "I don't remember losing anything."

"That's the interesting part." She smiled, and it was a good smile, warm at the edges, and August had been lied to by professionals enough times to recognize the craftsmanship. "Three years ago you signed a note for a storm-intercept rig. Cab, chassis, radar mast, instrument array—the works. Sixty-one thousand, four hundred marks, compounded quarterly. Your last payment cleared fourteen months ago. Since then you've been—" she glanced at the window, at the brown river, "—mobile."

"The word is 'hiding.'"

"The word is 'illiquid.'" She said it without malice, which somehow made it worse. "Mr. Ashgrove, I'm going to be direct with you, because you strike me as a man who respects weather more than manners, and weather is nothing if not direct. That item on the counter belongs to my employers. Not the gold—the gold is trivial. The *provenance*. The chest it came out of. Where is the chest?"

"Safe."

"Where?"

"Somewhere the river isn't."

Halvard had gone very still behind his loupe, the way small animals go still when larger animals negotiate over their territory. Sela Voss glanced at him, and something passed between them—an old recognition, a ledger entry August couldn't read—and Halvard abruptly found urgent business in the back room and left them alone with the bell ticking off its last swing.

Then it was just the two of them and the rings in their trays and the rain starting proper against the glass.

"Here's what happens now," she said, "if we do this the hard way. I make a call. By tonight, every licensed dealer within two hundred kilometers has your description and the chest's description, and by tomorrow morning the provincial constabulary has a warrant citing trafficking in cultural patrimony—a real charge, Mr. Ashgrove, I don't need it to be fake. You'll lose the chest, the truck, and roughly eighteen months of your life to proceedings you cannot afford to attend properly. I will get the chest. Everyone will simply have had a bad week."

"And the easy way?"

"The easy way is that you're sitting on the single most valuable object my employers have ever misplaced, and they are prepared to be *generous* about it." She reached into her coat and produced a folded document, cream stock, heavy as linen, and laid it flat on the counter beside the ingot. "Full satisfaction of the note. Sixty-one thousand marks, the interest, the collection costs—all of it, discharged. Signed today. Plus ten thousand in cash for your trouble and your silence. You walk out of here owing nothing to anyone for the first time since you were old enough to owe."

August looked at the paper. He was very good at reading skies and notably bad at reading people, but even he could see the document was real—that was the horror of it, that it wasn't a bluff, that the money was actually there, actually his, actually one signature away. Relief rose in him like floodwater, warm and total, wanting to fill every low place.

That was exactly why he didn't move.

Because he'd spent six years learning how storms worked, and the first lesson of storms was that the air before the worst of it is always the calmest, the warmest, the most forgiving. The sky doesn't threaten you. The sky *invites* you. It hands you soft light and gentle wind and lets you drive straight out onto the flats, and only later, when you're committed, does it show you what it was building the whole time.

Nobody forgives sixty-one thousand marks for a chest of papers. Papers are only worth that much if someone needs them to stay unread.

"Who drowned?" he asked.

Sela Voss blinked. "I'm sorry?"

"The chest came out of a river that just ate a bridge and a church and probably some people. Somebody lost this chest in that water, or upstream of it, or running from whatever put it there. So before I sign anything—I'm asking who died for what's inside it. Because your employers don't pay this kind of money for paper unless the paper is a body count."

For the first time, the professional warmth left her face entirely, and what was underneath was younger and wearier and, briefly, honest. She looked at the ingot—at the crest, the tower, the falling water—and August watched her look at it the way he watched greenhorns look at a wall cloud: knowing exactly what it was and pretending the knowing changed nothing.

"You have until Friday," she said quietly. "There's a number on the back of the satisfaction notice. Call it, bring the chest to the Consortium depot on the ferry road, and this all ends with you solvent and anonymous, which I suspect is the only version of happiness you currently believe in." She buttoned her coat, one motion, top to bottom. "Don't call anyone else first, Mr. Ashgrove. There are people looking for this chest who aren't offering to forgive anything."

She left. The bell rang. The rain came harder.

August stood a long moment with the cream-colored paper in his hand, feeling the trap's shape perfectly—the soft floor of it, the warmth, the invitation—and understanding that he was already standing in it, had been standing in it since the moment he'd pulled the chest from the willow snag, maybe since long before that, though he couldn't have said how.

Behind him, from the back room, Halvard said quietly, without coming out: "Son. Whatever's in that chest—don't sell it to the first people who ask. The ones who ask second are the ones who already know what it's worth."

August folded the satisfaction notice into his coat, next to his heart, where debts live.

Outside, the river kept moving south, patient as a creditor, carrying everything it hadn't finished with.
