## 3. A Nation With No Address

The rain had followed August out of the hills and into Corren's Ferry, thinning from a storm's fury to something more like an apology, and by the time he rolled across the old toll bridge at dusk the river beneath him had gone the color of pewter and was running fast and brown with everything it had stolen upstream. He parked the truck in the lot behind the Ferryman's Rest, where a night cost eleven dollars and the walls were thick enough to muffle the sound of your own thinking, which was what he paid for more often than not.

He carried the chest inside under his arm.

It weighed less than he'd expected—maybe twenty pounds empty of water now, the seals around its lid swollen tight—and it rode against his hip with a strange familiarity, the way a stranger's coat can feel like yours if you wear it long enough in bad weather. He set it on the bed between the lamp and the window, where the last gray light came through, and looked at it for a long time without touching it.

He should have opened it already. He knew that. A man in his position did not sit on mysteries; he converted them to cash as quickly as decency allowed, because decency was a luxury item and he had never once been able to afford it. There were three men—four, counting the lawyer, who counted himself twice—who held paper on his truck, his instruments, and nominally his future, and every one of them would have sold him for less than what might be sitting in that chest. The smart play was to pry the lid tonight, inventory whatever was inside, and be on the road south before the pawn shops opened.

Instead he ate a cold dinner at the counter downstairs, listened to two grain haulers argue about tariffs, and went back up and slept with the chest six inches from his head like a dog that hadn't decided yet whether it trusted him.

In the morning he did not open it either. He drove instead to the far end of Water Street, past the chandlery and the shuttered cannery office, to a shop with no sign except a hand-painted rectangle of green paint above the door, faded almost to white, that might once have read *Maps & Charts* or might have read anything else. The bell over the door was broken. The smell inside was paper, glue, linseed oil, and dust—the smell, August realized with a jolt he couldn't name, of somewhere he had been before.

"Closed," said a voice from the back, gravelly and unbothered.

"I need a mapmaker."

"You need a mapmaker the way most people need one, which is badly and briefly." An old man emerged through a curtain of hanging charts, wiping his hands on a leather apron gone black at the edges. He was tall the way dead trees are tall, stooped and dry, with a face like a riverbed in late summer—all channels and silt—and eyes the pale washed-out blue of very old paper. "Bram," he said, as though the word cost him money. "State your business or leave."

August set the chest on the counter between them.

Bram looked at it. Then he looked up at August, and something moved behind those pale eyes—not recognition exactly, but the shadow recognition casts when it passes over a room. He put both hands flat on the chest's lid, the way a doctor lays hands on a patient, and stood there so long that August began to wonder if the old man had fallen asleep standing up.

"Where," Bram said finally, "did you get this?"

"Alder River. Upstream of the gorge. It was hung up in a willow snag after the flood crest."

"The flood crest." Bram repeated it without inflection, but his thumbs had begun, very slightly, to press into the wood. "You pulled it out of the water."

"It was going to be there until the willow rotted. I figured the water was done with it."

"The water is never done with anything." Bram lifted his hands. "Open it."

August hesitated. "I was hoping you'd tell me what the seal means first. I didn't want to—" He stopped, because the honest version of the sentence was *I didn't want to open it alone*, and he could not account for that feeling and didn't like the look of any feeling he couldn't account for.

Bram studied him for a moment longer, then turned, crossed to a cabinet against the wall, and drew out a shallow drawer with the tenderness of a man handling a sleeping child. Inside, laid on yellowed linen, was a stamp—a seal matrix, brass and iron, mounted on a handle worn smooth by decades of some other man's grip. He set it beside the chest.

The impression matched. Crest, motto, the strange interlaced device at the center that August had spent two nights failing to describe even to himself: not a knot, not a star, something older than either, like three rivers braided into a single line and then braided again.

"That's Vhaloria," Bram said.

The word landed in the shop and just sat there. August found he had to say something, so he said, "I don't know it."

"No," Bram agreed. "Nobody does. That's rather the point." He lowered himself onto a stool behind the counter with the care of a man whose joints kept their own ledger. "Vhaloria was a country. Small. Upland and river valley, mostly—the Quarter, we called the low country, the eastern quarter of the basin where the Alder used to spread out before it learned to hurry. Good land. Orchards, mills, forty towns you've never heard of because they're not anywhere anymore. It had a king once, then a parliament, then a parliament that argued too much, like all of them. And then, forty years ago, the Quarter Flood came, and Vhaloria drowned, and afterward the maps were redrawn and the atlases reprinted and the country simply—stopped being printed. You can't strike a nation from existence. But you can strike it from the address books, and after a while nobody remembers to mail it anything."

"People died in the flood," August said. It wasn't a question. Something in Bram's voice made it impossible for it to be one.

"Eleven thousand by the official count. The official count was conducted by the people who owned the dams." Bram said it flatly, the way you'd report the price of nails. "That's the part nobody says aloud anymore, boy, because everyone who could say it loudly is dead or scattered or tired. The Quarter didn't drown. The Quarter was drowned. Three reservoirs upstream, built in secret during the drought years, filled to the brim and waiting. One wet autumn. One coordinated release—or one failure, depending on which lie you prefer—and eighty miles of valley went under in a single night. And the land, once it drained, was cheap. Very cheap. Bought for pennies by men who'd known the water was coming."

August's mouth had gone dry. Outside, the river ran past the end of the street, high and brown and full, carrying somebody's fence posts toward the sea.

"And the Consortium," he said. His voice came out wrong, smaller. "Kestrel—"

"Kestrel was a young man's name then. Dagny's grandfather signed the first land options six weeks before the water came down. That's the story, anyway. The story has no documents left to stand on, because documents burn." Bram's pale eyes came up and fixed on him. "There was an archive. Registry offices, parish records, the land rolls—everything a drowned country leaves behind on paper. It was housed in a customs house in Corren's Ferry itself, after the flood, while the lawyers argued about who owned what. Twenty-two years ago a fire took it in one night. Convenient fire. Cold night, no witnesses worth believing. The Consortium men who arranged it weren't careful enough about one thing, though—they were seen. By a boy who worked the docks." He tapped his own chest. "I have spent twenty-two years being a man who saw nothing, and it has kept me alive and made me dull, and I recommend both."

* * *

Afterward August could never reconstruct the order of things in that shop. What he remembered was fragments, the way floods leave things stranded at odd heights: the green-shaded lamp switched on against the morning gloom; Bram's long fingers working the swollen latch with a knife blade and a patience that seemed liturgical; the lid coming free with a sigh, like a held breath released after forty years.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, were papers. Hundreds of them. Land grants with wax seals intact. A registry bound in boards, its spine cracked, its pages furred at the edges with damp but legible—columns of names, parcels, acreages, a whole civilization filed in ink. Letters in a script August couldn't read, dense and slantwise. Photographs in a sleeve: orchards, a stone bridge, children on a dock, a town square with a clock tower. And at the bottom, wrapped separately, a single gold ingot stamped with the same braided device, small as a soap bar, heavy as a sin.

"This is the land registry," Bram whispered. He was holding the bound volume the way you'd hold a bird with a broken wing. "This is the *Quarter registry*. Names. Every parcel in the drowned valley, every family that held it. Do you understand what this is? This is the thing that was supposed to have burned. This is the reason there was a fire." He looked up, and for the first time the old man looked frightened, and the fright made him older. "Who else knows you have this?"

"Nobody."

"Then nobody yet. There's a difference, and the difference is measured in days." He closed the volume gently. "You cannot pawn that ingot in this town. You cannot show that seal to anyone with a suit on. If the Consortium learns this chest exists, they will not send a lawyer first. They burned a building full of clerks' work once; they'll burn a truck full of it faster."

August nodded slowly. And then, because the question had been rising in him since the lamp came on, since before the lamp, maybe since the willow snag, he asked the other thing.

"Bram. Why do I know this place?"

The old man went still.

"I mean the smell of your shop. I walked in and I knew it. And the script in these letters—I can't read it, but my eye keeps catching on it like it's almost a language I know. And the photographs—" His voice did something he didn't authorize. He picked up the top photograph anyway: the dock, the children, the brown water of some gentler river. Four kids squinting into sun. One of them, smallest, at the end, wearing a wool coat too big for him and a small round pin on the lapel. He turned it over. Handwriting, faded: *Last day of the orchard road, before.* No names. "I keep looking at this kid," August said, "and I keep feeling like I'm standing behind whoever took the picture."

Bram took the photograph. Held it close to the lamp. Looked at it for a long time, and then looked at August over the top of it, and August watched the old man arrive somewhere and decide not to say so.

"How old are you?" Bram asked.

"Thirty-eight."

"Forty years since the flood." Bram set the photograph down with exaggerated care, aligning its edge with the counter's edge, buying himself a moment. "How much do you remember of your childhood? Honestly. Not the story you tell people—the actual weather of it."

August opened his mouth to give the standard answer, the one he'd given landlords and employers and one doomed marriage: a little, not much, we moved around, my mother died when I was small. But standing in the paper-smelling shop with the drowned country's registry open on the counter, the standard answer collapsed like a bad bridge. He tried to reach for his childhood and found what he always found: a hallway. A specific hallway, narrow, with a runner carpet the color of wine, and a door at the end with light under it, and his own small hand on the wallpaper, and nothing—no faces, no rooms, no before or after. Just the hallway, recurring, unattached to any house or town or year, surfacing in dreams he'd stopped mentioning decades ago. And underneath it, fainter, a smell: river water and cut apples.

"There's a hallway," he heard himself say. "Wine-colored carpet. I've dreamed it my whole life and I've never been able to put it anywhere. And apples. Somebody cutting apples near water." He laughed, short and unhappy. "That's the entire estate of thirty-eight years. A hallway and some fruit."

Bram was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had changed—lower, slower, the voice of a man stepping onto ice and testing each inch.

"When the Quarter flooded, the ones who got out got out in the first hours, or not at all. Families came up the escarpment roads with what they could carry. Some went north, some west, some changed their names and dissolved into the towns along the range like sugar into tea. There were children on those roads, Ashgrove. Children who were four, five, six years old, who grew up in new towns with new names and mothers who never talked about it, because talking about it was dangerous and grief is heavy and silence travels lighter." He turned the photograph toward August again, toward the small figure in the borrowed coat with the round pin on its lapel. "Children who remembered only hallways. Smells. Pieces."

The shop seemed to tilt. Not really—but August gripped the counter's edge anyway, because the floor felt like the deck of a boat, and the lamp light felt like it was coming from underwater, and forty years of ordinary life—every foster house, every form with its blank line for *place of birth*, every unexplained flinch at the sound of rising water—rearranged itself all at once around a single terrible possibility, the way debris rearranges around a submerged obstacle in a current. He had spent his whole life believing he was a man with no origin, a self-made accident, and self-made accidents get to be anyone. If the hallway had an address—

"My mother," he said. His voice sounded like someone else's. "Her name was—"

He reached for it and it wasn't there. Not forgotten—*unfiled*. A drawer in him that had never once been opened, and now stood ajar, exhaling cold air. He could see her hands, suddenly, absurdly—cutting apples at a table near a window, rain on the glass—and beyond her hands nothing, a whiteout, snow or fog or the mercy of a mind too young to keep what it saw.

"Ashgrove," Bram said quietly. "It's not a common name. It's not a Vhalorian name either—it's lowland, southern. Which means someone gave it to you, or chose it, or it was bought off a clerk somewhere along the road." He paused. "Or it's real, and your mother married out before the water came. Either way." He straightened on his stool with visible effort, and when he continued, the mapmaker's dryness had come back over him like a shutter drawn against weather. "Either way, you need to hear the rest of it, and then you need to decide what kind of fool you're going to be, because there are several varieties available and all of them are fatal eventually."

"The rest of it."

"The archive that burned here was the public record. Deeds, registries, tax rolls. What the Consortium needed gone, they got gone." Bram rose, crossed to the great flat cabinet that dominated the back wall, and unlocked it with a key from around his neck. "But countries are bigger than their paperwork. There was a second archive. Unofficial. Started before the flood by people who saw the reservoirs going in and understood what they were for—a counter-record. Testimony. Copies. The things powerful men can't burn because they live in heads and kitchen drawers and church floors. It was kept moving, always moving, from keeper to keeper, up into the high country along the escarpment. Most of its keepers are dead now. The last one anyone I trust ever named was a woman called Odile Hark, who keeps—kept—a station above the tree line, past the shepherd valleys, near where the maps stop pretending." He drew out a sheet of heavy paper, hand-inked, and laid it on the counter beside the registry. It was a map, but like no atlas page: the escarpment rendered in fine brown lines, and threaded through it, in a different hand, a route. Passes that appeared on no printed chart. A dotted line climbing toward a summit marked with a tiny chapel symbol and the word *custos*.

"She's dying, if she's alive at all. She'd be ninety. And winter comes to that shoulder early—three weeks, maybe four, and then the passes shut until May." Bram looked up. "If that registry reaches her station, it joins the counter-archive, and the truth stops being one chest in one truck and becomes a chain of custody no fire can finish. If it doesn't—if you sell it, or lose it, or get stopped with it—then Vhaloria stays struck from the address books forever, and the men who drowned it die rich and respectable, and their grandchildren inherit the respectability along with the money."

Outside, the river ran. August became aware, distantly, that it had started raining again, soft against the shop's single window, and that the sound of it had always done something to him—something between dread and homesickness—that he had never once examined.

"Why are you telling me this?" he managed. "You said yourself you've spent twenty-two years seeing nothing."

"Yes." Bram began rolling the map, unhurried, precise. "And I'm seventy-one years old, and I am tired of the sight of my own cowardice, and you, boy, walked into my shop carrying the ghost of a murdered country under your arm like a loaf of bread." He held out the rolled map. "So. Decide."

August didn't take it. Not yet. He was looking at the photograph again—at the child in the too-big coat, at the small round pin, at the handwriting that said *before* as if everyone would know what came after—and the vertigo was on him fully now, the ground of his whole life tilting, every fact he'd built himself on sliding gently seaward like a town learning it stood on a floodplain.

He thought about his creditors. He thought about how easy the ingot would be to move, how clean the escape, how the chest could go into the river again and stay gone. He thought about a woman cutting apples in a window with rain on the glass, and a hallway with a wine-colored carpet, and a door with light under it that he had been walking toward in his sleep for thirty-eight years.

He took the map.

"Which way to the escarpment?" he said—and outside, far down Water Street, a black Consortium truck rolled slowly past in the rain, slowing, just slightly, as it passed the shop with no sign.
