## 4. What the Water Remembered

The Kestrel Consortium kept its regional office in a converted granary above Corren's Ferry, and Sela Voss had always thought the choice of building was deliberate—a joke only the founders would have understood. Grain was what the lowlands sold to the mountains before the Quarter Flood; grain was what Vhaloria had grown in the wide alluvial valley that no longer appeared on any map. Now the silos held paper instead of wheat: forty years of contracts, easements, water rights, and quiet acquisitions, stacked to the rafters like something being stored against a famine that never came.

She climbed the iron staircase at six in the morning, before the clerks arrived, because Dagny Kestrel took her calls early or not at all, and because Sela did not want to say what she had to say within earshot of anyone on the Consortium payroll.

The door at the top was oak, older than everything else in the building, with a brass plate worn nearly blank. She knocked twice.

"Come."

Dagny Kestrel stood at the window with her back to the room, watching the river. She was sixty-one years old and built like the escarpment itself—broad, weathered, immovable—and she wore her silver hair cropped short in a style that had gone out of fashion two decades ago and come back without her ever changing it. On the desk behind her lay a cup of coffee, untouched, going cold. It always went cold. Sela had worked for her nine years and had never once seen her finish a cup.

"You found him," Dagny said, without turning.

"I found him." Sela closed the door. "August Ashgrove. He's staying at the Ferryman's Rest, room four. He tried to pawn an ingot at Halvard's exchange yesterday afternoon."

"And?"

"It's Vhalorian gold." Sela set her report folder on the desk, then, after a moment's hesitation, laid the photograph beside it—the one Halvard's clerk had taken for the pawn ticket, showing the ingot's face and its stamped provenance mark. "The assay matches ore from the Alder headwaters. The stamp is the double-crescent seal. I ran it through the archive index myself. There are exactly eleven documented ingots with that mark, and all eleven were logged out of the Vhalorian treasury in the last week before the flood."

Now Dagny turned. Her face did what it always did when something interested her: nothing at all. But she crossed to the desk, and she picked up the photograph, and she held it under the lamp for a long time.

"Where is the ingot now?"

"Halvard's vault. Ashgrove didn't have papers of provenance, so Halvard sat on it pending verification—which means he'll sit on it until someone tells him not to. I can buy it back this morning for less than we spend on fuel in a month."

"That's not what I asked you about." Dagny set the photograph down. "You said he pulled a chest out of the river. Where is the chest?"

"He still has it. Room four, under the bed, if my source at the Ferryman's Rest is accurate. He hasn't opened it—or if he has, he doesn't understand what he's looking at. He's a storm chaser, Ms. Kestrel. He reads barometers, not land registries."

"But you do."

It wasn't a question, so Sela didn't answer it. Instead she opened the folder and turned it around so Dagny could see the pages she'd photographed in Halvard's back office at two in the morning: the ingot's assay certificate, the treasury log excerpt, and—because she had gone looking once she understood what the gold meant—a copy of the old Vhalorian customs manifest from the last barge season, listing the chest's probable contents by weight and classification.

*Registry, cadastral. One (1) chest, sealed. Quarter District.*

"The land registry," Sela said. "For the Quarter."

Dagny sat down. It was the first time in nine years Sela had seen her sit while an employee was still standing, and she understood it as what it was: not courtesy, but the posture of a woman who wanted to be sure her legs would hold.

"Say the rest," Dagny said quietly.

"The registry records every parcel in the Quarter as it stood before the flood. Ownership, boundaries, improvements, water rights, inheritance lines going back generations. If that chest is intact, it's a complete legal portrait of a country the day before it drowned." Sela kept her voice level, professional, the voice she used for briefing field teams. "And the reason that matters—the only reason it matters—is what the registry *doesn't* show. No sale records after the spring of the flood year. No transfers. No compensation filings. The people who owned that land never sold it, Ms. Kestrel. They never agreed to anything. The water just came, and then our founders' companies filed new titles on top of the drowned parcels, and every court in three provinces accepted them because there was nothing left to argue with. No claimants. No documents. No country."

The silence stretched long enough that Sela could hear the river below the window, running high and brown with snowmelt, the same river that had carried the chest to August Ashgrove's truck bed.

"You've been reading the restricted files," Dagny said at last.

"I've been reading everything. That's the job you gave me."

"Yes." Dagny looked at the window, at the river. "It is."

Sela had rehearsed the next part on the walk over, in the grey light before the town woke, walking the levee road with her collar up. She had rehearsed it because she knew it would be the hardest sentence she'd ever delivered to this woman, and because she wanted to deliver it cleanly, without the tremor she could feel sitting somewhere behind her sternum.

"There's one more thing," she said. "The flood timing. I pulled the hydrological surveys from the year of the Quarter Flood—the internal ones, the ones the Water Authority never published. The Alder headwaters were engineered. Diversion channels, retention dams, a spillway cut through the escarpment's western shoulder. All of it built in the eighteen months before the flood, all of it licensed to a holding company whose directors—" she turned the page, though she no longer needed to; she had read those names enough times that they were printed on the inside of her eyelids— "whose directors included Edvard Kestrel. Your grandfather. The signature on the final construction authorization is his."

She had expected anger. She had prepared for anger, for denial, for the cold surgical fury Dagny Kestrel deployed like a scalpel when someone brought her bad news. What she got was worse. Dagny simply nodded, slowly, the way a doctor nods at an X-ray confirming what the hands already felt.

"I know," Dagny said.

The words landed in the room and sat there.

"You *know*."

"I've known since I was nineteen years old. My father told me the summer before he died. He'd been drinking—he drank more in his last years—and he sat me down in the lodge cellar, where the family keeps the things it doesn't keep upstairs, and he showed me the construction authorization himself. Original. Signed." Dagny's voice was flat as a frozen lake. "He told me the family fortune was built on a foundation of water, and that someday the water would give something back, and that when it did, my job would be to make sure it gave back nothing that could be read."

Sela stood very still. Nine years she had worked for this woman. Nine years of retrievals, negotiations, quiet settlements, discreet disappearances of inconvenient paperwork. She had told herself, in the way professionals tell themselves things, that the Consortium was ruthless but not criminal, hard-nosed but not rotten, that the work was salvage and asset recovery and the moral arithmetic balanced out somewhere above her pay grade. She had been, she realized now, standing in a granary full of stolen countries, an optimist herself.

"What do you want me to do?" she asked, because the question was the only safe ground left.

"Retrieve the chest. Intact, if possible." Dagny rose and walked to the window again, and Sela watched her back, the broad shoulders squared against the river as if the river might be taking notes. "Ashgrove owes us two hundred and thirty thousand. Offer him debt forgiveness and a bonus. If he refuses—if he's already talked to anyone, if he's already opened it and understood it—then take it however it must be taken. By any means. Do you understand what I'm telling you, Voss?"

"I understand the words."

"Do you?" Dagny turned, and for a moment—just a moment, a crack in the escarpment—something moved behind her eyes that was not confidence and was not cruelty but looked terribly like fear. "That chest is the end of my family, Sela. Not embarrassment. Not scandal. End. Every title we hold in the lowlands descends from those drowned parcels. Every subsidiary, every trust, every share of every company with the kestrel mark on it. If that registry reaches a tribunal, we don't lose a lawsuit. We lose everything, retroactively, forty years deep. And everyone whose pension, whose mortgage, whose salary sits on our balance sheet goes down with us. Thousands of innocent people, ruined because one storm chaser fished a box out of a river." She let that settle. "So yes. By any means. And be quick. The passes close soon, and I want this finished before the snow makes the high country impossible—for him, and for us."

*Innocent people.* Sela turned the phrase over as she gathered her folder. It was true, probably. It was also, she noticed, the exact argument the men who built the diversion dams must have used: thousands of livelihoods depend on the water going where we send it. History, she thought, does not repeat itself so much as it recycles its excuses.

"One condition," she heard herself say.

Dagny's eyebrows rose a fraction. In nine years, Sela Voss had never named conditions.

"Ashgrove comes out of this alive. Whatever else happens—whatever the chest requires—the man walks away. He's not a witness to anything yet. He's a debtor with a lucky truck. Keep it that way."

For a long moment Dagny studied her, and Sela felt the study like a searchlight, like the assessment of an asset being revalued.

"You've developed an attachment."

"I've developed a preference for clean operations. Dead storm chasers generate questions. Questions generate investigators. Investigators generate exactly the kind of attention we're trying to avoid." It was a good answer. It was even mostly true. It was not the whole truth, which was that August Ashgrove had stood in Halvard's shop with river mud on his boots and hunger in his face, haggling over an ingot he clearly needed to sell and clearly hated selling, and something about him had snagged in Sela like a hook—the particular desperation of a man who owns almost nothing and is honest about it anyway.

"Agreed," Dagny said. "Alive, if it costs nothing. Alive, if it's convenient." She picked up her cold coffee, considered it, and set it down untouched. "But hear me clearly, Voss. If it comes to a choice between the chest and the man, the chest wins. Every time. Swear it or hand me your credentials now."

The river murmured below the window. Somewhere downstairs, the first clerks were arriving, unlocking drawers full of paper that had once been a nation.

"I swear it," Sela said.

* * *

She bought the ingot back from Halvard at eight o'clock, paying cash and over market, and watched the old fence's eyes go shrewd and knowing as he counted it. Then she sat in her car across the street from the Ferryman's Rest for two hours, watching room four's window, and made the call she'd been avoiding since dawn.

"Bram," she said, when the mapmaker answered. "It's Sela Voss. I need to know who else has been asking about the Vhalorian seal lately. Anyone. A stranger, a traveler, anyone at all."

A pause on the line, long enough that she could hear the tick of Bram's clock, the one he'd salvaged from the flooded district and never had the decency to feel guilty about.

"Now why would the Consortium's girl care who's asking about dead Vhaloria?" the old man said. His voice was dry as the paper he traded in. "Unless somebody's found something the water kept."

"Answer the question, Bram."

"Answer mine first." Another pause. "Or don't. I'll tell you anyway, free of charge, because I like the sound of my own generosity. Yes. There's a stranger asking questions. Been at it two days. Young fellow, drives a storm rig, pays cash, doesn't know what he's carrying. And here's the part you'll find interesting, Miss Voss, the part I'd charge anyone else for: he doesn't remember why the seal looks familiar to him. But it does. You should see his face when he looks at it. Like a man hearing his own name called in a language he's forgotten he speaks."

Sela closed her eyes. Through the windshield, room four's curtain twitched, and a shadow crossed it—tall, restless, moving the way people move when they've decided to leave and haven't admitted it yet.

"Bram. What did you tell him?"

"I sold him a map," the old mapmaker said cheerfully. "Best kind. The kind that leads somewhere the buyer doesn't believe exists. Up the escarpment, Miss Voss. To Odile Hark."

The line went dead in her ear, and Sela Voss sat very still in the cold car, doing the arithmetic of the next three days: the passes, the snowline, the distance to the burned edge of the high country—and the fact that somewhere inside that shabby storm rig, rolling north toward the mountains, a man who didn't know his own name was carrying the end of the Kestrel family in a sealed chest under his spare tire.

She started the engine. By any means, Dagny had said. Alive, if it costs nothing.

Sela pulled away from the curb and pointed the car north, toward the escarpment, telling herself—as the professionals do—that the flicker behind her sternum was only the weather coming in.
