## 5. The Long Way Up

He left Corren's Ferry before the lamps came on, which was the only way to leave a town where you owed money to men who owned the lamps.

The truck took the north road out of town with its lights killed and its engine running on the ragged edge of complaint. August drove by starlight and memory, hands at ten and two, the way you drive when you have decided that if anything comes out of the dark toward you, you will not swerve. Behind him the ferry landing glowed a dull orange against the low clouds, and somewhere in that glow Sela Voss was waking up or not waking up, deciding what to tell Dagny Kestrel or deciding nothing at all, and every mile he put between himself and that orange light felt less like escape than like the first turn of a longer race.

Bram's map lay open on the passenger seat, weighted down by the gold ingot he hadn't been able to sell.

That was the joke of it, he supposed, if there was a joke. He had walked into Halvard's pawnshop with an ingot stamped with a dead nation's crest and a story about scrap salvage, and Halvard—a man who could smell a lie the way August could smell rain—had turned it over twice in his soft hands and said, *Where did you get this, Ashgrove?* And August had watched the man's eyes go from the ingot to the window to something past the window, some calculation running behind them like water behind a weir, and he had understood in one cold drop that the ingot wasn't currency anymore. It was a signal flare. Somewhere in Corren's Ferry, the moment Halvard's thumb crossed that crest, a telephone had rung.

So he'd taken it back. Walked out with his lie half-spoken and his debt still sitting on him like wet snow on a roof, and gone back to Bram's cluttered shop above the chandlery, where the old mapmaker had unrolled forty years of silence and called it Vhaloria.

*They'll come for the chest,* Bram had said, rolling the map with hands that shook until they touched paper and then didn't. *Not for you. For it. You're just the vehicle, boy. Vehicles can be replaced.*

*Then I'll stop being a vehicle.*

*You'll be a courier, then.* Bram had looked up at him with eyes gone milky at the edges but sharp as awls at the center. *There's a difference. A vehicle carries what it's told. A courier chooses the destination.* He had tapped the map, high up where the ink ran into the hatched blue of altitude. *Odile Hark. The counter-archive. If any of it is still true—if any of them are still alive—it's her.*

Now the map rode beside him, and the chest rode in the truck bed under a tarp and two spare tires and a coil of winch cable, and August drove north into country that got poorer and steeper with every mile, and tried to feel like a courier instead of a man running.

It was strange how little difference it made to the road. Fear was fear whether you were fleeing something or pursuing it; the body did not distinguish. His mouth was dry either way. His hands ached on the wheel either way. But there was one difference, and he found it around midnight, when he stopped at a spring above the last of the farm country and let the engine tick and drank from cupped hands so cold they burned.

When he had run before—and he had been running, in one way or another, since the day the Kestrel Consortium's loan officer had smiled at him across a desk and said *storm chasing, Mr. Ashgrove, that's a growth industry*—every road had narrowed ahead of him. Every choice had been a choice of which wall to put his back against. But this road widened. This road went up, and up meant weather, and weather was the one country where August Ashgrove was not poor. He stood at the spring with the water running off his chin and looked north to where the escarpment stood against the stars—not visible, exactly, not at sixty miles, but present, the way a mountain is present, a blacker blackness where the stars stopped—and he felt something he had no name for and decided, drinking again, that it might be hope, or might just be altitude beginning already, thinning the air of everything except the next ridge.

Either way, he drove on.

* * *

The foothills began an hour after dawn, and the trucks began twenty minutes later.

He saw the first one in his mirror as a dust plume where no road work should be, five miles back on the switchbacks above the Alder's north fork. He told himself it was a ranch truck. Then a second plume appeared, farther east, paralleling him along a track that shouldn't have carried traffic, and he stopped telling himself things and started doing arithmetic.

Two vehicles. One behind, one flanking. Not closing—shadowing. That was worse than closing. Men who wanted to run you off the road wanted your truck. Men who wanted you to know they were there wanted *you*, calm, unhurried, driving yourself wherever they needed you to drive, all the way to whatever gate they'd built at the end of the road. Sela Voss's voice came back to him across Halvard's counter: *We're not thieves, Mr. Ashgrove. We're optimists.* And he understood now what she'd meant. Thieves take. Optimists wait for the future to deliver you.

Fine, he thought. Let them shadow. He knew these hills the way a fisherman knows a river—not the roads but the weather, and the weather would make the roads.

Because that was the thing about the foothills of the escarpment in late autumn: the whole country was a machine for making storms. Warm air still pooled in the valleys off the sun-baked southern slopes, and above the tree line the first arctic fronts were already sliding down off the high plateau like slow grey glaciers, and when those two masses met over the middle elevations, the sky did things that maps couldn't predict and men in trucks couldn't follow. August had spent eleven years and most of his solvency learning to read that meeting. He pulled over at a wide place below a granite shoulder, climbed onto the cab roof with his binoculars, and read.

West: cumulus building over the high divide, flat-based, hard-edged, tops going up like smoke in a windless room. That was the front arriving early, or the mountains squeezing it out of a sky that had seemed empty at dawn. South: the dust plumes, closer now, maybe three miles, patient as interest accruing. East: clear, for now, a corridor of sunlight lying along the valley like a rug someone had forgotten to take up.

And between west and south, a canyon—the Alder's original channel, before the dams, before the flood, a deep granite slot that Bram's map marked with a single word in the old man's cramped hand: *Wetherell's Cut. Passable. Weather-dependent.*

Weather-dependent. August almost laughed. Everything worth doing was.

He dropped back into the cab, checked the tarp over the chest, and turned east into the sunlight, letting the trucks see him do it, letting them settle into their patient formation, because the cut was west and he wanted them comfortable, wanted them thinking they had him read like a ledger. He gave them a full hour of easy driving through the sunlit valley, long enough for the western sky to build itself into something serious, and then, at a bend where a dry wash cut down from the ridgeline, he swung north, bounced up the wash for four hundred yards of teeth-rattling gravel, and looped back west under the shoulder of the ridge, out of his own mirror's reach and theirs.

Behind him, faint as a struck match, he heard—or imagined he heard—the change in their engines. The patience coming off. The optimists becoming something else.

* * *

He reached the mouth of Wetherell's Cut at midafternoon, with the storm front a hundred feet tall across the western sky and coming on like the end of an argument.

Up close the canyon was older and stranger than the map suggested. Granite walls rose sheer on both sides, streaked with a high tide line of pale mineral stain six feet above his head, and August stopped the truck in the shadowed mouth of it and stared at that line for a long time without understanding why it made the hair rise on his arms. It was flood debris. Old flood debris—driftwood wedged in cracks far above any water the Alder had held in living memory, silt pressed into seams, a snagged wagon wheel rusting to lace thirty feet up. The river had been here once, and higher than this, and angrier.

*The Quarter Flood,* Bram had said. *Forty years ago. They'll tell you it was rain. Rain doesn't leave wagon wheels in granite.*

August sat with his hands on the wheel and let the memory try to surface, the way it had been trying since the night he pulled the chest from the willow snag—that flickering, unplaceable childhood, water sounds, a woman singing in a language he could taste but not translate, the smell of lamp oil and wet wool. It rose partway and sank, the way it always did, like something drowned that couldn't quite give up its dead. He gripped the wheel until the ache in his knuckles matched the ache behind his eyes, and then he drove into the cut, because the storm was coming and the storm was cover and cover was survival, and grief could wait. Grief, apparently, had waited forty years already.

The canyon floor was good going for two miles—packed gravel, gentle grade, walls wide enough to drive abreast in—and then it narrowed, and the light went, and the sound of the storm arrived before the storm did: a low pressure-change hum in his ears, a pressure he felt in his fillings, the whole atmosphere leaning west and pushing. August stopped where the walls pinched to a single lane, got out, and did what he had done ten thousand times in eleven years: he listened to the sky.

Thunder, but wrong thunder—flat, rolling, continuous, the sound of a storm with no gaps in it, a storm that had committed. Wind coming downcanyon against the flow, which meant outflow, which meant the cell's heart was close and heavy with rain it hadn't dropped yet. And underneath it all, high and thin, a sound he knew better than his own heartbeat: the whistle of air through the notch in the western rim, the same note, always the same note, that meant flash-flood conditions in narrow drainages.

Any other year, any other life, he would have parked on the high bench and waited it out, because that was the rule and the rule kept you alive. But the rule assumed you had time, and the trucks behind him had taken his time away. He looked up at the notch, whistling its thin warning, and he looked back down the canyon at the dust of his pursuers, and he made the calculation that separated the chasers who died from the ones who merely went broke: the cell's core would cross the rim in forty minutes, drop its load in fifteen, and the water would take another ten to gather and come down this slot like the hand of God wiping a slate.

Seventy minutes. The far end of the cut was three miles. He could make it in twenty-five if the ford at the narrows was passable, and he'd know that in five minutes, and if it wasn't—

If it wasn't, he'd climb. There were ledges on the south wall; he'd seen them from below. The chest would go on his back. The truck would be a loss, and the truck was everything, and the thought of losing it opened a hole in him he refused to look into.

He drove.

The narrows ford was knee-deep and rising when he hit it, the water already carrying a tea-dark stain of upstream rain, and he went through it in second gear with the bow wave climbing his doors and the truck shuddering like a living thing that wanted to argue. On the far side the canyon bent north and began to widen, and he drove the widening with the storm's first fat drops hitting the windshield like thrown gravel, counting seconds between lightning and thunder, watching the gap shrink from four miles to three to two, and the whole time the strange feeling rode with him, the one he'd found at the spring, the one he was starting to trust:

He was not running away. For the first time in eleven years, every mile of this road pointed at something. Odile Hark. The counter-archive. A dying woman at the top of the world who supposedly kept the names of everyone the water had taken, and among those names, though he couldn't have said how he knew it, was one that belonged to him.

The rain arrived all at once, a white roaring curtain, and behind him, muffled by it, he heard the canyon's acoustics change—the trucks stopping, engines idling, men making the sensible calculation he'd already made for them. They'd wait out the storm on the near side of the narrows. Sensible. Optimistic.

August drove up out of the cut as the flash flood came down it, and from the high ground he turned once and watched the water arrive in the narrows like a brown train, twelve feet of the Alder's old anger running through a slot that had been built to hold it, and he understood, watching it, that he had just driven through the exact argument of the chest on his truck bed. This was what water did when men arranged it. This was what it had done to the Quarter.

He drove north. The storm walked with him, covering his tracks, and near dusk, on a high bench where the road bent around a shoulder of rock, he saw the headlights behind him—two pairs, far back, stopped at the flooded narrows, patient even now.

But there was a third pair of lights, much closer, tucked into a side draw a quarter mile ahead, lights that went dark the moment his own swept across them.

August slowed. His hand found the tire iron under the seat.

Whoever it was, they weren't the optimists. The optimists were behind him, on the wrong side of a flood. Whoever was waiting in the draw ahead had gotten there first, had known the route, had known the cut, had known where a man running north with a dead nation's seal would have no choice but to pass.

The rain hammered the roof. August eased his foot toward the brake, and in the draw, barely visible through the streaming windshield, a figure stepped out onto the road and raised one arm—not a weapon, he thought, not quite—and stood there in the headlights' spill, waiting for him to decide.
