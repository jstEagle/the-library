## 6. Weather Is a Witness

The canyon had a name on Bram's map—*Kestrel's Throat*, which August found less a coincidence than an insult—and it swallowed the road whole for eleven miles of wet granite and rattling water. He drove into it because the map said he must, and because behind him, three switchbacks down, a black Consortium truck with government plates said he could not.

He'd seen them first as a shimmer in his mirrors, the way you see heat distortion off summer asphalt, and then as a certainty: two vehicles, riding close, patient as vultures. They weren't trying to run him off the road. Not yet. They were herding him, keeping him in the canyon's throat where the walls rose sheer on either side and there was exactly one direction to go.

August checked his fuel gauge, then the sky, and did the arithmetic that had kept him alive through nineteen years of chasing weather other men fled from.

The sky was wrong. Beautifully, catastrophically wrong.

It had been building since dawn—a bruised lavender mass stacking up over the western rim, towers going up like smoke from a fire beneath the world. Most drivers would have called it a pretty afternoon. August saw the anvil spreading flat and wide, saw the beaver-tail of cloud streaming out ahead of the storm's core, saw the way the light had gone that particular greenish-brass color that meant the air was holding more water than it knew what to do with. The dewpoint at Corren's Ferry had been sixty-eight degrees. In the canyon, with the river breathing moisture up between the walls, it would be higher.

Somewhere over the divide, all that energy was looking for an excuse.

He pulled into a gravel pull-off beneath an overhang of rock, killed the engine, and got out his instruments the way another man might get out a cigarette in a moment of stress. Barometer: falling fast, a full millibar in twenty minutes. Wind at the rim: southwest, gusting, feeding the storm's rotation. And there—the thing that made his stomach drop even as his pulse quickened with something embarrassingly close to joy—was the sound. A low, continuous rumble from the west, not thunder exactly but the sky clearing its throat, the sustained roar of a mesocyclone organizing itself twelve miles away and closing.

A generational cell, maybe. Or close enough.

He stood in the pull-off with his anemometer raised and let himself, for ten full seconds, simply love it. That was the sickness of his profession and the truth of his heart: no matter what was chasing him, no matter what was in the chest wrapped in a tarp behind the seat, when the sky went to war August Ashgrove felt more alive than at any other hour God offered. The storm didn't care about his debts. The storm didn't care about Vhaloria or the Kestrel Consortium or the gold ingot he'd been fool enough to pawn. The storm cared about pressure gradients and instability and shear, and it would treat every man in this canyon with perfect, impartial violence.

There was a comfort in that he couldn't find anywhere else on earth.

Then he thought about the canyon walls, and the river already running high and brown between them, and the two trucks behind him full of men whose employer had once drowned an entire nation for profit, and the comfort curdled into something colder.

They were trapped together, all of them, in a stone throat with a flood coming down it.

* * *

The first rain hit at 4:40 in the afternoon, fat drops the size of coins that struck the rock like thrown gravel, and within four minutes it was a wall. August had wedged the truck under the overhang, nose pointed downslope, engine off but ready. Through the sheeting water he watched the road vanish—not fade, *vanish*—until the world beyond twenty feet was gray roar.

And still the trucks came.

He heard them before he saw them, engines laboring, tires hissing on flooded asphalt. They crawled past his hiding place without slowing—they couldn't see him, and they weren't looking; they were committed now, running for the far end of the throat before the river took the road. August counted seconds between lightning flash and thundercrack. Five seconds. Four. Three. The storm's core was almost overhead.

Then the leading truck braked.

He saw it happen with the terrible clarity of a man watching a mistake he'd predicted: the vehicle stopped dead in the middle of the canyon floor where the road dipped toward a culvert, its wipers flailing uselessly, and above it the western wall of the canyon had gone strange. Not waterfall-strange. *Vein*-strange. Water was coming out of the rock itself, dozens of threads of it, the mountain's saturated skin weeping from every crack, which meant the soil above was holding nothing back, which meant—

"Move," August whispered, as if the driver could hear him. "Come on, move."

The truck didn't move. Maybe the driver had decided to wait it out. Maybe the engine had flooded. It didn't matter, because at 4:57 the debris flow came around the bend upstream like a brown train.

It wasn't water, not really. It was the canyon liquefying—boulders the size of refrigerators surfing a wave of mud and timber, moving at the speed of a galloping horse, making a sound August had heard twice in his life and never forgotten: a grinding, groaning roar, like the earth being chewed. The flow hit the lead truck broadside and simply erased it, shoving it sideways off the road and into the cut, rolling it twice, and then the second truck, which had tried to reverse, caught the edge of it and spun like a toy.

August sat with both hands on the wheel and his heart going like a trip-hammer, and he did not feel triumph. He felt the specific horror of a man who has just watched the thing he loves kill people.

When the flow passed and the rain settled into a steady hammering fall, he waited ten minutes by his watch, then fifteen, then got out into the deluge with a flashlight and worked his way downstream along the road's shoulder.

The first truck was visible only as a roofline above the mud, canted against the culvert. The second had come to rest forty yards further on, passenger side crushed against the canyon wall, driver's door hanging open. There was blood in the water, thinning as it ran. August followed the door's arc with his light and found boot prints in the fresh mud, staggered, heading upslope toward a shelf of rock—two sets, one dragging.

Alive, then. At least two of them. Crawling out of the wreck of their own pursuit into a canyon that was still, even now, shedding its skin.

He should keep walking. Every instinct honed by nineteen years of debt said keep walking, take their misfortune as the gift it was, put miles between yourself and the Kestrel Consortium while the storm covered your tracks. The chest was behind the seat of his truck. The map was in his jacket. Odile Hark was dying somewhere above the escarpment and the snows were coming.

But the second set of boot prints was dragging, and the temperature was dropping, and hypothermia killed faster than any man with a gun, and August had spent his whole life driving toward disasters other people were trapped inside of. It was, he supposed, the only decent thing about him. It might also be the stupidest.

He found them in a shallow cave thirty feet above the road: two men, soaked through, one cradling a forearm bent at an angle forearms don't go, the younger one shivering so hard his teeth chattered like dice in a cup. Between them, half-buried in the mud they'd dragged in with them, lay the scattered contents of a rucksack that had come open in the wreck.

August's flashlight swept across it and stopped.

* * *

Later, he would try to reconstruct the order of things—what he saw first, what he understood first—but memory refused to cooperate. It gave him the scene all at once, the way a photograph develops: rope, pitons, a radio in a waterproof case stamped with the Kestrel kestrel-in-flight, a coil of detonation cord, and, lying face-up on a flattened patch of mud like something deliberately placed there, a photograph.

It was a photograph of a boy.

Maybe six years old, maybe seven. Standing on a dock in front of a white boat with a name painted on the hull in letters the water damage had blurred. Dark hair cut short. Serious eyes, the kind of serious that comes from being told too often to behave. And pinned to the collar of his small coat, catching the sun, a little metal badge—a bird in flight, wings swept forward, unmistakable even at this size, even after forty years.

The seal of Vhaloria. The same stamp pressed into the wax that had sealed the chest. The same crest on the land registry pages he'd read by lamplight in Bram's back room until his eyes burned.

August picked the photograph up with fingers that did not feel like his fingers.

He knew the boy. That was the impossible thing, the vertiginous thing, the thing that made the cave seem to tilt around him. He knew him the way you know your own reflection in a window at night—recognized and estranged at once. Because it was not a photograph of some stranger's child. It was a photograph of *him*. He was certain of it the way you are certain of your own heartbeat. The set of the mouth was his mouth. The ears were his ears—he'd spent a lifetime disliking those ears. Someone had taken this picture, someone had printed it, someone had carried it folded in a rucksack across mountains and canyons, and someone in the Kestrel Consortium had thought it important enough that the man hunting August Ashgrove should have it on his person.

"Why," he said aloud, and the word came out cracked. "Why do you have this?"

The older man—the one with the broken arm, gray-stubbled, fifty maybe, with the flat competent look of hired muscle gone soft—looked at the photo, then at August, and something moved behind his eyes that August had seen before in the faces of men who knew more than their pay grade allowed. Pity, maybe. Or fear wearing pity's clothes.

"You're him," the man said. His voice was hoarse from the cold. "You're actually him."

"I'm August Ashgrove. I drive a weather rig and I owe everybody money. Who am I to you?"

The man laughed, and it turned into a cough, and he shook his head. Rain drummed on the rock above them. Down in the canyon the river had risen another foot and was carrying branches past the cave mouth like a procession of dark arms.

"They didn't tell us anything," the man said finally. "That's the honest answer. We got a name, a vehicle, a route. And we got—" he nodded at the photograph with his chin, careful of the arm— "that. From upstairs. With instructions that if it came down to it, we were to show it to you." He paused. "I never asked why. You don't, in this line. But I'll tell you what I think, since I'm sitting in a cave with a busted arm and my partner's half-frozen and none of it matters anymore. I think somebody upstairs has known who you are a lot longer than you have."

The words went into August like cold water into a boot.

Known who he was. Known longer than he had. But he didn't know who he was—that was the whole architecture of his life, built on the absence. A childhood before age seven that came back to him only as fragments: a smell of beeswax and river water, a woman's voice singing in a language he'd assumed was nonsense, a recurring dream of stairs going down into green light. He'd built the rest from scratch—foster homes, a mechanic's apprenticeship, a truck, a debt, a road. August Ashgrove, self-made, which everyone praised and no one examined too closely, because self-made men are conveniently assumed to have made themselves out of nothing.

Out of nothing. Out of a drowned country. Out of a name filed somewhere in a land registry among the dispossessed.

The shivering young one stirred and mumbled something. August knelt, shrugged off his dry jacket, and got it around the kid's shoulders, working on autopilot while the older man watched him with that unreadable expression.

"There's a shelter cabin two miles down," August heard himself say. "Old maintenance hut, left side of the road, green door. Get him there, get him warm, splint that arm straight. There's a first-aid kit bolted to the wall." He was reciting it from the survey he'd done of this canyon years ago, chasing a hailstorm, noting shelters the way other men noted gas stations. "The river will drop by morning. Road's passable by noon if the rain holds off."

"And you?" the man asked. "Just like that? We were sent to take the chest off you."

"I know."

"You're leaving us alive with the truck radio. We call it in, you're done."

August looked at the photograph again. The boy on the dock squinted back at him across forty years and a flooded nation, wearing a country on his collar that no longer appeared on any map, and August understood suddenly, completely, that the question of whether these men lived or died was not actually his to answer by mercy or cruelty. The storm had answered it. The storm always answered it; that was the one honest witness left in the world. All August could do was decide what kind of man walked out of this canyon.

"The radio's probably mud," he said, standing. "But if it works—call whoever you want. Tell them the chest is going up the escarpment. Tell them August Ashgrove said to come and get it." He tucked the photograph into his inner pocket, against his chest, where it lay like a second heartbeat. "Tell them he knows now."

* * *

He drove out of Kestrel's Throat at dusk with the rain thinning to silver threads and the western sky torn open along its seams, gilded light pouring through onto the wet road so that the whole canyon gleamed like the inside of a shell. Behind him the river was already beginning its slow retreat, and somewhere back in the dark the two men were stumbling toward a green door, and one of them might radio it in and one of them might not, and August found he could not make himself care very much which.

What he cared about was the weight in his breast pocket.

At the top of the canyon, where the road climbed out onto the foothills' first long shoulder, he pulled over and held the photograph up to the last of the light. The boy. The boat. The pin. He turned it over. On the back, in faded pencil, in handwriting that made his breath stop because some buried room in him recognized it the way a dog recognizes a footstep at the door—one word, one name, written small and careful:

*Augustin.*

Not August. Augustin. A name with a country attached to it, a name that belonged to a dock and a white boat and a woman's voice singing in a language he'd been told was nonsense, a name that someone at the Kestrel Consortium had carried in a rucksack through the mountains like a weapon.

Because that was what it was. They hadn't sent that photograph as a kindness or a clue. They'd sent it as leverage, or bait, or a leash—proof that they knew the thing about him that he didn't know himself, and that they could produce it whenever it served them. His own past, held in trust by the heirs of the men who had drowned it.

August sat in the idling truck while the light failed, and above the escarpment the sky was already building tomorrow's weather—clean, cold, high clouds streaming in from the northwest like a banner army. Snow weather. Pass-closing weather. Six days, maybe five, before the high country shut itself away for the winter, and somewhere up there a dying archivist was waiting with whatever remained of a nation's memory.

He put the truck in gear.

Whatever his name was—August, Augustin, whatever the registry said, whatever the Consortium thought it owned—he was going to carry it up the mountain himself, before anyone else got the chance to tell him what it meant.
