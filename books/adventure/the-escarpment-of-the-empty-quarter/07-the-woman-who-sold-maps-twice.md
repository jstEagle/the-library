## 7. The Woman Who Sold Maps Twice

Sela Voss had sold maps twice in her life, and both times she had lied about what they showed.

The first time she was nineteen, working the tourist kiosk at Corren's Ferry, folding paper mountains into squares for hikers who wanted to believe the escarpment was conquerable. She'd sell them the pretty version—the one with the marked trails and the cheerful elevation notes—and pocket the difference when they asked for the honest one, the surveyor's copy with the washouts and the deadfall. It wasn't malice. It was arithmetic. Pretty maps moved; honest maps sat in the drawer.

The second time was eleven years later, in a windowless room on the fourth floor of a Kestrel Consortium annex, when she'd handed over the route August Ashgrove would take out of the lowlands. That map had been drawn by no cartographer. It was inference, pattern, and one small cruelty: she had flagged the gold ingot he pawned, and the flag had started everything. Dagny Kestrel had looked at her work for a long moment—longer than she looked at anything—and said, *Good.* One word, and Sela had felt it land like a medal pinned to skin that hadn't been numbed first.

Now she stood at the edge of a cut hayfield two days north of Corren's Ferry, watching dust hang above a tree line where a truck had passed not long ago, and she understood, with the slow cold clarity of a woman reading her own map too late, that she might be the third thing she'd ever sold.

Herself.

* * *

The morning had begun cleanly, which should have warned her.

She'd picked up Ashgrove's trail before dawn at the ford below Miller's Bend—tire tracks in the silt, still holding water, which meant he'd crossed within the hour after the river crested. He drove like a man who knew weather, because he did. He'd waited out the worst of the runoff and crossed at the exact window when the ford was passable but soft enough to read his passage like a signature. Sela had crouched there in the gray light with her fingers in the mud and felt something uncomfortably close to admiration. Then she'd filed it away where she kept everything that wasn't useful.

The trail ran north and west, toward the escarpment, exactly as Bram's map said it would. The old mapmaker had given her nothing directly—she'd tried him twice, and the second time he'd looked at her with those milky, unfooled eyes and said, "You have the look of a woman carrying someone else's errand, miss. Those never end well for the carrier." But she didn't need Bram. She needed only to think like Ashgrove, and thinking like Ashgrove had become, over the past three days, an occupation she performed more diligently than she'd ever performed loyalty.

He was running toward something. That was the anomaly. Debtors ran away—they scattered, they doubled back, they made noise in small towns buying time. Ashgrove ran in a straight line at the highest ground in the region, in late autumn, ahead of closing snows. Nobody ran *toward* the escarpment in October unless something up there mattered more than staying alive comfortably. Which meant the chest wasn't loot to him. It was cargo.

And cargo meant delivery. And delivery meant a recipient. And somewhere in the Consortium's files—she'd checked, quietly, from the road office in Harrow Gap—there was no record of any Vhalorian survivor community, any counter-archive, any dying archivist named Hark. The files had a hole shaped like a nation. Forty years of satellite surveys of the high country, all catalogued, all cross-referenced, and the quarter beyond the escarpment appeared in them as an administrative blank. Not unmapped. *Unmapped on purpose.*

Sela had spent her career believing the Consortium was ruthless the way weather is ruthless—impersonal, enormous, indifferent to individuals. Standing in the road office with the blank quarter glowing on her screen, she'd felt the first tremor of a different hypothesis: that the ruthlessness was personal, and always had been, and that the blankness was the oldest entry in the ledger.

She'd driven north anyway. That was the part she couldn't explain afterward, when she lay awake listening to the pines tick and shift. She'd seen the shape of the trap and driven into it, because the alternative—turning around, reporting the blank, asking Dagny Kestrel directly *what is in the quarter*—was unthinkable. You didn't ask Dagny questions. You brought her answers, and you hoped the answers were the ones she already owned.

* * *

She found the second team at noon, and she found them by their mistakes.

Ashgrove's track left the logging road at a switchback above the Alder's north fork and climbed into the foothills through scree and pine, the kind of ground where a single vehicle leaves a scar visible from a ridge a mile off. Sela had stopped her own truck in the trees and gone up on foot with binoculars, the way she'd been trained, and from the shoulder of the third ridge she saw them: three men in Consortium field gear, moving along Ashgrove's trail in daylight, in column, without a point man, without radio discipline—one of them smoking, the smoke rising straight up in the still air like a signal flare addressed to anyone looking.

They weren't tracking Ashgrove. They were following his tire marks with the diligence of men who had been handed a route and told to walk it. And they were moving too fast for pursuit and too slow for interception, which meant they weren't pursuing anything at all.

They were positioning.

Sela lowered the binoculars and stood very still among the pines while the arithmetic finished itself. Kestrel's first team—hers, nominally, though she'd worked alone since Harrow Gap—was supposed to bring Ashgrove in, or the chest, or preferably both, with paperwork. Second teams existed for a different category of problem. Second teams existed for problems that couldn't be photographed.

Loose ends. The phrase arrived in her mind wearing Dagny Kestrel's voice, calm as a ledger line. *Retrieve it by any means.* She had taken the order as license. She was beginning to understand it had also been a description of herself.

Because here was the thing she had done, three nights ago, alone in her room at the Ferry with the chest's inventory spread on the bed: she had copied pages. Photographs, actually—quick, angled, amateur, taken with the cheap camera she used for documenting condition reports. The land registry. A dozen folios of names and parcel numbers, and one cover sheet bearing the Vhalorian crest and a date forty years old, and beneath the crest, in the corner, a countersignature she hadn't recognized then and had spent every night since trying not to recognize now.

She hadn't reported the copies. She hadn't decided to keep them, either. They existed in the space between those two facts, the way a held breath exists between inhaling and lying.

If Dagny learned about them—and Dagny learned about everything eventually, the way water learns about a basement—then Sela Voss was no longer an asset retrieving property. She was a witness in possession of evidence. And witnesses, in the Consortium's forty-year accounting, had a documented history of becoming blanks on maps.

The smokers down on the trail flicked his cigarette away and the three of them resumed their climb, unhurried, patient, following a dead man's tire tracks—or a live man's, depending on how the next six days went—toward whatever junction Dagny had chosen for the erasure.

Sela did the math on the terrain instead of the morality, because the terrain was the part she could act on. Their route converged with hers at the notch below Raven's Tooth, where the foothills funneled every path toward the high country into a single defile. If Ashgrove came through the notch—and he would, unless Bram's map showed him another way, and she had to assume it did, because the old man had liked Ashgrove and disliked her, and men distributed their allegiances exactly that way—then the second team would reach him first, or she would reach him first and lead the second team to him by existing.

Either way, the loose ends gathered at the notch. Herself included.

She went down the ridge the careful way, on her heels, hand on every trunk, and got her truck moving south before the decision fully crystallized, because she had learned long ago that decisions are just rationalizations that caught up late. She was going to reach Ashgrove before they did. Not to save him—she was careful with that word, having watched what it cost people—but because he was currently the only person alive who could tell her what was really in the chest, and because a witness who wants to stay a person needs leverage, and leverage, in the high country in October, meant knowing things other people didn't.

And underneath the leverage, in the drawer where she kept the honest maps, a smaller and more dangerous thought: *if they'll burn Odile Hark's archive, and erase a nation, and spend a second team on a storm chaser, then the copies in my camera are either my death warrant or my pension, and I get to choose which by choosing whose side of the mountain I'm standing on when this ends.*

* * *

They hit her at dusk, four miles short of the notch, and the professionalism of it was the worst part.

No headlights. No challenge, no "halt, Consortium business," none of the theater that lets a person believe they're being arrested rather than deleted. Just her own truck's engine note suddenly doubled by a second engine behind her on the forest road, and then a third ahead, parked cockeyed across the lane with its lights off, and men stepping out of the dark with the unhurried economy of people doing a job they'd rehearsed.

Sela braked, assessed, and understood in one cold flash that she had driven into the funnel from the wrong side. The notch wasn't their objective. She was. The team on Ashgrove's trail was the visible hand; this was the other one, the quiet one, sent down the mountain to tidy the tidier.

"Ms. Voss." The speaker was a broad, gray-templed man with a Consortium field jacket and the flat courtesy of middle management. "Ms. Kestrel asks that you turn over your documentation and come back down with us. There's a debrief."

"At the lodge," Sela said.

"At the lodge," he agreed, and smiled with nothing above the cheekbones.

Every protocol she'd been trained in said comply. Comply, and you became a name in a room with no windows, and rooms with no windows in Consortium facilities had a way of producing confessions, transfers, resignations, disappearances—whatever the file required. Every instinct she'd sharpened in eleven years of selling certainty to frightened people said the same thing the mapmaker had said: *errands like this don't end well for the carrier.*

"I have it in the truck," she said. "Give me a minute."

He nodded, and that was the mistake they made—courtesy, again, the organization's oldest tell. She turned toward the cab as if resigned, and her hand closed on the camera case, and she threw it—not at the men, at the road's steep embankment, where it tumbled into black brush—and went over the tailgate into the ditch on the far side as the first shot cracked the night open.

The bullet took her high on the left arm, a white-hot stripe above the shoulder, spinning her half around, and she was already falling so the fall probably saved the rest of her. She rolled down the ditch through wet fern and stone, heard boots on gravel and a voice saying *the case, get the case,* which confirmed everything and cost them seconds, and then she was up and moving along the streambed with her left arm hanging hot and useless and her right hand doing all the work of being alive.

They fired twice more, both high, shooting sound in the dark rather than shape. She didn't blame them. Night work on strangers' ground was bad work, and she'd made it worse by not stopping, by not bleeding where she was supposed to bleed. By the time she reached the tree line a half mile on, lungs full of iron, the voices behind her had organized into a search line, methodical, spaced, sweeping the streambed with lights they kept hooded.

They weren't chasing her anymore. They were *processing* the area. And somewhere upslope, Ashgrove was driving toward a notch with three professionals on his trail and a second team at his back, and nobody—nobody in the whole apparatus of the Kestrel Consortium—had ever intended for either of them to arrive anywhere.

* * *

She walked through the dark for two hours, which is the honest length of grief if you refuse to sit down.

The graze had stopped bleeding properly. Her jacket was stiffening. She followed water downhill because that was rule one and rules were what remained when loyalty finished draining out of a person, and somewhere in the second hour, crouched in the shallows of a feeder creek washing the wound with water cold enough to make her vision ring, Sela Voss finally let herself say the sentence she'd been not-saying since Harrow Gap:

*Dagny Kestrel is going to burn it all. The chest, the registry, the archivist, the shepherd valleys if she has to, the passes, the winter, everything—and me, and him, and every name in that book, twice. Once with water and once with fire.*

Her father had worked Consortium roads for thirty years and died with a pension and a plaque, and she had grown up believing that an institution that reliable must be, in some deep ledger sense, *right*. Eleven years she'd spent inside it converting that belief into salary. And the whole time, the truth had been sitting in the high country like a body under snow, waiting for a flood to give it back.

She laughed once, briefly, painfully, at the symmetry—the flood had delivered the chest to a drowning debtor, and the drowning debtor was going to deliver it to the top of the world, and she, Sela Voss, who had sold maps twice, was going to help him do it. Not for justice. Justice was a word for people whose arms worked. For self-preservation, which was the only currency that had never once devalued.

But she knew herself well enough, washing blood out of a sleeve by starlight, to suspect the distinction wouldn't survive contact with the man himself.

She found a forester's hut near midnight—a drying shed, unlocked, smelling of resin and mouse—and sat against its wall with her knees up and her pistol across her thighs, and let the shaking come. In the camera case tumbling down that embankment were her copies of the registry, and the second team had been ordered to retrieve the case specifically, which meant Dagny knew about the photographs, which meant the countdown on Sela Voss's life had started days ago and she'd walked around inside it drinking coffee.

Except—the thought arrived slowly, the way the important ones do—they'd shot at the case *after* she threw it, and shouted for it, and gotten it. So they had the copies. So why shoot her at all?

Because the copies proved she'd made them. Because a photograph of a photograph is still testimony. Because Dagny Kestrel did not leave instruments lying around that had seen the inside of the chest, no matter how thoroughly she collected what the instruments had produced.

Sela looked at her ruined arm in the thin moonlight coming through the shed boards and felt, beneath the pain and the fear, something settle into place with an almost audible click—the sound a lock makes, or a decision.

In the morning she would go up. Not around, not back down to report and be debriefed into a windowless room. Up, toward the notch, toward the escarpment, toward a storm chaser who owed her nothing and would trust her less than nothing, carrying the only things she had left: knowledge of Kestrel's routes, and a bullet hole that served as credentials no forger could match.

Let him hate her. Hate was a form of attention, and attention was negotiable. Everything else—the loyalty, the salary, the plaque-worthy institution—had already been repossessed.

Outside, far to the north, the sky over the escarpment had gone the bruised, stacked gray that meant weather was building behind the peaks, and Sela Voss, who had spent her life telling people what the maps showed, lay in a drying shed with her blood drying stiff and thought: *he'll read that sky better than I ever read a map. I just have to be there when he does.*

She slept in shifts, pistol in her fist, and dreamed of a kiosk full of paper mountains, and woke before dawn already walking.
