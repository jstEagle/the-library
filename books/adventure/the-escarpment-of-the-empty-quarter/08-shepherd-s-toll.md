## 8. Shepherd's Toll

The valley announced itself before August saw it — a smell of woodsmoke and wet wool riding down the wind, and then the dogs, three of them, flowing out of the mist like gray water finding its level. They circled the truck at a respectful distance, hackles up but voices low, herding him rather than challenging him, and August understood he was being escorted whether he liked it or not.

He had meant to pass through the high country without stopping. Bram's map showed a track climbing the eastern shoulder of the escarpment, a smugglers' route marked in faded pencil with a single annotation: *Ask at the stone folds. Tell them the river sent you.* But the truck's temperature gauge had been climbing since dawn, and the light was failing early behind a lid of cloud that promised snow within two days, and when the valley opened below him — a green cup of terraced pasture ringed by peaks the color of old iron — his hands made the decision before his mind did. He downshifted and let gravity take him down.

The village had no name on any map he owned. It didn't need one; it had been here longer than maps, or at least it behaved as though it had. Dry-stone walls stitched the slopes together in patterns too old to be practical, more habit than husbandry. The houses were low and slate-roofed, built to shrug off weather the way old men shrug off bad news. And everywhere, worked into lintels and gateposts and the carved beams of the well-house, was the same motif: a stylized wave breaking over a seven-pointed star.

August knew that star now. He'd spent three days with it stamped into the leather of the chest, tracing it by firelight until his eyes ached. Vhaloria. A country with no address, struck from every atlas, surviving only in the memory of people who had never been told they were supposed to forget.

An old woman met him at the first gate. She was small and straight-backed, wrapped in a shawl the color of undyed wool, and she looked at the truck, then at August, then at the chest lashed in the bed under a tarp, and something moved across her face like cloud-shadow over a field.

"You came up the river road," she said. Not a question.

"From Corren's Ferry," August said. "My engine's running hot. I only need water and a night out of the wind."

She kept looking at the chest. "And what do you carry?"

"Personal effects."

A long silence. Somewhere above them a ewe bleated, and one of the dogs answered it with a single bark, and the whole valley seemed to lean in and listen.

"The river sent you," the woman said quietly.

It wasn't what he'd planned to say. But Bram's instruction surfaced, and August heard himself answer, "Yes. The river sent me."

The woman closed her eyes for a moment, the way people do when a prayer is answered or a fear confirmed. When she opened them they were wet. "Then you'll come to the hall," she said. "I am Maren Ostrevik. My grandmother walked out of the Quarter when she was nine years old. You will eat with us tonight." Her gaze dropped again to the chest, and her voice dropped with it. "And you will tell us what the water gave back."

* * *

They fed him barley soup with lamb and wild thyme, dark bread, cheese pressed with juniper, and a spirit distilled from some mountain berry that scorched a line down his chest and sat there glowing. Forty people crowded the hall — shepherds, their wives, children peeking from doorways, elders who moved like the walls themselves had grown them. August counted nineteen faces old enough to have been born before the Flood, and every one of them watched him the way sailors watch a horizon.

Maren sat at his right hand. At his left sat a broad-shouldered man named Tomas who had shaken August's hand with a grip that could have cracked walnuts and said only, "You are welcome under our roof," as though the words were a contract.

"It's the seal," Maren said, when the bowls were cleared. "On your chest. Ilsa saw it when you drove past the lower fold. She ran up the hill faster than I've seen her move in twenty years."

"I found it in the Alder," August said. "After the flood crest. Wedged in a willow snag."

"The Alder." An old man across the table — blind, his eyes gone the pale blue of glacier ice — turned his face toward August's voice. "The Alder runs through the Quarter. Or it did. Before they drowned it, the Alder was a small river. After, it was a lake's throat." He tapped the table once with a knuckle. "I am Joren. I was born in Milovar, on the Alder's east bank, in the last year of Vhaloria. Tell me what else was in the chest."

August hesitated. He had spent three days deciding what to tell people and what to keep close, and the arithmetic never came out clean. These were strangers. Strangers with a dead nation in their blood and a look in their eyes that made him want to give them anything they asked.

"Papers," he said finally. "Land documents. A registry of holdings. Sealed orders, some of them. Gold."

The word *gold* landed hard; he heard breath drawn around the table. But it was the other thing that moved them.

"A registry," Maren repeated. "Of whose holdings?"

"Parcels in the flooded district. Names of owners. Dates of expropriation." He had read enough of it by now to speak the language of it, though not enough to understand why reading it felt like reading letters addressed to someone else. "Dates of... removal."

No one spoke. The fire cracked. Outside, the dogs sang once at something in the dark and fell silent.

Joren reached across the table and found August's wrist with surprising accuracy. His hand was dry and light as paper. "Forty years," he said, "we have said the words *the empty quarter*, because saying them was cheaper than saying what really happened. They told the world the land was vacant. Marginal. That the flooding was hydrology, progress, the price of power for the lowlands. And we —" his voice caught, steadied — "we who walked out were told to be grateful we walked out. There was no one to say otherwise. No papers. Papers burn." His grip tightened. "You are telling me papers survived."

"Some did."

"And you brought them up the mountain. To us."

"I'm passing through," August said. "There's a place higher up. An archivist. Odile Hark."

The name went through the room like current through wire. Maren crossed herself — an old gesture, half-foreign even here, borrowed from some southern faith — and Tomas muttered something in a language August almost recognized, its rhythms familiar from nowhere he could name, and realized with a cold turn of his stomach that it might be Vhalorian. That he might be hearing, for the first time in his adult life, the sound of a country that lived only in kitchens and curses and lullabies.

"Hark is real, then," Maren said softly. "We hoped. We sent word twice, years ago. No answer came."

"She may be dying. Bram thought so."

"Bram." Joren smiled without much humor. "The mapmaker. He drew our escape routes once, before the passes closed. A good man. A careful man." The blind eyes found August again. "And you, Ashgrove. What is a storm chaser doing carrying a nation's bones up an escarpment?"

"I owe people money," August said. It came out flat and true. "Bad people. The chest was going to buy my way clear. Then I read some of it, and now I don't know what it's going to do." He looked around the table, at the faces lit amber by the fire, at the wave-and-star carved into the beam above the hearth. "I'm taking it to Hark because it seems like the only direction left."

For a moment nobody moved. Then Joren began, very quietly, to laugh — a dry, rusted sound, not unkind. "The only direction left," he echoed. "That is how all of us came here, boy. Nobody chooses the high country. The high country is what remains when everything else is taken."

* * *

They gave him a bed in the loft of the hall, above the warmth of the banked fire, and sleep should have come easily after four days of sleeping in the truck cab with a hand near the ignition. Instead August lay staring at the rafters, listening to the house settle, feeling the borrowed identity settle over him like the wool blanket they'd left at the foot of the bed — heavier than it looked, warmer than he deserved.

*A returning heir.* That was what they believed. He'd seen it in the way Maren touched his sleeve when she refilled his bowl, in the way the children had been brought to look at him, in the way Tomas had stood at the door when August went out to check the engine and said, "We will watch your truck as we watch our own," which among these people was clearly a vow of some weight. They had looked at the seal on his chest and seen a son coming home, and August had not corrected them, because correcting them would mean explaining the truth: that he was a debtor and a drifter, that he'd pulled the chest from the river the way he pulled everything from the world — opportunistically, hungrily, because everything he owned fit in a truck bed.

Except that wasn't entirely true anymore either, and that was the problem. Because when Joren had said *Milovar, on the Alder's east bank*, something behind August's sternum had shifted, the way ice shifts on a river just before it goes. And when the old man had hummed two bars of a song while the dishes were cleared — a slow tune in a minor key that the whole table unconsciously swayed to — August had known the next bar before Joren sang it. Known it the way you know your own heartbeat: not learned, just there.

He lay in the loft and put his hand flat against his chest, over the sternum, as if he could press the feeling back down.

Somewhere below, a floorboard creaked, and Maren's voice rose softly — she was awake too, talking to someone in the kitchen. August crept to the loft's edge and listened without meaning to eavesdrop.

"— the seal is right, Mama. Right in every detail. And he knew the road over Col de Sarr when I asked him, knew it before I finished naming it. Who knows that road? Nobody under forty knows that road."

"He says he's no one," the second voice said — older, flintier. Grandmother Ilsa, presumably, the one who'd run up the hill.

"They all say they're no one. His mother's people probably said the same, walking out with nothing."

"His eyes," Ilsa said. There was a pause long enough for the fire to pop. "I served at the border station at Vey Pass, the last year. I saw ten thousand people leave the Quarter. I remember the eyes. People carrying countries on their backs get a particular look." Another pause. "He has the look, Mama. Whatever he calls himself."

August withdrew from the loft's edge and lay back down and did not sleep.

* * *

Morning came white and still, the peaks across the valley wearing fresh snow like a warning. The whole village assembled in the dooryard to see him off, which was the last thing he wanted, because assemblies of this kind, he was learning, came with obligations.

Tomas brought the truck around, watered and checked, and pressed a sack of food through the window — bread, cheese, dried meat, a skin of the berry spirit. "For the road. The high route past the burned station is three days if the weather holds. It will not hold. Go fast anyway."

Then Maren stepped forward, and the crowd quieted, and August understood with dawning dread that this was ceremony, that he was standing inside a ceremony, and there was no map for getting out of one.

She held out her hands, palms up. In the right palm lay a small object: a pin, tarnished silver, the seven-pointed star beneath a breaking wave. It caught the morning light and threw it back thin and cold.

"This was my grandmother's," Maren said, loud enough for all of them. "She wore it out of the Quarter. It has waited forty years for a reason to go home." She took August's hand — his left, unresisting — and closed his fingers around the pin. The metal was warm from her palm. "Swear it, Ashgrove. Swear that what the river gave you, you will carry home. To the archive. To the summit chapel if it comes to that. Swear that the names in that chest will be spoken again in the land that erased them."

The whole valley was watching. The children. The blind old man with his face lifted toward the sun. Ilsa with her remembered eyes. Behind them the escarpment stood in its wall of iron and ice, and somewhere beyond it, three days' climb away, a dying woman guarded whatever remained of a country, and somewhere behind *him*, down in the foothills, Consortium trucks were moving, and Dagny Kestrel was spending her father's fortune to make sure those names stayed drowned.

Every honest answer he had died in his throat. He couldn't say *I'm taking it to sell*. He couldn't say *I haven't decided anything*. He couldn't say *I don't even know who I am, and lately the not-knowing feels less like ignorance and more like damage*.

So he lied.

"I swear it," August said. His voice came out steady, which surprised him. "What the river gave me, I'll carry home. The names will be spoken."

Maren bowed her head. Around the dooryard, forty people breathed out at once, and several of the old ones wept without embarrassment, and Tomas gripped August's shoulder hard enough to bruise, and the pin sat in August's closed fist like a coal that wouldn't cool.

The lie fit so well it frightened him. It slid into place against everything else — the song he'd known the next bar of, the road he'd known before it was named, the way the word *home* had come out of his mouth sounding like a fact instead of a figure of speech. He climbed into the truck and started the engine and waved, and they waved, and the dogs trotted alongside to the edge of the village as if seeing off one of their own.

At the top of the valley track he stopped and looked back once. The village was already small, already folding itself back into the mountainside, smoke rising straight from the chimneys into the windless cold. He opened his fist. The pin lay in his palm, and beside it, where Maren had pressed it into his hand, his own skin bore the faint imprint of the star.

He drove north. Behind him the weather was building along the escarpment like a debt accruing interest, and ahead, in the chest on the seat beside him, forty years of names waited in the dark — including, though he did not yet know it, two that would stop his heart when he read them by firelight tomorrow night.

One of them was his mother's.

The other was his own.
