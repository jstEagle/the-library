## 10. Kestrel Ascendant

The lodge at Verrin's Rest had been built by men who wanted the mountains to know they'd been conquered. Dagny Kestrel felt that every time she walked its long gallery—the blackened timber, the iron chandeliers forged from old rail spikes, the windows cut tall and narrow like rifle slits so that the escarpment beyond was always framed as something watched, something held at bay. Her grandfather had commissioned it in the year of the Consolidation, when the Consortium's money still smelled of river silt and new-minted coin, and he had placed it deliberately on this shoulder of granite, three thousand feet above the valley floor, so that anyone summoned here would have to climb to reach him.

She climbed it every morning now. Not because she had to. Because the climb reminded her what the air tasted like at altitude—thin, clean, unforgiving—and because a woman who made decisions affecting eleven thousand shareholders and four governments ought to make them slightly oxygen-starved. It kept her honest. Or it kept her sharp, which in her experience amounted to the same thing.

This morning the mountain wore its first white cap of the season. She had seen it from the gallery window while dressing: a dusting along the high shoulders, gone by nine o'clock in the sun but unmistakable, the way the first gray hair is unmistakable. Winter was thinking about coming down. The passes would hold for perhaps three weeks, four if the weather stayed vain and warm. After that, the escarpment would close like a book.

Dagny intended to be holding the pen when it did.

* * *

The board convened at ten in the map room, which was not a room for maps anymore but had kept the name the way families keep names—out of habit, out of a reluctance to admit that anything had ever changed. The long table was walnut, older than any person seated at it, and around it sat seven faces Dagny had known for most of her life: Halloran with his gout and his shipping interests; the Marchetti cousins, who between them controlled the water rights of two provinces; old Vess, half-deaf and entirely awake behind his milky eyes; the others, each a monument to some ancestor's appetite. Portraits of those ancestors lined the walls above them, painted in the heavy oils of the last century, all of them staring down with the same expression—the patient look of people who had never once needed to hurry.

Dagny stood at the head of the table and did not sit. Sitting was for discussions. This was going to be an announcement wearing the clothes of a discussion.

"You've all read the summary I circulated last night," she said. "I'll assume you read it, because if any of you didn't, you're about to waste the most expensive hour of your year."

Halloran shifted his gouty foot under the table. "We read it, Dagny."

"Then you know what came out of the Alder River three weeks ago." She let the silence do a little work before she continued. "A chest. Sealed, watermarked, stamped with the crest of Vhaloria. Inside it, the land registry of the Quarter—every parcel, every deed, every name of every family that held title below the escarpment before the flood."

"The Quarter has been underwater for forty years," said Lira Marchetti, who had a voice like cold tea. "Whatever's in that chest is paper. Wet paper, at that."

"It's waxed vellum and it survived forty years in a sealed strongbox, which tells you how carefully someone packed it, and where there's one chest there are others." Dagny turned to the wall behind her, where a survey map of the escarpment country hung—the same map, in fact, that had hung there since her grandfather's day, though she had updated it herself, quietly, over the years. "But let's talk about what the registry proves, because that's the part that should keep you up tonight."

She picked up the pointer. It had been her father's; the grip was worn to the shape of his hand, and hers fit inside that shape the way a daughter fits inside a legacy.

"In the spring of the flood year, the Alder's upper watershed recorded rainfall at 340 percent of seasonal average. Everyone remembers the rain. What nobody was permitted to remember—" the pointer tapped the map, once, twice, "—is that six months earlier, the Consortium's predecessor holdings completed purchase of the entire drainage easement above the Quarter. Every culvert. Every diversion channel. Every sluice gate on the high tributaries. Bought at fair market value, filed through nine shell companies, approved by a water commission on which our founder sat as chair."

Old Vess spoke without looking up from his folded hands. "That's been rumored for forty years, Dagny. Rumors rot. Deeds don't."

"No," Dagny agreed. "Deeds don't. And signatures don't." She set the pointer down. "The registry contains the commission's minutes. Bound into the back, where no one would think to look unless they already knew. My grandfather's signature appears eleven times. Eleven approvals of engineered diversion, dated across fourteen months, each one increasing the volume the high channels could carry away from the lowlands—which is to say, each one increasing the volume that would arrive at the Quarter all at once, when the gates were opened, when the rain gave everyone their alibi."

The room was very quiet. Outside, wind worried the eaves of the lodge, a sound like fingers testing locks.

"They drowned a valley," said young Tomas Marchetti, the younger cousin, who still had the misfortune of a conscience. "They drowned—"

"They drowned nothing," Dagny said, and her voice did not rise, which was how everyone in the room knew to be afraid. "There was no nation called Vhaloria. There is no nation called Vhaloria. There was a district with irregular borders, an unproductive population, and a hydrological accident waiting to happen. Our founders saw the accident coming and positioned themselves to survive it. That is not murder. That is foresight. It is the reason any of you are sitting in this room instead of pulling nets out of a river somewhere."

"And if the registry says otherwise?" Vess asked mildly.

"Then the registry is a document that must never be read aloud in a room with lawyers in it." She looked around the table, meeting each pair of eyes in turn. "I'm not asking you to approve anything illegal. I'm telling you what's already in motion, because you'd hear it within the week anyway, and I'd rather you heard it from me. The chest surfaced. A storm chaser pulled it out of the river—a drifter named Ashgrove, heavily leveraged, no family, no attachments, exactly the kind of man who can be bought or buried. Sela Voss has him located. Retrieval is underway."

"And the other copies?" Vess again. He was the only one who ever asked the second question, and Dagny both despised and privately honored him for it. "Chests don't travel alone. Registries get copied. Who else knows?"

"That is being handled." She paused. "There is a counter-archive. There has always been a counter-archive—we burned one station of it nineteen years ago and congratulated ourselves too thoroughly, because a woman named Odile Hark rebuilt it in the high country, above the tree line, in a weather station that doesn't appear on any survey we fund. Hark is old. Older than the flood. She's been collecting names and deeds and testimony for four decades, and everything she has corroborates everything in that chest."

"What do you want done about her?" Halloran asked.

Dagny looked at the portrait above his shoulder—her grandfather, painted at fifty, at the height of his authority, his hands resting on a closed ledger. She had spent her childhood in this room, doing homework beneath that gaze, learning arithmetic from the backs of share certificates. She had loved him the way children love lighthouses: absolutely, and without ever questioning what the light was for.

"What I want," she said, "is for winter to come early this year. I want the passes watched from now until they close—I've retained the Corren's Ferry men, all of them, both teams. Nothing goes up the escarpment that we don't count first. And I want Hark's station visited before the snows. Thoroughly. With fire, if fire is required." She straightened. "Hark is dying anyway, by every report. Let her die warm."

Tomas Marchetti opened his mouth. Dagny looked at him, and he closed it.

"Are there questions?" she asked, knowing there were none that would be spoken.

There were none.

* * *

Afterward she dismissed them all except Vess, who remained in his chair by the fire, working a boiled sweet against his teeth, watching her with those clouded eyes that missed nothing.

"You lied to them," he said.

"I simplified."

"You told them retrieval was underway. You told them Ashgrove was located. Sela Voss lost him two days ago outside Corren's Ferry—he bought a horse and went upcountry with a mapmaker's chart, and she's chasing a ghost through foothills full of shepherd settlements that hate us." Vess sucked his sweet. "You also didn't tell them about the photograph."

Dagny went very still. It was a skill she had practiced since girlhood, stillness, and she resented that Vess could always see through it.

"What photograph?"

"The one my man found in the abandoned gear at Miller's Bend. A child. Vhalorian pin on the collar. Face you'd recognize anywhere, because it's the face of the man driving that chest toward the high country right now." Vess leaned forward, firelight pooling in the hollows of his face. "Ashgrove isn't a drifter, Dagny. Ashgrove is a returned heir who doesn't know it yet—or worse, one who's starting to. Which means this stopped being a retrieval the moment that boy looked at his own past in the mud. It's a homecoming. And homecomings can't be bought off with debt forgiveness."

Dagny crossed to the window. Below, the valley fell away in terraced shadows, and beyond it the escarpment stood in its autumn rags, the high snow already melting off the shoulders, already lying about how long the season would wait.

"Then we treat it as what it is," she said. "Burn Hark's station. Burn whatever Hark gives up before she dies. Seal the passes. And find me everything about a woman named Ashgrove who died in the lowlands twenty years ago—there will be a record, there's always a record, someone signed something, someone took payment, someone looked away." She turned back to Vess. "My grandfather erased a country with a pen and a rainstorm. I will not be the Kestrel who loses it to a chest and a horse thief."

Vess rose, joints cracking like green wood. At the door he paused. "Your grandfather slept badly the last ten years of his life. Did you know that? Walked this gallery every night, three in the morning, checking the locks on a country that no longer existed." He smiled without warmth. "Power's a debt too, girl. It comes due like anything else."

He left her alone with the portraits and the wind.

Dagny stood a long time at the window, watching the light fail on the escarpment, and told herself—as she had told the board, as she had told herself for forty years—that this was duty. That consolidation was stewardship. That a family did not survive by confessing. The words fit her the way her father's pointer fit her hand, worn smooth by use, shaped by hands that were not hers.

She almost believed it. She had learned to live in the almost, the way her grandfather had lived in it, walking the gallery at three in the morning.

Then she rang for the steward and gave the order in writing, because written orders could be burned, and burning things was, after all, the family business.

By dawn, riders were moving toward the high country, and the first snow of the season was falling on a weather station above the tree line where an old woman was still awake, cataloguing names by lamplight, unaware—or perhaps, Dagny thought later, aware in the way of the dying, who stop fearing the inevitable and start simply noting it—that the fire was already on its way up the mountain.
