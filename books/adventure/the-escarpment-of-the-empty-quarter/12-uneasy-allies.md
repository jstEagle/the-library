## 12. Uneasy Allies

The smoke still hung over the station like a held breath.

August came around the last bend of the switchback with his hands raised before he saw her—because he had seen the truck first, parked in the lee of the burned archive's western wall, and because a man who had spent three days being hunted learned to raise his hands early and ask questions later. The fire had eaten through the roof of Odile Hark's station and collapsed part of the eastern wall into its own cellar, and out of that ruin rose a gray column that bent eastward on the wind, pointing down-mountain toward Corren's Ferry, toward everything he was running from.

She stood at the edge of the ash field with a pistol held low and professional, her left arm cradled against her ribs, and even at forty paces August could see the state of her: mud to the knee, a bandage at her temple gone rust-brown at the edge, a graze along her jaw that she had cleaned but not closed. Sela Voss looked like a woman who had been audited by something worse than an accountant.

"You're limping," she said.

"You're bleeding."

"We're both very observant." She didn't raise the gun, but she didn't lower it either. "Where's the chest?"

"Where it can't be found by people who ask questions like that."

"August." His name in her mouth sounded different than it had in Corren's Ferry. There it had been a label on a file. Here, wind-scoured and tired, it sounded almost like an apology. "I've driven four hundred kilometers since yesterday morning. I killed a tire on the Kestrel Cut and walked the last nine miles. I have been shot at by my own employer's second team, which is a sentence I did not expect to say aloud this year, and I am standing in the ashes of a woman who may be dead because of the organization that signs my paychecks—or signed them, past tense, as of about six hours ago when I decided they were trying to kill me too." She let the pistol drop to her side entirely. "So let's skip the part where we circle each other for an hour. You know what I was sent to do. I know what you're carrying. And neither of us is going to survive the next five days alone."

August didn't answer right away. He looked past her, at the ruin. Odile Hark was dead—he had held her while the light went out of her, had heard the last instructions delivered in a voice like paper tearing—and the satchel she'd saved lay strapped across his back under his coat, warm from his own body heat, as if it were something alive. Maybe it was. Documents were alive in exactly one way: they could be murdered, and they could outlive their murderers, and which happened depended entirely on who carried them.

"You flagged the ingot," he said. "In Corren's Ferry. That was you."

Something moved behind her eyes—not quite flinching. Recalculating. "Yes."

"So every mile since then is yours. The trucks in the foothills. The men in the canyon. Odile's station burning." He took a step forward, and the ash shifted under his boots with a sound like snow that hadn't decided to fall yet. "You want me to make an alliance with the person who started the hunt."

"I want you to make an alliance with the person who can end it," Sela said. "There's a difference, and the difference is that I'm offering it for free, which should tell you how desperate I am, because I have never in my life done anything for free."

* * *

Sela Voss had been trained to read rooms, and the room she was reading now was a man standing in front of a burned building with a dead woman's work strapped to his spine, deciding whether to trust her or put a bullet in the ash at her feet and walk away into weather she couldn't follow him into.

He wouldn't shoot her. She was almost certain. He had the look of a man who had never shot anything that wasn't a storm—someone whose violence was all patience, all waiting, all reading the sky. It made him predictable in one way and completely unpredictable in another, and she hated that combination, because it was her own combination, and she knew how little it could be trusted.

"Kestrel has three routes up," she said, when the silence had gone on long enough to become its own kind of negotiation. "The official pass at Veymar Gate—they'll seal it tomorrow with a toll checkpoint dressed up as avalanche control. The maintenance road through the Kestrel Cut, which is named after the family, which tells you everything about who owns the mountain. And the shepherd trails above Larkwater, which Dagny knows about because I told her about them two years ago, back when knowing things was my job instead of my death sentence." She ticked them off on her fingers, the gun hanging loose between her knuckles like an afterthought. "They'll watch all three. What they won't account for is timing. Dagny plans like an accountant. Everything in columns. She doesn't think in weather."

"And you do?"

"I think in whatever wins." She said it flatly, without pride, and watched him weigh it. Good. Let him see the whole ledger. A lie would have been easier to sell and fatal later. "Here are my terms. I get you to the summit chapel before the passes close. In exchange, when the truth comes out—if it comes out—I'm named in it. Not as Kestrel's agent. As the one who turned. I want my name on the right side of the record, August. That's all I want. I don't want money. I've seen what their money buys."

"The registry isn't a weapon I'm going to hand out to whoever helps carry it."

"I'm not asking for the registry. I'm asking to be *witnessed*." Her voice cracked on the last word, just slightly, and she covered it by turning to look at the burned station, giving him her profile and the graze along her jaw. "Do you know what I did for the Consortium for eleven years? I erased things. People, mostly. Not with guns—with paperwork. I made debts appear. I made histories disappear. I once unmade a village's water rights so thoroughly that the people who'd lived there for six generations ended up legally unable to prove they had ever been thirsty." She laughed, short and airless. "I was good at it. And then I opened your chest in a pawnshop office in Corren's Ferry and saw a land registry for a country that doesn't exist, and every parcel in it had names attached, and I thought: I have done this. This exact thing. Parcel by parcel. Name by name. I just never saw the whole map at once before."

August said nothing. The wind moved the smoke between them.

"Odile Hark," Sela said quietly. "Was she alive when you got here?"

"She died holding my hand. About twenty minutes after I reached her. The station was already burning." He paused. "She saved one satchel. She said the heart of the counter-archive is at the summit chapel. Six days before the snows seal it."

"Then we have six days, and Dagny knows about the chapel now too—the order went out from the lodge yesterday. Burn the station, watch the passes, and a third instruction I wasn't meant to hear." Sela turned back to face him fully. "They're bringing accelerants up the mountain, August. Not men. Chemicals. They intend to burn the chapel with everything in it, and anyone in it."

* * *

August walked the perimeter of the ruin while she talked, because walking helped him think, and because he wanted to check the truck she'd arrived in—a Consortium-issue high-clearance rig with the Kestrel stag stamped on the door, though someone had already taken a knife to the upholstery looking for trackers. He crouched at the wheel well, ran his fingers along the frame, found nothing but road grime and old wax. Either she'd already swept it or she was better than he was.

He thought about the canyon. Three days ago, mud to his hips, prying a photograph out of an abandoned pack—a boy of six or seven, squinting into sun, wearing a small enameled pin of a double-tower crest. Himself. He had carried that photograph against his chest ever since, next to the satchel, next to the chest's lockplate in his mind, and it had changed the arithmetic of everything. He was no longer carrying someone else's country. He was carrying his own, and he had only recently been informed of the ownership.

That was why he didn't walk away from her. Not because her offer was clean—it wasn't; nothing about Sela Voss was clean, including, probably, her motives—but because a man alone on a mountain against an organization that burned archives and poisoned wells of memory was a dead man with good posture. And because there was one thing she'd said that rang true in a way he'd learned to trust the way he trusted barometers: *I want my name on the right side of the record.* He knew that hunger. He'd discovered it himself ten days ago, standing over a river with a sealed chest in his arms. It was the hunger of people who had spent their lives as entries in other people's ledgers.

"All right," he said, coming back to her. "Terms."

Her shoulders came down half an inch. She'd been braced for refusal. He filed that away—she had expected to lose, which meant she had run out of alternatives, which meant everything she'd said was true, or true enough.

"One: you don't touch the satchel or the chest. Ever. You guide, I carry."

"Agreed."

"Two: if we're cornered, we surrender the documents before we die for them. Dead men deliver nothing. Odile died for a satchel and it nearly burned with her. I'm not romanticizing that."

Sela studied him. "You'd give it up?"

"I'd give it up *to survive and take it back*. There's a difference. Dagny thinks in columns. She'll assume possession equals victory. Possession is just a variable. Weather is the constant." He nodded east, where the sky over the escarpment had begun to build—a high gray lid, pressure falling in a way he could feel in his teeth. "Three: we split nothing else. No stories, no pasts, no reasons. You don't ask me about the pin I keep in my pocket, and I don't ask you what you did for eleven years beyond what you've already told me. We start from today."

"That's not an alliance," Sela said. "That's a ceasefire."

"It's what I can afford." He looked at her steadily. "You flagged the ingot. Every bad thing since then traces back to that moment, and you know it, and I know it, and pretending otherwise would be the first lie between us, and I'd rather we run out of mountain than run out of honesty. So no, I don't trust you. But I watched you stand in front of a burned building and tell me the truth about yourself when lying would have been easier, and that's worth more than trust. Trust is a feeling. That was evidence."

For a long moment she didn't move. Then she holstered the pistol—an actual decision, made visibly, the strap snapped home with a click that sounded louder than it was—and extended her hand.

"Evidence," she repeated. "Fine. Then here's more. Dagny's second team ambushed me at the Kestrel Cut yesterday. Two men. One's dead—I didn't do it; he fell. The other reported in before I got away, which means Dagny knows I'm compromised, which means every Consortium asset on this mountain now has my description and orders regarding me that don't include arrest." She shook his hand once, hard, her grip colder than the altitude explained. "I'm not your ally because I'm good. I'm your ally because I'm out of options and you're the only option left. If you ever get a better offer than me, take it. I would."

"Noted," August said. "And for balance: I'm not trustworthy either. I lied to an entire valley of shepherds last week. They asked me to swear I'd carry the registry home, and I said yes while planning to decide later whether 'home' meant the chapel or a buyer. So we're even. Two liars with complementary skill sets."

"What are your skills, exactly? Besides weather."

"Waiting," August said. "Reading skies. Knowing when a system will break early and when it'll hold past all reason. Knowing which side of a ridge the wind will fail on. And driving badly maintained vehicles faster than they deserve."

Sela almost smiled. It looked unfamiliar on her face, like furniture in a house that had never had any. "And mine are maps, routes, logistics, and knowing how Dagny Kestrel thinks well enough to hate it accurately."

"Then we might actually make it."

"Don't say that out loud," she said sharply, and glanced at the sky as if the words themselves might summon something. Superstition, from a woman who dealt in documents. Or maybe not superstition. Maybe she'd simply been in the presence of the Consortium long enough to believe that hope, spoken aloud, created a paper trail.

* * *

They broke camp an hour before dusk, because August wanted the last light for the river crossing below the station and none of the dark for it. The Alder's source-fed tributaries ran high with the same melt that had given him the chest, and Sela watched him read the water—slope, debris line, the color of the foam—for a full minute before declaring a crossing point fifty meters upstream of the obvious one.

"Why there?"

"Because the obvious crossing is downstream of a cutbank that's going to let go sometime tonight," August said. "Probably around two in the morning. Probably taking half the trail with it." He waded in without waiting for agreement, the water shoving at his knees, and held out his hand for her pack.

She gave it to him. It cost her something visible—her whole body resisted the motion—and then she followed him into the current, gasping at the cold, and they came out the far side soaked to the waist and both pretending it was fine.

They climbed until full dark, then made a cold camp in a hollow of boulders with no fire, because Sela vetoed the fire and August agreed—the glow would carry three miles in this clear air, and somewhere below them, headlights were arranging themselves along the roads like beads on strings. He could feel the storm building behind the escarpment's shoulder, a vast slow pressure turning in the dark like something asleep that would wake hungry. Two days, maybe three, before it broke. After that, the high country would close like a book.

Sela sat with her back against a boulder, cleaning the graze on her jaw by feel, and spoke into the darkness without looking at him.

"When this is over—whatever 'over' means—what happens to you? The debt. The Consortium holds your paper. If we win, does the debt burn with them?"

August considered it. Above them the stars were hard and close, and the wind had begun its evening shift, swinging north-northwest, which meant moisture by morning.

"I honestly don't know," he said. "I used to think the debt was the worst thing about my life. Now I think it might be the most honest thing about it. Everything else was fog. The debt at least told the truth: that I owed somebody." He pulled his coat tighter. "Get some sleep. We cross the shoulder of the world day after tomorrow, ahead of the front, and I need you sharp enough to argue with me when I pick the route, because I'll be wrong sometimes, and I'd rather find out from you than from the mountain."

In the dark he heard her breathing slow, uneven at first, then deeper. But just before sleep took her, she said one more thing, so quietly he almost lost it to the wind:

"There's a supply cache at Larkwater. Consortium-marked. Radios, medical kit, and—" a pause, a breath, "—a list. Of everyone Dagny considers a loose end. I put myself on it when I copied it. My name's third."

August stared up at the wheeling stars and felt the shape of the next five days settle over him like weather coming in.

Third, he thought. Someone was first.
