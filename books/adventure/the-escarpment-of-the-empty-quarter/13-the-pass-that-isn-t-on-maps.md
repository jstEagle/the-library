## 13. The Pass That Isn't on Maps

The shepherd who led them to the trailhead was called Marta, and she was ninety if she was a day, and she walked the first mile of it faster than either of them could.

August had expected a guide. What he got instead was an old woman with a crook of blackthorn and a face like weathered slate, who stopped at a wall of scree that looked, to his eye, exactly like every other wall of scree in the valley, and said: "It's here. You just have to stop looking for a pass."

She pushed through what appeared to be solid rock—a seam no wider than her shoulders, hidden behind a leaning slab—and there it was: a corridor between two faces of stone, floored with old gravel and the droppings of animals that had used this route when smugglers were still called something politer.

"The Kestrels sealed the high road at dawn," Marta said. "Two trucks at the toll bridge, men on the switchbacks with binoculars. They think the mountain has three doors. It has eleven, and we have always kept one open." She pressed a folded square of oilcloth into August's hands. Inside was a strip of hide, marked with notches and burned dots—the route, drawn the way shepherds drew it, in the language of water and sheep. "My grandmother carried salt over this path during the last war. Nobody followed her then because nobody believed a person could go where a goat goes. Remember that. Be a goat."

"And if we meet anyone?" Sela asked.

Marta looked at her for a long moment—at the bandage on her arm, at the Consortium-issue boots she'd had the sense to swap for shepherd's felt—and said nothing at all, which was its own answer.

Then she turned around and walked back down into the valley, humming something old, and August understood that he had just been entrusted with more than a route. He'd been entrusted with a door that had stayed shut for forty years on the strength of nobody's believing in it.

* * *

They climbed through the morning in a country that did not want them.

The seam opened after half a mile into a hanging gully, steep as a staircase, choked with loose stone that shifted and sighed under their boots. Above them the walls narrowed to a slot of sky so blue it looked cut from paper. There was no trail—only cairns the size of a fist, placed at intervals that made sense to goats and grandmothers and, gradually, grudgingly, to August.

He had spent fifteen years reading terrain for a living. He knew how a slope held its weight, where a mountainside kept its secrets, which faces shed rock and which cradled it. But this was different. This was a route designed by people whose lives had depended on its invisibility, and every choice in it was a small act of defiance: the path crossed the gully nine times rather than following any single line; it hugged slabs where footprints wouldn't print; twice it went directly up waterfalls frozen into blue glass, using ledges no wider than a hand.

"Your shepherds built this," Sela said, somewhere below him. Her voice came thin off the stone.

"They didn't build it. They remembered it." He reached down and pulled her over a lip of granite. Her hand was cold and lighter than he expected, and she let herself be pulled, which from Sela Voss was practically a confession. "Forty years is four generations of sheep. The route outlived the reason for it. That's what your Consortium never understood about erasure—you can burn the paper, but the mountain keeps minutes too."

"Is that supposed to be comforting?"

"It's supposed to be true. Comfort's extra."

She almost smiled. He saw it happen and then watched her put it away, the way you'd pocket something valuable in a crowd. They had been traveling together for two days and they had developed a grammar: short sentences, silences that meant agreement, silences that meant the opposite. Neither of them had yet said anything that could be called trust. But they had stopped sleeping back-to-back with knives loose, and in the economy of fugitives, that was a treaty.

By noon they had come up under the shoulder of a ridge the map called nothing at all, and August called the first rest. They sat with their backs against warm stone and ate hard bread and sheep's cheese wrapped in cloth, and below them, far across the valley, they could see the official pass—the one on maps, the one with a road—where tiny figures moved in the sun. Consortium patrols, sealing the doors Dagny Kestrel believed were the only ones.

"They're watching a road," August said. "We're watching them watch it. There's a lesson in that I intend to enjoy."

"Don't enjoy it too loudly." Sela shaded her eyes. "How far to the top?"

"By Marta's marks—" he counted notches on the hide strip "—we cross the crest tomorrow midday, if the weather holds. And the weather will hold." He said it with the flat certainty that had once won him bar bets and lost him everything else. "High pressure sitting over the whole range like a lid. Two days of clean sky. Then it changes."

"You're sure?"

"I'm a storm chaser, Sela. Certainty is the only thing I've ever owned outright."

She looked at him sideways at that—at the joke with a debt inside it—but didn't press. Below them, the patrols crawled along the road like ants along a fence rail, guarding a gate while the wall stood open eleven other ways.

* * *

The trouble came in the afternoon, as trouble does, without announcement or apology.

The route left the gully and traversed a face of broken limestone—a long diagonal shelf above a drop that went down and down into shadow, the kind of ground where the mountain keeps its rubble banked like money it intends to spend later. August felt it before he saw it: a wrongness in the slope's voice, a hollowness underfoot, the particular silence of stone that has stopped being a mountain and started being a pile.

"Single file," he said quietly. "No talking. Step where I step."

Sela nodded. For all her city sharpness, she had mountain sense—he'd noticed it the day before, the way she read the ground without being taught—and she moved behind him now with her weight low and her eyes up, the way he'd told her: never look at your feet, look at the line.

They were halfway across the shelf when the mountain spoke.

It wasn't loud. That was the terrible thing. A sound like a knuckle cracking, somewhere above and to the right, and August's whole body understood it a full second before his mind did. He turned—not toward the sound but toward Sela—and shouted the only word that mattered.

"Down!"

The face let go.

It began as a whisper—a few stones ticking past them like thrown gravel—and became a roar in the space of a breath. The whole shelf above the traverse peeled away, tons of limestone deciding at once to become gravity, and August threw himself flat against the slope as the first of it went by overhead with a sound like the world tearing paper.

Sela hadn't gotten down fast enough.

He saw her in the corner of his eye—one instant upright, the next swept off her feet, not falling but *carried*, the slide taking her the way a river takes a branch, tumbling her toward the edge of the shelf where the drop began. She didn't scream. Later he would think about that: that she went silent, hands out, grabbing at stone that refused to be grabbed.

August did the arithmetic in half a heartbeat, the way he'd once done it in a truck with a tornado bearing down and no road out. Rope—twenty feet of it, coiled at his chest, anchored to nothing. Slope—forty degrees, running out. Time—none.

He dropped the coil, clipped it to the piton he'd hammered into a crack an hour earlier without knowing why—some animal instinct, some shepherd's ghost—and dove.

The edge of the slide caught him like a wave. Stone hammered his shoulders, his back, filled his collar, his boots, his teeth. He slid with it, arms out, blind in the dust, and then his hand closed on fabric—her jacket, the sleeve, the arm inside it—and he stopped himself the only way left, by wrapping the rope twice around his forearm and letting the mountain decide how much skin the toll would cost.

The rope snapped taut. The piton screamed in its crack. And August Ashgrove hung off the end of it, holding a woman who owed him nothing and whom he trusted less than the rope, while the rest of the mountainside went over the edge above them and fell for four hundred feet into the dark, roaring like a train, like a flood, like a nation being erased.

Then it was done. Dust everywhere, gold in the light, settling slow as snow. His forearm was flayed raw where the rope had bitten. Sela dangled below him, both his hands fisted in her jacket, her boots kicking air over the drop.

"Climb," he said. His voice came out sanded flat. "Don't talk. Climb."

She climbed. Hand over hand up his arms, up his body, using him as a ladder without ceremony or apology, and he braced against the slope and took her weight and thought, distantly, that this was the most honest transaction of his entire adult life: her life for his grip, paid in full, no ledger required.

When she crested the edge and rolled onto solid stone beside him, they lay side by side in the settling dust, breathing like bellows, and neither of them said anything for a long time.

Finally Sela laughed. It was a strange sound—cracked, involuntary, half shock—and she pressed the back of her wrist to her mouth to stop it and couldn't.

"That," she said, "was the stupidest thing I've ever seen anyone do."

"I've done stupider. I financed a storm rig through a consortium."

"No—I mean it. You had no anchor worth the name. You calculated it and it didn't work and you jumped anyway." She turned her head and looked at him, dust in her eyebrows, a scrape bleeding along her jaw. "Nobody jumps when the math fails. That's the first thing they teach you."

"Where? Consortium school?"

"Everywhere. My whole life, everywhere." She sat up slowly, wincing, and looked down at the chute of fresh rubble where the mountain had tried to spend them. "You jumped anyway."

August flexed his forearm. The rope burn ran from wrist to elbow, weeping through the dust, and he bound it with a strip of shirt without comment, because complaint was a currency neither of them dealt in. But something had changed in the space between them, and they both felt it settle, the way dust settles: not dramatically, not all at once, but completely.

Dependence, he thought. That was the first honest coin they'd ever exchanged. Not trust—trust was a story you told afterward. Dependence came first, raw and undeniable, the way a river comes before the map of it.

"Thank you," Sela said at last, and it clearly cost her more than the fall had.

"Don't thank me yet. We've still got a crest to cross and I've used up our good luck." He stood, tested his legs, and offered her his unflayed hand. "From here on, we're even. Whatever else happens on this mountain—that's settled. Agreed?"

She took his hand and let him pull her up, and for a moment she held on longer than balance required.

"Agreed," she said. "Whatever else happens."

* * *

They made the last pitch of the day as the light went long and copper, climbing out of the shattered gully onto a bench of turf beneath the crest itself, where the wind came over the top of the world smooth and cold and smelling of ice. August pitched the small tent in the lee of a boulder, and they ate, and the stars came out in numbers that seemed personal.

Below and behind them, far down in the valleys, lights moved. Patrol trucks on the roads, sweeping the official passes, sealing doors that had never been doors at all. From this height the Consortium looked very small—headlights crawling the dark like insects trapped under glass.

"They'll figure it out," Sela said quietly. She was sitting with her knees drawn up, watching the lights. "Dagny isn't stupid. Sooner or later someone tells her about the smugglers' routes. About Marta's grandmother. About eleven doors."

"Sooner. The shepherd guide they took—Kestrel has him. If he talks, she knows this route exists." August fed the fire another stick. "But knowing a door exists and finding it are different countries. That's the whole genius of it. You can't follow a route that's written in water and sheep."

"And if she finds it anyway?"

"Then we'd better be through it." He glanced at the sky, reading it out of habit—the clean cold, the high haze beginning to feather in from the west, thin as breath on glass. His stomach tightened, the old forecaster's knot. The high pressure was still holding. But lids lift. Lids always lift. "Weather changes tomorrow night. Maybe the day after. When it does, it'll change big—this kind of calm doesn't break, it shatters. We want to be over the crest and down the far side before it does."

Sela looked at him for a moment. "And if we're not?"

"Then the mountain decides. Same as today." He looked at his bandaged arm, at the chute of rubble catching the last red light. "Today it decided in our favor. I don't plan to give it a second vote."

She almost smiled again, and this time she didn't put it away.

They slept in shifts, as always. But when August woke at midnight for his watch, he found Sela already awake, sitting up in the dark with her hand resting lightly on his pack—on the chest's strap, where the registry rode against his spine—and she wasn't guarding it. He understood suddenly that she was touching it the way you touch a door you're afraid of, and he lay back down and said nothing, and let her keep her secret the way she'd let him keep his.

Above them the crest stood black against the stars, one ridge of stone between them and everything that waited on the far side. Tomorrow, midday, they'd cross it.

And far to the west, over the edge of the world, the first thin clouds were already coming.
