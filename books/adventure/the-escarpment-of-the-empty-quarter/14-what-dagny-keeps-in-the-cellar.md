## 14. What Dagny Keeps in the Cellar

The cellar of the Kestrel lodge had been built to hold wine, and for the first thirty years of Dagny's life it had done exactly that. Her father had kept his vintages in ordered rows down there—Corvenne reds, pale mountain whites from the northern terraces—and he had taught her, when she was small enough that the racks rose over her head like a city, how to read a label by candlelight. *Year. Region. Provenance.* A bottle was a promise about where something came from.

Now the racks stood empty, and the room held a folding table, two chairs, a hanging lamp, and a shepherd named Tomas Ferreol who had been brought down from the high valley with his wrists bound and his dignity, so far, intact.

Dagny noticed the dignity first. It was the thing she looked for in people—the load-bearing wall of them. Most men, after two days in a truck bed and a night in a cold room, gave it up somewhere around dawn. This one had not. He sat with the particular stillness of a man who has spent his life watching weather come over ridgelines and knows that most of what looks like catastrophe is only passing through.

"You're the one they call the optimist," he said. His Common was accented but unhurried, each word set down like a stone on a cairn.

"I'm the one they call worse things," Dagny said. She took the second chair herself. No table between them; she'd learned long ago that furniture was where interrogations went to die. "You were guiding a man up the escarpment. August Ashgrove."

The shepherd said nothing. The lamp hissed.

"Let me tell you what I already know, and you can spend your silence on the parts I don't." She laid it out flatly, without theater: the route through the smugglers' pass above the valley of the Ferreol flocks; the burned station at Hark's archive; the chest pulled from the Alder River with Vhaloria's crest stamped into its lead seal. "I know he carries the Quarter's land registry. I know he means to take it to the summit chapel. What I want from you is smaller than all that. I want to know what else is in the chest besides names and parcels."

"And if I don't know?"

"Then you'll say so, and we'll both have had an honest hour."

Tomas Ferreol studied her the way she imagined he studied a sky going wrong—patiently, looking for the shape underneath the color. "You are Dagny Kestrel," he said at last. Not a question.

"Yes."

"Then there is something else in the chest that concerns you more than it concerns me." He shifted, and the chair creaked under him. "There is a timing ledger. The registry records who owned the land of the Quarter. The ledger records *when* the water came. Day by day, gate by gate. Which sluices were opened, in what order, against what rainfall. It is the kind of book that can only be written by the men who planned it."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere above them, floorboards ticked as the lodge cooled toward evening.

"The flood was forty years ago," Dagny said. "The official finding was a dam failure. Catastrophic, unforeseeable, mourned by everyone including the men who built the dam."

"Yes," the shepherd agreed mildly. "And the ledger says otherwise. That is why your trucks are on every road below the snowline, madam. If it were only a list of drowned farms, you would not need so many guns."

Dagny kept her face level, which was the discipline her whole life had been built on. Inside, something moved—not fear exactly, not yet. More like the sensation of standing on a frozen lake and hearing, far underfoot, a single long crack travel outward.

*Gate by gate. Sluice by sluice.*

She had grown up on the story of the Quarter Flood the way other children grew up on lullabies. Her father, Aldous Kestrel, had told it at dinner tables with a glass in his hand and grief worn like a good coat: the tragedy of the eastern ranges, the poor drowned nation, the terrible failure of engineering that no one could have foreseen. He had donated to the memorial fund. He had a plaque. Dagny had been nine years old when she'd first traced the engraved letters—ALDOUS KESTREL, IN MEMORY OF THE LOST—with her finger, and felt the warm pride of being related to a man who mourned beautifully.

"Who signed the order?" she asked. Her voice came out steady. She was almost proud of it.

The shepherd looked at her for a long moment, and in that moment Dagny understood that he had known the answer before he sat down in this cellar—that he had carried it up the mountain and back down again specifically to place it in front of her, the way you'd lay a dead bird on a doorstep as a message.

"The order authorizing the sluice sequence," he said, "is countersigned at the bottom. The hydrological engineers drew up the plan. The Consortium's land syndicate financed it. But an order is nothing until a man puts his name beneath it and makes it real." He paused, and gave her the courtesy, or the cruelty, of saying it plainly. "It is signed Aldous Kestrel. Your father did not mourn the Quarter, madam. He emptied it. The memorial fund bought the land afterward for eleven percent of its value. That is what the ledger proves. That is what Ashgrove is carrying up my mountain."

* * *

Afterward she did not remember climbing the stairs. She remembered the corridor instead—the long pine-paneled hallway of the lodge, hung with the photographs of Consortium chairmen going back four generations, her own father's portrait third from the stairs, and her boots making no sound on the runner because she had learned to walk quietly in this house before she'd learned anything else.

In her rooms she poured a drink she didn't touch and stood at the window while the last light went off the escarpment. The mountain stood black against a bruised sky, and somewhere up there, past the burned station, past the hidden pass, a debt-ridden storm chaser was carrying her family's signature toward a chapel at the top of the world.

She made herself think it through the way she thought through everything: as a ledger.

Asset: the registry, in enemy hands. Liability: the same. The arithmetic was simple enough that a child could do it. Burn the documents, bury the courier, close the passes, and the Consortium's founding remained a story about tragedy and generosity. Fail, and everything her grandfather had built—everything her father had *signed for*—became evidence.

Simple. Except that her hands were not steady, and Dagny Kestrel's hands were always steady, and she knew the difference between fear of failure and this other thing, this colder thing, which was the sound of a crack traveling under the ice of forty years.

Her father had taught her ledgers too. That had been his real inheritance, more than the vineyard, more than the lodge: *every entry must balance*. He used to say it with his hand flat on the account books, and young Dagny had taken it as wisdom. Now she heard it differently. Every entry must balance. Somewhere in the world's great book, the Quarter's eleven thousand souls stood on one side of the column, and on the other side stood the Kestrel fortune, and the two lines had never once been allowed to meet.

She had spent her life believing she was the heir to the mourning. She was the heir to the sluice gates.

Down in the cellar, the shepherd was waiting.

* * *

She went back down at midnight, alone, having dismissed the two men who usually stood at the door. This was noted—she saw it noted—in the way the guards exchanged a glance. Let them note it. Fear traveled faster than orders anyway.

Tomas Ferreol had not slept. He sat exactly where she'd left him, and when she entered he watched her cross the room, and whatever he saw in her face made him nod slightly, as if confirming a forecast.

"You've read it," he said. "Or you didn't need to. You knew."

"I suspected my father's charity was tax-efficient," Dagny said. "I did not know he'd flooded a nation to go bargain-hunting in the mud." She set the lamp on the table between them, and for the first time since the interrogation began, she sat down across from the shepherd rather than beside him. "Tell me about the chapel."

"No."

"It isn't a request I'll repeat."

"And it isn't an answer I'll trade," Ferreol said. "You think I'm afraid of you, madam. I buried three of my cousins' children in the spring melt when I was younger than your lieutenants. The mountain takes what it takes. You are only weather." He folded his bound hands in his lap. "But I will tell you something free, because you came down here alone at midnight and asked your question like a person instead of a company. Ask yourself what happens to you if Ashgrove reaches the chapel. Not what happens to the Consortium. To *you*. Because when the reading comes—and it will come, whether this winter or another—the tribunal will not stop at your father's name. They will ask who protected the signature for forty years. Who burned the archives. Who hunted the couriers." He tilted his head. "They will ask it of the daughter who doubled the bounty."

Dagny went very still. "I haven't doubled any bounty."

"Not yet," the shepherd said. "But you will tonight. I can see it arriving in your face like a front."

* * *

She burned the testimony at four in the morning, in the iron stove in her study, alone.

It was not the confession—Ferreol had given her nothing on paper, nothing she could have burned that mattered. It was the transcript her stenographer had made of the session: six pages of a shepherd's calm voice describing sluice gates opened in sequence against a clear forecast, describing the timing ledger, describing her father's signature. She fed the pages in one at a time and watched each one curl and go to orange and then to nothing, and she understood, with a clarity that felt almost like peace, that she was not destroying evidence.

She was completing a circuit.

Her father had signed the order and built the memorial. Her grandfather had financed the syndicate and endowed the museum wing that displayed, behind glass, a Vhalorian loom and a child's coat recovered from the mud. Forty years of Kestrels had tended the erasure the way you tend an orchard—pruning, grafting, burning off the blight. And now she stood at the stove doing the season's work, and the fruit of it would be another quiet year, and then another, until someone like August Ashgrove came pulling chests out of rivers again.

The last page caught. In its light she saw her own reflection faintly in the study window, superimposed on the black mass of the escarpment, and for one unguarded second she could not tell which of the two shapes was hers.

Then the page was ash, and the window showed only the mountain, and she was Dagny Kestrel again, which was the only self she had ever been permitted to keep.

She wrote the order before dawn. *Bounty doubled. Fifty thousand for the courier, twenty-five for the woman Voss, alive preferred but not required. All passes watched. The chapel at the summit is to be surveyed for burning—materials, wind conditions, deniability.* She hesitated over that last word and struck it out. Deniability was for people whose ledgers still balanced. She wrote instead: *No witnesses either direction.*

Her aide collected it at six, and she watched from the window as he crossed the frost-white yard to the radio hut, and she thought: *this is how it must have looked from the lodge windows forty years ago. A man walking across a yard with a piece of paper, and a nation dying at the end of it.*

* * *

At seven, Sela Voss reported in by field line from the foothill relay—a thin, static-ridden connection that Dagny had insisted on rather than radio, because paper and wire left fewer copies than the airwaves did.

"Status," Dagny said.

"He's past the burned station. Moving well—faster than he should be, given the season. He's got weather sense I've never seen outside of professionals twice his age." A pause on the line, filled with crackle. "He also has company he doesn't know about. My second team lost me two days ago. I found their cache site stripped. Someone's running parallel to us, and it isn't mine."

Dagny closed her eyes briefly. So the board had sent their own. Of course they had. When a family's whole fortune sat at the bottom of one signature, no one trusted anyone else's hands. "And your assessment of Voss?"

Another pause, longer. Static hissed like water over stones.

"My assessment," Sela said carefully, "is that the mountain is full of people learning what they're actually carrying."

"That wasn't the question."

"No," Sela agreed. "It wasn't. But it's the answer. He'll reach the chapel, Dagny. You can double every bounty on the books and it won't matter, because the snows close in six days, and after that the only thing that gets up there is the truth."

Dagny looked out at the escarpment, white-capped now under the first hard light, beautiful in the way that only irreversible things are beautiful.

"Six days," she said. "Then I'm coming up the mountain myself."

The line crackled. Far away, at the relay station, she heard Sela Voss draw breath to argue, and Dagny cut the connection before the argument could form, because she already knew every version of it, and none of them changed the arithmetic.

Every entry must balance.

She took her father's portrait down from the study wall—it had hung there since his death, her father in his memorial-fund years, silver-haired and sorrowful and magnificent—and she did not burn it. Burning was too clean. Instead she turned it to face the wall, the way you'd turn a chair away from a table when someone had died, and she left the room, and downstairs in the cellar Tomas Ferreol heard her boots pass overhead, steady and unhurried, heading for the garage where the mountain trucks were fueled.

Above the snowline, the first flakes had already begun to fall.
