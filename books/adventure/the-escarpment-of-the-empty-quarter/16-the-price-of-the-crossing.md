## 16. The Price of the Crossing

August knew they had made a mistake somewhere on the shoulder, though he could not have said where. Mistakes in weather were like that—innocent at the time, each one reasonable, each one survivable on its own, and then the mountain added them together and presented the sum all at once.

The wind had stopped screaming an hour ago, or perhaps it hadn't and his ears had simply stopped reporting. That was bad. He catalogued the damage the way he'd once catalogued hail cores: fingers—numb but answering; toes—he didn't want to check; face—a mask of tightness that might be frostnip or might be worse. Beside him, Sela moved by will alone now, her steps short and mechanical, her left arm pressed against her ribs where the graze had gone from angry to wrong. He could smell it when the wind gusted. Wounds had a smell when they turned, sweetish and rotten, like fruit going over in a closed room.

"Talk to me," he shouted, though shouting cost him breath he couldn't spare.

"I'm counting," she shouted back. "It keeps my feet honest."

"What are you counting?"

"Steps. I'm at four hundred and eleven since the cairn."

He looked back. The cairn was gone, swallowed by the white. They had come off the shoulder's exposed spine into the lee of a descending ridge, and the ridge was kind by escarpment standards, which meant it only wanted to kill them slowly. Below them, if Bram's map and Odile's directions and God's own mercy held, the high country opened into the valley country—the shepherd valleys, the counter-archive's doorstep, the last miles before the chapel.

Last miles. Everything was last miles now. The snows would seal the passes in days, and every step down was a step borrowed against that deadline.

"Four-twelve," Sela said.

* * *

Sela Voss had stopped feeling her hands somewhere above the cloud deck, and she understood, with the cold clarity that was the last gift of hypothermia, that this was what the end of a career felt like. Not a dramatic fall. Not a tribunal. Just a woman walking downhill in weather that did not care about her org chart, following a man she had hunted across half a mountain range because there was nowhere else left to follow.

The wound in her side had opinions now. It spoke in a low hot voice beneath the cold, and the voice was getting louder. She had dressed it twice with strips of shirt and snow-melt, and both times the dressing had come away stained the brown-yellow of infection rather than the red of healing. She knew enough field medicine to know what that meant, and she knew enough about herself to know she would not say it aloud. Not yet. Saying it aloud would make it a fact, and facts were things Dagny Kestrel taught you to control or destroy.

She thought about Dagny instead, because thinking about Dagny kept her angry, and anger was fuel. She thought about the lodge, the long table, the board members with their quiet voices and their quiet ledgers. She thought about the second team waiting at the burned station with silencers and no paperwork, and how the order for her own erasure had probably been typed on the same machine as the order for the archive's burning. She thought: *I was good at this. I was so good at this.* And the thought arrived without pride, which told her everything.

"Ashgrove." Her voice came out cracked. "I need to tell you something."

"Now?"

"It doesn't keep."

He stopped. The white world stopped with him. She saw him turn, saw the frost in his eyebrows, the raw skin around his eyes where his goggles had failed, and she thought absurdly that he looked like a man who had been arguing with God and losing slowly.

"When you pawned the ingot," she said. "In Corren's Ferry. You've been wondering how I found you so fast. A town full of strangers, and within a day I'm sitting across your table offering to buy your debt."

"You had informants."

"I had a flag. I set it myself." She made herself look at him. "The Consortium tracks precious metals through the border towns—assay houses, pawnshops, ferry brokers. Standard practice. But three weeks before you walked in, I put a standing watch on every assay house between the Alder and the escarpment. Not for you. For anything Vhalorian. Any seal, any stamp, any gold with the old mint marks. I'd been looking for the chest for two years, Ashgrove. Before you ever pulled it from the river."

The wind filled the silence. Somewhere below them, the mountain dropped away into cloud.

"You started the hunt," he said.

"I started the hunt." Her legs were shaking and she locked her knees. "Dagny didn't send me after you. I brought you to Dagny. Every truck behind us, every burned building, every dead shepherd guide—it traces back to a flag I planted in a pawnshop ledger because I wanted a promotion."

August said nothing. The nothing went on so long she began to count again, just to have something to do with her mouth.

* * *

August stood in the whiteout and let the fury come, because he was too tired and too frozen to do the decent thing and postpone it.

Two years. She had been fishing for the chest for two years, and he had been the hook that finally snagged it. All of it—the flight from Corren's Ferry, Bram's warning, the trucks in the foothills, the photograph of himself as a boy wearing a pin he now knew was his mother's, Odile dying in the ashes of her life's work—all of it had a first cause, and the first cause was a woman doing her job well.

"You lied to me at the burned station," he said.

"I omitted."

"You sat across from me and proposed an alliance and never said the words *this is my fault*."

"And if I had? Would you have left me on that mountainside?" She laughed, and the laugh tore into coughing. "You needed my routes. I needed your weather sense. It was a fair trade. Fair trades don't require confessions."

"No," he said. "They don't. But people do."

He turned and started walking. It wasn't noble. He wanted, very badly, to walk away from her entirely—to leave her standing in the snow with her rotting wound and her clean conscience, to let the mountain do what Kestrel's second team had tried to do. He imagined it for three full steps, and the imagining was warm, and then he looked at the slope ahead and the cloud below and thought about the arithmetic of survival, which did not care about anyone's feelings: one person alone in this cold, navigating by memory, was a corpse by nightfall. Two people watching each other was a chance.

So he kept walking, and after a moment he heard her boots crunching behind him, and he hated that the sound steadied him.

"Keep counting," he called back. "Out loud."

"Why?"

"Because if you stop counting, I'll know you've stopped being honest with yourself. And right now that's the instrument I trust most on this mountain."

Behind him, after a pause: "Four-thirteen."

* * *

They descended through the afternoon into a world that changed by inches. The wind fell away as the ridge bent around them, and the cloud thinned from wool to gauze, and suddenly there were colors again—grey stone, blue shadow, the green-black spears of larch far below—and August realized he had been half-blind for hours and had not noticed, the way you don't notice a headache ending until it's over.

The high country opened beneath them like a hand unclenching. Long hanging valleys, terraced slopes, a river braiding silver through the valley floor. Smoke rose from three points along the valley—shepherd fires, thin and domestic. Somewhere past those fires, up the far valley, the summit chapel waited with its candles and its witness roll and Brother Callow, who asked only that the truth be witnessed.

Sela stumbled. He caught her arm and felt the heat coming off her through two layers of sleeve.

"How long has it been bad?" he asked.

"Since the canyon. Maybe before."

"You should have said."

"And you should have left me at the burned station." She smiled, and it was the first real smile he'd seen from her, crooked and exhausted and stripped of professionalism. "We're even in our stupidities. Let's call it a ledger and move on."

He almost laughed. Almost. Instead he got her moving again, slower now, angling toward the nearest smoke, because a septic wound needed more than willpower and he knew it, and she knew he knew it, and neither of them said it.

* * *

They reached the shepherd camp at dusk, when the sky above the eastern peaks had gone the color of a bruise healing. Dogs announced them from two hundred yards, and by the time they staggered into the firelight there were a dozen figures waiting, wrapped in wool, faces brown and lined, and August watched their eyes go from his frost-burned face to the chest strapped to his pack frame to the seal stamped on its lid, and he watched recognition move through the group like wind through barley.

An old woman took charge without asking anyone's permission. She was small and iron-backed, and she put Sela on a bench by the fire and cut away the dressing with a knife that had clearly done this work before, and whatever she saw made her mouth go flat.

"Fever's in the blood," she said, in accented but fluent trade-tongue. "You'll drink this and hate it, and tomorrow we open the wound properly. If the fever breaks in two days, you live. If it climbs, you don't. Do you understand me, Consortium woman?"

Sela, grey-lipped, managed: "You know what I am?"

"We know what everyone is on this mountain. We've had forty years of practice knowing who people are before they say." The old woman looked at August then, at the chest, at the seal, and her voice gentled into something that frightened him more than the fever did. "And we know what you're carrying, son. Rest tonight. Tomorrow you'll tell us whether you mean to carry it home."

They gave them a hut, mutton broth, blankets that smelled of lanolin and smoke. August sat by the fire long after Sela slept, turning his hands toward the heat, wincing as feeling returned like needles. Through the wall he could hear the shepherds talking low in a language he did not know and yet—there was a rhythm in it, a cadence, that snagged on something deep and unreachable, the way a song can snag on a childhood.

He looked at his hands. Red, blistered at the fingertips, but his. Hands that had pulled a chest from a river. Hands that had signed promissory notes. Hands that, in a few days, God willing, would sign a witness roll under a name he was still learning to pronounce in his own head.

*Augustin Ashgrove of Vhaloria.*

Inside the hut, Sela stirred and murmured something. He went to her. Her forehead was hot, but the old woman's draught had her sleeping deep, and in sleep her face lost its competence entirely—she looked young, and tired, and human.

"You started this," he said quietly, to the sleeping woman, to the fire, to himself. "Every mile. Every grave."

But debt was a thing he understood better than most men, and he had learned the hard lesson of it in a hundred dingy offices: a debt forgiven is not a debt forgotten. Forgiveness doesn't erase the ledger. It just moves the entry to a different page—one you carry yourself, voluntarily, forever. He would carry hers. She would carry his. That was what an alliance actually was, underneath the routes and the weather sense. Two people agreeing to hold each other's pages.

Outside, the snows had begun in earnest—not the storm's violence but the patient seasonal fall, the kind that seals passes and ends arguments. He listened to it hiss against the roof and thought of the chapel, six days' climb, five now maybe, with Sela's fever to wait out or not.

Two days, the old woman had said. If the fever breaks.

August banked the fire, lay down beside his enemy-turned-ally, and did not sleep so much as fall briefly unconscious, and in the grey hour before dawn he woke to find Sela's hand gripping his wrist—not delirium, he realized; she was awake, her eyes open and clear and terrified.

"The fever's climbing," she whispered. "Ashgrove—I need you to promise me something. If it breaks wrong—if I start talking nonsense—you take the chest and you go. Don't wait for me. Don't bury me. Go."

He looked at her for a long moment, at the sweat shining on her brow in the firelight, at the woman who had started the hunt and was now begging him to finish it without her.

"No," he said.
