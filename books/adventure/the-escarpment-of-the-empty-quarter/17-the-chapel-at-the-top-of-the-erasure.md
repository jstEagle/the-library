## 17. The Chapel at the Top of the Erasure

The last hour of the climb, August stopped measuring in miles and started measuring in breaths.

The air above nine thousand feet came thin and metallic, like sucking on a coin. Sela leaned on his arm more than she'd admit, her fever broken sometime in the night but leaving her hollowed out, walking on borrowed strength. The frostbite had taken the tips of two fingers on his left hand—white as candle wax, numb past feeling—and he'd stopped looking at them because looking made them real. Behind and below them, the shoulder of the world they'd crossed lay under a fresh skin of cloud, and somewhere down in that whiteout were men with orders to burn what they were climbing toward.

"Tell me again," Sela said, when they paused against a wind-scoured boulder. Her voice was gravel. "Why we didn't shelter."

"Because sheltering was losing." He said it flatly, without pride. It hadn't felt like courage up there. It had felt like arithmetic: two days lost against six days remaining, and the snows coming down like a closing door. "Odile told me the chapel seals itself in. Brother Callow doesn't come down for anyone once the drifts stack."

"And if we'd died up there?"

August looked at her. Her face was burned brown by wind, cracked at the lips, one eye still swollen from the rockslide, and she was watching him with the level patience of someone who had decided that fear was a luxury she could no longer afford.

"Then we'd have died running toward something," he said. "I spent forty years running away from things. I wanted to know the difference before it killed me."

She almost smiled. It cost her something—her split lip reopened—and she wiped it with the back of her glove. "Optimist," she said, using the word like an insult and a compliment at once.

They climbed on.

* * *

The escarpment's summit did not look like a summit. It looked like the edge of everything—a long, wind-planed shelf of gray stone where the trees gave up entirely and the sky pressed down close enough to touch. The wind here had been blowing since before there were people; it moved in long combed strokes across the plateau, herding the snow into waves and ridges like water learning its trade. And at the far end of the shelf, half-swallowed by the last ridge of rock, stood the chapel.

It was smaller than August had imagined. Odile had described it in her dying rasp—the old station, the archive of the counter-record—and he had built it in his mind as something grand, a cathedral of stone and defiance. What stood before him instead was a low building of dry-laid granite, roofed in slate so weathered it had gone the color of the sky, with a single bell tower no taller than a man and a half. There was no cross on it. There was no crest either. There was nothing on it at all—no mark, no name, no claim—which August now understood was the oldest kind of marking there was. The chapel declared itself by refusing to declare anything. Only a nation that had been erased would learn to build like this: invisible on purpose, humble as camouflage, patient as stone.

A thread of smoke rose from the chimney, straight up in the dead-still cold, a gray pencil line drawn against the pale sky.

"Somebody's home," Sela breathed.

"Or somebody's waiting," August said. His hand found the chest strap over his shoulder without his permission. The registry rode against his back inside its oilskin wrapping, and he had carried it so long now that its weight had become part of his posture, a second spine. He thought of the shepherd valley, of Marta pressing the seal into his palm, of the oath he had lied about taking and then, somewhere on the storm shoulder, had stopped lying about. *Carry it home.* He was carrying it home. He just hadn't known, until this moment, how much home weighed.

They crossed the last quarter mile bent double against the wind, and when August raised his fist to knock on a door of blackened oak, it opened before his knuckles landed.

* * *

Brother Callow was the oldest human being August had ever seen.

He was small—not stooped so much as condensed, the way stone condenses under weight until all that remains is the densest part of itself. His face was a topography of creases, his eyes pale and quick behind lids gone thin as paper, and his hands, emerging from sleeves of undyed wool, were spotted and knuckled and steady as a mason's. He looked at August for a long moment. Then at Sela. Then at the oilskin bundle on August's back.

"You're Hark's boy," he said. His voice was dry and light, like wind through a keyhole, but it carried.

"I'm—" August started, and found he had no answer ready. Not Ashgrove, not yet. Not Augustin, not quite. "She sent me. She's dead. They burned her station."

"I know." Callow stepped back from the doorway. "Smoke carries forty miles up here, if the wind is right. Come in before the wind changes its mind about you."

Inside, the chapel was one long room, low-beamed, lit by a fire and a dozen candles whose wax had dripped and hardened into stalactites along every ledge, so that the whole interior glittered faintly, like the inside of a geode. There were no pews. Instead, shelves ran the length of both walls—hundreds of them, floor to ceiling, and on the shelves, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with waxed cord, sat documents. Thousands upon thousands of them. Ledgers, folios, map tubes, deed boxes, each one labeled in a fine antique hand. The smell was tallow and cold stone and paper aged into something like incense.

Sela stopped just inside the door and stared. August watched her do it—watched the agent of the Kestrel Consortium take in the sheer physical mass of the record, the tonnage of witness stacked on these shelves—and saw something move through her face that he had only seen there once before, in the canyon mud, when she'd looked at the photograph of the child wearing the pin. It was the expression of a person discovering that the thing they had hunted professionally was, in fact, real.

"This is all—" she began.

"Vhaloria," Callow said. He said the word the way other men say a prayer or a curse, with total economy, no ornament. "Names. Deeds. Births. Deaths. Land grants going back four hundred years. The registry of the Quarter, complete, in three copies, of which you carry the fourth and final piece." He turned those pale eyes on August. "Hark kept the western annex. I keep the heart. Between us, between the shepherds, between whatever fragments survive in attics and graveyards and the memories of old women—the nation persists. Not on any map. In the record."

"The Consortium thinks they burned all of it," August said.

"The Consortium has been trying to burn all of it for forty years." Callow moved to the fire and fed it a split of wood with those impossibly steady hands. "They burned my predecessor's copy in the south. They burned Hark's station two days ago. They will burn this place too, if they find it, and they will burn me in it, and they will consider it a tidy afternoon's work." He looked up, and there was nothing self-pitying in the old face at all—if anything, there was a kind of dry amusement. "Which is why I am very glad, young man, that you brought what you brought. Sit down. Both of you. You look like two people who have been introduced to altitude the hard way."

* * *

While Sela slept by the fire—she went down almost instantly, mid-sentence, the fever's debt finally called in—Callow unwrapped the registry with the reverence of a surgeon and the speed of a customs inspector.

By candlelight, August watched the old man read.

Callow read the way August read weather. No wasted motion, no performance, just attention, total and continuous, his spotted fingers hovering over the columns of names, turning pages by their corners, occasionally making a small sound in his throat—not surprise, exactly. Recognition. When he reached the land registry proper, the dispossessed parcels of the flooded Quarter, he went slower. When he reached the appended order—the flood authorization, signed and countersigned—he stopped altogether.

His finger rested beside the signature at the bottom of the page. Aldous Kestrel. Countersigned by two ministers whose names had been struck from every atlas along with everything else.

"So," Callow said quietly. "It's true, then. We always believed it. Belief is cheap. Now it's witnessed." He looked up. "Do you know what the difference is, between believing a thing and witnessing it?"

"No."

"A belief can be argued with. A witness cannot. A witness is a fact wearing a face." He re-rolled the page with care. "For forty years, the men who profit from the Quarter Flood have relied on the fact that everyone who could testify is scattered, dead, or bought. Documents are harder to kill than people, but documents can be stolen, forged, dismissed. What they cannot survive"—he tapped the registry—"is being *read*. Publicly. In front of witnesses who then carry the reading outward. You cannot un-hear your own name in a list of the drowned."

August's throat was tight. "Odile said you'd want it. That you'd guard it."

"Oh, I'll guard it. I've guarded it for sixty years, since I was younger than you and twice as foolish." Callow set the registry down on a table of black oak that had probably been a ship's hatch in some earlier life. "But I want to be clear with you, boy, about what this place is, because Hark was better at sermons than I am and she's beyond giving them. This is not an arsenal. It is not a weapon. Everyone who comes up that mountain carrying a grievance wants it to be a weapon—something to point at the Kestrels, something to make them pay. And I understand it. God knows I understand it." His eyes flickered, briefly, to Sela sleeping by the fire—to the Consortium boots she wore, the Consortium training written in the way she'd scanned the room's sightlines before sitting down. "But vengeance is just another kind of erasure. You burn the men who burned us, and forty years from now someone climbs this mountain carrying *their* names, wanting *them* witnessed. The wheel goes round. The only thing that stops the wheel is the reading."

"The reading," August repeated.

"Witness, not war." Callow folded his hands. "The truth gets read aloud—all of it, every name, the signature included—and it gets witnessed by living people who then go out into the world and refuse to let it be buried again. Copies travel. Tribunals convene. Capitals squirm. That is how nations come back—not by the sword, which only ever produces new orphans, but by the ledger, read aloud, in daylight, by people who owe the dead nothing but their attention." He smiled then, a small dry crease of a smile. "You may tell me I'm an old fool. Everyone does. But I've outlived every man who ever told me so."

August sat with that. Outside, the wind had begun to rise again, moaning around the bell tower, and the candles shivered in their hundreds of hardened drippings. He thought about Dagny Kestrel coming up the mountain with men and kerosene. He thought about the avalanche he had already, in some chamber of himself he hadn't examined, begun planning. He thought about the difference between protecting a truth and weaponizing it, and whether the line would still be visible when the snow came down and the men with torches arrived.

"Callow," he said slowly. "If they come here—"

"They will come here."

"—what do you want me to do?"

The old man looked at him for a long time. The fire popped. Somewhere in the shelves, the building settled against the wind with a sound like a sigh.

"I want you to do what the weather does," Callow said at last. "Nothing cruel. Nothing personal. Simply irresistible." He rose, joints cracking like green wood, and took a candle to the far wall, to a shelf near the ceiling where a gap waited—an empty space, dust-ringed, sized precisely for one document box. It had been waiting, August understood, for sixty years. "Put it there. It's home."

And August lifted the chest's contents—the registry, the deeds, the drowned nation's paper bones—and slotted them into the gap, and heard, with a click too soft to be called a sound, forty years of exile end.

* * *

He woke once in the night, in a cot that smelled of cedar, and lay listening to the wind interrogate the chapel stone by stone.

The fire had burned to a red memory. In its glow he could see Callow at the far table, writing—quill, ink, a ledger open—recording, August realized, the arrival. Date, names, provenance. The witness roll. The old man wrote the way glaciers moved, unhurried, certain of arriving.

August got up quietly and crossed the cold floor and stood at the table's edge, and Callow, without looking up, turned the ledger toward him and laid down the quill.

"One more entry tonight," the old man said. "Yours."

August looked at the page. Above the blank line, in Callow's antique hand: *Witnessed and entered by:* And below it, the space where a name should go.

He thought of the pawnshop in Corren's Ferry. Of Sela Voss saying his name like a debt being called in. Of Bram the mapmaker tracing the crest with one cracked fingernail. Of Marta in the high valley, pressing the seal into his palm, believing in him more than he deserved. Of his mother's name in the registry, and his own birth record filed in a country that officially did not exist. Of the photograph in the canyon mud: a child wearing a pin, standing in front of a house that was underwater now, that had been underwater for forty years, that he had never remembered and could never forget again.

Every name he had ever used had belonged to somebody else's purpose. Ashgrove, the debtor. Ashgrove, the fugitive. Ashgrove, the heir the shepherds invented out of hope.

Callow watched him with those pale, patient eyes, and asked nothing, which was the whole of the old man's genius—he asked nothing, and so the answer had to come from the only place answers are real.

August picked up the quill.

He wrote: *Augustin Ashgrove, of Vhaloria.*

The ink dried in the candlelight, ordinary and permanent. Somewhere below, far down the dark flank of the mountain, he knew, lanterns were moving through the passes—Dagny Kestrel and her dwindling, doubting column of men, climbing toward them through the storm's dying edge. Two days, maybe less. The snows would seal the chapel behind them, and the world would come up the mountain looking for a fire to start.

Let it come, he thought. Let it climb. The names were home now, and he was home among them, and for the first time in his life, August Ashgrove was not running, and not hiding, and not for sale.

He set down the quill. Callow blotted the page, closed the ledger, and said, in his dry keyhole voice:

"Now we read them aloud."
