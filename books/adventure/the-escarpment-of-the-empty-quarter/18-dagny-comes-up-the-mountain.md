## 18. Dagny Comes Up the Mountain

The storm was dying the way old animals die—slowly, and with a great deal of noise.

Dagny Kestrel stood at the mouth of the last pass with her hands in her coat pockets and watched it happen. The wind had spent itself against the escarpment for two days, hurling everything it had at the rock, and now it was retreating in ragged gusts that came down the valley like men leaving a losing argument. Between the gusts there were silences, and the silences were worse. In them she could hear the mountain breathing—vast, indifferent, entirely unimpressed by what she had come to do.

Behind her, nine men were checking gear with the exaggerated care of people who did not want to be spoken to.

She counted them without turning her head. Nine. She had started from the lodge with fourteen.

The arithmetic of the mountain was simple and she had done it every morning since they'd left the tree line: two frostbitten fingers on Halloran's left hand, which he hid inside his mittens like a guilty secret; one man turned back at the second camp with his heart hammering arrhythmically in his ears; one more who simply sat down at dawn on the third day and would not get up, not out of mutiny but out of something quieter and more frightening—a kind of forgetting, as if the altitude had reached into him and removed whatever it was that made a man want to arrive anywhere. They had left him a tent and half the fuel. He had waved at them, cheerfully, like a man seeing off guests.

And then there was the fourth. The deserter proper. Pell, who had gone in the night with a week's rations and Dagny's own spare rope, leaving behind his rifle, his paybook, and a note that said only *I have a daughter*. Four words. She had read them eleven times by firelight and could not decide whether they were cowardice or the most honest sentence anyone had spoken to her in twenty years.

"Mrs. Kestrel." Voss's voice—no. Not Voss. Sela Voss was somewhere above her now, or dead, or both; the trackers had lost her trail in the whiteout below the shoulder of the world. This was Renk, her field captain, a broad, patient man who had served the Consortium since before her father died and whose loyalty she had always assumed was structural, like rebar in concrete. "We should move while the wind's down. Window closes by midday."

"I know when the window closes, Captain."

"Yes, ma'am."

He didn't flinch. That was the trouble with Renk—he never flinched, and so he never told her anything. She looked at him now, really looked, and saw what she had been refusing to see for three days: the set of his jaw, the way his eyes went up the pass and then, briefly, back down it. Men look back down a mountain only when they are measuring the cost of going forward.

"You have something to say," she said.

"No, ma'am."

"Dagny. We're past ma'am."

A pause long enough to have weather in it. Then: "The men are asking questions. Not loudly. But they're asking." He kept his gaze on the middle distance, professional to the end. "They know why we're here. I told them it was document recovery. Some of them believe me. The ones who don't—" He stopped himself, which was its own confession.

"The ones who don't are wondering what's in the chest," Dagny finished. "What's worth four men and a fortune in equipment. What their employer wants burned badly enough to climb into the teeth of a storm for it."

Renk said nothing, which was agreement.

* * *

They climbed through the storm's corpse. It was not climbing so much as wading—the snow lay in waist-deep shelves sculpted by the wind into forms that looked almost architectural, cornices curling over the trail like the lips of drowned statues. Dagny led from the front because she had always led from the front, because her father had taught her that a Kestrel never asked a man to stand where he would not stand first, and because—and this was the thought she kept in the locked room at the bottom of her mind, the one with the good lock—if she stopped moving forward, she would have to think.

Her father had signed the order.

She had known it for six days. Six days since the shepherd guide, since the interrogation that wasn't an interrogation because the man had simply *told* her, calmly, in his terrible accent, everything August Ashgrove carried in that chest—the registry, the land rolls, and the timing order, the engineering assessment, the deliberate opening of the sluices forty years ago, timed to the hour to drown the Quarter while its people slept. And at the bottom of the authorization, in ink that had faded to the color of old tea: *E. Kestrel.*

Her father had burned villages with a pen. Had called it hydrology. Had come home from those meetings smelling of pipe smoke and satisfaction, had taught her chess on winter evenings, had told her that empires were built by men willing to make the hard call while others hesitated. He had never once mentioned water.

She had burned the guide's testimony herself, page by page, in the lodge stove, and doubled the bounty, and told the board nothing except that the operation was proceeding. And every night since, in whatever shelter the mountain offered, she had lain awake listening to the wind and understanding, with a clarity that felt like frostbite creeping up a limb, that she had not burned the testimony to protect the Consortium.

She had burned it to protect him. To keep him whole. Because if the paper was true, then everything she was—every instinct, every ruthlessness she had polished and called duty—was inheritance. Was complicity wearing her face.

*You can still stop this,* said a voice in her head that sounded disgracefully like her mother's. *You can turn around. You can let the snow take the story.*

But then she saw her father's study, the leather chairs, the board of directors rising respectfully when she entered, the forty years of quiet dividends paid out of drowned farms—and she understood that she could no more turn around than a stone could refuse to fall. Whatever she did next, she already knew how it ended. A Kestrel finished things.

"Ma'am." Renk again, pointing with his ice axe. "Smoke."

Above them, perhaps two kilometers along the ridge, a thin gray thread rose from behind a spur of black rock and bent sideways in the wind. The chapel. Someone was keeping a fire. Someone was alive up there, warming themselves beside documents that could unmake everything her family had built since before she was born.

"Then we're close enough to smell them," Dagny said. "Move."

* * *

It happened at the col below the summit spur, an hour later, in the flat gray light that comes before a mountain decides something.

Halloran stopped.

Not dramatically—he didn't throw down his pack or shout anything. He just stopped walking, mid-step, and stood in the snow looking back the way they had come, and Dagny watched the desertion happen in real time the way you watch a door swing shut in slow motion.

"Halloran." Her voice came out level. She was proud of that. "Keep moving."

He turned. His face was wind-scoured raw, his eyes wet and frozen at the lashes, and when he spoke his voice was strange—apologetic, almost gentle, the voice of a man declining an invitation rather than ending a career. "I'm sorry, Mrs. Kestrel. I truly am. My grandfather came from down there." He nodded backward, down the mountain, down toward the lowlands, toward the drowned country none of them ever named. "My mother used to sing songs nobody knew the words to. I always thought she'd made them up." He shifted his rifle off his shoulder and laid it in the snow, carefully, stock aligned with the drift, like a man setting a table. "I don't know what's in that chest. But I know what we're doing about it. And my mother didn't teach me to burn things."

"Halloran—"

"You'll want to take my rations," he added, practical to the last, and unbuckled his pack and set it beside the rifle. "There's chocolate in the side pocket. The altitude does something to it, makes it taste strange, but it's food."

Then he turned and walked down. Not running. Walking, with the unhurried gait of a man who has finally put down something heavy, and within ten minutes the white had taken him and it was as though the mountain had simply opened a hand and accepted him.

Seven left. Dagny stood in the snow and did the arithmetic again and found that it frightened her less than it should have. That was the thing no one warned you about power—that after a certain point, loss stops feeling like loss and starts feeling like clarification. Seven men. Enough. Enough to burn a building. Enough to carry her name back down if it came to that.

Except that Renk hadn't moved, and neither had the other six, and they were all looking at her—not with mutiny, nothing so useful as mutiny—with a question. The same question, hanging in the frozen air between them like breath.

"Say it," Dagny said. "Whatever it is. Say it now, because past that col I won't ask twice."

Renk took off his glove, flexed his bare hand once against the cold, and put it back on. When he finally spoke, his voice was very quiet, pitched for her alone beneath the wind.

"My father worked the river district," he said. "Before. Before the flood. I was a child. I remember a house with blue shutters and a woman who gave me plums off her tree, and I remember that one autumn everyone we knew was gone, and my father said the water took them, and I believed him, because children believe their fathers." He looked up at her, and his eyes were steady and terrible. "I've been reading the men's faces for three days, Mrs. Kestrel. Half of them have grandmothers from down there too. You didn't hire a team. You hired descendants. And every step we climb, they're remembering."

The wind filled the silence that followed, shrieking over the col like something let out of a cage.

"So I'll ask you plainly," Renk said. "When we get up there. If it's true—all of it, what the chest says, what the papers say—do you want us to burn it? Or do you want us to *need* to burn it? Because those are different orders, and I'll follow either one. But I'd like to know which man you think you're giving them to."

Dagny looked at him for a long moment. Snow gathered on his shoulders and he did not brush it away. Behind him, the six stood in a ragged line, their faces indistinguishable behind ice-crusted balaclavas, and she understood suddenly that she could not tell anymore which of them were hers and which were merely present—which of them would strike a match and which would watch it gutter in the wind and feel relief.

An empire, she thought, is just a debt that hasn't been called in yet. Her father had understood that. Had built forty years on the assumption that the call would never come, that the drowned don't testify, that paper burns faster than memory. He had been right for forty years.

But the mountain had narrowed his empire to seven men and a matchbox, and up ahead, past the col, a thread of chimney smoke rose straight and patient into the clearing sky, and somewhere beyond it a dying archivist's successor was reading names aloud by candlelight to shepherds who had never forgotten a single one.

"Both orders," Dagny said at last, and heard her own voice as if it belonged to someone standing slightly behind her. "Burn it if it's there. And if any man's hands shake—" she looked down the line, meeting each masked face in turn, "—he can walk down with Halloran, and there will be no consequences, and his pay will be waiting, and I will personally see that no one follows him."

No one moved. No one walked down.

That, more than anything, was the answer, and it settled over her colder than the wind: they weren't staying because they were loyal. They were staying because they needed to see. After forty years, the heirs of the flood had climbed up to witness their own inheritance, and no order she gave could change what they intended to do with their eyes.

"Move out," she said.

They crossed the col in single file, seven shadows on the white shoulder of the world, and as they topped the rise the chapel came into view at last—a small dark shape hunched against the summit rocks, smoke rising from its stove vent, candlelight visible in a window no bigger than a hymnal. Figures moved behind the glass. One of them raised a hand—not in greeting, Dagny realized, but in warning, or welcome, or benediction; from this distance it was impossible to tell which.

And beside the chapel door, planted upright in the snowdrift like a standard, someone had fixed a pole bearing a flag she had not seen in forty years, since she was a girl flipping through her father's forbidden atlases: the black-and-gold crest of Vhaloria, snapping hard in the wind.

Below it, cut fresh into the snowfield in letters a meter deep, readable from the air, readable from God knew where:

**WE WERE HERE.**

Dagny stood at the top of the pass with her father's empire dwindling in her fists and understood, finally, that she had brought too few hands to do what hands were for—and that the men behind her had already begun, silently, one by one, to open them.
