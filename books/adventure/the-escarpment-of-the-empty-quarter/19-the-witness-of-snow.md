## 19. The Witness of Snow

The storm came up the escarpment like a debt collector.

August had watched it build for two days from the chapel's narrow windows—a bruise-colored wall stacking itself against the western sky, swallowing the peaks one by one, and he had read it the way he'd once read storms over the Alder flats: the pressure falling in his ears, the wind swinging from south to northwest with a shudder like something turning over in its sleep. A generational storm. The kind that buried whole herds and rewrote the shape of valleys. He had told Callow it would arrive by nightfall, and Callow had only nodded, as if weather were simply another name arriving to be entered in the ledger.

Now it was here, and so were they.

He heard them before he saw them—engines laboring somewhere below the tree line, muffled by the first fat flakes already hissing past the chapel stones. He stood at the window with Sela beside him, her shoulder pressed to his, her breath fogging the glass, and together they counted headlights through the snowfall. Four vehicles. Maybe five. Rope teams spilling out of them, moving with the unhurried competence of men who had been paid to climb mountains before.

"They brought enough rope for a funeral," Sela said.

"They brought enough rope for several," August said.

Below them, the switchback trail climbed out of the pass and curled along the shoulder of the mountain—the last three hundred meters of exposed ground between the treeline and the chapel door. There was no other way up. That was the whole point of a place like this: Brother Callow had told him, on his first evening, that the chapel had never needed walls taller than a man because the mountain itself was the wall, and the snow was the gatekeeper, and God handled the paperwork.

The first true snows were arriving six days early, and they were bringing Dagny Kestrel with them.

* * *

They had spent the morning preparing the way you prepare a house for a flood you cannot stop—moving what matters to high ground and making peace with the rest.

Callow had carried the counter-archive's heart up into the bell tower himself, refusing help, though he was ninety if he was a day and his hands shook like leaves in a hard rain. The registry pages went into a lead-lined chest older than any nation August could name, and the chest went behind the bell, and the bell went silent, its rope cut and coiled away. "If they take the chapel," the old man said, "they will find stone and an empty room and their own faces looking back at them from the dark. Let them wonder what they burned."

The shepherds—eight of them who had come up from the valley when word passed along the smugglers' routes that the hour had come—stacked firewood, melted snow, sharpened things that did not want sharpening. An old woman named Petra, who had sworn August's borrowed oath back to him in the valley weeks ago, cooked a last meal of barley and mutton fat and made everyone eat all of it, including Sela, whose septic arm had finally turned the corner toward healing but whose color still wasn't right.

And August had gone out onto the shoulder of the world with a satchel of storm charges and done the thing he knew how to do better than anything else on this earth: he had read the mountain.

The slope above the pass was a cornice waiting for permission. Forty years of wind-deposited snow, layered like the pages of a ledger, each stratum a different winter's promise—and beneath it, the steep grass-and-scree face falling away toward the trail in a long, clean sweep. Any avalanche man would have looked at it and said *unstable, avoid, do not travel*. But August Ashgrove had spent twenty years chasing storms because he'd never learned to avoid anything, and he had stood under that cornice in the building wind and felt the truth of it settle into him with terrible calm:

The mountain was loaded. It only needed a reason.

He set three charges. Not on the cornice itself—that would have been crude, wasteful, likely to bring the whole face down on the chapel too—but on the rib of rock below it, where the fracture line would propagate upward and outward, shearing the slab loose and sending it across the switchbacks like a hand wiping a slate clean. He wired them to a single firing box salvaged from his truck, ran the wire up through the rocks in a slack catenary that the snow would bury and protect, and coiled the firing box's leads inside the chapel's woodshed, where no one would think to look.

Sela had helped him without being asked. She'd held the spools, tamped the charges, learned the wiring in an afternoon because that was who she was—she absorbed systems the way other people absorbed gossip. When they finished, dusk coming down blue and cold, she'd stood beside him looking down at the pass and said quietly, "You've done this before. Set charges."

"Storm charges," he said. "For avalanches. To protect roads."

"And now?"

"Now I'm protecting a road too." He'd looked at her. "Just not one anyone will drive on again."

She hadn't laughed. She'd understood. That was the strange, fragile, unearned thing between them—they understood each other in shorthand, two people who had both spent their lives being useful to institutions that would burn them the moment usefulness ended.

Now the institutions had come up the mountain to finish the job.

* * *

Dagny Kestrel did not come herself. August saw that through the glass as the light failed—one figure apart from the others, standing at the treeline where the vehicles had stopped, small in a white parka, watching the chapel through field glasses while her men roped up. She had sent her hands up the mountain and kept her own feet on the ground, and somehow that was worse. Empires always worked that way. The signature traveled; the signer stayed warm.

"She'll wait," Sela said. "She wants the storm to do half the work. If her team takes the chapel, fine. If the storm buries us all, also fine. Either way there's nothing left to testify."

"Then we give her neither," August said.

The plan was simple the way all last plans are simple. The shepherds would hold the lower wall of the chapel yard—stones, rifles, and fury, enough to slow the breach, not stop it. Callow would stay in the tower with the archive. August would hold the woodshed and the firing box, and when the Consortium team committed to the final traverse—when every one of them was on the open slope below the cornice, past the point of retreat—he would bring the mountain down on them.

"And me?" Sela had asked.

"You're on the door."

"I'm what?"

"The door has to look defended. It has to look like the fight is *at* the chapel, or they'll hesitate on the traverse, and hesitation means they're spread out, means some of them live, means they come back tomorrow with more rope and less patience. You hold the doorway. Make them believe the chapel is worth dying over. And when I fire the charges—" he had stopped, because the next part of the sentence was the part he couldn't say.

"When you fire the charges," Sela had finished for him, levelly, "I run inside."

"You run inside."

"Avalanches don't care about doorways, August."

"No," he'd agreed. "But they care about stone. Get behind the doorframe, below the lintel, and the chapel takes the blast wave. The chapel is four hundred years old. It has outlived everything that ever tried to knock it down."

It had sounded true when he said it. Standing here now, watching the first ropes start up the switchbacks into the thickening snow, it sounded like the kind of thing a man tells himself so he can sleep before the end of the world.

* * *

They came up through the whiteout in file, eleven of them, roped at ten-meter intervals, and the storm swallowed them and gave them back in pieces—a helmet here, a rifle barrel there, ghost-shapes flickering between the gusts like figures on a broken film reel. The wind had risen to a scream. Snow no longer fell so much as flew sideways, and the temperature was dropping through the floor of what August's instruments, packed away in his truck a hundred miles and a lifetime ago, would have called survivable.

Petra's people opened fire when the lead rope reached the second switchback—not to kill, August thought, watching the muzzle flashes stutter in the gloom, but to herd. Every shot drove the climbers closer to the rock rib, tighter against the slope, faster. Fear is a shepherd too. Within minutes the Consortium team had abandoned caution entirely and was moving at a dead rush up the final traverse, bunched together, exactly where the mountain wanted them.

August crouched in the woodshed with the firing box in his hands and waited for the moment he'd know when he saw it.

Through the gap in the boards he watched them come. Eleven shapes. Ten meters of rope between each. The last man just leaving the shelter of the rocks, the first man forty meters short of the chapel wall. All of them on the open face. All of them under the cornice.

All of them owed.

His thumb found the firing stud. Twenty years of debt and running and namelessness funneled down into a piece of Bakelite the size of a playing card, and he found, to his own surprise, that his hand was steady. Not calm—steady is not calm, steady is what's left after calm has left—but steady. Whatever else he was, whatever name he'd signed or failed to sign, whatever Vhaloria had stamped on him before memory began, he was a man who understood weather, and this was the one forecast in his life he had the power to make come true.

He thought of Odile Hark dying in the ashes of her station. Of Bram's map, and the burned archive, and the photograph of a boy wearing a pin he didn't remember. Of his mother's name in the registry, filed among the dispossessed. Of every name Callow would read aloud tomorrow if tomorrow existed.

He pressed the stud.

* * *

The mountain answered.

There was no sound at first—or rather, there was every sound at once, a crack so deep and total that it arrived not through the ears but through the bones, through the floor, through the fillings in his teeth. Then the cornice let go, forty years of accumulated winter peeling off the ridge in a single vast white slab, and the sound caught up: a roar like the sky tearing lengthwise, rolling down the valley and echoing off the far peaks and coming back again, and again, diminishing but never quite stopping, as if the range itself had begun to shout and couldn't be persuaded to stop.

August was already moving. He dove from the woodshed across the chapel yard as the blast front hit—a wall of displaced air traveling ahead of the snow, fast as a freight train—and it picked him up like a leaf and threw him against the chapel's eastern wall hard enough to crack something in his ribs. The world went white. Not snowfall-white but annihilation-white, powder and wind and darkness all compressed into a single screaming brightness, and he crawled blind along the stones with his hands until he found the doorway, and the doorway found him, and he hauled himself inside as the avalanche's tongue swept past the chapel in a thunderous white river, close enough that the spray of it lashed the walls like surf.

Inside, the silence afterward was the loudest thing he had ever heard.

Dust sifted from the rafters. Somewhere a timber groaned and settled. Candle flames leaned and recovered. And the roar outside faded by degrees into a deep, ringing quiet, the particular quiet that follows an avalanche, when the air is scoured clean and even the wind seems embarrassed to speak.

"Sela." His voice came out cracked. "Sela!"

He got his feet under him, ribs shrieking, and stumbled into the nave. The shepherds were rising from cover, dazed, alive. Old Petra crossed herself with a hand that wouldn't quite obey. Callow's voice drifted down from the bell tower, thin and wondering: "The pass is closed. Son—the pass is closed."

"Sela!" August reached the doorway and the cold hit him like a fist.

The door stood open. Beyond it, the world had been remade. Where the switchbacks had been there was only a smooth, featureless plain of debris, thirty feet deep, stretching down the mountainside into the murk—no rocks, no trail, no rope teams, no bodies, nothing. The Consortium's eleven men were somewhere under all that white, and so was every vehicle at the treeline, and so, probably, was the road out of the pass for the rest of the winter. Dagny Kestrel's expedition had ceased to exist in the time it took to press a button.

And Sela Voss was not behind the doorframe.

He searched the chapel twice, calling her name until his throat tore, checking the tower, the cellar, the woodshed, the yard's lee corners where the blast wave might have dumped her. Nothing. Then he found it, half-buried in the drift that had piled against the outer threshold: her rifle, stock splintered, sling snapped clean.

She had never made it inside.

He stood in the doorway with the rifle in his hands and understood, with a clarity that felt like frostbite, what must have happened. The blast wave. The timing. She had been holding the door—making the fight look real, drawing the climbers' eyes, doing the job he'd given her—and when the mountain came down she had been a half-second too slow, or the wave had been a half-second too fast, and the whiteout had taken her the way it took everything else: completely, indifferently, without leaving so much as a footprint.

He was shouting her name into the roaring silence before he knew he was doing it, out into the settling powder, into the enormous blankness where a switchback used to be. His voice came back off the far wall of the valley, thinner each time, until it was nothing.

Behind him, in the candlelit nave, Brother Callow had opened the great book on the altar and was waiting, patient as stone, for the reading to begin.

August stood in the doorway a moment longer, snow gathering on his shoulders like a hand, and then he stepped back inside and barred the door, because the dead deserved witnesses and the living had work to do, and somewhere under thirty feet of settled snow, in the cold dark at the bottom of the new-made plain, Sela Voss was owed a reading of her own.

He walked to the altar. He picked up the pen.

Outside, the first true snows fell on the sealed pass, soft and endless, covering everything—except, Callow said, what had been truly witnessed.
