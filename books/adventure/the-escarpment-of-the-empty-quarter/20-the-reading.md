## 20. The Reading

The snow came down on the chapel like a benediction, thick and unhurried, as if the sky had finally decided to finish what it had started forty years ago.

August stood in the doorway a long time after the last of the shepherds came inside, watching the pass he had sealed. The avalanche had taken the mountainside with it—trees, scree, the whole white throat of the route Dagny Kestrel's men had climbed—and beneath that new glacier of silence lay whatever remained of them. He did not let himself look for long. Some arithmetic you do once and never check again.

Behind him, Brother Callow was lighting candles.

The old man moved through the chapel with the economy of someone who had performed this ritual ten thousand times, touching flame to wick until the whole interior glowed amber. It was not a large building—a single stone room, a vaulted ceiling blackened by centuries of smoke, an altar of unpolished granite—but the candles made it feel vast, made the shadows climb the walls like congregants taking their seats. Along the eastern wall ran shelves from floor to eave, and on those shelves sat the counter-archive: oilcloth-wrapped bundles, tin boxes, ledgers bound in sailcloth, forty years of a nation's memory carried up a mountain one armload at a time.

Sela's chair stood empty by the fire.

August had covered her with a blanket before dawn, when her hands had finally gone still and the frost on her eyelashes had stopped melting. Callow had said the words over her—the old Vhalorian words, half prayer and half inventory, *what was given, what was kept, what is owed*—and then the shepherds had carried her out into the churchyard of stones behind the chapel, where the ground was too hard to dig more than a hand's depth, and laid her beneath a cairn they built in under an hour, working with the wordless efficiency of people who had buried their dead in high places for generations.

He had not wept. There had been no time, and then no space, and now the grief sat in him like ballast, steady and useful, keeping him upright.

"Augustin."

Callow's voice. Not August. Not Ashgrove. *Augustin*—the name from the registry, the name his mother had written in a child's careful hand on a birth record filed in a country that officially did not exist. The old man beckoned him from the altar, where a table had been cleared and draped in white cloth gone grey at the folds.

"It's time," Callow said. "The snow will hold them. It won't hold us forever. We read tonight."

* * *

They gathered by candlelight: eleven shepherds and one brother and one man who had spent forty years being no one in particular. August recognized faces from the valley—Marta Vey with her burn-scarred forearms; the twins, Ilo and Penna, who had guided him through the smugglers' pass; old Tomas Hark, Odile's distant cousin, who had come up the mountain two days ago carrying news of her death and had refused to leave since. They arranged themselves along the benches without ceremony, the way families sit, and Callow opened the first bundle on the altar table with fingers that trembled not from age but from something closer to reverence.

"The rule," Callow said, "is older than I am. My predecessor taught it to me the night he gave me these keys. A record that is only kept is a relic. A record that is read aloud is a witness. We do not read to accuse. We read so that the names exist again in the air, in the mouths of the living. Whatever anyone does afterward—with tribunals, with capitals, with vengeance—that is theirs. What happens in this room is only truth, spoken slowly."

He looked at August. "You brought the registry through the storm. By right and by custom, you read first."

August's throat closed. He shook his head. "I don't—I can't. My voice isn't—" He stopped, started again. "I'm not the one who kept it alive. Forty years, Brother. You and Odile and whoever came before you, carrying paper up a mountain while the world pretended none of this happened. You read. I'll witness."

Something moved across Callow's face—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would live in a younger man. "Odile said you'd say that," he murmured. "She said, *the boy will be stubborn about humility, it's the Ashgrove in him.*" He paused. "She knew your mother, you know. Ilse Ashgrove came through here in the spring of the flood year, carrying you wrapped in a storm tarp, heading down. Odile wanted her to stay in the high country where the Consortium's men wouldn't look. Your mother said the lowlands were the last place they'd search, because no one flees toward the people who ruined them." His voice roughened. "She was right for thirty-nine years."

August gripped the edge of the altar table. The wood was smooth as river stone under his palm.

"Read," he said.

* * *

Callow began with the land registry itself—the chest's heart, the document Sela had died beside, the thing Dagny Kestrel had burned a station and doubled a bounty to destroy. It was, on its face, almost boring: parcel numbers, acreages, water rights, the meticulous bureaucratic skeleton of a nation's interior. The drowned Quarter rendered in columns and figures. But as Callow's voice moved through it, the columns became streets, the parcels became farms, the water rights became the Alder River before the dams went in—and August found he could see it, all of it, the country that had existed below the flood line, rising out of the ledger like a body surfacing.

Then the second document. The engineering assessments. The dam proposals dated eleven years before the flood, revised seven times, each revision lowering the projected cost and raising the projected yield of the reservoir that would serve the Consortium's lowland interests. And then—the third document, which Callow handled with both hands, as if it might bruise—the authorization.

*By order of the Office of Interior Development, Vhaloria Provisional Authority.* One signature. *A. Kestrel.*

"Dagny's father," August said quietly.

"Antonin Kestrel," Callow confirmed. "Who signed it six weeks after the last evacuation deadline passed, knowing four thousand people were still inside the line." He set the page flat. "We'll come back to it. Last, as always. First, the names."

And so they read the names.

It took hours. Callow read until his voice frayed, then Marta read, then Tomas, then the twins trading off paragraph by paragraph, and August listened the way you listen to weather—as something happening to you, larger than you, that you cannot argue with and must simply endure. Four thousand one hundred and six names. Every soul registered in the Quarter as of the final census. The dispossessed whose land had been seized. The relocated who had been moved to camps and lost there. The drowned, listed separately, recovered and unrecovered, the unrecovered marked with a small notation in Vhalorian script that Callow translated in a whisper: *given to the water.*

Somewhere past midnight, August heard his mother's name.

*Ilse Vey Ashgrove, parcel 114-C, Alder headwaters, dispossessed.*

Marta Vey read it, and her voice caught on the surname—her own surname, or her family's, the shepherd clans and the Quarter farmers braided together generations deep—and she looked up at August across the candlelight and did not say anything, because nothing needed saying. The name hung in the air. It existed again.

Later—much later, near the end, when the candles had burned down to stubs and the snow against the windows had softened into a hush—came the birth records. The children born in the Quarter in its final years, entered in the national register of a country with less than a decade left to live.

*Ashgrove, Augustin. Born 14th of Thaw-month, Alder headwaters district. Mother: Ilse Vey Ashgrove. Father: entry withheld at mother's request.*

"Withheld," August repeated. His own voice sounded foreign to him, rusty, like a door long shut.

"Your mother came here first," Callow said gently. "Before she went down. She filed the birth herself—we have her copy, in her hand. She asked that the father's name be held back, and Odile's predecessor honored it, and the seal on that entry has never been broken. It doesn't need to be. You are in the book. That is what matters. You were never stateless, Augustin. You were never nameless. Somewhere above the flood line, in a stone room no atlas ever acknowledged, your existence was recorded and guarded for forty years by people who owed you nothing and did it anyway."

August looked down at his hands. Storm-chaser's hands, debt-collector's hands, hands that had pulled a sealed chest out of a flooded river because everything he owned fit in a truck bed. They were shaking.

"I spent my whole life," he said, "thinking I came from nowhere. Thinking the fog in my childhood was just—damage. Something broken in me." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "It wasn't damage. It was an escape. I was carried out of a drowning country too young to hold onto it, and everyone around me agreed to let the water take the memory too."

"That's why it fits," Marta said quietly. "The seal. The valley. Why the dogs didn't bark at you and the old songs came back to you after one hearing. Nobody teaches a person their own country. They just recognize it."

* * *

Near dawn, they reached the end.

The authorization order lay on the altar where Callow had placed it hours before, and now he stood over it, and the chapel went absolutely silent except for the wind testing the shutters and the small sounds of candles consuming themselves.

"Custom holds that the signature is read last," Callow said. "After every name of every soul the order destroyed. So that the signature arrives alone, with nothing left to hide behind." He bent close to the page. "I will read it as written. All of it. The preamble, the clauses, the schedule of compensation paid to the development consortium—paid, note, to the same families whose heirs sit on the Kestrel board today—and then the name."

He read. The preamble, in a bureaucrat's elevated prose about progress and the public good. The clauses, dry as bone. The compensation schedule, figures that made Tomas Hark mutter a curse in the old tongue. And then:

"*Given under my hand and seal, this 3rd day of the Harvest-month, in the final year of the Provisional Authority—Antonin Kestrel.*"

The name stood in the air. No one moved. Outside, the snow kept falling on the buried pass, on the cairn that held Sela, on the whole white shoulder of the world, falling as though it meant to keep falling until every loose thing was covered.

"He had a daughter," Callow said at last. "She was nine years old when the flood came. She grew up in the lodge below the escarpment, and she inherited the ledger, and she has spent her life protecting this signature as though it were her own heartbeat." He straightened. "Tonight it stops being a secret and becomes a fact. Facts are heavier. She'll feel the weight. We all do. That's what witnessing is."

Tomas Hark rose and brought forward a heavy book—leather gone dark with handling, its spine stitched twice. The witness roll. Callow opened it to a fresh page and set a pen down, and the pen was nothing special, a plain steel nib in a wooden barrel, but August stared at it as though it were a lit fuse.

"You understand what signing means," Callow said. Not unkindly. "Not legally. Personally. The moment your true name enters this roll, you stop being invisible. The Consortium's heirs are named in three copies of this registry that will go down the mountain when the passes open—to a tribunal, to two capitals, to history. Anyone who wants to find the man who carried it here will find a name waiting for them. August Ashgrove could vanish into another border town and start over. Augustin Ashgrove of Vhaloria cannot. This name comes with a country attached, and countries make claims on their own."

"I know," August said.

And he found that he did know, fully, in the way you know the weight of a storm front by the ache in your knees. For twenty years he had run—from creditors, from the Consortium, from the fog of his own beginnings—and the running had worked because there had been nothing at the center of him to catch. Now there was. A mother's name. A parcel number. A birth date in a dead calendar. A debt, he thought, and almost smiled: he had spent his adult life believing debt was money, and here at the top of the world he understood at last that debt was *belonging*—everything you owe to everything that made you—and that it always, always comes due.

He thought of Sela, who had traded her loyalty for survival and then traded her survival for the chapel door, and who had told him on the glacier, delirious with fever, *you're the only honest thing I ever chased.* He thought of Odile dying in the ashes of her station, pressing a satchel into his hands like a relay baton. He thought of his mother, wrapping an infant in a storm tarp and walking down out of the clouds toward the people who had ruined her, buying her son's anonymity with her own erasure.

Anonymity had been her gift. She would forgive him for giving it back.

He took the pen.

The nib scratched, loud in the silence. He wrote carefully, letter by letter, the way you write something you intend to be read by strangers a hundred years from now:

*Augustin Vey Ashgrove, of the Alder headwaters, Vhaloria. Witness to the reading.*

Below it, one by one, the shepherds signed—Marta, Tomas, Ilo, Penna, the others, eleven names climbing the page like a rope line—and last of all Callow, who added beneath his own name a line in the old script and translated it aloud: *"Kept, and given, and witnessed. Nothing further is owed by the dead."*

Callow closed the roll. The candles had nearly finished themselves. Through the east window, above the white immensity of the sealed pass, the sky was turning the color of pewter, then pearl, then a thin hard gold.

"It's done," the old man said. "Now the harder part begins."

August looked at him. "What could be harder than this?"

"Spring," Callow said. "The melt. The passes opening. Three copies going down the mountain into a world that has spent forty years agreeing not to know." He blew out the nearest candle, and its smoke rose straight as a plumb line in the still air. "Dagny Kestrel is not dead, Augustin. Buried men are not always buried men. And somewhere below us, the rivers are already starting to move."

August turned to the window. Far down the escarpment, catching the first light, the frozen falls above the high valley gleamed like a blade—and beneath the ice, faint but unmistakable, he could hear it: water, patient as memory, beginning to run.
