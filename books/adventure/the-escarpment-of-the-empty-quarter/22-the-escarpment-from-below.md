## 22. The Escarpment, From Below

The valley had gone green while the world above it stayed white.

August saw it first from the switchbacks, where the road dropped out of the snowline and the air thickened with the smell of thawed earth and sheep dung and something sweeter he couldn't name—some alpine flower that bloomed only in the weeks when meltwater ran loud enough to drown conversation. He pulled the truck onto the gravel shoulder and stood at the guardrail for a long time, letting the sound of the water work on him. The Alder had been a brown animal in flood when he'd pulled the chest from its teeth, ten months and a lifetime ago. Up here its source streams ran clear as glass over gray stone, and he thought: *this is what it looked like before we made it carry everything.*

He drove down into the valley with his window open.

The shepherd community had no name on any map, which was by then a matter of some pride to them. You found it by knowing where the track left the logging road, and you knew where the track left the logging road because someone had told you, or because you were meant to be there. August was meant to be there. That was still a strange sentence to think. For forty years he had been a man who fit nowhere, whose name opened no doors, whose face matched no record anyone kept. Now there were three capitals that knew the name Ashgrove, a tribunal clerk who had typed it into an official register with two fingers, and a valley of shepherds who pronounced it *Ashgrov* with a softening of the second syllable that made it sound older than it was. Made it sound Vhalorian.

They were expecting him. Of course they were. Old Maretta had eyes on every pass, and the tribunal's spring session had been the only news all winter.

"You look worse," she said, meeting him at the gate of the long stone house. She was eighty if she was a day, small and hard as river stone, with hands that had never once been idle in her life and showed no intention of starting now.

"I look better," August said. "You should have seen me in March."

"I did see you in March." She took his arm anyway, which was how she said things she wouldn't say aloud. "Come inside. There's food."

There was always food. There had been food the first night too, the night they'd sheltered him believing him a returning heir, before he'd learned the truth about himself and stopped being able to tell whether the lie he'd told them—that he would carry the registry home—had been a lie at all. It hadn't turned out to be one. That was the thing about debts, he'd come to understand. You could run from them your whole life and they'd still find a way to make you pay in a currency you didn't know you had.

* * *

He ate in the long room with its low beams and its smoke-darkened rafters, and after, when the dishes were done and the younger ones had drifted out toward the pens, Maretta sat across from him with tea going cold between them and asked the question everyone in the valley had been too kind to ask since winter.

"You're going up."

"I'm going up."

"To the pass."

"To the pass." He turned his cup a quarter turn on the table. "I brought stone. Good stone, from the quarry below Corren's Ferry—I know, I know, but it's the right kind, it'll take a chisel clean, and I wanted—" He stopped. Started again. "I wanted it to come from the low country. From the place that forgot her. So part of it stays here even if the rest of me doesn't."

Maretta looked at him for a long moment with those eyes that had watched sixty winters close over these passes and had buried enough people under them to fill a chapel yard.

"She'd have hated a marker," Maretta said. "She'd have said the mountain already knows where she is."

"I know," August said. "That's why it's not for her."

* * *

They went up the next morning, August and two of the young men, Ilan and Brek, who had guided him through the hidden pass in autumn and who now walked it in summer like men strolling through their own kitchen. The route was transformed. Where ice had sealed the chimney of rock they'd climbed in the storm's teeth, water now fell in bright threads; where the scree field had been a white horror of exposure, wildflowers had gotten a foothold in every crack, tiny and stubborn, blue and yellow, blooming as if the mountain were apologizing.

At the place where the rockslide had nearly taken Sela—the place where August had caught her wrist and hauled her back from a drop that went down and down into blue shadow—they rested, and Ilan pointed wordlessly at a cairn someone had built there over the winter. Small. Careful. Five stones stacked with the precision of people who understood that stones were the only letters mountains respected.

"Who did this?" August asked.

"The children," Brek said. "After the reading. They heard the names read out—all of them, all night—and afterward they came up and built it." He shrugged, embarrassed by his own tenderness. "They argue about her. Some say she died holding the door. Some say—" He glanced at Ilan, and Ilan looked away, up the trail, toward the high shoulder where the pass crested and dropped away into country none of them had any reason to visit.

"Some say what?" August asked.

"Some say the mountain doesn't keep what it takes," Ilan said quietly. "Some say it gives things back on the far side."

* * *

They raised the marker at noon, on the shoulder below the summit chapel, where the wind came off the icefields with a sound like slow breathing. It was a slab of gray granite taller than August's knee, and he had cut into it himself, over three nights in a borrowed shed, learning the chisel the way he'd once learned barometers: badly at first, then obsessively, then well enough that the letters came out square and certain.

It read:

**SELA VOSS**
**WHO HELD THE DOOR**
**AND WHOSE DEBTS WERE ALL HER OWN**

He'd argued with himself for a week about that last line. In the end it was the truest thing he knew how to say about her. She had started the hunt—she'd told him that herself, feverish and honest on the descent, confessing that she was the one who'd flagged the pawned ingot, who'd set the whole machinery of the Consortium turning toward him. And she had ended it differently than the machinery intended, standing in a whiteout at a chapel door so that an old man and a book could live. Whatever else she owed and was owed, she had settled her own accounts. Nobody else's hand had signed for her.

The young men helped him set it in a bed of packed stone and gravel. Then, at August's request, they withdrew down the slope a respectful distance, and he stood alone with the marker for a while.

The wind moved. Somewhere above, a raven rode the thermal off the summit, tilting, unhurried, entirely indifferent to the affairs of nations.

"Sela," he said. Just that. He didn't have a speech. He'd written three and burned all of them in the shed stove. "I don't know if you're under this or not. I don't know if you're anywhere. But the door held. Callow is copying the archive by hand now, all of it, four copies, going to four directions—he says redundancy is the only prayer that works. Dagny Kestrel is in custody pending trial, and her board has turned on her like a pack deciding who's weakest, which is exactly what she'd have done in their position, so maybe there's a kind of justice in that too. The Quarter has a name again. On paper, anyway. Paper's where it starts."

He paused. The raven wheeled once and slid away north.

"And I'm sorry," he said. "For keeping walking. You'd have done the same. That's not forgiveness, I know. It's just the truth, and you liked the truth better than comfort, so I'm giving it to you straight."

He put his palm flat on the cold granite for a moment, the way you'd steady yourself against a friend's shoulder, and then he turned downhill.

* * *

It was Brek who saw the prints.

They were descending past the crest of the hidden pass, single file on the narrow tread, when Brek stopped so abruptly that Ilan walked into his pack. Brek didn't swear. He just pointed, and August followed the line of his finger across the saddle of the pass to the far side—to the country beyond the escarpment, the high empty basin that had been Vhaloria's drowned heart, that was now, in summer, a land of green lakes and emerging shorelines, the waters of the engineered flood slowly being drawn down by the tribunal's order, revealing fields, foundations, roads, the bones of a nation coming up out of the water like a body rising from sleep.

Boot prints crossed the snowpatch at the head of the far descent.

Fresh ones. This morning's, by the crispness of their edges—melt refreezes fast up here, and by afternoon even hour-old prints go soft and glassy. These were sharp. A single line of them, evenly spaced, a walker's pace, heading down. Heading *away*. Into the basin. Toward the drowned lands.

Not August's prints. Not Ilan's or Brek's—they'd come up the other side. Not the shepherds', who wore felt boots in the high country that left round, blunt marks nothing like these. These were narrow, deliberate, the print of a light mountaineering boot laced tight, the kind issued to Consortium field teams and to nobody else.

And there was something else. At the edge of the snowpatch, where the prints began, someone had planted a stone upright in the dirt. A small one, fist-sized, wedged carefully so it would stand. It meant nothing to Ilan and Brek. It meant everything to August, because he had spent three nights with a chisel learning the grammar of stones, and because Sela Voss, in eleven months of knowing her, had done exactly one sentimental thing in his presence: on the night before the final crossing, sitting in the lee of a boulder with her septic wound wrapped in his spare shirt, she had picked up a flat pebble and stood it on end in the dirt beside her boot and said, "My grandmother did that. Everywhere she stopped. She said a standing stone means *I was here and I chose to be.*"

He knelt. The pebble was still warm from the sun. Around its base, half melted into the snowpatch, pressed deep by someone kneeling, was the ghost of a second print—a handprint, fingers spread, steadying itself against the ground the way you do when one leg doesn't fully trust the other anymore.

A graze wound, gone septic, healed wrong or healing still. A limp. A woman who should be dead, walking down into the drowned country on the morning of the finest day of spring, leaving behind a standing stone and a question shaped exactly like her.

"August," Ilan said carefully, behind him. "Do you want us to—"

"No," August said. He was still kneeling. His throat was doing something complicated that he chose not to examine. "No. Don't follow them. Don't tell the others yet. Let her—" He stopped, corrected himself, smiled at the correction. "*Let them* decide what the story is. If she wants to be found, she knows where the valley is. If she wants to be a story, then God help whoever has to write it down."

He stood the pebble back upright where she'd left it. He did not take it. Taking it would have been an answer, and the whole point of the thing, he understood suddenly, was that it wasn't one.

* * *

They camped that night at the tree line, and August slept badly, and woke before dawn, and lay in his bag watching the last stars go out over the escarpment while the question walked circles around him in the dark.

*Alive.* The word kept arriving and departing like weather. If she was alive, why hadn't she come down? Why walk *away*, into the basin, into the flooded lands—unless she was going somewhere. Unless there was something in the Quarter she needed to see with her own eyes before the water finished giving it back. Her family, maybe. The registry had names in it; he knew better than anyone what those names did when you found your own among them. Maybe she'd spent the winter reading hers. Maybe she'd gone looking for a house at the bottom of a lake.

Or maybe—his mind offered this in Sela's own dry voice—maybe she simply didn't want to be a monument. Maybe she'd held the door, and paid for it, and decided that the price of a legend was more than she cared to pay twice. She'd sold maps twice; she'd told him that once, laughing, in the bad days when neither of them trusted the other. *People buy the map they want, not the map that's true.* Perhaps she'd finally bought her own.

He got up in the gray light and boiled water and drank tea and did not wake the others, and when the sun cleared the eastern ridge and lit the far basin gold—lakes flashing, green shores steaming, the whole drowned nation lying open and glittering under the morning—he stood at the edge of camp and looked at it for a long time.

Then he took out his notebook, the same battered one that had ridden in his jacket pocket through the canyon and the pass and the storm on the shoulder of the world, and he wrote, beneath the pressure readings and the wind estimates and the name of the shepherd who'd taught him the smugglers' route:

*Saw prints. Did not follow.*

He considered adding more. He added nothing. Some entries you make for the record, and some you make so that someday, when the record is read aloud, there will be at least one line in it that leaves a door open.

* * *

The drive down took two days. He took it slow. There was no reason not to.

No one was chasing him. That fact kept arriving fresh each morning like a strange report from a foreign country. No trucks in his mirrors. No agents in the diners along the highway, pretending to read newspapers. The Kestrel Consortium was in receivership, its assets frozen, its founders' signatures circulating through three capitals attached to words like *tribunal* and *reparations* and, in one memorandum he'd been shown and would remember until he died, *exhumation*. The men who had owned his debt owned nothing now but their own histories, and their histories were public property.

His truck rattled through the river towns with the granite dust still on its bed, and at the ferry crossing outside Corren's Ferry—the same crossing, the same bored attendant, the same coffee machine that had eaten his last coins a year ago—he paid his fare with money that was, for the first time in his adult life, entirely his own. Not advanced. Not loaned. Not owed forward against some future catastrophe. Earned, saved, and spent, in that order, like a normal man.

The attendant squinted at him. "You look familiar."

"I get that a lot," August said.

"You're in the papers, aren't you? The flood thing. The—" The attendant snapped his fingers. "The Quarter. You're the witness guy."

August looked at him. Behind the man, the Alder River ran wide and calm and blue-green in the June light, carrying its meltwater down to the lowlands without taking anything from anybody. A year ago it had handed him a chest out of a willow snag, and he'd pocketed it because everything he owned fit in a truck bed, and he had not known—not remotely, not in his bones—that what he was really carrying was himself.

"Yeah," he said. "I'm the witness guy."

"Huh," said the attendant, and waved him through, and went back to his crossword, and that was the whole of it. That was fame, in the end. A moment of recognition, a snap of the fingers, and then the crossword. He found he didn't mind. He found, pulling away from the ferry landing with the river sliding past his window, that he minded very little about most things now, in the way of a man who has seen what the alternative to minding looks like when it comes down a mountain at seventy miles an hour.

* * *

He camped that night on a bluff above the Alder, in the spot—almost exactly the spot—where he'd pulled the chest from the water. The willow snag was still there, bleached silver, holding nothing now but driftwood and last autumn's leaves. He ate sitting on the tailgate with his boots off, and afterward he took out the notebook again and turned, not to the entry about the prints, but to the page at the very back, where for months he had been keeping a different kind of ledger.

It was short. It had taken him the whole winter to write it, a line at a time, and it was not finished and he suspected it never would be, because that was the nature of this particular account.

*To the Quarter: owed everything, repaying in copies.*
*To Odile Hark: owed a mandate. Repaid in witnesses.*
*To the shepherds: owed a lie that became true. Outstanding.*
*To Sela Voss: unknown. Possibly unknowable. Possibly not closed.*
*To myself: forty years, plus interest.*

He read it by flashlight. Then he clicked the light off and sat in the dark and listened to the river, and above the river, faint but unmistakable in the clear night air, he could hear—could almost hear—the escarpment, thirty miles west and eight thousand feet up, holding its snowline against the stars. Brother Callow was up there tonight, copying names by candlelight. Four copies, four directions. Redundancy as prayer.

In the morning he would drive south, then east, following the tribunals as they moved from capital to capital, testifying when called, correcting the record when it drifted, being the witness guy. In the fall, if the passes held, he would come back up. He would check the cairn the children built. He would stand the pebble upright again if the rain had laid it down.

And if, some evening on some high shoulder, he happened to look across a snowpatch and see a line of narrow boot prints heading deliberately away—if he happened to find, wedged in a stone cairn or tucked under a chapel door, a flat pebble standing on end with no note and no signature—well.

He knew what it would mean. *I was here, and I chose to be.*

He closed the notebook. The river ran on toward the lowlands, carrying the mountain's melt down into the world, the way it always had, the way it always would—taking nothing now, giving everything, a witness in water that had finally, after forty years, been believed.

August Ashgrove—Augustin Ashgrove of Vhaloria, witness, debtor, survivor, son of the drowned Quarter—got into his truck in the dark, and slept, and in the morning the road was there, going wherever he decided to point it.

For the first time in his life, that was the same thing as freedom.

He drove.
