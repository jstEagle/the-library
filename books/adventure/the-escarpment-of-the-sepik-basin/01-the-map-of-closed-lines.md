## 1. The Map of Closed Lines

There is a particular kind of invisibility available to a man who schedules radio transmissions, and I had come to Port Moresby specifically to purchase it.

It works like this: nobody looks at the radio man. The surveyors look at the surveyors, the expedition leader looks at her maps, the financiers look at their ledgers, and the radio man sits in a room with a set of frequencies and a logbook and becomes, functionally, furniture. I had spent eleven months being furniture in a shipping office on Lawes Road, and when the Sepik Basin Concession Survey posted a notice asking for a communications scheduler with "field experience and discretion," I read *discretion* as *disgraced* and applied within the hour.

My name is Ines Halloran. Eleven months ago it had been attached to a collapsed viaduct in the Highlands and a set of stress calculations that were, depending on which inquiry report you believed, either falsified by me or falsified over my signature by a man with better lawyers. The inquiry believed the second thing. The banks believed the first thing, because banks believe whatever gets them paid, and my debts had a way of surviving the collapse of my reputation the way cockroaches survive the collapse of a building.

So: the radio room. A corrugated shed behind the survey company's offices, one window, one desk, one Marconi set that had been state of the art when I was learning to walk. Perfect. A man could disappear in a room like that for years.

I had been there four days when they brought me the maps.

* * *

The expedition's leader was a woman named Dr. Amara Sesay, and she was the sort of person who did not understand that radio men were furniture, which should have warned me about her. She came into the shed on the Friday with a canvas tube under her arm and no knock.

"Halloran. You'll need to know where we're going."

"I need to know where you'll be at ten hundred each day," I said. "That's the extent of my geography."

She ignored this, which was also to become characteristic, and unrolled the tube across my desk, weighting the corners with my inkwell, my coffee mug, and a paperweight I'd stolen from the shipping office out of spite. It was a survey base map of the Sepik Basin, and over it, like veins over a hand, someone had drawn the old rail alignments in red.

I knew those alignments. Not the Sepik ones — nobody knew the Sepik ones, that was the point of the survey — but the *style* of them. The confident sweep of a proposed grade. The little tick marks at chain intervals. The way a rail line drawn by hand looks less like engineering and more like calligraphy, because a rail line is a sentence written across country, and the men who wrote them were vain about their sentences.

"Decommissioned lines," Sesay said, tapping the red. "Mostly built in the twenties and thirties for timber and copper, mostly abandoned by the fifties. The concession wants to know what's salvageable. Culverts, cuttings, formation. If the old grades hold, we can re-open freight corridors for a fraction of new-build cost."

"Who drew these?"

"Company archives. Some of the originals came out of a private collection, apparently. Why?"

Because the margins were annotated. Because the annotations were in a hand I would have known if you showed it to me upside down, in the dark, through the wrong end of a telescope. Because my father had a way of writing the numeral 4 that looked like it was trying to escape the page, and there it was, escaping, in the corner of a map of the Karawari headwaters: *grade suspect — recheck against original levels*, and beneath it, in smaller letters, almost apologetic, *I did what I was paid to do.*

I did not say any of this. I said, "Company archives," in a flat voice, and poured coffee into the mug that was holding down the Ambunti district, and studied the steam as if it were the most interesting thing in the basin.

Sesay looked at me for a moment longer than furniture warrants. "You've seen these lines before?"

"I've seen rail lines before," I said. "That's what the field experience was for."

She took the map back eventually. I remember being grateful for the window behind her, because I could look at the light coming through the corrugations instead of at the red veins and the escaping 4s. She gave me the schedule requirements — ten hundred and sixteen hundred checks, emergency frequencies, the works — and left, and I sat in my invisible room and discovered that invisibility is a thing you can lose.

* * *

Here is what you should know about my father, and then we will not speak of him again for a while.

His name was Eamonn Halloran, and he was a railway engineer of the old school, which is to say he believed that a man's word and a man's levels should both be exact, right up until the moment they were more profitable loose. He worked the colonial lines — New Guinea, the Territory, a stretch of something in Malaya that he never discussed and that my mother never asked about twice. He was gone from our house for years at a stretch, and when he was home he was a tall weathered man at the end of the table who corrected my arithmetic and never once asked what I wanted to be, because he had already decided it was him.

He was also, by the time I was old enough to understand money, a man who had taken payment for alignments that did not match the ground. I learned this at twenty-six, when the viaduct came down and the inquiry started pulling threads and one of the threads led back through thirty years to a junior engineer named Eamonn Halloran who had certified grades on paper that the ground had never agreed to. My disgrace was a small, late interest payment on his.

He died three years ago in a boarding house in Cairns, alone, and his lawyer — a man I have never met — sent me a box containing his effects. I have not opened it. It sits in the bottom of my kit bag like a stone at the bottom of a river, and I have moved it from four lodgings to five, and I have never once been tempted, and I want that on the record.

The handwriting on the maps was his.

* * *

I told myself, over the weekend, that it was coincidence. Half the engineers in the pre-war territories had learned drafting from the same three textbooks and the same two drunken instructors, and a slanting 4 is not a fingerprint. I told myself that the phrase *I did what I was paid to do* was a common enough sentiment among colonial railway men that it hardly constituted a signature. I told myself these things on Saturday, and on Sunday I went into the office on the pretext of checking the Marconi's earth bonding, which nobody does on a Sunday, and I pulled the archive copies of the concession maps, all nine sheets, and I spread them on the floor of the radio room like a man reading tarot.

The annotations were everywhere. Not on every sheet — the eastern sheets, toward the coast, were clean, official, the work of some anonymous draftsman — but the western sheets, the deep basin, the Karawari and the country beyond it, were crowded with marginalia in that escaping hand. Elevations disputed. Formation waterlogged — do not trust the 1934 report. Culverts at chain 41 blown from behind, note the direction of fall. And once, on Sheet 7, in the blank space where the unmapped country began, a single line:

*They will come back for this. Leave the truth where only a man who knows my hand will find it.*

I sat down on the floor among the maps, which is not the posture of a man maintaining professional detachment, and I read that sentence maybe forty times.

Understand: I had built a life on the principle that my father was a closed account. Written off. The man had ruined me at one remove, ruined my mother at closer range, and died without apology, and I had done the arithmetic of forgiveness and found the balance outstanding. I was not sentimental about him. I was not *anything* about him. He was a name I did not use and a box I did not open.

And now he was in the margins of my employer's maps, addressing me — because who else? *Only a man who knows my hand* — from the blank edge of a wilderness I was supposed to be invisible in.

* * *

Monday morning, Sesay came back for the final schedule confirmation, and I made a decision that I would like to describe as professional diligence but was in fact the first crack.

"Your western sheets," I said. "The archive originals. Where did the company get them?"

She paused in the doorway. She had a way of pausing that made you feel the question had been forwarded to a committee. "A private collection," she said again. "Why do you ask?"

"Because the annotations are field annotations. Whoever made them walked these lines. If he disputed the grades on Sheet 7, your survey should know before it stakes a camp on them."

"You've read the annotations?"

"Radio men read everything," I said. "It's the loneliness."

It was, I thought afterward, the most honest thing I'd said in eleven months. She studied me with those committee eyes, and then she did something I didn't expect: she pulled Sheet 7 from her tube and laid it on my desk.

"The archive copies are second-generation," she said. "These annotations were on the originals. The company's surveyor vouched for them — said the annotator was one of the best alignment men in the Territories, and that his disputes were worth taking seriously." She tapped the blank edge, the sentence about leaving the truth. "Did you know him?"

The room was very quiet. The Marconi hummed its idiot hum. Somewhere outside a truck changed gear on the coast road, and I was aware of my own heartbeat the way you're aware of a stone in your boot — not painful, just insistent.

"No," I said.

It was not the first lie I had told to protect my name. It was the first lie I had ever told to protect *his*, and I felt it go in like a splinter, and I understood, with the particular clarity of a man watching his own hand betray him, that the invisibility I had purchased was already spent.

"Then study them," Sesay said. "You'll be our last link if the field sets fail. I want you to know the country well enough to guess where we are even when we can't tell you." She rolled the map away, and at the door she stopped. "The annotator's name was in the archive file. Halloran. E. Halloran. Any relation?"

The question hung there. I had rehearsed answers to a hundred questions in eleven months — *where were you during the inquiry, aren't you the viaduct man, didn't your father* — and I had a whole wardrobe of them, tailored and pressed.

"No relation," I said. "Common enough name."

"Is it?" she said, and left, and I sat in my invisible room with the splinter in me and the hum in my ears, and outside the rain started coming down on the corrugated roof the way it does in Port Moresby in the wet — not falling so much as arriving, all at once, like a verdict.

* * *

That night I did not open the box. I want that noted too. I took it out of the kit bag, and I held it on my knees in the boarding-house room with the moths ticking against the bulb, and the string was right there, and the knot was a granny knot, the kind a man ties when he's in a hurry or doesn't care, and I sat with it for the better part of an hour.

Then I put it back, unopened, because opening it would mean the past had won, and I had a schedule to run in the morning. Ten hundred and sixteen hundred. Frequencies logged, furniture in place, a man with no history and no relations and no interest whatsoever in the handwriting of dead men.

I was asleep, or near it, when I understood the thing I should have understood on the floor of the radio room among the maps: the survey was going *west*. Sheet 7. The Karawari. The blank country where he had left the truth for a man who knew his hand.

And the schedule I had built, the invisible little timetable of my respectable disappearance, put the expedition in his country in nineteen days — and me, if anything went wrong, on the first boat after them.

I lay in the dark and listened to the rain and told myself it wouldn't come to that.

I have never been a good liar, except on paper.
