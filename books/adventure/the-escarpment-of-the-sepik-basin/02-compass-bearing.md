## 2. Compass Bearing

The spare kit lived in three green footlockers at the back of the company shed, and nobody had opened them since the last survey that came home, which was to say since the last survey that didn't. I'd been given the key on a loop of fraying twine and told to inventory everything twice, because the expedition's quartermaster had a habit of counting optimism as equipment.

So I spent the morning of my fourth day in Port Moresby kneeling on a concrete floor among canvas and brass, making lists. Sleeping bags with more mold than filling. A theodolite in a case lined with velvet gone grey as mouse fur. Signal flags for a code system two wars out of date. It was honest work, the kind where your hands are busy enough that your head can go quiet, and I was grateful for it. Gratitude was not something I had much practice in, but I was learning to fake it the way you learn any trade — badly at first, then well enough to pass.

The compass turned up in the second locker, wrapped in an oilcloth that crackled like old paper when I unfolded it.

I knew it before I saw it. I knew it by the weight of it settling into my palm, that particular heft of solid brass machined by someone who expected the thing to outlive him. Which, as it happened, it had.

My name was engraved on the reverse. Not printed — engraved, cut deep with a graver's steady hand: *E. HALLORAN*. My father's initials were E.H. too, but this wasn't his. This was mine. The lawyer in Brisbane had shipped it to me fourteen months ago, along with a letter I'd read once and burned in a hotel ashtray, and I had put the compass in a drawer in three successive rented rooms without ever once opening the drawer to look at it. When I left Moresby the first time I'd meant to leave it behind entirely. I'd packed it anyway. That's the kind of man I was — the kind who couldn't even abandon things properly.

Now here it was again, surfacing out of company property like a body out of a river, because apparently the universe had a sense of humor and no taste whatsoever.

* * *

Let me tell you what I knew about Edmund Halloran, since the compass seemed determined to make me say it out loud, if only to myself.

He was a surveyor and alignment engineer, which is a fancy way of saying he decided where railways went, and he was good at it in the way that matters least — good on paper, good in reports, good at convincing men with money that a line could climb a grade no line should climb. He worked the Sepik concessions in the years when the Territory still pretended the interior would be tamed by iron rails, and then the money dried up and the lines died and he came home to us with his hands clean and his eyes somewhere else. He drank the way other men went to church: regularly, communally, with a certain solemnity. He missed my graduation because he was in Lae arguing about culverts. He missed my mother's funeral because — well. There was always a reason, and the reasons were always load-bearing, holding up nothing.

When the scandal found me — and it found me; I never went looking for it, though I'll admit I stopped walking away from it sooner than a careful man would have — he sent one telegram. Four words. *Say nothing. Come home.* As if home were a place he'd ever maintained rather than an address he defaulted to. I did not go. Two years later he was dead of a liver that had finally filed its resignation, and the lawyer sent me a box containing the compass, a letter, and nothing else. No explanation of why a man who had forgotten my birthday for eleven consecutive years had gone to the trouble of having my name cut into an instrument I'd never asked for.

I'd read the letter once. What I remembered of it was mostly the smell of the hotel room and the sound of the ashtray. Something about being proud of me. Something about the lines. The rest I had burned along with the paper, and I told myself the burning was hygiene rather than cowardice, and mostly believed it.

And now the compass sat in my hands in a hot shed smelling of diesel and mildew, pointing — I checked, idly, the way you check a watch you don't trust — north-northwest, give or take.

Except that it shouldn't have been.

* * *

Here's the thing about compasses and railroads: they keep each other honest. A surveyed alignment is drawn against true north, corrected for declination, staked in the field, and the bearing lives in the paperwork forever. The expedition's maps — the ones I'd spent three evenings studying, the ones with the marginal annotations in handwriting I'd have known anywhere, because I'd grown up watching it crawl across engineering pads in our kitchen — those maps gave bearings for every decommissioned line in the concession. The Karawari spur ran 312 degrees from its junction marker. The old timber branch off it ran 287. Clean numbers, corrected numbers, numbers my father had written himself in blue pencil twenty-some years ago.

I set the compass on the locker lid and sighted it along the junction bearing as marked. It disagreed. Not wildly — compasses drift, needles sulk, local iron does strange things — but consistently. I tried the timber branch. Same disagreement, same direction, same stubborn few degrees west of where the map said the line ought to lie. I carried the thing outside, away from the lockers, away from the shed's corrugated skeleton, and stood in the yard sighting across open ground toward the hills. Same offset. Every time. As if the needle knew something the maps didn't, or the maps knew something they weren't telling.

A lazy man would have called it instrument error and put it back in the oilcloth. I am many things, but I have never been lazy about instruments; it was the one virtue my father passed down intact, like a gene or a curse. So I sat on the loading dock with the expedition's maps spread around me and the compass flat on my knee, and I spent two hours doing arithmetic I hadn't agreed to do.

The offset wasn't random. That was the first finding. Random error wanders. This held — within a degree, across every alignment I tested, always pulling west of true, always toward the same quadrant of the basin. The second finding was worse. If you extended the offset bearing past the end of the mapped spur, past the terminus where the blue-pencil annotations simply stopped, the line it described ran through country the maps left blank. Not unexplored blank — *expunged* blank. The kind of blank where the paper shows faint ghosting underneath, where something was erased hard enough to dent it.

I know what erased map sections look like because I grew up watching my father erase things. He had a particular way of doing it, thorough and slightly ashamed, pressing too hard so the paper thinned. I used to think he was correcting mistakes. Sitting on that dock with the sun going orange behind the fuel tanks, I considered for the first time in my life that maybe he'd been erasing testimony.

The third finding was the one I kept turning over after dark, back in my room above the chandlery, with the compass on the table and the mosquito coil smoking its slow gray rope into the ceiling fan's wash. The engraving. *E. Halloran.* He'd had it done professionally — the lettering was machine-cut, not field-work — which meant he'd taken the instrument to a shop, paid a man, waited days, all to put my name on a thing he then never gave me. He'd died with it still in the lawyer's keeping. Either he'd lost his nerve, or he'd been waiting for something, or — and this was the thought that made me get up and pace the room — he'd wanted it to reach me only after there was no chance I could ask him what it meant.

That was the cruelest possibility, and therefore the most likely. A living father can be interrogated. A dead one can only be read, and reading him meant handling everything he'd touched, and everything he'd touched was starting to look like evidence.

* * *

I want to be clear about what I felt, because what I felt was not grief. Grief implies you've lost something you had. What I felt was closer to burglary — the sense that a man had broken into my life through a locked drawer fourteen months after his death and rearranged the furniture. He had no right. He'd forfeited the rights years ago, one missed birthday at a time, and yet here was his handiwork in my kit, his offsets in my maps, his blue pencil steering my attention toward a blank place in the Sepik Basin that the needle insisted was worth looking at.

And underneath the resentment — I hate admitting this, so let's get it over with — underneath it, curiosity. Reluctant, furious, insomniac curiosity. Because the engineer in me looked at a consistent anomaly across multiple independent alignments and did not see a dead man's sentimentality. It saw data. And data wants explaining, and I have never in my life been able to leave an anomaly alone, which is precisely how I ended up disgraced in the first place, come to think of it. The scandal started with a number that wouldn't sit still. Maybe that's the family disease: we can't walk past a wrong figure. His version of it falsified alignments. Mine, allegedly, signed off on someone else's. Different symptoms, same blood.

I tested the compass one more time that night, from the window, sighting past the harbor lights toward the northwest. The needle settled, patient as a dog that knows where the bone is buried, and pointed its few degrees off true, toward the blank country, toward whatever my father had pressed too hard on the paper to hide.

Tomorrow the expedition flew upriver. Tomorrow the schedule began, my transmissions, my codes, my small invisible job keeping other men safe with arithmetic. I put the compass in my kit — not the drawer, not the oilcloth, my actual working kit, and I noticed myself doing it and didn't stop — and I lay in the dark listening to the fan and rehearsing all the reasons this meant nothing.

It was around midnight that I admitted the real reason I was bringing it. Not sentiment. Not closure, God forbid, that word that gets stamped on grief like a customs seal. I was bringing it because the maps were wrong in a specific direction, and my father had made them wrong, and a man who falsifies a map does it to hide something or to mark something, and either way the answer was sitting in blank country at the end of a bearing only one instrument in the world seemed to hold.

At four in the morning the radio room called to confirm the expedition's first scheduled check-in window. I confirmed it. Professional, clipped, anonymous. Then I sat on the edge of the bed with the compass in both hands and turned it slowly until the needle swung, and watched it settle back, every time, to the same stubborn point in the empty quarter of the map.

"Whatever you're pointing at," I told it quietly, "you'd better be worth it."

The needle didn't answer. Dead men's instruments rarely do. But it held its bearing, patient and certain, like a hand on a shoulder I hadn't asked for, guiding me — whether toward him or away from him I genuinely could not yet say — north-northwest, off true, into the blank.
