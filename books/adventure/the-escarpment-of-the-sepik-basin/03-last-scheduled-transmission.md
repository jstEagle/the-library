## 3. Last Scheduled Transmission

The Karawari check-in was due at 0640 local time, which in the upside-down arithmetic of expedition radio meant I had been awake since five, sitting in a canvas chair with my feet on a ammunition crate, watching the sky over Port Moresby go from bruise-colored to merely gray. There is no lonelier hour than the one before a scheduled transmission. You sit there rehearsing the conversation you're about to have with people you've never met, three hundred kilometers of jungle and river away, and you tell yourself it's just procedure. Just a voice check. Just numbers read into a handset by someone chewing breakfast.

I'd been running the schedule for eleven days. Eleven days of Sesay's clipped confirmations, of wind noise and generator hum, of a guide named Wari who always signed off with "good signal, good road," which was not a phrase anyone taught him but something he'd invented himself, and which I had come to like more than I could reasonably explain.

At 0641 there was nothing.

At 0643, static, the particular flat hiss that means either distance or dead batteries or dead men, and no honest radio man can tell you which until he's tried everything else.

At 0647 they came through.

"Port Moresby, Port Moresby, this is Survey One." Sesay's voice, thinner than usual, the way voices get when the atmosphere is doing something ugly between two points on the earth. "Check-in complete, position as logged, all stations—" and then she stopped. Not faded. Stopped. The way you stop mid-sentence when someone walks into the room behind you.

There was a sound after her voice cut out. I've played it back in my head maybe four hundred times since, and I still can't name it. Not a gunshot — gunshots have a crack to them, a snap at the front end. This was heavier, duller, like something large being set down on wet ground. Like a tree going over. It lasted perhaps a second and a half, and then the carrier wave held for another few seconds — someone's thumb still on the transmit key, or a hand fallen across the handset — and then nothing, and then the long indifferent hiss of a frequency that belonged to nobody.

I did what any trained operator does. I called back. "Survey One, this is Port Moresby, say again your last." I called at one-minute intervals for twenty minutes, logging each attempt with the time and the conditions, because procedure is a comfort and also a shield: if you can write it down, it isn't chaos yet. Then I ran through the contingency ladder. Alternate frequency. Alternate power assumption — if their generator had failed, the handset would be running off a battery pack with maybe forty minutes of transmit left, so I kept my calls short and spaced, leaving them room to answer. I checked the propagation forecast, which showed a solar flux reading that was boring in every direction. No geomagnetic event. No weather system between us and them heavy enough to swallow a signal whole. The ionosphere, for once in its life, was behaving.

Which meant the silence wasn't the sky's fault.

* * *

By nine o'clock I had filled three log pages and developed the specific headache that comes from listening hard to nothing. I went looking for my supervisor.

Gordon Voss occupied an office in the company compound that had once been a storage room and still smelled faintly of diesel and old tarpaulin, as if the building itself hadn't accepted its promotion. He was a small neat man who had spent thirty years in shipping logistics and regarded the interior of New Guinea the way a bank manager regards a vault: as a space containing assets, to be inventoried and not personally visited. He had hired me, I understood even then, precisely because I was cheap, discreet, and had reasons not to ask questions. A man with debts and a reputation in traction engineeering that had gone from gilded to scorched inside a single season does not make waves. I was, professionally speaking, ballast. I kept the boat steady by not rocking it.

I put the log pages on his desk and told him what I'd heard.

Voss listened with the expression of a man being told the tide table was wrong. Then he said, "Protocol says forty-eight hours."

"I know what protocol says. I wrote the schedule based on protocol."

"And the schedule is working. One missed check-in is within tolerance. Weather up there this time of year—" he waved a hand at the window, toward a horizon he had never crossed, "—squalls, atmospheric interference. You know how it is on those frequencies."

"Gordon, I know exactly how it is on those frequencies. I've been living on them for eleven days. This wasn't interference." I tapped the log page where I'd noted the cutoff. "She stopped mid-word. Mid-*word*. Propagation doesn't do that. Batteries don't do that. If the transmitter died, the carrier drops clean. If they lost power entirely, we'd have gotten some kind of sign-off, because Sesay is the kind of woman who signs off during an earthquake. Something happened in that camp between one syllable and the next, and whatever it was, it took the radio with it."

He looked at me over his glasses with the mild patience of a man managing a difficult subordinate, and I felt the familiar heat climb the back of my neck — the heat of being handled, of being told your expertise is a mood rather than a fact. I had spent a year being handled. Railroads are unforgiving institutions; when a bridge fails under a train, somebody's judgment has failed too, and the industry closes around that somebody like water closing over a stone. I knew what it was to have my judgment reclassified as temperament. I swore I wouldn't sit quietly through it again, and here I was, sitting quietly through it.

"The terrain argues for moving now," I said. "Their last confirmed position puts them above the Karawari confluence, on a bench of ground the survey maps mark as seasonal floodplain. If the river's come up — and the gauge readings I pulled yesterday suggest it has, upstream catchment's had rain for six days straight — that camp is either underwater or the party's moved and hasn't been able to establish a new antenna. Either way, the answer isn't waiting. The answer is asking Wewak to stand by, getting a charter quote, and having eyes on that ground inside a day."

"A charter costs money."

"So does a search. So does a recovery. So does explaining to Dr. Sesay's university why the company heard her disappear in real time and chose to spend two days checking whether it might clear up." I heard my own voice sharpen and made myself stop, because I recognized the tone — it was the tone of a man arguing his case to a review board, and I had promised myself I was done making cases. I was supposed to be invisible. Invisible men don't argue. They log the anomaly, file it, and go home.

But I couldn't make myself shut up entirely, so I said, quieter: "Forty-eight hours is a protocol written for parties that might be moving through country without fixed camps. These people have a fixed camp. They check in at the same minute every morning. When a clock stops, you don't wait to see if it starts again. You open the case."

Voss considered me for a moment, and I want to be fair to him, because I believe there was a flicker of something human in it — a man weighing whether the person in front of him was worth the trouble of believing. Then the arithmetic won, the way it always wins in rooms like that. "The financier's representative arrives Thursday," he said. "If there's a rescue operation on the books when he lands, it raises questions about the concession's safety record, and questions about the concession's safety record raise questions about the permits, and I am not going to manufacture a crisis because the radio had a bad morning." He slid the log pages back across the desk. "Wait the forty-eight hours. Log everything. Say nothing to anyone outside this office. That's an instruction, Halloran, not a suggestion."

I picked up the pages. At the door I stopped, because there was one more thing, and it had been sitting in my chest since 0647 like a swallowed stone.

"When she cut off," I said, "there was a sound. Underneath her voice. Did you listen to the tape?"

"There's no tape, Halloran. We don't record routine check-ins."

Of course we didn't. Routine check-ins are routine right up until they aren't, and then everyone agrees the absence of a recording was standard practice. I said, "Right. No tape." And I walked out into the white heat of the morning carrying the only copy of that second and a half, stored in the one medium no one could confiscate, which was my memory, which was already proving less reliable than I'd have liked. Had the sound come *before* her voice stopped, or after? I'd swear before, now. Yesterday I'd have sworn after. Memory is a radio too — it picks up what it wants and calls the rest static.

* * *

I waited the forty-eight hours. That sentence deserves its own paragraph, because waiting is not the passive thing it advertises itself as. Waiting is work. Waiting is waking at 0530 anyway, because your body has learned the schedule and doesn't care that the schedule has been suspended. Waiting is calling the dead frequency at 0640 sharp, every morning, twice more through the day, and logging each silence with a steadiness you don't feel. Waiting is pulling river gauge data from the hydrology office under the pretense of general professional interest, and discovering that the Karawari's upper catchment had taken two hundred millimeters in six days, and doing the arithmetic on a floodplain bench with a slide rule because the calculator reminded you of a man you used to be.

Waiting is also noticing things you weren't assigned to notice.

Like this: the expedition's route files, which I'd studied until I could draw the alignments from memory, showed their supply line running up the Karawari to a transfer point that didn't appear on any government chart I could find. I'd flagged it in week one as a cartographic gap — the Sepik Basin is full of gaps, the maps up there are half survey and half wishful thinking — and Voss had thanked me for my thoroughness and filed my memo somewhere memos go to die. But now, with nothing to do but listen to static and cross-reference paper, I went back through the concession documents in the shared cabinet, the ones a radio scheduler had no business reading and therefore, technically, hadn't. The transfer point appeared there. It had coordinates. It had tonnage projections attached to it, ore tonnage, which was strange, because the expedition I was supporting was charting *rail lines*, closed ones, decommissioned ghost alignments from the colonial era — and rail surveys do not generate ore tonnage projections. Ore tonnage projections come from mining assessments. Somebody had hung a mining operation's paperwork on a railroad survey's skeleton, and the fit was wrong in the specific way that a forged signature is wrong: close enough to pass at a glance, wrong everywhere you looked closely.

And there was a name on the financing annex that I recognized from the newspapers, back when I still read them for pleasure instead of for mentions of myself. The financier. The same man whose representative was arriving Thursday, whose arrival Voss had weighed against human lives and found lighter.

I sat with that for a while. I am not a naive man; I had worked for railroads long enough to know that concessions are where engineering goes to be laundered into politics. But there is a difference between knowing a thing abstractly and watching it move, and what moved me was simpler and worse: the schedule I was running, the check-ins I lived by, the whole apparatus of times and frequencies and confirmations — it existed to keep those people safe on paper while keeping them unaccounted for in fact. Forty-eight hours wasn't caution. It was latency. It was the gap between an event and anyone being obliged to care about it, and gaps like that are where companies do their best work.

I thought about my father, which I had been trying not to do since the compass arrived. He had been a surveyor on these same lines, forty years ago, and everything I knew about him suggested he had learned the same lesson I was learning now: that the map and the territory were kept apart deliberately, by men who profited from the distance. His margins full of annotations that matched no official drawing. His compass that pointed politely off true north, as if even the instrument had agreed to lie. I had built a career on being the engineer who made the map and the territory agree — bridges that stood, gradients that held, schedules that meant what they said — partly, I could admit in the dark, as a rebuke to him. And now here I was, employed by men who needed the disagreement, ordered to sit on my hands inside it.

At 2200 on the second night I called the frequency one last time. Static. I logged it, initialled it, dated it. Forty-eight hours, expired, per protocol. I wrote in the remarks column, in my neatest hand: *No contact. Recommend immediate ground verification.* And then I sat looking at what I'd written, understanding perfectly well that the recommendation would travel upward, meet the financier's representative somewhere in the administrative plumbing, and dissolve.

* * *

Here is the thing about the first real decision: it doesn't announce itself. Nobody hands you a moment and says, this is the one where you become someone. You're just copying files at midnight because you can't sleep, feeding pages into the photocopier in the records room with the light off and the door wedged, telling yourself it's for your own reference, purely professional, a man is entitled to understand the project he serves — and somewhere between the third annex and the seventh, you notice that your hands have stopped shaking and started working, and you understand that the decision got made somewhere below the level of words, the way a compass needle settles, and all that's left is the slow embarrassment of catching up to yourself.

I copied the route files. I copied the financing annex. I copied the permit applications, both versions — the one submitted and the one, in a folder marked DRAFTS, that appeared to be the original before someone improved it, and the improvement consisted of moving a boundary line roughly the width of a parish. I copied Voss's memo declining early verification. I copied my own logs, all of them, the silences included, because a silence with a timestamp is evidence too.

Then I wrote my resignation. Short, courteous, uninformative — effective immediately, personal reasons, thank you for the opportunity. Personal reasons. The truest false statement I ever composed. My personal reason was that I had heard a woman vanish mid-sentence and been ordered to treat it as weather, and I found I was no longer able to be the kind of man who did that, and the discovery had turned out to be expensive, as discoveries usually are.

I sealed the copies in a waterproof document bag, the kind survey crews use, and put it at the bottom of my kit under the spare socks, and I took out the compass.

It lay in my palm in the lamplight, brass worn bright along the edges where a thumb had worried it for years, engraved HALLORAN in a hand I refused for thirty years to admit I recognized. I set it on the route map, over the sector above the Karawari, and let the needle settle. It swung, wavered, and came to rest pointing not north but northeast, off true by a stubborn seven degrees, toward the blank space on the government chart where the transfer point lived, toward the spur alignment that appeared in the concession papers and nowhere else, toward whatever my father had seen at the end of a bearing he'd never explained to anyone, least of all the son who'd stopped asking.

A compass that lies consistently isn't broken. It's loyal — to something other than north. I'd told myself it was probably just magnetized by some old iron in the case, a defect, a curiosity. Sitting there at midnight with the copied permits and the resignation letter and the static still ringing in my ears, I ran out of ways to pretend. The needle wasn't defective. It was testifying.

In the morning I would need a boat, a cover story, and a route north to Wewak. I had a false work order half-drafted in my head and a debt collector's phone number I intended to stop answering. What I didn't have — yet — was any idea that I wouldn't be traveling alone.
