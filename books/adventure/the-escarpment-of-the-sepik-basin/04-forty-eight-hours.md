## 4. Forty-Eight Hours

The static had a texture to it. That was the first thing I learned in those two days, hunched over the company's receiver in a room that smelled of mildew and someone else's cigarettes. Static wasn't silence. It was weather, distance, the ionosphere doing its slow indifferent arithmetic overhead. And somewhere inside it, if you listened long enough, it started sounding like voices — which is either a comfort or a symptom, depending on how long you've been alone with it.

I'd logged forty-one hours of nothing when I stopped being able to tell the difference.

The protocol was clear enough. A missed check-in triggered a forty-eight-hour watch period, then a formal incident report, then whatever the company did instead of caring. I'd written the protocol myself, in a sense — adapted it from coastal shipping schedules into something the expedition could live by. Sesay had signed off on it with a brisk note: *Sensible. Let's hope we never need it.* Her handwriting slanted forward like a woman leaning into wind.

So I waited. That was the job. That was what they paid me for, and what they were paying me was barely enough to keep the Port Moresby debt collectors guessing about my address, so you can imagine how much patience it bought.

But waiting is not the same as sitting still, and by the second morning my hands wanted work. So I gave them work.

* * *

It started innocently, the way all betrayals of employment do. I pulled the expedition's route logs because I needed something to do besides listen to static, and because re-reading Sesay's daily position reports felt like keeping her company. Day one: upriver past Angoram, river gauge at Ambunti reading 4.2 meters and falling. Day three: the Karawari confluence, water brown and fast, campsites logged against named villages. Day six: the camp above the Karawari tributary where her last transmission had died mid-word — *barometer's dropped like a stone, Tobias says the water's coming up fast, we're moving the—*

And then nothing. Two days of nothing.

Here is what nobody tells you about radio silence in the Sepik: the river itself is a liar. The maps of that country are guesses wearing uniforms. Half the tributaries change course every wet season, rearranging themselves like furniture in a house nobody owns, and any survey that trusts its charts more than its eyes is already lost. I knew this. It was practically the only thing I still knew for certain, after everything.

So while the receiver hissed, I did what engineers do with other people's numbers. I checked them.

The weather reports came off the mission band and the government station at Wewak — a low-pressure trough parked over the middle basin for four days running, rainfall totals that would have made a monsoon blush. The river gauges told the rest: the Karawari had come up nearly two meters in thirty-six hours. Any camp on a low bench above that tributary would be underwater or scrambling. Which matched Sesay's last words well enough. Moving camp in a flood is dangerous but ordinary. Ordinary doesn't explain two days of silence, because even a swamped camp has a spare set, and even a broken set gets flagged by the next village downriver. Word travels on that river faster than electricity does.

Unless there was no village downriver. Unless they weren't where their log said they were.

I spread the route sheets across the desk and weighted the corners with coffee mugs, and I did the thing I used to do before anyone had heard of Ines Halloran except as a name attached to a collapsed culvert and a very creative set of load calculations. I triangulated.

Sesay's position reports weren't coordinates — she wasn't carrying survey-grade equipment for a radio schedule — but they were anchored. Distances paddled per day, bearings taken off peaks, named villages passed. And when I laid them against the concession maps the company had issued, the arithmetic went wrong. Not badly wrong. Nothing you'd notice unless you'd spent years making terrain lie flat on paper. But each day's reported distance was a few percent short of what the map allowed, as though the river between the villages had quietly grown longer than the cartographers believed. Errors accumulate. By day six, the discrepancy was better than ten kilometers.

Ten kilometers in that country isn't an error. It's a different world. Different tributaries, different ground, different people. If Sesay's camp was ten kilometers from where the map said, then every assumption built on that position — search grids, relief parties, the company's tidy little timeline — was built on sand.

And then the question underneath the question, the one I'd been avoiding since the static first swallowed her voice: why would the concession maps be wrong? Maps get old, sure. Rivers move, granted. But these maps weren't old. They'd been drafted within the decade, commissioned by the same men who owned the concession, and a surveyor who misses by ten kilometers doesn't keep his license. A surveyor who misses by ten kilometers *on purpose*, though — a surveyor who draws the Karawari tributary ten kilometers east of where God put it — that man has a reason. And reasons like that are never geographic.

I sat back and looked at the ceiling fan turning its slow corrupt circles, and I thought: you're doing it again, Halloran. You're finding the conspiracy in the contour lines because the alternative is admitting you're just a radio clerk whose employers have lost some scientists. It's vanity. It's always vanity with ruined men — we'd rather be important enough to be persecuted than honest enough to be irrelevant.

Then I opened the concession files.

* * *

I want to say I broke into something. It sounds better that way. The truth is the filing cabinet in the corner wasn't locked, because nobody locks anything in an office where the only person who cares about the contents is the man being underpaid to mind them. The cabinet held the expedition's administrative paper: permits, correspondence, the schedule I'd built, and beneath it all, in a folder the color of weak tea, the original permit applications for the concession itself.

I told myself I was just looking for the survey data. If the maps were mischarted, the underlying surveys would show it — field notes, instrument readings, the honest bones under the dishonest skin. An engineer checks the source. That was my story and I was sticking to it.

The field notes were there. What struck me first wasn't what they said but how they were signed. Every sheet carried an approval signature in the same confident hand, and the name meant nothing to me — some consultant, dead or retired or promoted — but the dates meant plenty. The surveys were dated in the dry season. Both of them. Field verification of river alignments, conducted in the months when the Karawari sits in its bed like a tired dog, when every sandbar shows and every channel looks tame. Nobody who knew that river would certify its course from a dry-season fix. The wet season moves it half a kilometer; a decade moves it five. Certify in the dry, and by the time anyone checked in the wet, your error would look like nature's fault, not yours.

That was sloppy. Or it was deniable. With paperwork, those are usually the same thing.

I should have closed the folder then. Instead I read the correspondence, because I am, at bottom, a man who cannot leave a locked door unlocked behind him without going back through it. Most of it was routine — schedules, budgets, a memorably petty dispute over outboard motor fuel allowances. Then a memo, three pages, from the concession's financial backers to the survey director, marked confidential, discussing "alignment flexibility" and "the desirability of routing documentation that accommodates future operational requirements." Translation, if you spoke fluent corporate: draw the line wherever we'll want it later, and make the paper agree.

Future operational requirements. Ore, most likely. The concession covered timber on its face, but timber doesn't need rail spurs, and the budget had quietly included rail spurs. Timber also doesn't care whose land it stands on. Ore does.

I copied it all. Not because I'd decided anything — I hadn't, I want that on the record of my own conscience, such as it is — but because my hands were shaking slightly and copying things is steadier than thinking. There was a duplicating machine down the hall, older than me and twice as stubborn, and I fed it the memo page by page in the small hours, listening to it groan like a man asked to carry secrets he didn't ask for. The copies went into the bottom of my kit bag, under a spare shirt and the brass compass I still hadn't decided how to feel about.

The compass. My father's compass, shipped to me by a lawyer along with a bill for the shipping. It sat in the bag pointing its steady wrong north, the way it had pointed since the day I tested it against the old rail alignments — off true by a consistent few degrees, always toward the same unmarked patch of map, as if the old man had magnetized it toward whatever he'd spent his life not telling me. I'd meant to sell it. I kept meaning to sell it.

Two days of static will make a man sentimental. I took it out and set it on the desk beside the mischarted maps, needle trembling toward its private truth, and I thought about my father drawing lines through this same country thirty years ago, taking money to bend steel around swamps, and I thought about whoever had drawn these newer lines, bending paper the same way for the same reasons, and I understood — coldly, without drama, the way you understand a load path or a failing grade — that I was looking at the family business.

* * *

At hour forty-six, the supervisor arrived. Verrall. A neat man, the kind whose shirts are always pressed and whose conscience is always dry-cleaned. He stood in the doorway holding a telegram and looking at me the way auditors look at ledgers they've already decided about.

"Nothing?" he said.

"Nothing."

"The forty-eight hours are nearly up. You'll file the incident report tonight. Standard language. Loss of contact, presumed weather-related, recommend assessment when conditions permit."

"When conditions permit," I repeated. "The conditions are a flooded river and a missing survey team. When do they permit?"

"They don't," Verrall said, with the mild honesty of a man who has never once been punished for candor. "They lapse. The concession's backers aren't inclined to fund a search for a survey that has — how shall I put this — served its purpose. The data was transmitted daily. Whatever remains to be collected can be collected by others." He set the telegram on the desk. "Your services conclude at month's end. You'll be paid through then. There's a reference letter, if you want it."

I looked at the telegram. It was from the financiers' office, addressed to Verrall, and it used the phrase "future operational requirements" in the wild, in daylight, as though it were a normal thing to say about a missing expedition. Maybe it was. Maybe I'd spent too many hours with static whispering to me.

"Sesay's team might be alive," I said.

"Sesay's team was insured," Verrall said, and left.

I filed the report that night. Standard language, loss of contact, presumed weather-related. I wrote it in my best hand, the careful engineer's lettering my father taught me before he taught me anything else about disappointing people, and I signed it, and I dated it, and then I wrote my resignation on the sheet beneath it, effective immediately, citing personal reasons. Personal reasons. The personal reason weighed four kilos and fit in a kit bag, and part of it was brass.

Before dawn I walked out of the compound with the bag on my shoulder and the copies warm against my spine like a second heartbeat. The guard at the gate nodded me through; I'd told him I was catching the early ferry to Wewak on company business, and there was a work order in my pocket saying exactly that, forged with the same loving attention I'd once given bridge loadings. It's strange what turns out to be transferable skill.

The harbor was grey and breathing. Somewhere north of it, up rivers that didn't run where the maps claimed, a woman who trusted my schedules was ten kilometers from anywhere anyone would think to look. The compass in my bag pointed its stubborn off-north toward her, or toward my father, or toward whatever junction of the two was waiting up there, and for the first time since the lawyer's parcel arrived, I didn't resent it.

I had stopped being an employee somewhere around hour thirty, though the paperwork only caught up at dawn. What I'd become instead had no title and no salary and a forged travel order, and it intended to go and find out what the river had done with the truth.
