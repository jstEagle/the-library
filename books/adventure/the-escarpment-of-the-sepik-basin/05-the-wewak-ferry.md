## 5. The Wewak Ferry

The work order was a forgery of my own manufacture, and I want it noted for the record that it was a good one. I'd spent four years signing other people's paperwork in Port Moresby — freight manifests, radio logs, the small bureaucratic confetti that keeps a colony's commerce moving — and I had learned that a document doesn't need to be true. It needs to be boring. Nobody interrogates a boring document. Boredom is the best camouflage there is.

So: *Ines Halloran, Communications Technician, seconded to Northern Reconnaissance Division, Wewak.* Stamped with a company chop I'd borrowed from a drawer in the scheduling office on my last night of legitimate employment, along with a ream of carbon paper and, because I am not entirely without sentiment, the expedition's spare signal lamp.

My supervisor, Mr. Okere, would have called this theft. I preferred to think of it as severance. The company had stolen forty-eight hours from me and two men's lives with them; a signal lamp seemed like fair interest.

The ferry to Wewak left Madang on a Tuesday, which felt appropriate. Tuesdays are the day things go wrong quietly, without witnesses. The vessel itself was called the *Kumul Star*, though "star" was generous — she was a coastal trader with rust bleeding down her flanks like old wounds and a cargo deck stacked with rice sacks, fuel drums, and one very unhappy crocodile in a crate marked FRAGILE, which struck me as optimism of the highest order.

I bought passage under the name on the work order, which is to say my own name, which is to say the name I was trying to keep off certain ledgers in Port Moresby. There is a particular foolishness in hiding behind your own signature, but I'd learned something in those two days of static: running under an alias requires you to remember a lie, and I was already carrying too many truths to risk the extra weight.

The truth being: I had copied the concession files. All of them. They sat against my ribs in a waterproof satchel, close as a second heartbeat, and every time a wharfie jostled me on the gangplank I flinched like a man smuggling diamonds instead of paper. Permits. Survey amendments. A schedule of ore assays logged against coordinates that, when I'd laid them over the rail alignments at three in the morning with cold coffee going stale beside me, sat squarely on land the concession did not own.

I wasn't sure yet what that made me. Thief, maybe. Whistleblower, if I was feeling grand. Mostly I felt like a man who had opened a door he couldn't close and was now walking through the house with his hands full.

* * *

I found Marta Ondoua on the second morning out, standing at the stern rail watching the coastline unspool, arguing with the sea.

Not literally. She was arguing with a notebook — a fat, salt-swollen thing she held open against the wind, writing in it with the short furious strokes of someone taking dictation from an enemy. She was small, broad-shouldered, with hair cropped close enough to be practical and glasses fogged permanently by the humidity. When I came alongside she didn't look up.

"You're blocking my wind," she said.

"There's plenty of wind."

"There's exactly enough, and you've disturbed the distribution." She glanced at me then, once, the way a surveyor glances at terrain — assessing grade, load-bearing capacity, likelihood of collapse. Her eyes were the flat grey-green of shallow water over coral. "You're the radio man."

"I'm nobody."

"Nobody doesn't travel north on a forged secondment order." She said it without heat, almost kindly, the way you'd tell a man his fly was undone. "I read documents for a living. Or I used to, before the company decided reading them was a liability."

I should have walked away. Every instinct honed across four years of low visibility said walk away, smile, find another stretch of rail. But she'd said *forged* with the certainty of someone who had checked the chop against a reference, and there are only so many people in the world who can do that, and most of them are dangerous, and the dangerous ones are usually the only useful company available.

"Halloran," I said. "Communications technician."

"Marta Ondoua. Geologist." She turned back to the sea. "Or geologist-shaped hole in an expedition roster, depending on how recently you've looked at the manifest."

I had looked at the manifest. Repeatedly. It was part of my job — knowing who was upstream, whose check-in codes belonged to whose voice. And I remembered the roster changes in the final week before departure, the shuffling done from above my pay grade: a surveyor swapped out, a medic downgraded to first-aid certification, and one line item removed entirely. *M. Ondoua, geological survey — position eliminated.*

"I saw your name come off," I said.

Her pen stopped moving.

"Did you," she said, in a different voice. Softer. More careful. The voice of a woman recalculating.

"Position eliminated, seven days before departure. No replacement listed. Which is strange, because you don't send a survey expedition into unmapped basin country without a geologist unless—" I stopped myself. Unless was a door I'd been walking through houses with lately.

"Unless the geology isn't the point," she finished.

We stood there in the wind and the diesel stink and the distant complaint of the crocodile, two people discovering they had been reading the same book from opposite ends.

* * *

She told me her version over lunch, which aboard the *Kumul Star* meant tinned beef and rice eaten squatting against the wheelhouse wall, out of the rain squall hammering the awning.

She'd been contracted through the university in Lae — ten years in New Guinea highlands and basin country, sediment cores, alluvial mapping, the slow patient work of asking stone what it remembered. The Sepik contract had been the good kind: eighteen months, proper funding, a field assistant. Then, a week out, a telegram. Contract rescinded. Position eliminated. The financier's office had countersigned the cancellation personally — she'd seen the signature block, because she'd spent the following week doing what she did best, which was pulling records.

"Vandermeer," she said, saying the name the way you'd spit out a fishbone. "Aldous Vandermeer. You know the type. Owns half the concession through three layers of holding companies so his fingerprints never touch anything warmer than a proxy vote. He bumped me himself. Not his office. Him."

"Why does a financier care about one geologist?"

"Because a geologist looks at ground." She tapped her notebook. "Everyone else on that expedition looks at lines on paper — rail alignments, permit boundaries, coordinates. Paper can say anything. That's the whole beauty of paper. But I would have stood on the ground and taken samples, and the ground only says what's true." She pushed her glasses up. "They weren't surveying the Sepik Basin, Halloran. They were logging it. Ore assays against coordinates inside a concession boundary that — I've since confirmed — expires in nineteen days. After that, if they haven't proven continuous commercial extraction, the whole thing lapses and reverts."

"Lapses to whom?"

"To the Crown, pending refile. And here's the elegant part." She smiled, and it was not a warm smile. It was the smile of someone showing you a trap mechanism. "If the permits lapse clean — no fraud documented, no challenge lodged — Vandermeer's people refile with amended boundaries, corrected surveys, all properly stamped, and they own it forever. Legally. Immaculately. The fraud only matters if somebody writes it down before the clock runs out."

Nineteen days. I did the arithmetic against the ferry schedule, the river distances, the state of the expedition's last known position, and arrived at a number I didn't like.

"And the expedition?" I asked. "Sesay's team. Were they writing it down?"

"They were surveying closed rail lines through country their own permits didn't cover. Either Sesay knew and was documenting the discrepancy, or she didn't know and was about to trip over it." Marta looked at me steadily. "Either way, when the transmissions stopped—"

"You don't know they stopped."

"I know a daily check-in schedule, and I know today is Thursday." She ate a forkful of beef with terrible calm. "Do you?"

* * *

Here is what I told her, there in the lee of the wheelhouse while the rain came down like a curtain being drawn across the world: nothing. Here is what my face told her: considerably more than nothing, because Marta Ondoua had spent a decade reading ground, and faces are just ground that talks.

"You were on that schedule," she said. "You took the check-ins."

"I processed them."

"You knew their codes. Their positions. Their route." She set down her fork. "And now you're traveling their route, alone, on a forged order, with a bag you keep touching like a rosary. In my professional opinion, Mr. Halloran, you are either running away from something or toward it, and the compass bearing says toward."

I want to say I held out longer than I did. I want to report a hard negotiation, terms defended, secrets rationed like wartime sugar. What actually happened is that she said *compass bearing* and my hand went, without permission, to my jacket pocket, where the brass compass rode — my father's compass, engraved HALLORAN, shipped to me by a lawyer with a bill attached, the instrument that pointed faithfully and consistently six degrees west of where any honest needle should point, toward something the maps refused to name.

Marta watched my hand move and understood everything, because she was the sort of person who understood things before they were said, which is either a gift or a curse depending on how much you enjoy being known.

"You have documents," she said.

"I have copies of documents."

"Of the concession files."

"Among other items."

She exhaled slowly, and some of the armor came off her shoulders — not all of it, not even most of it, but enough that I could see the woman underneath, who was tired and frightened and had been carrying her own satchel of knowledge alone for weeks.

"Do you know what I did when they cancelled my contract?" she said. "I wrote to the company. Politely. Twice. Requesting clarification. Do you know what happened to my letters?"

"I can guess."

"They went into a file. I am, as of last month, in a file. Possibly several files." She laughed, short and dry. "Ten years I believed in institutions. Universities, geological societies, the whole apparatus of careful people doing careful work. I thought the system was slow but honest, like a glacier. And then I watched a man with a proxy vote erase me from an expedition with a telegram, and I understood the glacier was made of something else entirely."

"What else?"

"Paper," she said. "Just paper, all the way down. Which means paper is also how you cut it."

* * *

That evening the squall passed and the sky did that New Guinea thing where it turns the color of a bruise healing, all violet and gold, too beautiful for a coast with this much rust in it. I stood at the rail with the compass in my palm, lid open, needle trembling.

Six degrees west of true. Toward the spur. Toward whatever my father had built and someone had tried to bury, and toward — if Marta's arithmetic and my dread were both correct — whatever remained of Dr. Amara Sesay's expedition.

Marta came to stand beside me. She didn't ask about the compass. She was disciplined enough not to, and I was grateful, because I didn't yet have language for what it was: an inheritance shaped like an accusation, pointing at a failure I'd sworn never to repeat and now appeared to be walking directly into.

"The ferry puts in at Wewak Friday morning," she said. "There's a mission boat going up the Sepik on Sunday, if the priest is agreeable. He owes me a favor. I mapped the erosion around his station for free, back when I believed favors were free."

"You're coming, then."

"I'm coming." She said it like a fact of geology. "You have documents and a route and, evidently, a compass that lies. I have nineteen days of deadline and a working knowledge of every rock between here and the Karawari. Neither of us trusts the company, the government, the university, or — I'm guessing — each other. Which makes us perfectly matched, because we'll both be checking the other's work."

"That's your pitch? Mutual suspicion?"

"Mutual verification." She adjusted her glasses. "It's how science works. It's how survival works. Trust is just verification you've gotten lazy about."

I looked at her — this stranger who had been erased from a roster by the same hand that had likely erased eleven men from a river valley, who carried her fury the way I carried my father's compass, close and constant and pointing somewhere specific — and I thought about Mr. Okere ordering silence, protocol, forty-eight hours, the great soft machinery of institutions grinding individuals fine.

"All right," I said. "Verification."

"Wonderful." She snapped her notebook shut. "One condition. Whatever's in that satchel — when the time comes to use it, we decide together. No lone-hero theatrics. I've read how those end."

"So have I," I said. "Usually with the lone hero becoming a footnote in someone else's ledger."

"Then we understand each other."

We did, more or less, which on the *Kumul Star* counted as a treaty ratified.

* * *

Friday morning, Wewak materialized out of the haze — a scatter of tin roofs and coconut palms, war wreckage still rusting offshore where the sea had claimed it and hadn't bothered to digest. The crocodile was unloaded first, still complaining. Then the rice, the drums, and finally the passengers, blinking in the light like men leaving a cinema into a war.

On the wharf, pinned to the customs shed board among shipping notices and a weather warning, was a company circular. Fresh. Glossy. The concession's colors at the top.

*NOTICE TO TRAVELERS: The Sepik Basin remains CLOSED to unauthorized entry pending completion of licensed survey operations. All persons found within the concession boundary without valid documentation will be detained and remanded to Port Moresby.*

Below it, a reward notice. For information regarding missing survey personnel. Missing — not lost, not overdue. *Missing.* As if they were keys, or luggage, or a sum of money that had wandered off.

Marta read it twice, lips moving slightly, doing what she always did with documents: measuring them against the ground truth.

"'Unauthorized entry,'" she murmured. "They've closed the basin. Officially. Which means officially there's no one in there to be missing."

"Which means whoever finds them finds them alone," I said.

She looked at me then, really looked, and I watched her arrive at the same arithmetic I'd been avoiding since Madang: two of us, nineteen days, a closed basin, a company with colors and gunboats and patience, and a box of paper apiece that the whole apparatus would burn us to keep quiet.

"Yes," she said simply. "Alone together. Sunday's boat. Bring your compass, Halloran. I want to see which way it lies."

I told her I'd bring it.

What I didn't tell her — what I was only beginning to admit to myself, standing on that wharf with the reward notice fluttering at my back — was that the compass didn't lie at all. It pointed, steady and stubborn, at the one place in the Sepik Basin where my father's sins and the company's crimes and eleven missing men all converged into a single bearing.

Six degrees west of true.

Toward the spur.
