## 6. Upriver

Tobias found me before I found him, which should have been my first warning about the man.

I'd spent the morning at the Wewak waterfront doing what I do best, which is standing around looking like I belong somewhere I don't. The ferry had deposited me with a false work order, a borrowed company stamp, and the particular nausea of a man who has committed his first crime against paperwork and discovered it feels less like falling and more like stepping off a curb you didn't see. Marta had gone her own way — she said she'd be in touch through the mission station at Ambunti, and she said it in the tone of a woman who expected to be lied to by everyone including me, and was prepared to be pleasantly surprised.

The boatmen worked the shallows like vultures work a kill. Most of them wanted too much money for the Karawari run, and the ones who didn't want too much money wanted to know why a railroad engineer needed to go upriver alone, and I had learned somewhere in the last forty-eight hours that lying badly is worse than not lying at all.

Then there was Tobias.

He was sitting on an upturned fuel drum mending a net he clearly had no intention of finishing, a man of perhaps fifty with a face like weathered hardwood and eyes that did the arithmetic on me before I'd finished walking over. His boat was a long aluminum thing with an outboard older than my career, tied up among a dozen identical boats, distinguished only by the fact that it looked like the one most likely to actually arrive somewhere.

"You're Halloran," he said.

"I don't think we've met."

"The lawyer's letter came through the mission. Father Anselm read it out." He went back to his net. "You want the Karawari. You want to go fast and quiet and you don't want to tell anyone why. And you have no idea how much this costs."

"That's four statements," I said. "How many are wrong?"

"One." He folded the net away. "I know exactly how much it costs."

* * *

We left at first light, because Tobias held the firm conviction — which he shared with me at length and without invitation — that the river belongs to itself until mid-morning, and after that it belongs to whoever is watching it. He piloted us past the last of the palm-and-tin outskirts of Wewak's reach and into country where the map I carried became less a document and more a dare.

I had the expedition's route log copied into a waterproof notebook, Amara Sesay's own hand, precise as a surveyor's conscience: *Camp 4, left bank above confluence, bearing 214 from the split channel.* I had the concession maps, the ones with the decommissioned rail alignments drawn in faded ink. And I had the compass, riding in my breast pocket like a small brass accusation.

It had been pointing wrong for three days now. Consistently wrong. Not wobbling, not sulking the way cheap instruments sulk near iron ore — but holding a steady deviation of some eleven degrees east of true, as if there were something out here worth pointing at that no cartographer had bothered to name. I'd checked it against the sun, against the old rail bearings, against every honest reference I had. Eleven degrees. Every time. My father's handwriting in the margin of the expedition's base map read, in one place: *deviation not error — follow it.* I had decided to hate him a little more for writing that, since hating him for anything else required more information than I had.

The Sepik Basin doesn't announce itself. It seeps. By the second day the banks had dissolved into a green so total it felt less like scenery than like a condition, water braiding through sago and swamp oak, birds making sounds I refused to believe were produced by throats. Tobias ran the channels from memory, cutting the engine at stretches where the water went glass-still, poling us through with the unhurried competence of a man who had never once been lost and resented the concept.

"Surveyors came through here," he said, apropos of nothing, on the third morning. He nodded at a gap in the pandanus where someone had cut a sightline. "Six weeks back. Two boats. The doctor woman stood right where you're standing and argued with the men in the other boat."

"What men?"

"Company men." He said it the way you'd say *crocodiles*. "Blue and white flag on their aerials. They told her she'd gone off the permitted corridor." His mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "She told them the permitted corridor was a fiction. There was shouting. Then they all drank tea together, because that is how it works here."

"Which way did she go after?"

"Up the left branch." He paused, and I watched him decide whether to give me the rest. "The company men went the same way. Half an hour behind. Not following." He met my eyes. "Escorting."

* * *

The villages confirmed it in pieces, the way a wreck confirms itself plank by plank.

At Yentchen, a woman selling smoked fish remembered the survey party buying salt and quinine, and remembered the blue-and-white flags arriving the next day and asking different questions — not *where did they go* but *what did they carry out*. At a fishing camp on the middle bend, two brothers described men with the company colors walking the bank with instruments, shooting bearings toward the hills, well outside any corridor marked on my maps. One of them had paid for information in kerosene and cigarette cartons, generous as a bishop, and had asked whether the old railway track was still walkable anywhere north of the junction.

"The old railway," the younger brother said to me, spitting betel nut into the brown water. "Your people blew it up themselves. Culverts behind them. Why would anyone ask about a dead line?"

"Why indeed," I said, and felt the compass shift against my chest like something turning over in its sleep.

That night, moored in the lee of a mudbank with mosquitoes performing their evening oratorio, I laid the maps out under the torch and did the thing I am actually good at, the thing that got me ruined in Port Moresby and got me hired in the first place: I read the lines.

Railway alignments don't lie. A gradient is a confession; a curve radius is a signature. The decommissioned spur on the concession map ran from the junction at the old kilometer post 61 up toward the foothills, drawn with a casualness that fooled nobody who'd ever built track — the grades were impossible, the curves were suicidal, and the whole alignment had the look of a line drawn by someone who wanted it to look useless. But underneath the printed lie, faint as breath on glass, the paper showed ghost impressions of another drawing beneath. Someone had traced a real alignment, then printed the fake one over it.

And the compass, held flat over the map, pointed along the ghost line. Eleven degrees off true. Straight up the spur. Toward whatever my father had helped build and someone had very recently tried to erase.

"Tobias," I said. "How far to the junction at the old kilometer sixty-one?"

He was cleaning the outboard's carburetor with a rag and a patience I envied. "Two days, if the water holds. Three if it doesn't." He didn't look up. "And if you're thinking of walking north from there, I should tell you the company boat passed us yesterday going downriver. Blue and white. They counted our canoe twice."

"They counted us?"

"With binoculars. Slowly." He reassembled something with a click. "In this country, when a crocodile looks at you twice, you stop swimming at night."

* * *

The fourth day the river narrowed and the forest leaned in, and I understood why Sesay had logged her camps by bearing rather than landmark: landmarks here were negotiable. The whole basin rearranged itself seasonally, channels opening and closing like the water was editing its own manuscript. We passed a village — Tobias called it Wagu — where the headman came down to trade tobacco for batteries, and the conversation that followed was conducted half in Tok Pisin, half in gesture, and entirely over my head until Tobias translated afterward, his voice flatter than usual.

"The surveyors camped here nine days. The doctor woman was sick — fever. Her guides carried her. Before they left, men with the company flag came and asked the headman to say they had never come through." He fed the outboard more fuel. "They offered a radio. New, in a box. The headman said no."

"And then?"

"Then the offer changed shape." Tobias let that sit. "The headman sent his nephew to warn the next village instead. The nephew hasn't come back."

I thought about Amara Sesay logging her check-ins every evening at 1800, disciplined as a metronome, and how the transmissions had stopped mid-sentence — not faded, not drifted, *cut*, like a wire snipped. Company protocol said wait forty-eight hours. My supervisor had said wait longer. Everything I'd heard on this river said the protocol hadn't been designed for safety. It had been designed for delay. Forty-eight hours of silence was enough time for a survey party to become unfindable, for evidence to become rumor, for a mischarted campsite to become a permanent address.

"You knew the doctor woman?" I asked.

Tobias poled us around a fallen snag, considering. "Everyone knows Dr. Sesay. She treated children at Ambunti two seasons ago. She argues fairly and pays debts." A pause. "This is rarer than you would think."

We ran on. The afternoon went copper and then violet, and the compass sat against my sternum doing its eleven-degree trick, patient as a debt collector.

* * *

On the fifth day we reached the split channel, and Sesay's logbook bearing of 214 took us up the left branch into country the concession maps rendered as blank green wash — the cartographic equivalent of a shrug. Blank space on a map is never innocent. Somebody chose to leave it empty, and in my experience the emptier the space, the harder somebody had worked to keep it that way.

Tobias tied up at dusk below a bluff where the current ran slack, and while he cooked rice and tinned fish I climbed the bluff with the torch and the compass and stood in the dark listening to the basin breathe.

Northward, maybe eight kilometers as the crow flies — though nothing out here flew straight except the rain — the land rose into low hills. And along the base of those hills, invisible in the dark but absolutely present in the instrument, ran the pull. Eleven degrees east. The ghost alignment. The spur line my father had surveyed, built, abandoned, and dynamited behind him, according to a ledger I hadn't yet read but already dreaded.

I thought about what it meant that the company men had asked about the dead line. That culverts had been blown from behind, which is a strange thing to do to a railway unless you want to make sure nobody ever uses it again — or ever finds what's along it. That Sesay's party had been escorted upriver by men flying colors, and had then gone silent. That the permits expired in nineteen days, and Marta's voice in my head kept doing arithmetic I didn't want finished.

The wilderness was confirming the map's lies one village at a time, and each confirmation landed in my chest with the specific weight of my father's handwriting. He had known. Decades ago, taking bribes to bend alignments, he had known what the falsification was *for* — and he had shipped me a compass that pointed at the truth anyway, eleven degrees off honest, like a man confessing in code because he lacked the courage to confess in words.

"Halloran." Tobias's voice from below, low and even. "Come down. Now."

I scrambled down the bluff. He was standing in the boat, head cocked, listening. Far upstream, around the bend, an outboard engine — running without lights, throttled low, the sound of someone moving the way you move when you don't want to be heard but can't afford to stop.

"How long has it been there?"

"Twenty minutes. Holding position." He was already coiling the anchor line. "There's a side channel behind the bluff. Narrow. We go in dark, no engine, poles only, and we stay in it until past the shoals."

"And then?"

"Then tomorrow we reach the junction." He pushed the boat into the reeds and handed me a pole, and in the starlight his face was unreadable and completely clear at once. "Kilometer sixty-one. Where your dead railway begins."

We poled into the black channel without another word, the pursuing engine thrumming somewhere behind us like a pulse in the water, and the compass in my pocket pulled north-northeast, steady and insistent, toward the junction, the ghost line, and whatever my father had left buried at the end of it.
