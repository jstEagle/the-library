## 7. What the River Kept

There are two kinds of geologists in this world: the ones who fall in love with rocks because rocks don't lie, and the ones who fall in love with rocks because everything else does. I am the second kind. I learned it early, in a core shed outside Limbe, watching a company man reclassify a zinc seam as "anomalous" with the serene confidence of a priest declaring a miracle. The rock said one thing. The report said another. The rock got fired; the man got promoted.

So when the Halloran Expedition — that was its official name, which struck me as odd even then, since Halloran had never set foot on it — bumped me from the manifest eleven days before departure, I did what any self-respecting scientist does with an insult. I kept the paperwork.

* * *

The substitution memo arrived on a Tuesday, typed on letterhead so heavy it could stop a bullet. *Due to logistical constraints, Dr. M. Ondoua's berth has been reallocated. The expedition thanks her for her contributions to the preliminary survey design.* My contributions to the preliminary survey design were four hundred hours and the only reason anyone knew where the Karawari tributary wasn't. But fine. Logistical constraints. These things happen.

What happened next was that a man named Aldous Vane joined the expedition in my place. Not a geologist. Not a surveyor. A "logistics consultant" whose photograph in the annual report of Meridian Pacific Concessions showed him shaking hands with a minister whose ministry had just approved — you'll forgive the coincidence — a Meridian Pacific concession.

I sat with that for a while. Then I did what I should have done at the core shed in Limbe, and didn't.

I started pulling thread.

* * *

Paper trails are like rivers. They look calm from the bank, but underneath there's a current that will take your legs out, and the trick is never to assume the surface tells you anything about the depth. Vane's paper trail ran wide and shallow — consultancy fees, travel vouchers, a Wewak hotel account — all of it boring, all of it designed to be boring. Boredom is camouflage. Nobody audits a bore.

But rivers have tributaries, and so did Vane. A payment to a firm called Kairuku Land Services. Another to a "cultural liaison office" in Angoram that shared a post box with a security contractor. A third, my favorite, to something called the Sepik Basin Heritage Trust, which turned out to be a shelf company registered to a lawyer who also happened to be the brother-in-law of the lands officer who countersigned the concession's boundary maps.

I spread it across my landlady's dining table in Wewak — she'd gone to Lae for a month and left me the key and a warning about the termites — and by the third night the table looked like a murder board, if murder boards came with contour lines and cadastral overlays. Which, in a sense, this one did.

Here is what the river gave up when I dragged it.

* * *

The concession permits — the ones authorizing Meridian Pacific to survey, sample, and log along the upper Karawari — were issued under a provincial instrument that expired nineteen days after the expedition's scheduled departure. Nineteen days. I checked the date three times, then a fourth, because arithmetic that convenient makes you distrust your own eyes. It held. Whatever Meridian intended to do in that basin, they had a legal window narrower than a coffin lid, and they'd spent real money — Vane's salary, the bribes dressed as heritage fees, the security contract — to squeeze through it.

Now, a legitimate operation looks at a window like that and plans accordingly: mobilize fast, sample efficiently, file extensions early. What did the expedition's own logistics show? Equipment manifests weighted toward bulk extraction gear no survey needs. Fuel orders triple the survey's burn rate. And — this was the detail that made me put down my tea and laugh, the ugly kind of laugh — a purchase order for forty kilometers of demarcation ribbon and two thousand hardwood survey pegs stamped with the Meridian Pacific crest.

You don't peg boundaries for a survey. You peg them for a claim.

* * *

I want to be precise here, because precision is the only weapon people like me get, and I've watched too many good arguments die of vagueness. So: the permits covered Block 4 of the Sepik Basin concession, defined by coordinates in Schedule C of the provincial instrument. I overlaid Schedule C against the actual ground using the survey baselines I myself had drafted before being so graciously "reallocated." The overlap was partial. Roughly sixty percent of the territory the expedition's route threaded through — including every village landing Tobias-class boatmen use on the middle Karawari, including the sago stands, including ground where people have buried their grandmothers for longer than Australia has existed — lay outside the permitted block entirely.

Meridian Pacific was not surveying its concession. It was surveying everyone else's, and logging ore reserves as it went, so that when the permits lapsed and they refiled — clean, corrected, generous with the ministers — they would arrive at the negotiating table holding reserve estimates for land they had never legally touched, dressed up as due diligence.

The survey wasn't a survey. It was reconnaissance for a land grab, timed to finish before the law caught up with it.

And the expedition — Sesay's expedition, with its honest instruments and its honest daily radio checks — was the fig leaf. Or possibly the fall guy. With men like Vane aboard, possibly both.

* * *

I'll admit something here, because a paper trail this ugly deserves a witness who admits her own failures. I found the first three threads months ago and did nothing. I told myself the expedition was legitimate science wrapped around whatever Vane was doing, that Sesay's data would stand on its own merits, that my job was rocks and not politics. That is the lie every one of us tells, the comfortable one, the one that lets you keep your berth and your salary and your clean hands. Rocks don't lie, but geologists are very good at lying to themselves about rocks.

Then the expedition went quiet above the Karawari, and the comfortable lie stopped being available.

I heard about the missed check-in from a radio operator I know at the Wewak post — small town, big ears. Forty-eight hours, protocol says. Company says wait. And I sat in my dead landlady's dining room surrounded by the anatomy of a fraud, listening to static on a borrowed receiver, doing the arithmetic I'd been avoiding for weeks.

Nineteen days until the permits lapse. If nothing surfaces before then — no testimony, no documents, no journalist with a spine and a deadline — Meridian refiles clean. The fraud evaporates. The villages become "vacant crown land" on some future map, the way inconvenient places always do. And the men who engineered it will shake hands at a conference in Singapore while I'm giving papers to rooms of twelve people about fluvial geomorphology.

Righteous anger is a luxury good, I've decided. It keeps you warm for an evening and costs you everything afterward unless you convert it into something with a schedule attached. So I converted it. I made lists. Who prints in this country and isn't owned? Who at the mines department still answers letters? Which provincial officers owe Vane nothing? I wrote letters. I made copies — three sets, three hiding places, because I grew up in a country where copies have a life expectancy measured in house fires.

And then, on the fourth night, sitting under the mosquito coil with the whole rotten architecture spread out before me, I understood the part that mattered most, the part I'd been circling without naming:

They hadn't bumped me off the expedition because I was inconvenient. Plenty of people were inconvenient. They bumped me because I was the one person on the roster who had read Schedule C closely enough to notice that the survey route didn't stay inside it. Vane's job was to make sure nobody aboard ever did the overlay I'd done at my landlady's table. I had done it six weeks before departure. Somebody read my preliminary report. Somebody did the same arithmetic I was doing now, in reverse, and reached for a pen.

That's not paranoia. That's inference, and inference is the whole trade.

* * *

Which left the question of what a bumped geologist with a murder board and nineteen days actually does.

Waiting was the institutional answer. Wait for the inquiry. Wait for the company's statement. Wait for the authorities to assemble a committee, which would meet, deliberate, and produce a document recommending further deliberation. I have watched that machine work before. It grinds fine and slow, and by the time the flour comes out, the bread's been eaten.

The other answer was upriver.

I knew roughly where the expedition had been when it went silent — the last logged position, a camp above the Karawari confluence, though the coordinates in the radio log never quite matched the terrain the way they should have, a discrepancy I'd flagged in my preliminary report and been thanked for and ignored over. I knew the river. I'd spent two field seasons on that water. I knew which boatmen would go and which would laugh at me, and I knew that a woman traveling alone with a waterproof case full of documents reads, on certain stretches of that river, as either a missionary or a problem, and that both identities have uses.

I also knew — this is the part I keep turning over, the part that keeps me awake past the mosquito coil's last smoke — that somewhere between the company's silence and the river's silence, someone else had done the same arithmetic I had. The radio operator mentioned it casually, the way small-town operators mention everything: a scheduling clerk from the Port Moresby office, name of Halloran, had resigned by telegram and taken the northbound ferry under a work order nobody in the office had authorized.

Halloran. The expedition carried his name and none of his presence, and now the absent man had moved. Either he was running from something — clerks who resign by telegram usually are — or he'd read the same manifests I'd read and drawn the same conclusion: that the silence upriver wasn't weather.

Two people, independently, pulling the same thread out of the same fabric. In my experience that's either coincidence or conspiracy, and I no longer believe in the first kind.

* * *

I packed that night. Not much: instruments, the document copies, a change of clothes, the malaria tablets I'd been rationing out of habit, and my father's machete, which is sentimental and also the single most useful object ever forged. I wrote two letters — one to the journalist in Madang whose byline I'd been circling for a week, one to the mines department man who owed me a favor from a landslide inquiry — and posted them with instructions to open them if I didn't come back inside a month. Insurance. You don't walk into a river carrying the only copy of the truth. The river keeps things. That's rather the point of rivers.

At dawn I stood on the Wewak waterfront while the fishing boats came in, and I looked north up the coast toward the mouth of the Sepik, gray-green and patient behind its wall of mangrove, and I thought about what it keeps. Silt, certainly. Logs, canoes, the occasional crocodile's dinner. And now, somewhere up in that brown immensity, an expedition's silence, a fraud's paperwork, and one disgraced scheduling clerk moving against the current with God knows what plan and God knows what chance.

Nineteen days. Eighteen now, by the time the ferry whistled.

I bought my ticket, and I went to find out whether the man whose name was on the expedition was worth the ink it was written in — or whether I'd have to save the basin twice, once from the company and once from him.

The ferry left on the morning tide. So did I.
