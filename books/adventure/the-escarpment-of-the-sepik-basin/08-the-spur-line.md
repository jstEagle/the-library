## 8. The Spur Line

The compass had been lying to me for three days, or telling me the truth in a language I hadn't learned yet.

Every morning I'd take it out on the bank of the river while Tobias smoked and pretended not to watch me, and every morning the needle swung off true north by a stubborn eleven degrees and settled, quivering, toward a wall of green that no map in my possession acknowledged as anything but empty country. I'd checked it against the sun. I'd checked it against the river's course, which Tobias swore ran true to the northwest like a drunk running for the last ferry. The compass didn't care what the river did. It wanted to go its own way, and it wanted me to come along.

"You keep looking at that thing like it owes you money," Tobias said on the third morning.

"It might," I said. "It belonged to my father."

He nodded as if this explained everything, which, in his cosmology, it probably did. Tobias had a gift for accepting the supernatural without letting it interrupt breakfast. When I'd told him I was following a dead man's compass up an unmapped tributary to find a survey expedition that had gone silent, he'd scratched his chin, named a price with a surcharge for "trouble," and asked whether I wanted to leave at dawn or after dawn. That was the whole of his skepticism. I had decided then and there that I liked him better than most men I'd worked with, which is a low bar, but he cleared it without breaking stride.

We left the boat at a bend where the Karawari narrowed and the water went shallow over gravel, because Tobias said the channel ahead would eat a propeller and possibly the rest of us. From there it was walking. He came with me as far as the first ridge — "to see you don't drown in a puddle" — and then settled down under a pandanus to wait, having extracted a promise that I'd be back by dark or he'd assume I'd been eaten and charge my estate accordingly.

So it was alone that I crested the ridge and saw, for the first time, the thing the compass had been pointing at all along.

A cutting. A man-made cutting through the forest canopy, straight as a ruled line, running off to the northeast until the haze took it. Trees grew along its edges like spectators leaning over a fence, but the line itself was open, floored with old ballast gone grey and furred with moss, and every few hundred meters a rotting telegraph pole leaned at the angle of a man who has given up arguing. It was a railway. Or it had been. Nobody had drawn it on any map I'd seen, and I had spent two years of my life staring at maps of this country trying to make them confess something useful.

I stood there longer than I should have, doing arithmetic I didn't want to do.

My father had been a railway engineer in New Guinea. That was the one fact about him I'd carried out of childhood intact, the way you carry a splinter too deep to dig out: he built lines, and then he left, and the leaving was the part everyone remembered. What he'd built, and where, and for whom — those details had been scrubbed out of our family history so thoroughly that when the lawyer shipped me the brass compass after his death, with my surname engraved on the case like a brand on livestock, my first thought had been that even his bequest was an accusation. *You are mine and you don't know what that costs.*

Now I was standing on top of what he'd built, and nobody knew it existed.

I walked down into the cutting. The ballast crunched under my boots, a sound so ordinary and so wrong in the middle of primary rainforest that it made the hairs on my arms stand up. Stone doesn't belong here. Someone had quarried it, hauled it, spread it, graded it, and then walked away and let the forest pretend none of it happened. The forest is a diligent liar. It had already swallowed the rails themselves — I found only stubs where they'd been pulled up, and one length of track half-buried near what might have been a siding, rusted to the color of dried blood, a sapling growing straight up through the fishplate bolts as though the tree were making a point.

But the culverts. The culverts were the thing.

There was a stream crossing maybe four hundred meters in, and where the line crossed it there should have been a culvert — a pipe, an arch, something to let the water through. Instead there was a crater. Not erosion, not collapse. Dynamite. I've blown enough things up in my career, legitimate and otherwise, to know what a shaped charge does to masonry, and this had been done by someone who knew their work. The culvert hadn't failed. It had been executed. And the debris — I walked the streambed and checked — the debris had been thrown backward, away from the line, into the cut face behind it. Blown from the front. From downstream. Whoever did it had stood where I was standing and made sure the wreckage fell in a way that read, to any future inspector, like a flood had done it.

Except floods don't throw masonry uphill.

I crouched at the edge of the crater for a long time, and the wry voice in my head — the one that narrates my life back to me when things get bad, the one that kept me sane through the inquiry and the bankruptcy and the two years of scheduling other people's radio calls in Port Moresby — that voice said, quietly: *They didn't just abandon this line, Ines. They un-built it. Somebody paid good money to make sure this railway could never be remembered.*

And underneath that voice, older and less welcome, another one said: *Your father helped build this. Your father helped bury it. And now you're walking it in reverse.*

* * *

I followed the cutting northeast for the rest of the morning, and the further I went, the more the place felt less like a ruin and more like a wound that someone had tried to close badly.

At a second stream crossing, same story: culvert destroyed, debris thrown back, the damage dressed up with a scatter of river stones that someone had hauled in to simulate scouring. At a third, the demolition had been lazier — or later, or done in more of a hurry — and a chunk of the arch still stood, with drill marks visible in the fracture face. I put my thumb in one of the drill holes and thought about the man who'd set that charge. Had he known what he was erasing? Had he been told it was just salvage, just clearing an old alignment? Or had he known exactly, and taken the money anyway?

I knew how that question got answered. I'd answered it myself, once, in a smaller key. You never take the money knowing. You take it *not quite* knowing, and you let the not-quite do the work, and ten years later you're Ines Halloran, disgraced, reading other people's maps because nobody will let you draw your own.

The cutting climbed gently through the afternoon, following the contour the way a well-engineered line does, and twice I passed evidence that the abandonment had stages. There were old camp platforms — terraces cut into the slope, postholes still visible — that predated the demolitions, and then newer, smaller clearings that postdated them, as if people had come back after the killing of the railway and used its corpse for something else. Survey markers, some of them. Fresh ones. Plastic flagging tape in concession orange, tied to saplings, dated in marker pen: dates within the last two months. The company's colors. Marta Ondoua had described them to me on the ferry with the flat contempt she reserved for institutions: orange tape, orange caps, orange everything, "like safety equipment for people who intend to cause an accident."

Someone had surveyed this ghost line recently. Carefully. Along an alignment that officially did not exist.

That was when the unease in my stomach stopped being atmospheric and became informational. Because here is what I know about railways, and about paperwork, and about the particular sin my father's ledger — if it existed, if Sesay's expedition had actually found it — would describe: you don't falsify an alignment on paper unless the real alignment matters. You don't dynamite culverts behind you unless the route itself is evidence. This cutting wasn't a forgotten mistake. It was a confession somebody had tried to shred, and the shredder had missed a strip, and now the strip was leading me — eleven degrees off true north, patient as guilt — toward whatever it was my father couldn't destroy.

* * *

Near dusk I found the boot prints.

They were pressed into a patch of soft ground where the cutting dipped through a seep, and they were unmistakably fresh — the edges still sharp, the mud at the bottom of each print still dark with moisture, no rain on them since. Rain comes every evening here; it had come last night, a brief hammering shower that Tobias and I had sat under the tarp listening to. These prints were from after the rain. Within hours. Maybe six, maybe eight.

Two sets. One large, one smaller, the smaller one favoring the left foot — a shorter stride on that side, the print deeper on the right, the gait of someone carrying weight unevenly or nursing an injury. I knelt there with my heart going like a telegraph key and read the ground the way I'd been trained to read drawings: size nine boots, military pattern, and trail shoes, women's or a small man's, left ankle compromised.

Sesay had twisted her ankle on the second day out; her check-ins had mentioned it, offhand, the way leaders mention things they don't want to sound worried about. *Dr. S reports the ankle is holding.* I'd logged those words myself, typed them into the schedule sheet, filed them under ROUTINE. Now here was her stride printed in the mud of a railway that wasn't supposed to exist, moving northeast, toward whatever the compass wanted.

I stood up too fast and the cutting tilted. Eight hours. They were eight hours ahead of me, at most, and injured, and being pursued — I was sure of the pursuit the way you're sure of weather before it breaks; Calloway's security operation didn't send two-man teams strolling through swampland for their health — and I was standing in their footprints like a man reading a letter addressed to him that had arrived too late to matter.

*Move,* said the wry voice, dropping the wryness entirely.

* * *

I found the supply cache twenty minutes later, in the last grey light.

It was tucked into the lee of the standing culvert fragment — the lazy demolition, the one with the drill marks — and it had been burned. Deliberately, thoroughly burned: tins burst and blackened, fabric reduced to a crust of char, a melted lump that had been a radio battery, the whole mess doused afterward with something that smelled faintly of diesel even now. But fire is a careless archivist. At the edge of the burn I found a corner of canvas that had been sheltered by a stone, and stitched into it in thread, in a hand I recognized from the expedition's requisition forms, were the initials **A.S.** and a number: **7-11-KARAWARI**. Camp seven-eleven. The camp above the tributary. The camp whose transmission had died mid-sentence in my headset while I sat in a room in Wewak with my hand hovering over the transmit key, obeying protocol, obeying silence, obeying a supervisor who told me to wait forty-eight hours while the people who trusted my schedule disappeared.

They had cached supplies here and moved on. Someone else had found the cache first and burned it — not to destroy them, I understood suddenly, but to *starve* them forward. Burn a cache and the people who need it can't stop. Can't regroup. Can't turn around. You don't burn supplies to kill; you burn them to herd.

I sat down beside the burnt cache in the dark, because my legs informed me we were done negotiating, and I ate the last of my own rice and looked at the compass by torchlight, cupped in my hand like something alive.

Eleven degrees off north. Same bearing it had held since the ferry, since Wewak, since the night in Port Moresby when I'd first laid it against my father's marginal annotations on the closed-line maps and felt the past put its hand on the back of my neck. My father had owned this compass. My father had walked this cutting — I was certain of it now, certain in the way you're certain of a signature — and somewhere along this line, between the murdered culverts and the junction the needle kept insisting on, he had hidden something he couldn't bring himself to mail. A confession. Duplicates. Whatever a man buries when he has finally understood the shape of what he sold.

And the expedition had come this way. His route, their route, my route — three generations of people walking the same failure in different directions.

I turned the compass so the needle pointed at my chest instead of the junction, and I said, out loud, to the dark and the dead and the eight-hours-ahead:

"All right. I'm coming. Try to stay alive until I get there."

Then I packed the canvas scrap with the initials, because it was evidence and because it was hers, and I started walking, because the prints were fresh and the night was short and somewhere ahead of me, in the static between the ridges, I was almost certain I could hear — faint, rhythmic, three pulses and a pause — a radio key.
