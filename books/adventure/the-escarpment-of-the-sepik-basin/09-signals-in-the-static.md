## 9. Signals in the Static

The trouble with listening to static for six nights running is that it stops being noise and starts being a language. You begin to hear intention in it. A hiss with a rising edge sounds like weather coming down off the mountains. A flat, dead band sounds like somebody's battery dying. And every so often — this is the part I'd have mocked, a month ago, from the safety of an office chair in Port Moresby — you hear nothing at all and it sounds like someone choosing not to speak.

I had made camp where the spur line crossed a creek whose name Tobias gave as something I could pronounce only if I broke my jaw first. He called it *the cold one* in Tok Pisin, which was accurate enough. The water came down off the escarpment at a temperature that made your teeth ache, and I'd rigged the radio under a lean-to of sago fronds because rain was a certainty and electronics up here drowned faster than missionaries' dogs.

Tobias had gone to sleep in the boat, or pretended to. He'd made it clear on the second day that whatever I did after dark with that box of wires was my own business, and that if it attracted soldiers he would be gone before I finished saying the word *soldier*. Fair enough. I hadn't hired a conscience. I'd hired a boat.

So there I was, alone with the set, headphones on, running through the expedition's scheduled frequencies one more time out of what I told myself was diligence and what was actually just the inability to stop. Sesay's check-in codes were burned into me by now. Three days of transmissions, then silence, then my resignation, then all of this — the ferry, Marta, the villages, the burned cache, the boot prints going up the spur line like a sentence written by someone in a hurry. I kept replaying her last transmission in my head. *Position confirmed against* — and then nothing. Mid-sentence. As if a hand had come down over the microphone.

Company protocol said wait forty-eight hours. I'd waited forty-eight hours. Then I'd done something better than waiting: I'd stolen the concession files and gotten on a boat. Which meant that everything I was doing now was, by any legal definition, trespassing on my own former employer's operation while carrying documents I had no right to. There was no version of this where I went back to Port Moresby and explained myself and got my old life back. I understood that somewhere around the third village, when the men with the concession company's colors watched us pass and wrote something down. The invisible man I'd been hiding as since the scandal — the engineer nobody remembered, the name nobody checked — that man had died somewhere on the Karawari. What was left was a person who did things that could be traced.

I should have found that terrifying. Mostly I found it restful.

The frequency was one of the expedition's emergency channels, the ones printed on the laminated card in the spare kit. I'd been sweeping them nightly without expectation, the way you check a lock you know is bolted. On the sixth night, at something past eleven, between the second and third frequencies, the static changed.

Not much. A texture. The way a room changes when someone else is breathing in it.

I sat very still. The creek ran. Somewhere across the water a frog made a sound like a saw going through wet wood. And under the static, faint enough that I doubted it even as I heard it, there was a voice. Or the ghost of one. Two syllables, clipped, gone.

I tuned by fractions, the way my father must have taught me once, before teaching me anything became more than he could bear. The voice swam up and sank again. I marked the dial position with a grease pencil, swept away, swept back. It was still there. Someone was transmitting on low power, probably a handheld with a dying battery, probably from inside terrain that ate signal the way this basin ate everything — roads, railways, reputations, men.

Then, clear as a bell for three full seconds, the way a gap in cloud lets sunlight through onto one specific tree:

"—Sesay actual, code group Kestrel, we are moving—"

And it was gone again.

Kestrel. I knew the code groups. I'd built the schedule; the schedule was mine; the code groups were the one part of the job I'd taken seriously because they were the only part that mattered if things went wrong. Kestrel was the expedition's distress group. Not "we are lost." Not "we need supplies." Kestrel meant *we are being pursued.* It was the group Dr. Amara Sesay herself had insisted on adding to the card, over the objection of the company liaison, who'd thought it alarmist. She'd looked at him with an expression I now understood completely and said, *In this country, alarmist is a compliment.*

Moving toward the spur line. That was the other thing the fragment gave me, or half gave me — *we are moving*, and then the carrier dropped. But I'd spent two days with the maps and two more days walking the ground, and there was only one direction anyone in that country would move if they wanted cover: up the spur, into the ghost railway's cuttings, where the culverts and the dynamited embankments made a landscape that didn't exist on any map the company held. Where, incidentally, I happened to be sitting.

They were coming toward me. And behind them, judging by the low power and the broken transmission and the fact that Sesay — Sesay, who checked in on schedule to the minute, who treated radio discipline like a religion — was transmitting in fragments at night: someone was following.

*

I woke Tobias. This took diplomacy. His method of sleeping was total, and his method of being woken was to open one eye and assess whether the person doing the waking was worth the effort of consciousness.

"Radio," I said. "The doctor. She's alive."

He considered this. "Alive is good."

"She's being chased."

"Chased is bad."

"I need to answer her."

Now both eyes were open, and the look in them was the look of a man recalculating a fare. "You want to talk on the radio. At night. With your voice. So that anyone with a receiver knows exactly where you are." He said it not as a question but as a man reading back coordinates to confirm a fix.

"That's the general shape of it."

"Ines." He used my name maybe twice a week, and always as a preface to disappointment. "There is a reason people do not do this."

"There is a reason people don't jump rivers either, and yet here we are, in the jumping business." I crouched by the fire pit, which I'd already banked down to coals, and laid it out, because Tobias deserved the arithmetic even if he was going to refuse it. "If I stay silent, she keeps moving blind. She doesn't know the spur line. She doesn't know which cuttings are passable and which are dynamited into ravines. She's injured — I'm guessing, but the transmission pattern says injured, because Sesay walking healthy doesn't transmit in two-second bursts. If Calloway's men catch her in the open, this ends, and everything she's carrying ends with her."

"And if you talk?"

"If I talk, whoever's tracking her hears me too. They get a bearing. Maybe a rough one, maybe a good one. But they already have her trail — boot prints, a burned cache, six days of pursuit. My voice doesn't tell them much they don't know. It tells *her* something she doesn't know: that there's high ground ahead, that the third cutting is passable, that there's fresh water at the junction, and that when she gets there, someone will be waiting with a fire and a radio and no intention of handing her to anybody."

Tobias poked the coals with a stick, which was his version of deliberation. "You sound like a man who has decided and wants me to agree so it becomes two men's decision instead of one fool's."

"That's exactly what I'm doing."

"Good. Honesty." He threw the stick into the dark. "Answer her. But make it short. And afterward we move the camp, because I am not sleeping in the place where a white man talked on a radio."

*

Here is what the rulebooks say, and I mean all of them — military, maritime, the company's own procedures manual, which I'd read out of boredom during the monsoon and remembered out of spite. You do not break silence to an unknown station. You do not confirm your position. You use coded brevity, you keep transmissions under five seconds, and above all you never, never transmit from a location you intend to occupy. Every rule exists because some man died learning it, and I knew that, and I recited the whole catechism to myself while I warmed the set's valves and waited for the hour when the atmosphere would be kindest.

And then I thought about Sesay's voice saying *position confirmed against* and stopping mid-word, and about the box of documents those people had carried through a swamp on their backs, and about my father, who had learned every rule of his profession and broken the one that mattered, quietly, for money, thirty years ago, and left the bill for me to find.

There are inheritances you can refuse. This one I couldn't. Not because of blood — blood had nothing to do with it, or less than I'd have liked. Because I was the only station on that frequency. Because competence that arrives too late to help anyone is just a hobby.

I keyed the mic at 0340, when the ionosphere settles and the band goes quiet as a held breath.

"Sesay actual, Sesay actual, this is Halloran, expedition radio schedule. Authenticate: November, Kestrel, nine-seven. I say again, this is Halloran. I am on the spur line at the junction, third cutting south is passable, fourth is blown. Water at the junction. I have your route logs and the concession files. You are expected. Out."

Eleven seconds. An eternity, by the book. Enough for a direction-finder to chew on, if one was close enough and awake enough. I put the handset down and discovered my hands were shaking, which annoyed me, because fear is fine but fear you can't use is just noise.

Nothing answered. Static, patient and complete. I swept the band for twenty minutes, telling myself that no news meant she'd heard, that a woman being hunted doesn't linger on the air, that silence was discipline and discipline was good.

Then, at 0412, four seconds, low power, a voice worn down to wire:

"Halloran. Authenticate received. Count: seven souls. We are—" and the carrier folded, and the dark took the rest.

Seven souls. The expedition had been eleven. I did the arithmetic I didn't want to do and got four, and then I made myself stop, because four was a number for daylight, and daylight was hours away, and there was work between here and there.

*

We moved the camp before dawn, exactly as promised. Tobias carried the radio; I carried the kit and the compass, which rode in my shirt pocket the way it had since the spur line began, its needle lying off true north by the same stubborn few degrees it had held since Wewak, pointing at the junction like a dog pointing at a bird. I had stopped pretending the compass was a coincidence around the time I found the boot prints. Whatever my father had buried out here — in the ground, in the ledgers, in the geometry of a railway he'd helped build and then helped erase — the instrument he'd carried was still pointing at it, and now, apparently, so was I.

By full light we were two hundred meters from the old camp, dug in above the third cutting with sightlines down the overgrown rail bed in both directions. I built a small fire, smokeless, dry wood, screened with green fronds, because seven exhausted people coming out of the bush at dawn deserved hot water and I was past the point where caution and decency could both be maximized. You pick a ratio and live with it.

Tobias watched me lay out cups — actual cups, tin ones, seven of them, counted out like ammunition — and shook his head slowly.

"You have known these people how long?" he asked.

"I've spoken to most of them twice on the radio."

"And you are counting cups."

"It's what I'm for," I said. "Schedules. Counts. Making sure the numbers arrive where they're supposed to." I turned a cup in my hands. "Everything else I've ever been good for fell apart. But I can keep a count."

He grunted, which from Tobias was a paragraph.

All morning I listened. The band stayed empty. I swept the emergency frequencies, then the secondary ones, then, in a moment of weakness I'm not proud of, the company's own working channels, where I heard exactly what I expected: routine traffic, supply requests, a supervisor in Wewak complaining about diesel. Nothing about the spur line. Nothing about a search. Which was worse than chatter, if you thought about it. Organizations hunting something don't discuss it on frequencies strangers might hear. They go quiet, and they move, and the first thing you learn of them is the thing itself.

Around midday I caught movement on the rail bed below — and had my rifle halfway up before my brain finished identifying it, which tells you what six days in that country had done to me. Not men. Birds. A pair of hornbills, absurd and prehistoric, working their way along the old ballast like commuters. I lowered the rifle and felt the shaking start in my hands again and this time let it run its course.

Because here was the thing I'd finally admitted, somewhere in the fourth cup-counting hour of waiting: there was no invisible way out anymore. I'd understood it intellectually on the ferry, understood it practically when I copied the files, but it landed differently at noon above a dynamited cutting with seven tin cups lined up in a row. Whatever came up that rail bed — Sesay and her survivors, or Calloway's men, or both, in whichever order — I was a fixed point now. Marked on the air, marked on the ground, marked in the memory of every villager who'd watched the foreigner with the radio ask questions about the survey party. The man who'd come to the Sepik Basin to be unfindable had spent eleven seconds making himself the loudest object in it.

And the strange thing, the thing I kept turning over like a stone with something alive under it, was that I didn't regret the eleven seconds. Not one of them. For the first time since the scandal, since the debt, since the long gray years of being carefully no one, I was exactly where I had decided to be, doing the thing I had decided to do, and the fear that came with it was clean. It had edges. It pointed somewhere.

My father had spent his last years, I suspected, trying to buy his way back to a moment like that — a moment where a choice cost something and he paid it. The ledger in the files suggested he'd failed. The compass suggested he'd tried. I sat above the cutting with the brass instrument warm in my pocket and understood that I was holding both answers at once, and that which one won depended entirely on what I did next.

At 1547, the band breathed.

"Halloran. Sesay actual." Her voice was wrecked — gravel and fever and the particular flatness of a person conserving everything, including hope. "Two hours. We see your smoke. We are followed at distance. Do not light the fire after dark." A pause, long enough that I thought the battery had finally died. Then, quieter, almost amused, the first human note in six days of static:

"You're late, Halloran. Out."

I laughed alone above the cutting, a short ugly laugh with no humor in it, and started counting cups again, and checked the compass, and watched the rail bed to the south where the shadows were beginning to pool between the sleepers like water finding its level.

Two hours. Followed at distance.

I banked the fire, loaded the rifle, and went down to meet the train.
