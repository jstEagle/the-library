## 10. Company Men

There is a particular pleasure in a well-kept ledger, and I have kept mine for eleven years: every man hired, every man paid off, every man disappeared, each entry dated and initialed in the same blue ink so that any auditor could trace my work from first payment to final silence without ever once encountering the word *violence*. I do not use that word. It is imprecise. Violence is what happens to other people's operations — the sloppy ones, the ones run by men who drink at lunch and leave witnesses. What I do is *resolution*. A problem is identified, costed, and resolved. The ledger balances. The concession proceeds.

I was thinking about this on the morning of the ninth day, standing on the veranda of the company house at Angoram with a cup of terrible coffee, watching two of my trackers wash blood out of a canoe in the river shallows. They were thorough about it. I had trained them myself, back when training was still part of the budget, before the financier decided that security was a line item that could be optimized. He optimized it down to me and nine men and a radio set, which is why we are having this conversation at all — because an operation that should have required four professionals now requires one professional doing the work of four, and even I have limits.

The coffee was terrible because the good machine had been allocated to the survey camp, and the survey camp no longer existed as an accounting entity. That is the phrase I used in my report. *No longer exists as an accounting entity.* My superiors appreciate that sort of thing. It lets them read the morning brief over breakfast.

* * *

Let me reconstruct the situation as I presented it, because I presented it twice — once by coded cable to Port Moresby, once by voice on the evening schedule, when the financier himself came on the line. He does not usually take the evening schedule. He takes the Tuesday summary and the quarterly review. When a man like that changes his routine, you learn something about him, and what I learned is that he is frightened, and that frightened men make decisions quickly, and that quick decisions are the raw material of my profession.

The facts were these.

Fact one: the survey expedition under Dr. Amara Sesay had not been lost. This was the official position — *lost, presumed drowned or taken by illness, regrettable, the Basin is unforgiving* — and it was a good position, well-constructed, the kind of story that explains itself. Rivers drown people. Fevers take them. Nobody asks follow-up questions about a river. But the camp above the Karawari had not been overwhelmed by water or fever. It had been burned, selectively, by men who knew which crates mattered, and Dr. Sesay had walked out of it with her two guides and a document case that she had gone back into the fire to retrieve. I know she went back into the fire because one of my men saw her do it, and reported it, and I entered it in the ledger with a note: *subject prioritizes documents over own survival. Reclassify from nuisance to threat.*

That reclassification is important. A nuisance runs away and writes an angry letter. A threat reaches the coast. Sesay had spent six days moving west along the old alignments instead of south toward the patrol posts, which told me two things: she knew the country better than her file suggested, and she was not running. She was *delivering*. There is a difference, and the difference is everything. A woman who is running can be caught anywhere. A woman who is delivering has a destination, a schedule, and probably a contact, and must be intercepted before the last leg, because after the last leg there is no interception, only testimony.

Fact two: the documents. I want to be precise here, because precision is the whole discipline. What Sesay carried — or what I believed she carried, based on the burned crate inventory cross-checked against what the camp had requested by radio in its final week — was the original permit surveys. Not copies. The originals, pulled from the archives under a records request that our own legal department had approved without reading, because the request came through channels and channels are how institutions sign their own death warrants. Those originals showed the alignments as they were actually flagged on the ground forty years ago, in the hand of the surveyor who did the flagging, and they did not match the alignments in the concession filing. The filing claimed mineral-bearing land inside the permit boundaries. The ground said otherwise. The ground, as surveyed by a man named Halloran, said otherwise in ink that had not faded, because whatever else could be said about Halloran, he had used good ink.

You will notice I say *Halloran* without ceremony. I will come to the son.

Fact three: the rogue engineer. This is the part of the report where the financier stopped drinking his coffee, though he held the cup throughout, because men like him never put anything down; putting things down is for people who expect to stay in a room. An engineer named Ines Halloran — yes, *that* Halloran, the surveyor's son, and no, I did not believe in coincidence either, which is why I had pulled his file the day the expedition went quiet — had resigned his post in Port Moresby, forged a work order, and traveled north on the Wewak ferry under a name that fooled a clerk but would not have fooled a dog. He had then hired a boatman and gone upriver along the expedition's logged route, alone, carrying a spare radio kit and, according to a mission station clerk at Timbunke, a brass compass he consulted the way other men consult scripture.

A disgraced engineer, following his dead father's route, with his dead father's compass, toward a spur line his dead father had built and then helped erase. I laid this out for the financier in flat sentences and watched him arrive, in real time, at the conclusion I had arrived at three days earlier: this was not a rescue. This was a reckoning. The son had found something in those margin annotations — the old man had annotated the expedition's maps, we discovered later, through the shipping manifests of the map case — and whatever he had found had turned a radio scheduler into a man walking into the Basin with a forged work order and no extraction plan.

Men with no extraction plan are either fools or converts. Halloran's file said engineer, not fool. So: convert. And converts are worse than rescuers, because rescuers want their people back alive, whereas converts want the truth out, and the truth does not need to survive the trip. Only the documents need to survive the trip. You cannot ransom paper.

* * *

I gave the financier my assessment sitting in the radio room with the door shut, which is a formality I maintain because it makes the conversation feel deniable to both parties, and deniability, properly managed, is load-bearing. Outside, the rain was doing what it does here, which is fall with the patience of something that has already won.

"The survivors are moving toward the spur line," I said. "They'll reach the junction within — " I checked my notebook, though I didn't need to; I keep numbers where other men keep sentiments — "within four days, five if Sesay's leg slows them further. She took shrapnel or a machete at the camp; the guides have been fashioning stretchers from what the mission stations describe as sago frond and optimism. The engineer is ahead of them on the same alignment. Sooner or later they converge, and when they converge, the documents and the man who understands them are in one place."

"And then?" the financier said.

"And then they come downriver," I said, "to the coast, to a journalist, on or about the day the permits lapse. Nineteen days from the camp's destruction, give or take. I've identified the journalist. A stringer in Wewak who has been asking questions about the concession since March. Someone has been feeding him. I'm looking into that separately." I let that sit. Separate inquiries are how you remind a superior that the rot may be closer than he thinks, and a nervous superior approves budgets.

He was quiet for a moment. On the line I could hear the specific silence of a man calculating exposure. Then he said, "Options."

I had prepared options. I always prepare options, ranked, because giving a frightened man one choice frightens him more, and a frightened man with choices feels in control, and a man who feels in control signs things.

"Option one," I said, "we let them come. They reach the coast, they talk to the journalist, we litigate. Our permits expire, we refile clean — the falsified surveys are decades old, the responsible parties are dead or unfindable, and the original filings were approved in good faith by officers since retired. We lose the concession for perhaps eighteen months while the paperwork regenerates. Cost: the concession. Probability of success: moderate. I include it for completeness."

"You don't recommend it."

"I don't recommend it," I agreed. "Option two: intercept on the river. Boats, checkpoints, a customs pretext. We seize the documents as evidence in an internal investigation, detain the survivors for their own safety pending repatriation, and the engineer dies resisting. Accidents happen on the river. Everyone knows the Sepik eats people." I paused. "Probability of success: high, if executed early. Declining daily. Every day they move west, the terrain works for them and against us. Two boats can't watch three hundred kilometers of water."

"Option three," the financier said. He said it the way a man says a word he's been avoiding.

"Option three," I said, "is resolution. Permanent. All parties. Before the junction."

* * *

Here I should explain something about myself, since a report of this nature deserves a narrator who owns his methods. I am not a sadist. Sadists are a liability; they improvise, they linger, they collect souvenirs, and souvenirs end up in evidence bags with photographs attached. I have fired two men for cruelty in eleven years, both times with genuine regret, because they were otherwise competent, and competence is scarce out here. What I am is tidy. I believe that a problem handled cleanly stays handled, and a problem handled messily breeds successors. The guide we lost at the culvert — the second guide, Ekwale, shot by my tracker Vuri during the ambush at the cache — was, I will note for the record, not part of the plan. The plan at the cache was documents only. But Vuri panicked when the guides came around the bend early, and panic is what happens when you staff an operation at thirty percent, and I logged that too, in the ledger, under *costs of optimization*, because I keep honest books even when the honesty indicts my employer.

So when I said *permanent*, I meant it in the administrative sense. No spectacle. No mass grave that a patrol finds in 1991 and turns into a cause. The Basin is generous that way — it swallows things whole and keeps no receipts — but generosity is not a plan. A plan has sequence, redundancy, and a story. The story, in this case, was already written: fever. It had been written the day the camp burned. Four more deaths by fever, upriver, in the wet season, would surprise no one who mattered. The country would do the paperwork. I would merely ensure the country had the opportunity.

The financier was silent long enough that I checked whether the line had dropped. It hadn't. He was deciding, and I could hear the shape of the decision in his breathing, the way you can hear rain coming in the leaves.

"There's a girl on the expedition," he said finally. "One of the guides. Young."

"There are two guides besides Sesay," I said. "Both local hires. Both signed the same indemnity forms as everyone else."

"That's not — " He stopped. I waited. Men like him always finish; the sentence is a debt and they cannot bear to default. "That's not what I mean. If it goes wrong. If anyone talks afterward. I want it understood that the local engagement was yours. Your hires, your protocols, your discretion. The operational record should reflect that the field decisions were delegated."

And there it was. Not a refusal — a firewall. He was not saying *don't*. He was saying *not me*. I wrote the instruction down verbatim, because instructions like that are worth more than confessions; a man who delegates his crimes in writing has handed you a lever, and I keep levers the way other men keep whiskey. Filed under *protection*, sub-filed under *self*, because I did not rise from a shipping office in Surabaya to be some financier's disposable pen.

"Understood," I said, in the tone that means nothing and commits to less. "Then I'll need the budget released today and the boats fueled tonight. And one more item."

"Go on."

"The engineer. Halloran." I looked out the window at the rain, at the river beyond it, brown and patient, going about its business of carrying everything away. "His father built that spur line. His father falsified the surveys. His father, near as I can tell from the correspondence in the archive, spent his last years trying to buy his way out of it and died before he managed it. The son is walking the route in reverse with the old man's compass in his pocket. Do you understand what that is?"

"A problem," the financier said.

"It's a witness," I said. "Not to events — he wasn't there. To the *pattern*. He can read an alignment the way a priest reads a soul. Put him in front of a magistrate with those originals and he doesn't just prove the fraud, he explains it, elegantly, with diagrams, in a voice that juries trust. Engineers are the most dangerous witnesses in existence. They're credible and they enjoy being right." I closed the notebook. "Sesay proves the fraud happened. Halloran proves it *mattered*. I want both resolved, and I want the engineer first, because a dead messenger is a tragedy and a silenced expert is a footnote."

"You're asking me to authorize killing a man whose only crime is reading a map."

I considered pointing out that the man's only crime, as far as the law would ever see it, was forgery of a work order, and that mine included several entries the ledger kept in pencil. I considered mentioning that everyone on this line had been comfortable right up until the comfort required a signature. I did none of these things. Instead I said, "I'm asking you to decide whether the concession survives the month. Everything else is phrasing."

He decided. Of course he decided. Frightened men with optimized consciences always decide; the hesitation is theater they perform for themselves. He authorized the budget, the boats, and the phrase *field discretion*, and he rang off before I finished confirming the figures, which was wise of him, because confirmation is where men start hearing what they've agreed to.

* * *

Afterward I stood on the veranda again with fresh coffee — equally terrible; the machine was still an accounting entity that no longer existed — and reviewed the disposition. Nine men. Three boats. Vuri and the Kandrian brothers on the upper water, below the junction, where the alignment funneled toward the crossing at the old culvert. Two men at Timbunke watching the mission telephone. Myself mobile, because a security chief who commands from a veranda is a security chief whose men begin making their own decisions, and I had seen where Vuri's decision-making led.

It was good dispositions, sound dispositions, and I want it recorded that I felt no particular satisfaction in them. People imagine that men in my trade relish the hunt. Mostly we resent it. Every operation is a failure of someone upstream — a falsified permit, a bumped geologist, a burned crate that should have stayed buried — and we are simply the interest accruing on other men's debts. Somewhere in Port Moresby a financier was sleeping better because I existed, and somewhere on a ghost railway an engineer was checking a brass compass against a dying star, and between us lay nineteen days, three hundred kilometers of brown water, and a box of paper that refused, with the stubbornness of all paper, to burn.

I opened the ledger and made the entry. Date, disposition, authorization reference, initials in blue ink. Under *expected resolution* I wrote: *all parties, prior to junction.* Under *contingencies* I wrote: *none anticipated.*

Then, because I am honest in small things if nowhere else, I struck *none anticipated* and wrote instead: *the river keeps its own schedule.*

I did not yet know how right I was.
