## 11. Reunion at the Culvert

The compass had been lying to me for six days, or telling me a truth no map wanted to hear — I'd stopped being able to tell the difference somewhere around the second dynamited culvert. Either way, by late afternoon on the seventh day it swung hard east and held there, quivering like a dog that had caught a scent, and I knew I was close. Not to anything marked. To something *kept*.

I came down through the kunai grass with the sun going copper behind the ridge and the river talking to itself below, and that's when I heard it: not voices exactly, but the particular silence of people trying not to be heard. You spend enough years around radio operators and you learn what deliberate quiet sounds like. It has a texture. It hums.

I did the stupid thing first, because apparently that's my signature move. I stood up in open ground and said, "Dr. Sesay? It's Halloran. From Port Moresby. The radio schedule."

Nothing answered except a bird that sounded offended I'd interrupted it.

Then, from behind a slab of concrete that had once been a culvert headwall — my father's culvert, though nobody present knew that yet, and I wasn't ready for anyone to know it — a voice said, "Come forward slowly. Hands where I can see them."

I came forward slowly. Hands where they could be seen. It's remarkable how much of your life reduces to following instructions from strangers with rifles when you're tired enough.

* * *

There were four of them, and only three looked alive in any way I recognized.

Two guides — one young, one older, both Sepik men whose names I would learn were Boas and Kila, though at that moment they were mostly just two sets of eyes over rifle barrels. A third man sitting against the culvert wall with his leg wrapped in what had been an expedition shirt and was now mostly blood and improvisation. And Dr. Amara Sesay, standing, which surprised me, because everything else about her suggested she should have been horizontal weeks ago.

She was thinner than her expedition photograph, which had been pinned above the radio desk in Wewak like a saint's icon. The photograph showed a composed woman with a clipboard. The woman in front of me had a splinted forearm, a gash along her jaw that someone had stitched with what I hoped was fishing line, and eyes that had done arithmetic with death and gotten an answer she didn't like but had learned to carry anyway.

"Halloran," she said. Not a question. Which meant either she remembered me from the briefing — unlikely; I'd been introduced as furniture — or someone had told her a rogue engineer might come upriver, which meant someone had been transmitting, which meant the emergency code fragments I'd answered three nights ago hadn't gone into a void.

"You got our signal," she said.

"I got part of it. Enough." I kept my hands visible anyway. Old habit from creditors, newer habit from Calloway's men, who I'd seen at a distance two days back and who had taught me, briefly and vividly, what professional patience looks like when it's pointed at you.

"Part of it," she repeated, and something in her voice closed like a ledger. "You'll forgive me if I don't throw a parade. We've had people claim to be help before."

"We have," said the injured man, from the ground, with the flat delivery of someone repeating a sentence he'd already said too many times. His name, I'd learn, was Okafor — the expedition's surveyor. The leg was bad. Not dying-bad, but the kind of bad where every hour of delay is a decision you'll answer for later.

"What happened to your party," Sesay asked, "the rest of it?"

"There was no rest of it. It's me. One boatman, Tobias, holding downstream at a village with a man named Yawan who owes me nothing and is risking a great deal. And a geologist, Marta Ondoua, who's supposed to meet us at a mission station south of here with a journalist's frequency and a deadline." I hesitated. "Nineteen days when we started counting. Fewer now."

Sesay absorbed this the way stone absorbs rain. Then: "You're alone. An engineer who resigned his post, traveling under false papers, following a compass with your father's name on it, toward a rail line the company dynamited behind itself forty years ago. Do you see how that reads?"

"I do," I said. "It reads badly. It read badly to me too, most of the way upriver. But here I am, and I didn't lead anyone to you, because I couldn't have. I found you because your own emergency transmissions gave me a bearing, and because the compass—" I stopped myself. She didn't need the compass story yet. The compass story required context, and context required trust, and trust was currently being weighed on scales that had recently held dead men.

"Show me," she said.

"Show you what?"

"Whatever you used to find us. All of it. Slowly."

So I laid it out on the concrete like a man emptying his pockets at customs: the compass, the copied concession files, the route log I'd reconstructed from the daily check-ins, the fragmentary transcript of their emergency code. Boas watched the compass the whole time with an expression I recognized — the look of a man deciding whether an object is a tool or a trick.

Sesay picked up the transcript. Read it. Her face did something complicated.

"This is our code," she said quietly. "Day codes, position shifts, the whole sequence. Nobody outside the expedition had all of it."

"I know," I said. "That's why I answered it. Against every rule of tradecraft I've ever been taught, which, granted, I learned from a manual with a coffee stain on page one. I broadcast on your frequency, in the clear, and told anyone listening exactly where I was. If Calloway's people had been monitoring — and they were — I made myself the loudest thing on the band for ninety seconds so they'd look at me instead of you."

Silence. The river filled it. Somewhere upstream a log went over a shallow and knocked, knocked, knocked against stones like a slow clock.

"That was either very brave or very stupid," Sesay said.

"In my experience those are the same thing wearing different shirts."

Okafor laughed from the ground — a short, pained bark, the laugh of a man whose ribs objected — and something in the group's posture shifted half a degree. Not trust. Trust is a bridge built over months. This was more like a gate left unlatched.

But Sesay wasn't finished. She was an expedition leader; she verified things the way other people breathe.

"The check-ins," she said. "Camp Karawari, day nine. Recite them."

I could feel everyone watching. I closed my eyes, and the static came back the way it always did, that afternoon in the Wewak shack, the signal fraying mid-word:

"*Position confirmed above the confluence, grid correction minus two easting — hold on, Kila says the marker's wrong, the marker's wrong, we're re-sighting off the—*" And then nothing. Forty-eight hours of company-mandated silence after that, while I sat in a chair doing arithmetic with river gauges and hating every man in the Port Moresby office.

I recited it back to her word for word, including the hesitation, including the way her voice had dropped half a register on *re-sighting*, the way it does when someone realizes the ground under them disagrees with the paper about it.

Sesay sat down slowly on the culvert slab. For a moment she looked less like an expedition leader than like a woman hearing a voice from a room she'd escaped.

"That's it," she said. "That's the last thing I said on the air. I've thought about that sentence every day since. *The marker's wrong.* Four words. I keep thinking if I'd said five — if I'd said *the marker's wrong and we're moving* — maybe—"

"Maybe nothing," I said, more gently than I felt equipped to be. "You were cut off mid-transmission by people who wanted you cut off. That's not a failure of vocabulary. That's an ambush with paperwork."

She looked at me then — really looked, the assessment moving from my face down to my hands, engineer's hands, scarred and steady — and I understood that the recitation had done what no amount of explanation could. Anyone could claim to be friendly. Only someone who had sat in the dark with her last words could prove where he'd been standing all along.

"All right," she said. "Halloran. Sit down. We're going to trade everything we know, and then we're going to decide what to do, and nobody leaves this culvert until we've decided."

* * *

What they knew took an hour to tell and rearranged the inside of my skull.

They'd been ambushed eleven days ago at a camp they never should have been at — a site that appeared on the concession maps as a survey station but was, in fact, an active ore-sampling operation on land the company had no permit to touch. The permits expired soon; Sesay had figured out the shape of it before I had, working from the inside. The company's solution to the problem of witnesses had been to arrange an accident — a staged rockfall on the river cutting, timed to catch the survey team in the narrows. Three men died. The guides pulled Sesay and Okafor out of the water. And in the chaos, Boas had done the single most important thing anyone had done in this entire affair: he'd gone back, under fire, for the document box.

He showed it to me. A steel ammunition case, dented, water-stained, wrapped in oilcloth. Inside: the original permit surveys. The ones filed in Port Moresby were copies — altered copies, boundaries moved, dates adjusted. These were the field originals in Sesay's own hand, countersigned by the government surveyor who had since died conveniently. Alongside them: correspondence, sampling logs, a payment schedule to a landowner association that existed only on letterhead. Everything the company had spent money and lives to bury, packed in a box that two men had carried through swamps on a stretcher-frame while bleeding.

"They nearly died carrying it," Sesay said, watching my face as I turned the pages. "I want you to understand that. Every kilometer of this river, we discussed leaving it. Sinking it. Burying it and running light. And every time, we decided the weight was the point."

I understood. God help me, I understood better than she knew yet. Because folded inside the correspondence — I saw it as I lifted a sheaf — was a familiar hand. Slanted, impatient, the letters leaning like fence posts in wind. My father's hand. The same hand that had annotated the expedition's maps in margins forty years stale. The same hand that had written a confession he never mailed, which I'd read by lamplight in Wewak and hated myself for weeping over.

I didn't say anything. Not yet. There are things you can't hand across a trust that's still wet concrete. But I put the documents back in order, carefully, the way you handle evidence and grief with the same motion, and Sesay watched me do it and said nothing either, which meant she'd noticed which pages had slowed my hands.

Later, maybe. Later there would be time for the ledger, and the bribes, and the long ugly inheritance of it. Tonight there was only the next decision.

* * *

We made camp inside the culvert's shadow — Boas insisted on the concrete at our backs, sightlines down both banks, and I wasn't going to argue terrain with a man who'd survived eleven days of being hunted in his own country. Okafor's leg was cleaned and re-dressed; I contributed a proper splint from the spare kit and some of Marta's precious stock of sulfa tablets, and he thanked me with a nod that cost him visible effort, because Okafor struck me as a man who rationed gratitude like everything else.

Kila cooked rice and tinned fish over a smokeless fire, a skill I intend to steal if I live long enough. And while the others settled, Sesay drew me aside to the culvert mouth, where the river ran loud enough to cover conversation.

"How many does Calloway have?" she asked.

"Six that I counted, plus trackers. Maybe eight. He moves them like an accountant — no waste, no noise. He burned a mission boat two days south of here to strand Marta. He doesn't rage. He *files*."

"And you answered our signal knowing he was on the band."

"Yes."

"Why?" It wasn't hostile. It was the question of a scientist who needed the mechanism explained before she could rely on the result.

I looked at the river for a while. "Because I spent two days listening to static on your behalf and doing nothing, because protocol said wait, and two men died in that gap between protocol and sense. I'm not doing that twice. Also," I added, because honesty seemed to be the currency of the evening, "because I've spent my whole life being invisible on purpose. Invisible is safe. Invisible is also how the company wins. Somebody has to be loud."

Sesay studied me. "You know they'll offer him a deal eventually. Calloway. They'll offer you your name back. Your debts cleared. Men like you are cheaper to buy than to bury."

"Men like me," I said, "have been bought before. That's rather the family business." And before she could ask what I meant, I said it plainly, because the concrete was setting and I wanted it set true: "My father worked these lines. He falsified the original alignments for the men who founded this concession. There's a ledger in that box in his handwriting, and a confession, and I suspect by morning you'll have read both. So whatever you decide to think of me, decide it knowing I'm not here despite what my family did. I'm here because of it."

The silence stretched. Downstream, Tobias was a day away and unreachable; upstream, Calloway was a day closer and very reachable. Nineteen days had become fourteen.

Then Sesay said, "The journal will want testimony. Documents convince lawyers. People convince juries — and editors. A disgraced engineer who walked into the basin alone to finish a job the company abandoned?" She almost smiled; the gash on her jaw made it lopsided. "That's not a liability, Halloran. That's a headline."

"I've been a headline before," I said. "It didn't suit me."

"It'll suit you better than the alternative." She turned back toward the fire, then paused. "One condition. The box travels with Boas. Always. If we're separated, you don't get it, I don't get it — Boas carries it, because Boas hasn't lied to anyone in thirty-one years and I can't say that about either of us."

"Agreed," I said, and meant it, and felt lighter for meaning it, which was its own small warning sign.

I took first watch, at the culvert mouth, with the compass open on my knee out of habit more than need. It held its bearing east-northeast, steady as a pulse, pointing at the old junction I now knew lay a day's march ahead — the place my father had chosen, decades ago, to hide duplicates of everything. The place the map called nothing. The place we would have to go, because fourteen days and one journalist demanded it, and because Calloway's trackers would follow us anywhere we went regardless.

Around midnight, Kila touched my shoulder and pointed downstream without a word.

Far off, past the last bend, where the river flattened into dark — a light. Small. Moving. Not a village light; villages don't travel at night on this stretch, not since the company's boats started asking questions.

A lantern on a boat, coming upriver, patient as arithmetic.

I woke Sesay with a hand on her shoulder, and she was fully awake before her eyes opened, which told me everything about what the last eleven days had taught her.

"They found the culvert trail," she said quietly.

"Not yet," I said. "But they're reading the river the same way we are. And the river only goes one way."

Above us the stars wheeled, indifferent. Below us the light grew, one degree at a time, like a debt coming due.
