## 12. The Father's Ledger

The box had traveled further than most men I've known.

It sat between Sesay and the fire while the guides boiled water, wrapped in oilcloth and lashed with cord that had gone soft as rope made from old rope. Nobody touched it. It had that quality — the way a coffin has it in a room. Everyone aware of it, nobody willing to be the first to put a hand on the lid.

Sesay was propped against a fallen sago trunk with her leg splinted from a guide's shirt and two lengths of sapling, and she watched me watch the box with the flat patience of a woman who had carried something heavy for a long time and wasn't going to hand it over just because a stranger asked nicely.

"You recited my codes," she said. Not a question. We'd settled that by the culvert, or thought we had.

"I did."

"That gets you to the fire. It doesn't get you into the box." She shifted, winced, kept her eyes on me. "The documents inside are the only reason three of us are alive instead of five. The other two died on the river keeping them dry. So you'll understand if I'm particular."

"I'm not asking to keep anything," I said. "I'm asking to look."

"Why?"

Because my father's handwriting is all over your maps, I didn't say. Because a brass compass shipped to me by a lawyer points at nothing on any chart and I've been following it anyway like a man walking into his own funeral. Because I have spent thirty years being ruined by a man I never met twice and I need to know what he actually did before I decide what I am.

"Because I'm an engineer," I said instead. "And whatever's in there, someone falsified alignments. If you want this to hold up — legally, publicly, in front of men paid to make it not hold up — it needs to read like engineering, not like grievance. Grievance gets dismissed. Elevations get checked."

She considered that for long enough that the fire popped twice. Then she nodded at one of the guides, who cut the cord.

* * *

The top layer was survey originals. Sepik Basin concession surveys, dated forty-odd years back, the paper gone amber and brittle at the folds. Route alignments, bench marks, cross-sections of ground along a rail corridor that had officially never existed. I'd spent my career reading drawings like these; I could feel their wrongness before I could name it. The contours were too tidy. Rivers don't negotiate, but these ones appeared to have.

Then the ledger.

It was a school exercise book, the cheap kind, quarter-bound cloth gone gray, its cover soft as cloth from handling. And across the top of the first page, in a hand I would have known anywhere on earth because I'd spent my childhood trying to forge it on permission slips —

*Received, per agreement.*

My father's handwriting. My father's dates. My father's arithmetic, neat as a hymn book, running down columns of payments received against services rendered, and the services were listed in his own shorthand, the same shorthand I'd found in the margins of the expedition maps in Port Moresby: *adj. 3m W at km 14*, *bench reset*, *contour eased*. Adjustments. Resets. Easings. A whole vocabulary of small dishonesties, each one priced.

He hadn't just taken bribes. He'd itemized them. There was something so perversely honest about it — a man who lied for money but couldn't lie to himself about the amounts — that I laughed, once, out loud, and the laugh came out wrong and turned into something else halfway through, and I put my hand over my mouth like a boy caught at it.

"Ines." Sesay's voice, careful.

"It's fine." It wasn't fine. "Keep going," I told myself, and turned pages.

Payment after payment. Dates spanning six years. Names abbreviated to initials — R.C. appearing again and again, and beside it, in later entries, a second set of initials I recognized from Marta's paper trail, the financier's family firm, young then, hungry, buying its concessions with other people's geography. At the back of the book, folded in quarters and gone soft as cloth, four pages in different ink, different mood, written fast:

*I, Thomas Halloran, being of sound mind and unsound character, do state—*

A confession. Unsent, unfiled, addressed to no authority in particular and every one of them at once. He wrote it the way men write letters they mean to post and then sleep on for twenty years. The ink changed mid-page where he'd stopped and come back. He described each adjustment. He named the men who paid him. He calculated — of course he calculated, he was an engineer to his marrow — the tonnage of ore the company had logged from land the falsified lines had quietly stolen, and the figure ran off the edge of the page and he'd continued it up the margin, small, cramped, appalled at his own sum.

At the bottom of the last page: *I have enclosed duplicates of the originals. They are hidden where the line itself will remember. If anyone finds this, find the junction. The compass knows.*

The compass knew. I had it in my pocket. It had been pointing at this ledger, at this fire, at this exact circle of exhausted people, since Wewak, and I had followed it like a dog following a whistle from a man who'd whistled for everyone else but never for me.

I closed the book.

* * *

The guides slept. Sesay dozed sitting up, feverish, her breath rasping. I sat with the ledger open on my knees and let thirty years arrive all at once.

Here is what I had built my life around: a woman in Port Moresby telling me, gently, that my father had walked out when I was four and never sent for us, and then the longer architecture of ruin that followed — the debts, the disgrace, the slow education in how a name can be a weight. I had made him a monster of ordinary size. A man who left. That was the whole story, and I had lived comfortably inside it, the way you live comfortably inside a grudge, because a grudge keeps the weather out.

Now here was the same man in his own hand, and he was worse than my monster and better than it, both at once. Worse, because he hadn't left us for another woman or another life; he'd left us because he'd sold the ground out from under strangers and couldn't stand to be looked at while he spent it. Better, because somewhere in the last pages, in the changed ink, he had tried. Too late, cowardly, in secret, in a school exercise book — but he had tried, and he had hidden the proof where only someone who understood alignments would ever find it, which meant he had hidden it, whether he admitted it or not, for me. For the engineer he must have known I'd become, or hoped I'd become, or feared I'd become.

Grief and fury don't blend. They take turns. All night they took turns, and near dawn they did something neither intended: they wore each other smooth, the way water wears stone, and what was left under them wasn't forgiveness exactly. It was more like recognition. He was a man who took the money and kept the receipts. I was a man who took the job and kept the codes. We were the same weakness, one generation apart, and the difference between us was going to be what I did next.

I looked at the compass. Off true north, steady, patient, pointing at a junction ahead through swamps and pursuit and everything Calloway could bring.

"He hid them twice," I said quietly. Sesay stirred. "Once in the concession files, once at the junction. Duplicates of duplicates. He wanted it to survive even if one cache burned."

"Your father?" she said.

"My father," I said, and found the word fit differently in my mouth than it had for thirty years. "And the man whose initials are in that ledger bought his silence with the same company colors flying over your camp. This isn't just your evidence, Dr. Sesay. It's mine. It always was. I just didn't know I was carrying it."

She studied me through the fever, and whatever she saw, she decided to trust it, or to trust the alternative less.

"The junction," she said. "How far?"

I checked the compass against the stars, then against the dark shape of the ridge line, doing the arithmetic I'd been doing since Wewak.

"Three days," I said. "If the weather holds and Calloway doesn't."

As if summoned, far downriver, a boat engine coughed once in the night and went quiet — listening, the way we were listening.
