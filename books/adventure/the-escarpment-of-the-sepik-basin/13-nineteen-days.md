## 13. Nineteen Days

The mission station at Ambunti sat on a bluff above the river like a man who had made his peace with the water and no longer bothered to watch it. A corrugated roof gone the color of old zinc ointment, a chapel with one shutter hanging, a garden of tapioca and despair. Father Anselm, the priest who ran it, greeted me with the enthusiasm of a man who hadn't seen a new face since the last flood, which I gathered had been recent enough to still be discussed in the past-perfect tense.

I had come up from Wewak on a government mail boat that smelled of diesel and dried fish, carrying a satchel of photostats, a slide rule, and the particular nausea of a woman who has spent eleven days reading other people's signatures and knows exactly what they mean.

I was expecting Ines Halloran to be difficult. What I was not expecting was for him to look like that.

He stood at the edge of the compound with a brass compass in one hand and an expression I recognized from mirrors — the look of a person who has just learned something about a parent and hasn't yet decided whether to grieve or burn something down. Beside him, propped against a post with her leg splinted in what appeared to be bamboo and adhesive tape, sat Dr. Amara Sesay, expedition leader, gray as paper but upright, the way some people stay upright specifically so that collapsing would be an act of will rather than accident. Two guides — Tobias, who piloted like he argued with the river and usually won, and a younger man called Yawane whose left arm was bound to his chest — completed the picture.

And on the table between us, wrapped in oilcloth that had been opened and rewrapped perhaps forty times, the box.

"Sit," said Father Anselm, "and I'll bring tea."

"Whisky," said Sesay.

"We're a Methodist mission," said Anselm, with the serenity of a man who kept whisky anyway, and went inside.

* * *

Let me tell you how I came to be sitting at that table, because the arithmetic only makes sense if you know where the numbers came from.

In March, the Sepik Basin Concession Company filed for mineral survey rights over four hundred square kilometers of basin land — land held under customary title by villages that had never signed anything, never been asked, never been told. The permits were granted on the strength of survey documentation: boundary maps, access alignments, consultation records. All of it bearing the stamp of a licensed surveyor. All of it, as far as I could determine from the company's own filing copies, fabricated or altered.

I know because I was supposed to be on that expedition. I'm a geologist. I'd done two seasons of basin work before; I knew the Karawari country, the sago swamps, the way the rivers move their beds every wet season like restless sleepers. The company hired me to do the geological assessment. Then, nine days before departure, a cable arrived bumping me from the roster. No reason given. My replacement was a man named Voss, whose qualifications consisted, as far as I could discover, of being the nephew-by-marriage of the concession's principal financier, a Melbourne money man named Aldous Renwick.

When your employer replaces you with the financier's relative eleven days before an expedition into terrain you know and he doesn't, you don't get offended. You get curious. Curiosity is just professional resentment with a work ethic.

So I stayed in Wewak. I pulled the permit filings — public registry, anyone can read them, nobody does. I pulled the cadastral overlays. I pulled the 1958 alignment surveys for the old Sepik spur line, decommissioned and officially erased, because the new concession boundaries ran suspiciously along the ghost of them. And I found, in the margin annotations of those old surveys, corrections in a hand that matched nothing else in the file — a hand that had measured things honestly and recorded them anyway, like a man keeping receipts for a crime he intended to report and never did.

That hand, I would learn at this table, belonged to Ines Halloran's father.

But that was his chapter. Here is mine: the numbers.

* * *

Father Anselm brought tea, and whisky for Sesay, and we arranged ourselves around the table while the afternoon rain arrived on schedule and hammered the tin roof into a drum solo.

"Show them," I said.

Ines unwrapped the box. Inside: the original permit surveys. Not the filing copies — the originals, with the draftsman's pencil marks beneath the ink, the erasures, the paste-overs. Sesay's people had carried these out of the basin at a cost I was only beginning to understand. Yawane's arm. Whatever had happened to the others who weren't at this table. The guides didn't look at the documents; they looked at Sesay, as if she were the document, as if she were the thing they'd carried.

"Walk me through it," I said to her. "From the top. Slowly. I need to hear what you saw, not what you concluded."

Sesay took a sip of whisky like a woman taking medicine she approved of. "We surveyed the eastern boundary first. The line as filed runs along the old rail spur — the one your father's generation built and abandoned." She glanced at Ines when she said *your father*, not unkindly, just precisely. "Except the filed map shows the spur running through low ground. It doesn't. It runs along a ridge. Whoever drew the filing map moved the alignment south by three kilometers to make the corridor cross only 'unoccupied state land.' On the ground, the actual corridor crosses village land. Fourteen villages. We walked it. We flagged it. We have photographs and witness statements from headmen who thought they were signing a receipt for our passage."

"And the ore samples," I said.

"Voss took core samples along the ridge. Copper, and indications of gold in the quartz stringers. He logged the coordinates using the falsified grid — so every rich sample in the company's books sits on land the company has no legal claim to. They've been logging reserves for eight months along a corridor they never owned, based on permits that expire—"

"In nineteen days," I said.

Everyone looked at me. Even the rain seemed to lean in.

"Nineteen days," I repeated, and did what I do, which is put the numbers on the table where everyone has to look at them. I took out my notebook. "Here's the shape of it. The concession permits lapse on the thirtieth. If they lapse quietly — no challenge filed, no fraud documented publicly — the company simply refiles. New application, same territory, cleaned-up paperwork. And here's the beautiful part, from their perspective: after the lapse, there is no active permit to contest. The objection window closes with it. Refiling isn't automatic approval, but the registry treats a refiled application over 'previously surveyed' land as substantially de-risked. The falsified surveys become precedent. Their own fraud becomes the foundation of their legal title. They win forever, and it's all perfectly legal, retroactively."

"That can't be right," said Yawane. "There must be someone to appeal to."

"There is always someone to appeal to," I said. "Appeals take years. Years cost money. The villages have gardens; Renwick has a bank. It's not a fair fight, it's a fight on a clock, and the clock is theirs unless we break it."

Ines had been quiet through all of this, turning the compass over in his fingers. Now he set it down flat on the table between us. "Say the rest," he said. "You didn't come upriver on a fish boat to deliver bad news elegantly."

"No," I agreed. "I came because I found the rest of it." I passed across my photostats: correspondence, invoices, a memorandum on Renwick Exploration letterhead discussing 'alignment adjustments' with the surveyor of record — the surveyor of record whose signature appeared on the falsified filings, and whose margin notes on the 1958 originals told a different story. "Your father wasn't just a bribed engineer," I said to Ines, gently, because there is no gentle way to say it. "At the end, he was building a case. The ledger Sesay's team recovered, the cache at the junction — he kept duplicates. He was going to expose the same man, thirty years ago, and he lost his nerve, or lost his leverage, or both. But he documented everything. Which means the fraud isn't just current. It's a pattern. Thirty years of pattern. That matters legally and it matters editorially."

"Editorially," said Sesay.

"I've been writing letters," I said. "Eleven days of letters. To newspapers in Port Moresby, Sydney, London. Most of them will sit on desks until the story is safe to print, meaning never. But one of them answered. A woman named Ruth Callaghan, works the resources desk for a wire service out of Sydney. She's been chasing Renwick for two years on a nickel deal that went sour in Kalimantan. She can't print allegations. She can print documents. Originals, verified, with named witnesses willing to go on record." I looked around the table. "She needs the material in her hands before the permits lapse. Not after. After, it's history. Before, it's a scandal, and scandals stop refilings, because no minister signs off on a fresh grant while the wire service is asking questions about the last one."

"So we need—" began Ines.

"Eleven days of travel and one journalist who will print it," I said. "That's the whole equation. Eleven days to get from here to the coast at Wewak, documents intact and witnesses alive. Then transmission, verification, publication. Callaghan has agreed to hold copy ready. She's agreed to run it the moment she has authenticated originals in hand — she says she'll charter to meet us if we can reach a point with radio contact. There's a mission relay at Marienberg with a working set, if the fathers there haven't traded it for outboard parts. That's day nine, if we're lucky. Day ten if we're honest."

Silence, except the rain.

"The swamps," said Tobias at last. He said it the way other men say *the war*. "Wet season's early. The cutoffs between the Sepik and the coastal creeks are flooded. Eleven days is possible. Eleven days is also a number men say before the river corrects it."

"I know," I said. "I've done the hydrology. The gauge readings at Ambunti are forty centimeters above the seasonal mean. Which cuts both ways — flooded portages, but high water means the channels are navigable further inland than usual. We can run the southern route through the sago country instead of portaging. If we don't stop."

"Sesay can't walk a portage," said Ines. Flatly, not cruelly. A man doing sums.

"Sesay," said Sesay, "can sit in a canoe and hold a rifle, and has done both worse things in worse country. Don't plan around my leg. Plan around the deadline."

But I watched Ines look at her, and I watched him do a different sum behind his eyes — the one where you count the people you've already failed to keep safe and decide how many more risks you're willing to spend. He'd been the radio man. He'd heard the check-ins cut off. Whatever happened upriver before I met them, it lived in him now, and it made him careful in a way that could save us or kill us, depending on the hour.

"Then we should talk about Calloway," I said.

The name landed the way names do when everyone at the table has already met the man's work. Rhys Calloway, security chief for the concession. The trackers. The burned boats. I'd pieced together enough from Sesay's account and from a Wewak harbormaster who owed me a favor: Calloway knew the survivors had the documents. He'd known for days. He wasn't hunting us out of malice — malice takes passion, and everything I'd heard described a man without any. He was hunting us out of bookkeeping. We were a discrepancy in Renwick's ledger, and discrepancies get reconciled.

"He doesn't need to catch us," I said. "He needs to delay us. Every day he costs us past day nineteen, he wins without firing another shot. So he'll burn boats. Block channels. Buy the ferry schedules. He'll be patient, because patience is free when the law is on your side and the calendar is too."

"Then we aren't patient," said Ines.

"No," I said. "We are the opposite of patient. We leave tomorrow, at first light, before he's finished being certain about where we are. We run the southern channels. We push hard for six days, rest half a day at Marienberg for the radio, then three days down the coast to Wewak. And somewhere on that route, we get word to Callaghan so she's standing on the wharf with a charter plane and a lawyer when we arrive, because the moment those documents touch her hands, they're duplicated, wired, and beyond anyone's ability to burn, drown, or shoot."

Yawane laughed — a short, surprised sound, the laugh of a man realizing he'd already decided. "You've been planning this alone for eleven days."

"Somebody had to," I said. "You were all busy being hunted."

Sesay raised the whisky bottle an inch, a toast to the general idea. "Marta Ondoua," she said, "you were bumped from my expedition by a financier's nephew, and it is the single luckiest thing that has happened to any of us."

"Don't tell that to the men who died carrying that box," I said. Luck that arrives by way of other people's graves deserves to be spoken of quietly, and I meant to keep speaking of it that way. But I won't pretend I felt nothing when she said it. Eleven days alone in Wewak with nothing but paperwork, I had wondered if I was doing geology or just nursing a grudge with footnotes. Sitting at that table, watching Ines Halloran look at his father's handwriting in the ledger like a man reading a letter addressed too late — I knew. This was the work. This had always been the work. Everything else had been waiting for the documents to surface.

* * *

We planned until the rain stopped and the light went bronze and the mosquitoes rose off the river in their evening battalions. Tobias marked channels on Father Anselm's ancient chart, annotating from memory: sandbar here, logjam there, a channel that had migrated east since the chart was drawn and now ran through what the chart insisted was dry land. Ines worked the fuel arithmetic — how much petrol Tobias's boat could carry, where we might buy more without alerting Calloway's agents, what weight we could shed. Sesay dictated a list of names: the dead, the missing, the witnesses among the villagers, each with a location and a statement she'd witnessed herself. Yawane cleaned a rifle with the serenity of a man saying grace.

I sat apart for a while on the veranda steps with my notebook, checking my figures one final time, because if you're going to bet fourteen lives on arithmetic, you check the arithmetic.

Nineteen days. Minus one for tomorrow's start. Six to Marienberg, weather permitting, which it wouldn't. Half a day for the radio, assuming the set worked and the operator hadn't been bought, assumptions I rated at fifty percent each. Three days to the coast. One day of margin for disaster, which was either generous or absurd depending entirely on Calloway, and Calloway was not a variable I could solve for. Eighteen minus twelve left six days of slack on paper and none at all in the world.

Behind me, inside, Ines was showing Sesay the compass — how it pointed consistently off true north, toward the junction, toward the cache, toward whatever his father had wanted found. I heard him say, quietly, "It got us there. I don't know what it's for now."

"It's for knowing which way you're facing," Sesay said. "Everything else is walking."

I wrote the date at the top of a fresh page. Nineteen days. Underneath it I wrote the only two numbers that mattered anymore: eleven, and one. Eleven days of travel. One journalist who would print it.

Then Tobias, who had gone down to check the boat lines, came back up the bluff at a pace that made the whole compound turn, and said, in the tone of a man reporting weather:

"There's a launch below the bend with no lights. Been sitting there since dark. Flying no colors at all — which on this river means it's flying somebody's."
