## 14. The Hunt Begins

There is a particular kind of man who believes that violence is a failure of planning. I have met him in boardrooms from Perth to Port Moresby, and he is always wrong in the same way: he mistakes his own discomfort for a moral law. Violence is not a failure of planning. It is a line item. It has a cost, a schedule, and — if you do it properly — a paper trail that leads nowhere at all.

My name is Rhys Calloway, and I am the paper trail.

I had been on the Karawari for six days when the trackers found the first sign worth finding. Before that there had been the usual noise of the bush — mud that swallowed boot prints whole, rain that erased everything above ankle height, a hundred villages where a party of five could pass like smoke through a fence. The Sepik Basin does not hide people out of malice. It hides them because hiding things is simply what water does.

But water also reveals. That was the irony my employer never appreciated. He wanted the river to be a grave; I knew it was a ledger. Everything that goes into the Karawari comes out somewhere downstream, and if you are patient, you can read the entries.

* * *

The tracker who found the sign was named Yawan, and he was the best of the four I'd hired out of Angoram, which is to say he was the only one who didn't drink his advance before we left. He crouched over a stretch of black mud at the edge of a sago stand and pointed without touching, the way a good tracker does, as though the ground were a document he was citing rather than disturbing.

"Five," he said. "One carried. One dragged."

"One dragged," I repeated. "Injured."

"Leg. Left." He indicated with two fingers the width of a boot heel dragging a shallow furrow beside the prints. "Not fresh. Two days."

Two days. I did some arithmetic against the map pinned inside my waterproof case. Two days at the pace of a party carrying an injured woman and a box they wouldn't put down even to sleep — because they wouldn't, I was certain of that, men don't nearly die for paperwork and then set the paperwork in the mud — put them somewhere south of the old mission station at Ambunti-side, near where the concession's eastern boundary dissolved into swamp that nobody had ever bothered to survey, because surveying it would have meant admitting it existed.

"You see," I told Yawan, mostly because talking helps me think and partly because he deserved to know what he was walking into, "most people think a hunt is about speed. Speed is for amateurs and journalists. A hunt is about knowing where the other man must go, and then arriving before him, unhurried, with breakfast already eaten."

Yawan looked at me with the flat patience of a man who has been talked at by white men his entire working life and has learned that agreement costs nothing. "We go slow," he said.

"We go exactly as fast as they do," I said. "No faster. A frightened animal runs itself into the ground. We want them tired, not dead. Dead men drop boxes in rivers, and rivers keep secrets better than I do."

That last part was true, and it was the whole problem, and I had been chewing on it since Wewak.

* * *

Let me explain the situation as I understood it, because I find that antagonists in stories are usually portrayed as either idiots or demons, and both portraits flatter us. I was neither. I was a man doing a job with incomplete information and a deadline, which is the human condition wearing a uniform.

The financier — I'll not name him here; he paid me to be the name — had built a concession on a foundation of permits that were, to use the technical term, decorative. The original surveys had been falsified forty years ago by a railroad engineer whose name I had only recently learned mattered, and the company had spent those forty years logging ore reserves off land it did not legally hold, secure in the knowledge that the permits, once renewed, would launder the past forever. Permits expire in nineteen days. If they lapsed undocumented, the company refiled clean, the fraud became history, and history, as everyone knows, belongs to whoever pays the archivists.

Against this stood a survey expedition that had gone silent upriver, three or four survivors carrying the original permit surveys — the true ones, the ones that would hang us — and now, apparently, a rogue engineer loose on the spur line. Ines Halloran. I had read his file. Disgraced, indebted, competent in the way that dangerous men are competent: quietly, and only when it matters. His father had been the one who falsified the alignments. There was a poetry to it that I noted professionally and dismissed personally. Poetry does not stop bullets, and neither, thank God, does justice.

My instructions were precise in their vagueness: *make the problem permanent.* Which is corporate for *do what needs doing and don't tell me how,* which is corporate for *if it goes wrong, you were freelancing.*

Here is what most people don't understand about my position: the law was on my side. Not morally — morality is a luxury good — but procedurally. The survivors were trespassing on concession land. Halloran was traveling under false work orders. Every one of them was, in the eyes of any magistrate between here and the coast, a criminal. I could detain them. I could, with a modest bribe and a straight face, have them charged. The documents they carried could be seized as evidence of theft from the company. The system did not need me to kill anyone.

But systems are slow, and deadlines are fast, and so the question was never *whether* the law permitted me to act. The question was whether the law could act faster than nineteen days. It couldn't. Nothing moves that quickly except weather and violence, and I controlled my access to only one of them.

So: make the problem permanent, but quietly, and preferably in a place where permanence requires no explanation. The swamps were ideal. The Sepik Basin eats people the way the sea eats ships — routinely, without comment, and with no body to produce. A man drowns in the sago swamps and the coroner writes *misadventure* and goes back to his lunch. I had seen it done. I had, on two previous contracts, arranged for it to be done. I am not proud of this. Pride is another luxury good, and I have never been able to afford it.

* * *

We followed the drag-mark south for a day and a half, moving at the pace I had set, which drove my younger men half-mad. There is a type of security contractor — young, gym-built, action-movie-educated — who thinks pursuit means running. I had two of them, Kessler and a Tongan called Fifita, and by the second afternoon Kessler was audibly suffering.

"They're making maybe twelve kilometers a day," he said. "We could cover thirty. Why are we letting them stay ahead?"

"Because ahead is where I want them," I said. "Look at the map, Kessler. What's ahead of them?"

He looked. He was not stupid, merely impatient, and impatience is just stupidity with good posture. "The mission station. Ambuti — Ambunti-side. The Catholic one with the boat landing."

"The mission station," I agreed, "has a boat. A longboat, diesel, reliable — Father Anselm keeps it well, God bless him. Now ask yourself: what happens to our fugitives' arithmetic if they reach that boat?"

Kessler frowned. "They get to the coast fast."

"They get to the coast *fast,*" I said, "with the documents, before the permits lapse, and every hour they gain is an hour a journalist gains, and journalists are the only enemy in this business who can't be bought, bullied, or buried. So we will not race them to the boat." I tapped the map. "We will arrive first, and the boat will not be there. And then they will have a choice: turn back into country we control, or go into the swamps, where nobody comes out clean and somebody always doesn't come out at all."

Fifita grinned. Kessler still looked like a man being denied dessert. Yawan watched the treeline, because Yawan understood that while the white men discussed the hunt, the hunt was happening around us, and the bush does not pause for strategy.

It was Yawan, that evening, who found the second sign: a cold camp, five days old, with the ashes carefully scattered — someone disciplined, someone who had done this before — and pressed into the mud beside it, half-erased by rain, the print of a boot with a railroad-issue heel plate. Old pattern. The kind issued twenty years ago, to men who worked the closed lines.

Halloran. Walking his father's railway in reverse. I confess the symmetry gave me a moment's professional pleasure. I have always appreciated a well-made thing, even when the well-made thing is trying to destroy me.

"He knows the spur line," Yawan said, reading the print the way I read a contract. "He walks the ballast, not the mud. Ballast keeps feet dry."

"His father helped build it," I said. Yawan raised his eyebrows slightly, which from Yawan was a full conversation. "Yes," I told him. "Families are complicated. Fortunately, graves are not."

* * *

We reached the mission station on the morning of the ninth day, eleven hours ahead of the party's pace, and I will say this for Father Anselm: he was the only honest man I dealt with in the entire affair, which made deceiving him unpleasant in a way I was not expecting and did not enjoy.

I arrived with six men in company khaki, a forged district security writ — beautifully done; my forger in Wewak charged by the seal and earned every kina — and a story about armed trespassers, stolen company property, and a warrant that required me to impound all river transport at the station to prevent flight. None of it was strictly true. All of it was procedurally immaculate. This is the trick of my profession: never lie where a truth will serve, and never tell a truth that can be checked before it stops mattering.

Father Anselm was seventy, German-trained, and had been in the Basin long enough to have buried three generations of the same families. He listened to my story with his hands folded and his eyes doing something I did not like, which was seeing me.

"There are travelers coming," he said. "Survey people. They stopped here going upriver in March. Good people. One of them fixed the station's generator and refused payment."

"People change," I said.

"Yes," he said. "Usually for the worse. Usually when they start carrying papers." He looked at me for a long moment. "You will take the boat regardless of what I say."

"Impoundment order," I said, and showed him the seal, and watched him weigh his parish against a piece of stamped paper and understand, with the weary clarity of men who have lived under many governments, exactly how much his word weighed against mine.

"Take it," he said finally. "And know that I will write down today. Date, time, your name, your faces. I have written down every soldier, policeman, and company man who has passed through this station in thirty-one years, and those notebooks have been read in courtrooms twice." He went to his desk, wrote, blotted the page, and looked up. "God forgives quickly, Mr. Calloway. Courts are slower. But they do come."

I took the boat. I had my men siphon the diesel into drums — waste nothing; budgets exist even for atrocity — and then, because an empty longboat at a mission dock is a message, and messages should be legible, I had Kessler burn it. Not dramatically. No theatrics, no flames visible from the river. Just oil, a match, and enough heat to reduce a perfectly good vessel to a black ribcage on the shingle, smoking gently into the canopy, saying to anyone approaching from downriver: *whatever you were counting on, count again.*

Father Anselm stood on the veranda and watched it burn and did not say anything, which was worse than anything he could have said. I found I preferred the ones who shouted.

* * *

That night I sat on the mission dock with a lamp and a radio and did the thing I do best, which is wait. The others played cards in the station house; Yawan sat at the treeline facing the way the party would come, because Yawan did not believe in nights off, and I respected him for it, and had already decided he would be paid double and quietly retired from my employment afterward, because men who watch treelines see things, and things seen have a way of becoming testimony.

The radio hissed. Somewhere far to the south, a fragment of transmission stuttered through the static — expedition emergency code, three short bursts, badly broken up — and I logged it with the mild satisfaction of a man watching his own forecast verify. They were alive, they were moving, and they were talking on frequencies I owned. Every word they transmitted was a breadcrumb laid toward my table.

I thought about Halloran then, more than I meant to. A disgraced engineer, hiding from debt, pulled back into his father's ruin by a brass compass and a box of paper. I had read the file until I could have drawn him. And I will admit — privately, here, where it costs nothing — that I understood him better than I understood my own employer. We were both men living inside other men's decisions. The difference was that Halloran seemed to be trying to climb out of his, and I had long ago furnished mine and called it home.

Nineteen days had become eight. The math was simple and it favored me. They were on foot, injured, burdened, and about to discover that their exit had burned. Ahead of them lay the sago swamps: forty kilometers of drowned forest, leech water, fever, and mud that gives nothing back. Behind them, me, moving at their exact pace, patient as interest accruing.

People imagine that men like me enjoy the violent part. We don't, particularly. The violent part is a cost center. What I enjoyed — what I have always enjoyed — was this: the lamp, the static, the certainty. Knowing where the other man must go, and sitting down to wait for him, with breakfast already planned.

Yawan stirred at the treeline and raised one hand. Downriver, faint through the dark, came the sound I had been waiting eight days to hear: an engine coughing, oars knocking, voices low and urgent, approaching a dock where the boat was already ash and the swamps were already waiting.

I turned the lamp down to a coal and smiled at the static.

Come on, then, Mr. Halloran. Let's see what you carry.
