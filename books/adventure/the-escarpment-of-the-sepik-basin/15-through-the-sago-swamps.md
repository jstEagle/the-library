## 15. Through the Sago Swamps

There is a particular sound a man makes when he pulls the third leech off his own neck in an hour. It isn't a scream. It isn't a word. It's somewhere between a prayer and a complaint, and by the second day of the sago swamps, all four of us had it down to a fine art — a kind of liturgy, muttered at intervals, that I suspect the swamp itself found amusing.

The guides, of course, said nothing. Kofi and young Beni had grown up with water like this, water that wasn't water so much as a rumor of land that had given up on the idea of being dry. They moved through the sago in a silence I came to think of as professional courtesy toward the swamp: you don't insult a thing that can swallow you by complaining about it.

I complained. Silently, mostly. Out loud I was busy being useful, because being useful was the only currency I had left.

Dr. Amara Sesay was dying. Not quickly — she was too stubborn for that — but steadily, the way a rail settles into a bad ballast bed: a millimeter at a time, each one negligible, the sum of them a derailment. The wound in her side had gone from angry to quiet, which was worse. Quiet meant the fever had moved in and made itself at home. By the third morning she couldn't stand without two of us under her arms, and by the third afternoon she'd stopped apologizing for it, which I took as a good sign. People who apologize for needing help are still fighting the fact of it. People who stop have accepted it and started conserving strength for the actual fight.

"Stop looking at me like that, Halloran," she said, when I checked the dressing at the midday halt. We'd wedged her upright against a sago palm whose trunk was wider than the survey hut she'd once worked out of in Lae. "You have the face of a man composing a report."

"I was an engineer, not an assessor."

"You were a man who filed reports. I could tell from your radio schedule. Everything in its column, everything in its place." Her eyes were too bright. "So compose one. How bad am I?"

I tied off the bandage and gave her the truth, because lying to a scientist is a waste of good material. "The wound's infected. You need a proper camp, dry ground, and antibiotics inside four days or the fever makes the decision for you."

"Four days." She looked out at the swamp, at the brown water threading between the palms like the veins of something vast and indifferent. "And the junction?"

The compass was in my breast pocket. I didn't take it out. I didn't need to. "Two days, if the water cooperates. Three if it doesn't."

"Then we don't have a problem," Sesay said. "We have a schedule. You people love schedules."

I went to help Kofi break camp, and I will confess that her phrasing warmed something in me that the swamp had spent three days trying to drown.

* * *

The swamp did not cooperate.

It never does. Water in the Sepik Basin isn't a thing you cross; it's a thing you negotiate with, and the negotiations are conducted entirely on the water's terms. The trick — the trick Kofi knew in his bones and I had to learn with my boots — is reading it. Which channels were moving and which were stagnant. Which brown meant silt and which brown meant something rotting underneath. Where the surface lay flat and glassy in a way that looked inviting and meant a bottom of two meters of sucking peat that would take a man to the hips and keep him.

"Look for the floating grass," Kofi told me on the first morning, when I'd nearly led us down a channel that ended in a peat sink. He said it without judgment, which somehow stung worse. "The grass rides the current. No current, no channel. No channel, no crossing. The swamp tells you everything. You just have to stop talking long enough to listen."

I stopped talking. I listened.

And it turned out that thirty years of reading water for a living — gauging rivers for bridge footings, calculating flows, standing on the banks of half the rivers in this country with a level and a notebook — wasn't useless after all. It was just that I'd always read water as an adversary, something to be measured and beaten. Kofi read it as a text. Once I made that shift, the swamp opened up like a document I'd been holding upside down.

By the second day I was picking the channels. Not as fast as Kofi, never that, but well enough that he stopped correcting me before I committed, and started correcting me after, which in guide language is a medal. When we came to the first real obstacle — a channel too deep to wade, too wide for the fallen palm we'd used that morning, with Sesay gray-faced on Beni's back and Calloway's men somewhere behind us — it was me who found the answer.

A dead tree had come down across the channel a hundred meters upstream, its trunk half-submerged, its crown tangled in a living tree on the far bank. Everyone had looked at it and seen a fallen tree. I looked at it and saw a compression member under load.

"It'll hold," I said, "if we distribute the weight and keep the line taut. Kofi, you cross first and anchor the far end. Beni, you're the lightest, you go last with the doctor between us on the rope. We rig a tension line from this bank to that root mass — see it, the one like a fist? — and we don't put more than one body on the trunk at a time."

Kofi looked at the trunk, then at me, and did something I hadn't seen him do before: he asked my opinion about something that wasn't water. "The root mass will hold?"

"The root mass is holding that whole tree up against the current. It's the strongest thing in this swamp. Everything else here gave up years ago. That tree didn't."

He grinned, quick and white in the gloom, and went out along the trunk like it was a sidewalk.

The crossing took forty minutes and cost us a crate of tinned meat to the water, because Beni's foot slipped at the far end and we had to shed weight fast. Nobody minded. Tinned meat is replaceable. What we gained was harder to price: from that crossing on, when I said *this way*, the guides went this way without the long look they'd been giving me since Wewak, the look that said *we are following a radio man into a swamp because the alternative was worse*. The look was gone. In its place was something I hadn't earned since the scandal, since before the scandal, maybe since ever: the simple, unglamorous trust of men who had watched me read a problem and solve it with my hands and my head, and decided that was enough.

I'd spent years believing my competence was the thing that had ruined me — that if I'd been less good at my work, less certain of my own calculations, I'd never have signed the alignment I signed, never have trusted the numbers I was handed. Out there in the sago, with leeches in my boots and a feverish woman on my back, I understood for the first time that the competence was never the corruption. The corruption was the men who'd handed me the numbers. The competence was mine, and it was clean, and it was keeping four people alive.

That night I checked the compass by the light of a smoke fire we dared only because the drizzle would hide it.

It pointed the same way it always pointed: off true north by eleven degrees, steady as a sermon, toward the junction. Toward whatever my father had buried out here besides his reputation. I held it in my palm and thought about the man who'd owned it, and about the ledger in Sesay's document box with his handwriting crawling across the pages like something that had died and kept moving, and I felt — not forgiveness. Not yet. Maybe not ever, in the clean way the word implies. But I felt the resentment change weight. It had been a stone I carried to punish him. It was becoming a stone I carried to build with.

"You always check it at night," Sesay said. She'd propped herself up on one elbow, wrapped in the tarp, watching me. "The compass. Every night, same ritual. Your father's?"

The swamp dripped. Somewhere far off, a bird made a sound like a hinge that needed oiling.

"He shipped it to me after he died," I said. "Through a lawyer. No letter. Just the compass and a forwarding address he must have gotten from a file somewhere, because I certainly never gave it to him."

"What did you do with it?"

"Threw it in a drawer for two years. Then I threw it in my kit when I took this job, and I told myself it was because it was a good instrument." I turned it over. The engraving caught the firelight: HALLORAN, and beneath it, worn almost smooth, something older I still couldn't read. "Turns out it's not a good instrument. It points eleven degrees off true. It's been pointing eleven degrees off true since before I was born, straight at a rail junction that isn't on any map. Either it's broken, or my father spent his whole life navigating by something that wasn't true north, and wanted me to find out where it led."

Sesay was quiet a while. The fever had her, but her mind moved through it like a boat through reeds — slower, not stopped.

"My grandfather left me a Bible," she said finally. "With all the passages about forgiveness underlined. He'd been a collaborator during the war. I didn't learn that until after he died. I used to think the underlinings were hypocrisy." She lay back down. "Now I think they were instructions he didn't know how to follow, so he left them where someone else might."

I didn't sleep much after that. Not from fear. From the strange, uncomfortable sensation of being understood by a dying woman in a swamp, which is either the worst or the best kind of company a man can keep.

* * *

On the fourth day, Sesay stopped walking.

Not collapsed — she was past wasting strength on drama — she simply sat down on a root at the morning halt and said, "Go on ahead and I'll follow," in the voice of a woman who knew perfectly well she wouldn't, and was testing whether we'd let her.

We did not let her. Beni took her pack. I took her arm, and she leaned on it with the full weight of her opinion of the arrangement, which was considerable, and we went on.

The channels were getting wider. That was the thing I didn't say out loud. The swamp was opening toward the river system, which meant the water was getting deeper and the crossings were getting harder, and my four-day estimate to the junction had been made by an optimist wearing my face. We waded a channel to my chest that morning, holding the document box above the waterline like a reliquary, Sesay floating it ahead of her on a raft of bound sago fronds that Kofi lashed in four minutes flat, watching me with an expression that said, *engineer that.*

I engineered it. We all did. That was the discovery of the swamp, if the swamp had a lesson, which I doubt — swamps don't teach, they only select: you learn or you don't come out. By the fourth day the four of us moved like one animal. Kofi reading the water, me reading the structure, Beni carrying more than his body weight with a grin, Sesay directing her own rescue from the rear with the flat authority of a woman who had led an expedition and intended to go on leading it from a stretcher if it came to that.

It was Beni who heard it first, at dusk on the fourth day, as we made our halt on the one dry hummock in a kilometer of water. He held up a hand, and we all went still, and beneath the frogs and the dripping and the hinge-bird, I heard it too.

An engine. A long way off, across the water. An outboard motor, running at low throttle, the way a boat runs when it's searching rather than traveling.

Kofi's face in the last light said everything his mouth didn't. We killed the fire we hadn't lit yet, and I took out the compass, and I checked it one more time — not for navigation. For reassurance. It pointed through the dark, eleven degrees off true, steady as ever, at the junction.

And then, faint across the black water, carried on the wind that was finally moving in our favor, came a sound I knew better than my own name: three clicks, a pause, two clicks. The expedition's emergency code. The rhythm Sesay had drilled into every member of her team at Wewak.

Someone ahead of us was still alive — and they were signaling, over and over, into a dark that had just grown a searching engine behind us.

"Tell me that's your people," I whispered to Sesay.

She was already sitting up, fever and all, her eyes fixed on the dark. "That's my people," she said. "And Halloran — they wouldn't signal unless they were out of time."
