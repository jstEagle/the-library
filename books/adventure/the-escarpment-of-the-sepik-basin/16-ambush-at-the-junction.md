## 16. Ambush at the Junction

The compass had been lying to me for three weeks, or telling me the truth in a language I hadn't learned to read yet. Every night when we made camp I set it on a flat stone and watched the needle swing off true north by eleven degrees, patient as a debt collector, pointing into the tangle of country ahead of us where no map showed anything but green hatching and a river that changed its mind every wet season.

The guides thought it was broken. Tobias thought it was cursed. Sesay, feverish on a stretcher of lashed saplings, thought it was sentimental nonsense and said so with the crisp authority of a woman running a hundred-and-two temperature.

"It's a compass," she'd told me from under the tarpaulin, through chattering teeth. "It points north. That's its whole personality."

"Except it doesn't," I said.

"Then it's broken."

"Then it's been broken the same way since 1961," I said, "because my father's margin notes on the old alignment maps correct for exactly this error. Eleven degrees west of grid north. He wrote it in the same hand he used to sign my birthday cards, which is to say a hand that couldn't be bothered."

Sesay closed her eyes. "Your father was a strange man."

"He was a thief," I said, "who happened to be precise about it."

But here's the thing about precision: it survives the man. The compass wasn't broken. It was *tuned* — weighted, I suspected now, or simply manufactured with an offset, so that whoever carried it would always know which way was true north by subtracting the lie. And the lie pointed somewhere. It had been pointing somewhere since Wewak, since upriver, since the sago swamps where we'd lost two days and most of our dignity to waist-deep water and leeches with ambitions. Eleven degrees west of north, always, like a finger pressed against the back of my neck.

On the morning of the ninth day out of the mission station, the finger stopped pressing. We crested a ridge of compacted laterite — ballast, I realized, actual crushed-stone ballast, laid in a bed sixty years dead — and the needle settled dead center for the first time since Port Moresby.

We were standing on the junction.

* * *

You'd have missed it if you weren't looking. The bush had taken the spur line back the way the bush takes everything in the Sepik Basin — politely, thoroughly, without malice. But railbeds are stubborn things. They drain themselves. A hundred meters below the ridge, cutting through the secondary growth, ran a straight corridor of drier ground, weeds waist-high instead of head-high, and along it, surfacing through the mud like bones through skin, the rusted spines of rails.

I walked down alone. Some things you don't want witnesses for, and some things you do; I hadn't decided yet which this was.

The junction itself was a triangle of three alignments meeting at a point where someone had once installed a water tower. The tower's legs were still there, four iron columns gone to orange lace, and beneath them the ground was littered with the usual archaeology of abandonment: a coupling pin fused to its socket, the neck of a bottle, a boot sole. And then, half-buried in the silt where the third leg met its footing, a length of pipe capped with a threaded plug, too clean.

Sixty years of rain doesn't leave brass threads bright. Someone had maintained this.

I knelt. My hands were steady, which surprised me, because inside they were doing what my hands used to do before exams, before board reviews, before the inquiry that ended my career — that low-frequency hum of dread that feels almost like excitement if you don't look at it directly. I unscrewed the cap. Inside the pipe, wrapped in waxed canvas over oiled silk over more waxed canvas, in layers applied by someone who understood moisture the way priests understand sin:

Documents.

Duplicates. Originals of the originals. The 1959 permit surveys in my father's drafting hand — and beside them, the *actual* surveys, the ones showing the alignments ran through land the concession never held, the ones that had been falsified down to the contour lines. Correspondence on letterhead I recognized from the expedition's files: the financier's own signature, instructing, approving, acknowledging. Dates. Amounts. Names of officials long dead whose signatures had been purchased for sums itemized to the shilling.

And a ledger. His ledger. The one I'd already seen a copy of among Sesay's documents, except this one had pages the copy didn't — final pages, written later, in a hand gone shakier, recording not bribes but *returns*. Money paid back. Confessions drafted and redrafted. One letter, addressed to a ministry office in Port Moresby, stamped but unsent, found after his death in a drawer by a lawyer who'd shipped it to a son who'd thrown it in a drawer of his own and left it there for six years while his life caught fire.

I sat down in the mud under the dead water tower and read my father's handwriting until the light went long and gold through the weeds, and I will not pretend I felt nothing. I felt the whole ugly braid of it — fury that he'd done it, grief that he'd tried to undo it, and underneath both, the humiliating discovery that he'd trusted *me*, or the idea of me, enough to hide the truth of his life within reach of a compass bearing only I could follow. He'd built me a treasure hunt out of his crimes. He'd died before I opened the first clue.

Tobias found me there at dusk. He looked at the papers, looked at my face, and said, in his unhurried way, "You have been sitting in the rain."

It had started raining. I hadn't noticed.

"We should move," he said. "This place is too easy to find."

He was right, and I knew he was right, and I stayed another twenty minutes anyway, photographing every page with Marta's camera while the rain came harder, because triumph makes you stupid in exactly the way grief does, and I was full of both.

* * *

Marta was triumphant in a different register — the cold incandescent kind, the kind that makes lists. She spread the duplicates under the tarp next to Sesay's stretcher and began cross-referencing against her own reconstruction, page by page, murmuring numbers like a woman counting her way out of a burning building.

"This closes it," she said. "The correspondence names him directly. Not 'the company.' Him. With dates that match the permit file. Sesay — Sesay?" She touched the older woman's shoulder. Sesay's fever had broken that morning, leaving her weak and clear-eyed and meaner than ever, which we'd all agreed was a good sign.

"I heard you the first time," Sesay said. "Get them dry. Paper rots faster than men out here, and we've proven the second part."

The guides — old Yawane and his nephew Boas — took the news differently. Boas grinned and wanted to know if the papers were worth money. Yawane wanted to know if we were leaving tonight. When Marta explained, again, that the papers were worth justice, Boas exchanged a look with his uncle that I'd learned to read over nine days of swamp: the look of men who have heard about justice the way coastal people hear about snow.

Yawane said we should leave tonight anyway. For different reasons. He'd found a cigarette end near the eastern alignment that afternoon, fresh, and he didn't smoke, and neither did anyone in our party, and he considered this information sufficient.

I should have listened to Yawane. I want that on the record, wherever records go. I should have listened to Yawane.

But the junction was the first dry, defensible ground we'd held in a week, and Sesay was too weak to travel hard, and I told myself one night behind the dead water tower, with sentries rotated and fires small, was arithmetic any reasonable man would sign off on. I was reasonable. I had been reasonable my whole life, right up until the moment reason stopped being available to me, and I signed off on the night.

* * *

They came an hour before dawn, which is when professionals come, because that's when sentries are tired and the dark is thinnest and men's minds are already halfway to morning.

I was awake. I'd been sleeping badly since the swamps, in the shallow greedy way of a man who keeps checking that his hands still work. I heard it first as absence — the frogs along the railbed corridor going quiet in a moving line, west to east, like lights being switched off down a street. Then Yawane's hand on my shoulder, no shake to it at all, just pressure, and his voice at my ear: "Company."

There were six of them, maybe seven, coming up the old railbed in loose file, and they were good. No lights, no talk, moving on the ballast where their boots made no sound. Calloway hadn't come himself. This was work for employees.

What happened next happened in pieces, and I've assembled it since from what I saw, what Tobias saw, and what Boas told us afterward in a voice that had aged forty years overnight.

Yawane did not wake the camp. He woke the camp's *escape*. He took the document case — I'd slept with it under my head like a fool child, and he lifted it from beside me without disturbing my breathing, which I still think about — and he put it in Boas's arms, and he pointed Boas, Marta, and the stretcher with Sesay down the southern alignment, away from the water tower, toward the river cut we'd scouted that evening and dismissed as too steep for a stretcher party. Too steep for a stretcher party in daylight, with time. Yawane had apparently held a different opinion, formed sometime during the night, and he did not share it with me because sharing it would have cost seconds.

Then he picked up the machete and the old signal lamp we'd salvaged from the cache, and he walked toward the eastern alignment, and he turned the lamp on.

That was the plan. I understand that now. I didn't understand it then; I understood it around the fourth heartbeat, when the lead man's rifle came up toward the light and Yawane's voice rang out across the junction — loud, mocking, in pidgin and then in English, calling them thieves and worse, naming Calloway, daring them — and every muzzle in the dark swung toward the lamp and away from the south.

They shot the lamp. Of course they shot the lamp; it was glass and kerosene and it exploded beautifully, and in the flare of it I saw Yawane silhouetted, already moving, already angling north along the western alignment, drawing them after him the way a wounded bird draws a cat, and I understood that this whole performance — the lamp, the insults, the noise — was a man spending himself deliberately, buying us minutes with the only currency he had left.

"Tobias." My voice came out wrong, high. "South. Now."

We went. Me, Tobias, Boas with the case strapped across his back, Marta half-carrying, half-dragging Sesay's stretcher down a cut that in daylight I'd have called impassable and in the dark called nothing at all, because there was no time for words, only for foot placement and breath. Behind us the shooting started — not wild, controlled, professional bursts — and then stopped, and started again further north, chasing, and I counted shots the way you count anything when counting is all you can do.

Boas stopped at the top of the river cut. Turned around. The firing had moved off north, but it had also gone quiet in a way that meant something, and Boas was nineteen years old and his uncle was somewhere in that dark making noise on purpose, and I watched the boy do the arithmetic every family in this country has had to do, and I watched the answer come.

"He is buying us the river," Boas said. "So we take the river."

"Boas—"

"If I go back, he has died for nothing twice." He said it flatly, without drama, the way you'd state a survey result. Then he turned and went down the cut ahead of us, and did not look back again, and I have seen men hold their nerve under fire with less than that boy showed climbing down a cliff in the dark carrying his uncle's life's dignity in a canvas case.

* * *

We reached the river at first gray light. Tobias knew a fishing camp — two canoes under palm thatch, hidden, his own arrangement from the upriver days, because Tobias arranged things the way rivers arrange gravel: quietly, continuously, and with total commitment. We got Sesay into the larger canoe. Marta sat with the document case between her feet and her arms wrapped around it, and for a long moment nobody spoke, and the river did what the river does, which is carry on regardless.

The firing had stopped completely by then. Somewhere north of us, in the gray beginning of the morning, the junction was quiet.

Boas stood in the shallows holding the painter of the canoe, and he looked north, and I looked north, and neither of us said the thing.

"He'll come downriver," I said finally. "He knows this country better than—" 

"Ines." Marta's voice. Not unkind. Just finished. "You know what a rearguard is."

I knew what a rearguard was. I was a railroad engineer; I'd studied the retreats of armies the way other men study cricket scores. A rearguard buys time with itself. That was the entire transaction. Yawane had known it when he picked up the lamp, and Boas had known it when he turned south, and I had known it too, in the part of me that had been screaming since the frogs went silent, and I had let a seventy-year-old man walk into the dark and spend himself because I was busy being triumphant over a pipe full of paper.

Sesay, propped against the canoe's gunwale, gray-faced and clear-eyed, watched me stand there drowning in it.

"Halloran," she said. "Look at me."

I looked at her.

"You're carrying his sacrifice now. Both of them. The old man and your father, and God help us, probably mine before this is over." She coughed, a deep wet sound. "You don't get to drop them in the shallows and grieve. You get to carry them to the coast and put them in front of the whole world. That's the only funeral they get. So get in the boat."

I got in the boat.

Tobias pushed us off into the current, and the junction fell away behind us into the mist, and the last thing I saw of it was the four rusted legs of the water tower standing over the empty railbed like the ribs of something enormous that had lain down there sixty years ago and was only now, finally, being allowed to die.

The compass, in my breast pocket, pointed eleven degrees west of true north, straight back the way we'd come.

Ahead of us lay five days of river, one security chief who now knew exactly what we carried, and a coast where everything would be decided by whether we could arrive before the permits expired.

I put the compass away. I didn't need it anymore. From here on, there was only one direction, and it was downhill, and everyone on the river knew the way.
