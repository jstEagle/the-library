## 17. The Bargain

There is a particular kind of silence that comes over a man when he realizes the arithmetic has finally gone his way. I have felt it twice in my life: once when I closed my first contract, and once now, standing on a mudbank above the Karawari with four exhausted fugitives pinned against dead water and my own patience.

I want to be clear about something, because history — if anyone bothers to write it, and someone will, because I intend to pay them to — will paint me as a thug. I am not a thug. Thugs are men who enjoy the work. I do not enjoy it. I respect it. There is a difference, and the difference is that thugs make mistakes out of appetite, and I have never once made a mistake out of appetite. Every move I've made on this river has been cold, sequenced, and defensible in a courtroom, which is more than I can say for the people I'm hunting.

Take tonight. A lesser man would have burned their boat at the mission and called it done. I did burn their boat at the mission — that was Tuesday, and it was elegant — but burning boats only strands people. Stranded people get creative. Creative people find other boats, or walk, or die of fever in ways that generate questions from missionaries, who are the single most dangerous class of witness in this country because they cannot be bought and they write everything down.

So instead of chasing them into the sago swamps, where my trackers would lose a week and possibly a man to the leeches, I simply read the map better than they did. The swamps end. Everything ends. Water runs downhill, and downhill from the junction there is exactly one stretch of bank firm enough to camp on, one channel deep enough for a loaded boat, and one place within walking distance where a party carrying an injured woman could conceivably stop. I put two boats on the water and waited three days, and this morning they came to me like freight on a schedule.

That is not violence. That is logistics. I wish more people understood the distinction; it would save everyone a great deal of unpleasantness.

* * *

I watched them come down the cut bank through my binoculars and I confess I felt something adjacent to admiration. Halloran had rigged a crossing over a flooded creek with rope and two felled saplings, and he'd carried the Sesay woman across it himself, which told me two things: first, that the guides trusted him now, which was a problem; second, that he had stopped thinking like a fugitive and started thinking like an engineer, which was a bigger problem. Fugitives run until they're caught. Engineers build things, and then you have to deal with what they built.

The guides saw us first — they always do, they're worth every kina — and there was a long moment on the water where Tobias, the boatman, put his paddle down and looked at me across forty meters of brown river with an expression I can only describe as professional assessment. He was pricing the situation. I respected that too. Everyone on this river is pricing something.

"Mr. Halloran," I said, when the boat grounded and the moment had settled into its final shape. "Dr. Sesay. Gentlemen."

I use honorifics deliberately. It costs nothing and it establishes that I am a man who notices titles, which suggests I am a man who notices everything, which is true, and fear travels faster through detail than through shouting.

They were in poor condition, all of them. Sesay was gray around the mouth and lying flat in the bottom of the boat with her arm splinted by someone who clearly knew what he was doing — Halloran again, presumably; the man has a whole second career waiting for him in field medicine if this one falls through. One of the guides had a bandage at his ribs. Marta Ondoua stood at the bow with her chin up and her hands empty, which I noted, because geologists always keep their hands near rocks, and hers weren't, which meant she'd decided in advance how this meeting would go. Good. Decisions made in advance save time.

Halloran stepped onto the bank and looked at my men — six of them visible, two not, which he probably guessed — and then at me, and I watched him do the thing I knew he would do, which was count exits. There were none. I'd chosen the bank myself, weeks ago, off a survey map nobody else had bothered to read closely. The concession pays me to read maps closely. It is genuinely the only part of this enterprise that functions as advertised.

"You've been following us since the mission," he said.

"Since before the mission." I let that sit. "Since the culvert. You moved well through the swamps, Mr. Halloran. Better than I expected. I don't say that as flattery. I say it because it's relevant to what I'm about to propose, and I want you to understand that I'm proposing it to a competent man, not to a cornered animal. Cornered animals bite, and then we shoot them, and then I have paperwork. Competent men negotiate, and then I have signatures. I prefer signatures."

Ondoua said something sharp in French under her breath — she thinks I don't speak it, which is charming — and Sesay lifted her head from the boat boards and looked at me with the specific hatred of a scientist who has been lied to with data. I have been hated by experts before. It doesn't trouble me. Experts hate accurately, and accurate hatred means they understand their position, which makes my work easier.

But I wasn't here for them. Not really. They were leverage, context, the furniture of the negotiation. I was here for the box, and for the man carrying it, and most of all for the arithmetic inside the man carrying it, which I had spent eleven days reconstructing from his file, and which I now laid out on the table between us like a hand of cards I already knew he held.

* * *

Here is what I know about Ines Halloran, and I want it on the record that I did my homework, because amateurs threaten and professionals offer:

He owes money. Not abstractly, not morally — specifically, verifiably, to men in Port Moresby whose patience is measured in weeks and whose methods are measured in hospital stays. His debt came due the same season his name came apart, and both trace back to the Kandrian viaduct, where a stress calculation bearing his signature turned out to have been signed by a man who was, at the hour of signing, drunk in a hotel bar two provinces away. Whether he actually falsified it or merely inherited the blame from a superior who retired comfortably before the inquiry — and my reading of the file says the latter, though the file also says the inquiry was steered, and steered by whom is a question I found interesting for reasons that will become apparent — the result was the same. License suspended. Name ruined. Creditors circling. A man with no future hiding in a radio room scheduling transmissions for wages so low they were practically a form of charity.

Which raises the question of why such a man copied our concession files before resigning, walked into the interior alone, and followed a dead railway to find strangers who owed him nothing. Sentiment, Ondoua would say. Conscience. I have another word for it, but I'll hold it, because holding words is most of my job.

And here is the other thing I know, the thing that took my researchers three days in the Wewak land registry and one very expensive afternoon with a retired clerk who remembered everything: the father. Thomas Halloran. Assistant alignment engineer on the original Sepik spur construction, 1959 through 1963, the line the company now claims never legally existed. The same man whose compass the son carries — yes, we know about the compass; my trackers described a brass instrument checked nightly at every camp, and a man checking a compass nightly in terrain he's navigating by river is not navigating, he's communing — and the same man whose ledger sits in that box Dr. Sesay nearly died carrying. Bribes taken. Alignments bent. A confession written and never sent.

The son does not know yet that the confession is in the box. Or he learned it recently — the way he moved after the junction changed, heavier, like a man carrying two loads. Either way, he knows enough. And a man who has just learned that his entire ruin has a family resemblance — that dishonesty is not something that happened *to* the Hallorans but something practiced *by* them, one generation funding the next disgrace — a man like that is standing in a doorway between two lives, and doorways are where I do my best work.

Because consider his position honestly. Strip away the romance of it, the swamp heroics, the dying-in-a-good-cause of it all, and look at the ledger of outcomes:

If he goes forward: he delivers documents to a journalist, the story breaks, the permits lapse, the company refiles or collapses — either way, the matter ends in lawyers that will consume five years, during which Ines Halloran remains what he is today: broke, disgraced, unlicensed, and indebted to creditors who do not care about his conscience. The financier might even walk; financiers usually do; they are built of shell companies precisely so that justice, which is a slow animal, loses their scent. And Halloran gets, for his trouble, a paragraph in the reporting. *A source close to the expedition.* Maybe, if the journalist is generous, a name.

If he deals with me: the debt disappears by lunchtime tomorrow — not paid, *dissolved*, purchased through channels that exist for exactly this purpose and that I know how to operate blindfolded. The viaduct file is revisited. I cannot promise a full restoration of his license — I am many things but I am not a liar about mechanics, only about motives — but I can promise a hearing, and I can promise that the evidence at that hearing will be curated by friends of mine, and hearings with curated evidence go one way. Within a year, Ines Halloran is an engineer again, with a name, with a wage, with a future that does not require him to sleep in sago mud and watch his friends bleed.

That is the offer. It is a good offer. I want to be honest about that, because honesty in the mouth of a man like me is more persuasive than any threat, and because — this is the part I ask you to believe — I mean it. I have no intention of double-crossing him. Double-crossing is for men who need to double-cross. I need a signature and silence, and both are cheaper than a grave, and graves on this river have a way of surfacing, literally, after floods, with belongings attached, and belongings generate inquiries, and inquiries are the one weather I cannot forecast.

* * *

So I made the offer standing up, on the bank, in front of everyone, which was deliberate. Negotiations conducted privately can be denied later. Negotiations conducted publicly become commitments, and commitments are cages, and I wanted him to see the cage while the door was still open.

"The documents," I said. "All of them. The ledger, the permit surveys, whatever your father cached at the junction — and yes, Mr. Halloran, we know about the cache, we found the site the day after you left it, and I'll note that leaving it intact cost one of your guides his life, which I regret, because that man was following orders from a leader who didn't know when a position was untenable, and knowing when a position is untenable is the entire job."

Sesay made a sound at that. I let it pass. Cruelty is a tool with a narrow handle and I try not to pick it up without cause, but sometimes a wound properly located saves three wounds badly placed later.

"In exchange: your debt, extinguished. Your name, restored through a process that will take months and cost me more than the documents are worth, which should tell you something about how much the documents are worth. Safe passage for everyone in this boat — everyone, including the doctor, including the geologist, including the boatman, who I'm aware has been listening to every word and pricing every word, and Tobias, if you're wondering, your rate just went up, see me after. And Mr. Halloran — a career. A real one. Railways, if you want railways. There are lines in the Highlands that need rebuilding by men who understand why lines fail, and you understand why lines fail better than any engineer currently licensed in this territory, because you failed one personally."

I heard Ondoua exhale through her teeth. I heard one of the guides shift his weight, and I noted which hand moved toward which pocket, and I noted that my second boat had drifted ten meters closer during my speech, which it was supposed to do, timed to my phrasing, because I rehearse.

Halloran stood very still. That was the thing I hadn't fully priced — his stillness. Most men, offered rescue from ruin, leak relief somewhere. A twitch, a swallow, a glance at the sky as if checking whether God is watching and whether He approves. He did none of it. He looked at me the way I imagine he looks at a bridge showing hairline cracks: measuring whether it holds, not whether he likes it.

"And the others?" he said. "Sesay's report. Ondoua's findings. What happens to what *they* know?"

"They keep their knowledge," I said. "Knowledge isn't contraband. But knowledge without documents is testimony, and testimony without corroboration is a newspaper article, and newspaper articles have the shelf life of fish. I'm not asking them to lie. I'm asking them to be unpublishable. There's a difference, and the difference is that no one gets hurt enforcing it."

"That's not true," Sesay said from the boat, quietly, without lifting her head further than the effort allowed. "You've hurt people already. The guide at the junction. The men at the villages who talked to us. You burned a mission's boat."

"I burned a boat," I said. "The guide died because your position was indefensible and your leader defended it anyway. As for the villagers — I pay them. Generously. Ask them. The difference between me and the government survey teams is that my money arrives." I paused, and then I gave her the one sentence I had been saving, the one I'd rehearsed in the boat at dawn, because it was true and because truth, deployed correctly, is the sharpest object I carry. "Doctor, I have read your interim reports. You are a better scientist than the company deserves and a worse negotiator than I am, and I say that with genuine respect. Respect is not mercy. But it is not contempt either, and I want you to know which one you're getting."

She looked at me for a long moment, and I had the uncomfortable sensation — rare, unwelcome — of being *read*. Scientists do that. They take measurements of you whether you consent or not.

Then Halloran spoke, and what he said was not what I had scripted for him in any of my rehearsals, which is why I hire trackers instead of fortune-tellers.

"One night," he said.

I waited.

"I'll give you your answer at dawn. Not before. And nobody moves, nobody follows, nobody burns anything between now and then. You want a considered answer from a competent man? Then you get the whole package. I think better in the dark, and I think best near water."

I considered refusing. A night is a variable; variables breed escape attempts; my instinct said take the box now and negotiate terms from strength. But the box was in the boat, and the boat was in my reach, and taking it by force in front of witnesses converted my clean offer into a seizure, and seizures invite inquiries, and inquiries are the weather I cannot forecast. Besides — and here I'll admit something I admit to no one — I wanted him to spend the night with the offer. I wanted him to feel its actual weight, not the weight he'd assign it in the heat of the moment, when refusing me is easy because refusing is free. Refusing at dawn, after a night of honest arithmetic, with his creditors' faces and the viaduct file and the word *restored* sitting in his head like a lamp — that refusal would mean something. And if he accepted, it would mean more, because it would be chosen rather than broken.

"One night," I agreed. "My boats stay on the water. My men stay armed. If anyone enters the river before dawn, the agreement converts to the other arrangement, and I'll grieve the conversion briefly and professionally." I extended my hand. He didn't take it. I withdrew it without comment; the non-handshake went into my ledger too, filed under *data*.

He turned to his people and said something low in the language the guides use among themselves, and Tobias laughed — actually laughed, a short bark of a man who has just heard a joke about drowning — and then they pushed off to a sandbar midstream, thirty meters out, beyond wading, within sight, and made camp in the open with a small fire like people who had decided that dignity was also a resource and they intended to spend some.

I sat on the bank with my thermos and my notebook and I wrote down the terms, and then I sat a long time watching their fire, and I will tell you plainly what I felt, because a man who lies to himself about small things eventually lies about large ones:

I hoped he'd take it.

Not for the company. The company is a structure; structures survive any outcome; if the permits lapse tomorrow the principals refile under new names by Christmas and I will be employed by whoever signs the new papers, because security chiefs are load-bearing and everyone knows it. No — I hoped he'd take it for the reason I built the offer the way I did: because it was fair. Because the man behind him on that sandbar had been ruined by a system that ate his father and then ate him, and here, at last, at the bottom of the river, at the end of every road his compass pointed down, someone was offering to make him whole, and the price was only the truth.

It seemed, sitting there in the dark with the fire reflected in the current, like a reasonable price. Truth keeps poorly on rivers. Truth is heavy, and wet, and it gets people killed — I could point to the fresh mound upstream where they'd buried the guide, if pointing were permitted.

Dawn was six hours off. I finished my tea, checked my watches — I carry three, set to different authorities, because dawn is a legal boundary and I do not guess at legal boundaries — and waited for Ines Halloran to decide who he was.

Across the water, the fire burned low. Once, near midnight, I saw him take out the brass compass and hold it flat on his palm for a long time, longer than navigation requires, turning slowly, the needle finding whatever it finds.

I wrote one more line in the notebook, dated and timed, in case it mattered later:

*Subject awake all night. Subject looking north.*

I did not like what the entry implied. I entered it anyway.
