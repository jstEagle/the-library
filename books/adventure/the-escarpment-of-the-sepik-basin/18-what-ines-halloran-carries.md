## 18. What Ines Halloran Carries

I did not sleep. That was the arrangement, after all—one night to decide, and a night is only worth having if you spend all of it awake.

Calloway's men had withdrawn to the far bank of the oxbow, and we could see their fires through the screen of pandanus, three orange points arranged with the tidy geometry of men who expected to be paid by the hour. They had left us the ruined mission boat, half-sunk in the shallows, as a kind of courtesy. Or a kind of message. With Calloway the two were usually the same thing.

I sat against a sago palm with my father's ledger open on my knees, though I had stopped reading it hours ago. The words had worn grooves in me instead. *Alignment adjusted, Station 41, per instruction. Consideration received.* Page after page of it, in that confident slanted hand I had first seen in the margins of a survey map and had spent my whole life pretending not to recognize. He had written numbers the way other men wrote prayers—neatly, devoutly, and in service of something rotten.

Two hundred and forty pounds. That was what he'd taken at Station 41. I knew because I had inherited a different sum: eleven thousand, four hundred, and change, owed to men in Port Moresby who expressed their disappointment through intermediaries with cricket bats. My father's sin had been smaller and larger at once. Smaller in the arithmetic. Larger in everything else.

"You're going to do the accounting," Sesay said.

She had come to sit beside me without my noticing, which for a woman with a bullet-grazed hip and three days of fever behind her was its own quiet indictment of my situational awareness. She lowered herself with the care of someone handling her own body like borrowed equipment.

"I'm always doing the accounting," I said. "It's the one thing I'm qualified for."

"Then do this one out loud. It helps."

So I did, because she was Dr. Amara Sesay and because there was nothing else left to do with the night.

"Item," I said. "Rhys Calloway offers to wipe eleven thousand four hundred from my ledger. Item: restoration of my name, my license, my standing—the whole disgrace reversed, stamped and filed, presumably with the same efficiency his employer falsifies permits." I turned a page I didn't need to turn. "Item: all I have to do is hand over the box."

"The box being every original document proving the concession was built on fraud."

"The box being that, yes. And item—" I stopped. The river made its small patient noises against the hull of the dead boat. "Item: Ekwueme."

Sesay said nothing for a while. Our guide. Our friend. The man who had stood in the junction clearing with a machete and no rifle and shouted so that Calloway's trackers would look at him and not at us, and who had bought our forty seconds with his life. We had buried him under a cairn of rail ballast, because even out here, even on a line that had been murdered twice—once by dynamite and once by paperwork—we gave him railway stones.

"Calloway didn't pull the trigger," Sesay said finally. "But he signed the order that put the man with the trigger on that trail. In your profession, Mr. Halloran, what do we call the person who signs off on a load the bridge can't carry?"

"We call him liable."

"And if he offers you money afterward?"

"Then we call him worse things, but some men take the money anyway." I closed the ledger. "That's the whole history of my family in one sentence."

She looked at me then, and the fever had burned most of the softness out of her face, leaving something cleaner. "Some debts aren't paid in money," she said. "You know that already. You've known it since the culvert. You're just hoping one of us will say it so you can stop carrying it alone."

* * *

Dawn came up the way it does in the Basin—not arriving so much as seeping, the grey water turning pewter, then pearl, the mist lifting off the oxbow in slow rags. The birds started their argument. Somewhere across the river, Calloway's camp woke in orderly increments: voices, the clatter of a kettle, an engine coughing itself awake.

An engine. I lay still and listened to it the way I used to listen to locomotives from the footplate—a full inventory taken through the ears alone. A diesel auxiliary, small, running rough on the low end. Then a second one catching. Then the particular rhythmic slosh-and-thrum of a pump transferring fuel between tanks.

Fuel caches. Of course. Calloway ran a long game down long rivers; he would have staged drums at every bend where a fleeing party might need them or might pass within stealing distance. Control the fuel and you control the map. It was the oldest trick in logistics, and I had been a logistics man before I was a disgraced one.

Marta found me staring at the far bank. She had leaves in her hair and the expression of a woman who had spent the night doing sums she hated.

"He'll renew the offer at first light," she said. "They always do. It's cheaper than violence."

"I know."

"And you're going to refuse."

It wasn't a question, but I made her ask it properly, because I owed her that much honesty. "Am I?"

"Ines." She sat down beside me, and Tobias drifted over too, silent as ever, folding himself onto a root with the patience of furniture. Even the guides, at a respectful distance, were watching. Everyone knew. There are no private decisions in a party of seven.

"Here's my problem," I told Marta. "If I say yes, I sleep indoors again. I get my name back. I get to walk into a room and have people assume I'm honest before I've opened my mouth. Do you know what that's worth to a man who hasn't had it in six years?"

"Yes," she said. "And here's mine. If you say yes, the company refiles those permits clean on Monday morning, and everything Ekwueme carried that box for becomes weight they died around. And I will have to go back to Wewak and write a paper about soil samples while men like Calloway own the river." She shrugged. "So I'm not neutral. I never claimed to be."

Tobias spoke for the first time since the junction. His English came slowly, each word set down like a stone in a crossing. "My uncle says a man who sells the road walks on it once more only. To leave."

I looked at him. "Your uncle sounds like a railroad man."

"All old men are railroad men now," Tobias said. "The line built them and left."

* * *

Calloway came himself at first light proper, which surprised me until I understood it as theater. He crossed in the aluminum skiff with two men and an umbrella of false politeness, and he stood on the mudbank with his boots dry and his hands visible, a man performing harmlessness.

"Mr. Halloran." He nodded at the ledger in my lap as if greeting a mutual acquaintance. "I trust you slept on it."

"Not a minute of it."

"Good. Decisions made at night are emotional. Decisions made tired are honest." He smiled with exactly the amount of teeth the situation required. "The offer stands. Debt cleared. Name restored. Your license reinstated with an apology from the board, if you want one—you can frame it. In exchange, the documents. All of them. And you and your people walk away rich enough to forget this river."

"And the permits lapse tomorrow," I said. "Which is why today's price is so generous."

"Today's price reflects the market." He glanced past me at Sesay, pale against her palm fronds, and at the guides, and at Marta, and his eyes did the arithmetic of them and dismissed each column. "You should know that if you refuse, I am authorized to continue. This stops being negotiation and becomes operations. People have already died, Mr. Halloran. The number can go up. It rarely goes down."

"That's true," I agreed. "Though in my experience the number goes up fastest for the men holding the pen, not the ones holding the box. Somewhere out there is a journalist named Okon, and somewhere between us and the coast is a ridge with radio line-of-sight, and somewhere in your employer's filing system is a version of these events that doesn't survive daylight. You're not hunting documents anymore, Calloway. You're hunting witnesses, and witnesses multiply."

Something moved behind his calm—not doubt, nothing so useful, but recalculation. "Last chance," he said.

"No," I said.

Just that. No speech. I had rehearsed speeches all night and discovered that every one of them was really addressed to my father, and my father wasn't listening, and hadn't been for thirty years. What remained when I stripped the rhetoric out was very small and very heavy and entirely mine: *No.*

Calloway studied me for a moment the way an engineer studies a structure he suspects is cracked. Then he nodded, almost respectfully, and turned to his men. "Operations," he said, and stepped into the skiff.

* * *

We moved within the hour, because refusing a bargain and surviving the refusal are separate engineering problems.

It was Tobias who solved the second one, or rather it was Tobias who remembered, in the flat way he remembered everything, that the mission station's second boat—old Father Anselm's fishing punt, half-rotted, worthless—lay beached in the reeds two bends downstream. Worthless for escape. But a punt carries weight, and weight, positioned correctly, is called something else.

We reached it by wading the reed shelf, Sesay between Marta and me, her hip held together with strapping and stubbornness. The punt sat in the shallows like a dead animal. I got inside it and looked at what it offered: a flat bottom, a hull that would hold maybe four hundred pounds of cargo, and a bilge full of rainwater and frogs.

Four hundred pounds. Calloway's fuel cache sat on the point downstream, six drums of petrol under a tarpaulin, guarded by one bored man whose attention was fixed on our known position upstream. Six drums, and the company boats moored alongside.

"You want to float a bomb down a river," Marta said. Not disapproving. Checking.

"I want to float a *fire* down a river. The bomb part is incidental and I'd thank everyone to keep it that way." I was already calculating. Current speed, call it three knots. Distance to the point, eight hundred meters. Wind off the water, light, from the east. "Petrol fumes are the payload. The drum seals on those company caches are cheap—I've seen the procurement. Half of them vent. We fill the punt's bilge with fumes, seal it under tarpaulins, weight it to ride low and slow, and let the current deliver it into the middle of their moorings. Then someone puts a flare into it from the far bank."

"Someone," Marta repeated.

"Tobias has the best arm and the best cover position. I'll run the punt into the channel and step off at the last sandbar."

"And if the current is faster than your arithmetic?" Sesay asked from the reeds, her voice thin but level.

"Then I'll have finally done something my father would recognize," I said, "and I'd rather not. Help me load."

We worked fast and silently. Two jerry cans of petrol, siphoned from the punt's own abandoned stores, decanted into shallow pans set in the bilge to evaporate. Tarpaulin lashed tight. Ballast trimmed so she rode with barely a hand's width of freeboard, a grey shape among grey shapes. Tobias took the flare pistol and vanished into the mangrove on the east bank with one of the guides. Marta and the others pulled back along the reed shelf with Sesay, who refused to be carried and refused, also, to say goodbye, which I understood completely.

I pushed the punt into the current and rode it.

There is a strange fellowship between a man and a vessel he expects to die with. I crouched in the bilge fog with my hands flat on the wet planking and felt the river take us both, and I thought—not of the debt, not of the name, but of my father at Station 41, signing his neat numbers, taking his two hundred and forty pounds, telling himself whatever men tell themselves at the moment they choose the smaller life. I had spent six years believing I was running from his shadow. Crouched in a floating bomb on the Karawari, I finally understood I had been running toward the exact spot where I could set it down.

The sandbar came up on the left. I judged it, misjudged it, corrected, and went over the side into chest-deep water with the punt continuing on without me, riding low and patient, a letter addressed in fumes.

I waded ashore and lay in the mud next to Marta, and we watched it drift down the bright morning river toward the moored boats and the bored sentry and the six drums.

"Now," I whispered, though Tobias couldn't possibly have heard me.

The flare went up from the mangroves in a red arc, hung for a heartbeat against the white sky, and dropped.

What happened next happened quickly and loudly. The punt's tarpaulin lifted off whole, a grey wing, and the fumes beneath it caught with a sound less like an explosion than like the world's largest door slamming, and then the fire walked. It crossed the punt to the mooring lines, up the wet rope as if climbing stairs, and found the first drum's cheap vent seal, and after that the morning belonged entirely to noise. The sentry ran. The company boats burned at their moorings, two of them, then a third, black smoke standing up straight in the windless air like columns holding up nothing.

Calloway's men came out of their camp firing wildly at the river, at the fire, at the general concept of betrayal. By the time anyone thought to look upstream, we were already moving—Sesay on Marta's shoulder, the guides flanking, me in front with the compass open in my palm, its needle swinging off true north and settling, faithful as ever, on the bearing that led to the coast and to Okon and to tomorrow's deadline.

Behind us the smoke bent east. Ahead, nineteen days had become one.

"Downriver," I said, and we went.
