## 19. The Descent

Tobias ran the river the way a man argues with someone he loves — fast, intimate, and with a great deal of shouting that wasn't really anger.

"Sit down, engineer!" he bellowed over the engine's hammer. "You want to help, count snags!"

I counted snags. It was not a skill I'd have put on any résumé, but three days in the Sepik's arteries had taught me that the river advertised its lies if you knew how to read them: the V of current folding around a submerged log, the oily smoothness where a sandbar had just shifted, the birds standing on water that wasn't water at all. I called them and Tobias threaded us through, the long boat leaning into turns with a grace that seemed, given its cargo of fevered scientist, two exhausted guides, one geologist, one disgraced engineer, and a waterproof box of documents worth more than everyone aboard combined, frankly miraculous.

Sesay lay in the stern under a tarp we'd rigged against the spray. The wound in her side had gone bad somewhere back in the swamps — Marta said the word "sepsis" quietly, the way you say a word when you don't want it to hear itself — and her fevers came now in waves, each one higher than the last. Between them she was lucid, sharp, and merciless about the schedule.

"How far to the ridge?" she asked me during a lull, her voice scraped thin.

"Half a day, maybe less if the river cooperates."

"The river doesn't cooperate. It tolerates." She almost smiled. "Ask it anyway."

* * *

Calloway was behind us. We knew because Tobias knew — he had cousins along this stretch of the Karawari, and word travels faster than boats on a river where every family is related to every other family by blood, debt, or grievance. Two company boats had passed downriver yesterday, running dark, engines muffled with sacking. The third cousin, a boy of maybe sixteen who poled a dugout loaded with sago, had paddled alongside just long enough to say, in his own language and then in broken Tok Pisin: *The bald one is asking after you. He has money.*

"He always has money," Marta muttered. "It's his entire personality."

We couldn't outrun him forever. Two boats against our one, and his were newer, heavier-engined, and driven by men who didn't have to care whether their hulls survived the trip. But Calloway had a problem too, though I doubted he'd articulated it yet: he needed the documents intact. Dead men floating downriver with a burned box would tell him nothing except that he'd failed. Every hour he spent being careful was an hour we gained, and I clung to that arithmetic like a man clinging to a rail in floodwater.

Marta had her own arithmetic, and hers was worse. Six days left on the permits. Four, maybe five, to reach the coast. One journalist who might or might not still be in Wewak, might or might not still be reachable, might or might not be brave enough — or paid enough by someone else — to sit on what we gave her.

"One night," Marta said to me as we made camp in a bend thick with bamboo, the guides taking first watch without being asked. "Tomorrow I go up to the ridge above the junction where the old survey beacon stands. Line of sight to the relay tower at Ambunti, if the weather holds. I can raise Wewak on the expedition's emergency set."

"You'll get maybe ninety seconds before they triangulate you."

"Ninety seconds is a paragraph." She said it flatly, like a woman quoting scripture. "A paragraph is enough. You give a journalist a paragraph with dates and names in it, she either runs with it or she's bought, and either way we know which world we're living in."

"And if the fragments are wrong? If she prints half a story?"

"Then half a story exists," Marta said, "and half a story cannot be unprinted. That's the whole point. Calloway's company survives on silence. Silence is a thing you can maintain. A fragment is a hole, and holes spread." She looked at me across the fire, and for once there was no irony in her face at all. "Ines. I've been bumped off expeditions, passed over, excluded, erased. I know exactly how institutions make things disappear. You don't fight disappearing with a whole truth delivered neatly. You fight it with splinters they can't pull out."

* * *

Sesay woke at midnight, fever-bright, and asked for me specifically.

I crouched by her tarp. Her eyes were too clear, the way eyes get when the body has decided clarity is cheaper than anything else it could spend.

"Halloran. If I'm not coherent tomorrow, you speak for the expedition. Not Marta — she'll want to argue with the journalist about methodology. You. You understand schedules, deadlines, what a document is *for*." She gripped my wrist with a hand hot as an engine casing. "My father kept ledgers. Did I ever tell you that? Stationmaster, upcountry line, forty years. He used to say a ledger is a promise made to strangers. That's all any of this is. Promises made to strangers, and my lot broke every one."

"You're going to deliver them yourself," I said.

She looked at me with something close to pity. "Engineer. Look at the track ahead of us and tell me what you see."

I looked. I told her the truth, because she'd earned it and because lying to Amara Sesay was like lying to a bridge under load — structurally unsound and morally contemptible. "Six days. Fever. Company boats behind us."

"There it is." She let go of my wrist and settled back. "So. You speak for the expedition. And Halloran — when you give them the documents, you give them everything. My name on the cover sheet. The guides' names. The boy who died at the junction." Her voice caught, just barely, the first crack I'd ever heard in it. "He carried that box four days with a bullet through his thigh. His name goes in the record or none of this means anything."

"It goes in," I said. "First page."

"Good." She closed her eyes. "Now go count snags. It's the only useful thing you do."

* * *

The ridge rose out of the mist the next morning like the spine of some drowned animal, and Marta went up it with the radio in a rucksack and me carrying the spare battery, because she'd decided — correctly — that if she fell off the ridge, the battery shouldn't fall with her.

From the top, the basin opened below us in every direction: green to the horizon, braided silver where the rivers ran, smoke rising thin from villages that had no idea they were sitting on top of a lie worth millions. The relay tower at Ambunti stood up out of the haze to the south, a needle of man's certainty in all that wilderness. Weather held. Blue sky, high cumulus building but not yet built.

Marta assembled the set with hands that didn't shake, which impressed me more than I said aloud. She tuned, found the frequency, and keyed the mic twice — the journalist's call sign, then ours, then a pause long enough to drive a patient man insane.

Static. Then a woman's voice, clipped, wary, professional: *"Identify."*

"Marta Ondoua, geologist, concession survey 7-C. I have original permit documentation indicating falsified alignments and ore extraction on land never legally held. Permits expire in six days. Names, dates, and originals available. Confirm reception."

Static. Longer static. Long enough that Marta's jaw tightened and her finger hovered over the key.

Then: *"Reception partial. Say again your central claim."*

"Falsified permits. Originals in hand. Expiring in six days."

*"Partial again. I have 'falsified' and—" static swallowed the rest "—six days. Repeat location."*

And here was the moment. Here was where I understood what Marta had meant by splinters. Because the smart play — the tradecraft play, the play Calloway would expect — was to repeat everything, full message, giving the triangulation teams time to fix us and the company time to intercept. Ninety seconds was a budget, and budgets get spent.

Marta spent it differently. She sent the fragments. Deliberately. Claim, deadline, a single name — the financier's — and then, as the static chewed the transmission apart, she stopped transmitting entirely and started packing.

"They got maybe sixty percent," I said.

"I know."

"That's not enough to run a story."

"It's enough to start one." She slung the rucksack. "A journalist with sixty percent of a story doesn't wait for the rest. She starts pulling the public record — permits, filings, company registrations — and the public record will give her the other forty percent, because fraud always leaves a paper shadow. All we had to do was tell her the shadow was there." She started down the ridge, fast, sure-footed. "By tonight she'll either be digging or selling us out. Come on. Either way, we've just become expensive."

Behind us, faint but unmistakable, the sound of an engine on the river. More than one.

* * *

Tobias took the news of the pursuit with a shrug that contained multitudes.

"So they come," he said. "Good. Now I stop wasting fuel being careful."

What followed was the worst eight hours of my life and the finest piece of boat-handling I will ever witness, possibly including anything done by professionals with insurance. Tobias ran the lower Karawari like a man reading his own handwriting: channels I'd have sworn were dead ends opened at the last second; rapids I'd have portaged around he took head-on with a shout that was half prayer and half insult; and twice he cut the engine entirely and drifted us, silent as driftwood, past company camps close enough that I could hear a radio playing inside a tent.

Between rapids, I worked on Sesay. Marta had left instructions written on the back of a map — antibiotics, doses, times — and I administered them with the grim precision of a man filling out forms, because forms were the only language my hands knew how to speak when they were frightened. The fever broke once, around midafternoon, and she surfaced long enough to ask whether the transmission had gone out.

"Partially."

"Partially is how everything true gets said," she murmured, and went under again.

The guides, Kila and young Anis — Anis, whose brother was the boy from the junction, whose name I had promised would go on the first page — took turns watching the river behind us. At the fourth hour Anis tapped my shoulder and pointed.

Two boats. Far back, but closing. Running open throttle now, no more pretense of stealth. Calloway had stopped being careful, which meant he'd stopped believing he could take the documents intact, which meant the arithmetic I'd been clinging to had just expired.

"Tobias," I called forward. "How much farther to the main channel?"

"Two hours. Then the current helps us." He glanced back at the specks of the pursuing boats, and something in his face made me understand that he'd already done this calculation several times and hadn't liked any of the answers. "But they reach the main channel one hour after us. One hour is nothing, engineer. Their boats are faster."

"Then we don't race them."

"No?"

"We break the race." I pulled the concession files' route notes from my shirt — the ones describing the fuel caches the company had staged along the lower river for its patrol boats, marked on maps drawn by men who assumed no one outside the company would ever read them. "There's a cache at the confluence. Diesel drums, probably a skiff, maybe a generator. If we get there first—"

Tobias grinned, and it was not a comforting sight. "If we get there first, there is no race."

* * *

We reached the confluence as the light went copper, and we got there first by eleven minutes, which Tobias informed me was exactly enough time to ruin a man's entire operation if you spent them correctly.

We spent them correctly.

Kila and Anis punctured what drums we could and rolled the rest into the current, where they bobbed downstream like fat brown ducks toward the pursuing boats. The diesel sheen spreading on the water was, I'll admit, an environmental crime, and I intend to answer for it someday, cheerfully, to anyone who asks. The skiff we holed and sank. The generator Tobias disabled with a machete and a philosophy I chose not to inquire into.

Then we were back on the river, running hard for the main channel, and eleven minutes later the first company boat rounded the bend into a confluence that had nothing left to offer it, and I heard — faintly, gloriously — the sound of an engine coughing on contaminated fuel, and then a second engine coughing worse.

Anis laughed until he had to hold onto the gunwale.

But I didn't laugh, because I'd learned on this river that Calloway did not stay beaten; he recalculated. Somewhere behind us a man with a satellite phone was calling ahead to Wewak, to the wharves, to whoever owned whatever waited between us and the sea. We had taken his boats. We had not taken his money, his lawyers, or his patience, and only one of those three was finite.

Five days left on the permits. Three, maybe, to the coast. A journalist holding sixty percent of a story. A fevered woman in the stern who had made me promise to speak for the expedition, and a box of promises made to strangers riding against my knees.

The main channel opened ahead of us, wide and brown and running fast toward the coast, and Tobias opened the throttle, and the river — tolerant, at last, for whatever reason rivers have — carried us down.
