## 20. Permits Expiring

The river gave us the sea at half past four in the morning, and it did it the way the Sepik does everything — without ceremony, without a shoreline so much as a suggestion, the water going brown to grey to something older and saltier that Tobias could taste before any of us could see it.

"Salt," he said, and spat over the side with what I can only describe as professional contempt. "River's done with us."

I wished I shared his certainty. Behind us, somewhere in that brown vastness, Calloway's boat was running dark with its engine muffled, and ahead of us was a wharf at Wewak that I had been imagining for eleven days as a kind of salvation and was now, in the last hour, beginning to imagine as a killing floor. Sesay lay in the bow with her fever finally broken but her leg swollen to twice its proper architecture, and she had spent the night doing something that frightened me more than Calloway did: she was reading the documents. All of them. Page by page, by the light of a shielded torch, while the boat slapped through the dark.

"You should rest," I said.

"I should know what I'm dying over," she said. "In case I have to testify about it from a horizontal position."

Marta was at the stern with the radio, running her last battery down to nothing, sending fragments to a journalist named Okon in Port Moresby. Fragments — that had been her strategy, and I'll admit I'd thought it was desperation dressed up as tactics. Transmit in pieces, never enough for the company's monitors to reconstruct, just enough that a man three hundred kilometers away would know the story existed and that other people knew he knew. A partial transmission, Marta said, is a loaded gun on a table. Nobody in the room can pretend it isn't there.

"Okon's confirmed," she said now. "He's on the morning flight. He lands at seven-forty."

"Permits lapse when?" Tobias asked. He asked it the way he'd asked it every day for a week, and every day one of us answered, and every day the number had been smaller.

"Midnight tonight," Marta said. "Company time. Which means if the documents aren't public before midnight, they refile clean tomorrow morning and everything Sesay's guides bled for becomes a rounding error."

* * *

We came into Wewak with the sun, and I want to record honestly what I felt, because I have spent most of my life recording things dishonestly and I'm trying to break the habit. I did not feel triumphant. I felt like a man carrying a box of glass across a floor he knows is rigged. The wharf was there, tin-roofed and rust-streaked, smelling of diesel and dried fish and the particular sweet rot of a coastal town that has been promised prosperity by four separate governments. There were copses of people — stevedores, a woman selling betel nut, two policemen leaning against a Land Cruiser with the particular bonelessness of men who have been on shift too long.

And there was a boat.

It was moored at the far end of the wharf, white-hulled, too clean for the harbour, and standing on its deck with a thermos in one hand was a man in a khaki shirt who did not look at us, which is how I knew he had been waiting. Calloway's men were never obvious. That was the whole art of them. They stood where a man would naturally stand, holding what a man would naturally hold, and you only understood afterward that the entire waterfront had been arranged around you like a sentence with you as the final word.

"Tobias," I said.

"I see them." He was already turning the boat, casual as a man finding a parking spot, taking us toward the beach landing two hundred meters down instead of the wharf proper. "You go by land. I go by water. We arrive at the same place."

"The documents—"

"Stay with you. That was always the plan." He glanced at me, and there was something in his face I hadn't seen before, or hadn't let myself see: he was afraid, and he was doing it anyway. "Ines. Eleven days I've watched you carry that box. You carry it better than any of us could. Go."

* * *

We landed on the beach and came up through the town, and I will not romanticize it: we were a procession of the half-dead. Sesay on Marta's shoulder, hopping on one leg with a crutch Tobias had carved from a mangrove root somewhere around day nine. The two guides, Wari and Kila, walking with the loose-hipped watchfulness of men who had spent two weeks being hunted and had not yet been told it was over. And me, with the documents in a canvas satchel against my chest, wrapped in oilcloth, with my father's ledger on top.

My father's ledger. I had stopped opening it around day six. Not because I was done with it — I don't think I will ever be done with it — but because I had finally read the last page, the confession he never sent, the one that said, in a railroad man's cramped hand, *I took their money and I straightened their lines and I told myself the river would cover it, and now the river is asking me to account, and I find I cannot pay.* He had written that and then he had sat on it for thirty years. I had carried it for eleven days. The difference between us, I had decided somewhere in the sago swamps, was that I intended to put mine down.

The airstrip road ran parallel to the shore, and at the corner where it met the wharf approach, a Land Cruiser was parked. Not the police one. A second one, clean, and beside it stood three men, and one of them was not pretending to lean.

"Ines." Marta's voice was very quiet. "That's them."

"I know."

"Okon lands in forty minutes."

"Then we have forty minutes to be somewhere these gentlemen can't follow." I looked at the wharf, at the tin roof, at the crowd of stevedores and fish-sellers and the two boneless policemen, and I did the thing I had been trained to do my whole career, the thing that had made me good at railroads and bad at everything else: I looked at the structure and asked what it would bear.

* * *

Here is what I knew about Wewak's wharf at eight in the morning. I knew that the cargo shed in the middle of it was a public building during working hours. I knew that the copra buyers came at nine. I knew that the two policemen were underpaid, bored, and — this was the part that mattered — that one of them had spent the last ten minutes watching Sesay hop along the road with the expression of a man trying to remember where he'd seen her before, and that he'd seen her before because her face had been in the newspapers when the expedition was declared missing, and missing scientists are the one kind of news that even a bored policeman reads.

So I did not run. Running is what guilty men and hunted men do, and Calloway's whole method was to make you feel hunted until you behaved like it. Instead we walked. Slowly. Toward the wharf. Toward the men by the Land Cruiser. And when the taller of them stepped into our path — a broad man with a radio clipped to his shoulder, polite as a customs officer — I stopped, and I smiled, and I said, loudly, in the flat carrying voice I used to use across construction yards:

"Morning. Can I help you?"

Because that was the thing about the wharf. It was public. It was *public*. Every man Calloway had was a man who needed the morning to stay ordinary, and I had eleven days of Sesay's testimony in my head and a satchel of fraud on my chest, and the only weapon I had left was witnesses.

"Mr. Halloran." The tall man knew my name. That should have frightened me more than it did. "There's been a misunderstanding. Some company property's gone missing. We'd just like to look in your bag."

"Company property," I said. "You mean the permits?"

Something flickered in his face. Just for a second. The flicker of a man realizing the script has been read back to him.

"Those documents," I went on, louder, "are the original surveys for the Karawari concession, which expire at midnight tonight, and which were falsified thirty years ago by men who are mostly dead and one man who is currently, I believe, having coffee on a very clean white boat." I turned my head and addressed the wharf at large — the stevedores, the betel-nut woman, the policemen. "Any of you gentlemen know a lawyer? Because I'm about to need one, and I'd rather it be a local."

The betel-nut woman said something in Tok Pisin that made the stevedores laugh. The taller man's hand moved toward his radio, and Wari — who had been drifting, loose-hipped, unhurried — was suddenly simply *there*, between the man and the radio, not touching him, not threatening him, just occupying the space with the calm of a man who had spent two weeks in swamps with worse company.

"You'll want to be careful," the tall man said to me, quietly now.

"I've been careful my whole life," I said. "It's the one thing I'm giving up today."

* * *

The plane came in at seven-forty, and Calloway's men did not stop it, because there was nothing they could lawfully do to stop it, and Calloway, whatever else he was, was a man who understood the architecture of the lawful. What he did instead was what I would have done, and I say that with no compliment intended: he came himself.

I saw the white boat's tender put in at the wharf steps at seven-fifty, and I saw him climb the ladder, and I will grant him this — he was better in person than his reputation, which is rare. A compact man, grey at the temples, with the unhurried manner of someone who has never once needed to raise his voice because the paperwork always raised it for him. He walked up the wharf through the stevedores like a man walking through his own garden, and he stopped three meters from me, and he looked at the satchel, and then at me, with something that was almost professional respect.

"Mr. Halloran."

"Mr. Calloway."

"You've cost my employer a great deal of money."

"He can afford it. He's been spending money he never legally had."

Calloway smiled slightly, as if I'd made a decent point in a seminar. "Here's where we are. Those documents are, as you say, evidence of a fraud. But they're also, technically, company property — original surveys commissioned and paid for by the concession. If you hand them to a journalist, I will have you charged with theft before lunch, and I will win, because the law is slow and my employer is not. If you give them to me, you walk away. All of you. The debt, the charges from Port Moresby, the name. Wiped. I have the authority. You have my word."

"Your word," I said.

"I have never broken it."

"I believe that," I said, and I did, and it frightened me, how much I believed it. "But here's my problem, Mr. Calloway. I've read my father's ledger. I know what your employer's word was worth to the men who built that spur line. Four of them drowned when the culverts went. My father took their money and wrote down every payment, and then spent thirty years not sending the letter that would have made it right. I've spent eleven days carrying his cowardice through a swamp." I shifted the satchel on my shoulder. "I'm not going to spend thirty years carrying mine."

Calloway looked at me for a long moment. Then he looked past me, at Sesay, sitting on a cargo crate with her ruined leg stretched out before her, and at Marta, and at the two guides, and last at the wharf clock, whose hands stood at nine minutes past eight.

"It's a long time until midnight," he said.

"Yes," I said. "But not for him." And I pointed down the wharf, to where a small man in a crumpled suit was coming off the airstrip road at a half-jog, a leather bag in one hand and a press card in his hatband, already pulling a notebook out as he walked, because Marta's fragments had done their work and Gabriel Okon was a man who could smell a deadline the way Tobias smelled salt.

* * *

What happened next was not dramatic, and I want to be precise about that, because the dramatic version would be a lie and the true version is stranger. Okon reached us, and I put the satchel in his hands, and he opened it right there on the public wharf, in front of the stevedores and the policemen and the betel-nut woman, and he began to read. And Calloway did not lunge, did not signal his men, did nothing at all except stand very still, because there was nothing left to take. The documents were no longer in my possession. They were in the possession of a journalist, on public land, in front of forty witnesses, and the story had passed from being a thing that could be seized to being a thing that would have to be denied, and denial, in the end, is just a slower form of losing.

"You understand," Calloway said to me, quietly, while Okon read and Marta dictated and Sesay, from her crate, recited check-in codes and coordinates in the flat voice of a woman giving evidence, "that this ruins you too. Your name. The Port Moresby business. It all comes out."

"I'm counting on it," I said. "A man can't be anonymous and honest at the same time. I tried it for six years. The hours are terrible."

He almost smiled. Then he looked at the clock again — twenty past eight, twelve hours and forty minutes until midnight — and he said, "The company will refile. Clean permits. New names on the paperwork. You've delayed us, Mr. Halloran. You haven't stopped us."

"Maybe," I said. "But my father delayed them thirty years, and I'm the proof it mattered. Somebody always reads the margin notes, Calloway. Somebody always finds the ledger."

He turned and walked back down the wharf to his tender, and the stevedores watched him go, and the betel-nut woman spat, with tremendous precision, into the water where his boat had been.

* * *

Okon worked through the morning and most of the afternoon, right there in the cargo shed, because he said — and I have come to believe he was right — that the story should be filed from the exact spot where the documents changed hands, timestamps and all. Sesay gave him four hours of testimony with a fever sweat on her forehead and never once lost her place in a coordinate. Marta laid out the paper trail like a surgeon laying out instruments. The guides told their part through Tobias, who translated, and when Wari described the ambush at the junction, and the guide who didn't come out of it, the whole shed went quiet, and even the copra buyers stopped weighing.

At eleven minutes to midnight, Okon transmitted the last page of his file. At midnight exactly, the Karawari concession permits lapsed, unfraudable, unrefilable — because a fraud, once published, is not a thing you can quietly reapply for. Marta had a battery left for exactly one sentence on the radio, and she spent it sending Okon a single word: *Filed.*

We stood on the wharf in the dark, all of us, too tired to be happy. Sesay was asleep sitting up. Tobias was smoking, looking at the black water with the expression of a man already planning his next river. And I stood at the rail with my father's ledger in my hands, the last thing in the satchel, the one document Okon had photographed but let me keep — *for the family,* he'd said, and I still don't know which family he meant.

The permits had expired. The fraud was on the record. And somewhere out in the dark harbour, a white boat sat at anchor with its lights off, and I found I was not finished with Rhys Calloway after all — because at dawn, a launch came alongside the wharf carrying a lawyer with a court order, and the first name on it was mine.
