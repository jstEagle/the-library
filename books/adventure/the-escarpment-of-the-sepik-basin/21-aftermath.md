## 21. Aftermath

The indictment came down on a Tuesday, which felt appropriate. Tuesdays are the day bureaucracies choose for their small violence, and this was, in its way, a small violence — a man in a good suit standing in a courtroom in Port Moresby while a clerk read out charges he would spend the next decade denying.

I read about it in the *Post-Courier*, over coffee that had gone the texture of river silt, and I confess my first feeling was not triumph. It was arithmetic. Nineteen days had become eleven days had become nine days, and on the ninth day we had come out of the swamps looking like something the Karawari had chewed and declined to swallow, and Ines Halloran had walked into a newspaper office carrying a box of documents wrapped in a tarpaulin, and the permits had expired at midnight.

That was the whole machine of it. That was justice, when you finally saw its gears up close: not a grand thing at all, but a deadline met by exhausted people.

* * *

They asked me to testify in September. The hearing room in Wewak had a ceiling fan that turned with the conviction of a man who has already given up, and the company's lawyers sat along one table like crows on a fence, patient, well-fed, waiting for the corpse of the proceedings to be worth picking at.

I had prepared for them the way I prepare for anything: obsessively, with charts. I had reconstructed the financier's paper trail twice over — once in the field, by lantern light, with mosquitoes drinking from my wrists as I worked, and once again in clean ink for the tribunal. I knew every falsified survey coordinate by heart. I could recite the dates the concession's permits were issued against the dates the land beneath them had actually been surveyed, and the gap between those two facts was where four men had nearly died and one man had died outright.

The lead lawyer — an Australian, sunburned at the hairline, with the manner of someone who bills by the condescension — asked me whether I could confirm that my conclusions were, in his phrase, "interpretive."

"Everything is interpretive," I said. "Gravity is interpretive until you drop something."

He smiled thinly and turned a page. "But you did not personally witness the falsification of the permit surveys."

"No."

"You rely on documents whose provenance you cannot fully establish."

"I rely on documents," I said, "that your client spent three weeks and at least one human life trying to burn, drown, or bury. If they were worthless, Mr. Okereke, you wouldn't have needed a security chief with a rifle budget."

There was a sound from the gallery then — not laughter exactly, more the exhalation of forty people who had been holding the same breath. I found Ines in the back row. He was sitting very still, hands folded, wearing the only jacket he owned and the expression of a man watching someone else defuse the bomb. He caught my eye and gave me the smallest nod, the kind you'd give across a crowded station platform, and I turned back to the table before either of us could make a scene of it.

Here is what nobody tells you about testimony: it is boring. Justice, in practice, is mostly transcription. The drama all happened months ago, in the swamps, in the dark, in the space between a radio transmission and a decision. By the time it reaches a hearing room it has been flattened into exhibits A through F, and the most anyone can feel about it is a kind of procedural relief, like watching a fever break.

But afterward, on the steps outside, an old woman took my hand. She was from one of the villages upriver — Angoram way, maybe, or beyond — and she said, in Tok Pisin that Tobias later translated for me more gently than she'd spoken it, that her father had worked the spur line. That he had carried rail iron on his back through country no map admitted existed. That when the company left, they dynamited the culverts behind them so that no one could follow the route and ask what had been taken out of the ground.

She said thank you. She said it the way you'd say it about rain after a dry season — not gratefully, exactly, but with the tired recognition that the world occasionally does what it is supposed to do.

I am not a sentimental person. Geology cured me of sentiment early; rock does not care what you hoped. But I stood on those steps holding that woman's hand and I understood something I want to set down here, because I will forget the texture of it otherwise:

Justice did not arrive because the system worked. The system had been purchased, thoroughly and legally, years before any of us got involved. The permits were real paper. The ore surveys were logged in the right registries. Rhys Calloway never broke a law that mattered — he burned a mission boat, yes, and there was an inquiry, and the inquiry went precisely nowhere, because Calloway understood something fundamental: the law is a fence, and fences are built by whoever owns the land.

What tipped it was smaller than the law. It was a radio operator who answered a forbidden transmission. It was a geologist who'd been bumped from an expedition and couldn't stop doing the sums. It was boatmen and guides who kept walking when walking was the only thing left, and a dying woman who refused to let go of a box of papers, and a disgraced engineer who stood on a wharf at midnight and handed the truth to a journalist instead of taking the deal that would have bought his name back.

Ordinary courage. That's the whole mechanism. Not heroism — heroism implies an audience. This was done badly, in mud, by frightened people, with no guarantee whatsoever. If Ines had taken the bargain — and God knows the bargain was fair; I've run the numbers myself, and wiping his debt and restoring his license was worth more than any principle pays — if he had taken it, the company would have refiled clean permits within the month, and the Sepik Basin would belong to men who had never seen it except as columns of figures.

We came within one honest night's sleep of losing everything. I think about that more than I let on.

* * *

The concession was voided in October. The financier — Aldous Vane; even the name sounds like it was purchased — was indicted on eleven counts, and I am told his lawyers are already negotiating, because men like Vane do not go to prison so much as they are briefly inconvenienced by them. I have made my peace with partial outcomes. Partial outcomes are how geology works too: the continent moves a few centimeters a year, and no one applauds, and yet here we are, mountains.

Sesay is recovering. "Recovering" is a generous word for what she calls it herself — she lost two fingers to the swamp fever complications and walks with a stick and refers to both losses with the flat contempt of a woman who has been inconvenienced by her own body and intends to file a complaint. She came to see me last week in Wewak, where I have taken a teaching post at the mission school, of all things. Me. Explaining stratigraphy to teenagers who would rather be swimming. It turns out I'm good at it, which is the most humiliating discovery of my career.

"They're offering Sesay's family compensation," she told me, over tea I suspect was mostly hot water. "For the guide. For Enoch." Her mouth did something complicated. "The number is insulting. The number is designed to be accepted."

"And?"

"And I told them Enoch's family wants the spur line surveyed. Properly. Mapped, documented, put on the record — every culvert, every cut, every place the company dug and ran." She looked at me over the cup. "They want it named for him."

"That's not compensation."

"No," she agreed. "It's better. Compensation says it's over. A map says it happened."

I have started that survey. Provisional title: *The Enoch Kandak Line*. It will take years. The swamps took a week off my life per kilometer last time, and I was younger then by several disasters. But there is a pleasure in it I hadn't expected — the pleasure of drawing a thing that was erased, of putting coordinates where the company wanted blankness. Every line I draw is a small act of perjury against their version of events.

* * *

Ines writes to me sometimes. His letters are terrible — terse, misspelled, structurally identical to a freight manifest — and I reread them anyway. In the last one he said he'd taken work with a rehabilitation crew on the old coastal trunk line, actual rails, actual schedules, the kind of engineering that gets checked by other engineers and held to account by timetables. He sounded, insofar as a freight manifest can sound like anything, lighter.

He also mentioned, in the final paragraph, in the offhand tone people use for things that are not offhand at all, that he'd been up to the spur line. That he'd stood at the repaired culvert for a long time. That he'd brought something with him and hadn't brought it back.

I didn't ask. Some inheritances you bury yourself, and the burial is the point.

* * *

Which brings me to now, to this evening, to the reason I am writing this down at all instead of sleeping.

A letter came today, hand-delivered, no return address. The handwriting was careful in the way of someone relearning penmanship after years of typing — or after years of having other people hold pens for him. Inside was a single sheet, and on it, in that careful hand:

*Dr. Ondoua — You don't know me. My name is Ruth Vane. My husband's trial begins in March. There are documents he doesn't know I have. I have read about you in the papers. I would rather they went to someone who counts.*

An address followed. Somewhere outside Lae. And a date, three weeks from now.

I sat with it for a long time, the fan turning its defeated circles overhead, the gecko ticking in the corner like a slow clock. I thought about deadlines. I thought about how justice is never finished, only adjourned — how the fence gets rebuilt by whoever holds the deeds, unless someone keeps walking the line and mapping what's true.

Then I got out my old survey kit, blew the dust off the transit case, and began, methodically, to pack.

Three weeks. I've worked with worse notice.
