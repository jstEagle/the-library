## 22. True North

The culvert took eleven days to rebuild, which was nine days longer than I'd promised the foreman and four days shorter than he'd bet against me. He paid up in warm beer and a handshake that didn't quite hide his surprise, and I decided I could live with being underestimated for the rest of my life. It's a comfortable condition. Nobody asks you to give speeches.

The spur line runs along the shoulder of the escarpment like a scar someone finally agreed to look at properly. When my father helped lay it — and then helped abandon it, and then helped dynamite its own culverts from behind so the route would die quietly in the bush — he was working for men who thought a railway was a promise you made only as long as it was profitable to keep. The company that hired me last year thought the same thing. Different decade, same arithmetic. I have come to believe the Sepik Basin is where bad arithmetic goes to be buried, and where, if you're patient, it climbs back out of the grave and asks for its receipts.

I know because I carried some of them.

* * *

The morning I buried the compass, the mist hadn't lifted off the river yet, and the new concrete of the culvert headwall was still cool enough to leave your palm print in if you were careless. I was careful. I'd been careful with that pour for three days straight, checking the mix, checking the forms, checking the gradient twice because a man named Tobias once told me that water always remembers where you lied to it. Tobias says things like that with the serene authority of a man who has never once been wrong about a river and knows it annoys people.

The compass sat in my shirt pocket while I worked, the way it had sat there through swamps and ambushes and one very undignified sprint down a wharf at Wewak with Rhys Calloway's men shouting behind me. Brass case worn smooth on one side where a thumb had worried it for thirty years. The needle that never pointed north — not true north, not magnetic north, but some private north my father had calibrated into it, or stumbled into, or been led to by whatever mixture of guilt and greed had made him the man he was.

For most of my life I'd assumed the compass was a joke he was playing on me from beyond the grave. That felt right. It fit the pattern. The man missed my graduation, missed my mother's funeral, missed every birthday until the ones after which no further birthdays were possible, and then a lawyer shipped me a brass compass engraved HALLORAN like a punchline delivered by registered post.

It took a burned supply cache, a dead guide named Yawan whose face I still see when the rain comes in sideways, and a box of documents worth more than my life to understand what the needle was actually doing.

It wasn't broken. It was pointing home.

* * *

The others came up from the coast for the burial, which surprised me. I hadn't asked. I'd sent word through the mission station — a single sentence, *culvert done, coming to finish something* — expecting nothing back, because that's the exchange rate I've always expected from the world. Instead Marta Ondoua arrived on the Wednesday boat with her field kit and an expression that suggested she'd audited my invitation and found it structurally unsound but habitable anyway. Sesay came two days later, walking with a stick now and refusing, on principle, to call it anything but a walking stick. Tobias simply appeared, because Tobias appears wherever boats are needed and disappears wherever gratitude is attempted.

We buried the compass at noon, when the mist burned off and you could see the whole length of the rebuilt spur glinting down toward the junction. Marta read nothing, because Marta doesn't do ceremony; she did the next best thing, which was to state the facts plainly. "Your father surveyed this alignment in 1961," she said. "Falsified the bearing reports for the concession holders. Took payments for eleven years. Then wrote a confession he never sent, hid duplicate originals of everything at a rail junction, and spent the rest of his life navigating toward them with a compass he modified himself." She looked at me over her glasses. "That's not a eulogy. That's just what the paper says."

"That's a eulogy," I said. "Where I'm from."

Sesay lowered the box into the hole we'd dug at the headwall — a cavity left deliberately in the concrete, a small vault in a structure built to hold back a river. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, was the compass and a copy of the ledger. Not the original. The original ledger was going to Port Moresby, to Amara Sesay's family, who had waited long enough for the truth about what their mother had found and what it had cost her to carry it out of the basin alive.

"Say something, Ines," Marta said.

"I've got nothing prepared."

"You never do. That's why we're here."

So I stood over the hole with river mud on my boots and said the only honest thing I had. "You pointed the wrong way your whole life," I told the compass, and meant my father, and meant myself, "and it turned out you were pointing exactly right, and none of us could read it." Then I let the concrete go in, and Tobias troweled it smooth, and that was that. Thirty years of resentment, set firm in a day. Concrete cures slower than grief but holds better.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about stopping running: it isn't a decision. It's a series of small refusals, each one easy to make and impossible to undo, until one morning you notice you've stayed somewhere long enough for the place to start expecting you.

The first refusal was Calloway's bargain, on a night in the swamps when he offered me my debts dissolved and my name restored, and I asked for one night I already knew how I'd spend. The second refusal was smaller and harder: declining the company's settlement offer after the indictment, the one with the confidentiality clause folded into it like a razor in a cake. Marta called the clause "standard practice," which is what people say about things that should be neither standard nor practiced. I declined. My name stayed disgraced in the industry papers for another four months, right up until the inquiry published, and then it stayed obscure, which is different and better. Disgrace fades. Obscurity you can build on.

The third refusal was the strangest. The reconstruction consortium — the one formed after the concession was voided, staffed half by engineers and half by people who'd spent a year learning exactly how badly railways can be used as weapons — offered me a senior posting. Design authority on the spur realignment. My name on the drawings.

I turned it down and asked for a crew job instead. Grade setter. The fellow who walks the line ahead of the machines with a level and a staff and tells everyone else whether the world is flat today.

Marta thought I'd lost my mind. Sesay understood immediately, which is one of about four hundred reasons I trust her. "You spent twenty years being blamed for other men's numbers," she said. "You want work where the numbers are yours and the ground checks them by lunchtime."

"The ground," I said, "is the only auditor I've ever respected."

* * *

So this is the work now. Up before the birds, out ahead of the ballast train with a staff and a level and a notebook I buy with my own money. I set grade for a gang of forty, most of whom initially assumed a disgraced railroad engineer would be about as useful as a chocolate teapot and have since revised the estimate upward, mostly, I suspect, because I'm the only supervisor they've had who'll stand in the cut in the rain and argue about drainage with anyone, including the drainage.

There's a boy on the crew — Kila, seventeen, all elbows and opinions — who has appointed himself my apprentice without consulting either of us. He wants to know everything: how to read water, how to hear a bad culvert before it fails, how to tell which way a river will move in ten years. Last week he caught me checking the old compass bearing against the new alignment out of pure habit, my thumb finding the worn spot on a pocket that's been empty since the burial.

"What are you measuring?" he asked.

"Nothing. It's gone."

"Then what are you pointing at?"

And I realized I was pointing at the junction, three kilometers south, where my father cached the documents that ended a company, and beyond it the escarpment, and beyond that everything I'd spent my life fleeing. "North," I said. "More or less."

"It's not north," Kila said, squinting at the sun with the confidence of youth. "It's northwest."

"More or less," I said again, and put my hand back in my pocket, and found it empty, and found that didn't hurt the way I'd expected.

* * *

The ledger went south on the same boat as Sesay. I watched it go from the wharf at Angoram, a parcel in brown paper tied with string, containing the account of every bribe my father took and the confession he never mailed and a letter I added myself, addressed to her daughters, explaining who I was and why their mother had trusted me with it. It was the hardest letter I've ever written, and I've written resignation letters to companies I was stealing from, which sets a high bar.

I kept nothing else. That was the point. A man can inherit a debt or an inheritance, and for years I couldn't tell which one my father had left me, and the answer turned out to be that he'd left both and I got to choose the accounting. I chose to pay. Not because he deserved it — he didn't, not fully, maybe not at all — but because Yawan deserved it, and Sesay's family deserved it, and the villages along the Karawari who watched surveyors measure land that was never legally for sale deserved it, and because somebody has to be the Halloran who balances the books.

Tobias came by the camp last evening, unannounced, as is his custom, and sat on a ballast pile eating rice out of a pot while I checked the day's levels by lamplight.

"They're talking about extending the line past the junction," he said. "All the way to the coast. Through your father's country."

"It's not his country. It's nobody's country. That's rather the point of doing it properly this time."

"Mm." He scraped the pot. "They'll need someone to run the schedule. Radios, check-ins, weather. Boring work. Low visibility." His eyes had the particular shine they get when he's handing you a truth disguised as a job offer. "Good work for a man who wants to stay found."

I laughed, because it was funny — the whole arc of my life compressed into a recruitment pitch for radio scheduling — and because underneath the joke it wasn't a joke at all. Somewhere upriver there are men doing survey work again, honest men this time, checking permits that expire and get renewed in daylight, and they'll need someone on the radio at dawn and dusk saying *all well, position confirmed, bearing true.* Someone whose whole job is confirming that the map matches the ground. Someone who learned, the expensive way, what happens when those two things drift apart.

"All well," I said into the lamplight, practicing.

Tobias nodded once, satisfied, and took his pot down to the boat, and the river took him the way rivers take everything, without malice or memory.

I stood a while longer on the finished culvert with the lamp burning and the water running clean beneath me, going where it was graded to go, holding the line it was built to hold. Northwest, more or less. True enough.
