## 1. The Mood of Sandbars

Every sandbar on the Fly has a mood, and I know them the way other people know their mother's face. The bar below D'Albertis Junction sulks in the dry season, all sharp edges and gravel, and will gut a propeller that treats it casually. The long spit opposite Obo village is a liar — flat and welcoming at dawn, then three feet under by noon when the flood comes down from the Star Mountains like a debt collector. And the bar at Wabuda Bend is friendly enough but gossips: whatever you do there, half the river knows about it by supper.

I learned them all from my brother. That was the arrangement, back when there was an arrangement — Danny read the water and I read the cargo manifests, and between us we kept the *Kestrel* moving and the bank more or less at bay. Then Danny went into the Strickland gorge after a rumor of alluvial gold and came out of it in a box built by men who charged his estate for the nails. What he left me was the boat, the debts, and a river full of people who still called me "Danny's sister" as if I were a footnote to a better book.

Two years now. Long enough that the footnote has learned to walk on its own.

* * *

It was the tail end of the wet, which meant the river was high, brown, and in one of its moods. I had a run booked down from Kiunga — fuel drums for the mission at Morehead, rice and kerosene for the trade stores along the lower reaches, and a crate of roofing iron for a teacher at Balimo who'd been waiting on it since Christmas and had stopped being polite about it. Routine work. The kind of work that pays for diesel and doesn't pay for much else.

Tamu had the engine running before first light, the way he always did, standing over it with a rag and an expression that suggested the engine had personally disappointed him overnight. Tamu is seventy if he's a day, claims he's older, and has been keeping diesels alive on this river since before the war. He calls the *Kestrel*'s engine "she" and me "captain" with equal irony, and I've never once caught him out on either.

"Number two injector's thinking about retiring," he told me, wiping his hands. "I told her we can't afford the pension."

"Did she listen?"

"She never listens. She just complains louder." He nodded toward the fuel gauge, which sat at three-quarters like a man pretending to be richer than he is. "We'll make Balimo. After that, pray."

Pita came aboard not long after, seventeen years old, all elbows and appetite, carrying a sack of kaukau his mother had sent for the trip because she didn't trust me to feed him. Smart woman. Pita had turned up at the wharf six months ago looking for deck work and I'd taken him on mostly because he showed up twice — once to ask, once to prove he wanted it — and on this river, showing up twice puts you ahead of most of the field. He was still at the age where everything was either a joke or an emergency, and usually both.

We cast off at grey light, and the river took us the way it takes everything: without comment.

The first day was uneventful, which on the Fly is a gift you don't acknowledge out loud. We ran the channel past the junction on Danny's old marks — a dead tree on the left bank, a rock that only shows at low water, a bend where the current piles up against the outside wall hard enough to lean the whole boat over like a drunk against a fence. I stood at the wheel and felt the river through the hull the way you feel a horse through the saddle, every boil and eddy translated into steel and vibration, and I thought, not for the first time, that this was the only conversation I'd ever been any good at.

By afternoon we were into the lower reaches, where the banks go soft and the country opens out into grassland and sago swamp and the sky gets so big it makes you feel like an apology. The clouds were building over the mountains inland, purple-black towers that would dump somewhere else tonight, and the air had that thick, sweet, pre-rain smell — mud and rot and green things growing too fast.

Pita spotted the smoke first. A thin column off the port bow, maybe two miles up a side channel, rising straight in the dead air.

"Village burning?" he asked, and there was a note in his voice that made me look twice.

"Too small," I said. "Cook fire, or someone clearing a garden." But I marked it, because on this river you mark smoke the way a banker marks signatures. Smoke tells you who's where, and who's where tells you whether the price of copra moved, or whether somebody's dead.

We tied up that night at a mooring Danny had found years ago — a cutbank with deep water close in, sheltered from the main current, within walking distance of nothing. I liked it for exactly that reason. There are nights when the best company on the Fly is nobody's.

* * *

Morning came hot and still, the river running glassy under a skin of mist that burned off by seven. We made an early start, and it was mid-morning, running the long reach above Wabuda Bend, when Pita called out from the bow in a voice that wasn't joking and wasn't an emergency but was something worse — confused.

"Captain. There's a man on the bar."

I brought her down to half throttle and looked. The sandbar off the starboard bow was the low-water kind, a pale crescent of mud and sand just breaking the surface, and sprawled across the upstream lip of it was a man. Not sitting. Not waving. Lying the way things lie when they've stopped arranging themselves.

My first thought was crocodile. It's everyone's first thought on this river, and it's right often enough to keep the thought employed. I swung closer, easing the *Kestrel* in over the bar's shoulder with the current helping, and put Pita ashore in the dinghy with the boathook, which is not a medical instrument but does keep you from having to touch strangers.

The man was alive. That was the surprise. White man, sixty-odd, dressed in the kind of clothes prospectors wear — canvas gone grey with washing, boots ruined past saving, a hat that had given up its shape sometime last season. There was blood on him, a lot of it, soaked through the canvas at the belly and crusted black down his thigh, and his skin had the grey-yellow tone of a man whose body had already started closing the accounts.

"Gut-shot," Pita said, and then, because he was seventeen, "Is he—"

"He's shot," I said. "Go get the tarp from the fo'c'sle. Move."

Here is the thing you should know about me, the thing Danny used to say made me hard to be friends with: I have a rule about strangers. It isn't a complicated rule. It goes: no strangers. The river is full of men who want a ride, a favor, a share, a witness, or a place to hide, and every one of them is a story that ends with someone else holding your debts. I'd watched it happen to Danny — half his troubles arrived as passengers. So I don't take passengers. I take cargo, I take crew I've chosen, and I take my own counsel, and that policy has kept the *Kestrel* afloat through two floods, one mutiny, and a customs officer with ambitions.

And there was a dying man on a sandbar, and the rule lasted approximately four seconds.

Not because I'm good. I want to be clear about that, because I've heard myself described as generous by people who owed me money, and generosity is a costume that fits me badly. It was because of the arithmetic. A gut-shot man on a bar in the middle reach of the Fly is not an accident. Nobody walks two hundred miles of swamp to bleed out in the open unless they were put there, or unless they were running from whoever put them there, and either way, what lay on that sand was information. Information is the one cargo I've never been able to refuse.

Also — and this part I'll admit to, quietly, the way you admit things to yourself at the wheel with nobody watching — he looked like a man who had been left. Left deliberately, by people who knew exactly what they were doing, and I have some professional familiarity with being left.

We got him aboard on the tarp, two of us hauling, and laid him in the shade of the wheelhouse awning. He weighed less than a man his size should, which is what blood loss does; it takes the weight out of you before it takes anything else. His eyes found me and focused, which surprised me — most men that far gone stop bothering — and his mouth worked, and what came out was barely a whisper.

"Boat," he said. "Going down?"

"Downriver. Balimo, then the delta."

Something happened in his face then, something I want to describe correctly because it mattered later. Relief. Not the relief of a rescued man — the relief of a man who has been looking for a specific thing and has just recognized it. He'd been lying on that bar, I'd swear to it, watching the traffic lane, waiting for a boat going downriver, and here was one, and it was mine.

"Good," he said. "Good boat." And then he said nothing for a while, because his body had decided talking was a luxury.

Tamu appeared with the medical kit, which contained iodine, gauze, aspirin, and optimism, in roughly that order. We rolled him gently and saw the wound proper: entry low in the belly, no exit we could find. Small caliber. The kind of wound that doesn't kill you today or tomorrow but kills you, certainly, somewhere in the middle distance, and lets you think clearly the whole way there. Whoever shot him hadn't wanted him dead fast. They'd wanted him dead eventually, and alone, and findable by no one.

I've seen that kind of shooting before. You see it once on this river and you recognize it forever.

"Can you save him?" Pita asked.

"No," Tamu said, flatly, and he was right, and we both knew it, and the man lying there probably knew it too, because he'd had hours on that bar to do his own arithmetic. "Maybe three days. Maybe four. Not with what we carry."

So we did what could be done, which was not much: cleaned the wound, packed it, dosed him for pain, got fluids into him a spoonful at a time because he couldn't manage more. I rigged an awning shade and we made him as comfortable as a man can be on a working deck with a hole in him. Through all of it he watched me, and I mean watched — tracked me around the deck, noted where I stowed things, listened to how I spoke to Tamu and Pita, taking inventory. A man building a case for trust, or spending the last of his strength deciding whom to spend it on.

By late afternoon he was strong enough, briefly, to talk, and what he said first was his name.

"Elias Vane."

"Mara Ishikawa. This is my boat."

"I know boats," he said, and almost smiled. "Knew a hundred. Worked the river when the steamers ran." His voice was a ruin, gravel dragged over gravel. "You're running light."

"Cargo's not heavy this season."

"You're running light," he repeated, and I understood he wasn't talking about tonnage. He was looking at me the way you look at a person you've been sent to find, and I didn't like it, and I couldn't say why yet.

He asked how far to Balimo. I told him. He asked if anyone would ask questions there. I told him the truth: everyone asks questions everywhere on the Fly; the trick is finding questions worth answering. He considered that for a long moment, eyes drifting closed, and I thought he'd gone under again, but no — he was filing it.

Then he asked the question that rearranged my week.

"How far are you from Kiunga?"

"Above it. Two days' run, if the river's kind."

"And you're headed away from it."

"I'm headed where the cargo goes."

"Yes," Elias Vane said, and closed his eyes, and I had the distinct, uncomfortable sensation of a man crossing an item off a list. "That'll do."

* * *

That night I sat in the wheelhouse with the lamp turned low and did the accounting I always do, the real one, the one that isn't money. Danny's face, which I keep in a drawer and take out less often than I used to because grief is a tool and tools wear. The debts, which had shrunk from terrifying to merely large, a distinction I'd worked two years to earn. The river, high and quiet outside the windows, carrying on its ancient indifferent business.

And the man on my deck, breathing shallow and regular, tended by Pita, who had appointed himself nurse with the enthusiasm of youth and none of its judgment, checking the packing every hour and reporting each time with the gravity of a boy who has discovered mortality and finds it interesting.

I should have been worried. I was worried. A gut-shot stranger aboard is a floating set of questions, and questions attract the kind of attention that sinks small boats. If whoever shot him came looking — and men like that always come looking — they'd come to a thirty-meter riverboat with one old engineer, one teenage deckhand, and a captain whose policy on trouble was strict avoidance. I ran the scenarios at the wheel until midnight and didn't like a single one.

But underneath the worry, quieter, there was the other thing. The thing I'd felt when he said *that'll do*, like a key testing a lock it wasn't cut for and turning anyway. This man had been looking for a boat going downriver, and he had looked at mine and been satisfied, and satisfaction like that isn't random. Somewhere upstream there was a story with my boat's name in it, or near enough, and I don't believe in coincidence on the Fly. I believe in currents, and currents deliver things where they're needed, and I had the growing, unwelcome certainty that I had just been delivered something.

I went down and checked on him myself, finally, past midnight. He was awake. Dying men sleep in fragments, and in the gaps they're clearer than the living.

"Can't sleep?" I asked.

"No." He shifted, winced, settled. "Been doing my accounts too."

"Any of them settle?"

"One." He looked at me, and in the lamplight his eyes were very steady for a man three days from dead. "Captain Ishikawa. How much do you know about the Upper Fly?"

"The gorge? Nothing worth knowing. Nobody runs it. Too fast, too narrow, falls in the wrong places. The salvage companies hold permits up there and even they don't go past the second bend."

"That's what they tell people." He coughed, wet and long, and I held the cup for him and hated every second of my own helplessness. When he could speak again, he said, softly, like sharing a secret with a cellmate: "There's a steamer up there. Been up there since before the war. Sank with her holds full, and nobody's ever brought her up, and there's a reason nobody's ever brought her up, and the reason is worth more than what's in her holds."

I stood there in the dark of my own wheelhouse with a dying stranger telling me about sunken treasure, feeling like a mark in a penny dreadful. Every bar on this river has a man who knows where the gold is. It's practically a local industry.

"If this is a sales pitch," I said, "you picked a strange venue."

"It's a confession," he said. "Different product entirely." And then, because dying men don't waste breath on persuasion: "Ask me tomorrow. Ask me what I was doing on that bar, and who shot me, and why a man runs two hundred miles to die facing downriver. Ask me tomorrow, when I've slept, and I'll show you something that'll answer faster than I can."

I should have refused. I want that on the record too. Every instinct I'd built since Danny died said refuse, walk away, let the river keep its secrets, they're cheaper that way. But I stood there, and I thought about the way he'd said *good boat* like a verdict, and the way he'd asked about Kiunga like a man confirming an escape route, and the grey certainty in Tamu's voice when he said *not with what we carry*, and I heard myself say:

"Tomorrow, then."

He smiled, properly, for the first time. It transformed him — took twenty years off and put ten back in some other currency.

"Tomorrow," Elias Vane agreed. "You won't regret it."

"Everyone says that."

"Not everyone means it," he said, and closed his eyes, and within minutes was asleep — really asleep, deep and even, the sleep of a man who has finished waiting.

I stood over him a while longer than I needed to. Outside, the river ran on in the dark, high and silent, and somewhere in it, downstream, the mood of every sandbar between here and the delta was already changing, though I wouldn't know that for days yet.

I went up to the wheelhouse and sat with the lamp off, watching the black water, and told myself the deal wasn't struck yet. Told myself I could still put him ashore at Balimo, hand him to the mission, wash my hands, keep my rule intact.

But I knew, the way you know a bar has shifted under a familiar anchorage, that the rule was already broken. It had broken the moment I swung the wheel toward that crescent of mud instead of past it. Everything after was just paperwork.

Dawn came up grey-gold over the swamps, and Elias Vane woke asking for me by name, and I went down to hear what a dying man had spent his last strength swimming two hundred miles to say.
