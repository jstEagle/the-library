## 2. A Map for a Promise

Pain is a tide. Nobody tells you that until you've been gut-shot and lived long enough to notice the pattern, but it's true — it comes in, swells over everything, and then it draws back and leaves you stranded on some bare stretch of yourself, gasping, counting the hours until the next one.

I'd been counting since the mudbank. Two hundred miles of river behind me, most of it swum or dragged, and every mile of it paid for in blood I could no longer afford. The girl — Ishikawa, she said her name was, Mara Ishikawa, captain of this rust-bucket called the Kestrel — had stitched me up below decks with a sailmaker's needle and a bottle of antiseptic she clearly resented spending on a stranger. She hadn't said much. That was how I knew she was serious. Chatty people are negotiating with themselves out loud; quiet ones have already decided.

The bunk they'd given me smelled of diesel and old rope, which is to say it smelled like every honest boat I've ever known, and I lay there while the engine thumped beneath me like a second heart, and I thought: well, Elias, old man. This is where fifty years of running finally catches up and sits down beside you.

The pain came in again. I rode it out.

* * *

She came at dawn, as promised, or threatened, depending on how you read her face. She sat on an upturned crate across from my bunk with her arms folded, and she looked at me the way a bank manager looks at a loan application from a man with two names and no references.

"You swam two hundred miles," she said. "Nobody swims two hundred miles to die quietly."

"Nobody swims two hundred miles for their health either."

"Then say what you came to say. The Kestrel doesn't carry passengers. Cargo only. So either you're cargo, or you're off at Balimo."

I laughed, and laughing was a mistake — something tore hot and wet down my side, and I spent a while just breathing through it while she watched without pity, which I respected. When I could speak again I said, "Balimo's got a mission hospital with no surgeon and a graveyard with room. I've seen both. I'm not getting off at Balimo."

"Where, then?"

"Nowhere," I said. "That's rather the point of me."

Her eyes didn't move off my face. Brown eyes, flat as the river at slack water, and underneath the flatness something moving, some current she kept down deep where passengers couldn't feel it pull. I'd spent enough years around water people to know that look. It was grief wearing competence as a coat.

"There's a daughter," I said.

Something shifted. Not much. A degree of list, maybe.

"Nell," I went on, because once you open the hold doors you might as well let the cargo breathe. "Eleanor Vane. Port Moresby. Works at the Steamships trading post, or she did when I last had the courage to write, which was eleven years ago." I pulled myself up the bunk until I was half-sitting, and the pain obliged by receding, the way it does when it wants your full attention. "I left when she was nine. Left her mother with nothing but debts and my name, which was worth less than the debts. I told myself I was prospecting. Told myself I'd come back rich. You know the arithmetic men do when they want to leave? How every number comes out right if you squint?"

"I know it," she said, and her voice had gone quieter, and I understood I'd found the current under her flatness. Her brother, Tamu had muttered to me the night before, over the engine noise. Brother drowned three years back; boat came to her wrapped in his loans. Everyone on the Fly knows the story. Everyone tells it differently, and all of them wrong.

"So here's my arithmetic now," I said. "I'm dying. The doctor at Daru would tell me so politely, and the mission sisters would tell me so kindly, and neither of them can do anything about it except make it tidy. Fine. I accept the terms. But I have something that needs delivering, and I can't deliver it myself, and I am not — " and here my voice cracked, damn it, fifty years of gravel and it chose that moment to crack — "I am not going to meet my maker owing my daughter a letter."

* * *

The wax-cloth map was rolled inside my shirt, against the skin, where it had ridden for six weeks. Unrolling it took both hands and most of my remaining dignity. She watched me do it without offering help, because she was that kind of woman — the kind who lets you keep whatever scraps of independence you've got left, even while measuring you for a coffin.

It was a good map. I'd earned it the hard way, over four seasons, trading tobacco for stories and stories for bearings, sketching by firelight on cloth stiffened with beeswax so the damp couldn't eat it. The Upper Fly gorge ran down its length in ink lines and pencil corrections, sandbars marked and unmarked, rapids named in three languages, and there, in a side channel no chart bothered with, a cross I'd drawn and redrawn until the ink shone.

"The Meridian," I said. "Colonial steamer. Went down in '09 with a cargo manifest that officially says copra and trade goods."

"And unofficially?"

"Gold. And worse than gold." I tapped the cross. "She's sitting upright in thirty feet of green water, in a gorge so narrow the sun touches her maybe two hours a day. I found her four seasons ago. I've been trying to decide ever since whether finding her was the best thing I ever did or the worst."

Mara looked at the map for a long time. Long enough that I started cataloguing the things a captain notices: the draft marks, the load lines, the way her thumb hovered over the gorge section without touching it. She wasn't seeing treasure. She was seeing water — depth, current, whether her boat could live in that channel.

Good. Trust a captain to see water first. Gold makes people stupid; rivers keep them honest.

"Why hasn't anyone claimed it?" she asked.

"Because the men who sank her don't want her found, and the men who could find her don't know where to look, and I bought my silence with the same coin that bought everyone else's." I reached into my kit and brought out the brass key. It was heavy, old, hand-cut, with a bow shaped like a ship's wheel. I'd carried it so long my palm had worn it smooth. "Strongroom key. Meridian pattern. There's exactly one lock it fits, and it's been waiting sixty years for someone stubborn enough to dive for it."

"What's in the strongroom?"

I held the key out to her. She didn't take it. Not yet. She wanted the whole weight of it first, the way you test a mooring line before you trust it with your boat.

"The thing that should have hung me," I said.

That landed. I watched it land. Her jaw tightened and her arms unfolded and refolded, and for a moment she wasn't a captain assessing cargo, she was a person hearing something true, which on the Fly is rarer than gold and worth more.

"In 1909 I was seventeen," I said. "Deckhand on the Meridian's last run. There were men aboard — witnesses, you'd call them now, though we didn't have words like that then. Men who'd seen what the company did upriver, and were being taken down to the coast to say it in front of officials. Somewhere in the gorge, in the dark, the ship went down. All hands. Officially an accident. Officially a tragedy of the river." I laughed, or tried to. "I wasn't aboard when she sank. They put me ashore at a mission two days before, with a purse of sovereigns and instructions to forget. Seventeen years old, and I took the money. I've been taking it every year since. Hush money has excellent punctuality."

"And the others?"

"Drowned. Or worse than drowned — some of them were still alive when the water came in. I've heard things, over the years, from men who helped load her last. Scratch marks inside the hull." I made myself say it plainly. She deserved plain. "There were people in that strongroom corridor hammering on steel while the river closed over them, and the men who arranged it are grandfathers now, respected, some of them with their names on company letterheads and charity boards. One of them signs the cheques that keep the salvage fleet running up and down this river like he owns it. Which, legally speaking, he nearly does."

"Corrigan," she said.

So she'd already done her sums. Good. I'd picked the right boat after all — not because of the boat, but because of the woman steering it.

"Corrigan," I agreed. "And the ledger proving it is in that strongroom, along with the gold they used to buy silence, and I am the last living man who knows where both lie and holds the key to reach them. Which is why certain gentlemen recently decided I'd outlived my usefulness." I gestured at my middle. "Hence the swimming."

* * *

The pain tide came in hard after that, and I lost some time. When I surfaced, she was still there. She hadn't moved from the crate. Maybe she had; maybe hours had gone by; below decks on a working boat, time goes soft as river mud. But she was there, and she'd laid the map flat on her knees, studying it, and the brass key sat in her palm now, turning over and over.

"You could sell it," I said. "The location. Corrigan would pay more than the gold's worth just to keep the ledger at the bottom of the gorge. Men like him always pay best for graves."

"I know."

"Or you could take the gold and forget the paper. Thirty feet, a good diver, a week's work. Retire the Kestrel's debts twice over."

"I know that too."

"But?" I prompted, because there was a but; I could hear it standing in the corner of the cabin like a third passenger.

"But you didn't swim two hundred miles to give me a treasure map." She looked up. "You swam to give your daughter a letter. Everything else is ballast. So talk about Nell."

And God forgive me, I did. I talked until my voice wore down to a whisper, and then past it. About a girl who used to sit on the wharf at Moresby drawing ships in the dust, naming them all Kestrel before I ever heard of this one — no, that's not true, I invented that detail, strike it, the truth is better: she drew ships and gave them all names braver than mine. About her mother, who died hating me efficiently and completely, the way she did everything. About eleven years of letters written and never sent, sealed and stacked in a tin box because every year I thought, next season, when I've got something worth saying, and every year the something turned out to be more gold and less soul.

"They're in the tin," I whispered. "Green tin, tea tin, false bottom. Under my bedroll. Every letter I never sent her. She should have them. Even now. Especially now. A girl deserves to know her father was a coward on paper if nowhere else."

Mara was quiet a long moment. Then: "What exactly are you asking me to promise?"

"Just this." I made myself sit up straight, which cost me what the pain tide had left me, and I looked her in those flat brown eyes and put my whole wrecked life into the words. "Take what I found to Nell Vane in Port Moresby. The letters. The truth. Whatever the strongroom holds — she decides what happens to it, not you, not Corrigan, not the courts, not me. I'm not asking you to make it right. Nobody can make it right. I'm asking you to make it *hers*."

"And if she doesn't want it? If she takes one look at your name on the envelope and throws it in the harbour?"

"Then she throws it, and you come home, and you'll have lost nothing but fuel." I managed something that had once been a smile. "But she won't. She's her mother's daughter, and her mother never threw away anything that might be useful, including me, right up until I made it easy."

The engine thumped. The river ran. Somewhere above us a heron screamed down at the water, the way herons do, complaining to management.

Mara looked at the key in her palm. Then at the map. Then at me, and I watched her do the thing captains do — run the numbers, check the weather, weigh the cargo against the draft. Except the cargo was a dead man's guilt and the draft was her own conscience, and I had no idea which way she'd come out until she closed her fist around the key.

"One promise," she said. "Delivered to Nell Vane, Port Moresby. What's in the strongroom goes to her first, and what she does with it is hers. I keep nothing for myself unless she gives it freely. That's the promise. That's all of it."

"That's all of it," I said.

"And if I find out you lied to me — about any of it —"

"A dying man's promises are the only ones that can't be renegotiated," I said. "Ask any priest. Ask any lawyer. Ask the river."

She almost smiled. Almost. On her it probably counted as a celebration.

* * *

I slept after that, properly, for the first time since the shot. Dreamed of the wharf at Moresby, dust and ship-names, a small girl looking up. Woke to lamplight and Tamu's face — the old engineer, checking on me with a cup of something bitter and medicinal, muttering that the captain had gone soft, that strangers were bad luck, that in forty years on the river he'd never once seen a good end come of picking up drowning men.

"She's not soft," I told him.

"No," Tamu agreed, with the satisfaction of a man whose complaint had been purely ceremonial. "That's what worries me."

He left the lamp low. I lay listening to the engine, that great patient heart below decks, carrying us upriver through the dark toward a bend I'd loved and a grave I hadn't dug yet, and I did my last arithmetic, the kind where every number finally comes out honest.

One map. One key. One promise, witnessed by a heron and a river.

It wasn't much of an estate. But it was the first thing I'd ever owned outright that I wasn't ashamed of, and I fell asleep holding that thought like a child holds a coin — too tightly, and for no reason anyone else would understand.

The pain tide came in one more time before dawn, higher than the others, and I rode it out alone, and when it drew back I knew — the way you know a bar has shifted, the way you know a season has turned — that it wouldn't be coming back. Which meant the clock was running now, for all of us.

I hoped the girl captain could steer fast.
