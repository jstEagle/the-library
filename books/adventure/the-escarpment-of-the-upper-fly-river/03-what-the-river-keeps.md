## 3. What the River Keeps

The river gave me two days to decide what to do with a dead man, and I spent both of them arguing with him.

Not out loud. I'm not the type, whatever Tamu says about me talking to the wheel when I think nobody's listening. But Elias Vane lay wrapped in his own canvas on the aft deck, in the shade of the cargo awning where the heat couldn't get at him too quickly, and every time I passed him with a coil of rope or a tin of tea leaves I found myself having the conversation we hadn't finished.

*You could have told me more,* I'd think, and the canvas said nothing, because canvas is better at keeping secrets than most men are.

*You could have told me less,* I'd think an hour later, because what he had told me was already sitting in my chest like a swallowed hook.

He'd loved this bend. That was the one thing he'd said clearly enough that even a dying man's slurring couldn't blur it — he'd asked for it by name, or rather he'd described it and let me name it for him, because prospectors don't carry maps of rivers in their heads so much as they carry grudges against particular stretches of water. *Where the current leans left and the fig trees lean back,* he'd said. *Bury me there, girl, and I'll owe you nothing but the debt I've already given you.*

I didn't like being called girl. He knew that. He did it anyway, twice, which told me something about how much time he thought he had left to be polite about it.

So on the third morning, with the mist still sitting on the water like a lid on a pot, I brought the Kestrel around the bend where the current leans left and the fig trees lean back, and I cut the engine, and we let her drift in sideways on the slack water the way you beach a boat you intend to leave.

Tamu dug. There wasn't any discussion about it. My engineer is seventy-one years old and has opinions about proper burials the way other men have opinions about football, and his opinion is that a grave is dug by whoever isn't sick, dying, or the captain, and since Pita was below decks sulking about being made to scrub the bilge — a punishment I invented for a crime he didn't commit, because a boy with idle hands on my boat finds trouble faster than a bilge finds water — that left Tamu, who attacked the riverbank clay with a shovel like it owed him money.

I stood over him and held the map.

That was the arrangement, see. Elias had traded me a wax-cloth map folded into eighths and a brass key wrapped in oilskin, and in exchange I'd promised a dying stranger that I'd carry what he found to a daughter in Port Moresby who probably hated him. One promise. Clean terms. The kind of deal I'd have shaken on with anyone, and had shaken on with worse.

But a promise is a strange kind of cargo. It doesn't sit in the hold. It sits in you, and it shifts with the weather, and somewhere between the mudbank where I'd found him gut-shot and this bend where the fig trees leaned back, the promise had started to weigh more than the man had.

I unfolded the map while Tamu dug.

Wax cloth is a prospector's medium because it forgives rain, sweat, blood, and lying. This one had been drawn by someone who knew the Upper Fly the way a knife knows a sheath. The gorge came up off the cloth in ink and confidence: the narrows marked with soundings, the rapids named in a hand that had gotten shakier toward the top of the page, as though he'd drawn the lower river young and the upper river old, which is exactly backwards from how most men live them.

And there, in the elbow of the gorge where the current doubled back on itself, a small cross in red wax, and beside it, in letters pressed hard enough to emboss the cloth beneath:

**MERIDIAN. 1909.**

I sat down on the gunwale with the map across my knees and read that date four times.

Nineteen hundred and nine. I did the arithmetic the way you do arithmetic when you don't want the answer — slowly, twice, hoping the numbers will flinch. Sixty years. Sixty years that steamer had been sitting in the gorge, and every permit, every concession, every salvage claim staked along the Upper Fly in living memory had been written on paper that postdated her sinking.

Here's the thing they don't teach you at the registry, because the registry has no interest in teaching it: a concession covers what's licensed. A license covers what's claimed. And a claim — this is the part the companies keep quiet and the elders keep shouting — a claim only reaches down as far as the ink is wet. A wreck that went down before there were permits to permit her belongs to no company, no fleet, no ledger of holdings. She belongs to the river, and the river, by every law older than the ones printed in Kiunga, belongs to the people whose dead are in her.

Whatever was aboard the Meridian wasn't Corrigan's. Wasn't anybody's. Except maybe the families of whoever she'd been carrying, and the word *carrying* had begun to itch at me, because Elias hadn't said cargo. He'd said witnesses. He'd said *the thing that should have hung me.* You don't hang a man over gold. Gold gets you robbed. It takes something else entirely to put a rope in a man's future.

"Tamu," I said.

He didn't stop digging. "Captain."

"Come look at this when your arms need an excuse."

"They'll need one shortly." He drove the shovel in again. "The ground here is argumentative."

He came anyway, wiping his hands on his trousers, and bent over the map with the particular stillness of a man reading something he'd hoped never to read again. His eyes went to the name before they went to the cross. Old engineers read names first; it's how they're built.

"Meridian," he said, and then he said nothing for long enough that I counted the drips off the awning. Eleven of them.

"You know her," I said.

"I know the story." He straightened up and looked at the gorge on the map the way you look at a scar on someone else's body — politely, briefly, and then away. "Everyone my age knows the story. They just don't tell it." He tapped the date with one cracked fingernail. "Lost in '09, coming down through the gorge with the wet season early. Official finding was pilot error. Rocks. Current." Another silence, longer. "She wasn't carrying cargo, Captain. Not the kind that gets listed."

"Witnesses," I said. "Elias said witnesses."

"He would know." Tamu looked at the grave, half-dug, and then at me, and his voice dropped into the register he uses for engine talk, low and level, the register that means the news is bad but the machinery will hold. "There were men on that boat who saw something done upriver. Something done by men whose names are still on buildings in Port Moresby. The Meridian went down with all of them aboard, and the finding was rocks, and the gold that bought the finding is probably still in her strongroom, because no one spends money that well-tracked."

"How do you know all this?"

"I was nine years old," Tamu said, "and my uncle crewed the boats that went looking for her. They never found her. The men who paid for the search didn't want her found." He took the map from me gently, the way you take something breakable from someone whose hands have started to shake without their permission. "This cross is either a lie or the truest thing anyone's ever drawn on cloth."

Below us, the hole was deep enough. We buried Elias Vane where the current leans left and the fig trees lean back, with his boots on, because Tamu insisted a man shouldn't meet whatever comes next barefoot, and with the brass key's oilskin wrapping emptied of everything but the smell of him, because I kept the key itself. Tamu said words in his mother's tongue that I didn't understand and didn't need to. Pita stood at the rail and watched with the expression of a boy deciding whether death was going to be interesting or frightening, and settling, for once, on neither.

I filled the last of the clay in myself. It seemed right that the promise should start under my hands.

* * *

We ran downriver through the afternoon with the map open on the chart table and my finger pinned to the gorge.

The thing about a decision is that it usually makes itself while you're busy doing something else. I'd been telling myself since the mudbank that I'd take the map to Nell Vane in Port Moresby, hand it over, say *your father's regards*, and wash my hands of the whole affair. That was the promise. That was the clean shape of it. Deliver the parcel, collect nothing, go back to hauling diesel drums and copra and living inside my own skin where it was quiet.

But promises, as I've said, shift with the weather. And the weather had changed.

Because here's what sixty years buys you: it buys you outside every claim ever staked. The Corrigan Salvage Fleet holds permits on the Upper Fly — everyone knows it, the way everyone knows the tide times, the way everyone knows which registry clerk takes which bribe. Those permits expire. And when they lapse, the gorge reverts — not to the Crown, not to the fleet, but to the customary owners, the people whose grandfathers fished those narrows and whose grandmothers sang the crossing songs. If the Meridian sits in that gorge unclaimed when the permits die, she belongs to them. To the descendants. To the very people whose dead might be aboard her.

Elias hadn't just handed me treasure. He'd handed me a clock.

"You're doing the face," Tamu said from the engine room doorway. He had a rag over one shoulder and grease to the elbow and the satisfied air of a man whose machinery is running sweeter than the day deserves.

"What face?"

"The one where you've decided something expensive."

"I haven't decided anything."

"You've decided something," he said. "You've been steering with your knees and staring at that map for an hour. That's a decided woman. Undecided women steer with their hands."

I almost smiled. It didn't quite make it. "If I go up there, Tamu — if I take the Kestrel into that gorge — I'm not going as a salvager. I can't claim her. I wouldn't. Whatever's in that strongroom belongs to the people downstream of it, and I mean that literally and legally and every other way there is. I'd be going as a courier."

"For a dead man."

"For a dead man's daughter. And for whoever's been waiting sixty years under that cross for somebody to come and count them."

Tamu was quiet for a moment. Then he wiped his hands properly, slowly, folding the rag into quarters the way he does when he's buying time to think.

"My uncle searched three seasons for that boat," he said. "Came home thin and silent and never went back on the water. I used to ask him why. He said the river keeps what it's given, and some things are given wrong." He looked at me, and behind the seventy-one years there was a nine-year-old boy watching boats come home empty. "If you're going to give it back right, Captain, you'll want more than a crew. You'll want witnesses. Divers. Someone who knows the fleet's tricks from the inside."

"I know exactly one diver the fleet blacklisted," I said.

"Sefina."

"You've been reading my chart table."

"I've been reading your face for thirty years. Same skill, better pay." He turned back to his engine. "Kiunga's two days downstream. Registry opens with the morning tide. If you're going to learn when Corrigan's permits lapse, that's where you learn it."

"And if Deacon Rill is asking after dead prospectors in every bar between here and the coast?"

Tamu shrugged, a slow geological event. "Then we'd best arrive before his questions do."

* * *

Night came down on the river the way it does up here — all at once, like a lid — and I took the wheel alone while the others slept, because I do my best thinking at speed and my worst thinking asleep.

The Kestrel ran dark except for the running lights the law requires and my conscience requires me to keep trimmed. Behind me, at the bend where the current leans left, a fresh-cut mound of clay was already going green at the edges, because this river doesn't waste time on grief. Ahead of me, somewhere up past the first rapids, a steamer full of dead men waited under a red wax cross with a secret in her belly worth more than the gold packed around it.

I thought about Elias's face at the end. Not the pain — pain is common and tells you nothing. The relief. The specific, unmistakable relief of a man handing over a weight he'd carried so long his spine had grown around it. He'd looked at me on that mudbank like I was the last honest pair of hands in the world, and I still don't know whether that was trust or desperation or the same thing wearing different clothes.

*The thing that should have hung me.* He'd said it like a man setting down a bag. And now the bag was mine.

I checked the compass. Checked the depth sounder. Did the thing I always do at night on open water, which is press my palm flat against the wheel and feel the river answer through the wood — the shoves and shrugs of current, the long lean of the channel, the way she talks if you shut up long enough to listen.

*All right,* I told her. *Two days to Kiunga. Then we find out what the tide book says, and who else is reading it.*

Somewhere astern, a fig tree leaned back over fresh-turned earth, and the current leaned left around the bend, and the river kept what it had been given.

It was starting to look like it had been given me.
