## 4. Six Tides

The registry office at Kiunga smelled of mildew, stamp ink, and the particular despair of men who had come to ask the government for something. I'd been in and out of that building a dozen times over six years — renewing the Kestrel's cargo license, contesting a fuel levy, once, memorably, to report that a crocodile had eaten my fender and I wanted it noted for insurance purposes. The clerk had looked at me as though I'd asked him to notarize the crocodile.

This morning I came with Elias Vane's map folded inside my shirt, against my sternum, where it sat like a second heartbeat.

I want to say I walked in calm. I want to tell you I was thinking clearly about wax cloth and brass keys and promises made to dying men on mudbanks. What I was actually thinking was: *Tamu will kill me if I've let the paper get damp.* And beneath that, quieter, the thing I hadn't said aloud to anyone, including myself: *a dead man gave me his life's work because he thought I was honest, and I would very much like the universe not to test that opinion.*

The clerk — same one, older, balder, still wearing the expression of a man personally betrayed by paperwork — looked up when I set my cargo manifest on his counter.

"Kestrel," he said, without checking. He knew my boat the way you know a dog that's bitten you once. "Ishikawa. You're late on the—"

"I'm not late on anything. I checked twice."

"Then you're early on something." He squinted at me. "That's worse."

He wasn't wrong. I slid the manifest across, waited while he stamped it with the ceremonial slowness of a man paid by the hour and bitter about the rate, and then, casual as asking directions to the latrine, I said: "While I'm here. Salvage permits. Upper Fly gorge. Who holds them?"

His stamp paused mid-air.

That pause told me more than his answer did. Clerks at the Kiunga registry are not easily surprised; they have seen every variety of greed and grief walk through that door. But a woman who ran cargo asking after salvage rights in the gorge — that was new, and new things made him nervous, because new things made work.

"Why?" he said.

"Curiosity."

"Curiosity costs nothing except when it doesn't." He finished the stamp anyway, pushed the manifest back, and then leaned forward on his elbows, lowering his voice in the way of officials everywhere, which is to say loudly enough for everyone to hear. "Corrigan Salvage Fleet. Three permits, contiguous, covering the gorge from the confluence to the falls. They expire—" he consulted a ledger with visible reluctance, "—in six tides."

Six tides. Twelve days, give or take, if you counted the way river people count, which is by water, not sun. The moon would drag the tide up the delta twice a day, and each time it fell back it took a little of Corrigan's claim with it.

"And after?"

"After," he said, with the small satisfaction of a man delivering bad news he didn't have to author, "the gorge reverts. Customary owners. The council upstream has been petitioning for eleven years." He tapped the counter. "You'd need their leave to so much as dip a bucket past the falls, after that."

I thanked him. I even smiled, which cost me something, because my face isn't built for it and he flinched.

Outside, the morning market was in full roar — sago and smoke fish, betel nut, a man selling outboard spark plugs laid out on a tarp like relics. I bought green bananas I didn't need, because standing still in a market gives you a reason to be there, and I needed a reason to stand still and think.

Here is what I knew, laying it out like cards:

Elias Vane had found a wreck in the gorge. A wreck old enough that no living permit could touch it — Tamu had all but sworn the name on the map matched a steamer lost before the Great War. If the wreck predated every concession, then legally it belonged to whoever recovered it first, or possibly to nobody, or possibly to the Crown, or possibly to the customary owners after six tides, depending on which clerk you asked and how much you were willing to pay him to keep asking.

Which meant the clock wasn't just Corrigan's clock. It was mine.

And Corrigan — I knew the name the way you know a weather pattern. Corrigan Salvage Fleet ran half the dredges on the lower river, owned two barges and a seaplane and, rumor insisted, three magistrates. Old man Corrigan had died rich and hated. The son ran it now, from Port Moresby, and never came upriver himself, which told you everything: men who do their own dirty work at least have the decency to look at what they've dirtied.

I was still arranging these thoughts when I saw the man in the white shirt.

* * *

There's a bar across from the registry called the Anchor, which is the kind of joke that tells you nobody in Kiunga has ever seen an anchor. It has a tin roof, a concrete floor, warm beer, and cold company. Rivermen drink there when they're flush and when they're broke, because the beer is the same either way.

I went in for water, honestly. That's my story and I'm keeping it.

He stood at the end of the bar with a glass of soda water — soda water, in the Anchor, which should have been warning enough — and he was buying rounds. Not drinking them. Buying them. There's a difference, and the difference has a name, and the name is *expense account*.

He was tall in the way that's meant to be unremarkable, brown-haired, mid-forties, with a face like a well-kept ledger: balanced, legible, nothing entered twice. His shirt was pressed. Nobody presses a shirt up the Fly. You press a shirt in Moresby and you wear it upriver as a message, and the message is *I am not like you and I don't intend to become like you.*

I took my water to a corner table under a slowly rotating ceiling fan and listened.

"—prospector," he was saying to the barman, sliding notes across. "Old fellow. Grey beard, missing some teeth, talked too much. Would've been through here maybe two, three weeks back, heading downriver." Another note joined the first. "Name of Vane."

The barman, who had sold me diesel advice and gossip in equal measure for years, shook his head with the smoothness of a man who has learned that information is also currency and he sets the exchange rate. "Lots of prospectors."

"This one's dead now," the man said pleasantly. "So it hardly matters what he was. What matters is whether anyone spoke to him. Whether he left anything behind. Papers, maps, trinkets." He smiled. It was a good smile. It was the smile of a man who has practiced smiling at people he intends to hurt, until the practice wore grooves. "A boat picked him up off a mudbank, they say. Small trader. Cargo boat."

Every neck in the Anchor rotated toward the window, toward the wharf, toward the Kestrel sitting at her mooring fat and obvious as a target at a fair.

I kept my eyes on my water and my heart somewhere below my ribs where it couldn't be read off me.

"You know," the man continued, conversational, to the room at large now, "it's a strange thing, this river. Things wash up. Wrecks, bones, stories. Most of them are worth nothing. Some of them are worth a great deal, and the difference is usually just — knowing which is which." He set another bill on the bar. "Drinks on me. And if anybody remembers anything about a prospector named Vane, or a cargo boat that pulled a dying man off a sandbar — I'll remember you too. I'm good at remembering."

He turned then, and I got my first full look at him, and I'll tell you what struck me wasn't the size of him or the money. It was the patience. Some men look at a room like they're hunting in it. This man looked at it like he was filing it. Every face, cross-referenced, archived, retrievable later at need.

Later, at the wharf, Pita — who'd been minding the boat and eavesdropping shamelessly along the rail of the next pier — told me his name. Deacon Rill. Corrigan's man. The one they send when letters and lawyers fail, which is to say the one they send first, quietly, before the letters and lawyers make failure official.

"He bought the whole bar drinks," Pita reported, with the awe of a sixteen-year-old encountering professional evil for the first time. "Nobody even drank them. They just held the bottles. Like — like holding a snake somebody gave you."

"That's exactly right," I said. "Hold it gently. Don't drink it. Wait and see what it wants."

* * *

Tamu heard me out in the engine room, which is where he hears everything out, because Tamu regards open air as a place where opinions can escape and breed. He sat on his stool beside the main engine — an ancient Gardner he talks to more tenderly than he's ever talked to any person — and he listened with his head cocked, the way he does, and when I finished he was quiet for long enough that I started counting rivets to pass the time.

"Six tides," he said finally.

"Twelve days."

"Tide's not days." He held up a hand, fingers spread, and folded one down. "Spring tide comes day after tomorrow. Bigger water, bigger drop. Then neaps. Then springs again at the end. If you're diving in the gorge, you dive on slack water, and slack water in the gorge happens maybe ninety minutes a day, and only if the rain upstream agrees." He folded a second finger. "So it's not twelve days. It's maybe eight working windows, and half of those the current will argue with you."

"Thank you," I said. "I was feeling optimistic."

"You were feeling stupid, which is different and more comfortable." But there was no bite in it. Tamu's insults are load-bearing; remove them and the whole structure of his affection collapses. He swung off the stool and wiped his hands on a rag that had achieved a kind of geological permanence. "Show me the map again."

I unfolded it on the workbench, weighting the corners with spark plugs. Wax cloth, grease-pencil lines, Elias's handwriting cramped and urgent in the margins. The gorge drawn in section, depths marked, a bend indicated with a symbol like a hook. And the name, written twice, once in confidence and once, it seemed to me, in penance: *Meridian.*

"Lost in '09," Tamu said, for the third time since the mudbank, as though repetition might change the answer. "Or 'ten. My father crewed boats on this river before the war — he used to sing a song about her. Four verses. The last verse is just names." He looked at the map the way other men look at graves. "They never found her. Officially she went down in flood water with all hands. Unofficially—" he shrugged, a big slow shrug that moved his whole shoulders, "—unofficially is why songs get four verses instead of three."

"Elias knew where she was."

"Elias knew where she was and sat on it for fifty years." Tamu tapped the margin, where Elias had written something I hadn't yet fully deciphered — a date, a depth, and a word scratched out so hard the wax cloth had thinned. "Men don't sit on gold for fifty years, Mara. Men sit on *shame*. Gold's just what the shame is buried under."

I looked at the scratched-out word. I'd see it again soon enough, under better light, and wish I hadn't.

"So we have eight windows," I said, "and a dead man's guilt, and a permit that expires with the moon, and a man in a pressed shirt buying soda water for a bar full of liars."

"Yes," Tamu said. "Also the engine's starboard injector is worn, the bilge pump takes prayer to start, and we owe four months' moorage at the Kiunga wharf, which is why I was surprised you spent money on bananas."

"They were strategic bananas."

"Mm." He began packing tools into the canvas roll he'd carried up and down this river longer than I'd been alive. "Then we'll want a diver. Not a fleet diver — a real one. Someone who can work dark water on slack tide and keep her mouth shut. And someone young with strong arms for the winching. And—" he paused, rag in hand, and gave me a look over the top of his spectacles, "—and you'll want to decide, before we cast off, whether we're doing this for the gold or for whatever your dead prospector actually buried down there. Because those are different voyages, and they need different crews, and only one of them comes home clean."

I didn't have an answer. I want to be honest about that, since honesty is apparently the family business now — a dead man's honesty, inherited unpaid. I stood in my engine room among the smell of diesel and hot metal and I did not know what I was doing, only that stopping felt worse than not knowing.

"The tide turns tomorrow morning," Tamu said, shouldering his tool roll. "If we're going, we go on it."

* * *

I stood the evening watch alone on the roof of the wheelhouse, which is where I do my best thinking and my worst sleeping. Kiunga lay along the bank in its scatter of lamplight. Upstream, the Fly ran dark and enormous, carrying more water than any argument men might have about it, going where it went regardless of permits.

Six tides. I said it aloud, testing it. Somewhere downriver, a man in a pressed shirt was also counting, and he had barges and lawyers and a bar full of bought memory, and I had a worn injector, strategic bananas, and a promise.

On the far side of the ledger, though — and I've learned to check both sides, being a woman the river has already robbed once — I had something he didn't. Rill was buying the past. Drinks, memories, silence. You can purchase a lot of that in a country where everyone's poor. But you can't buy the future, and the future was falling out from under Corrigan's permits one tide at a time, and when the last one drained away, the gorge belonged to the people upstream who had been singing four-verse songs about the Meridian for fifty years.

All I had to do was get there first, get down, and get out — with whatever it was Elias Vane had really buried in that hull.

Simple. Rivers love that word. It's how they lure you.

Below me, Tamu's lamp burned late in the engine room, and I heard him talking low to the Gardner, coaxing the worn injector, promising it one more voyage. Pita was asleep in the fo'c'sle with the hatch open to the breeze. The Kestrel rocked on her mooring lines like something breathing.

Tomorrow's tide turned at 0540. We'd be on it.

I was halfway down the ladder when I saw the light — a skiff, running dark, sliding past the wharf on the last of the ebb. One figure at the oars, unhurried, and as it drew level with the Kestrel the man shipped oars for a moment, just a moment, and looked up at my boat sitting black against the lamps.

Even at forty meters, in the dark, I knew the posture. Patient. Filing.

Deacon Rill had come to look at the Kestrel the way a man looks at a door he intends, eventually, to open.

Then the oars dipped, and the skiff went on downriver, and I stood on the ladder in the dark doing arithmetic I didn't like: he hadn't needed to search the boat tonight. He'd only needed to know which boat. The searching would come later, politely, with papers.

We had six tides, and now we had less than that, and I went below to wake Tamu and tell him we were leaving on the midnight water instead — because the clock hadn't just started.

It had started running.
