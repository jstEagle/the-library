## 5. Crew of Strays

### Mara

The letter I sent to Port Moresby came back in eleven days, which surprised me. I'd half expected it to drown, or be eaten by the postal system, or simply cease to exist the way promises do. Instead a woman stood on the Daru wharf at first light holding it, and she was reading it again as she walked, and she walked like someone who had learned that doors open faster if you look like you own them.

Nell Vane was not what I'd pictured. Elias's letters had described a girl who liked eels and asked too many questions; the woman on the wharf had kept the questions and traded the eels for a canvas satchel and a stare that could strip varnish.

"You're Ishikawa." Not a question. She stepped aboard without being invited, which told me two things: she'd grown up around boats, and she had no intention of leaving until she got what she came for. "You buried my father in a river he never shut up about, and you're telling me he left me *letters*?"

"Letters and a debt," I said. "The letters are yours now. The debt is mine."

"I don't want his debts."

"Good," I said. "Neither did he. He took them anyway. That's most of what being a person is."

She looked at me then — really looked, the way you check a mooring line for rot — and whatever she saw made her shoulders drop half an inch. Not trust. Just a decision to postpone judgment, which on a river is the same thing wearing different clothes.

"Show me where you buried him."

* * *

We buried him at a bend he loved, so we went back to it, and Nell stood at the head of the grave with her jaw set like a woman daring the ground to say something. She didn't cry. I respected that, and also worried about it, because grief that won't come out through the eyes comes out through the hull eventually.

"He wrote to me every year," she said finally. "Birthday, Christmas. Same handwriting getting shakier. Same lie getting longer — *things are going well up here, Nell, thinking of coming down soon.*" Her voice stayed level. Her hands didn't. "Thirty years of *soon*. And then a stranger writes to tell me he's dead and there's something I need to hear in person."

"There is," I said. "But not on a wharf, and not today."

"Then when?"

"When I can promise you'll get more than grief out of it. Grief alone isn't worth the fare."

She turned on me with all of Elias's stubbornness and none of his tiredness. "You don't get to decide what I can afford to know."

"No," I agreed. "I don't. But I get to decide what my crew carries into the gorge, and a crew member who learns a hard thing at the wrong moment drowns everyone else. So: sail with me or don't. If you sail, the truth arrives when the river's ready. If you don't, here are the letters anyway, and my apology for the timing."

She stared at me for a long time. Then she said, "I need work anyway," in the tone of someone conceding a point they intend to win later, and carried her satchel below decks, and claimed the bunk across from mine without asking, which told me everything about how this was going to go.

Old Tamu watched her stow her gear and muttered, "Captain's got herself a daughter now."

"She's not my daughter."

"No, Captain," Tamu said, with the serene confidence of a man who has been right about engines and people for forty years.

### Sefina

They blacklisted me for one survey. One.

The Meridian's sister site, down at the delta — Corrigan's people wanted the depth readings shallower so the insurance payout would stretch further, and I put real numbers on real paper because that is the entire job. Numbers that lie kill divers. I have buried enough divers to have opinions.

So when the woman from the Kestrel found me mending nets in Kiunga and offered me work that was either salvage or piracy depending on how the permits fell, I said yes before she finished the sentence. Not for the money. For the chance to dive something Corrigan wanted kept dark, with witnesses who weren't on his payroll.

She showed me the map in her cabin, wax-cloth cracked along its folds, and I looked at the gorge markings and felt my stomach do the slow cold roll it does before bad dives.

"You know what's down there," I said.

"A wreck predating the permits," she said. "That's all I know."

"That's what you know. What's *down there* is the Meridian, if your prospector's lines are honest. Forty meters of green water, current that reverses with the tide, rock overhangs that shear a lifeline clean if you drift two meters wrong." I tapped the map. "And a company that will burn villages to keep people away. You've met Rill?"

"I've survived him twice."

"Then you understand my price. I don't falsify readings, I don't leave anyone down there, and I don't dive for gold. Gold sinks. Truth floats, if you build it right. We recover the truth, and anything else is ballast."

Mara Ishikawa looked at me for a while, and then she laughed — short and rough, like an engine catching. "Elias Vane said almost the same thing before he died. You'll do."

I should have asked what else Elias Vane said. I learned later that not asking questions on the Kestrel is a skill you acquire by needing it.

### Pita

I did not exactly join the crew. I was on the boat.

This is a distinction Pita would like explained to the magistrate, should it ever come to that. The Kestrel was tied up at Kiunga and I needed to be not-at-Kiunga, because a man named Bo Sef had decided I owed him for cargo that fell off a barge I was nowhere near, and Bo Sef collects debts the way Deacon Rill collects villages — thoroughly and on fire.

So I went up the gangplank at night, meaning to hide till dawn and slip off downstream, except the engine room was warm and the bilge was dry and nobody woke up, and by morning the boat was moving and hiding aboard a moving boat is a completely different crime than hiding aboard a tied-up one, and by the second morning I'd fixed the freshwater pump that had been rattling, and the old engineer caught me doing it and instead of throwing me overboard he handed me a wrench and said "hold this," and that was that.

The captain found me on day three. She sat down across from me in the galley, which I learned later she only does when she's deciding something, and said, "You eat like a crew member, you work like a crew member, so you'll sign on like one. Wages, rations, and if anyone comes looking for you, they find a locked chart room and a captain who lies beautifully."

I signed. I have never signed anything so fast, including things I later regretted less.

The new woman — the dead man's daughter — taught me knots on the fourth day, mostly to stop asking her about her father. The diver taught me helm work, because she said everyone on a river boat should know how to steer it even badly. "Especially badly," she said. "Bad steering done early beats good steering done late."

I held that thought the way you hold a rope in current. It pulled harder than I expected, later.

### Mara

By the fifth day I had a crew, and no two of them trusted each other, which is normal. What wasn't normal was how fast they started behaving like they did.

It happened over the fuel manifest, of all things. Tamu ran the numbers and came up short — not dangerously yet, but short, the way a cough is short of pneumonia — and instead of hiding it, he laid the figures on the galley table after dinner, and Sefina immediately began recalculating consumption against gorge currents aloud, and Nell started drafting a resupply plan for the last fuel stop before the permits expired, and Pita just listened with that hungry look he gets, filing everything away somewhere behind his eyes.

Nobody said *we're in this together.* Rivers don't run on declarations. But I watched four strangers argue about my boat's fuel like it was their funeral procession, and I understood I'd stopped hiring hands and started collecting family, which is a far more dangerous thing to lose.

Later, at the rail, Nell joined me with two cups of terrible coffee and gave me one without ceremony, which from her is practically a hug.

"My father's letters," she said. "I haven't opened the ones from the years I was angry at him. I'm saving those."

"For what?"

"For when I can afford what they say." She looked upstream, toward the gorge. "He always said the river keeps accounts better than banks. Was he talking about money?"

"No," I said.

She nodded slowly, and drank her coffee, and didn't ask. Yet.

Ahead of us the sky over the gorge had gone the bruised color it gets before weather, and behind us, far back downriver, I saw a launch running lights-dark against the dusk, keeping our pace exactly.

Not following. *Pacing.*

Tamu appeared at my shoulder, uninvited, the way he does. "Captain," he said quietly, "that boat's been matching us since the fuel stop."

"I see it."

"Rill doesn't pace, Captain. Rill waits. There's a difference, and the difference is that waiting men already know where you're going."

I watched the dark launch hold its distance, patient as a sandbar, and I put my hand flat on the rail above the engine room — right over the spot where Tamu had found the listening device wired under a floor plate three days ago, the one we'd left in place and fed harmless chatter, and hadn't told the others about yet.

Time, I thought. Running shorter than the fuel.

"Secure for night running," I said. "We make the gorge tomorrow."
