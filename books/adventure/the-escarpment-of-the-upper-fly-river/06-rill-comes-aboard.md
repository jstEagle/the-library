## 6. Rill Comes Aboard

There are two kinds of men who board a stranger's boat. The first kind comes aboard to see what's there. The second kind comes aboard to see what isn't.

I am the second kind, though I've made a career of being mistaken for the first.

The Kestrel sat at the Kiunga moorings like a woman pretending not to watch the door — which is to say she sat very still and noticed everything. Forty feet of river iron with a wheelhouse that had been repainted in a hurry and an engine room that had been patched more times than a missionary's trousers. I'd read her registration before I ever set foot on her gangplank. Mara Ishikawa, master. Debt of record: considerable. Prior incident of record: one brother, deceased, cause listed as drowning, which on this river is the polite way of saying the water took its fee.

A boat like that doesn't go upstream into the gorge for cargo rates. A boat like that goes because someone paid her, or promised her, or died on her and left her holding something worth the trip.

I intended to find out which.

* * *

I wore my company face for the occasion. It's a good face — gray at the temples, apologetic at the mouth, the face of a man who has been sent by his superiors to do paperwork he personally finds tiresome. I put on the linen jacket that makes me look slightly too warm and slightly too honest, and I carried a clipboard, because nothing disarms a suspicious person like the suggestion that you are bored.

"Ms. Ishikawa." I showed her the laminated card before she asked. "Deacon Rill, Corrigan Salvage Fleet, compliance division. Routine safety inspection of all vessels registered above the confluence. New insurance underwriting, nothing personal."

"Nothing's ever personal with insurance," she said, and stepped aside to let me aboard.

That was the first thing worth noting about Mara Ishikawa: she didn't argue. Arguing tells me where the valuables are buried. Compliance tells me nothing, which means the person doing it knows exactly what compliance costs. She let me aboard the way a card player lets you cut the deck — graciously, because refusing would cost more than allowing.

She was smaller than the stories suggested and harder than either. Sun-browned, hands like knotted rope, eyes that did their accounting without moving. I have been looked at by policemen, priests, and one very memorable crocodile, and I rank her gaze comfortably among them.

"Compliance division," she said, walking me aft. "Didn't know Corrigan had one."

"We didn't until last quarter. Now we do. The river's getting crowded, Ms. Ishikawa. Crowded rivers get regulated. That's just physics with paperwork."

"You'll want the engine room, then."

"I'll want everything," I said pleasantly. "But yes. Start there. People hide things in engine rooms because they think grease is privacy."

She almost smiled. Almost. There's a version of this woman, I thought, who could be very dangerous given a reason, and I filed that thought where I file all the others, in the drawer marked Later.

* * *

The engine room smelled of diesel, hot iron, and old sweat, and beneath those, faintly, of incense — someone burned something down here recently, and not for mechanical reasons. Grief does that on boats. You bury what the river gives back, or you burn what it won't.

An old man stood amid the machinery with his sleeves rolled and his forearms scarred the color of mahogany. He didn't stop working when I entered. He didn't acknowledge me at all, which is its own kind of message, and one I respect.

"Tamu," Ishikawa said. "My engineer. Tamu, this gentleman wants to inspect."

"Gentleman," said Tamu, to the fuel pump.

"He's deaf in one ear," Ishikawa told me, "and selective in the other."

"And yet he heard me perfectly," I said.

Tamu wiped his hands on a rag that had outlived several governments and pointed at the main coupling with the air of a priest indicating scripture. I looked where he pointed. I even asked a technical question or two, and received answers of great precision and zero warmth. Old engineers are the hardest men on any boat to lie to and the easiest to ignore; I gave him the courtesy of the second treatment.

While they talked injectors I walked the room.

Engine rooms confess. They tell you how long a vessel's been running hard, whether the crew eats well, whether money flows or trickles. This one told me the Kestrel ran lean and loved deep. Every repair done twice as well as it needed to be, because nobody was coming to fix it later. And there — low, behind the spare oil drum, a wax-cloth wrap, the kind prospectors use to keep maps dry, tucked with the casualness of a man hiding a wallet in plain sight.

Except.

Except it was too casual. A real hiding place is boring. This one wanted to be found by a man in a linen jacket carrying a clipboard. Which meant the actual map was somewhere I wasn't clever enough to look, and the woman watching me from the companionway knew it.

I crouched, lifted the wrap, and admired the fishing charts inside. Genuine. Current. Baited hook, line, and sinker.

"Cargo manifests?" I called up to her.

"Fishing charts," she said. "We run a little line off the stern when the cargo's light. Cheaper than buying fish."

Of course it was. Everything about this boat was true except the parts that mattered.

* * *

I did the rest of the inspection properly, because thoroughness is not a performance you can skip on the days it matters most. I measured her freeboard, checked her flares, counted her life vests — four short, and I wrote it down, because writing small truths down makes people believe your large lies later. I inspected the wheelhouse, where a photograph of a young man stood propped against the compass binnacle, grinning like the river owed him money. Brother, presumably. Dead brother, certainly. Grief is ballast, Ms. Ishikawa. It keeps a boat steady and it keeps her from turning fast.

I inspected the deck cargo — kerosene drums, rice, salt, roofing tin. Honest freight for dishonest margins. I inspected the galley, where a girl's hair ribbon hung on a nail, which was interesting, because the registry listed no women aboard, and interesting is the coin I trade in.

And then I asked the question I'd come to ask, the way I always ask it: sideways, in passing, while looking at something else.

"Busy stretch coming up," I said, tapping the gorge on her chart. "Company's got survey permits through there. We'll be running barges past the bends, so keep your whistle loud. Oh — and we're asking after independent traffic generally." A shrug, man-of-the-world shaped. "There was a prospector working the upper reaches. Elias Vane. Went quiet some months back. If he flagged you down for fuel or food, the company would be grateful to know. He owed debts in several directions."

Silence, of the kind that has furniture in it.

"Vane," Ishikawa repeated, tasting the name like a suspect coin. "Don't know him."

"No?"

"The Upper Fly's full of men who went quiet. Half of them owe Corrigan money. If we stopped for every debtor we'd never make way."

It was a good answer. It was, in fact, a better answer than the truth requires, because it contained a small insult aimed at the company, and innocent people don't usually bother insulting you. But I have watched a hundred faces deliver the word *no*, and hers delivered it a half-second late — the half-second in which the mind walks down a corridor, checks a door, and comes back.

Somewhere on this boat, Elias Vane had existed. Recently. Possibly as cargo. Possibly as ballast over the side. Possibly as paper.

I thanked her for her time. I shook her hand — calloused, dry, unafraid — and I told her the Kestrel was one of the sounder vessels on the register, which was true, and that the company appreciated operators who kept their papers straight, which was also true, and that both of us were businessmen who understood that the river was big enough for everyone, which was the biggest lie I told all day, and I told it beautifully.

"Mr. Rill," she said, at the gangplank. "For a routine inspection, you sure looked at everything twice."

"Everything's worth looking at twice," I said. "Once to see what's there. Once to see what isn't."

Her face did nothing at all, which told me she understood me completely, and I walked down the plank into the heat feeling the particular satisfaction of a job begun well.

* * *

Here is the thing about patience: everyone praises it and almost no one practices it, because patience looks like losing. I have spent twenty years learning to lose gracefully in public while winning privately. So I did not search the Kestrel's cabins. I did not bribe her harbor watchman — not yet, anyway; a man bribed too early talks too soon. I did not follow her when she cast off two days later, because following is a confession and I don't confess.

Instead, in the engine room, while Tamu explained fuel filters to me with the tenderness other men reserve for grandchildren, I left a gift.

Small. Black. Weight of a beetle. I placed it inside the housing of the auxiliary generator, where vibration would mask its hum and where no one listens because everyone assumes engines only speak to mechanics. It would report every word spoken below decks for ninety days. Voices, mostly. Names, if the river was kind.

I don't love the devices. They feel like cheating, and cheating is for men without craft. But craft takes tides, and tides were something I suspected we were short of. My employers' permits expire in six, and after that the gorge belongs to people who remember things the company prefers forgotten. Somewhere between Kiunga and the gorge, Mara Ishikawa had acquired a dead prospector's secret, and secrets, on this river, travel toward water the way blood travels toward a wound.

So I listened. Patiently. Politely. Thoroughly.

Or I would have — if the engineer hadn't found it first.

* * *

I learned this three days later, in the worst possible way: by listening to silence.

Ninety hours after the Kestrel cast off, my receiver — a modest box in my cabin on the salvage tender *Perseverance*, disguised as a radio spare — went quiet. Not dead; dead units die mid-word. Quiet. The unit reported a power interruption at 0400, then a single sentence transmitted in a burst before it went dark forever, and the sentence was recorded by my own equipment with cruel fidelity:

Tamu's voice, unhurried, almost gentle: *"Found you, little beetle."*

Then a click. Then nothing.

I sat with that for a long while, in the dark, with the river going by.

Understand what it meant. It meant the old man swept his engine room the way a monk prays — daily, kneeling, without hope of reward. It meant he'd found the device and let it run for three days rather than kill it immediately, feeding it three days of ordinary conversation about fuel and weather and the price of rice, three days of nothing, which is harder to fabricate than a lie and more damning than a confession. It meant they had known I was listening while they spoke, and had chosen what I heard.

And it meant, finally, that when Mara Ishikawa smiled at me over her gangplank and wished me calm seas, she had already decided I was not a compliance officer, and had let me board anyway — let me count her life vests, look at her dead brother's photograph, ask my question about Elias Vane — because a boarded boat is a known quantity and an unboarded enemy is not.

I had gone aboard to read her. She had let me, the way you let a child win at cards, and all the while she'd been reading me back: my questions, my clipboard, my careful sideways mention of Vane. I had shown her everything. She had shown me a fishing chart.

I poured myself the drink I allow myself on failures — one measure, no ice, drunk standing — and I corrected my ledger.

Item: Mara Ishikawa is not a courier. Couriers get nervous. She had been courteous, complete, and calm, which is the behavior of a principal.

Item: the prospector is dead. Men who owe Vane-shaped debts don't ask after living men with that particular gentleness. Which meant somewhere behind her lay a grave, and somewhere ahead of her lay whatever he'd been carrying, and the two were connected by a promise, because boats like the Kestrel only go upstream for promises.

Item: six tides. Five now. And a woman with a fast boat, a loyal crew, and nothing left to prove to anyone except the dead.

I am often accused of cruelty, and I accept the charge the way a scalpel accepts blame. But cruelty is just attention with nowhere else to go, and I had nowhere else to go. I stood at the rail of the Perseverance and watched the gorge country darken in the distance, blue and folded and patient as a closed fist, and somewhere up inside it a forty-foot boat was climbing toward something my employers would burn villages to keep buried.

"Master Rill," said the bosun, behind me. "Do we follow her up?"

I considered it. Following is pressure, and pressure makes people drop things — but it also makes them hurry, and hurried people sometimes arrive.

"No," I said. "We know where she's going. Let her get there." I turned back to the dark water. "Dig out the chart of the gorge narrows. And find out everything there is to know about an engineer called Tamu — where he's from, who he mourns, what he loves." I smiled at the river, which smiles at no one. "When the time comes to take something from a man, it's best to know what he'll trade for it."

Five tides.

I have robbed braver men with fewer.
