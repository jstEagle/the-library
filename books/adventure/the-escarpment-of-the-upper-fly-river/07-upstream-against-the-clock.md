## 7. Upstream Against the Clock

The gorge announced itself the way bad news does — not all at once, but as a change in the quality of the silence.

I was on the foredeck when the Kestrel rounded the last bend of open water and the walls came up on either side of us, green and dripping, close enough that a strong man could spit at both. The river narrowed from a quarter mile to less than a hundred meters and the water went from brown to that deep bottle-green the Fly keeps in the gorge, the color of something holding its breath. Old Tamu eased the throttle back and the engine note dropped like a man lowering his voice in church.

"First rapids in four kilometers," he called up to Mara at the wheel. "Then they don't stop."

I had run water like this before. Not this water — nobody runs this water but the people who were born to it and the fools who come to die — but water with the same intentions. Rapids are honest in a way people aren't. They tell you exactly what they're going to do to you, and then they do it, and the only variable is whether you've been paying attention.

I had been blacklisted from the Corrigan fleet for paying attention. There's a lesson in that somewhere, though I've never had the leisure to learn it.

Mara Ishikawa came down from the wheelhouse with the map rolled in her fist and that look she gets, the one that's half captain and half woman doing arithmetic she doesn't like. "Sefina. You've run gorge water."

"Once. In a company boat, with a company diver's certification that the company has since decided I never earned." I shrugged. "The water won't care about my paperwork. Neither will you, if you're smart."

"Am I smart?"

"You hired a blacklisted diver, a runaway boy, and the angry daughter of a dead prospector. You're a great many things, Captain. Smart is the one I'd have to see demonstrated."

That earned me half a smile, which from Mara is a decoration worth more than the company medals they took back. She turned to the deck, where Pita was coiling line with the frantic energy of a boy who'd discovered that work is safer than conversation, and Nell Vane was sitting on the cargo hatch with her arms crossed, watching Mara the way a cat watches a bird it has decided not to chase yet. For now.

"Everyone," Mara said. "Before the water gets loud, you're going to learn what Sefina has to teach. Because if this goes wrong — when something goes wrong — the difference between a crew and a crowd is whether you know your jobs cold."

I'll give her this: she knows how to hand a person a stage.

* * *

I started with rope, because rope is where diving starts and where most divers end.

"Say I'm down there." I pointed at the green water sliding past the rail, already quickening, its surface beginning to wrinkle over the submerged rock shelf ahead. "Say I'm tangled, or out of air, or unconscious. What do you do?"

"Panic," said Pita.

"Correct answer. Everyone panics. The trick is to panic in the right order." I took the line from his hands and showed him the knot — not a bowline, though it wore a bowline's clothes, but a release hitch you could work blind and gloved and shaking. "You tie this. You throw it. You count. If I haven't pulled twice in sixty seconds, you don't come in after me, you bring the boat. Because a dead diver is a tragedy, and two dead divers is a habit."

"Whose habit?" Pita asked.

"Every salvage outfit's. Corrigan's especially. They write it in the margins of the safety manual, in pencil, where the auditors don't look." I put the line back in his hands. "Again."

He did it again. And again. The boy learned knots the way some people learn songs — once heard, never lost. By the fourth repetition his hands had stopped shaking. I made a note of that. In my experience the ones who tremble at the start are the ones who hold when it matters, and the ones who start smooth are the ones who fold. The river taught me that. The river teaches everything, if you can afford the tuition.

Nell watched all of this from the hatch with her arms still crossed. She hadn't asked to learn anything. She had, in the two days since Kiunga, asked Mara Ishikawa approximately four hundred questions, none of which Mara had answered to her satisfaction, and I include in that count the ones Mara had answered fully and truthfully. Some questions aren't looking for answers. They're looking for a weakness in the wall.

"Show me the signals," Nell said finally, not moving from her seat. "The hand ones. If I'm topside and you're down."

I showed her. Fist for hold. Flat palm for stop. Throat-cut for come up now, now, now. She repeated them back with a precision that surprised me — she'd been watching longer than she'd let on, cataloguing. There was something of her father in the way she learned, if the stories about Elias Vane were true. The old man had supposedly been able to read a river's surface like a ledger.

The thought of ledgers made me think of what we were actually doing here, and I put it away. There's a discipline to salvage work: you don't think about what's in the hold until you're in the hold. Think about the gold and you get greedy, and greedy divers die. Think about the dead and you get careless, and careless divers die. Think about nothing but the next meter of line and the next breath, and you might live long enough to think about everything at once, on the surface, with a drink in your hand.

The gorge took that discipline away from me by force. The first rapids came up on us like a decision already made.

* * *

I have run rapids in boats made of aircraft aluminum and boats made of lies, and I will tell you what the Kestrel was made of: stubbornness, riveted. She was a cargo boat, forty years old, built for the broad brown reaches below, not for this green throat of a gorge. Her hull was deep and her draft was wrong and her engine was old Tamu's religion, and she went into that first rapid the way a grandmother goes into a fight — slowly, and then all at once, and then with terrifying commitment.

I was at the forward line with Pita. Mara had the wheel, and I will say this for Mara Ishikawa: I have watched company pilots with ten times her tonnage and twice her certification handle water half this mean, and handle it worse. She didn't fight the current. She negotiated with it. She gave the river its head where the river insisted, and took it back where the river was only bluffing, and the Kestrel went through that first rapid like a cork with opinions — slammed, spun, shipped a ton of green water over the bow that caught me full in the chest and knocked the sarcasm out of me for a full five seconds.

Pita laughed. Actually laughed, high and startled, a boy's laugh from a boy who I'd begun to suspect didn't laugh much.

"Don't get attached to it," I told him, wringing out my shirt. "The river does that to everyone. It's how it makes friends."

Tamu's engine roared and coughed and roared again, and we clawed out the far side into a stretch of relative calm, and behind us the rapid kept up its white argument with the empty air where we'd been.

Mara's voice came down from the wheelhouse, level as a spirit gauge. "Four more like that before the middle gorge. Then worse."

"Cheerful," Nell said. But she'd come forward during the rapid without being told, had braced herself against the mast and kept her eyes on the water the whole way through, reading it, and I saw Mara see that, and I saw something pass between them that wasn't quite warmth but was at least a ceasefire.

It lasted about a kilometer. Then Nell went back to her interrogation.

* * *

I wasn't meant to hear it. That's the thing about a boat — there is no *not* hearing anything on a boat. Forty meters of deck and an engine room that amplifies everything, and every conversation is a public utility.

Nell cornered Mara amidships, in the stretch where the gorge walls narrowed the world to water and rock and the two of them. I was coiling line ten feet away, invisible, the way a diver learns to be.

"You knew him," Nell said. "Elias. My father. You ran him up and down this river for what — six years?"

"Five. Cargo mostly. Sometimes he rode as passenger." Mara's voice was careful. She was steering between the words the way she'd steered the rapid.

"Five years. And in five years, what did you learn about him?"

"That he drank less than he pretended to. That he paid his debts early. That he looked at the gorge like a man looking at a cell he'd been let out of." A pause, the sound of the water filling it. "That he was afraid of something back upriver. Not the river itself. Something the river was keeping for him."

"Did he ever say what?"

"No."

"Did you ever ask?"

"Every way a person can ask without asking. He answered every way a person can answer without answering." Mara's voice didn't change but it got quieter, which on the water is the same thing as changing. "Nell. I ran cargo for a man for five years and I didn't know his daughter existed until he was dying on my deck. Whatever I could have learned from him, I ran out of time to learn. I'm not going to pretend otherwise to make this easier for you."

"And what did he give you?" Nell's voice cracked on it, fury and grief in the same syllable, which is the worst way for a voice to crack because you can't tell which one is winning. "A map. A key. A promise to keep. And what did I get? Twenty-three years of nothing. Letters that stopped. Money that stopped. A man who was a legend on this river and a rumor in his own daughter's life."

I tied the same knot over and over and listened and hated myself a little for listening and kept listening.

"He talked about you," Mara said.

The silence that followed had a different texture. Even the river seemed to lean in.

"He was dying and in a great deal of pain and he talked about you. He said you were stubborn. He said it the way you'd say it about someone you were proud of. He said you'd be angry, and that you had a right to be, and that the anger was — his word — *earned income*. He said you'd know what that meant."

Nell made a sound that was almost a laugh and almost the other thing. "He used to say that. About everything. About the years he was away. *It's earned income, Nell, it all goes in the ledger.*" Her voice dropped. "He had a ledger for everything. Chores. Allowance. What he owed my mother. I thought it was a game when I was small. Then I thought it was a sickness."

"And now?"

"Now I think it was a man keeping accounts because he couldn't stand what the balance was."

The water said what water says, which is nothing and everything. I kept my eyes on my rope. Some doors you hear close and you know better than to look.

"I have something else," Mara said, and I heard her move, heard the cabin door, and when she came back her boots had a different weight to them. "He gave me these the night he died. Sealed. He said — his words — *she opens them when she's ready, not when she's angry, and she'll know the difference.*"

Paper. I heard paper. An envelope's particular dry whisper, and then a longer silence than any river makes.

"You kept these for two weeks," Nell said finally, and her voice had gone very flat, very controlled, which in my professional experience is the most dangerous condition a human being can be in short of drowning. "You sat on my father's letters for two weeks while I asked you everything I knew how to ask."

"I sat on them because he asked me to. And because I've delivered enough things in my life to know that some cargo you don't rush." A breath. "And because I was a coward about it. Both things are true. The river holds two currents at once. So do I."

Another silence. Longer. When Nell spoke again the flatness had cracked along one line, just one, like ice before it goes.

"Did he say anything else? That night?"

"He said he was sorry. He said it more than once. He said it like a man who'd been practicing."

I heard the envelope open. Not then — later, I learned, she waited until the middle gorge, until the second rapid, until the noise was too great for even a boat's terrible acoustics, and she read her father's words with the river roaring over them so that no one, not even a listening diver, could hear her cry. That was Nell. She'd learned privacy the hard way, from a man who'd practiced it.

But that came later. What I knew then was the sound of paper unfolding on the deck of the Kestrel, and the long quiet of a daughter reading, and Mara Ishikawa standing at the rail beside her not reading, not speaking, just keeping watch the way you keep watch over water you can't control.

* * *

The second rapid nearly took the rudder.

I'll compress the story of it, because the story is mostly water in the face and rope burns and Tamu in the engine room doing things with a wrench and a prayer that I suspect violated several laws of both mechanics and the church. The gorge's second rapid was a standing wave over a drowned shelf, and the Kestrel hit it wrong by half a boat-length, and for about nine seconds we were not a boat but a rumor of one, sideways, green, and full of shouting.

Pita held the forward line. I'll say that plainly, because nobody said it to him afterward and it deserved saying: the boy held the line, in water to his chest, with the deck pitching like a deck of cards, and he did not let go, and when we came out the other side his knuckles were white and bleeding and he was grinning like he'd been baptized into something.

Nell had lashed herself to the mast — my knots, I noticed, the release hitch, tied blind and correct — and she had the signal flags in her fist and she used them, throat-cut at the right moment, fist at the right moment, and Mara read them and answered, and the two of them worked the Kestrel through the worst water I'd seen in six years of diving for men who paid in company scrip.

Afterward, in the calm below, Nell came to find me at the bow. Her hair was down and half-drowned and her eyes were red in a way she wasn't going to discuss.

"The letters," she said. "You knew about them."

"I knew there was a sealed envelope. I don't read other people's mail."

"You listen to other people's conversations, though."

"Boats don't offer alternatives." I wrung out a line. "For what it's worth — I've been salvaging for eleven years. I've seen what men leave behind when the water takes them. Most of it's debt and rust. Your father left a map and a key and a stack of sealed letters addressed to you. In my trade, that makes him one of the good ones."

"He abandoned me."

"Yes," I said, because I've found that agreeing with the true thing is faster than arguing around it. "And then he spent whatever was left of his life trying to make the balance come out. Men are rarely one thing, Nell. The river taught me that too, and the tuition on that lesson was high."

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she held out her hands, palms up. "Show me the signals again. All of them. If I'm going down with this boat, I'm going down useful."

I showed her. She learned them in an hour. By the third rapid she was calling them from the mast like she'd been born to a wheelhouse, and Mara was answering, and the Kestrel went up the gorge like a debt being collected — slowly, against the current, but going.

* * *

We made the middle gorge pool by late afternoon, that deep still green water where the rapids pause to consider their options, and Tamu killed the engine to let it cool, and the silence that fell on us was the enormous kind, the gorge-wall kind, the kind that makes a boat feel like a seed in a crack of stone.

That was when Pita, up on the cargo hatch keeping watch the way I'd taught him — eyes on the water, eyes on the walls, eyes on the water — went very still and said, in the careful voice of a boy who has learned that what he sees matters now:

"There's a light. Back there. In the gorge we came through."

I looked. High up, far back, where the second rapid's spray hung in the air like gauze — a point of light that was not the sun, because the sun was behind the western wall, and this light was in the eastern shadow, and it moved. It moved the way a lamp moves when a man is carrying it along a ledge.

Mara was beside us before I'd finished looking, binoculars up.

"Someone's following the gorge trail," she said quietly. "On foot. Running lights dark on the water, a lamp on the cliff path." She lowered the glasses, and I saw her jaw set in that way I was learning to read the way she read the river.

"Deacon Rill doesn't need a boat," she said. "He knows where the gorge goes. There's only one place it leads."

Behind us, in the green throat of the gorge, the small light kept coming.
