## 8. The Burned Village

I have a theory about rivers. A river never lies to you outright — it just tells you the truth in pieces, and lets you decide which piece to swallow. The Fly had been telling us something all morning, and I'd been swallowing the wrong parts.

First it told us there were no canoes. For two days we'd passed fishing platforms, dugouts pulled up on the mud, kids waving from the banks like we were a floating circus they'd paid admission to see. Then nothing. Empty water for hours. I mentioned it to Sefina at breakfast and she said maybe it was a fish-closing day, some taboo, and I said sure, and Tamu said nothing at all, which from Tamu was louder than either of us.

Then the river told us about the smoke. Not cooking smoke — cooking smoke sits up straight and smells of sago and fish. This smoke lay down along the water like something that had been beaten and left where it fell.

"Kestrel's coming up on Wabunari," Mara called from the wheelhouse. "Should be roofs by now."

There were no roofs.

* * *

We came around the last bend slow, engine idling down to that heartbeat thud Tamu keeps her tuned to, and the village opened out in front of us like a sentence with the middle torn out.

Wabunari had been one of those places you pass and think, *that's what living looks like*. Houses on stilts above the mud, gardens climbing the slope behind, a church with a corrugated roof gone orange with rust, laundry strung between palms like flags of some small proud nation. Now the stilts stood bare, charred black to knee height, holding up nothing. The church roof had collapsed into itself, a burnt ribcage. Ash lay over everything in a grey skin, and where ash hadn't settled, the mud showed footprints — dozens of them, all pointing away from the village, none pointing back.

Nobody spoke. Even the river seemed to go quiet under the hull, as if it were ashamed.

"Pita." Mara's voice came down flat and careful. "You know these people."

It wasn't a question. She knew my mother's people traded up this stretch; she'd hired me partly because of it, though neither of us ever said so out loud. I was the deckhand nobody trusted with anything except rope and dishes, but I was also the only soul aboard who could walk into a village here without being a stranger.

"I know them," I said. My mouth had gone dry. "Some of them."

"Take the dinghy. Take Nell. Look for survivors. Don't touch anything that looks like it might be evidence — Elias taught me that word and now I can't stop using it." She paused, and her eyes went downriver, back the way we'd come, doing arithmetic I didn't want to see her finish. "And keep your eyes open for who did this. Because somebody did."

"Somebody always does," Nell said quietly. She was already climbing down the ladder, rifle slung across her back like she'd been born wearing it. For a schoolteacher's daughter from Port Moresby she carried it better than I carried a mop.

The dinghy scraped ashore on grey sand that crunched underfoot. Crunching sand is wrong. Sand should sigh. This sand was ash and broken glass and the calcined bones of a cooking fire that had been everything at once — house, kitchen, history.

* * *

We walked the main path between the house-stumps. Things had been sorted through before the burning — drawers pulled out, boxes split, mats thrown aside. But it wasn't looting, exactly. It was searching. Whoever came through hadn't wanted the outboard motors or the trade goods. They'd wanted paper. Photographs. Names.

"They were looking for someone," Nell said. She crouched by a fallen doorpost, turning over a scrap of half-burnt exercise book. Schoolwork, a child's careful pencil letters. She put it down gently, the way you'd put down something sleeping.

"Or looking for whether someone had been *here*," I said. And then, because saying it made it real: "Asking after a dead prospector. Like Rill was doing in Kiunga."

Nell looked at me. In the four days since she'd come aboard angry and armed, she'd softened toward everyone on the Kestrel except the subject of Rill, and on that subject she'd gone hard as a cleat. "Clearing witnesses," she said. "Along his route. My father's map goes right past this village, doesn't it."

I didn't answer. The map wasn't mine to discuss, and besides, the answer was standing all around us in charcoal.

We found the first survivor an hour later, or rather he found us — a boy of maybe ten, soot-streaked, standing in the treeline with a bush knife too big for him held in both hands like a sword in a story. He didn't run when he saw us. He just watched, and I remembered being that age, remembering that running tells people you're prey.

"Wanem nem bilong yu?" I asked. What's your name?

He thought about it for a long time, deciding whether we deserved it. "Joel," he said finally. "My grandmother is hurt. Come."

* * *

His grandmother was in a garden hut up the slope, a lean-to of poles and banana leaves that the fire had somehow skipped, maybe out of respect. Her name was Maria, and she was old the way driftwood is old — weathered down to the essential part, still hard enough to be useful. Her leg was burned from ankle to knee, wrapped in leaves that Joel changed twice a day, and she let Nell clean and rebandage it without once taking her eyes off me.

"You are Kavanagh's boy," she said. "From the mission side."

"My mother's people," I agreed. "Downstream."

"Your mother fed my son once, when his canoe broke in flood year." She said it like a receipt. Like a line in a ledger. I was beginning to understand that out here, memory *was* money, and every kindness and every injury was entered in a book no bank could burn.

"What happened here?" Nell asked.

Maria told it plainly, the way old women tell terrible things, because they've buried worse. Three nights ago, a boat came upriver after dark with its lights off. Men in company shirts — she named the salvage fleet the way you'd name a disease. They asked questions. Who had come through, who had been given food, who had talked to a white prospector with a bad cough. The village headman, her cousin, answered honestly: a stranger had passed through months ago, bought sago and medicine, gone on upriver. That was all anyone knew.

"They burned it anyway," she said. "They burned it while we stood on the beach and watched. The headman argued with their boss man — a quiet one, soft voice, polite like a snake is polite — and the boss man said, 'Honest men have nothing to fear from fire.' And then he lit the match himself." Her hand found Joel's shoulder. "Eleven houses. The church. The boats, so we cannot follow or flee. Two elders too slow to get out." She looked at the bandage on her leg and smiled, and it was the worst smile I've ever seen. "I got this pulling my sister's grandson from the church. He did not live."

Nell had gone very still. When she spoke, her voice was level in the way a river is level right before it goes over the falls. "The quiet one. Soft voice. What did he look like?"

"Tall. Grey at the temples. Gloves, even in this heat. He gave us rice afterward." She spat, delicately, with great precision, into the ash. "Company rice. So we would owe them our lives instead of hating them. He said, 'Now we are even.'"

* * *

We stayed the afternoon. There wasn't a choice about it — you don't sail past a burned village with survivors in the hills, not if you're the kind of crew Mara had been building, and I think by then we were that kind whether she'd planned it or not. We ferried down what food the Kestrel could spare. Sefina splinted a fisherman's broken wrist. Tamu, who speaks maybe forty words a week, spent three of them promising to send word to the government station at Balimo, and then spent the rest of the day silently fixing the village's one surviving water drum, which struck me as the more honest promise.

Mara walked the ashes alone for a long time. I watched her from the dinghy, and I saw her stop at the church and stand there with her hat in her hands, and I understood that she was adding this to some private account of her own. She'd taken Elias Vane aboard against her rule about strangers, and strangers had died for it before she ever met them. You could see her deciding how much of that weight was hers to carry.

When the light went long and gold, the elder came down to the beach.

Maria came leaning on Joel's shoulder, and behind her shuffled maybe twenty others, drawn out of the bush by the smell of cooked rice and the sight of the Kestrel riding at anchor like a promise. They lined up on the sand, and I felt my stomach drop, because I knew what a line-up on a beach means. It means ceremony. It means debt.

Mara waded ashore to meet them. Maria spoke to her in Pidgin, then in her own tongue, and Tamu translated in his forty-word way, and the gist of it was this: the village could not repay the food and the medicine. The village did not forget debts, in either direction. And the people who burned Wabunari had made themselves owed-to and owing at the same time — they'd given rice and taken blood, and the books did not balance, and unbalanced books fester.

Then Maria turned away from Mara and came to me.

Me. The deckhand nobody trusted with anything. I actually glanced over my shoulder to check if there was someone important standing behind me. There wasn't. Just Nell, arms crossed, watching with an expression I couldn't read.

"You," Maria said. "Kavanagh's boy. Your mother's people fed mine in flood year. Tonight my people feed yours." She pressed something into my palm and closed my fingers over it with hands surprisingly strong for driftwood. "Where you go, this goes. What you carry, carry true. The river remembers who owes whom."

I opened my hand. It was a carving, small enough to hide in a fist — dark hardwood, oiled smooth by generations of handling. A crocodile, coiled, its tail wrapping around its own body until the tail-tip rested in its open jaws. On its back, scratched fine as hair, were marks I couldn't read: clan signs, or a count of something, or a name.

"It's beautiful," I said, because it was, and because I didn't know what else you say when someone hands you a blood debt the size of a fist.

"It is a witness," Maria corrected. "Fire burns houses. It does not burn accounts. When you find the man with the gloves — and you will find him, because your boat goes where his boat has been — show him this. Tell him Wabunari remembers. Tell him the river keeps better books than any company."

Behind me, I heard Nell exhale slowly. When I looked at her, she was staring at the little crocodile like she'd seen a ghost, and then at me, and something in her face had shifted — the same shift I'd seen the night Mara handed her her father's unopened letters. The look of a person realizing the errand is bigger than the errand.

"We're not treasure hunters anymore," she said quietly. "Are we."

I didn't answer. I closed my fist around the crocodile and felt its carved jaws press into my palm like a small patient bite.

* * *

We weighed anchor at dusk. Mara stood at the wheel with the last light on her face, and she didn't ask me what the token meant, because she'd heard everything Maria said, and anyway Mara only asks questions she doesn't already know the answers to.

"Course?" Tamu asked from the engine room hatch.

"Upstream," Mara said. "Same course. Faster."

I took the bow watch with the crocodile in my pocket, and the river carried us north into the blue dark, past the black stumps of Wabunari, past the empty fishing platforms, past all the places where the banks had gone silent as held breath. And that was when I understood what had been nagging me all day, the thing the river had been telling me in pieces:

Every village we'd passed empty, every canoe missing, every stretch of silence — it wasn't the river clearing its throat ahead of us.

It was Rill, somewhere upstream, working his way toward the same wreck we were, erasing witnesses house by house, and we weren't chasing him at all.

We were sailing straight into whatever he'd left standing.

Ahead, far up the gorge where the water narrowed, I saw a single point of light blink on, hold for three seconds, and go dark. A signal. Or a trap. With Deacon Rill, I was learning, the two words meant the same thing.
