## 9. Dead Reckoning

I have been an engineer for forty-one years, and in that time I have learned three things worth teaching. One: the river does not hate you, it simply does not know you exist. Two: every machine is a liar until you've taken it apart yourself. Three: when something goes wrong on a boat, it is never the thing you were watching.

So when the Kestrel came off the portage at Wawoi Falls with her stern riding two inches light and her engine coughing like an old man climbing stairs, I did not look at the fuel lines first. I looked at everything else. That is my penance for being right too often.

* * *

The portage itself had gone the way portages go — badly, but survivably. We'd winched her up the rollers with Pita on the winch handle and Sefina chalking the skids, Mara standing hip-deep in the shallows calling angles like a woman arguing with God about geometry. Nell had carried the strongroom key around her neck the whole way, touching it every few minutes like a rosary bead, which I pretended not to notice because noticing would have meant admitting what we all knew: that little brass key was the only reason any of us were still breathing this far upriver.

The gorge was closing around us by then. Walls of green stone going up and up into mist, the river squeezed between them into something fast and mean and beautiful the way a knife is beautiful. The air smelled of wet iron and crushed leaves. Somewhere above us, cockatoos screamed at each other about territory, which struck me as reasonable.

We floated her again below the falls at mid-morning, and I went straight to the engine room, because a boat is only ever as honest as the person who keeps her insides.

That's when I heard it. Not saw it — heard it. A hiss under the idle, thin as a snake's opinion, coming from aft of the fuel pump where nobody's hands should have been.

I knelt. I put my nose down there like a dog. And there it was: the main supply line to the day tank, the union fitting backed off half a turn. Not stripped. Not cracked. Loosened. Cleanly, deliberately, by someone who knew engines well enough to make it look like vibration. Half a turn more and she'd have let go entirely somewhere in the worst water of the gorge, and we'd have died with our tanks still reading full, which is the kind of death engineers find particularly insulting.

I sat back on my heels and did not say anything for a long moment. The engine idled above me, oblivious, faithful, betrayed.

Here is the arithmetic of betrayal on a small boat. There are seven of us aboard. One of us loosened that fitting, or one of us let someone aboard who did. Rill had been through the Kestrel once already, polite as a customs man, and Tamu had found his listening device in the bilge wiring within a day — found it, and on Mara's orders said nothing, and left it running so Rill would keep hearing exactly what we wanted him to hear. But a bug only listens. This thing killed. Someone had graduated from eavesdropping to murder, and they'd done it while forty pairs of hands were busy hauling a boat over a mountain of rollers.

Or — and this thought arrived cold and unwelcome — someone hadn't needed to be aboard at all. The portage path ran past a fishing camp. There had been men there. Men who took our tobacco and smiled and asked no questions, which on this river should have been the first alarm. Nobody asks no questions for free.

I tightened the union. I checked every other fitting on that line, then every line, then the fuel lines twice more, then the cooling intake, because paranoia is just professionalism with the volume turned up. Then I climbed out onto the deck, wiped my hands on a rag that had been black since before the war, and said nothing to anyone.

Not yet. You don't shout "sabotage" on a boat with six people on it unless you're ready to learn things about them you can't unlearn. And besides — I had a worse number to run first.

* * *

The number was this: how much fuel did we actually have?

A sabotaged fuel line doesn't just threaten to kill you. It robs you on the way. Every hour that fitting had been seeping — and judging by the oil-slick rainbow in the bilge, it had been seeping since before the falls — we'd been losing diesel into the river, drop by patient drop, the river drinking our margin without us knowing. I pumped the tanks by hand with the sounding rod, the old way, because gauges lie and brass doesn't.

Main tank: sixty-two percent. Day tank: nearly full. Reserve cans lashed aft: four, and one of those sounded hollow in a way that made me open it and find it half-water, either from a bad seal or from somebody's idea of a joke. I stood there holding the can and doing sums in my head, and the sums kept coming out the same ugly way.

Upstream to the wreck site, through the narrows and the second rapids, fighting current the whole way: call it eleven hours of steaming if the river behaved, and the river has never once behaved for me. Idle time on station while Sefina dove: hours more, burning against the current to hold position or swinging on anchors in water that ate anchors. And then back downstream — well, downstream was cheap, the river would carry us home like a drunk carrying a friend.

But here was the thing about the return that the sums wouldn't soften: we weren't planning to come back the way we came. Not if it worked. Not if Nell got her ledger and the council upstream got their reckoning and Corrigan's fleet came apart the way Mara intended. We'd be running loaded, low, and hunted, and every liter I rationed now was a liter of running room later.

I did the arithmetic three times, sitting on the reserve cans in the hot dark with my shirt stuck to my back. Three times it told me the same thing: we could make the wreck with perhaps ten percent to spare. We could not make the return without finding more fuel — trading for it, taking it, or praying for it, and I have never trusted prayer with anything mechanical.

Forty-one years, and I had finally met a problem I could fix with a wrench but not with honesty. Because telling Mara meant telling everyone, and telling everyone meant either a traitor among us learning how scared we were, or five innocent people lying awake all night wondering which of them I suspected. Fear is contagious on a boat. It gets into the bilge with everything else.

So I made the decision that old engineers make, the one nobody thanks you for: I would carry the number myself. I'd trim every burn, nurse every throttle, cheat the current wherever cheating was possible, and if the margin ran out, it would run out on me, quietly, the way margins do. Mara had enough weight on her shoulders to bend a mast. She didn't need mine too.

Besides — and I will admit this only here, in the privacy of my own skull — there was a part of me, the part that had been keeping boats alive since before Mara Ishikawa was born, that was almost relieved. A problem with a number attached is a problem you can fight. Numbers don't ambush you. People do.

* * *

Mara came down into the engine room that evening. She did it sometimes, when the wheel watch was dull — stood in the doorway with her arms crossed, watching me work the way people watch a fire. Tonight she watched longer than usual.

"You've been down here since the falls," she said.

"I've been down here since Kiunga," I said. "You've only just noticed."

"Talk to me, Tamu."

There it was. Forty years of friendship folded into three words, and both of us pretending it was smaller than that. I held up the union fitting, the one I'd removed and pocketed as evidence, turning it in the lamplight so the threads caught.

"Somebody backed this off," I said. "Half a turn. Before the portage. Clean work — looked like vibration unless you were looking." I let it sit between us. "It would have let go in the worst water we'll see. Full stop, dead engine, and this gorge eats dead engines whole."

Mara took the fitting from me. Turned it over. Her face did the thing it always did — went very still, very flat, the river-smooth face she wore so the crew would never see the current underneath. But I've known her since she was seventeen and crying over her brother's homework, and I can read the water under the ice.

"The fishing camp," she said.

"Maybe. Or maybe our polite visitor left more than a bug behind last time he came aboard. Or maybe—" I stopped myself, because the next name on that list was one of ours, and saying it aloud would have made it real in a way neither of us could afford. "Doesn't matter who. Matters that it happened, and matters that it can happen again. From tonight, nobody comes down here alone. Not even you. Especially not you — you're too polite, and politeness is how they get in."

"And the fuel?" she asked. Which was the question I'd been dreading, because Mara Ishikawa misses nothing except the things people are hiding from her, and she was looking directly at the reserve cans, at the sounding rod still leaning against them, at the rainbow slick I'd scrubbed out of the bilge but not out of the smell.

"Seeped some," I said. "Tightened everything. We're fine."

She looked at me for a long moment. The lamp hissed. Upstairs, Pita laughed at something Sefina said, and the sound came down through the deck planks like warmth through a wall.

"We're fine," she repeated, in a voice that meant *I don't believe you but I'm choosing to let you carry it*, and I have never loved her more or lied to her harder in the same breath.

"Aye," I said. "Fine."

She nodded slowly, put the fitting down on my bench like a promise she'd collect later, and went up. I listened to her boots cross the deck, and then I turned back to my numbers, and I shaved another hour off the plan by deciding we'd drift the long pool below the narrows instead of steaming it, engine off, current doing the work, everybody quiet. Little cheats. A minute here, a liter there. An engineer's rebellion is always conducted in fractions.

* * *

I didn't sleep much that night. Old men on boats rarely do; we've heard too many hulls die in our time to trust the silence. Instead I sat in the engine room with the lamp turned low and re-torqued every fastener on that engine, one by one, by feel, the way a blind man reads a face. And somewhere around the third hour, with the gorge walls black against the stars and the river muttering its one long sentence underneath us, I let myself think about who.

Not accusingly. Accusation needs evidence, and evidence needs daylight, and I had neither. But thinking isn't accusing. Thinking is just maintenance of a different kind.

Rill had walked these decks once, smiling, counting. He'd left a bug in my bilge — *my* bilge, which I took personally, the way a priest takes vandalism in his own church. A man who plants ears might plant leaks. But the timing argued against him; he'd been days away when the fitting moved. The fishing camp men had the opportunity and the motive — Corrigan's money moves quietly along this river, buying smiles the way it buys witnesses — but camp men don't usually know marine unions from cooking pots.

Which left, unwillingly, the boat herself. Seven souls, and one of them had been alone below decks during the portage longer than the rest. I turned each name over like a coin, checking the weight of it. Sefina, who'd been blacklisted by Corrigan for refusing to lie — a woman with every reason to hate the fleet and none to help it. Pita, who was sixteen and terrified and had cried the night we buried Elias, badly, thinking nobody heard. Nell, who had boarded us with a gun and a grudge and had spent three weeks becoming the daughter of the river her father abandoned — and whose grief was the rawest thing aboard, and raw grief does strange arithmetic in weak moments.

And then there was me. Alone below decks most often. Knowledgeable enough. Old enough that nobody watches an old man anymore — that's the great camouflage of age, and I'd be a fool not to count myself in any honest suspect list, if only to keep the list honest.

I found no answer. Coins all weighed true. So I did what the river taught me: I stopped asking *who* and started asking *what*. What could I control? The fittings, the fuel, the fractions. What couldn't I? Everything else. And everything else belonged to Mara.

Near dawn I allowed myself one luxury I hadn't earned: I thought about the wreck. The Meridian, sitting in her green grave with her dead arranged around the strongroom like a court in session, waiting forty years for somebody brave or stupid enough to come knocking. Elias Vane had carried his guilt up and down this river for half a lifetime and finally handed it to a stranger on a mudbank because strangers are the only ones who'll take delivery. And now his guilt was in my engine room, in a sense — in the form of eleven hours of fuel I didn't have, feeding an engine somebody had tried to kill.

I patted the Kestrel's engine block the way you'd pat an old horse. "Get us there," I told her. "I'll worry about getting us back."

She ticked and settled, the way old machines do when they agree to something they shouldn't.

* * *

We made the narrows two days later, and I want it recorded that we made them on drift and cunning and perhaps nine liters less fuel than any sane plan required, and that when Mara eased us through the standing waves with her jaw set like a cleat, I stood in my doorway with grease on my hands and felt something dangerously close to pride.

Then Pita, up on the bow with the binoculars, went very still in the way young bodies go still — all at once, like a dropped rope.

"Smoke," he called back. "Ahead. In the side channel. And—Captain—it's a barge. Dark hull. No lights."

Mara was beside him before I'd finished climbing the ladder, and I came up behind her, and we all looked together at the thin gray smudge rising off the water where no smoke had any business being, and I felt the number in my chest — the secret number, the thin one — tighten like a belt pulled one notch further.

Because whatever waited for us in that channel, we would need every liter I had hoarded and every trick I hadn't invented yet. And I had not yet told a living soul how close to empty we truly were.

I put my hand in my pocket, closed it around the loose union fitting, and followed my captain toward the smoke.
