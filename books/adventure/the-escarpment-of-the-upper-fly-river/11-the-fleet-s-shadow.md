## 11. The Fleet's Shadow

The river told me before my eyes did.

You learn to read water the way a priest reads scripture — every riffle a verse, every slack patch a warning. For three days above the gorge the current had been talking to me in its usual dialect, all gravel and gossip, and then on the fourth morning it went quiet in a way that didn't sit right. A long, flat, deliberate quiet, like a room full of people who've just heard footsteps on the stairs.

"Engine's off," I said, though Tamu had already killed it. He was standing at the rail with his ear cocked, listening to the river the way he did, and whatever he heard made his face do something I didn't like. Tamu's face has exactly two settings: patient and very patient. This was neither.

"Smoke," he said. "Downstream. Not cooking smoke. Boiler smoke."

I took the glasses and swept the bend below us. The morning haze was doing its usual trick of turning the world into a watercolor nobody had finished, and for a moment I thought I'd imagined it. Then the haze shifted, and there it was: a smudge of dark against the pale water, moving upstream against the current, which meant an engine with money behind it. No dugout fights the Fly. No trading boat does either, not this far up, not at that speed.

"Corrigan," Nell said. She'd come up without a sound, which was becoming a habit of hers that I both admired and resented. She had her father's eyes, which was a cruel joke on the part of genetics, and her father's habit of asking questions like she was loading a rifle.

"Could be anyone," I said, because I'm an optimist in the way a man falling off a cliff is optimistic about the ground.

"It's not anyone."

It wasn't anyone. We watched the smudge resolve into a shape, and the shape into a barge — a proper one, steel-hulled, with a crane arm folded flat along its spine like a sleeping mantis. Corrigan's lead salvage barge. I'd seen her tied up at the Kiunga registry, all fresh paint and menace, and I'd thought then what I thought now: nobody paints a working boat that pretty unless the boat is a message.

"She's running dark," Pita said. He was fourteen and had the eyes of a cat and the judgment of a cat as well, which is to say both excellent and entirely absent. But he was right. No navigation lights. No working lights. A barge moving upstream through known water with her lights out is either smuggling or hunting, and salvage barges don't smuggle.

"Rill's aboard," Sefina said. She'd surfaced from below with a mug of tea she wasn't drinking, and when she said it, it wasn't a guess. She'd worked the fleet. She knew its rhythms the way I knew the river's. "He runs dark when he wants to arrive without being announced. It's a habit. He thinks it's polite."

"It's not polite," Pita said.

"No," Sefina agreed. "It's not polite."

* * *

We held a council of war in the wheelhouse, which is to say four people and a boy crowded around a chart table that had seen better decades, arguing in whispers as though Rill could hear us across four miles of water. Maybe he could. I wouldn't have put it past him.

The geography was simple, which is the river's one mercy. Below us the gorge country began in earnest — the Fly pinching down between walls of green stone, the channel braiding into a maze of side passages, oxbows, and dead sloughs that the maps recorded with a confidence they hadn't earned. The main channel was the fast way and the obvious way, and the obvious way was exactly where a dark-running barge would be waiting, because Deacon Rill was not a clever man's clever man. He was a patient man's patient man. He didn't set traps. He set appointments, and he simply waited for you to keep them.

"He knows where we're going," Nell said. It wasn't a question, and I was tired of pretending otherwise. "He's known since Kiunga. Maybe before. Maybe he's known since the day my father died, and everything since has just been him walking slowly toward us while we run."

"Then running's done," I said. "Running's what he expects. A man who sets appointments expects you to keep them or to bolt. Both are in his ledger." I put my finger on the chart, on a wrinkle of blue that the surveyor had drawn and then, I suspect, gone to drink about. "This is the old side channel. Kusa Cut. It rejoins the main river below the narrows. It's shallow, it's snagged, it hasn't been run by anyone with sense in twenty years."

"And the alternative?" Tamu asked, though he knew. Tamu always knew. He asked so that the rest of us would have to say it out loud and be responsible for it.

"The alternative is we go down the main channel and meet that barge in the narrows, where there's room for exactly one boat, and it isn't ours."

Silence. The engine ticked as it cooled. Somewhere on the bank a bird made a sound like a rusty hinge complaining about the morning.

"The Cut," Sefina said finally. "I ran part of it once, years ago, before the fleet blacklisted me. It's passable at this water level. Barely. There's a log jam at the second bend that might have moved and might have grown."

"Might," I said. "The river's favorite word."

"And the fog?" Nell asked.

I'd been watching the fog the way you watch a card player. The gorge makes its own weather, and the last two mornings the mist had come off the water late and thick, burning off only when the sun got high enough to bully it. If we moved at first light — or better, before first light, in the gray hour when the river can't decide what it is — we'd have a screen. Not a wall. A screen. Enough to be a rumor instead of a fact.

"We don't just run it," I said. "We run it and we leave him something to look at."

They looked at me. Pita looked at me the way boys look at adults who've just said something interesting and possibly criminal.

"Decoys," I said. "We anchor lanterns in the Cut — three, four of them, spaced out, hung off snags at boat height. In fog, a lantern is a boat. Every pilot knows that. Rill's pilot sees lights moving in the side channel, he reports a boat in the Cut, and the barge turns to chase a rumor while we're already through and gone."

"That's not a plan," Tamu said. "That's a prayer with rigging."

"Most plans are," I said. "The good ones just admit it."

* * *

We went in the dark, because the dark was the only thing on the river that wasn't for sale.

I'll say this for my crew of strays: they complained exactly as much as was healthy and no more. Tamu took us off the bank at the gray hour with the engine at half and the exhaust baffled with a tarp he'd rigged in an hour of profanity, and the Kestrel slid into the Cut like a secret being told very quietly.

The Cut was everything the chart promised and less. The channel wandered between walls of pandanus and rotting bamboo, the water the color of steeped tea, the snags rising out of it like the fingers of drowned men asking for one more chance. I steered by instinct and by Sefina, who stood at my shoulder calling the channel from memory — "bear left past the fallen fig, the deep water hugs the near bank, trust the current not the chart" — and I trusted her, which for me is a full religious conversion performed at speed.

Pita and Nell went ahead in the dinghy with the lanterns. That was the part of the plan I liked least, sending two of my people into the fog ahead of the boat, but it was also the part that had to work. They hung the first lantern off a dead tree leaning over the water at the Cut's mouth, lit it, and let it hang there — a single warm point of light in the gray, exactly what a pilothouse window looks like when you're a quarter mile off and can't see straight.

The second lantern went on a snag mid-channel. The third they hung from a fishing platform an abandoned village had left behind, which felt like a small desecration and a small blessing at once. Four lights now, spaced the way a cautious boat spaces her anchor lights when she's stopped for the night in a channel too narrow to turn around in.

"Any pilot seeing that will think we're holed up waiting for daylight," Sefina murmured.

"That's the idea. Rill's pilot sees a boat stopped in the Cut, the barge comes up slow to board her, and by the time they figure out the boat is made of kerosene and spite, we're in the main channel and the fog's lifting behind us."

"Rill doesn't figure things out," Sefina said. "He confirms them. He'll board those lights, and when he finds nothing, he'll know exactly what it means. But knowing takes him an hour, and an hour is a country mile on this river."

She was right, and I hated how right she was, because it meant the trick would work exactly once and would cost us exactly the thing I was spending it: Rill's certainty. A man like Rill forgives everything except being made to look foolish in front of his own crew. He'd come for us after that not because it was his job but because it was his pride, and pride is a fuel that never runs out.

The log jam at the second bend had grown.

It came at us out of the fog like a barricade from some older war — whole trees, root balls the size of rooms, woven together by flood debris into a wall that the current hit and moaned against. The channel, according to Sefina's memory, threaded through it on the left where a gap had been. The gap was now the size of a coffin. Maybe two coffins, if you stacked them.

"Kestrel's beam is—" Tamu started.

"I know her beam," I said. "I've apologized to it my whole life."

We backed water and looked at it, and the fog looked back at us, and somewhere downstream — too far to see, close enough to imagine — a dark barge was keeping an appointment with our lanterns.

"There," Pita said.

He was pointing at the right side, where I'd already looked and seen nothing but wall. But Pita's eyes were Pita's eyes, and after a moment I saw what he saw: a seam in the debris, dark water moving through it, maybe a hand's width of grace on either side of what we needed. A gap that a boat goes through once, if the boat is lucky, and if the boat is not lucky, the gap is where the boat ends and the story of the boat begins.

"That's not a channel," Tamu said. "That's a rumor."

"So was the Kestrel, once," I said. "Take us through."

He did. I won't pretend I was calm. I'll tell you instead what I remember: the sound of the current through the jam, which was like breathing; the smell of wet wood and rot; Sefina's hand on my shoulder steering me as much as I steered the wheel; Nell's face at the rail, lit by nothing, watching the gap come on with her father's eyes and none of her father's fear, because she hadn't had time to inherit that yet; and the scrape — one long, terrible, fingernail-down-the-world scrape along the starboard side as the Kestrel went through the coffin-gap with inches to spare on the water and less than that in my chest.

Then open water. Then the bend. Then the Cut giving us back to the main river like a mother handing over a child she's decided not to drown after all.

Behind us, in the fog we'd left, four lanterns burned over four pieces of nothing, and a dark barge was coming to call on them.

* * *

We cleared the narrows at mid-morning with the fog burning off around us in rags, and I won't lie to you: when the Kestrel came out into the open channel below the gorge, running clean, unshot, unbroke except for a scar on her starboard rail she'd wear like a dueling mark, I laughed. Out loud. A real laugh, from the floor of me, the first one since my brother died and left me this boat and its debts.

Tamu looked at me like I'd finally gone properly round the bend, and Pita laughed too, because fourteen-year-olds will follow a laugh anywhere, and even Sefina smiled, which on Sefina is roughly the equivalent of a parade.

"We did it," Nell said. She said it flatly, without joy, and the laugh went out of me the way a match goes out in wind.

"We did it," I agreed.

"They'll find the lanterns."

"They'll find the lanterns."

"And then?"

I looked downstream, where the river ran wide and brown and innocent, carrying its secrets the way it carries everything — without opinion, without mercy, without ever once offering to take anything back. Somewhere behind us, Deacon Rill was standing on the deck of a salvage barge, looking at four lanterns hung on nothing, and doing the arithmetic that men like him do. Not the arithmetic of where we'd gone. He'd have that inside an hour; the Cut's exit was the Cut's exit, and Rill had probably stood on its banks before I was born. The arithmetic he'd be doing was the other kind. The kind where you stop chasing and start waiting. The kind where you realize you don't have to catch a boat on the river at all, because every river goes somewhere, and every somewhere has a dock, and every dock has a night when the lights are low and the crew is tired.

"He knows exactly where we're headed now," I said. "He knew before. But now he knows we know he knows, and that changes the shape of it. A chase, you can outrun. A man who stops chasing—" I didn't finish it, because there wasn't a good ending to the sentence, and I've lived long enough to know that unfinished sentences are the river's native language.

Nell went below to her father's letters. Sefina went forward to check the dive gear, touching each piece the way you touch a rosary. Tamu went to his engine room, where I suspect he did arithmetic of his own — the kind involving fuel and hours — and told no one the answer, which is Tamu's way of loving us.

I stood at the wheel and drove us upriver toward a wreck, a fortune that wasn't a fortune, and a truth that had already killed once and would not object to killing again.

We'd beaten the barge. I want that on the record. We'd beaten it fair, with fog and kerosene and a boy's eyes and an old woman's memory, and for one hour on the Fly River we had been the clever ones.

But the fog was burning off, and the river ran on ahead of us, straight and visible and full of nowhere to hide, and somewhere in my chest, under the triumph like a stone under moss, sat the cold and patient knowledge that Deacon Rill had not lost.

He had merely noted the time.
