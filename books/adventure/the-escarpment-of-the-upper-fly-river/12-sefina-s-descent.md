## 12. Sefina's Descent

I have been diving since I was nine years old, when my uncle lowered me into the harbor at Port Moresby with a rope around my waist and told me to bring up whatever my hands found. He said the sea was honest. He said it never lied to you, only showed you what was there and let you decide whether you could live with knowing.

My uncle was a liar about most things, but not that one. The water is honest. That's exactly the problem with it.

* * *

The gorge swallowed the Kestrel's sound before we even dropped anchor. Walls of black basalt rose on either side, close enough some mornings to spit across, and the river between them ran green — not the brown-green of the lowland Fly, all silt and commerce, but a deep bottle-green, cold, lit from above like light through church glass. Nothing grew on those walls except a stubborn moss that made every handhold treacherous. Birds didn't sing in there. They just watched.

"Cheerful place," Pita said, leaning over the rail.

"It's a drowned cathedral," I told him. "Show some respect."

Tamu had anchored us in an eddy behind a rock shelf, out of the main current, and Mara stood at the bow with Elias Vane's wax-cloth map held flat against her knee, checking landmarks against the drawing like a woman reading a letter from someone she wasn't sure she trusted. Which was fair. The map was drawn by a dying man with a guilty conscience, and guilty men draw beautiful maps because they want you to follow them.

But the landmarks matched. The split rock shaped like a broken tooth. The waterfall thread down the eastern wall. And there, where the map promised, the riverbed shelved away into darkness at forty feet, then sixty, then into a blackness that my depth gauge would eventually call ninety-one feet.

The Meridian lay in that blackness, upright, whole, and waiting.

* * *

I'll say this for myself: I am not a nervous diver. Eleven years commercial work, two years of it for Corrigan Salvage before I learned what their surveys were actually for, and I once spent six hours on a bottom in the Torres Strait with a torn glove and a shark that kept circling closer each pass, and I came up bored. Fear is a tool. You hold it by the handle or it cuts you.

But standing on the Kestrel's deck in that green gloom, kitting up, I felt the handle slipping a little.

It wasn't the depth. It was the quiet. Commercial wrecks are noisy places — fish, current, the rattle of your own gear against growth and rust. This water was silent as a held breath. And the Meridian hadn't been salvaged, hadn't been stripped, hadn't even been looted by locals, which after a hundred years is its own kind of wrong. Wrecks get picked clean. This one had been left alone the way people leave alone a house where something bad happened.

"You don't have to go first," Nell said. She'd appointed herself tender, which meant she'd be handling my line and my air counts, and which also meant she wanted, more than anything, to be useful to this errand without having to feel it yet.

"I do have to go first," I said. "That's the job. You don't send the client down before you've checked the stairs."

Mara came aft. She looked at me the way she looks at everything — like she's pricing it, and like the price might break her heart. "Sefina. Whatever's down there, you come back up. The ledger can wait another tide if it has to. You can't."

"I know how long I can stay," I said. "I wrote the tables Tamu uses."

She almost smiled. "Then go find out what my promise actually bought."

* * *

The first ten feet were the worst, the way the first ten feet always are — the moment the green closes over your head and the world above turns to wavering silver and everything you are becomes the size of your lungs.

Below that, it got better and worse at the same time.

Better, because the visibility opened up. Cold water holds no silt. At thirty feet I could see the whole gorge floor spread beneath me like a landscape under snow — boulders, a fallen tree bleached white as bone, and then the hull.

Worse, because of what the hull looked like.

She was a side-paddle steamer, colonial-built, maybe a hundred and eighty feet stem to stern, and she sat upright on the bottom with her decks mostly intact, her paddle boxes collapsed inward like folded wings. A hundred years in cold fresh water and she hadn't gone to rust and ruin the way salt wrecks do. Fresh water preserves. Her paint still clung in patches — I could see the ghost of a name board near the bow, letters I couldn't read yet through the faint silt of a century, but the shape of them was there. Her wheelhouse windows were empty holes. Her railings stood. Somewhere below her main deck, a strongroom door waited with a lock that a brass key in Mara's cabin was supposedly cut to open.

And she was full of cases.

They were stacked in the cargo hold through a collapsed section of deck planking, wooden crates maybe two feet square, strapped in iron, and the straps had gone to lace but the wood had barely darkened. I hooked one with my knife tip and turned it over. Stenciled on the top, still legible: MERIDIAN GOLD CONCESSION — ASSAY BULLION — and a number. There were dozens of them. Maybe hundreds. Enough gold to buy the whole Fly River valley twice and bribe the government that sold it.

I felt nothing looking at it. That surprised me. I'd expected my heart to hammer — every diver dreams of treasure, we're liars if we say otherwise. But gold at the bottom of a river where nobody has laughed in a hundred years doesn't look like wealth. It looks like ballast. It looked like the heaviest thing in the world, sitting in the lightest place, and I understood suddenly why Elias Vane had carried his map for fifty years instead of coming back for it himself.

Some cargoes you don't want to raise. Some cargoes raise themselves, and then you're the one carrying them.

* * *

I swam the length of the main deck, running my light along the structures, cataloguing for the survey Mara wanted — dive points, hazards, the state of the hull plating, whether the strongroom could be reached from inside or only through the collapsed cargo section. Textbook work. I'd done it a hundred times on a hundred wrecks, and my hands moved through it automatically while my mind stayed pleasantly, professionally numb.

Then I reached the area aft of the cargo hold, near the strongroom bulkhead, and the numbness ended.

Bones.

Not scattered. Not the random drift of remains that current and time produce on every old wreck, the sad arithmetic of the sea. These were arranged. Laid out in rows on the deck plating, shoulder to shoulder, maybe twenty of them, aligned with the ship's keel like passengers who had lain down together. Some still wore the buttons of their clothing, brass buttons gone green. Near the row's head, set upright on the deck facing them, was a single chair — a wooden chair, bolted to the deck, the kind that belongs at a captain's table — and on the chair sat a skeleton wearing what remained of an officer's coat, positioned so that whoever put him there made sure he faced the ones on the floor.

I hung in the water above it and my light shook and I let it shake.

Somebody had done this. In the minutes or hours after the Meridian went down — while she settled, while survivors, if there were survivors, breathed the last air in her — somebody had arranged the dead. Had laid them out. Had seated one man in judgment over the rest, or in witness, or in shame. It was deliberate. It was careful. It was the most human thing I have ever seen underwater, and I have seen divers drown and I have seen ships take crews down with them, and none of it was ever this.

You arrange bodies when you're saying something. When there's no one left alive to say it to.

I thought about the ledger. About names. About a young deckhand named Elias Vane who had somehow not been aboard when she sank, or had gotten off, or had been put off — and about the word that had been chasing him across fifty years and one continent and finally caught him gut-shot on a mudbank, handing his guilt to a stranger with a boat.

I checked my gauge. Fourteen minutes. Bottom time on this dive, with the depth, gave me twenty-five total. I should have turned back. Every rule I've lived by for eleven years said turn back.

I didn't turn back.

* * *

The strongroom door was on the lower deck, aft, and it was shut. Iron, riveted, a lock the size of my fist, and around its frame — this is the part I will carry until I die — there were marks.

Scratch marks. Inside the reach of the door, scored into the iron from the inside, four parallel gouges repeated over and over, hundreds of times, in patterns that started deep and controlled and became shallower and wider and finally just desperate smears. Someone had been locked in that room — locked in with the gold, in the dark, in the cold — and had clawed at the door until their fingers gave out, and then, judging by the lower marks, until their boot heels gave out too.

The room was sealed. The scratches went nowhere. Whoever made them died in there, in the richest little room in New Guinea, listening to the river settle over them.

I put my gloved hand flat against the door, over the marks, the way you'd touch a grave marker. The iron was cold through both gloves. I said something. I don't know what — bubbles carry words away, and maybe that's why divers talk underwater, because it's the only place where what you say stays private even from yourself.

Then my gauge read nineteen minutes and my training took my body back up the line while the rest of me stayed down there in the dark with them.

* * *

I broke the surface doing the thing you're not supposed to do, which is gasping, and Nell hauled me to the ladder with her fists full of my harness, and Mara was there, and Tamu, and Pita with his eyes enormous, and I let them pull me aboard because my arms had decided the day was over without consulting me.

I sat on the deck plates with the sun on my neck and couldn't stop shivering, though the water temperature hadn't touched me and the air was warm. Nell wrapped a blanket around my shoulders. Mara crouched in front of me.

"The hull?" she asked. "Can we work her?"

"She's intact," I said. My voice came out strange, flattened. "Hull's sound. Cargo hold's accessible through the collapsed section. The cases are there. All of them." I heard myself pause and hated the pause, because I knew what was coming next and some cowardly part of me wanted to keep it one more minute.

"And?"

"And the strongroom's reachable from the lower deck passage. Door's shut. Your key might fit." I pulled the blanket tighter. "There are bodies on the deck. Twenty or so. Laid out in rows. Deliberately. Somebody arranged them after she sank — there was a chair, an officer in it, facing them. And the strongroom door—" I stopped. Started again. "There are scratch marks on the inside of the door, Mara. Deep ones. Hundreds of them. Someone was locked in there alive."

Nobody spoke. Downriver a bird called once, the only sound the gorge had allowed all morning, and even it sounded apologetic.

Nell sat down slowly on the hatch coaming. She was holding my slate, reading my notes upside down, and her face had gone the color of the water. "My father," she said. "He knew. He knew they were in there."

"He knew," I said. Because there was no kind version of it. "He was paid to know."

Pita turned away and gripped the rail. Tamu took off his cap, which from Tamu is a funeral.

Mara stayed crouched in front of me. She didn't touch me — Mara doesn't touch people unless they ask — but she stayed there, eye level, steady as a mooring post.

"Sefina," she said. "Is the ledger down there? Is it real?"

"It's real," I said. "Behind that door. With the gold. With—" I gestured vaguely at the water, at everything. "Whatever else he kept in there with them."

She nodded slowly, and I watched her do the arithmetic I'd already done at ninety-one feet: that we hadn't come for treasure at all, that we'd known it somewhere underneath, and now the underneath was on the deck of her boat in daylight, dripping, undeniable.

"We go back down," she said. "Tonight, on slack water. We get the ledger and we get whatever we can of—" her voice caught, just slightly, "—of theirs. Effects. Buttons. Anything families could hold. We treat that deck like a cemetery, because that's what it is."

"Aye," Tamu said quietly.

"And Sefina." Mara's eyes came up to mine. "You don't have to make the second dive. Nobody on this boat thinks less—"

"I'm making the second dive," I said. "Those people have been down there a hundred years waiting for somebody to come back for them. I'm not letting Rill's divers be the next faces they see."

* * *

We spent the afternoon preparing, and I'll record here, for honesty's sake, that my hands shook through all of it — rigging the lift bags, staging the spare bottles, marking the shot line. Not fear-shaking. Something adjacent. The shaking you do when your body has seen a thing your mind hasn't finished admitting.

At dusk, Mara gathered us in the wheelhouse and unrolled the map one more time, and that was when Pita, who had the middle watch and the sharpest eyes aboard, said, very quietly:

"Lights. Downriver. Running dark, but I counted three engine wakes."

Mara went to the glass. We all watched her watch the dark water, and I saw her shoulders settle into the particular stillness I'd learned meant trouble being priced.

"Rill," she said. "He's not following us anymore. He's ahead of us. He knows where the wreck is, and he means to be there when we arrive."

Tamu grunted. "Then tonight we dive fast."

"No," Mara said, and turned from the window, and there was something in her face I hadn't seen before — not the wariness, not the grief. Resolve, banked like a fire in a stove, giving heat without light. "Tonight we dive slow. We do it right. Let him wait in the dark a while." She looked at each of us in turn. "He's had a hundred years of other people waiting on him. It's his turn."

Outside, the first stars came out over the gorge walls, and below them, ninety-one feet down, the Meridian kept her dead and her ledger and her scratched door, patient as only the drowned are patient, waiting to find out which of us the river had decided to keep.
