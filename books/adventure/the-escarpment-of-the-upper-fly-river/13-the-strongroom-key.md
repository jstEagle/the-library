## 13. The Strongroom Key

The Meridian sat in her green cathedral like a thing the river had decided to keep.

I'd been down twice myself that morning, once to check Sefina's lines and once because I couldn't stay on deck pretending I wasn't curious. The gorge swallowed sound. Forty feet down, my own breathing through the regulator was the loudest thing in the world, and the wreck loomed out of the murk with her masts still standing, draped in silt-fine weed, her wheelhouse windows gone to black holes. She wasn't a ruin. Ruins fall down. She was a held breath.

Sefina had marked the strongroom hatch for us — a small iron door set low in the superstructure, aft of the engine casing, its brass fittings still bright where the current kept them scoured. She'd also told us what she'd seen near it, in the flat voice she used when she didn't trust herself to use any other: bones arranged in a row outside the door, deliberate as fence posts. Men who had died waiting to be let in, or been made to wait there first. She wouldn't say which. Neither would I, out loud.

So we went down in pairs, Nell and me, while Tamu held the Kestrel against the current above us and Pita watched the downstream reach with the glasses, looking for Corrigan's barge. The water was cold enough to ache through the suit, cold enough to make you believe you were somewhere you shouldn't be.

The hatch lock was a brass cylinder the size of my fist, crusted green but sound. Elias's key fit it the way a key fits a lock it was cut for, which is to say perfectly, which is to say the dead man had done his homework. I felt Nell beside me go very still when the mechanism turned over — one heavy clunk I heard through my own skull more than my ears — and then the door swung inward on hinges that hadn't rusted because they'd been greased, deliberately, before she went down. Somebody had planned for this door to open again someday.

Inside, the strongroom was dark as a closed eye. My torch beam found shelving, found canvas-wrapped shapes stacked with military neatness, found a desk bolted to the floor with a drawer half pulled out, as though someone had been interrupted mid-theft a hundred years ago and the river had simply frozen the moment.

Nell went to the shelf first. I went to the desk.

The wrapped shapes were gold. There was no mistaking it even through canvas and slime — the weight of the bars when I lifted one, the dull imperial yellow where I scraped the wrapping back. Case after case after case, maybe two hundred pounds of it, more, stamped with assay marks from a smelter that hadn't operated since before the war. Enough money to buy the Kestrel ten times over. Enough to buy every boat on the river, and the registries they were registered in, and the men who signed the permits.

I looked at it for a long moment and felt nothing you could spend.

Because the desk drawer was what mattered. It came out stiffly, groaning on runners no one had oiled, and inside it lay a ledger bound in grey sailcloth, swollen with water but intact, its pages fused into a solid block that would take days of patient steaming and drying to open without destroying. And on top of it, loose, a single sheet in a waterproof oilskin sleeve — a covering letter, the ink faded but legible, written in a clerk's careful hand:

*Manifest of disbursements, S.S. Meridian, final voyage. To be delivered to H. Corrigan Esq., upon recovery. Contents not for publication.*

*Not for publication.* A hundred years old and already somebody had known exactly what this room contained and exactly how much trouble it would be if it got out. The letter was signed at the bottom by the ship's purser, and beneath his signature, in a different hand — heavier, blunter, a hand used to signing things that made things happen — the initials *H.C.* and a date eleven days before the Meridian sailed.

Henry Corrigan. Grandfather to the man whose salvage barges were crawling up this river right now, running lights dark.

"Nell." My voice sounded strange in my own ears through the mask. I tapped her shoulder and pointed.

She turned from the gold cases and saw the ledger, and I watched something move across her face behind the mask plate — I couldn't tell you what it was, grief or vindication or just the sheer weight of finally holding the thing her father had died face-down on a mudbank rather than name out loud. She took the oilskin letter from me and read it, her torch steady on the page, and when she looked up at me she nodded once. That was all. Some conversations don't need air.

We bagged the ledger and the letter in the wax-cloth Elias had left folded under his map — the man had thought of everything, which should have told me sooner what kind of life he'd led — and we left the gold where it sat. All of it. I want to be clear about that, because later, when people asked, some of them never quite believed it: we left two hundred pounds of gold sitting in an unlocked strongroom forty feet down in the Fly River gorge, and we swam away from it.

Not because I'm noble. Because I'd finally understood what the stuff was.

It wasn't treasure. It had never been treasure. It was hush money — literally, physically, the actual payment, sealed in a strongroom with the receipt for the crime it bought. Elias had carried the knowledge of it for fifty years and it had eaten him alive; he'd told me as much with his last breaths, the *thing that should have hung me*. The gold wasn't the prize at the bottom of this river. The gold was the leash. Take it, and you were Corrigan's — owned, hunted, bought, same as every man whose name was going to be in those pages once we got them open. The Meridian hadn't sunk with a fortune aboard. She'd sunk with a bribe aboard, and bribes only work if you accept them.

Elias had refused, in the end. Fifty years late, but he'd refused. Dying broke on a sandbar rather than touch it. And now it was our turn to be offered.

We surfaced into grey afternoon light and Pita hauling us over the gunwale, and Tamu already had the kettle going, which is Tamu's way of saying he knows when something has gone wrong or right and either way tea is the answer. We spread the wax-cloth bundle on the chart table in the cabin, and Nell began the work of opening the ledger — bowls of fresh water, gentle heat from the stove pipe, peeling pages apart one at a time like leaves off a wet book of scripture, which is more or less what it was.

It took until well past dark. None of us slept anyway.

The first pages were ordinary — cargo manifests, crew lists, stores accounts. The Meridian had been a working steamer, licensed to carry passengers and freight upriver, and her paperwork was boring right up until the eleventh of the month, when the entries stopped being about copra and started being about people.

There was a payment schedule. That's the only way I can describe it. Names down the left-hand column — thirty-one names, thirty-one men — amounts in the right, and against each name a date and a place, and against each place a number. Not a cargo lot number. A village. I won't write here what the numbers stood for; the ledger does that itself, in the flat clerical shorthand of men who knew exactly what they were ordering and wanted deniability in the record. But every entry balanced. Every man paid. Every payment initialled *H.C.*, and at the foot of the final page, a longer signature, flourished like a man proud of his work:

*Approved — Henry Corrigan, Corrigan River & Salvage Co., for services rendered and discretion assured.*

Services rendered. Thirty-one names. Villages along two hundred miles of the Middle Fly that to this day are smaller than they should be, or aren't there at all, and everyone always said disease, migration, the war, anything, everything except the truth sitting in a strongroom under forty feet of green water.

And there at the bottom of the list, young, barely more than a boy's uncertain hand among all the confident ones, one signature that made Nell put her palm flat on the table and breathe for a while before she could keep reading:

*E. Vane, deckhand — received in full.*

He'd signed it. Of course he'd signed it. He was sixteen years old and the alternative was probably joining the row of men outside the strongroom door. They'd made him sign so he'd be complicit, so the paper would own him forever, and it had — fifty years, a whole life, right up until he'd spent his last strength handing a stranger a map and a key and a promise to carry.

Nell read every name aloud. All thirty-one, slowly, while we sat around the chart table with cold tea, and it was the strangest funeral I've ever attended and the strangest christening too, because those names had been locked in the dark for a century and now they were in the lamplight and in our keeping, and there was no putting them back.

Tamu spoke last, as usual. He'd been turning the covering letter over and over in his big careful hands, and he set it down and said, "The grandson doesn't know yet whether we have this."

"He suspects," I said. "Rill suspects. Suspecting and knowing are different animals."

"And when the grandson knows," Tamu said, "he will not send Rill to bargain. Bargaining is finished. He will send Rill to bury — the ledger, the boat, and everyone who has read it." He looked around the table, at Pita who was seventeen and trying to look like he hadn't understood, at Sefina who had understood completely, at Nell with her father's guilt drying in the lamplight. "So we decide tonight what this paper is worth and who it belongs to. Because come morning, someone else decides for us."

Outside, the gorge had gone quiet in the way it does before weather, the current dropping a note, the birds shutting up all at once. And far downstream, past the bend, past the dark, I'd have sworn I could feel it — engines, patient and polite, coming upriver.

* * *

We argued until the lamp burned low, and I'll give the crew this: nobody said sell it.

Sefina wanted the courts, or the newspapers, or both — sunlight, lawyers, the whole machinery, because she'd spent years watching that machinery get bought and she figured this much evidence might actually be too heavy to buy. Pita wanted to give it to the descendants' council upstream, quietly, the way debts are settled between families, and he said it with such simple certainty that none of us laughed, because he was righter than any of us wanted to admit. Nell said nothing for a long time. Then she said her father's debt was incurred on this river, against these people, and it would be paid here first or it wouldn't mean anything — and that whatever we did, we did it fast, because the gold downstairs was a message as much as a fortune.

"It's bait," she said. "Corrigan doesn't know what we've taken. He thinks we came for the gold. Every hour we sit here, he's calculating what we'd sell it back for." She looked at me. "How long do we have?"

"Tides," I said. "Two, maybe three, before the permits lapse and this stretch reverts to the customary owners. After that, Corrigan can't log the wreck legally — but he can still sink us, and the ledger with us, and claim the river did it."

"So we don't be here when he arrives," Sefina said.

But the river had other arithmetic. Because it was Pita on watch who came down the companionway at first light, white as bilge foam, and said, "Smoke. Downstream. Two barges and a launch, running the channel markers like they own them."

They did own them. That was rather the point.

I went out on deck and looked at the dust-smudge on the horizon and did the sums I'd been avoiding. We couldn't outrun them loaded and low. We couldn't hide in a gorge with two exits. And somewhere between here and the council villages upstream, Deacon Rill was going to offer me a price for what lay wrapped in wax-cloth on my chart table, and I already knew it would be generous, and I already knew that generosity was the most dangerous thing on this river.

"Get us underway," I told Tamu. "Slow. Let them see us running."

"Why slow?" Pita asked.

"Because a boat that runs scared gets chased," I said. "And a boat that's waiting gets met. I want to choose which one we are before he's close enough to board."

Behind us, forty feet down, the strongroom door stood open on its greased hinges, empty of everything but gold — and I understood, finally, why Elias had smiled when he gave me the key. He hadn't handed me a treasure. He'd handed me a bill, a hundred years overdue, addressed to the House of Corrigan.

And the collector was already coming upriver to see it paid.
