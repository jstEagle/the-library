## 14. Bargaining with Ghosts

They laid the ledger on the chart table like a body at a wake.

That's not a figure of speech I use lightly. I've sat enough wakes since my father died — first the fake one, the one where nobody knew he was dead, and then the real one, twenty years late, in a room above a Port Moresby chandlery where three people showed up and two of them were creditors. You learn what grief looks like when it's been left out too long. It looks like a book bound in rot-stained canvas, swollen fat with river water, its corners furred green, sitting under the Kestrel's swaying lamp while five people who barely know each other decide what it's worth.

Mara had spread a tarp over the table first. She's careful that way — careful with everything except herself, which is a thing I'd noticed about her by then and filed away without understanding. Tamu sat closest to the engine-room hatch, one ear cocked to his machinery even now, listening to it breathe. Sefina perched on the ammo crate with her feet up on a coil of rope, arms wrapped around her shins, still wringing river out of her hair. Pita lay along the settee pretending to sleep, which is how he listens to everything. And Mara stood at the head of the table with her hands flat on the tarp on either side of the ledger, the way you'd stand over something that might bite.

I stood apart. That felt right. It was my father's ghost on that table, after all. The rest of them were just helping me carry him.

"So," Mara said. "Nobody touches it until we've said our piece. Nobody decides anything tonight. But we decide before Kiunga, because once we're in radio range of Kiunga, every choice gets harder and more expensive." She looked around the lamp-lit circle. "Newspaper. Ransom. Or the council upstream. Those are the doors. Somebody talk me out of each one."

* * *

Sefina went first, because Sefina always goes first — she says it's because she spent six years waiting for other divers to finish their checks and learned that hesitation underwater is just drowning with better manners.

"Newspaper," she said. "Port Moresby Gazette, or whatever's left of the papers that aren't owned by somebody Corrigan plays cards with. We sell them the story. They print it big, names and dates, and for about nine days everyone in the country is furious, and then the mining companies buy ad space, and the editor gets a promotion to somewhere with no news section, and the story dies in a drawer." She shrugged. "I've seen surveys die that way. Beautiful work. Forty pages. Gone."

"You could sell copies," Pita said from the settee, eyes still shut. "Not the original. Copies. Then it doesn't matter if they bury it."

"Then it doesn't matter if they believe it either," Sefina said. "A photocopy is a rumor. A rumor against Corrigan Salvage is worth exactly what they'll pay you for it, which is nothing, or worse than nothing, which is a visit from Deacon Rill."

The name landed the way it always landed now — a small cold draft through the cabin. We'd all heard the foghorn downstream. We all knew what was coming upriver behind us.

"Ransom, then," Tamu said. He said it slowly, the way he does everything, like each word has to be winched aboard. "You take the ledger to Corrigan himself. You say: this names your father and thirty-one others. You say: pay us, and it goes into the sea off the barrier reef, and no court ever sees it."

"And he pays," Pita said, "and then he kills us anyway, because that's cheaper than trusting us."

"He might not," Tamu said.

"He sent men to burn a village," I said.

Everyone looked at me. I hadn't meant to speak yet. My voice came out flatter than I intended, the way voices do when they've been rehearsed in your head for hours without your permission.

"I've read the letters," I said. "All of them. Twice. So let me tell you what's in this book before we pretend it's money." I put my hand on the canvas cover. It was cold and damp and it felt less like paper every time I touched it, more like skin. "It's not a list of payments. That's what my father thought it was, maybe. A ledger of hush money — who took what, who signed. But it's also a passenger manifest. The Meridian carried forty-one people upriver in 1909. Deckhands, a customs officer, two missionaries, a family named Okuk with a baby. The gold in the strongroom was payment for making sure none of them reached the coast to testify about what happened at Wabag Bend. And the last page—" I stopped. My throat did something inconvenient. "The last page is a receipt. Signed by Corrigan's father. For forty-one souls, delivered."

The lamp swung. Shadows moved across all their faces and none of them spoke, and I understood suddenly that they were letting me have the silence, and that this — this small courtesy from strangers — was the first thing since Elias Vane died that had made me want to cry.

"So when we talk about ransom," I said, "understand what we'd be selling. Not gold. Forty-one people, twice. Once in 1909 and once next Tuesday."

* * *

Pita sat up then, all at once, the way teenagers do, as if his body had been waiting for permission.

"The gold's real though," he said. "There's cases of it. Enough to fix the boat, pay the debts, never cargo-run again." He looked embarrassed immediately, but he didn't take it back, which I was starting to understand was the bravest thing about him. "I'm not saying sell the ledger. I'm saying — someone should say the number out loud. Someone always knows the number. If nobody says it, it wins anyway."

"That's the wisest thing anyone's said tonight," Mara said quietly, "and I hate it."

She pulled the chart closer, the gorge chart with the wreck site marked in grease pencil, and stared at it like it might argue back.

"Here's my piece," she said. "I'm the one holding the debts. This boat is mortgaged down to her rivets, and the man who holds the paper is friendly with the man whose father signed that receipt. If we go to the newspaper or the council and Corrigan doesn't fall, I lose the Kestrel inside a season. Maybe sooner. Ransom solves that by Tuesday." She held up a hand before anyone could speak. "And ransom is a lie I'd be telling with my whole chest, because there's no version where Deacon Rill lets us sail away knowing what we know. So ransom isn't a door. It's a wall painted like a door. I just wanted us all to see me admit it."

Tamu nodded slowly, as if she'd passed some inspection only he conducted.

"Which leaves the council," Sefina said. "The descendants' council upstream. Customary law." She said the words carefully, testing them, a woman who'd grown up three islands away being polite about other people's traditions. "You give the ledger to the families of the people who were killed. And then what? They can't sue. Half of them can't read English. The courts won't touch a 1909 massacre — statute of limitations, standing, jurisdiction, pick your fence. You hand them a book and a debt they have no way to collect."

"They collect it the old way," Pita said.

Everyone turned to him again. He was standing now, and there was something in his face I hadn't seen before — not the runaway's watchfulness but something older, inherited, like he was speaking words that had been handed to him along with a carved token from an elder in a burned village.

"The river remembers who owes whom," he said. "That's not a saying. That's a system. If the ledger goes to the council, then Corrigan owes forty-one families, openly, in front of every river town between here and the delta. He can buy a judge. He can't buy a debt that everybody can see. He'll spend the rest of his life paying it — in land, in permits, in shame, in whatever the council decides. And every year at the reconciliation ceremonies, someone will stand up and read his name out loud, and there is nothing his lawyers can do about it."

"That's not justice," Sefina said. "That's a siege."

"It's both," Tamu said. "Best kind."

* * *

I walked to the porthole then, because I needed to look at the river while I said the thing I'd actually come in here to say, and looking at people while saying it would have undone me.

The Fly at night is a black ribbon with a few stars caught in it. Somewhere downriver, past the dark, Corrigan's barge was crawling toward us with its lights out, patient as a crocodile. Somewhere upriver, at a bend my father had loved enough to mark on a wax-cloth map, there was a grave with no marker that I'd never seen and would now never dig. And somewhere in between was a village of ashes where an elder had pressed a carving into a boy's hands and told him the river keeps accounts.

"My father spent twenty years paying for a crime he didn't commit," I said to the glass. "Did you know that? He didn't kill anyone on the Meridian. He was nineteen. He was a deckhand who saw it happen and took the money and ran, and then he couldn't spend a shilling of it, because every coin was a person. So he drank, and he prospected, and he wrote letters he never sent, and he kept a key on a string around his neck for two decades like it was a rosary." I turned around. "He tried to pay his debt alone, in secret, for twenty years, and it ate him alive. That's what guilt does when you privatize it. It doesn't shrink. It compounds."

Mara was watching me with an expression I couldn't read — or could, and didn't want to, because it was recognition.

"So no," I said. "We don't sell it to a newspaper, because newspapers make grief into content and then wrap fish in it. And we don't ransom it, because ransom is just my father's mistake with better arithmetic — taking the money and calling it settled. We give it to the council. Upstream. Where the Meridian was going when it was sunk. Where the families still live. Where the debt was incurred." I swallowed. "My father's debt has to be paid where it was incurred. Not in a courtroom. Not in a newsprint. There. In front of the people he helped rob. Even if it costs us everything. Especially then."

The cabin was quiet except for the engine and the river slapping the hull, and for a moment I thought I'd broken something — the mood, the plan, the fragile alliance of strays — and then Sefina unfolded herself off the ammo crate, crossed the cabin, and put her hand on the back of my neck the way you'd steady a diver before descent.

"All right," she said. "Council. I'm in. But hear my condition, Nell Vane. We make copies first. Notarized, witnessed, sealed — Tamu knows a magistrate at Kiunga who owes him. Because customary law is strong, but it's stronger when there's paper behind it, and I have watched too many good people get told their memory isn't evidence." Her grip tightened, gentle and iron. "Your father's truth goes upstream AND into a vault. Both doors. Agreed?"

"Agreed," I said, and my voice cracked on it, and nobody pretended not to notice, and nobody minded.

"One problem," Tamu said, and pointed at the chart with his pipe stem. "Council meets at Wabag Bend. That's two days upstream. Permits lapse in four. Corrigan's barge is behind us, running dark, and Rill knows where we're going." He tapped the map twice. "Whatever we choose, we choose fast, and we row uphill doing it."

Mara straightened up off the table and rolled the chart closed, and when she turned to face us she had the look of a woman who has just stopped bargaining with ghosts and started giving orders to the living.

"Then we don't wait for morning," she said. "Tamu, give me everything the engine has and then some. Sefina, break out the floodlights — we'll run the narrows by night if we have to. Pita, get forward and clear the anchor chain." She was already moving, already rolling up the ledger in its oilcloth, already becoming the captain the river apparently required. "Nell — you're with me. Your father found this river's worst secret. Least we can do is outrun it."

I grabbed the oilcloth's trailing edge and followed her up into the dark, and behind us the ledger lay rolled tight as a wound, and ahead of us the gorge waited with its black water running the wrong way, uphill, against the clock, against the tide, against everything — carrying us and Corrigan's barge alike toward a bend in the river where forty-one ghosts had been waiting since 1909 for somebody, anybody, to bring the account home.
