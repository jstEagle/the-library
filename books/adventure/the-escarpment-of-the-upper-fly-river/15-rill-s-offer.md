## 15. Rill's Offer

I have been called many things in my working life. Enforcer. Fixer. The Corrigan Fleet's bad conscience, though that last one flatters me — a conscience implies I feel something about the work, and mostly what I feel is the weather.

But the word I like least is *thug*, because a thug is a man who breaks things before he has to. Breaking things is expensive. Breaking things leaves witnesses, and witnesses are the one commodity this river produces in inexhaustible supply and infinite inconvenience. No. What I am is an accountant of a particular kind. I count what a problem costs to solve, and then I solve it for less than that figure, and if I do my arithmetic properly nobody ever learns there was a problem at all.

The Kestrel was becoming an expensive line item.

* * *

I had her mapped by the third day out of Kiunga. Not the boat — boats are simple, steel and rot and stubbornness in roughly equal measure — but the people. That's where problems live. Old Tamu in the engine room, who'd found my listening device within six hours of me planting it, which told me he was either very good or had done this dance before. Probably both. The diver, Sefina, blacklisted from our own rolls for having the temerity to write down what she actually saw on a survey — Corrigan senior had hated that woman with a passion I never fully understood, since she'd only cost him money, and money was the one thing he forgave. The boy, Pita, all elbows and fear, worth nothing as a threat and everything as a leak. You can always find a boy like that. You can always buy a boy like that. I'd made a note to try.

And Nell Vane. Elias Vane's daughter, aboard a boat running hard upriver toward a wreck her father had spent thirty years not talking about. That detail alone told me most of what I needed. Elias hadn't sent for her. Elias had died and left a map, and she'd come hunting anyway, which meant she wasn't bought yet either. Everyone starts unbought. It's one of the small sadnesses of the trade.

Mara Ishikawa herself was the variable I couldn't price. I'd read her file. Brother dead, debts inherited along with the boat, a reputation for running cargo through water other captains wouldn't cross at gunpoint. On paper she was exactly the kind of captain you buy: leveraged, isolated, hungry. But files don't tell you how a person stands when they lie to you, and Ishikawa had stood on her own deck with my hand extended and lied to me with such flat, unbothered courtesy that I'd walked back down my gangplank knowing two things. One: she had the map. Two: she would not sell it cheaply, and possibly not at all.

Possibly not at all. There it was. The phrase that ruins budgets.

So I did what I always do when the numbers stop adding up. I went to see the problem in person, and I brought an offer instead of men, because offers are cheaper than funerals and I say that as a man who has attended more of both than he'd like entered into evidence.

* * *

The narrows above Wawoi Falls bend are the kind of geography God designs when He wants to make a point about humility. The gorge pinches down until the whole Fly runs through a throat barely wide enough for two hulls abreast, cliffs going up sheer and dripping on either side, and the current in the middle moves like it's late for something. Any captain coming upstream has to take that stretch slow, hugging the slack water on the eastern wall, and any captain coming downstream has to commit to the channel and pray.

Which means a barge anchored mid-channel with her engines idling owns the river for half a mile in either direction. I'd chosen the spot the way you choose anything: carefully, and without apology.

We came around the bend at first light, my launch riding low with six of my steadiest men below decks and visible nowhere, and I watched the Kestrel come up against us and have no choice but to fall off her heading and drift. Good seamanship, actually — Ishikawa killed her way and let the current carry her rather than fighting for room she didn't have. She came alongside my barge with maybe four feet of green water between the hulls, and she stood at her rail with her arms folded like a woman waiting for a delivery she intended to refuse.

"Captain Ishikawa." I tipped my hat. It's a small thing, the hat, but courtesies are load-bearing. "You're a long way from your run."

"You're a long way from anywhere anyone wants you, Mr. Rill."

There it was again. That flatness. Most people, facing me, perform something — bravado, or terror, or the elaborate boredom of the very frightened. She performed nothing. She looked at me the way Tamu looks at a seized bearing: assessing the damage, already pricing the repair.

"I'll be brief," I said. "You've had a long week and I've had a long life, and neither of us benefits from theater."

"I doubt you know how to be brief."

"I know how to be paid," I said. "That's briefer."

* * *

Here is what I offered her, and I want it recorded that it was a generous offer, because generosity is also load-bearing:

Triple the appraised value of whatever gold lay in the Meridian's strongroom. Clean papers for every soul aboard — Sefina's blacklist struck, the boy's warrants dissolved, the Kestrel's debt ledger closed and stamped. And passage. Safe conduct downriver, past my barges, past my men, past the whole machinery of the Corrigan Salvage Fleet, with my personal word attached, which on this river is worth more than the company's signature and everyone knows it.

"And in exchange," Ishikawa said, when I'd finished, "you want the map, the wreck, and everything in it. And we forget we ever heard the word Meridian."

"Forget is a strong word. I'd settle for discretion."

"Discretion." She said it like she was tasting something gone off. "My father—" she stopped, corrected herself, and I noted the correction, because grief that corrects itself mid-sentence is grief being managed, and managed grief means there's someone alive she's doing it for. "Elias Vane. You bought him too. Didn't you."

It wasn't a question, so I didn't insult her by treating it as one. "Thirty years ago," I said. "He was a deckhand. Nineteen years old, standing watch when he shouldn't have been, and he saw what happened at the Meridian's rails, and afterward a man very much like me sat across from him and made him an offer very much like this one." I spread my hands. "He took it. He lived a long time on that money and hated every year of it, from what I understand. But he took it. Men usually do."

"And you're telling me this because?"

"Because I'm honest about the work, Captain. It's the only professional advantage I have over the company. Corrigan will tell you the Meridian is a salvage opportunity. The lawyers will tell you it's a permit question. I'm telling you it's a grave, and the man who dug it pays well to keep it undisturbed, and you can stand on principle until the permits lapse and the gorge reverts to the customary owners and my replacement — who will not be me, and will not be polite — comes up this river with fewer scruples and more rifles. Or you can take the money, clear your debts, free your crew, and go back to hauling cargo, which is, I'm given to understand, the thing you actually love."

I watched her take that in. I want to be clear that I meant it as kindness. It is a strange kind of kindness, mine, but it is real. I have watched idealists die on this river for forty years and they die the same way every time: certain, upright, and full of holes, while the men who took the deal grow old and fat and haunted in comfortable chairs. I know which chair I'd rather haunt from. I assumed she'd do the arithmetic eventually. Everyone does the arithmetic eventually.

"You said triple," she said.

"Triple. Appraised by an independent assessor in Port Moresby, funds released on delivery of the map and my verification that the strongroom's contents come to me unopened."

"Unopened." Something moved behind her eyes — not temptation. I'd expected temptation and prepared for it, had the figures arranged in ascending order like a staircase built specifically for her to climb. This was something else. "Rill. Do you know what's in that strongroom? Because I do. And it isn't gold."

"Gold is what I was sent to recover."

"That's not what I asked."

"No," I said. "It isn't."

For a moment — and I record this because it matters, because it's the moment the budget broke — neither of us spoke, and the river filled the silence, that deep gorge-hum the Fly makes when the walls press close, like the water itself is thinking.

"There were people in that hold," Mara Ishikawa said quietly. "When she went down. Some of them were still alive when she settled. My diver found the marks inside the hull. And the men who put them there signed their names in a book, and your employer's father signed first, and you're offering me money to keep that book shut."

"I'm offering you money to give it to me. What I do with it after is company business."

"The same business your father's generation conducted at those rails."

"My father drove a taxi in Rabaul," I said, "and died sober and poor, and I have no generation, Captain. I have an employer and a set of instructions. It's a cleaner arrangement than most marriages."

"But here's my problem with your offer." She unfolded her arms, and I confess my hand drifted toward my jacket, old habit, though we both knew six of my men below decks changed nothing against a woman with a river at her back. "Elias took your deal. Thirty years of silence, and it ate him alive from the inside, and the last thing he did on this earth was hand a stranger a map and ask her to undo it. So I've seen what your clean papers buy, Mr. Rill. They bought Elias Vane three decades of waking up at night. I've read his letters. I know exactly what your price does to a man."

"And I've seen what refusing it does," I said. "It makes widows."

"Yes," she said. "It does. Sometimes it makes them anyway."

* * *

I gave her one last chance, because I am thorough, and thoroughness is the whole of my religion.

"The crew," I said. "Think about the crew. The old man is already hurt — yes, we know about the engine room, we know about the fuel lines, don't look surprised, I told you I'm honest about the work. The boy is a child. The diver has spent ten years paying for one refusal. You can end all of it this morning. Triple the gold. Papers for everyone. Nobody bleeds. That is the offer, Captain, and it expires when your hull clears this narrows going down."

She looked at me for a long moment, and I want to describe accurately what I saw, because I've thought about it since. It wasn't hatred. Hatred I can work with; hatred is just heat, and heat bends. It was pity. She pitied me, standing there with my hat and my figures and my six quiet men, and the pity was worse than a drawn gun, because you can't counter-offer against it.

"Elias Vane took your deal," she said, "and it killed him slowly. You're offering me the same bargain and calling it mercy. So here's my answer, and I'll make it plain so you don't have to wonder on the trip home." She leaned on the rail, close enough that I could see the salt lines on her collar and the exhaustion underneath her eyes and the absolute, infuriating calm beneath both. "No. Not triple. Not ten times. Not with papers, not with gold, not if you threw in the whole fleet. There is a book in a strongroom at the bottom of this gorge with names in it, and those names belong to the people whose parents those men drowned, and I am going to put that book in their hands if it costs me this boat, this river, and every tide I have left. That's the offer I'm making you, Mr. Rill. Take it or leave it."

I took my hat off entirely then. Held it. Put it back on. It seemed owed.

"You understand," I said, "that I now have to take it from your corpse."

"I'd be disappointed if you didn't. You seem like a man who finishes his paperwork."

"I do my best." I stepped back from the rail. Below decks, my six men waited on a signal I would not give, not here, not in a narrows where a scuttled hull could block the channel and a witness could be anyone on either cliff. Besides — and this is the part I have never said aloud to anyone — some part of me, the part that keeps accounts even of itself, wanted her to have the chance to be wrong on open water, fairly, with the current involved. The river should get a vote. The river always gets a vote. "For what it's worth, Captain, I hoped you'd take it. It would have been easier for everyone, and easier is the only currency I actually believe in."

"Easier," she said, "is what Elias bought. Look how he died."

"Gut-shot on a mudbank," I said, because I'd read the report too, "alone."

"Not alone," she said. "He died on my deck, among my crew, with his daughter coming to meet him. You should check your sources, Mr. Rill. Your reports are out of date."

And she rang her engine bell, and the Kestrel swung her bow upstream into the throat of the narrows, and I stood at my rail and watched the smallest, poorest, most stubborn boat on the Upper Fly drive straight into the worst water in the gorge rather than spend one more minute alongside me.

* * *

My men surfaced from below decks and gathered, waiting. Boyer, my second, who has been with me eleven years and has never once asked me a question I couldn't answer with a number, looked at the wake the Kestrel left and then at me.

"She's mad," he said.

"She's priced," I said. "Everything's priced. Hers is just denominated differently." I took out my notebook and did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since Kiunga: six of us against five of them, but their five included a diver who could hold her breath longer than my best man could hold his nerve, an engineer who knew every trick that hull had, and a captain who had just demonstrated, in front of witnesses, that she could not be bought at any figure I was authorized to offer. Which meant the remaining method was the old method. The expensive one. The one that leaves witnesses.

Unless there were no witnesses left to leave.

"Boyer," I said. "Signal the barge. We move tonight, before they reach the wreck. And send word downstream — I want the village at the second bend looked at. Anyone who saw us come up this river, anyone who sold them fuel, anyone who waved." I closed the notebook. "Cleanly, Boyer. As cleanly as it can be done."

Boyer nodded and went aft, and I stood alone at the rail watching the gap in the cliffs where the Kestrel had disappeared, and I will admit to you, whoever you are, keeping these pages, that for perhaps ten seconds I envied her. Ten seconds. Then the account balanced, as accounts do, and I went below to plan a dawn I did not intend either of us to enjoy.

The trouble with being an accountant of problems, you see, is that sooner or later you meet one that won't stay solved — and Mara Ishikawa, I was beginning to understand, was not a problem at all.

She was a debt. And the river, as the old woman's carving had said, remembers who owes whom.
