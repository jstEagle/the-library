## 16. Fire on the Water

I have been on boats my whole short life and I can tell you this: nobody trusts a boy at the wheel. They'll trust a boy to scrub bilge, to haul line until his palms bleed, to sit up all night watching for logs — but put him behind the helm and every grown man aboard suddenly remembers a hundred reasons why he shouldn't. Too young. Too green. Too much like his father, whoever that was.

So when Mara Ishikawa said, "Pita, you're on the wheel tonight," I thought she'd finally lost her mind to the river. Then she said, "Not because I trust you. Because everyone else is doing something harder." Which was honest, at least. Honesty is the Kestrel's whole religion. That and never running out of diesel.

It was the last night before the narrows, and nobody slept anyway. We could smell Rill coming the way you smell rain on hot stone — not seeing it yet, but knowing it was already falling somewhere upstream of you. Tamu had the engine room lit up like an altar, oiling everything that moved and some things that didn't. Sefina sat on the foredeck sharpening a diving knife she had no business using above water. Nell read her father's letters again, though by then she knew them by heart, mouthing the words like prayers in a language she was still learning. And Mara stood at the bow with her arms folded, talking to the river the way she does, quiet, like they were old friends settling a debt between them.

Me, I held the wheel and watched the black water slide past and tried to remember everything Sefina ever taught me about current.

"Read the boils," she'd told me a dozen times, pointing at the little domes of water that rise where the bottom drops away. "The river tells you where it's deep and where it's lying. Boils mean depth. Smooth glass means rocks under a skin of calm. Trust the ugly water, boy. The pretty water is what kills you."

I used to think she meant rivers. By that night I understood she meant people too. Deacon Rill was the prettiest water I ever saw — soft-spoken, patient, always wiping his hands on a white cloth like the world was something he was cleaning off them. Ugly water everywhere else. Trust the ugly water.

* * *

They came at dawn, because of course they did. Dawn is when the fog lies thickest on the gorge and the light comes sideways and gold, so beautiful you almost don't mind being murdered in it.

I heard the engines before I saw anything — two of them, low and rough, idling down to a whisper the way men do when they want to hear your engine instead. I rang the bell twice, which was our signal for company, and the Kestrel woke up around me like a body remembering it has bones.

Mara came aft at a walk, not a run. She never runs. "How many?"

"Two engines. Maybe three boats. Port side, upstream."

She looked at the fog, then at the water boiling over the shallows ahead, then at me. "You know the channel through the Teeth?"

"The Teeth" is what Tamu called the rapids below the narrows — a half-mile of water that came apart into six channels around drowned rock, only one of which would let a boat the Kestrel's size through without peeling her hull open like fruit. I'd run it once, in daylight, with Tamu standing behind me telling me which boil to trust. In fog, alone, with bullets coming? No. I didn't know it. Nobody knew it. You didn't know the Teeth; you survived them or you didn't.

"I know how to read it," I said, which was as close to yes as honesty allowed.

"That'll do," Mara said, and went forward to fight.

* * *

Here is what I saw of the battle, from the one place on the boat where you see nothing: the wheelhouse.

The first shot took out the starboard lamp. Glass rained across the deck and Nell shouted something and fired back with the rifle she'd carried aboard like a grudge, and the fog swallowed both sounds. The second shot hit the wheelhouse frame a hand's width from my ear and I learned something about myself, which is that I scream in a register usually reserved for river birds. Fine. Noted. Keep screaming and steer.

"Tamu!" Mara's voice, forward. "Give me everything!"

"She's got nothing left to give!" Tamu's voice from below, and then the engine roared anyway, coughing and climbing, the old man wringing revolutions out of her like blood from a stone. The Kestrel surged and I felt her answer the wheel the way she answered nothing else — sullen, faithful, alive.

The lead boat came out of the fog on our port quarter, long and low, four men in it, one of them standing with a rifle braced against his shoulder. Sefina rose out of nowhere beside him — I mean that literally, she'd been flat on the foredeck behind the cargo netting — and threw something that arced and flashed and burst into smoke, and the rifleman's shot went wide and high and took a piece out of the gorge wall instead of out of us.

"Hard to starboard!" Mara bellowed. "Through the gap! Pita — the gap!"

Which gap? There were six gaps and five of them were coffins. But there was no time to ask, and asking was cowardice dressed up as caution, so I did the thing Sefina taught me. I stopped looking at the water I was afraid of and looked for the ugliest channel I could find — the one where the current came apart into chaos, boils bursting everywhere, foam standing up in ragged columns. Pretty water kills you. Trust the ugly water.

I put the wheel hard over and drove us straight into the mess.

* * *

Running rapids in fog while men shoot at you is not an experience I recommend, but since you're reading this, I'll describe it. The world becomes noise. Water hits the hull like fists, like crowds, like the whole river deciding at once that it hates you personally. The wheel fights you — not resists, *fights*, shoving back with opinions of its own, and you wrestle it the way you'd wrestle a frightened animal, not to beat it but to stay in the conversation. Spray comes over the top and blinds you and you steer by the feel of the hull in the current, by the sound of the water under you, by faith and stubbornness and whatever Sefina poured into you during those long afternoons when I thought she was just keeping me busy.

Behind us, the first pursuit boat made its choice. They took the pretty channel — smooth, inviting, a clean lane through the rock — and I heard the crash before I understood it, a grinding boom like the river clearing its throat, and their engine died mid-scream. The pretty water. It killed them exactly like she said it would. I felt sick about it even then, even with bullets still coming, because those were men, and the river doesn't care, and I was starting to understand that neither did the war we were in.

The second boat was smarter. They hung back at the head of the rapids, pacing us, shooting whenever the fog thinned. A round went through the wheelhouse window and took the compass off its bracket. Another hit the deckhouse and I heard Nell yell, not hurt — furious — and return fire, and this time somebody on the other boat screamed too.

Then the third thing happened, the thing I will carry longer than any scar.

The engine died.

Not slowed. Died. One moment the Kestrel was driving through the worst water on the Fly, and the next she was silent, and silence in rapids is death with good manners. We began to swing broadside, and broadside in the Teeth means the river picks you up and shows you to the rocks like a parent showing a child something instructive.

"TAMU!" Mara's voice cracked on it, and I heard her boots hammering aft along the deck, and I heard Sefina say, low and calm, "Pita. Wheel. Now more than ever," and I understood that I was the only one who could keep the hull pointed downstream, engine or no engine, and that steering a dead boat through rapids is like praying — it changes nothing except everything.

So I steered. I read the boils and I hauled the wheel and I talked to the boat under my breath, *come on, girl, come on, this way, not that way, trust me*, and she yawed and staggered and kept her nose mostly downstream, and the rocks slid past close enough to count the weed streaming off them, and I did not die, and the boat did not die, and below my feet I heard Tamu's voice saying something I couldn't make out, and then tools, and then a sound I knew from a hundred mornings — the starter catching, failing, catching —

The engine caught. Roared. Died. Caught again and held, ragged as a drunk but holding, and the Kestrel leapt like a dog slipping a rope.

We came out the bottom of the Teeth into long brown water and none of us said anything for a full minute. The second boat turned back upstream. Whatever Rill had paid them, the Teeth charged more.

Then Mara came up the wheelhouse ladder and her face was gray and wet and wrong.

"Pita," she said. "Tamu's hit."

* * *

He'd taken a round through the engine room door when the shooting started — stepped into it, Sefina said later, stepping between the doorway and the fuel lines, because a bullet in the tank would have ended all of us and Tamu had done the arithmetic faster than the bullet could. It caught him high in the chest, and he'd plugged the hole with a rag and finished the repair one-handed, bleeding into the bilge, getting the engine running before he let himself fall.

Nell worked on him on the deckhouse floor, and she was steady, steadier than I've ever seen anyone, tearing cloth and packing wounds and counting his pulse out loud like a rosary. Sefina held his hand and told him about dives he'd never see, and Mara stood over all of them with her hat in her hands like a woman in church, and I stayed at the wheel because that was my post and because if I left it I was going to cry where the crew could see, and a helmsman doesn't cry where the crew can see. That's rule one. I just made it up. All the rules are ones I make up now, apparently, because everybody who made rules before is lying on the floor.

Tamu opened his eyes near noon. He looked at me first.

"Boy," he rasped. "You held her true through the Teeth with no engine?"

"Yes, sir."

"Hm." His eyes closed. "River's going to be insufferable about that."

Nell laughed and sobbed in the same breath. Sefina pressed her forehead to his knuckles. Mara put her hat back on, and when she turned to me her eyes were wet and she pretended they weren't, and I pretended not to notice, and that's how family works, I think. Everybody pretending not to notice the love so it can keep moving cargo.

"He'll live," Nell said finally. "If the wound stays clean and he rests. He cannot hold another watch for days."

"We don't have days," Mara said quietly. "We have hours. Permits lapse tomorrow's tide."

"Then you need an engineer."

"I need a miracle with grease on its hands."

And that's when Tamu, half-conscious, bleeding, smiled like a man who'd been waiting all voyage to say it: "You've got one. He held her true through the Teeth." His eyes found mine. "Engine room's yours, boy. Try not to sink us."

I looked at Mara. Mara looked at me. Somewhere upstream, a floatplane engine droned through the fog, coming fast, and I knew — we all knew — that Corrigan himself was coming to finish what Rill started, and that the last tide was already turning beneath us.

"Show me the fuel lines," I told Tamu, and went below.
