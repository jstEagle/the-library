## 17. The Last Tide Begins

The floatplane came in low over the gorge at midmorning, silver and wrong, like a heron that had learned to hate.

I was on the Kestrel's roof with a mug of tea gone cold, watching it circle once, twice, before it settled onto the water below the wreck site with the arrogance of something that had never been told no. The spray it threw up caught the light. Somewhere behind me, Pita swore with real feeling.

"That's not Rill's boat," he said.

"No," I said. "It isn't."

Rill was a knife you could see coming if you were lucky. This — this was the hand that held the knife, arriving to collect whatever the blade had failed to bring home. Corrigan Salvage had a lead barge anchored downstream, running dark for two days now, patient as a spider. And now the man himself had come up from the coast in his own aircraft, because permits didn't wait, and neither did men who'd spent forty years making sure the past stayed sunk.

"Hours left," Tamu said from the hatchway. He was moving better than he had any right to, bandaged under his shirt where the bullet had gone through him in the rapids, refusing to sit down, refusing to be an invalid, refusing everything except work. "Permits lapse at the next slack water after sunset. After that, the gorge belongs to the council."

"And Corrigan knows it," I said. "Which is why he's here."

* * *

We watched through Sefina's glasses as the floatplane taxied to the shingle beach below the old mission landing, where the Meridian's masts still broke the surface at low tide like the fingers of a drowned man reaching for a riverbank that never came. Corrigan stepped out onto the floats himself. I'd expected a flunky. I don't know why. Men like Corrigan come personally when the stakes are personal.

He was smaller than I'd imagined. That's always how it is with them. You build a monster out of rumor and burned villages and a dead prospector's dying words, and then the actual man steps off a floatplane in a linen shirt and a sun hat, and he's somebody's grandfather, neat and pink and unhurried, and the horror of it is that he looks like he's never done anything worse than cheat at cards.

He wasn't alone. Six men fanned out along the beach, and one of them carried a surveyor's transit case, and another carried a strongbox that took two of them to lift, and Deacon Rill stood at Corrigan's shoulder like a shadow that had learned to stand upright.

"He's brought a surveyor," Nell said beside me. She'd been quiet all morning, working through her father's letters again, folding and unfolding them like a woman trying to fold a map back into its original creases. "A licensed one. With witnesses."

"To log the wreck legally," I said. "Before the permits die."

"And the strongbox?"

I'd been asking myself the same question. A strongbox that heavy, carried ashore by two men, on a day when the whole point was paperwork — there was only one thing Corrigan would bring that weighed like that. Kerosene. Or thermite. Or simply gold, new-minted, to buy the last witnesses who hadn't yet been bought.

"Either way," I said, "he means to leave this gorge owning both versions of the story. His, in the registry books. And nothing else anywhere."

* * *

We held a war council in the wheelhouse, which is a grand name for a room the size of a confessional, smelling of diesel and Tamu's liniment.

Sefina laid the chart out on the chart table and weighted its corners with mugs. "Tide turns at fourteen hundred," she said. "Falls hard through the narrows until eighteen hundred. Slack around nineteen. Then it floods back up until midnight, and the next slack after that is—" she tapped the paper, "—the moment the permits expire. Sunset plus slack. Corrigan will file his survey at exactly that window. It's tidy. It's legal. It's how he does everything."

"So we beat him to it," Pita said. "Dive tonight, take the ledger out, and—"

"And do what?" Nell asked. Not hostile. Just tired, and honest. "Carry it past six armed men on a beach? We've got a wounded engineer, a boy, a diver who nearly drowned two nights ago, and me." She looked at me. "And you. What's the plan, Captain? Swim it out?"

The truth was I didn't have a plan yet. What I had was worse than a plan. I had clarity, the kind that comes when every door but one has already closed and you can finally stop pretending you have options.

"Corrigan thinks he's racing a clock," I said. "He thinks the ledger is in that strongroom, and that if he files first and dives second, everything inside becomes his property the moment the ink dries. Legal salvage. Clean title." I looked around the wheelhouse. "But the ledger isn't in the strongroom anymore. Sefina moved it."

Sefina went very still. So did I, because I'd just decided, in front of all of them, to spend the secret we'd been keeping since the night dive — the night Sefina jammed that hatch and nearly drowned doing it, the night we came up with the ledger wrapped in oilcloth against her chest while Rill's divers hammered on steel above her.

"I moved it," Sefina said quietly. "After the collapse. I didn't say anything because—" she glanced at Pita, at Tamu, "—because the fewer people who knew, the fewer people Rill could make talk."

"Where?" Nell asked.

"The old mission cistern," Sefina said. "On the bluff. Dry stone, sealed, above any flood line. The victims' effects are with it. Everything."

Tamu let out a breath that sounded like a valve releasing. "Then Corrigan's diving for an empty room."

"Corrigan's diving for an empty room," I agreed, "and filing papers on a wreck that won't belong to him by morning. But that's not enough. Understand that. If we just sit here, he logs the Meridian today, and tomorrow his lawyers argue that everything recovered before the lapse — including, somehow, a ledger nobody can produce — is company property. He doesn't need the ledger itself. He needs the *title*. Title lets him bury the truth under forty years of litigation while the people who are owed grow old waiting."

"So what do we do?" Pita asked.

I looked at the chart. At the gorge, the narrows, the falling tide, the mission bluff with its dry cistern full of dead men's names. And then I understood what the river had been trying to tell me since Elias Vane died on my deck with a map in his fist: you cannot outrun a rich man's lawyers, and you cannot outfight his guns, and you cannot hide from his patience. But a river moves everything eventually. The trick is not to hold the evidence. The trick is to make it *move* — tonight, on the falling tide, out of the gorge entirely, into hands that were never part of Corrigan's arithmetic.

"We move it tonight," I said. "Not us. Them." I put my finger on the chart, on the string of villages upstream — the council villages, the descendants' villages, the ones whose elders had given Pita the carved token that said the river remembers who owes whom. "The falling tide runs down. But the overland track from the mission bluff to Taviri village is four hours' walk, downhill most of the way. Pita knows it. The elder who gave him the token walks it twice a year."

"They'll be watched," Tamu said. "Rill's men watch the tracks."

"Some of them," I said. "Tonight, while Corrigan's divers are busy opening a strongroom that's already empty, and while his surveyor is measuring masts by lantern light — tonight, every eye he owns will be on this gorge. Because he believes the ledger is still down there. That's the one gift we've got left: he's spending his attention on the wrong room."

Nell was staring at me. "You want to give it to the council. Tonight. Before the permits even lapse."

"I want it *gone*," I said. "Out of the gorge, out of my boat, out of my hands, into theirs. Once it's in the council's keeping, it stops being salvage and starts being testimony. No court in the coast gives Corrigan title to testimony. And no amount of money un-rings a bell rung in front of forty witnesses."

Sefina smiled for the first time in days. It was a small smile, tired, and it made her look younger and harder at once. "You're going to make him dive for nothing," she said, "and then make him watch the truth walk uphill on legs he can't buy."

"That's the plan," I said. "Such as it is."

"It's a terrible plan," Tamu said. "It relies on a boy, a track, and the tide."

"Yes," I said. "It does."

Pita stood up straighter than I'd ever seen him stand. "I know the track," he said. "Every crossing. Every fallen tree. I can be at Taviri before midnight."

"You'll take the token," I said. "And you'll take company. Nell goes with you — the letter, too. If the council hears it from Elias Vane's own daughter, it isn't a stranger's story anymore. It's a debt being paid by his blood."

Nell looked at me for a long moment. I thought she'd argue. She'd earned the right to argue; she'd read her father's confession more times than any of us, and she knew better than anyone what it cost to carry a dead man's errand. But she just folded the letters along their worn creases and put them inside her shirt, against her skin, the way you carry things you intend to keep.

"Midnight," she said. "We'll be there by midnight."

* * *

We spent the afternoon being boring, which is the hardest work there is.

That's the other half of the trick, and it's the half that kills you. While Pita cached rope and dried fish and a waterproof satchel in the longboat, and while Nell sat with the elder's token in her lap learning its story from Tamu, the rest of us performed normalcy for the benefit of whatever glasses were turned our way from the beach below. Tamu clanked in the engine room loud enough to wake the dead. Sefina mended nets on the roof in plain view. I ran the Kestrel's dinghy back and forth to the wreck site with sounding lines, taking depths like a captain with nothing on her mind but survey numbers, close enough to Corrigan's beach party to wave.

Rill waved back.

That's the thing about Deacon Rill. He waved back. Standing on the shingle in his neat dark clothes with the sun hammering down, he lifted one hand, unhurried, almost friendly, and held it there a beat too long, and I understood the message perfectly: *I know you're doing something. I don't know what. Enjoy the interval.*

Corrigan didn't wave. He stood at the waterline with his surveyor, pointing at the Meridian's masts, and once — only once — he turned and looked straight at the Kestrel across the half mile of green water, and even at that distance I felt it like a hand flat against my sternum. Not anger. Appraisal. A man pricing a boat, or a problem, or a grave.

At sixteen hundred the tide began to fall, and the gorge started to speak in its evening voice, water sliding off the shoals, the wreck's masts rising inch by inch out of the current like something being slowly returned.

* * *

They left at dusk.

I put them ashore myself at the mission bluff, running the dinghy in quiet on the ebb, and I won't pretend my hands were steady on the oars. Nell climbed out first, letters against her heart, and stood a moment looking back at me across the water. In the last light she looked so much like her father it hurt — same set to the jaw, same way of standing as if the ground owed her an argument.

"Mara," she said. "If we don't—"

"You will," I said. "And if you don't, then the ledger stays in a dry hole in a rock, and Corrigan owns a wreck full of gold he can't spend and a lie he can't keep, and we start again. There's no version of this where he wins clean. That's the whole point of what your father left us."

She nodded once, and then she did something I didn't expect. She reached out and gripped my forearm, hard, the way sailors grip — not affection, exactly. Recognition. One person holding on to another in weather.

"Pita," I said. "Track rules."

"Downhill fast, crossings slow," he recited. "Never cross on a rising sound. Trust the dog, not the path." He grinned at me in the gloom, and for once the grin didn't look like a boy playing at being crew. It looked like a man accepting a job.

I watched them go up the bluff trail until the dark took them, Sefina beside me in the stern, and then I shipped the oars and let the ebb carry the dinghy back down toward the Kestrel's riding light, small and stubborn on all that black water.

"Now," Sefina said, "we keep their eyes."

"Now," I agreed, "we keep their eyes."

Because that was the last piece of it, the piece I'd saved for myself: every minute Corrigan's divers spent wrestling the strongroom hatch, every lantern his surveyor lit over the masts, was a minute the track stayed empty of watchers. Our job tonight was to be worth watching. To run lights, make noise, lower a decoy crate on a line over the wreck site — to be, in short, exactly the kind of busy, guilty, treasure-drunk fools Corrigan had been expecting since Kiunga.

Back aboard, I broke out the good lanterns, the ones I'd been saving for a night I couldn't imagine, and hung them along the Kestrel's rail like festival lights.

"Captain," Tamu said from the engine room hatch, and there was something in his voice I hadn't heard before. Not fear. Arithmetic. "There's something you should know about the fuel margin."

"Not tonight, old man."

"Captain. Tonight is exactly when you should know."

I looked at him — at the bandage showing at his collar, at the grease to his elbows, at the face of a man who had been rationing a secret the way he'd been rationing fuel, and I felt the bottom drop out of the evening.

"How thin?" I said.

He wiped his hands slow, buying the words time. "Enough to reach the wreck site," he said. "Enough to fight the ebb out of the narrows tomorrow. Enough, if the river's kind, to make the council landing." He met my eyes. "Not enough to come back."

The lanterns swung gently at the rail. Downstream, Corrigan's floodlamps bloomed over the water, and somewhere in the dark above the bluff, two figures were walking uphill carrying the truth in a satchel, and the tide was falling, falling, taking the gorge apart hour by hour, and I understood that I had known this was coming — had felt it in the way Tamu measured everything twice and smiled at nothing — since the day we found the loosened fuel lines.

"All right," I said. My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "Then we'd best make sure we're going somewhere worth going."

Tamu almost smiled. "That," he said, "is the first sensible thing anyone's said on this boat in weeks."

Above the gorge, the first stars came out. Below, Corrigan's divers splashed into the black water, heading down toward a strongroom that had nothing left to give them but the dark. And on the falling tide, in the hills, the truth began to walk.
