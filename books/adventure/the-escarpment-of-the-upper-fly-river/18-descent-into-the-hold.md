## 18. Descent into the Hold

**Sefina**

There is a moment on every dive — usually about ten metres down, when the light goes from gold to green and your ears pop and the world above becomes a rumor — when I ask myself why I do this. And every time, the answer has been the same: because down there, nobody can lie to me. The water doesn't care what a survey says. The water shows you exactly what it shows you.

Tonight the water was going to show me a graveyard.

Mara laid out the plan on the chart table with the lamp turned low, and I'll give her this: she didn't dress it up as anything prettier than it was. "We go down on the falling tide," she said. "We take the ledger, we take what belongs to the dead, and we're back up before slack. If Rill's divers come early, Sefina buys us time."

"Buys you time how?" Nell asked.

"With my body, probably," I said, and Mara had the decency not to correct me.

The thing nobody tells you about being blacklisted from Corrigan's fleet for refusing to falsify a survey is that it doesn't make you brave. It just makes you unemployed and honest, which is a worse combination in most ports. But somewhere between then and now — between the burned village and Tamu bleeding in his engine room and Pita holding a helm like he'd been born to it — honest had started to look like something worth dying for. Not dying for, exactly. Let's say: worth drowning near.

I checked my gear twice, then a third time out of pure superstition. Old regulator, older torch, the knife Tamu had sharpened for me without being asked, because that's what Tamu did — he fixed things quietly and told no one, including himself, how much of himself went into the fixing.

Nell came down the companionway in a borrowed wetsuit two sizes too big, sleeves rolled, looking like a child playing at her father's trade, except her eyes were nothing like a child's.

"You don't have to come," I said.

"Yes, I do." She said it the way people say things they've decided so hard the deciding has already scarred over. "It's his mess. I'm the only one he left to clean it."

I could have argued. I've argued with better swimmers than Nell Vane. But there was something in her face that reminded me of me, twenty years ago, standing on a fleet barge while a supervisor explained that the numbers would say whatever the numbers needed to say. Some lessons you can't be talked out of. You have to dive into them.

"Stay on my left," I told her. "Touch my arm twice if you're out of air, three times if you're scared, four times if you see anything that used to be a person. Don't look at them longer than you have to. They were somebody's family once, and staring doesn't honor anybody."

"And if Rill's divers find us?"

I buckled my weight belt. "Then we improvise, and we pray the river likes improvisers."

* * *

**Nell**

The gorge at night is a different country. By day it's green stone and white water and birdsong that sounds almost cheerful. At night it's a throat. We slipped off the Kestrel's stern into water so black that my own hands disappeared the moment they left the surface, and the only world left was the circle of Sefina's torch and the sound of my own breathing, loud and stupid and human.

Down, down. The Meridian rose out of the dark the way ghosts do in stories — gradually, then all at once. First the mast stump, furred with sixty years of silt and weed. Then the funnel, lying on its side like a fallen tree. Then the hull itself, vast and patient, its plates furred green, portholes staring.

My father had been here. My father had stood on this deck or one like it, young and silent and paid, while people died inside this iron. I put my glove against the hull and felt nothing but cold, which felt right. Cold is what he deserved to feel from me. Cold is also, I'm learning, what forgiveness looks like before it thaws.

Sefina tapped my arm and pointed. The cargo hatch gaped amidships, a black mouth in a black face, and she went through it the way she does everything — without hesitation, as if the water had personally invited her.

Inside, the hold was a cathedral of ruin. Her beam swept across collapsed crates, a ship's bell furred white, the ribs of the deck above arcing overhead like the bones of some enormous animal that had lain down here to die and been patient about it. And then the beam caught the first of them.

Four times, I'd promised myself. Four taps means a person. But when the light found the shapes arranged against the bulkhead near the strongroom door — arranged, deliberately, side by side, hands folded by someone who still believed in decency even as the river came in — I tapped five times, six, seven, because counting had stopped meaning anything.

They weren't horror. That's what undid me. They were orderly. Somebody had laid them out in the last minutes, in rising water, in the dark, and made them neat for whoever came after. For us, maybe. Sixty years early, but for us.

Sefina's hand closed on my shoulder and squeezed once. Stay with me. I stayed.

* * *

**Sefina**

The strongroom door was brass-edged and stubborn, and Elias Vane's key — which Mara had pressed into my palm at the ladder like a woman handing over her firstborn — turned it on the second try. Of course it did. Dead men keep their appointments.

Inside: the cases. Gold, real gold, stacked in the corner like firewood, and I confess my heart did a small greedy dance before I saw what sat beside it. A ledger, oilcloth-wrapped, tied with tarred string. And around it, wrapped separately, small bundles — a wedding ring, a child's shoe, a locket, a folded cloth that might once have been a shirt. Effects. The dead man's inventory of the dead.

Nell took the ledger first. Even underwater, even in the dark, I watched her hold it against her chest the way you hold a thing that is heavier than gold, which is to say: the way you hold the truth about someone you loved.

That's when the light came.

Not ours. Ours was warm and steady. This was a cold blue blade sweeping through the main hatch behind us, moving the way fleet divers move — fast, efficient, arrogant. Rill hadn't waited for slack water. Rill hadn't waited for anything, because Rill had never once in his life waited for permission.

Two of them. Lead diver big as a door, second man hanging back at the hatch. They'd seen our bubbles or our light or simply guessed, because Rill always guesses right; that's his whole terrible talent.

I pulled Nell behind the strongroom door and put my mouth close to her ear. "Out the cargo hatch. Take the bundles. Go now, slow, no bubbles."

"What about you?"

"I'm going to close a door."

She understood one heartbeat later, and I watched her decide not to argue, which is the bravest argument a person can lose. Then she was gone, a shadow among shadows, and I turned to the mechanism of my bad idea.

Here is the thing about jamming a hatch on a wreck: it's easy. Here is the thing about unjamming it: it isn't. I knew this when I started. I want that written down somewhere. I knew exactly what I was doing, and I did it anyway, because the lead diver was already swimming for the strongroom and the ledger was already leaving in Nell's arms and arithmetic is arithmetic.

I wedged the crowbar Tamu kept in the dive kit across the hatch frame and behind the dogs, then hammered it home with the flat of my tank. The metal screamed — a long, ringing note that the whole wreck must have heard. The lead diver froze in the water, light swinging toward me, and for a moment we looked at each other across the hold like two men meeting in a church neither belonged in.

He came at me anyway. Of course he did. Fleet men always do; obedience is their whole religion.

We fought the way you fight underwater, which is badly — grabbing, rolling, burning air, all fury and no leverage. He got a hand on my regulator hose. I got the knife Tamu sharpened and put it against his throat, not cutting, just saying what I meant. His eyes changed. Behind the mask they went from soldier to person, and the person wanted to live, same as me.

Then the second diver, panicking at the hatch, did the stupidest possible thing: he fired the wrecking charge they'd brought to blow the strongroom open.

* * *

**Nell**

I heard it through the water more than through the air — a deep concussion, felt in the teeth, in the chest, in the old bones of the ship herself. Above me the Kestrel's running lights swung wildly as Mara shouted orders I couldn't hear. Below me, where Sefina was, the Meridian groaned like something waking up angry.

I was fifteen metres up with a ledger against my heart and a bag of the dead's belongings in my fist, and every instinct I owned said swim, swim, surface, breathe. Instead I hung in the black water like a coward on a string and looked back down at the glow of Sefina's torch, still moving, still alive, please still alive.

The wreck shifted. I felt it — a shudder through the whole hull, silt blooming up from below like smoke. Sixty years the river had held this ship gently, and tonight we had asked it to hold her through a fight, and the river was done being gentle.

The torchlight burst upward out of the cargo hatch trailing a storm of bubbles, and with it came Sefina, mask askew, one fin gone, swimming like the devil himself had hold of her ankle. I grabbed her harness and hauled, and together we clawed for the surface while behind us the Meridian settled another half-metre into the mud of her grave, and the strongroom hatch — jammed shut, crowbar fused into the frame by the blast — closed over the lead diver's prison like a book.

Whether he lived in there, sealed in an air pocket in the dark, I didn't know then. I know the river will decide, and I know the river keeps better accounts than any court.

* * *

**Sefina**

They pulled me over the gunwale and I lay on the deck coughing up half the Fly River while Mara knelt over me asking questions I answered in the wrong order. Nell had the ledger. Nell had the bundles. The wreck was settling but standing. The lead diver was entombed in his employer's own strongroom, behind a door his own explosives had welded shut, and I had maybe killed him, or maybe saved him from drowning in the open river instead, and I genuinely could not tell you which of those sat worse in my chest.

Tamu, propped against the engine room door with his bandages soaking through, looked at me longest of anyone. He didn't ask what happened down there. He just nodded, once, the way one mechanic nods to another across a job well ruined.

"The hatch won't open again," I said finally. "Not without cutting gear and a week."

"Rill has cutting gear," Mara said quietly.

Yes. Rill has cutting gear. And Rill now knows precisely where the ledger is not — which is anywhere he can reach — and precisely who has it, which is everyone aboard this battered, debt-ridden, impossible boat.

I rolled onto my back and looked up at the stars wheeling over the gorge, and I thought about the shapes in the hold, laid out neat with their hands folded, waiting sixty years for someone to carry their names into the light. We had them now. All of them. Every name, every ring, every small shoe.

And downstream, running lights dark, coming up fast against the last of the tide, came the man who would burn the whole river rather than let those names be read aloud.

Mara stood at the rail with the ledger in her hands, and I heard her say, mostly to the water, "One tide left."

The river said nothing. It never does. It just carried us toward him.
