## 19. Corrigan's Reckoning

There is a particular silence that falls on a river at the end of a dry season's last tide — not peace, exactly, more like the water holding its breath before it decides what it's going to do with you. I'd grown up on that silence. I'd buried my brother in it. And now I stood on the deck of the *Meridian Star*, Corrigan's flagship barge, listening to it while forty tons of salvage machinery idled around me like a sleeping animal that hadn't yet been told whose throat to go for.

I had come aboard under a white rag tied to a boat hook, which was either the oldest trick in negotiation or the oldest joke, depending on how it ended.

Edmund Corrigan received me in his wheelhouse the way a man receives a bill he intends to dispute. He was smaller than his reputation — most men are — with the soft hands of someone who had spent thirty years paying other people to touch things. His uniform was pressed. His coffee smelled of real beans, flown in, no doubt, at prices that would have kept three villages in diesel for a year.

"Captain Ishikawa." He didn't offer a chair. "You've cost me a great deal of money this week."

"I've heard that about rivers generally," I said. "They're notorious."

Something flickered behind his eyes — the brief irritation of a man who has bought every room he's ever stood in and keeps encountering furniture that won't behave. "Rill tells me you have a document."

"Rill tells you a lot of things. Does he tell you about the villages?"

The flicker again, faster this time. "I don't know what you mean."

"No," I said. "You wouldn't. That's rather the point of you."

* * *

Let me back up, because the hour before that conversation deserves its own accounting, and because a climax without its plumbing is just noise.

We had moved the ledger off the *Meridian* at two in the morning, on the falling tide, in the green-black dark of the gorge where the walls came so close together the stars ran out of sky. Sefina went down one last time with Nell beside her on the line, and they brought up the strongroom's contents wrapped in canvas: the ledger itself, fat as a Bible and twice as damning; the gold cases, which we took mostly because leaving them for Corrigan felt like handing a knife back to the man who'd ordered the cutting; and a bundle of personal effects we'd cut free from the wreck's aft cabin — a woman's hairbrush, a child's shoe, a pocket watch stopped at twenty past four on some morning in 1909 when the river swallowed a ship full of witnesses.

The descendants' council had come down from the upper villages through the night, eleven elders in a pair of dugouts, paddling waters their grandmothers' grandmothers had fished before any permit or company existed. The elder who'd given Pita the carved token led them — old Waima, with a voice like gravel poured slow. They did not ask us for the ledger. That was the thing that undid me, quietly, standing watch in the fog. They simply waited for it, the way you wait for rain you already trust.

Nell carried it to them herself. She'd earned that, and she knew it, and so did Elias Vane wherever he was — probably complaining about the current.

But the council could not read English, not all of it, and the ledger was written in the careful clerk-hand of a colonial paymaster. So we needed witnesses who understood what they were witnessing. We needed Corrigan himself. And we needed him somewhere public enough that the river towns would see it done.

Which is how I came to be standing in Edmund Corrigan's wheelhouse at dawn, holding a dead prospector's promise in a canvas bag, with my crew arrayed behind me on his own deck like a jury that had rowed a long way to convene.

* * *

"You should understand your position," Corrigan said. He said it kindly, which was worse than if he'd said it with a gun. "Your permits are void by tomorrow's tide. Your engineer is in no condition to run those rapids — yes, I know about Tamu; Rill is thorough. Your diver is blacklisted, your deckhand is a runaway, and Miss Vane has no legal claim to anything her father touched. You are, Captain, a boatload of nobodies sitting on property my fleet holds paper for. I can make all of you rich, or I can make all of you disappear, and I confess I've grown tired of deciding which."

"That's funny," I said. "Because I've spent the whole week being told I'm nobody, and here I am in your wheelhouse drinking your coffee."

He looked at the cup in my hand as though noticing it for the first time. I set it down on his chart table, deliberately, next to a chart of the gorge marked up in his own hand — the *Meridian*'s position circled, dated, annotated with an estimated cargo value. He'd known where the wreck was for years. He'd been waiting out the permits same as us, except he'd planned to arrive with divers and dynamite instead of a promise.

"You're going to give me the ledger," he said, "or Rill will take it from your corpse. He told you that, I believe. He's a man of his word."

"He is. It's one of the few things I respect about him." I lifted the canvas bag. "So let's skip to the part where neither of us gets what we want."

I walked out onto his deck, and the morning came up over the escarpment behind us, and I opened the book.

* * *

Here is what I know about reading aloud: it is the oldest technology of justice there is. Older than courts, older than police, older than companies with fleets and enforcers. Before anyone wrote laws down, somebody stood in the middle of the village and said the names, and everybody listened, and the man who'd done the thing had to stand there and hear himself named. There is nowhere to hide inside your own name spoken out loud. I learned that at my brother's funeral, saying his name into the river until my voice broke, understanding finally that grief and testimony are the same act performed at different volumes.

The *Meridian Star*'s deck crew numbered thirty-one. Most of them were river men. Some were from the very villages Rill had burned. All of them had been hired the way Corrigan hired everyone — well enough paid, carefully kept ignorant, each man assured that whatever the rumors said, the paperwork was clean.

I stood at the rail with the ledger open against it, and I read.

"'Paid to J. Okoro, deckhand, ten pounds sterling, for services rendered the Meridian passage, April 1909.'" A pause. "'Paid to T. Warimbu, boatman, ten pounds.' Paid to H. Callaghan, mate, fifteen pounds, and a bonus of five for discretion. Paid to A. Corrigan, master and owner, sixty pounds, and the gratitude of the administration.'"

I read the names the way the paymaster had written them, in columns, in order, forty-one names down the first page alone. And then I read what the payments were for, because the paymaster had been a careful man and had entered it in the margin in pencil, thinking perhaps that pencil was private: *"Passengers removed per owner's instruction. No survivors logged. Cargo manifest amended."*

Forty-one names. Forty-one men paid between five and sixty pounds to help drown a shipload of witnesses — witnesses to what, the ledger didn't say directly, but the second section did, the requisitions, the arms signed out, the order initialed *A.C.* authorizing "removal of the native deputation aboard the Meridian prior to arrival at the coast," the deputation being eleven headmen coming downriver to petition against the concession that was stealing their land. The same concession. The same family. The same signature, sixty years apart, on the paper that had made Corrigan Salvage Fleet possible.

The deck got very quiet.

Not hostile-quiet. Worse. Listening-quiet. Thirty-one men hearing names that belonged to their uncles, their grandfathers, their villages — hearing them read out in a flat captain's voice with the dawn coming up gold on the water, and understanding, all at once and together, what the payroll had bought and what it had cost people who never saw a shilling of it.

Old Waima stood at the gangway with the council behind him, and he did not say anything. He didn't need to. His presence was the sentence.

"Stop," Corrigan said from the wheelhouse door. His voice had lost its kindness. "That is private commercial property. Ishikawa, you are committing theft, defamation—"

"Read along with me," I suggested. "It helps."

"'Paid to M. Delaney, engineer,'" I continued, "'twenty pounds, for services rendered, and a further sum upon confirmation that the pumps were seen to fail.'"

An engine failure. That was the method. They hadn't even shot anyone. They'd loosened something, watched the *Meridian* take water in the gorge, and stood by in a launch while she went down with everyone aboard who might have testified. And a man named Delaney had made sure the pumps failed, and been paid twenty pounds, and somewhere downstream of that payment there were children and grandchildren alive today because their great-grandparents drowned.

I found out later that one of the *Star*'s own engineers was a Delaney. Grand-nephew. He stepped away from the winch he was tending around name nineteen, walked to the rail, and spat into the river — a long, deliberate, ceremonial spit — and after that the stepping-away became general, the way a current becomes a flood. Not all at once. Man by man. A deckhand here, a coxswain there, drifting to the far side of the deck, putting distance between themselves and the wheelhouse door, until Edmund Corrigan stood alone on his own barge with a radio in his hand and no one willing to be handed orders through it.

He never reached for the gun I later learned he kept in the chart drawer. I think he understood, even then, that the arithmetic had changed. A shot fired now would be witnessed by thirty men who had already left him, by eleven elders who remembered everything, by a river full of dugouts closing slowly in. You cannot shoot your way out of being named.

"Captain Ishikawa." He came out onto the deck, and I'll grant him this: he walked upright. "What do you want? Name it. The permits, the concessions, a partnership — you'd make a better offer than any of these people ever could. They'll take your ledger and give you a feast and forget you by Christmas. I'm offering you a fleet."

And there it was. The same deal, third time around — Rill's version, Elias's version, now the father's own mouth. Buy the witness. Price everything. Assume that everyone, given the choice between gold and grace, takes the gold, because *he* always had, and so had his father, and it had worked, hadn't it, for sixty years?

"Elias Vane took your deal," I said. "You want to know what it bought him? Sixty years of a fortune he couldn't spend, a daughter who thought he was dead, and a gut wound on a mudbank with nobody but a stranger to hear him say sorry. That's your product, Mr. Corrigan. I've seen it up close. I'm not buying."

I closed the ledger.

Then I turned to the deck, to the crew who remained within earshot, to the elders at the gangway, to Nell and Sefina and Pita standing at the rail of the *Kestrel* alongside with Tamu propped up below in the engine room he'd refused to leave — and I held the book up so they could see it.

"This belongs to the descendants of the *Meridian*. Not to me. Not to the courts — the courts can have the copies, and they'll get them, notarized, whether this man likes it or not. But the original goes home." I handed it across the gap to old Waima, and his hands closed on the waxcloth wrapping the way you close your hands on a child returned to you. "Where it was incurred, it gets settled. That's river law. It's older than yours."

Waima weighed the ledger in both palms, and then he did something I will carry to my grave. He opened it right there, standing on the enemy's deck, and began reading the names himself — in his own language, slowly, giving each one back its sound, its weight, its village — and the elders behind him answered each name with a low refrain, call and response, a litany, a roll of honor for men murdered in 1909 and only now, on a salvage barge at sunrise, formally declared dead.

Thirty-one crewmen stood bareheaded through all forty-one names. When it finished, the eldest of the council — a woman, small and straight-backed, who had lost, I learned afterward, a grandmother on the *Meridian* — looked at Edmund Corrigan for a long moment.

"The river remembers who owes whom," she said. "Now everyone does."

* * *

What happened next happened without a shot, which is the part the newspapers will eventually get wrong, because newspapers need blood the way Corrigan needs permits.

His crew did not mutiny. They simply resigned, en masse, on the spot, in the traditional manner of river men everywhere — they started unloading their kit into the dinghies. Quietly, without hurry, without a word to their former employer, thirty-one men ferried themselves ashore to the council's side of the river, and Edmund Corrigan stood on the *Meridian Star* with a fleet of barges and not one soul willing to crew them.

Deacon Rill, I'm told, watched from the shore with his arms folded, and when one of the departing crew asked him — genuinely curious, the way men ask about weather — what he intended to do, Rill considered the question for a long time.

"My job was to keep this quiet," he said. "It isn't quiet anymore. You can't enforce a secret forty-one people have already heard read aloud." And he picked up his pack and walked into the treeline, toward the interior, toward the places where debts go to wait, and I have thought about that walk more often than I'd like, because I believe it was the most honest act I witnessed all week.

Corrigan made it as far as his floatplane. He sat in the pilot seat a long while — Pita, watching through the glass, swore the man was writing something, and Nell guessed a statement for the courts, and I guessed a letter to a lawyer, and we were all wrong, because when the plane finally lifted off the water and banked upriver instead of down, toward the coast, toward the officials who could no longer look away, the paper he'd been writing stayed pinned under the chart weight in his wheelhouse. Tamu found it later, when the council took possession of the barge.

It was a list. Forty-one names, copied out in Corrigan's own careful hand, each one checked off.

I don't know what he meant by it. Maybe nothing. Maybe a man who has spent his whole life believing people are line items finally reads a list and sees, too late, that the entries were souls. Or maybe it was just inventory, taken to the end, because that was the only language he'd ever learned. The river doesn't explain itself, and neither, in the end, did he.

* * *

By afternoon the *Kestrel* rode easy at anchor off the council's landing, and the gorge belonged — on paper, in law, in every way that matters — to the people who had always called it theirs. The permits lapsed at the next tide. Nobody claimed the wreck. The *Meridian* would stay where she lay, a grave now properly attended, her gold to be raised by the villages' own hands and their own seasons, for schools and clinics and boats, or maybe just held, some of it, the way you hold a thing that cost too much to spend quickly.

Sefina found me at the rail, watching the light go long on the water.

"You're not celebrating," she said.

"I'm waiting to find out what it costs." I nodded at the escarpment, going purple in the evening. "Justice outside the law still happens inside the world. Corrigan's got friends at the coast. Rill's owed favors in every port between here and Thursday Island. And we're six days from Port Moresby with a hold full of gold that half the territory would kill for and a letter I still have to deliver."

Sefina leaned on the rail beside me, shoulder to shoulder, the way crew do when words would only get in the way.

"So we run fast," she said.

"We run fast," I agreed.

Below decks, Tamu coughed once — a deep, wet sound I pretended not to catalog — and Nell laughed at something Pita said, and the Kestrel rocked gently on the last tide of the season, carrying us all toward whatever the current decided we were worth.

Behind us, upstream, in the darkening gorge, the *Meridian* kept her dead, and the ledger kept its names, and the river — forgiving nothing, forgetting less — carried the rest of us forward anyway.
