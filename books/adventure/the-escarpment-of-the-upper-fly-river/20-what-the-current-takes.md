## 20. What the Current Takes

I have been shot at, scalded, drowned once in the Sepik, and bitten by a cassowary I still maintain had it coming. None of it prepared me for the particular indignity of lying on my back in the mission hospital at Daru while a nurse named Grace changed the dressing on my side and told me, with genuine warmth, that I was lucky.

"Lucky," I said. "The bullet went through me."

"Through and through. Clean entry, clean exit. You should see some of the men who come off the dredges." She smoothed the tape down like she was finishing a piece of work she was proud of. "The river decided to keep you."

I did not argue. In my experience the river keeps what it wants and returns what it doesn't, and there is no use appealing the decision. It had kept my blood pressure, apparently, and returned me to the Kestrel's people with a hole in me that ached whenever I laughed, which was inconvenient, because lately everything made me laugh — the absurdity of Corrigan's floatplane sitting on the water like a dead albatross, the sight of Pita at the helm wearing the captain's cap two sizes too big, the fact that I, Tamu, who had sworn off heroics somewhere around my fiftieth year, had taken a bullet standing in an engine room doorway because it was mine and no one else was going to hold it.

Grace left, and Mara came in with her hat in her hands, which is how I knew the news was either very good or very bad. Mara wears her hat everywhere except when she is about to say something honest.

"Tamu." She sat on the edge of the chair, not the bed — she knows better than to crowd a man his own sickbed. "You want it straight?"

"I'm an engineer. Straight is all I understand."

"Corrigan ran last night."

I closed my eyes. "Upriver?"

"Upriver, then overland to the coast road, then a chartered plane out of Tabubil before anyone could think to stop him. He thought he'd outrun it." She let that sit a moment, and I watched her face do the thing it does when she's holding back the good part out of politeness. "Except we'd already sent the copies. Notarized. Sefina's cousin works in the lands office in Moresby, and Nell wrote letters to every newspaper, every magistrate, and one very persistent woman at the fisheries department who has hated Corrigan since 1974. By the time he landed at the coast, the papers were already on three desks and a radio broadcast."

"And?"

"And they arrested him at the wharf at Port Moresby. Two constables and a customs officer. No guns drawn. He just stood there." She turned her hat slowly in her hands. "He looked, Sefina said, like a man who'd been arguing with a river and finally heard it answer."

I laughed, which cost me, and it was worth it.

* * *

Recovery, I will tell you, is mostly boredom punctuated by visitors who think you want drama. What I wanted was news of my engine, and what I got instead was the whole crew rotating through that little ward like a tide table.

Pita came first, still wearing the cap, which he now refused to take off even indoors. He sat with me for an hour and told me, in exhaustive detail, about the maneuver at the rapids — the hard reverse-rudder into the eddy behind the rock, the way the Kestrel swung her stern around like a dog shaking off water. He described it as if he had planned it. I let him. The truth, which only Sefina and I know, is that he screamed the whole way through it and afterward vomited over the rail, and both of those things are exactly what courage looks like from the inside. Nobody tells you that. Courage is mostly nausea and doing the thing anyway.

"You saved the boat," I told him.

"I learned it from Sefina."

"You learned it from Sefina. You *did* it yourself. There's a difference, boy, and the difference is your whole life." He went red and studied his boots, and I let him off the hook by asking him to check my engine room, which he promised to do daily, and which I knew he would, because Pita does not yet know how to promise lightly.

Sefina came next and said almost nothing, which from Sefina is a eulogy. She brought me betel nut against doctor's orders and sat chewing while I chewed, and after a long time she said: "The gorge is ours now. The council flew their flag on the point this morning." Then she squeezed my ankle, the way divers do instead of hugging, and left.

Nell Vane read me parts of her father's letters. Not the hard ones — she kept those — but the ones where Elias, forty years ago, described an engine room on the Meridian, and a young Fijian stoker who taught him how to listen to a machine and hear what it was afraid of. That was me. I had forgotten the boy entirely; the river forgets nothing and neither, it turns out, do dying men. "He wrote that you were the only honest man aboard," Nell said.

"He exaggerated. I was being paid to be honest."

She smiled, and it was her father's smile, and I had to look out the window at the water until it passed.

Mara came every evening. She never stayed long. She would report the day — the permits lapsed at noon on the sixth tide, formal as a funeral; the gorge reverting to the customary owners whose elders had waited eighty years with the patience of stone; the salvage barges impounded at Kiunga pending inquiries that would, everyone agreed, go precisely nowhere, because inquiries always do — and then she would sit in silence with me for ten minutes, and leave. On the fourth evening she broke pattern.

"There's something you should hear from me before you hear it from the radio."

"Rill," I said.

Her jaw tightened. "Rill."

* * *

Here is what I know about Deacon Rill, and I have known men like him my whole working life, on dredges and in company offices and once, briefly, in a church choir that turned out to be a front for gunrunning: he is not a monster. Monsters are simple. Rill is worse — he is a professional. He was paid to bury a past, and when the past refused burial, he did not rage or blunder. He assessed, recalculated, and withdrew. The morning after Mara read the ledger aloud on Corrigan's barge, while Corrigan's own deckhands stepped away from him one by one like men leaving a sinking ship, Rill simply was not there. His boat was found later, fuelled and provisioned, tied up above the gorge with the keys in the ignition, as if he had stepped ashore to relieve himself and been swallowed by the country.

They found signs of him for a week. A trade store up the Ok Tedi tributaries where someone bought rice and cartridges and paid too much, deliberately, so the storekeeper would remember him fondly and remember nothing else. A village where an old woman admitted a tall stranger had slept in the men's house one night and left before dawn, and had fixed her grandson's bicycle chain without being asked. Then nothing. The interior took him the way the interior takes everything — without malice, without ceremony, completely.

"They'll send police after him eventually," Mara said. "A patrol, maybe, next dry season."

"It won't find him," I said. "Or it will, and it won't matter. Men like Rill don't get caught. They get collected."

Mara looked at me. "Meaning what?"

"Meaning the river remembers who owes whom. An elder gave Pita a carved token and told him that, and I've been thinking about it ever since. Rill burned villages. He owes more than any court can count, and courts don't collect those debts anyway. But the interior keeps its own books." I shifted, and my side reminded me of its opinion. "Somebody up there will decide the account is due. Maybe next year. Maybe ten years. And one morning Rill will walk out of the bush into a place where nobody will meet his eyes, and the canoes will be gone, and the paths will be wrong, and he'll understand that he isn't lost — he's being *kept*. Like a debt. Until it's called."

"That's not justice," Mara said. "That's just another kind of drowning."

"Yes," I agreed. "But it's the kind the river does best."

She put her hat back on, which meant the honesty was over, and left me with my thoughts and the sound of the tide going out.

* * *

On my ninth day, Grace pronounced me well enough to be a nuisance elsewhere, and Mara brought the Kestrel around to the Daru jetty herself, handling her solo with the tenderness of a woman returning to a horse that had carried her through fire. They loaded me aboard like cargo — I insisted on walking up the gangway myself, and made it halfway before Pita and Sefina each took an arm without discussion, and I pretended not to notice, and they pretended not to help.

My engine room broke my heart and mended it in the same hour. There was a scar along the bulkhead where the bullet had passed, patched with plate steel that was, I could tell by the welds, Pita's work — ugly, overbuilt, absolutely sound. He had welded it the way he now did everything: terrified, thorough, and twice as strong as it needed to be. Beside the patch, somebody had bolted a small shelf, and on the shelf sat the elder's carved token, varnished against the damp.

"We figured the engine room should keep it," Pita said, hovering. "Since. You know. Since it held."

I touched the carving and said nothing, because there are moments when a man's voice would only spoil the welding.

We ran light the first week — cargo of roofing iron and kerosene down to the delta villages, the ordinary bread-and-butter work that pays for diesel and dignity. The Kestrel felt different under my hands. Lighter, somehow, though she carried the same debts she always had, minus the ones we'd settled. Crews are engines too, if you want the truth of it, and ours had been rebuilt around a missing cylinder and run better than before, which any engineer will tell you is impossible and any family will tell you is routine.

At the bend where we had buried Elias, Mara slowed her, out of habit or ceremony, and Nell stood at the rail with her hand flat on the wood. The grave itself we'd left to the river's keeping — a cairn above the flood line, a marker of driftwood that the first big wet would take, which is what Elias would have wanted, being a man who understood that permanence is a story land tells itself. Nell didn't cry. She'd done her crying in Port Moresby, she told us, at a grave marker she'd commissioned with her own money, cut with the words *HE CAME BACK THE LONG WAY*. She'd said it was the truest epitaph she could afford.

"Current's strong here," Mara observed, at the wheel.

"Always is," I said. "That's why he liked it."

* * *

It was on that stretch, watching the water fold and unfold against the bank, that I finally let myself do the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since the portage — the arithmetic I had kept to myself through the whole upstream run, through the sabotage and the battle and the bullet, because a crew fighting for its life does not need to know its engineer has calculated the margin of survival down to the liter.

We had made the wreck with four hours of fuel to spare. Four hours. I had rationed, siphoned, and lied my way up that gorge knowing that if anything went wrong — one extra rapid, one missed eddy, one day of headwind — the Kestrel would have died in the shallows with all of us in her, and the ledger would have rotted unread, and Corrigan would have logged his permits and gone on buying silence until the end of his natural life. I had gambled eight lives on a calculation, and the calculation had held, and I had never told a soul.

I told Mara that evening, at anchor, with the lamp low and the others asleep.

She listened without interrupting, which is her gift, and when I finished she was quiet for a long moment. "Four hours," she said.

"Four hours and eleven minutes, if you want precision."

"Why didn't you tell me?"

"Because you'd have slowed down to be safe, and safe wasn't going to be fast enough. Because it was my weight to carry. Because—" I stopped, because the true reason was harder. "Because I've spent fifty years believing that a man holds things alone so the people around him can stay light enough to move. My brother believed that too. It's why he's dead and I'm not, and I've never been sure which of us got the better bargain."

Mara reached over and put her hand flat on the deck between us, the way you steady yourself against a hull. "Tamu. The next time you carry something like that alone, I'm going to throw you overboard myself."

"Noted," I said. "I'll factor it into the calculations."

And then, because the moment demanded it and because my side hurt less when I laughed than when I didn't, I laughed, and she laughed, and somewhere aft Pita yelled at us to keep the noise down, and the Kestrel rocked gently on the falling tide like a creature sleeping.

* * *

The orders came the following week, and they were, as I predicted, tidy in the way official paper is tidy — which is to say, not tidy at all, merely folded.

Corrigan, remanded, awaiting trial on charges that would take years to dwindle down to the two or three that would actually stick. The salvage fleet's permits voided, the equipment to be auctioned, the auction already contested by three lawyers and a bishop. The gorge formally returned to the customary owners, whose council had immediately posted their own rules at the mouth of the rapids: no dredges, no dynamite, no strangers without invitation, and a fee for passage that Mara paid gladly and the elders waived anyway, because the Kestrel had carried their token and their token was good.

And Rill — nothing. A line in a police bulletin, wanted for questioning, last seen in the upper reaches. The paper used the phrase *at large*, which made Sefina snort into her tea. "Nobody's at large in that country," she said. "There's no *large* to be at. There's just the bush, and the bush decides."

I think about him sometimes, when the engine is idling and my mind wanders the way old engines' minds do. I imagine him moving upriver through country that gets quieter the deeper he goes, spending money that buys less and less, fixing bicycle chains for old women who watch him with eyes that miss nothing. I imagine the night he realizes the paths are wrong. And I find — this is the strange part, the part I'd have denied a year ago — that I don't wish him dead. I wish him *found*. Found by whatever finds such men, whether it's a patrol boat or a conscience or the slow patient arithmetic of a river that remembers who owes whom. Justice outside the law, Mara called what she did with the ledger. The interior runs on the same principle. It just has longer to wait.

We were loading at Kiunga when the letter came. Nell brought it up from the post office herself, turning it over in her hands, and handed it to Mara without a word, and Mara read it standing in the sun, and her face did something I had not seen it do in all the months I'd served aboard her brother's boat and hers.

"What is it?" I asked.

Mara looked up at the three of us gathered there — Nell, Sefina, Pita with the cap pushed back — and held out the page. "It's from the daughter of one of the names in the ledger," she said. "One of the Meridian families. Upstream. She writes that the council has finished the mourning ceremonies, and the gold cases have been divided as the elders ruled, and—" her voice caught, just slightly, the way a rope catches on a cleat before it runs free— "and they're inviting the crew of the Kestrel to the feast. All of us. As family."

Pita grinned. Sefina raised an eyebrow, which from her was cheering. Nell was already calculating the tide.

Mara looked at me over the top of the letter, and I saw the question in her face before she asked it, the question she always asks, the one that has nothing to do with cargo or current: *are we equal to this?*

"Well, Captain," I said, and patted the engine housing beside me like an old dog. "Fire her up. We've got a high tide to catch, and for once in my life, I intend to arrive early."
