## 22. Every Sandbar's Mood

The river teaches you its moods the way a difficult relative does — slowly, grudgingly, and mostly after it's already made you pay tuition. Every sandbar has one. The long bar below D'Albertis Junction sulks in a dry season, showing its spine like an insult. The shoal at Wabo is cheerful in the wet, all brown laughter, and then turns on you without warning when the current shifts and it moves two hundred meters overnight just to prove it can. Old Tamu used to say the sandbars were the river's ledger, keeping accounts of every hull that ever scraped them, and I used to tell him he'd been drinking the bilge again.

He's alive to be wrong about things, which these days strikes me as the whole of good fortune.

We came down from Kiunga light-loaded — copra, some sheet iron, a crate of school books for the mission at Morehead that Pita insisted on stowing himself because, as he put it, "books sink worse than anything." He said it with such gravity you'd think he'd done the research. Maybe he had. The boy reads now. Somewhere between holding a helm through rapids under fire and watching me read the Corrigan names aloud until grown men stepped off a barge like men stepping off a sinking deck, Pita decided he was going to be somebody, and reading seemed to him the cheapest way to start.

The Kestrel ran sweet beneath us. That's not sentiment; that's Tamu's work. He convalesced for six weeks at the mission with his shoulder stitched and strapped, swearing at the sisters in three languages they pretended not to understand, and the moment he could lift a wrench he rebuilt her fuel lines, her injectors, half her plumbing, out of what I suspect was pure stubbornness. A man nearly dies at his post, and his revenge is to make the post better than it ever was. There are worse philosophies.

Sefina stood beside me at the rail, watching the water the way she watches water — which is to say like an old rival she respects too much to trust. Her hands had healed. The rest of her, she says, takes longer, and she doesn't say much more than that. Some mornings she goes down to the engine room and sits with Tamu while he works, saying nothing, handing him tools before he asks. That's her way of talking.

Nell was aft, mending a net badly. She'll never mend well — her knots are a scandal, her father would have wept — but she insists, and we let her, because the net isn't the point. The point is that Nell Vane stands on my deck with salt drying on her forearms and her hair tied back with a bit of cargo twine, and six months ago she came up a gangplank with a pistol in her bag and murder in her heart, aimed at a father who'd had the poor manners to die before she could finish being angry at him.

Grief is a cargo like any other. You can't dump it overboard; the river won't take it. You just learn where to stow it so the boat keeps her trim.

* * *

The copies went out ahead of us, the way seeds go out on the wind.

Tamu's cousin runs a duplicating office behind the printing shop in Port Moresby, a man who owes Tamu money from a card game in 1974 and has been looking for a debt he could pay without embarrassment ever since. Two hundred copies of the Meridian ledger left that office in sealed envelopes before Corrigan's lawyers finished their first cup of coffee: newspapers, churches, the Native Land Councils, two magistrates who owed their seats to nobody, the archives in Suva, a law lecturer at UPNG who'd been writing about concession fraud for years and had been ignored with great politeness by everyone who mattered.

Corrigan tried everything you'd expect. Injunctions. A claim the ledger was forged. An offer, relayed through intermediaries, to buy the originals "for historical preservation." When that failed, men visited the print shop, and found Tamu's cousin had already moved his press twice and taken to sleeping somewhere with a dog. The dog helped. Dogs always help; they ask for so little and mean every bark.

What finally did it wasn't the courts. It was the coast towns. It was the descendants' council reading the names aloud at meetings along the river — the same thing I did on Corrigan's barge, but slower, week after week, village after village, until the names stopped being paper and became people again. Men whose grandfathers had loaded copra beside them. Women who remembered their mothers refusing to talk about '09 and now understood why. You cannot un-name the dead once the living have said them back into the world. Corrigan learned this the expensive way, which is the only way men like him learn anything.

He was arrested at the coast in March. Not dramatically. No shots, no chase. Two officials met his floatplane at the mooring with papers they could no longer pretend not to have, and he walked between them to a government launch, and according to witnesses he looked less like a ruined man than a man interrupted mid-sentence. Perhaps that's what he was. His whole life was a sentence he'd been dictating since 1909, and someone had finally lifted the pen out of his hand.

Rill, though. Rill simply went upriver.

That's the entry in my own private ledger that doesn't balance. I've turned it over at anchor more nights than I'll admit. A man like Deacon Rill doesn't vanish; he relocates, the way a crocodile relocates. He owes debts — the elder's token in my cabin says the river remembers who owes whom — and the interior is full of people patient enough to collect. I find I'm not afraid of him anymore, exactly. I'm something worse: I'm curious. And curiosity about Deacon Rill is a luxury I intend to keep postponing.

* * *

We took the bend slow, the way you do.

It's nothing to look at — a curve where the current shoulders against the cut bank and the water goes glass-dark and deep, with a fig tree leaning out over it like a man craning to see what's coming downstream. Elias Vane picked it himself, in the intervals when the morphine let him speak. "Good bend," he kept saying. "River slows here. Lets a man think." Then, darker: "Lets a man answer for himself."

We buried him on the point above it, under the fig, facing the water. Tamu read something in his own tongue that I didn't understand and didn't need to. Sefina folded a diving weight into the grave wrap — "so he stays put," she said, and then looked embarrassed, and then didn't, because it was true and true things don't need decorating. Nell hadn't yet forgiven him. I don't think she has fully now. But she cut her palm on a tin lid that day and let three drops fall into the hole, which in her family, she told me later, was how you signed a document.

I go past that bend maybe eight times a year. For months I couldn't look at it. Then I couldn't stop looking. Now it's just a bend again, except that it isn't, and never will be for me.

This time, coming down with the copra and the school books and my strange, patched-together crew, I slowed the Kestrel to bare steerageway and let her drift the curve, and I did what I'd been circling for half a year like a bird over a carcass.

I talked to him.

"You should know it's done." My voice sounded ridiculous over the engines, so I throttled down, and Tamu, God love him, killed them entirely without being asked, and we drifted in the hush with only the water talking against the hull. "The ledger's out. Copies everywhere. Your name's on page nine, Elias — what they paid you, what you took and never spent. The council's got the original. The gold went where you'd have wanted it or near enough — schools, the memorial house at the burned village, compensation paid proper through the council and not through any lawyer who'd skim. I kept nothing. Well. I kept the boat."

The fig tree leaned. The current carried a leaf past my rail and away downstream, unhurried, like a man walking to a meeting he'll arrive at whenever he arrives.

"Your daughter's aboard right now, tying knots that would shame a drowned sailor, and she's staying. She read your letters. All of them. She yelled at you pretty thoroughly, if it helps — and I think it did help her, so don't feel flattered. She knows what you were and what you weren't, and she's chosen to stay anyway, which is more than most of us manage with our dead."

I looked at the point, at the dark soil under the roots, at the small wooden marker Pita carved — rough letters, E. VANE, RIVERMAN, and beneath them, in smaller cuts that must have cost the boy an afternoon: HE TOLD THE TRUTH LATE. IT COUNTED.

"Done," I said. "You hear me? Done."

And the river, which forgives nothing — which keeps every account, every hull, every name, every body it was given and every body it was denied — the river carried us on around the bend anyway. That's the bargain it offers. Not forgiveness. Passage. I've come to believe passage is worth more.

* * *

That evening we rafted up at the mission landing to deliver the school books, and Father Anselm pressed cold tea and gossip on us, and the gossip was good: the concession registry at Kiunga had posted the lapse notices, the gorge country had passed formally back to the customary owners, and there was talk — real talk, with dates — of a council-run ferry service, and wouldn't the Kestrel's master consider the contract?

"I'd consider it," I said, "if the rates were honest."

"They're terrible," said Father Anselm cheerfully. "It's the missions. We pay in prayer."

"We'll take half prayer, half cash," said Sefina, and everyone laughed, even Nell, who was trying very hard to look dignified and failing in the specific way that means she's happy and resents it.

Later, on deck, the others turned in one by one, and I stayed up with the wheel and the moon and the long brown slide of the river. This is the hour I used to hate most, back when the Kestrel was just a debt with a hull around it and I was just the last Ishikawa, running cargo alone because alone was simpler and grief was easier to carry in a small boat. Alone, you see, is simple. Alone is one set of hands, one plate, one silence. What nobody tells you about alone is that it's also the slowest way to drown, because there's nobody aboard to notice the water rising.

I looked down the deck. Tamu's snore came up through the engine room hatch, steady as a pump. Sefina slept with her door open — she always will now, I think; closed doors and she have reached an understanding. Nell had fallen asleep in the deck chair with a letter of her father's open on her chest, rising and falling with her breath, and Pita had draped a blanket over her without waking either of them, moving with the particular exaggerated care of a boy practicing, rehearsing, becoming the kind of man who does such things without needing to be thanked.

My brother left me this boat and a mountain of other people's money. Elias left me a map and a promise. The river left me nothing but passage.

And somehow, between the three of them, I'd ended up rich in the only currency I ever secretly wanted, the kind you can't bank and can't steal and can't spend alone: a crew. A family assembled from wreckage, mine and theirs, riveted together with shared danger and bad jokes and Nell's criminal knots.

The current held us. Tomorrow there'd be cargo, and the contract to haggle over, and a hundred sandbars each keeping its own mood, and a river that remembers everything and forgives nothing and carries us forward regardless — which, I've decided, is as close to grace as this world honestly comes.

I put my hand flat on the wheel, old wood gone smooth as skin, and steered us into the dark, and for the first time in longer than I could reckon, the mood of every sandbar ahead was one I recognized.

It was mine.
