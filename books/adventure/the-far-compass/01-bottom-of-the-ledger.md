## 1. Bottom of the Ledger

The wreck was forty fathoms down, and Ansel Vasquez had been told twice — once by letter, once by a man with a sidearm and a very patient voice — that it did not exist.

She went down to it anyway at first light, because the tide was right and the rent was due and the navy's patience cost nothing she could spend.

* * *

Dawn on the water was a gray rumor. She'd anchored over the site an hour before sunrise, engine off, running lights dark, and sat in the wheelhouse of the *Kestrel's Debt* — her boat, though the bank owned most of it and the harbor master owned the rest in fees — drinking coffee gone bitter in the thermos and watching the sonar screen paint the hull below like a bruise.

The charts called it a shoal. The charts were wrong. Something two hundred feet long lay on its side in the silt there, broken amidships, and every chart for sixty miles had been amended within a year of its sinking. Amended by whom, filed where, under what authority — that was the kind of question you didn't ask if you wanted to keep your license. Or your fingers.

Ansel had asked once. The answer had been a visit.

So she didn't ask anymore. She just dove when the visibility was good and the patrol schedule was predictable, which it was, mostly, because navy men liked their routines the way they liked their coffee: hot, regular, and unexamined.

She suited up in the dark of the cabin, checking seals by touch. Her shoulder ached where the old rotator tear lived — a souvenir from a trawler winch three years ago, aggravated by every dive since. She taped it anyway. You worked around pain or you worked for someone else.

The water took her at 5:47 by her watch, cold enough to squeeze the breath out of her even through the suit. She descended hand over hand down the shot line, and the world narrowed to the circle of her torch: green-black water, drifting motes, the slow bloom of her own exhaust bubbles rising past her face like thoughts she couldn't hold onto.

At thirty meters the wreck resolved out of the murk, and as always, the sight of it stopped her heart for one full beat.

A ship should look like a ship. This looked like a wound. The hull lay canted into the silt, plates peeled back along the break like petals of something that had bloomed violently and died. Sixty years of sediment had softened it, grown it over with pale anemones and the white lacework of hydroids, but the lines underneath were still wrong — too fine, too naval, built for speed and then denied a name. No registry number. No port of call. Just a smooth stretch of plating where the identification should have been, ground off so thoroughly it looked like it had never existed.

Ansel swam the length of the cargo hold, following the route she'd mapped across four illegal dives. Most of what was worth taking was already in her bilge: brass fittings, a ship's chronometer, silverware stamped with a crest she'd been careful not to research. Enough to keep the creditors circling but not enough to make them leave.

Today she was after the sealed compartment aft. She'd found it last week — a door welded shut from the inside, which was the kind of detail that got into a diver's head and stayed there. Somebody had wanted whatever was behind it kept in. Or kept safe. On a ship with no name, those might be the same thing.

Her cutting gear made short work of the hinges. The door groaned open into a compartment the size of a closet, silt swirling up in a brown cloud that took a minute to settle. When it did, her torch found shelves. Racks. And on the lowest rack, half-buried, a strongbox.

It was smaller than she'd expected — maybe two feet long, steel, crusted with rust and coral but fundamentally sound. She worked it free with both hands, shoulder screaming, and turned it in the light.

And that was when she saw the stamp.

Not the shipping marks she knew. Not the navy's broad arrow. A crest pressed deep into the lid: a mountain peak above crossed lines — a compass rose, maybe, or surveyor's marks — ringed by text worn nearly smooth. She scraped at it with her dive knife, careful, and got one word clear through the corrosion:

*FARLIGHT.*

She didn't know the word. That bothered her more than the box itself. Ansel Vasquez knew wrecks the way priests knew scripture; she knew every salvage flag, every shipping line, every government marking that meant *put this down and walk away.* This crest belonged to none of them. It was private. Expedition-grade. The kind of stamp you put on equipment when you expected it to come back, and somebody had gone to the trouble of making sure it hadn't.

The box was heavy. Forty pounds, maybe fifty, dense in a way that wasn't lead ballast. She clipped it to her lift line, checked her air — a third remaining — and started up.

Halfway through her decompression stop, hanging in the empty blue with the box swaying beneath her, she let herself imagine what was inside. Gold, probably. Salvage divers who imagined anything else were lying to themselves. Gold meant the debt gone. The *Kestrel's Debt* free and clear, the harbor fees paid five years forward, her name scrubbed off the ledger at Marrow & Finch that they read aloud every quarter like a sentence being passed.

Gold meant she could stop diving other people's secrets.

She held onto that thought all the way up, because the alternative thought — the one about names ground off hulls and compartments welded from the inside — was the kind of thought that made divers check their tanks twice and sleep with the lights on.

* * *

The sun was fully up when she broke the surface, and the world was ordinary again: gulls, gray water, the smell of diesel and salt. She hauled the strongbox over the gunwale with a block and tackle, breathing hard, and stood over it dripping on the deck while her heartbeat came back down to something human.

Up close, the crest was worse. Better. Worse-better. The metal around the stamp was stamped clean, deliberate — this hadn't been added in haste. Under the word FARLIGHT ran a second line, smaller, and she worked her thumbnail through the crust until she could read it:

*EXPEDITION — CHARTERED.*

Chartered by whom? There was no nation's mark, no company registration format she recognized. Just the word, sitting there like a dare.

She got the pry bar from the toolbox, set the edge against the hasp, and paused.

Sixty-year-old steel, sealed from inside a compartment welded shut. Whatever was in there had been put away by people who expected to return, and hadn't. People whose names had been ground off a hull. Ansel had a rule about boxes like that, a rule she'd never had occasion to use before: don't open what the dead locked, until you're sure you want to know why they locked it.

But she also had a payment due in nine days, and rules were for people with margins.

She was still standing there, pry bar in hand, when the engine noise reached her.

Not a patrol launch — she knew those sounds the way she knew her own pulse, twin diesels at cruising rpm, arrogant. This was a single outboard, small, laboring. A dinghy coming around the headland, riding low, moving with the desperate rhythm of an engine about to die.

Ansel looked at the box. Looked at the dinghy. Threw a tarp over the box and kicked it behind the wheelhouse just as the outboard coughed its last and a shape began paddling the final hundred yards.

The man in the dinghy was older than the sea itself, or looked it — gaunt, gray-bearded, hands like knotted rope on the paddle. His clothes were expedition-grade and expedition-worn, the kind of technical shell that cost more than her boat's anchor and was currently held together with duct tape. He was shivering hard, lips gray, and when he looked up at her his eyes were burning bright in a way she recognized from the faces of men she'd pulled out of the water before: not fear. Certainty. The terrible calm of someone who has decided how he dies and only needs a witness.

"Vasquez," he said. His voice was gravel and wind. "Ansel Vasquez. I've been looking for you."

"You've got the wrong boat." She said it automatically, the way you say it to everyone.

"I watched you dive the Farlight site." He smiled, and there was blood on his teeth, or rust, or something. "Don't insult us both."

Behind her, under the tarp, the strongbox sat silent with its secret crest. Ahead of her, the day she'd planned — sell the fittings, stall Finch, buy nine more days — was already dead.

"How long have you been watching?" she said.

"Long enough." He gripped the gunwale, hauled himself halfway up, and she caught his arm on reflex — he weighed nothing, a bundle of sticks wrapped in expensive fabric. Up close he smelled of woodsmoke and sickness and altitude, somehow, altitude, though the nearest mountain was eighty miles inland. "I need passage," he said. "And I need to give you something. Before it's too late for both of us."

"My passenger list is full."

"Your passenger list is a lie," he said, gently, almost kindly, "and your boat is named after what you owe. Let me aboard, Ansel Vasquez. I'm dying either way. The only question is whether I die useful."

The gulls wheeled overhead. The tide was turning. Somewhere down in the green dark, a nameless ship kept its silence, and somewhere behind the tarp, a stamped crest read *FARLIGHT — EXPEDITION — CHARTERED,* and waited.

Ansel looked at the dying man's hands on her gunwale — rope-knotted, frost-scarred, shaking — and felt the morning tilt beneath her, the whole careful architecture of her small, survivable life beginning, slowly, to slide.

"Thirty seconds," she said. "Talk fast."

He smiled again, and this time it was almost warm.

"Oh," he said. "We're going to need longer than that."
