## 2. The Man Who Sold Weather

The storm came in off the water an hour after dark, and with it came the man.

Ansel heard him before she saw him — a dragging, wet-footed shuffle on the dock planks, the kind of sound a body makes when it has stopped caring whether it arrives anywhere. She was below, counting what was left of her money for the third time that day, as if the numbers might improve with repetition. They did not. Four hundred and eleven dollars, a fuel bill due Friday, and a boat that ate money the way the sea ate wrecks.

The knock, when it came, was barely a knock. More like a man falling politely against her hull door.

She opened it with the dive knife already in her hand. Old habit. The harbor at night belonged to creditors and worse.

He was sixty, maybe seventy, hard to say — the kind of face the mountains had been eroding for decades, all ridges and scree. His coat was good wool gone gray with rain, his pack military surplus and older than she was, and he was bleeding from a cut above his eyebrow in a way that suggested he'd fallen and not bothered to mention it.

"Boat's the *Far Compass*," he said. Not a question. "You're Vasquez."

"I'm closed."

"You're the only boat in this harbor with a dive crane that still works." He swayed, caught the doorframe. "I need to get off the water. Storm's got teeth tonight."

"Then get a room."

"Rooms have doors that open from the outside." He said it flat, without drama, the way you'd say *the sky is up*. "I've been followed. Maybe. I don't know anymore. I need one night where nobody knows where I am, and you need — " his eyes flicked past her, to the ledger open on the chart table, the red column of it — "you need about nine thousand dollars, if my arithmetic's right."

The knife came up an inch before she could stop it. "How the hell — "

"Salvage diver, thirty-two, boat mortgaged twice, working the *Meridian* shelf last week where nobody's supposed to work." He smiled, and it was a terrible smile, all apology. "I was a radio operator for thirty years, girl. I listen. And I owed a man at the chandlery a favor, and he talks when he drinks."

Ansel stood in the doorway with rain blowing in past him and did the math she didn't want to do. A stranger who knew her name, her debts, and her illegal dive site was not a guest. He was a liability with a pulse.

But he was also, she noticed, dying. Not dramatically — no theatrical collapse — but the way a lamp dies. The light in him was the wrong color. His lips were gray. There was a smell under the rain and salt that she'd learned on her father's boat, the summer the engine room took him, and she'd hoped never to smell again.

"One night," she said. "You sleep in the fo'c'sle. You don't touch my charts. You don't touch my radio. At first light you're gone, and I never saw you."

"First light," he agreed, and came aboard.

* * *

She didn't sleep. She sat in the main cabin with the knife open on the table beside the lamp and listened to the storm work the harbor, and around two in the morning the sounds from the fo'c'sle changed — the rhythm of a man lying still became the rhythm of a man talking to someone who wasn't there.

She went back anyway. Stupid. She knew it was stupid and went anyway, because the voice had a frequency to it, a distress-call cadence, and thirty years of salvage divers don't get to ignore a distress call just because the water is dry.

He was sitting up in the narrow bunk, blanket around his shoulders, eyes open and fixed on the bulkhead as if the mountains were painted there.

"They're above the cloud line," he was saying. "I put them there. I carried the last of it up myself, and then I walked down and I never told a soul, thirty years, not a soul — "

"Hollis." She'd gotten the name from the chandlery man too, secondhand. "Hollis. You're on a boat."

His eyes found her. For a moment they were clear, and what was in them was worse than the raving — it was arithmetic. A man calculating how much time he had left and how much he needed.

"Vasquez," he said. "Sit down. I want to make you an offer before I stop making sense, and I can feel it coming, so we should be quick."

She sat. Not because she trusted him. Because her body decided before her pride did.

"You worked the *Meridian* shelf," he said. "Navy wreck. Sealed site. You went down there anyway because the ledger's red and the navy doesn't pay salvage claims it doesn't acknowledge." He coughed, wet and long, and held up a hand before she could answer. "Don't confirm it. I don't want to know. What I want is this."

He fumbled at his pack, which he had never let out of arm's reach, and drew out a tube of oilcloth, wrapped twice and tied with wire. His hands shook getting the wire loose. Inside was a chart, rolled, and when he spread it across her chart table — across her father's chart table, she noted, with some distant resentment — she saw it was hand-drawn. Not surveyor's work. Not Mara-perfect, if she'd known the word for it yet. It was the work of someone who had walked the ground and refused to forget a single step of it.

A mountain range she half recognized from ferry crossings. A river valley. A pass marked in careful ink. And above the pass, above a contour line annotated *cloud line, do not trust your eyes past here*, a small square marked with a symbol she'd seen once before — twelve hours ago, forty fathoms down, stamped into the lid of a strongbox she'd left sitting on the seabed because her lift bags were rated for less weight and her nerve for less trouble.

The crest was the same. A compass rose with a blade through it.

"Where did you get this?" she asked.

"I drew it," Hollis said. "I was there when they built what it points to. I was the radio operator on the Kestrel Expedition, nineteen years ago, and I am the only man who came down off that mountain, and I have spent nineteen years being the only man who came down off that mountain, and it is a weight, girl, it is a weight like you would not — " He stopped. Breathed. The lamp guttered in a gust and steadied. "There's a cache above the cloud line. Depot crates, sealed. What's in them isn't treasure. It's worse than treasure. It's *true*, and the true is worth more than the gold and worth less than nothing, depending on who's holding it."

"You're raving."

"I'm dying," he said, with terrible mildness. "Those are different things, though they rhyme."

Ansel looked at the chart. She looked at the crest. She thought about the strongbox on the seabed, and about how she'd surfaced from that dive with her heart going like a bilge pump and a name in her head she couldn't read, and about how the navy launch had passed her at dusk, close enough to read her hull number, close enough that she'd lain awake all night waiting for the knock that hadn't come.

Yet.

"What's the offer," she said.

"Two options. You choose one. I don't care which, I swear to you on whatever you like, I genuinely do not care which — I've wanted both for nineteen years and I never had the spine for either." He laid one cracked hand flat on the chart, over the little square above the cloud line. "Option one: you carry this over the range before the first snow. You open the depot, you take what's in it, and you make it public. Loud. Newspapers, courts, whatever it takes. You read it out loud where it can't be put back in the box."

"And option two?"

"You burn it." He said it simply, the way you'd say *scuttle her*. "The chart, the cache, all of it. You go up there with fire and you end it, and whatever's in those crates dies with the mountain, and the dead stay dead, and nobody else gets hurt chasing the truth of a thing that happened nineteen years ago in a place God has clearly tried to forget."

The storm hit the hull. Ansel became aware that her hands were flat on the table on either side of the chart, braced, as if the boat might take a wave while she stood in her own cabin.

"Why me?"

"Because you're broke and desperate and you already dive where you shouldn't, which means you'll go where you shouldn't." He smiled that awful apologetic smile again. "Because you're the only boat in this harbor. And because I watched you come out of the water yesterday at first light, and you came up slow and careful, checking your own gear before you checked your haul. That's a person who finishes what she starts. That's the whole job. The whole job is finishing what you start."

"That's not an endorsement, that's a diagnosis."

"Same thing, at my age."

She should have said no. She knew it the way you know weather coming — in the teeth, in the old break in her shoulder. A dying stranger, a hand-drawn map, a navy crest, and a choice between a mountain and a fire. Every instinct she had, the instincts that had kept her alive at forty fathoms with a fouled line, said: *this is how divers die. Not in the water. In the story they tell themselves on the way to it.*

But the ledger was open on the table. Four hundred and eleven dollars. And a cache above the cloud line that a man was offering her like a hand of cards he couldn't play anymore.

"Say the terms," she said. "Exactly."

"Carry it over the range before the snows, or burn it. Both. Either. Your choice, made at the top, with it in your hands." His eyes were going glassy now, the lamp in him flickering toward its color. "I only ask one thing. Don't give it to the navy. Whatever else you do — they'll offer you money, they'll offer you forgiveness, they'll offer you your boat. Don't give it to the navy."

"Why not?"

"Because I know what they did," Hollis said, "and worse, I know what they were right about."

And then he went somewhere else, mid-sentence, into the mountains, talking to men who'd been dead nineteen years, calling coordinates into a radio that had been silent since before she owned a boat. She sat with him until near dawn because there was nothing else to do, and because some part of her — the part that had surfaced slow and careful yesterday, checking her gear before her haul — understood that she had just been handed cargo, and cargo doesn't get to choose its weather.

* * *

At first light the storm had spent itself and the harbor lay flat and gray as sheet metal. Hollis was still alive, barely, and lucid in the way of the very sick — clear windows between the fog, each one shorter than the last.

She made him tea he couldn't drink. He held the cup anyway, for the warmth.

"You didn't answer," he said. "Which option."

"I said I'd take it over the range," Ansel said. "And I said I'd burn it. I promised both."

Hollis looked at her for a long moment, and then he laughed — a dry, rusted sound that turned into coughing that scared her more than the raving had.

"Both," he said, when he could speak. "That's the most honest answer I've had in nineteen years. Everyone else picks a side before they've seen it." He pressed the oilcloth tube into her hands, wire re-tied, his fingers cold as mooring line. "Keep both promises, Vasquez. Right up until you can't. Then keep the one that lets you sleep."

He slept after that, a real sleep, and she went up on deck to breathe air that didn't smell like the end of a man, and that was when she saw the launch.

Navy gray, no running lights, standing off the harbor mouth in the flat morning light like a knife laid on a table. Not approaching. Waiting. Watching her boat the way you watch a burrow before you dig.

Ansel stood at the rail with a dead man's map under her arm and a promise in each hand, both of them lies, and watched the launch watch her, and understood that the storm last night had not been the weather.

It had been the forecast.
