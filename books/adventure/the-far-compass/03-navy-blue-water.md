## 3. Navy Blue Water

The patrol launch came out of the morning haze like a rumor getting serious.

Ansel saw it first as a gray shape off the southern point, running without lights, which was itself an answer. Nobody ran dark at dawn off this coast except smugglers and the navy, and smugglers knew better than to run dark this close to the channel buoys. She stood at the wheel of the *Magpie* with a mug of coffee going cold in her hand and did the arithmetic she always did when a hull appeared on the water: Can I outrun it? Can I outmaneuver it? Can I afford to try?

The launch was a thirty-meter patrol boat, twin screws, a bow that could open her old trawler like a tin. No on any count.

She set the mug down, checked that the strongbox was under the floor panel in the forward berth where it had spent the last two nights, and then checked again, because checking twice was the whole of her religion these days. The panel sat flat. The rug over it sat flat. Her heart did not sit flat at all.

Then she went topside, opened the transom gate, and waited.

The launch closed the distance with the unhurried confidence of something that had never once been chased. White ensign at the masthead. A number on the bow she didn't bother to read — the numbers changed, the boats didn't. Two sailors in gray worked the rails. A third stood at the bow with a loudhailer he hadn't raised yet, which meant they wanted her to make the first mistake, or the first move, which amounted to the same thing.

"Vessel *Magpie*," the loudhailer said at last, mild as a weather report. "Heave to. We're coming alongside."

Ansel killed the engine and let the swell take her. The launch slid in on her port side with a competence that bordered on showing off, fenders kissing fenders, and the first sailor across was already looking at her deck like a man taking inventory for a bill he hadn't written yet.

"Morning," Ansel said.

"Morning," the sailor said, and didn't smile, and didn't not smile, and went aft with his eyes working.

The second one was younger and worse at hiding what he was. His gaze snagged on the salvage gear lashed amidships — the lift bags, the hook line, the dive ladder — the way a man's gaze snags on a bruise. She watched him do the math. Trawler, salvage rig, anchored three miles off a wreck that did not officially exist.

"Captain Vasquez?" said the man coming across last.

He came up the transom ladder without being helped, which told her he'd done it before, and stood on her deck in a uniform so clean it looked like an argument against the sea. Lieutenant, single stripe of gold at the shoulder. Mid-thirties, maybe, with the kind of face that would still be handsome when it was old because the bones had been designed by someone patient. Dark hair cropped short. Eyes the flat gray of deep water, the kind that didn't glitter so much as register.

He put out his hand. "Lieutenant Corvane. Coastal Patrol."

She took it. His grip was exactly calibrated — firm, brief, a handshake like a receipt.

"Ansel Vasquez."

"Captain Vasquez." He said her name the way a man sets a stone down on a table, carefully, so it doesn't roll. "I hope we're not inconveniencing you."

"You are, a little. I was about to dive."

"At dawn?" He glanced at the light, which was still the color of weak tea. "Cold work."

"It's cold work all the way down. I like to get it over with."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not a smile. A filing. "May I?" He gestured at the wheelhouse with an open palm, the universal gesture of a man who had never in his life been told no by anyone whose no mattered.

"It's not much of a wheelhouse."

"Neither is my launch." He stepped inside ahead of her, which was also an answer.

* * *

The wheelhouse of the *Magpie* smelled of diesel, coffee, and old chart paper. Corvane stood in the middle of it with his hands clasped behind his back and looked at nothing in particular, which was how he looked at everything: the chart rolled on the shelf, the logbook lying open where she'd left it, the tide tables, the mug she'd abandoned. A man reading a room the way she read a wreck — by what had been disturbed.

"You work alone, Captain?"

"Mostly."

"Mostly." He let it sit. "You were seen at the Kestrel Shoal wreck on Tuesday. And Thursday. And yesterday."

"Kestrel Shoal is a designated salvage site. I hold the permit."

"You do." He didn't reach for the logbook. He didn't need to. "Permit 4-117, issued through the Harbor Office, fourteen months ago. You've been working her for fourteen months?"

"Fourteen months of silt. She's a coastal freighter that went down in the war. Cargo holds are collapsed. I'm cutting access."

"Mm." He turned, finally, and looked at her, and the look had weight to it, like a hand laid flat on a table. "You know what I admire about salvage divers, Captain? Patience. The willingness to go back to the same cold hole, day after day, for something you can't see from the surface. Most people need faster rewards."

"Most people can afford them."

"Ah." The corner of the mouth again. "The debts."

Her spine went to iron. "My finances are—"

"Public record, Captain. Harbor liens, fuel credit, a repair yard in Port Anselm that has been very patient with you." He said it without cruelty, which made it worse. He said it the way a doctor reads a chart. "I'm not here about the money. I'm here because three nights ago a man was carried into the fishing camp at Gull Point with a bullet in him, and this morning that man is missing, and the last person anyone saw him talk to was you."

The wheelhouse got very quiet. Somewhere below, the bilge pump cycled on and off, the boat's small honest heartbeat.

"I don't know anything about a shooting," Ansel said.

"I believe you." He said it fast enough that it wasn't a comfort. "But you did have a visitor. An older man. Bad leg. Bad lungs. Talked too much."

"Hollis." The name left her before she could decide whether to spend it, and she watched it land. Corvane didn't blink, didn't lean, didn't do any of the things a man does when he's surprised. He'd known. He'd known before he ever came alongside, and everything since — the handshake, the chart, the admiring little speech about patience — had been a road built toward this one word so he could watch her walk onto it.

"Hollis," Corvane repeated. "Yes."

"He paid for a night aboard. Food and fuel. That's all."

"That's all," Corvane said again, and turned to the chart shelf, and drew out the top roll with two fingers.

It was her chart. Her working chart of the coast, the one with the Kestrel Shoal site marked and her dive grid penciled in faint squares. He unrolled a hand's width of it and studied the coastline as if he were considering buying it.

"Tell me what he talked about, Captain. Take your time. I have a launch full of sailors who would very much like to get warm."

"He talked," Ansel said. "Old men alone talk. Weather. His leg. A dog he used to have."

"Did he talk about the mountains?"

"No."

"About a cache?"

"No."

Corvane rolled the chart closed with a soft, exact motion and set it back exactly where it had been. "You're lying," he said, in the same tone he'd used to ask about the weather. "That's fine. I expected it. Hollis lied to me for eleven years and he was dying the whole time, so you're in good company."

* * *

He made her a cup of coffee.

That was the thing she turned over later, the detail that wouldn't sit still: he found her kettle, found her second mug, and made her a cup of coffee while her boat rocked under them both, and he did it well, no fumbling, as if he'd made coffee in a hundred wheelhouses belonging to a hundred people he was about to ruin.

"Let me tell you a story," he said, handing her the mug. He leaned against the chart shelf, arms folded, the picture of a man with nowhere else to be. "There was an operation, some years back. Survey and recovery mission, inland. Joint tasking. It went into the mountains in the spring and it did not come out in the autumn, and by the following spring it had never existed at all. No report. No inquiry. No graves with names on them. There is a wreck at Kestrel Shoal, Captain, because one of its boats came back down the river and went into the sea, and the navy decided the sea should keep it."

Ansel held the mug and didn't drink. "I dive a freighter."

"You dive a freighter that was carrying that operation's equipment out of the country when it sank. Radios. Survey instruments. Pay ledgers." He watched her over the rim of his own mug. "You found something last week. I know because you changed your pattern. Fourteen months of methodical grid work, and then suddenly you're diving one spot, over and over, and your fuel purchases double, and you start asking at the chandler about deck cargo straps." He shrugged, an elegant little surrender. "I read fuel receipts the way you read silt."

The floor panel in the forward berth might as well have been glass.

"What did Hollis tell you?" Corvane asked. Gently now. The gentleness was the loudest thing in the wheelhouse.

"Nothing."

"Captain." He set his mug down on the chart shelf with a click. "I'm going to do you the courtesy of not pretending this is a conversation between strangers. Hollis was the radio operator on that expedition. He was the only man who came off the mountain. Do you understand what that means? Everything that happened up there — every decision, every order, every name — it went through his headset. And for eleven years he has been carrying it around this coast like a man carrying a bomb with a slow fuse, waiting for someone to hand it to. And now he's dying, and he's handed it to a salvage diver with debts, and I am standing in your wheelhouse asking you, politely, as one professional to another, to tell me what he gave you."

"Or what?"

The word was out of her mouth before she could weigh it, and she watched him receive it with something that was almost — almost — approval.

"Or nothing," Corvane said. "That's the honest answer. I'm not going to arrest you, Captain. There's nothing to arrest you for. Diving a permitted wreck. Feeding a vagrant. I could impound your boat on a paperwork technicality, and we both know your paperwork would survive the afternoon." He pushed off the shelf and stood, and up close his eyes were the exact color of the water over the wreck, forty feet down, where the light stopped meaning anything. "But I'll tell you what happens instead. The snows come early this year. Everyone says so. And a woman with a bad shoulder, a dead man's map, and no mountain experience goes up into a range that eats survey teams with radios and pack trains and army boots. And in the spring, if anyone thinks to look, there's a story about a diver who went hiking. That's all. No report. No inquiry."

The bilge pump cycled. The coffee had gone cold in her hands, and she hadn't tasted a sip.

"You know about the map," she said.

"I know Hollis drew maps the way other men pray." He moved to the transom, unhurried, and paused at the top of the ladder with one hand on the rail. "Here's my last courtesy, Captain, and then I'll leave you to your dive. Whatever he promised you — and he promised everyone something, it's how he survived — understand that he wasn't selling you a treasure. He was handing you a funeral. His own. He wanted it delivered somewhere it could be read out loud, and he didn't care who had to die carrying it." He looked at her, and for one unguarded half-second the flat gray water in his eyes moved, and there was something underneath it that looked almost like drowning. "I knew those men, Captain. I grew up on their letters. Burn it. Burn it and dive your wreck and pay your debts and live."

Then he was down the ladder, and the launch was pulling away, and the wake came in a long slow minute later and rolled the *Magpie* twice, like a hand turning over a stone to see what was under it.

* * *

Ansel stood at the rail until the launch was a gray rumor again, going south toward the harbor, toward the port where her creditors kept their ledgers and her life was measured in days.

Thirty days. That had been the number this morning, and this morning it had been the whole sky.

She went below, knelt, and lifted the floor panel. The strongbox sat in its nest of shadow, salt-rimed, stamped with a crest she still couldn't read — a compass rose broken by a peak, and beneath it a number that meant nothing except to the men who'd carried it up a mountain and not come back.

Corvane had told her to burn it. A navy officer had stood in her wheelhouse, spent an hour, and told her to destroy the thing he'd come to find. Which meant either it was worthless, or it was worth more than he could afford to let her learn — and a man doesn't spend an hour being polite about worthless.

She closed the panel. Her hands were steady, which surprised her.

The debt was thirty days. Corvane was now. And somewhere behind her, back up the coast, a dying old man had looked at her with his bad lungs and his bad leg and chosen her — out of every hull in every harbor — to carry his bomb over the mountain.

She was pulling her dive suit on, because the wreck was still there and silt didn't care about navies, when she saw the small boat.

It came out of the haze to the north, a dinghy with a lugsail, running close-hauled and competent, and at its tiller sat a woman with a canvas map case strapped across her chest like a bandolier, watching the *Magpie* with the fixed attention of someone who had been looking for her a long time.

Ansel stood on her own deck in a half-zipped dry suit and understood that the day had only just begun with her.

The dinghy turned toward her transom.
