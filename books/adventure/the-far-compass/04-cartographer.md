## 4. Cartographer

Mara Okonkwo had been watching the boat for three days.

Not the woman — the boat. A salvage tug called *Ledger's End*, which was either a joke or a confession, moored at the end of the fuel dock where the harbor master didn't like to send customers because the pilings groaned. Mara had learned to read boats the way other people read faces, and this one had a face like a man who'd stopped expecting good news: rust blooming along the waterline, a crane arm patched with welds of two different colors, and a name board repainted so many times the letters sat on top of each other like old arguments.

The woman was harder to read. Ansel Vasquez came down the dock every morning before light with a thermos and a diver's walk — that rolling, careful gait of someone whose body had been negotiated with for years rather than trusted. She worked on the boat alone. She dove alone. And twice now she had come up from the water too fast, too early, hauling something wrapped in canvas that she stowed below without looking at it twice, which meant she was looking at it constantly.

Mara knew what she was hauling. Not its contents — its shape. A strongbox, expedition-stamped, pulled from a wreck the charts marked with a navy exclusion ring and no name. Six years of her life had gone into learning everything there was to know about that ring.

She waited until the fourth morning, when Ansel surfaced from a dive gray-lipped and shaking, and walked down the dock carrying a paper bag of coffee and bread rolls like an offering, because sometimes the fastest way onto a stranger's boat is through their hands.

"You're diving that wreck on an empty tank schedule," Mara said, standing at the gangway. "Your reserve gauge is lying to you. I can see it from here."

Ansel looked up from her gear, and her eyes did something Mara would think about later — they went flat and assessing, like a shopkeeper pricing a stranger, then flicked to the exits. Dock behind Mara. Water beside her. Nothing else.

"I don't take passengers," Ansel said.

"I'm not offering to be one." Mara held up the bag. "I brought breakfast. I brought information. And I brought six years of work you're going to want to see."

"Six years of what?"

"The Meridian Expedition." Mara said the name plainly, watching for it — the flinch, the blankness, the tell. "1941. Eleven people went into the range above Kettle Pass with survey equipment and government funding, and eleven people never came out. The navy sealed the file, sank the supply ship, and paid off every family quiet enough to stay bought. Except nobody ever found the bodies. Nobody ever found the depot. And nobody," she said, stepping onto the gangway before Ansel could decide otherwise, "ever explained why a salvage diver with debts is pulling boxes stamped with their crest out of a wreck your government officially says doesn't exist."

Ansel hadn't moved. But she hadn't told her to leave, either, and in Mara's experience that was as good as an invitation.

* * *

Below decks, the cabin smelled of diesel, wet neoprene, and coffee reheated past all decency. Mara set the bag on the chart table and watched Ansel hang back by the companionway, arms crossed, positioned exactly between Mara and the door.

"You went through my effects," Ansel said.

"I went through public records. There's a difference, and the difference is I didn't have to break anything." Mara took out the rolls, set one near Ansel's elbow, and opened her own satchel. "Hollis. Elias Hollis, radio operator, Meridian Expedition, listed dead 1941, unlisted alive 1987 when he filed a claim on a mining parcel above the cloud line under a false name. He died last week on your deck. Before he died, he gave you something."

The silence stretched long enough that Mara heard the pilings groan below them.

"You've been following me since the dock," Ansel said finally. "How."

"Because I've been following this expedition since I was twenty-three years old, and you're the first person in six years who's touched anything real." Mara pulled a folder from her satchel and laid it open on the table. Maps. Dozens of them, hand-drawn, layered, annotated in three colors of ink — survey grids over aerial photographs over sketches copied from archives, and running through all of it, a route line drawn and redrawn so many times the paper had gone soft. "Six years. Two universities fired me for pursuing it. One archive burned with half my sources in it, which I do not believe was an accident. I reconstructed the route from pay ledgers, letters home, a surveyor's field notes sold at estate auction, and one photograph of a glacier taken from a ridge that let me triangulate the rest." She tapped the route line. "I know every pass they crossed. I know where they camped. I know where the supply line broke. What I don't know — what nobody knows — is where they were actually going, because the destination was cut out of every document before the files were sealed."

"And you think Hollis gave me that."

"I think Hollis *was* that. He was the radio operator. He heard every transmission. He kept the coordinates in his head for forty-six years because writing them down got ten people killed." Mara leaned forward. "So here's my offer, and I'll only make it once. You have the map. I have the way. Without me, you'll be lucky to find the foothills before the snows close the pass, and Corvane will find you before that."

Ansel went very still. "You know about Corvane."

"I know Lieutenant Corvane boarded a patrol launch yesterday and asked the harbor master for your berth number. I know his father's name is on the expedition roster as commanding officer. And I know that men like him don't chase salvage divers across a mountain range for fun." Mara sat back. "You're being hunted, Ansel. I'm offering to be the one person standing between you and the terrain, which is the only thing that's killed more expeditions than navies have."

For a long moment Ansel said nothing. Then she moved — not toward Mara, but toward the locker beneath the bunk, and came back with a canvas roll she dropped on the table without ceremony. It wasn't the map itself; it was a diving slate, water-marked, with a few lines copied onto it in Ansel's blunt hand. Coordinates. A bearing. A phrase in Hollis's handwriting, transcribed: *ABOVE THE CLOUD LINE, WHERE THE ICE REMEMBERS.*

"That's all you get until I trust you," Ansel said. "And I don't trust you."

"Good," Mara said. "Distrust keeps people alive in the mountains. Trust gets them killed." She pulled the slate closer and studied the bearing, and felt the six years compress into a single point behind her sternum. "This is real. This is the missing piece." She looked up. "Here are my terms. We go together. You navigate by the map, I navigate by the terrain, and if they disagree, we stop and argue it out instead of dying about it. Tam—"

"Who's Tam?"

"A mechanic I haven't recruited yet. Sixteen, runs engines better than men twice his age, and wants off this coast so badly he'll work for passage. We'll need him. Your boat isn't making the river crossing to the trailhead, and neither is mine, because I don't have one." She folded her hands. "In exchange: whatever's in that cache, I get to read it first. All of it. Before you sell it, burn it, or trade it to Corvane, whichever you're planning."

Something moved behind Ansel's eyes — not quite guilt, but its neighbor. "What makes you think I'm planning any of those?"

"Because everyone who holds that map is planning one of those. Hollis spent forty-six years not deciding. I'd rather know which way you'll jump before we're roped together on ice." Mara stood, gathered her folder, and left the slate where it lay. "You have until the tide turns to say yes. After that I start negotiating with Corvane instead, and believe me, I'd rather not."

"That's not much of a threat. He'd just arrest you."

"He'd just *believe* me," Mara said. "Which is worse."

At the companionway she paused, one hand on the frame, and made herself say the true thing, because alliances built on anything less didn't survive altitude:

"For what it's worth — I'm not after treasure. I'm after the truth of what happened up there. If the map leads to gold, you can keep the gold. If it leads to answers, those are mine." She met Ansel's eyes. "Do we have terms?"

Ansel looked at her for a long time — at the folder, at the six years of ink, at the coffee going cold between them.

"Tide turns in an hour," she said. "Bring your maps. And bring the boy."

* * *

Mara was halfway down the dock when she saw the patrol launch come around the breakwater, white hull, navy grey superstructure, moving at the unhurried speed of a vessel that owned the water it stood on. At its rail, a figure in a pressed uniform raised binoculars — not toward the harbor, but toward the *Ledger's End*, toward the small lit window of a cabin where a woman was already moving below decks, hiding things.

Mara stood very still in the rain and understood, with the clarity of someone who has spent six years being right about terrible things, that the negotiation was over and the clock had started.

She turned and walked faster.
