## 5. What Hollis Carried

Hollis had been dead for six hours when I finally made myself open his bag.

I'd covered him with the tarp and sat in the cockpit drinking cold coffee while the harbor went about its business around us — gulls arguing over fish heads, a fuel barge chugging past with its wake slapping the hull like a slow hand. Nobody looked twice at my boat. A dead man under a tarp on a salvage vessel was nobody's business but mine, and I intended to keep it that way until I decided what to do with him.

The coroner's office wanted paperwork. The harbormaster wanted fees. Corvane's people, if they came looking, would want everything else.

So: his effects first. Know what you're carrying before you carry it. That was diving logic, and it applied just as well to dead prospectors.

His bag was army surplus, older than Tam would have been if Tam existed yet — which he didn't, not to me, not for another week or so. Canvas gone soft as cloth, straps replaced with wire, buckles rusted to sculptures. It smelled of kerosene and wool and something underneath both, something mineral, like the inside of a cave.

Mara Okonkwo sat across from me at the little table, her hands flat on the surface, not touching anything. She'd been aboard since dawn, having talked her way past my better judgment somewhere around midnight. Six years reconstructing a route that officially never happened. She hadn't slept either. Her eyes had the wet-bright look of someone running on conviction instead of rest.

"You should let me catalog it," she said. "Provenance matters."

"He's not an artifact. He's a man who died on my floor."

"Which is exactly why provenance matters." She didn't move her hands. "If we're going to do what he asked, we need to know what *it* is. You promised to carry it over the range. Carrying blind is how people die in mountains."

She wasn't wrong, and I hated her a little for being right in that even, patient voice, like she was reading coordinates off a chart.

I unbuckled the first strap. It gave with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

* * *

The bag held a life packed down to essentials, the way men pack when they've been leaving places for forty years.

A bedroll, oilcloth-wrapped. A cook kit black with decades of fire. A rifle, bolt-action, bore still oiled — Hollis might have been dying but he'd kept his tools honest. A tin of tea, a tin of salt, a tin of hard candy gone cloudy with age. Spare socks. A wool coat rolled tight.

And paper. Paper everywhere, folded into every pocket and seam, because Hollis had been a man who wrote things down the way other men breathed.

Receipts from outfitters in towns whose names I half knew. A laundry ticket, twenty years old. A photograph of a woman standing in front of a building with a sign I couldn't read, her hand raised mid-wave, caught forever between greeting and goodbye.

And at the bottom, wrapped in the oilcloth with the bedroll where no damp could reach it, two things that stopped my hands.

The map — the one he'd pressed into my palms last night with fingers like winter branches, the one I'd promised to carry or burn. I set it aside without opening it. I already knew its weight.

Beside it, a journal. Bound in gray canvas, string-tied, fat as a brick. The cover was stamped with a crest I recognized now, because I'd seen it three days ago stamped into the lid of a strongbox at the bottom of a wreck the navy forbade anyone to discuss: a compass rose crossed by a mountain peak, and beneath it, letters worn nearly smooth.

*M.E.R.*

Mara saw the crest and made a sound I'd only heard from divers who've found a body. Small. Involuntary. Final.

"That's them," she said. "That's the Meridian Expedition."

"I don't know what that is."

"No." She exhaled slowly. "You wouldn't. That was rather the point of what happened to them."

* * *

Here's what I knew going in, which was almost nothing:

Three days ago I'd dived a wreck off the point at first light, against every rule I pretend to follow, because the navy pays salvage fees through intermediaries who don't ask questions and I owed eleven thousand to a man named Dessel who'd started sending people to sit in my wheelhouse. The wreck was old, war-era hull, sitting wrong on the shelf like it had been put there rather than lost there. Sealed hatches. Navy stenciling painted over with civilian marks, badly.

In the hold I found a strongbox. Not gold — I know what gold feels like through gloves, the specific dense promise of it. This was documents-weight, paper-weight, the weight of things somebody wanted sunk. Stamped with the compass rose and the peak. I'd brought up one box, hidden it under my bilge boards, and told myself I'd figure out what it meant later.

Then Hollis had washed into my life on the evening tide, coughing blood into my cockpit, talking about a cache above the cloud line.

Now Mara was telling me the rest, in the clipped way of someone who has rehearsed a story for six years and never expected an audience.

"The Meridian Expedition left the coast nineteen years ago. Eleven members. Surveyors, geologists, a military liaison. Officially they were mapping the high passes for a road that was never built. Unofficially—" she spread her hands "—nobody official will say. They crossed the range in late summer. In October, the navy declared them overdue. In November, they declared them lost. By December, the file was sealed. No recovery mission. No inquiry. Eleven names, one line in a registry, and then nothing."

"And you spent six years on nothing."

"I spent six years on the *shape* of nothing." Her finger traced an invisible line across the table. "Their last confirmed position. Their planned route. Weather records for that season. If you lay it all out, there's a gap — a hole in the record exactly the size of a decision somebody made and didn't want read. I've been trying to reconstruct what filled it ever since."

I looked at the journal. Gray canvas, string-tied, patient as a mine.

"Hollis," I said. "Was he on it?"

Mara went very still. "Why do you ask?"

"Because he talked like a man giving directions to his own house." I picked up the journal. It was heavier than it looked, or maybe I was just tired. "Because he said *above the cloud line, third moraine, you'll see the cairn from the glacier* like he'd seen it. And because—" I turned the journal over, and there, inked inside the back cover in a young man's careful hand, was a name and a rank.

*Signalman T. Hollis, M.E.R., Radio Section.*

Mara closed her eyes. When she opened them, six years of reconstruction had rearranged themselves behind them into something new and terrible.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, you're the survivor."

* * *

We read it in shifts, because neither of us could stand to read it alone and neither of us would admit that.

Hollis had been twenty-three when the expedition crossed the range. His handwriting was beautiful then — a radio operator's hand, trained for clarity, every letter formed like it might be the last thing anyone understood. The early entries were almost cheerful. Ration weights. Antenna repairs. A joke about the cook's bread that ran three entries long, an entire serialized feud.

The mountain came on gradually, the way weather does in a log written daily. Passes closing early. A bridge out. A detour that added nine days. Then the entries shortened. Then the dates stopped matching the weather, because Hollis was writing from memory, catching up, writing by lamplight in whatever shelter they'd found.

On October 9th, the handwriting changed forever.

*The liaison has ordered us off the ridge. Says the weather window is closing and command wants the survey terminated. Half the party says push for the col anyway — we're close, we can see it. The other half says go down. The captain is siding with the ridge. I have never heard grown men argue like this. I am keeping the set warm. Whatever happens, someone should have the record.*

October 11th:

*They split. God help me, they split. Eight going up, three going down with the sick ones. I stayed with the set because the set stays with the majority and the majority went up. I did not think about it. You cannot think about it and still function.*

October 14th:

*Storm. Four days now. The set draws too much power. We are rationing transmissions to six minutes a day. Last contact with the lower party was two days ago. They were making good time down the valley.*

October 19th:

*No contact with the lower party. The liaison says they may be out of range. I keep transmitting anyway. It costs us battery we need. I do not care. Someone has to be listening for them.*

October 22nd:

*I am the only one left who can walk.*

He described it the way he must have lived it — in fragments, in inventory, in the flat voice of a man reporting facts because feelings would kill him faster than the cold. The eight who went up had made the col. Two had come back down for the others and none of the four returned. The storm had taken the camp piece by piece. The liaison — and here Hollis's pen pressed so hard it tore the page — *the liaison took the last of the fuel and the good tent and went down alone on the 21st, saying he would bring help. He did not say he was taking the pay ledger with him.*

I looked up. Mara had both hands over her mouth.

"The ledger," she whispered. "He took the *ledger*."

"Keep reading," I said, though my voice wasn't steady either.

October 30th:

*Rescue came today. Or what the navy calls rescue. A helicopter, one pass, no landing — weather, they said later. They dropped supplies that landed a kilometer away in the wrong valley. I watched the crate disappear into white and I laughed until I cried and then I walked toward where it fell because what else is there.*

November 2nd:

*I found the crate. Inside: rations for four, a signal lamp, and a sealed order instructing any survivor to proceed to the coast and report to naval intelligence, where the expedition's records would be collected and classified. Classified. Eleven men. I am the eleventh. I am what gets classified.*

The last entries were months apart, the handwriting aging decade by decade, the young man's clarity dissolving into an old man's scrawl.

*I went down the mountain. I reported. They took my logbook copy and thanked me and asked what I remembered of the liaison's departure. I told them. They wrote it down and then the man who wrote it tore the page out and burned it in front of me and said, "Signalman, the Meridian Expedition was lost to weather. There was no liaison decision. There was no abandonment. Do you understand?" I said yes. I have been saying yes for nineteen years.*

*But I kept this journal. And I kept the route. And above the cloud line, at the third moraine, there is a depot the eight of them stocked before the split — emergency stores, sealed, and in it the duplicate records, because the captain was a careful man and duplicated everything. Pay ledgers. Orders. Letters home, held for the return. Everything the liaison thought he'd buried by walking away with the originals.*

*It is all still there. Ice keeps better than archives.*

*Someone should read it aloud. Before I die. Before everyone who remembers their faces dies. Someone should stand on that mountain and say their names into the wind that took them.*

*If you are reading this, I am sorry. I am sorry I stayed with the set. I am sorry I walked away. I am sorry I said yes.*

*Carry it over the range, or burn it. Those are the only mercies left.*

*— T.H.*

* * *

For a long time neither of us spoke. The harbor sounds came through the cabin bulkhead, ordinary and obscene. Somewhere a radio played a song about somebody leaving on a train.

"So it's not gold," I said finally. My voice sounded like someone else's. "The cache. It's evidence."

"It's testimony." Mara wiped her face with the heel of her hand, briskly, like fixing a chart error. "Nineteen years of it. Ledgers proving the liaison abandoned his own party. Letters those men wrote home that their families were told were lost. Hollis carried this—" she touched the journal "—across nineteen years and however many thousand miles, waiting for someone to take it up that mountain."

"Why up? Why not just publish it down here?"

"Because down here it can be confiscated, discredited, filed. Because the navy sealed the wreck and sealed the file and would seal this too." She tapped the map, still folded beside the journal. "But a depot above the cloud line, opened by civilians, witnessed, documented, *read aloud* — that's different. That can't be un-happened. Hollis isn't asking us to retrieve treasure. He's asking us to make it public where no one can reach it."

I turned the journal over in my hands. Signalman T. Hollis. Twenty-three years old, staying with the radio because the radio stays with the majority. Nineteen years of saying yes.

Last night, dying in my cockpit, he'd given me a choice: carry it or burn it. I'd promised both, meaning neither, the way you promise drunk strangers and crying children — words shaped like comfort, hollow at the center.

Now the words had weight.

Carry it, and I was carrying a declaration of war against the navy — against Corvane, whose polite questions on my deck yesterday had been the gentlest thing about him, against whatever machine had torn pages out of reports and burned them in front of a broken kid thirty years ago. Carry it, and Dessel's eleven thousand became a rounding error in my problems.

Burn it, and eleven men stayed lost to weather. Families stayed lied to. Hollis's nineteen years of silence stayed permanent, and the mountain kept its secret the way the sea keeps its wrecks — perfectly, and without mercy.

Burn it, and nobody else died.

That was the part I kept circling. I'd read enough of the journal to know what the mountain did to parties that split, to men who stayed, to men who walked down alone. Corvane had a patrol launch and, I suspected, more than that. The pass closed in twelve days. My shoulder was held together with tape and stubbornness.

Burning it would be mercy. For Hollis, maybe. For me. For whoever came after me.

"It would be easier," I said aloud, "to burn it."

Mara looked at me for a long moment. She didn't flinch and she didn't argue, and somehow that was worse than either.

"Yes," she said. "It would. Now you understand why he gave you the choice instead of the order. He knew exactly how easy it would be." She gathered the journal gently, the way you handle something that used to be a person. "He also knew that some things get heavier the longer you carry them, and some things get lighter. He spent nineteen years finding out which kind this was." She stood, cradling the gray canvas book against her chest. "So. Diver. Which are you carrying?"

Through the cabin window, out past the breakwater, a gray shape sat at anchor where it had no reason to be. Patrol launch. Navy blue water, patient as a mine.

I looked at it, and at the map, and at the woman holding a dead man's voice in her arms.

"We provision tomorrow," I said. "First light."

Mara nodded once — and then her eyes went past my shoulder to the porthole, and went wide.

"Ansel," she said quietly. "There's a dinghy coming alongside. Two men. Civilian clothes, wrong posture."

I already had my hand on the bilge boards, over the strongbox, doing arithmetic I didn't like.

Boots hit my deck.
