## 6. Last Port

The rain found them before the harbor did.

Mara felt it first as a change in the wind's texture — a softness, a weight, the air going thick the way it did when the sea was loading itself up for weeks of violence. Then Ansel's boat came around the headland and the rain arrived all at once, not falling so much as arriving, a grey curtain drawn across the whole coast. By the time they tied off at the fuel dock in Port Merrow, the decks were running with water and Mara had stopped bothering to wipe her face.

"Charming place," she said.

Ansel didn't answer. She was looking at the town the way she looked at sonar returns — reading it for what lay underneath. Port Merrow sat on its shelf of rock above the harbor like something that had been set down there by accident: a single street, a chandlery, two taverns, a customs shed with a roof patched in three different colors of tin. Fishing boats crowded the inner moorings, riding low, their owners nowhere in sight because no one sane worked in weather like this.

"It'll do," Ansel said finally. "It's ugly enough that nobody looks twice at anyone."

"You'd be surprised how much I've learned about nobody looking twice." Mara pulled her hood up. "It never works."

The plan was simple, which was good, because simple plans were the only kind that survived contact with either weather or governments. They needed food for twelve days on the mountain. Fuel for the truck they hadn't yet arranged. Rope, stove fuel, cold-weather gear none of them owned, because none of them had planned to be climbing anything above the cloud line this season. And they needed it fast, quietly, and without leaving a paper trail a certain lieutenant could follow.

Mara counted the days again while she watched the rain hammer the harbor. It was becoming a habit, counting. The monsoon was ten days out — maybe less; the harbormaster's board said the fleet was being called in early, which meant the weather service knew something the calendar didn't. The pass closed in twelve. Somewhere behind those numbers, closing like a hand, was Corvane.

Ten. Twelve. She did the arithmetic the way you do it when you already know the answer is bad. Two days of margin, if everything went perfectly. Nothing ever went perfectly. Something always went wrong, and wrong cost a day, and a day was the difference between a mountain crossing and a winter trapped in it.

The clock wasn't ticking anymore. The clock was walking toward them, patient, wearing the weather as a coat.

* * *

The chandlery smelled of rope tar and kerosene, and the man behind the counter looked at their money more than he looked at their faces, which suited Mara fine. She bought in the voice she used for border towns — flat, bored, local enough. Rice, lentils, salt, tea, a block of tallow, dried fish packed in oil. Ansel hauled crates while Mara negotiated, and between them they spent most of what Hollis's coat pockets had contained, minus what the fuel dock had already eaten.

It was in the chandlery that Mara saw the noticeboard.

She'd been scanning it out of habit — harbors ran on noticeboards the way churches ran on hymnals — and her eyes went past the crew-wanted slips and the missing-cat flyers and snagged on a sheet of navy stock paper, crisp and new against the curling notices around it. Typed. Signed. A stamp she recognized now whether she wanted to or not.

She read it twice, standing very still, rainwater dripping off her hem onto the floorboards.

*Seeking information regarding three persons believed traveling together.* The descriptions were good. Better than good. Hers was almost flattering: *female, thirties, dark-skinned, foreign accent, carries rolled charts, walks with purpose.* Ansel's was thinner — *harbor woman, salvage diver, vessel under thirty feet, name unconfirmed* — but the third line was the one that made Mara's stomach tighten:

*A boy, approximately sixteen, mechanic by trade, may be seeking work.*

Corvane wasn't just hunting them. He was hunting who they might become.

"Mara." Ansel's voice, low, at her shoulder. "I see it."

"He knows we're provisioning here."

"He knew before we did. That launch didn't turn back because it lost us." Ansel took the crate from her hands and set it down, deliberate, buying them both a second of stillness. "We take what we have and we go. Back door, not front."

"There's always a back door in a port town. There's also always someone watching it." Mara turned from the board, casual, a customer done browsing. "He posted the boy, Ansel. He doesn't know Tam exists yet. He's guessing. Which means he's guessing well, and we need to move faster than his guess."

Ansel's jaw tightened. "Then we move."

They were halfway to the door when the back room curtain moved and a voice said, "You'll want to buy the lantern too."

Mara's hand found the knife at her belt before her brain caught up. The curtain swung aside on a narrow storeroom and a figure stepped out of the shadows between grain sacks — small, wiry, grease-blackened hands, a cap pulled low over eyes that were older than the face they lived in. Sixteen, maybe. Maybe fifteen. The kind of thin that came from deciding between eating and escaping.

"I heard your names on the board," the kid said. "And I heard mine. Funny thing, my name being on it when I haven't even met you yet."

Ansel put herself slightly forward, the way she did. "Who are you?"

"Depends who's asking." The kid grinned, quick and crooked. "Board says a mechanic. Board says you're in a hurry. Board says the navy wants you, which — no offense — makes you interesting instead of frightening, because anybody the navy wants this badly is going somewhere worth going." The grin flickered, and underneath it was something rawer. "I'm Tam. I fix engines. I've been fixing engines in this port since I was twelve, and I'm done with this port."

"We're not hiring," Ansel said.

"You're climbing a mountain in eleven days with gear you bought an hour ago and no one who knows a carburetor from a crankcase." Tam ticked it off on greasy fingers. "You've got a truck arranged with Ovid Harl at the yard, and Ovid's truck dies every third hill. You need me. Also—" the grin again, but careful now, testing load-bearing ice "—you need someone the navy hasn't got a real file on. I'm a rumor. Rumors travel light."

Mara watched Ansel weigh it. She knew the weighing herself — the arithmetic of strangers, the cost-benefit of trust in a market where trust was the only currency that couldn't be counterfeited. Six years of reconstructing a dead expedition had taught her that crews were made or broken in moments exactly like this one, in storerooms, in rain, by people who said *take me* for reasons they only half admitted.

"Why a mountain?" Ansel asked. Not *why us.* Why a mountain. Good question. Mara filed it away next to her growing list of reasons to keep watching this woman.

Tam's shoulders dropped half an inch. "Because there's nothing left down here to run from that I haven't already run from." A beat. "And because the board says you pay."

"The board lies," Mara said. "But we pay. Food, a share of whatever's at the top, and passage out after. That's the whole contract. Break it and I leave you wherever we are."

"Fair terms." Tam stuck out a hand, grease and all. "For the record, I'd have come along free. But don't tell the board."

* * *

Ovid Harl's truck yard sat at the landward edge of town, where the road gave up on ambition and became a track. The truck itself was a flatbed older than Tam, canvas-backed, with an engine that caught on the fourth try and settled into a rhythm like a man arguing with himself. Tam walked around it once, laid both palms on the hood like a doctor taking a pulse, and said, "She'll make the foothills. After that she's a prayer."

"A prayer will do," Ansel said.

They loaded through the afternoon while the rain kept coming, steady as bureaucracy. Mara drew the route on the tailgate in chalk dust — the coast road north to the last bridge, then the valley, then the green stair of the foothills where the map began to mean something. She marked the distances in hours, not miles, because hours were honest. Eleven days to the pass. Ten until the monsoon sealed the coast. The chalk numbers looked small and insufficient, like a fence around a field the size of the sky.

It was Tam, hauling the last crate, who said what Mara had been thinking.

"This says nine days of food for four people."

"Three people and a mechanic," Mara said.

"Three people eat like three people." Tam set the crate down. "I've been hungry before. I'm saying we should know we're saying it."

Ansel looked up from the rope she was coiling. Something passed across her face — not guilt, exactly; something harder to name, the look of a woman doing sums she didn't want done. "We resupply at Kettle Pass," she said. "There's a station on the maps."

"The maps are six years old," Mara said quietly. "The station may be a memory."

"Then we walk faster."

That was the whole conversation, and it was enough, and it wasn't, and Mara climbed into the cab and let the engine noise fill up the space where the argument would have gone.

They ate that night in the cab of the truck, parked in the yard among Ovid's rusting herd, because a tavern meant faces and faces meant recognition and the board in the chandlery had made recognition expensive. Rain drummed the canvas. Tam talked — about engines, mostly, about a motorcycle rebuilt from scrap at thirteen, about a father who drank and a mother who left in whatever order was worse — and Mara listened with half her attention and gave the other half to the map, working it by lantern light, tracing the contour lines the way you'd trace a scar.

Six years. Six years since the surveyor she'd been going to marry walked into these mountains with nineteen other people and became a rumor, a file, a sealed navy operation. Six years of drawing other people's routes for money while her own route sat in a drawer, unfinished, unspeakable. Now she had Hollis's map in an oilcloth sleeve against her ribs and a diver with a debt and a runaway with grease under their nails, and the strangest part — the part she wouldn't say out loud yet — was that it felt less like a hunt than a homecoming.

"You're smiling at the map," Tam said. "Maps don't usually do that to people."

"This one owes me an apology."

"Don't they all."

Ansel slept in the truck bed with her boots on, one hand near the knife, a woman who trusted weather more than walls. Mara watched her breathe for a moment longer than she meant to, then killed the lantern.

* * *

The knock came an hour before dawn.

Three knocks, spaced, polite — the rhythm of someone official or someone pretending not to be. Mara was awake before the second one, knife out of the sheath, and through the cab's fogged glass she saw the lantern-light at the yard gate and the shape of a uniform under it. Navy blue, water-darkened, patient.

"Harbor office," the shape called. "Routine check. Vessel and crew registration for any outbound traffic. Monsoon regulations."

Beside her, Tam had gone very still. On the truck bed, Ansel's breathing hadn't changed, which meant she was awake, which meant she'd been awake the whole time.

"Yard's locked till morning," Mara called back, keeping her voice rough with sleep. "Come back at seven."

A pause. Rain on canvas. Then, pleasantly: "Understood. Though I should mention — there are persons of interest reported in the district. If you see anyone matching the posted descriptions, the harbor office pays for accurate reports. Pays well."

The lantern swung away. Boots on gravel. The gate clanked.

Nobody moved for a full minute. Then Ansel sat up in the bed, silent, and put her eye to a gap in the canvas, and Mara joined her at the cab window, and together they watched the patrol launch's searchlight sweep the harbor mouth, slow as a lighthouse, painting the rain silver as it went.

"He's not searching," Ansel said softly. "He's herding. Every check, every notice, every paid informant — he's building fences. Funneling us to the one road out of here."

"Which means he knows the one road out of here," Mara said. "Which means the pass road has eyes on it already."

"Or he wants us to think it does."

"Same effect." Mara rolled the map closed. Her hands were steady; she noted that with distant professional interest, the way you note a fact about someone else. "We can't wait for daylight now. We roll tonight, before the fences close, and we drive the first stretch dark. Tam—"

But Tam was already in the driver's seat, key in the ignition, grinning back at them through the gloom with that quick crooked grin that was starting to feel less like a stranger's and more like a crew's.

"Been ready since the chandlery," Tam said. "One condition. When we get where we're going — I want to know what we're actually carrying. Nobody climbs twelve days for cargo they're not allowed to ask about."

Rain hammered the canvas. Down at the harbor mouth, the searchlight swept past the yard gate and kept going, and the clock in Mara's chest — ten days, twelve days, less — took another step toward them, patient, inevitable, wearing the storm like a coat.

"Fair condition," Ansel said, and turned the key.

The engine caught on the second try, and Port Merrow fell away behind them into the rain.
