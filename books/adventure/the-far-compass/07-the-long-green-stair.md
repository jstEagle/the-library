## 7. The Long Green Stair

The foothills began where the road gave up.

Ansel felt the change in her boots first — the packed clay of the coast giving way to something softer, greedier, ground that sucked at each step like it wanted a toll. Then in her shoulders, which had been wrong since the river of her old life, the salvage yards, the cold water that had made her. The pack straps found the bruised places and pressed, patient as a creditor.

Behind her, Tam was singing.

He'd been singing for an hour, some tuneless thing he'd invented himself, all rhythm and no melody, the way a boy keeps his courage up when he doesn't want anyone to know he's counting how far they've come. Mara walked ahead with the compass and said nothing about the singing, which Ansel had decided was its own kind of kindness.

The green went up forever. That was the thing nobody told you about mountains from below — you thought you were looking at a wall, but you were looking at a staircase, thousands of steps of it, terraced by water and time into folds of jungle so dense it seemed the land itself was holding its breath. Mist moved through the valleys in slow ropes. Somewhere above it all, invisible, was the pass. Above that, the snows. Above everything, Hollis's coordinates, folded now in oilcloth inside Ansel's jacket, against her ribs, where she could feel them like a second heartbeat.

*Carry it over the range before the snows, or destroy it.* She'd promised both. She still meant neither. She just kept walking.

"Water stop," Mara called back, and they gathered under a fig tree whose roots had cracked an old survey marker — a rusted brass pin stamped with letters Ansel didn't recognize but Mara did. She watched Mara's thumb move over it once, quickly, like touching a scar.

"How far today?" Tam asked, shrugging off his pack and immediately regretting it, judging by his face.

"Mara says eight miles," Ansel said. "Mara lies."

"Seven and a half," Mara said, not looking up from the map she wasn't using, because she didn't need it. Six years of reconstruction lived in her head; Ansel had seen her navigate by memory through fog on the boat ride north, calling bearings to herself like prayers. "And the last mile is the stair."

"The what?"

"You'll see."

Ansel drank, checked the supply rope — sixty meters of braided line they'd strung between packs for the river crossings to come, coiled now across Tam's shoulders like a huge pale snake — and put the world away again. One step. Then another. The ledger of her debt was thirty days old tomorrow, or twenty-nine, or however many were left; out here the numbers had gone soft. Out here there was only uphill.

* * *

They hit the stair at midafternoon, and Mara hadn't lied, exactly. It just wasn't stairs the way Tam had hoped, with edges and railings. It was a track cut into the hillside by hands long dead — expedition hands, maybe, or older — switchbacking up a face so steep the trees grew sideways out of it, their roots gripping rock like fingers. The mist came up to meet them, cool and smelling of crushed leaves, and the world below disappeared, and there was only the next step, and the one after that.

Tam went first, because he was lightest and because he'd argued he should, and the rope went with him, looped over his shoulder in careful coils. Ansel climbed behind him. Mara took the rear with the pack containing most of the food, which Ansel had protested and lost.

For two hours it was fine. For two hours it was terrible. Both were true.

Then the mist thickened all at once, the way water goes from clear to soup, and Tam's voice drifted back thinner: "There's a gap here — there's a whole section gone, the track's washed out—"

"Stop," Ansel said. "Don't move."

"I'm not moving. I'm *looking*—"

"Tam." She heard it in her own voice, the diver's voice, flat and absolute, the voice she used on crew when the bottom was bad. He stopped.

She edged forward along the cut until she could see it: four feet of trail simply missing, sheared away into green air, the slope beyond dropping away into cloud with nothing visible below — no trees, no ground, just white. Rain-slick stone ended at his heels. His boots were planted on a slab the size of a door, and the slab was weeping water, and the boy stood there with sixty meters of rope on his shoulders like a man carrying his own gallows.

"I can jump it," he said. "It's not that far."

"It's far enough." She looked at the gap, then at the rope. "Uncoil ten meters. Clip it to your harness. Leave the rest coiled on the slab — I'll come get it."

"Why can't I just—"

"*Because if you go over the edge,*" she said, and the words came out colder than she intended, "*the rope comes with you, and then it's three of us on this mountain with no line, and I am not diving into whatever is down there to find you.*"

Silence. The mist beaded on everything. When Tam spoke again his voice was smaller. "You'd dive after me?"

She hadn't meant to say that part. She busied herself unclipping her own sling. "Feed me the rope. Slowly."

He fed it. She tied off to a root the size of her thigh, tested it with her full weight — the root held, groaning but honest — and stepped onto the broken slab beside him. Up close she could see he was scared, actually scared, the bravado burned off, and something about his face — sixteen and trying so hard to be useful that he'd volunteered for the heavy end without telling anyone — made her chest hurt in a way she filed away to examine never.

"Ten meters," she said. "Jump when I say. I'll take your weight if you need it."

He jumped on the third count. It was a good jump, honestly — clean, balanced — but his landing foot skated on wet stone and he went down hard on his hip, sliding, arms windmilling, and the coil of rope still on his shoulder — the coil he was supposed to have left behind — shifted, caught the slick rock, and started to pull him sideways toward the gap like the mountain had hooked him.

Ansel didn't think. That was the thing she would remember afterward, lying awake: there was no decision in it at all. Her hand shot out and closed on the rope where it ran across his chest, and her boots slid six inches, and her shoulder — the bad one, the one the glacial river would finish later — took the whole sudden weight of a falling boy plus sixty meters of wet line, and she held. White pain lit up from elbow to neck. She held anyway, the way she'd held lines in black water with things pulling on the other end, because letting go wasn't a thing her body knew how to do anymore.

"Up," she gritted. "Get your feet under you. *Up.*"

He got up. She hauled the coil off his shoulder and threw it across the gap to solid ground, then pushed him across after it, then crossed herself, breathing like a bellows, arm hanging half-dead at her side.

Mara appeared out of the mist behind them, having heard everything and seen nothing, which somehow made it worse. Her eyes went from Tam, pale and sitting down abruptly, to Ansel's arm.

"You're bleeding," Mara said.

"It's the old wound. It does that." Ansel flexed her fingers. They worked. Mostly. "Rope stays with me now. Tam carries food."

"That's my punishment?"

"That's your *promotion*. Food is heavier than rope."

He almost smiled. In the mist, on the broken stair, with the drop breathing cold air up at them, it was enough.

* * *

They camped that night on a shelf of flat ground beneath an overhang, out of the wind, with a view of nothing but cloud turning copper as the sun went down somewhere above it. Ansel got a fire going small — small fires, always, Mara had drilled her on that, though Ansel suspected Mara's real fear wasn't discovery but something older — and they ate rice and dried fish and a sauce Tam had concocted from their stores that tasted mostly of garlic and optimism.

Her shoulder throbbed in time with her pulse. She sat with her back to the rock and let the fire do its work and watched the other two. Tam had already fallen half asleep sitting up, the way teenagers and dogs do, his head nodding, jerking awake, nodding again. Mara sat cross-legged with her journal open on her knees, drawing — not the route, Ansel realized. Drawing the survey marker from that morning, the brass pin, working it into a larger diagram with arrows and question marks.

"You knew him," Ansel said quietly, so as not to wake the boy. "Hollis. Before the beach. Before he came to my boat."

Mara's pen stopped.

"I've been watching you not talk about it for six days," Ansel went on. "The way you touched that marker today. The way you said his name that first night — like it was a key turning. So either you're very good at hiding it, or you wanted me to ask."

Mara closed the journal. For a while the fire crackled and the mist moved past the mouth of the overhang like slow animals.

"He recruited me," she said finally. "Six years ago. I was twenty-four, doing contract survey work, broke, the usual. A man walks into my office with government paper and an expedition crest I'd never seen, and offers me triple rate to draft routes into a range nobody maps because nobody's allowed to. I said yes, obviously. I was twenty-four and broke." A thin smile. "Hollis was the radio operator. We spent eleven weeks together before launch. He taught me the mountain's names. He had a terrible singing voice — worse than Tam's, if you can believe it. He used to sing to test the radios, and we'd all beg him to stop."

Ansel waited. She was good at waiting; the sea had taught her that too.

"The expedition went in with fourteen people," Mara said. "Fourteen, and a support ship, and a mandate from a ministry that officially did not exist. They were going to establish a depot above the snowline — supplies, instruments, records. A forward position. For what, nobody below a certain clearance ever knew. Hollis was the last voice from inside. I was on the relay station on the coast when the transmissions stopped. Day forty-one. Mid-sentence, one transmission. Then static. Then the navy sealed the whole file, recalled every chart, paid off every contractor — including me, with a bonus and a signature line I signed." Her jaw tightened. "I spent six years telling myself I'd taken the money because I was broke. I spent six years redrawing those routes anyway, from memory, at night, like a woman scratching at a locked door."

"And Hollis?"

"Washed up on a beach nine hundred miles south, three years later, half dead, with no explanation anyone would give him and a map sewn into his coat." Mara looked at the fire. "He came to find me, two years ago. I turned him away. I told him I had a life. I told him the past was the past." Her voice did something complicated. "He died on your boat instead, with a stranger, making a stranger promise what he couldn't get me to promise."

"You couldn't have known—"

"I could have known. That's different." She opened the journal again, but didn't draw. "I'm not coming on this climb for evidence, Ansel. Whatever's in that cache — documents, ledgers, whatever Hollis believed in — I'm coming because I owe a dead man eleven weeks of conversation and one apology, and the mountain is the only place left to make it."

Above them, the mist had cleared, and the first stars were out, hard and cold. Tam slept. The fire settled.

Ansel found she had no clever answer, so she gave the true one. "Then we carry it together. All the way. And when we get there—" she touched the oilcloth over her ribs, "—we'll decide what it was for."

Mara nodded slowly, and for a moment, in the firelight, the wariness between them — the negotiation, the mutual need dressed as alliance — simply wasn't there anymore. Three people around a small fire on a green stair, no longer strangers.

Ansel was nearly asleep when Mara spoke again, barely above the fire.

"There's one more thing. About the crest on that strongbox you pulled from the wreck." A pause. "I recognized it too. And Ansel — the man who designed that expedition's routes, the man whose drafts I studied for six years to rebuild what they erased — his name was Corvane. The lieutenant hunting us isn't chasing a map."

Mara's eyes caught the firelight, steady and grim.

"He's chasing his father."
