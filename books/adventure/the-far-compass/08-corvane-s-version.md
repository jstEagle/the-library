## 8. Corvane's Version

The portmaster's office smelled of kerosene and wet wool, and Lieutenant Elias Corvane had been in it for three hours.

Rain came off the harbor in sheets now, hammering the tin roof the way it would hammer everything for the next hundred days if the forecasts held. Through the salt-fogged window he could see his patrol launch riding at anchor, lights doubled and smeared on black water. Somewhere out there — or already upriver, or already on the trail — a salvage diver named Ansel Vasquez was carrying a dead man's map toward a mountain that had eaten eleven men twenty-two years ago.

Twelve men, if you counted the one who'd walked away.

Corvane counted him every night.

"Again," he said. "From the dock."

Portmaster Grell was sixty, heavy through the shoulders, with the permanent squint of a man who'd spent forty years reading manifests in bad light. He'd been cooperative for the first hour. He'd been frightened for the second. Now he was simply tired, and tired men told the truth more often than scared ones did.

"I told you from the dock," Grell said. "Hollis came in on the evening tide, Tuesday. Alone. Paid his mooring fee in coin, which nobody does anymore. Bought diesel he didn't need and bread he didn't eat. Sat in his skiff all night talking to somebody who wasn't there." The old man's hands were flat on his desk, as if holding it down against weather. "Then Wednesday morning he was gone, and Vasquez's boat went out at first light like she always does, and I didn't think about either of them again until your sergeant started pulling my records apart."

"You didn't think about it," Corvane said, "when a man who's been dark for six years walks back into your harbor and dies of something that isn't age within a week?"

"He didn't die here."

"No. He died aboard her boat. Which means she took him in. Which means whatever he was raving about, she heard all of it." Corvane turned from the window. Rain ran down the glass between them like something trying to get in. "What was he raving about, Portmaster? You said he talked to somebody who wasn't there. What did he say?"

Grell looked at his own hands for a long moment. Outside, a mooring line creaked against a piling, rhythmic as a pulse.

"He said *the ledger's still dry*," the old man said finally. "Over and over. *The ledger's still dry, I kept it dry, I kept it dry.* And then he'd laugh, this terrible little laugh, and say, *Elias will come. Elias always comes.*"

Corvane did not move. He had learned, in fifteen years of wearing the uniform, that stillness was the only honest answer to a sentence like that. Anything else — a flinch, a question asked too fast — gave the room to you.

"Elias," Grell repeated, softer. "That's you, isn't it. I wondered why a lieutenant commander comes all the way out to a mudhole like this over a dead prospector and a debt-ridden diver. That's not navy business. That's family business."

"My father's name is not part of this conversation."

"It's the whole conversation," Grell said. "It has been since before you were born."

* * *

Corvane made himself sit down across the desk from the old man. It was a tactical decision, nothing more. Standing put him near the window, and the window put the harbor behind him, and the harbor was where his father's boat had last been seen heading north into weather exactly like this, and he did not want to spend this interview looking at it.

"Talk," he said.

Grell poured two cups of kerosene-thin coffee without asking. His hand shook only slightly, which Corvane noted and filed. Men whose hands shook slightly had done this kind of telling before, or something adjacent to it, and survived it.

"I was harbor clerk here when the expedition outfitted," Grell began. "Twenty-two years ago, spring. Eleven men, one ship — the *Meridian*, converted trawler, navy registry but civilian crew. Officially a hydrographic survey. Unofficially—" he shrugged, an old man's shrug, carrying decades of not-asking, "—unofficially they bought rope and pitons and cold-weather rations enough for a mountain, and hydrographic surveys don't climb."

"The file says the *Meridian* was lost with all hands in a coastal storm."

"The file," Grell said, "was written by the same office that sent them."

Corvane drank the coffee. It tasted like the office it had come from.

"My father commanded her," he said. "Captain Tomas Corvane. There was no inquiry. No wreckage recovered. No board of review. A ship and eleven men written off in a paragraph, and the paragraph classified." He set the cup down with care, because his hand wanted to do otherwise. "I've spent fifteen years trying to open that paragraph. Every request denied. Every archive sealed. Do you understand what that does to a boy who grew up on stories about his father? You grow up with a hero and no evidence. Just weather, and silence, and a mother who stopped speaking his name by the time I was ten because it hurt too much to say it into nothing."

"And now you know there's something in the mountains worth sealing," Grell said. "And a dying man came out of those mountains saying he kept a ledger dry for twenty-two years, and expecting *you*."

"Not me. He couldn't have known—"

"Elias." Grell leaned forward. "He said your name. Not *Corvane*. Your name. Like he knew you."

The rain filled the pause. Corvane let it, because he needed the seconds. Hollis. Radio operator, per the fragmentary manifest he'd finally pried loose two years ago — the one name on the crew list that matched a living man, and the navy had known it, and had watched him for six years the way you watch a fuse. Corvane had read the surveillance summaries himself. Hollis drifting south. Hollis drinking. Hollis, once a year, buying a newspaper and checking the shipping news, as if waiting for a name to appear.

Waiting for *Elias* to appear. For the son to be old enough, ranked enough, reachable.

"He was my father's radio operator," Corvane said. "He must have heard me on the traffic nets. My voice carries certain patterns. Family patterns." Even as he said it he knew it was thin, and Grell's face said he knew it too, and neither of them reached for anything better, because the truth underneath was worse and both of them could feel its shape without touching it: Hollis had waited for the son the way you wait for the tide. Knowing it would come. Knowing what it would bring in with it.

"What's in the cache?" Corvane asked. "You've heard the stories. Every man in this port has heard some version. Gold. Survey plates. Government secrets."

Grell sat back. For a moment he looked older than sixty, older than the harbor, older than the rain.

"I'll tell you what I heard," he said, "and then you can decide whether to arrest me for it, which I assume is why you haven't yet."

"That's accurate."

"The cook's brother lived here. Still does, up past the cannery, though he doesn't talk. Before the *Meridian* sailed, the crew got drunk one last night, the way crews do, and the cook got sad-drunk, which is worse than loud-drunk, and he told his brother that the survey was a lie twice over. That they weren't mapping the coast and they weren't mapping the mountain. They were going up to close something. To make sure that what happened up there stayed up there." Grell's eyes came up. "And that Captain Corvane had argued against sailing until the weather window closed, and been overruled by the ministry man attached to the expedition, and had gone anyway, because a captain goes where he's ordered even when he knows the order is wrong. That's what the cook said his captain was. A good man carrying a bad cargo."

Corvane kept his face level. Fifteen years of practice.

"And the other version," he said. "The one that isn't sentimental."

Grell hesitated. This was the cost of the story; Corvane could see the old man weighing it, deciding how much a lieutenant commander could be trusted with the thing that would break him.

"There's a version the mountain people tell," Grell said. "The Kettle Pass families. That when the storm pinned the expedition above the cloud line, and the food went, and the radio died, the captain made a choice. That there was a chance to bring some of them down — a window, a day maybe two — and he took the strongest four and went for help and never sent anyone back. That the others starved in the ice listening to each other, and one of them, the radio operator, lived because he was meaner than the cold, and crawled out in spring with a pack full of paper." The old man's voice dropped. "Paper being the only thing up there worth keeping dry."

The fire in the little stove ticked. Somewhere below, a dog barked twice at the rain and gave up.

"You believe that version," Corvane said.

"I believe a man crawled out of those mountains with a pack full of paper and spent twenty-two years not showing it to anyone," Grell said. "If it was gold, he'd have sold it. If it was secrets, someone would have paid him to keep quiet, and someone else would have paid him to talk, and he'd be rich or dead, and he's neither. He's a drunk on a borrowed skiff, dying slow, guarding paper." He spread his hands. "Men guard two kinds of paper, Commander. Paper that proves they're innocent. And paper that proves someone they loved is guilty."

* * *

Corvane walked the seawall after midnight, alone, hatless, letting the rain do what it wanted.

This was the discipline his mother had taught him without meaning to: when the ground opens under you, walk. Keep walking until the ground closes. She had walked the length of their grief so many times the path wore into the garden, and he had inherited the habit along with the name.

*Tomas Corvane abandoned his men.*

He turned the sentence over the way you turn a shell, looking for the crack, the flaw, the place where it was hollow. The cook's version said a good man with a bad cargo. The mountain version said something else. And between them, the only living witness had handed a map to a stranger and made her promise to carry it over the range before the snows — carry it, or burn it.

Carry it. Or burn it.

Corvane stopped at the end of the seawall. Below him the harbor heaved, black water stacking against the pilings, and his launch strained at its anchor light like a held breath.

Because here was the arithmetic he had been refusing to do for three days, and the rain made it impossible to refuse any longer:

If the cache held proof of abandonment, then opening it destroyed his father. Publicly, permanently, in ledgers and letters and pay rolls — the bureaucratic machinery of guilt, which never rusts. Fifteen years of searching for the truth, and the truth was sitting in the ice waiting to convict the only man he had ever worshiped.

But if the cache was never opened — if the map burned, if the depot stayed sealed under its cairn above the cloud line — then his father remained what he had always been: a paragraph, a silence, a hero without evidence. Untouched. Unproven. Safe.

Vasquez wanted to burn the map. He'd seen it in her file, in the creditor's reports, in the shape of a woman with nothing left to lose and one promise she hadn't meant to make. She might burn it herself. She might save him the trouble.

Or she might carry it. And Mara Okonkwo — he had her file too, six years of obsessive reconstruction, a woman who wanted the truth the way other people wanted air — would make sure it was carried, and opened, and read aloud.

So the mission wrote itself, and it was not the mission in his orders. His orders said recover the map. Fine. But recovery meant seizure, and seizure meant the cache got opened by committee, by archivists, by men with no blood in it. What Corvane needed was simpler and darker: he needed that map destroyed — or, failing destruction, he needed to reach the cache first, control what was found, control what was read. A ledger could lose pages. A letter could lose a signature. History was just paper, and paper answered to whoever held it.

His father had taught him that, in a way. All those bedtime stories about command. About what a captain owes his crew.

*Maybe,* said a voice in him that sounded like Grell, like the rain, like the truth he'd been outrunning since Tuesday, *maybe the stories were the point. Maybe he told you those stories so you'd grow up believing, so that someday you'd stand exactly where you're standing and choose the story over the paper.*

"Shut up," Corvane said to the rain, quietly, and the rain ignored him, as it should.

He turned back along the seawall. Halfway to the launch, Sergeant Adeyemi fell into step beside him without being summoned — she had a talent for that, materializing at the exact moment a commanding officer became a man with a problem.

"Portmaster's statement?" she asked.

"Consistent with the diver's movements. Vasquez and the cartographer provisioned yesterday, took on a third party — juvenile male, mechanic, runaway. They're moving inland. If they hold pace, they reach the foothills inside two days."

"And us?"

Corvane watched the water slap the stones. He thought about giving her the clean version — pursuit, interception, recovery of government property — the version that would go in the log.

"We follow," he said. "At distance. We let them lead us to the cache."

Adeyemi was quiet for three steps. She was very good at quiet; it was one of the reasons he'd requested her.

"Sir," she said. "Recovery doctrine is to secure the objective before the civilians compromise it. If we let them open that depot—"

"If we open it ourselves, we open it blind," Corvane said. "They have the map. They have Hollis's knowledge of the approach, secondhand but real. Let them find the door. Then we decide what gets carried down off that mountain." He heard his own voice and recognized, distantly, the cadence of it — the bedtime-story cadence, the captain's cadence, a voice inherited from a man who might have been a hero or might have been the worst thing his son could imagine, and who had made sure, either way, that no one would ever know.

Adeyemi saluted in the rain and peeled off toward the launch.

Corvane stood a moment longer at the foot of the gangway, water running off his chin, looking north to where the mountains stood behind the weather, invisible and patient.

"Hold on, Father," he said. "I'm coming. And whatever's up there — I'll decide what it says."

Above the cloud line, unseen, the snows were already starting early.
