## 9. River That Argues Back

The river had been a blue line on my maps for six years. On paper it was elegant — a single confident stroke descending from the col, labeled in Hollis's cramped hand: *Kettle Water, fordable at low season.* I had traced it a hundred times with my fingertip, the way you might touch a scar on someone else's body. It had never once occurred to me that the line could argue.

It argued all morning.

We'd come down out of the foothills into a valley where the monsoon had arrived early, ahead of schedule, ahead of us — rain falling in gray sheets since dawn, the kind of rain that doesn't fall so much as relocate, sideways, in bulk. The trail had become a stream; the stream had become a rumor of a river running fast and brown and swollen with snowmelt from peaks we couldn't see. Where the map showed a ford — a gentle braided crossing, stones exposed, water to the shin — there was instead a single churning channel twenty meters wide, the color of wet cement, carrying whole trees like matchsticks.

"We're not crossing that," Tam said. He said it with the flat certainty of a sixteen-year-old who has recently discovered that adults are frequently wrong about things.

I crouched at the bank and studied the water. Ansel stood beside me, arms crossed over her chest, her bad shoulder — the one she'd wrenched on the wreck, before any of this — held carefully still. She didn't say anything for a long time. That was how I'd learned to read her: by what she didn't say.

"Low season," I said finally. "Hollis crossed here in September."

"It's not September," Tam said.

"No," Ansel said. "It isn't." She looked upstream, then downstream, then back at the ford. I watched her do the arithmetic every diver does without thinking — depth, current, load, margin. "There's a gravel bar mid-channel. See it? If the bar's solid under the surface, we can rope across. Three points of contact, one at a time. If it's not solid—" She shrugged. "Then we walk downstream until we find something gentler, and we lose a day we don't have."

"The pass closes in five days," I said.

"Four and a half," Ansel said. "Thank you for the weather report, cartographer."

There was no venom in it. That was the strange thing about us now, three weeks out from Last Port — the edges between us had worn smooth, the way river stones do, not because we'd stopped being hard but because we kept knocking against each other. I knew her silences. She knew my maps. Tam knew both our tempers and navigated them better than either of us navigated anything.

He was already uncoiling the supply rope.

* * *

Ansel went first, because of course she did. We anchored the rope around a granite boulder the size of a sleeping ox, and she clipped in and stepped off the bank into water that hit her mid-thigh and tried immediately to take her knees out from under her. I watched her lean into the current at an angle that made no sense to me and perfect sense to her — diving again, always diving, reading water the way I read contour lines. She moved in short deliberate steps, boot finding stone, stone finding boot, the rope singing taut behind her.

Halfway across, standing on the gravel bar, she turned and shouted something against the wind. The river ate most of it. What reached us was: "*—solid—*" and then, after a pause, "*—send him!*"

Tam went second. I insisted on it. I told myself it was because he was lightest and quickest, and if anyone slipped, Ansel could catch him before I got wet. The truth, which I admitted only later, in the dark, in the tent, with Ansel breathing slow beside me: I sent him second because I was afraid, and fear makes cowards of cartographers. We draw the route. We don't walk it first.

Tam was halfway between the bar and the far bank when the river changed its mind.

Later, I would reconstruct it a hundred times, the way I reconstructed everything — frame by frame, evidence by evidence. A wall of water coming down from upstream, brown and sudden, some release higher in the watershed, a slide, a burst culvert, the mountain simply shrugging. No warning except a sound like a freight train deciding to be a river. Ansel heard it first — she was already shouting, already hauling the rope — but Tam was mid-channel, and the flood hit him broadside like a door swinging open.

He went under. He came up. He went under again, and when he came up the second time his hands were empty and the rope was streaming away downstream, torn loose or dropped, I never learned which, and it didn't matter, because the boy was gone, tumbling, one arm windmilling, his face doing something I will see for the rest of my life — not screaming, past screaming, just open, just astonished, as if the world had broken a rule in front of him.

Ansel didn't hesitate. That is the part I keep returning to. There was no calculation, no weighing, none of the arithmetic she'd done at the bank an hour earlier. She unclipped from the anchor, took two running steps along the gravel bar, and dove into water cold enough to stop a heart, after a boy she'd known for three weeks, with a shoulder that had been held together by stubbornness and salvage-grade tape since before the foothills.

I have been asked, since, why I didn't go in too. People who ask this have never seen glacial water in flood. You don't swim in it. You don't fight it. You get one choice, made in half a second, and then the river makes the rest. Ansel made hers. I ran the bank.

I ran the bank with the coil of spare rope over my shoulder, slipping on wet granite, tearing my palms, screaming things I don't remember. Twice I lost sight of them both. Once I saw only Tam — a flash of his red jacket, there and gone, like a card trick. Then, maybe two hundred meters down, the river slammed them both against a fallen spruce that leaned out over the current, its trunk half-submerged, and I saw Ansel's arm come up over the trunk — the left one, the good one — and lock around it, and I saw that her other hand had a fistful of Tam's collar, and I understood that she had caught him underwater, blind, in current that could strip paint, and had held on.

I got the spare rope to them. It took four throws. My hands were shaking so badly the first three went anywhere but there. On the fourth, Tam — God, Tam, half-drowned, lips gray-blue, seventeen years old and shaking apart — caught it, wrapped it twice around the trunk, and hauled himself hand over hand while Ansel held his collar and held her own body against the current with nothing but that locked elbow and whatever it is inside certain people that refuses, absolutely refuses, to let go.

When they were both on the bank, when Tam was on his side coughing up half the Kettle Water and I had Ansel's jacket in my fists and was pulling her up the gravel like landing a fish, she looked at me and said, in a voice slurred with cold:

"Bar wasn't solid."

And then her left shoulder — the good one, the one that had done all the holding — gave out with a sound I felt through my hands rather than heard, and she sat down very suddenly in the shallows, and started laughing, or crying, or both, and I put my arms around her and held on the way she'd held on, which is to say without any plan whatsoever beyond *not letting go.*

* * *

We made camp in the lee of the fallen spruce, because none of us could take another step and because the tree had, in its way, earned it.

Tam was hypothermic and knew it, which was worse than not knowing. He sat wrapped in both sleeping bags with a pot of the last of our tea going cold in his hands, and he kept saying, quietly, "You came in after me," and Ansel kept saying, "Shut up and drink," and neither of them could look at each other, which is its own kind of language.

Her shoulder was bad. Not dislocated — she'd have screamed differently — but something torn or shifted deep in the joint, and when I eased the wet jacket off her I saw the whole left side of her torso was already blooming purple, a bruise mapping itself in real time, continent by continent. She let me strap it with the tape from her kit, jaw tight, eyes fixed on the fire I was trying to coax out of soaked wood.

"How bad?" I asked.

"I've had worse."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer I've got that doesn't end the expedition." She flexed the fingers of her left hand. They worked. She almost smiled. "Arm works below the elbow. Above that, it's advisory."

I did the inventory aloud, because someone had to and because numbers calm me the way they've calmed me since I was a girl drawing coastlines in the margins of my schoolbooks. Two days' food gone — the dry bag Tam had carried, the one with most of the rice and all of the flour, ripped open somewhere in the flood or jettied in panic, gone. The stove fuel survived. The rope survived, barely, kinked and abraded and shorter than it had been that morning. One tarp lost. Ansel's dive knife lost — she'd had it clipped inside her jacket, and she grieved that more than the food, briefly, privately, in the way she grieved everything: by going very still.

"Two days' food," I said. "Five days to the pass. Four and a half."

"I heard you the first time."

"There's a waystation marked on Hollis's route. Old survey hut, above the confluence. If it stands, there may be supplies. Emergency cache. Expedition depots usually stocked them."

"Hollis's expedition," Ansel said, "was forty years ago."

"Emergency rations keep. Fat, sugar, hardtack. Ask any mountaineer." I met her eyes. "It's two kilometers off-route. Maybe three hours, round trip, if the trail holds."

She looked at the fire. She looked at Tam, asleep now, or pretending, curled in his cocoon of bags with his hair drying into salt-stiff spikes. She looked at her own strapped shoulder, and I watched her arrive, visibly, at the place I'd been dreading she'd arrive at: the arithmetic. Load, current, margin. Except this time the variable was herself.

"You can't climb with that shoulder," I said, before she could say it.

"I can climb with one arm."

"And carry a pack?"

Silence. The river argued on behind us, unrepentant.

"Then you stay with Tam," I said. "I'll go to the hut."

"Mara." Her voice had an edge I hadn't heard since the boat. "You've never walked terrain like that alone. Off-route. In the rain."

"I've drawn terrain like that for six years. Every contour of that valley exists twice — once on the mountain and once in my head." I heard my own voice go flat and precise, the voice I used in negotiations, the voice I hated. "This is what I'm for, Ansel. This is the entire point of me being here."

"That's not the point of you being here."

The words landed between us and neither of us picked them up. The fire popped. Somewhere upstream, the river dropped something heavy against stone, a dull boom like a distant door.

"Tomorrow," she said finally. "Nobody moves tonight. Tomorrow, if the fever's out of him and the shoulder holds a knife grip, we all go together. And if it doesn't—" She exhaled. "If it doesn't, you go, and you take the map."

That stopped me. The map had lived inside her jacket since Hollis died, oilcloth-wrapped, next to her skin. She had slept with it. She had checked it the way other people check a pulse.

"You're giving me the map."

"I'm lending you the map." But her mouth twitched, tired, and for a moment the edge was gone and she was just a woman with a ruined shoulder and a boy to guard and a promise she'd made to a dying man, sitting in the wreckage of a day that had nearly taken two of the three people she — 

I didn't finish the thought. I didn't let myself. There would be time, I told myself, after the pass, after the cache, after whatever waited for us in the ice. There is always a *after* you promise yourself, and it is always a lie, and you promise it anyway.

"Get in the bag," I said. "You're shivering."

"Divers don't shiver."

"Divers lie," I said, and zipped the bag up to her chin myself, and stayed there, kneeling beside her in the dark, longer than the task required. She didn't tell me to move. After a while her breathing slowed, and her good hand found mine in the dark and held it — lightly, the way you hold something you're afraid of breaking, or of wanting — and neither of us said a word, because we were both fluent in the same silence now, and the silence said enough.

* * *

I woke before dawn to the sound of Tam vomiting.

By the time I got out of the tent he was on his hands and knees at the treeline, retching up bile and river water, and when I knelt beside him he waved me off with a hand that shook. "Fine," he croaked. "Just — fine."

"You're not fine."

"I'm fine *enough*." He sat back on his heels. His face was gray in the pre-dawn light, and older than it had been yesterday, and I understood with a lurch that something had gone out of him overnight — some last comfortable illusion about rivers, or luck, or the kindness of large cold things. "I heard you two. Last night. Before I crashed."

My stomach dropped. "Heard us what?"

"About the food. About splitting up." He wouldn't look at me. He stared at the river, which had dropped half a meter overnight and now ran merely furious rather than apocalyptic, exposing the gravel bar Ansel had trusted, or half-trusted, the morning before. "Don't split up. Please. I know it costs us a day. I know the math. But I've done the math my whole life — my mum did the math, every month, rent against food against heat, and the math always ate somebody." He finally looked at me, and his eyes were wet and furious and sixteen. "So don't. Find another way. Steal the food. Eat the boots. Whatever. Just don't make her send you up that mountain alone with her map and her promise and me sitting here useless."

I opened my mouth to give him the sensible answer, the cartographer's answer, the answer with contours in it.

Behind us, the tent flap moved. Ansel stood there in the gray light, strapped and bruised and pale, and I couldn't tell how long she'd been listening, or which words she'd caught, hers or Tam's or mine, the ones spoken or the ones we'd held in the dark between our hands.

"Pack up," she said. Her voice was rough with sleep and something else. "We move together. All three."

"The shoulder—"

"Holds a knife grip." She flexed her left hand, and I saw the cost of it flicker across her face and get filed away where she filed everything. "Besides," she said, shouldering her pack one-armed, awkward, refusing help, "there's something I need to show you both. Hollis's journal. The part I haven't read out loud yet."

Tam and I exchanged a look.

"What part?" I asked.

Ansel looked up-valley, toward the pass, toward the ice, toward whatever the navy had buried and Hollis had carried forty years on his back. When she spoke, the wind took the first word and gave us the rest:

"The part where he says he wasn't the only one who made it off the mountain."
