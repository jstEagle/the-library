## 10. The Hermit of Kettle Pass

The storm found them an hour before the cabin did.

It came off the high country without warning — no build of cloud, no drop in the wind, just a sudden gray wall of sleet that turned the trail into a river and the world into a rumor. Ansel called a halt, and they stood under an overhang of granite with their backs to the stone and watched the temperature fall like something dropped from a height.

"We can't camp in this," Mara said. She had her hood up and her voice flattened against the wind. "Not here. We'd freeze before morning."

"Then we walk."

"Into that?" Tam's voice cracked on the word. He was fourteen and wet to the skin and trying very hard to be neither.

"Into that," Ansel said, and hated herself a little for how easy it came out. "Cold camp kills faster than a wrong turn. We keep the ridge on our left and we move until we find something."

"Find what? There's nothing up here. There's nothing anywhere up here."

"There's a cabin," Mara said.

Ansel turned. Mara was looking at the sleet, not at her, the way she did when she was reading something off a map in her head — six years of the lost expedition's route, drawn and redrawn, memorized down to the switchbacks.

"There's a cabin on the north shoulder of Kettle Pass," Mara said. "It was on the expedition's resupply notes. A guide lived there. Hollis's journal mentions him twice — a man who carried their mail down and brought their boots up. If the cabin's still standing, someone's living in it."

"Or it's a ruin full of snow."

"Then we sleep in the ruin and complain about it."

They walked. The sleet turned to snow, which was worse in its way — quieter, more patient, filling their tracks behind them as if the mountain were erasing the idea that anyone had ever come this way. Ansel led with her bad shoulder angled into the wind, and every hundred steps the ache in it climbed another notch, and she let it, because pain was a compass too and right now it was the only one pointing anywhere useful.

They smelled the smoke before they saw the cabin.

Woodsmoke, real and close, threading through the snowfall. Tam caught it first — the boy had a nose like a dog for anything that meant warmth — and after that they moved faster, half-stumbling, until the trees opened and there it was: a low cabin of gray timber, sod-roofed, one window lit the color of strong tea. A rack of split fish hung under the eaves. A woodpile stacked with a precision that looked less like habit and more like doctrine.

Ansel knocked the way you knock on a door you might have to defend: twice, loud, then stepped to the side of it.

The door opened anyway.

The man who stood in it was old in the way mountains are old — not bent, just weathered down to what would last. Dark skin gone gray at the edges, a beard trimmed short, eyes the pale amber of old resin. He looked at Ansel, then at Mara, then at Tam, and Ansel had the distinct feeling of being counted and found to be a number he'd already done.

"Three of you," he said. "One hurt shoulder, one map, one boy who's about to fall over. You'll want the fire and the fish rack and I'll want to know why, and we can trade those in either order."

"You know about the map," Mara said.

"I know about the *shape* of people who carry maps over this pass." He stepped back from the door. "In. Boots off. If you bleed on my floor you clean it."

* * *

The cabin was one room and a loft, and it was warm the way only a room someone has kept warm for decades can be — heat in the walls themselves, in the timbers, in the iron stove that had clearly been fed without interruption through more winters than Ansel wanted to guess. Dried herbs hung in bundles. Tools hung in rows, each with its outline painted on the board behind it, so that a missing tool would announce itself by its absence.

Everything in order. Everything watched.

"Sit," the man said. "I'm Sefu. You'll eat first, because hungry people lie badly and I'll want the truth after."

He fed them smoked fish and barley boiled with pine tips, and it was the best food Ansel had eaten in a year, which she resented, because she didn't want to owe this man anything before she knew what he wanted. Tam ate like the food was a personal enemy and fell asleep sitting up halfway through his second bowl, sliding sideways until Mara caught him and steered him onto a bunk without waking him.

Then Sefu sat down across the fire from them, folded his hands, and waited.

Ansel gave him the truth in the shape he could use: a woman named Hollis, dying, a promise, a map, a pass that closes in five days now, and men behind them in navy blue who wanted the map more than they did. She left out the cache's contents. She left out the ledger. She left out the part where she'd promised to burn it, because she still hadn't decided which promise she'd made.

Sefu listened with his eyes half closed, like a man hearing a song he knew the words to.

"Kettle Pass," he said at last. "You're on the right route. I can tell you that much for free, since you found me in a blizzard and ate my fish. The route Hollis's people took is the route you're on. It goes over the shoulder here" — he tapped the table, drawing lines only he could see — "past the cairns, up the col, and down the far drainage. Four days if the weather holds. Three if you're stupid. Six if you're dead."

"And the weather?" Mara asked.

"The weather is going to do what the weather does." He said it without drama, and that was the frightening part. "Snow's early this year. You have a margin, but the margin is thin, and thin margins are where the mountain keeps its teeth."

"Then we leave at first light."

"You leave at first light," Sefu agreed. "But not by the cairns."

Ansel felt it before she understood it — a small cold shift in the room, like a draft from a door she hadn't seen.

"There's another way," Sefu said. "A shortcut. It cuts two days off the route. It goes through the old survey cut, under the north wall, and comes out above the cache basin itself. Hollis's people never used it. They didn't know it. I did the original survey of it, thirty years ago, before any of them ever came to this country."

"Why didn't they use it?" Mara asked.

"Because I didn't tell them about it," Sefu said, "and I didn't tell them about it because by the time they came, I had been paid not to."

The fire popped. Tam, up in the loft, had gone very still — Ansel could tell by the absence of his breathing sounds, the way a boy holds his breath to hear better.

"Paid by whom?" Ansel asked, though she knew.

"The navy." Sefu said it the way you'd say the name of a weather pattern. "They came to me eleven years ago, after the expedition was lost. Two officers and a lawyer and a check with a lot of zeroes. They wanted the route forgotten. Not closed — forgotten. They wanted it so that no one could say the navy knew this country and let men die in it. So they bought my memory." He looked at his own hands. "And I sold it. I took the money and I told myself the men were dead either way and the money fed me through some hard winters, and I have had thirty years of quiet nights to know exactly what that was worth."

"And now?" Mara said quietly. "Why tell us?"

Sefu was quiet for a long moment.

"Because a man came through here last month," he said. "Young. Navy coat, but wearing it like a costume. Asking questions about the pass, about the old survey, about whether anyone still remembered the route. He had a photograph of a woman." He glanced at Mara, then at Ansel. "He had photographs of both of you. And he asked me the same question the officers asked me eleven years ago: *is there another way over the mountain.*"

"And what did you tell him?"

"I told him what I told them." Sefu's amber eyes didn't blink. "I told him there was no other way. I took his money too."

Ansel's stomach turned over. "You're a man who sells the same thing twice."

"I'm a man who survived," Sefu said. "There's a difference, and the difference is that I've never sold anyone *into* the mountain. Only away from it. The officers went east and forgot. The lieutenant went east and forgot. And you—" he pointed one gnarled finger at Ansel, then at Mara, then up at the loft where Tam lay breathing again, slow and deep, "—you I'm going to tell the truth, because you knocked on my door in a blizzard and the boy is asleep in my bunk and that buys more from me than money does. It's been a long time since anyone bought anything from me with weather."

"The shortcut," Ansel said. "Is it real?"

"It's real. It's surveyed, it's cut, and it's fast. It comes out above the basin — above whatever it is you're carrying that map to — in half the time. And I'll draw it for you, every switchback, every water source, because a route drawn from memory is a route drawn from conscience, and I'd like to spend some of mine."

"And the catch," Mara said. It wasn't a question.

"The catch," Sefu said, "is that I haven't walked it in thirty years. The catch is that the north wall sheds rock every spring, and what was a cut when I surveyed it may be a canyon of rubble now, and I will not know, and you will not know, until you're under it. And the catch—" and here his voice dropped, and the firelight made his face into something older than the cabin, older than the mountain, "—is that the lieutenant knows the old route, because the old route is the one he paid me to forget. If he's behind you, he's behind you on the cairn trail. He is not expecting you under the north wall."

"Unless he's already there," Ansel said.

"Unless he's already there," Sefu agreed. "I can't tell you the odds. I'm an old man who sells weather and forgets routes. I can only tell you that the safe way has men on it, and the fast way has stone over it, and you have five days, and you get to choose which kind of trouble you'd rather meet."

* * *

He drew the route that night on the back of an old flour sack, working by lamplight with a pencil worn to a stub, and he drew it fast, without hesitation, and that frightened Ansel more than anything else he'd said — because a man hesitates when he's inventing, and Sefu didn't hesitate. Whatever else he was, the route in his head was real.

"Here." He tapped the sack. "You leave the cabin and bear east below the tree line for two hours. There's a stream — you'll hear it before you see it, and you'll want to fill everything you have, because after it there's no water for a day. Then the cut begins. You'll know it because the wall goes up on your left like a cathedral and the talus goes down on your right like a staircase, and the path runs along the seam. Half a day under the wall. Then it climbs — three switchbacks, all of them cut, all of them old, so don't trust the edges — and it comes out on a bench above the basin. You'll see the whole cache country from there. And from there," he said, and paused for the first time all night, "from there you're on your own, because I never went past the bench. That part of the mountain, I never surveyed. That part, nobody surveyed. That's why the expedition went the long way."

"Why are you giving us this?" Mara asked. She'd been quiet through the whole drawing, watching his hands. "Really. The money answer, the conscience answer — I don't buy either one all the way. What do you *want*, Sefu?"

The old man put down the pencil. For a moment he looked at the fire, and Ansel saw something move behind his face that he'd been keeping off it all evening.

"Hollis," he said. "The woman who gave you the map. She was the radio operator. She used to call this cabin every Sunday night, eleven years ago, on a schedule she kept like a religion. Weather reports. Nothing else. *Clear at the pass. Snow on the col. Clear at the pass.*" He smiled, and it was a terrible thing to see, because it was a young man's smile on an old man's face. "I never met her. I knew her voice for two years and I never met her, and when the expedition was lost, the calls stopped, and I sat in this cabin every Sunday night for a year with a radio that had no one on the other end of it. So." He folded the flour sack and handed it to Mara. "You tell her — if she's alive, and I think she is, because the mountain doesn't take the stubborn ones — you tell her the man in the cabin kept her schedule. You tell her he never missed a Sunday."

Mara took the map. Her hands weren't quite steady. "I'll tell her."

"And the lieutenant," Ansel said. "The one who came last month. What was his name?"

"Corvane," Sefu said. "He gave it without being asked. He's the kind of man who thinks his name is a weapon." He looked at Ansel, and his voice gentled into something almost like pity. "He paid me to forget the route, and then he sat where you're sitting and drank my tea and asked me if anyone else knew. And I said no. And he believed me, because men like that always believe the man they've paid." He stood, joints cracking like green wood. "Sleep. You leave at first light. And woman—"

He stopped at the ladder to the loft, one hand on the rail.

"The cut under the north wall," he said. "If you get under it and the light goes wrong, or the rock sings, or anything feels like the mountain is holding its breath — you turn around. You turn around and you take the long way and you lose the days and you stay alive. Promise me that much. I've sold enough men to this mountain. I won't have sold you too."

Ansel looked at the flour sack in Mara's hands, and at the fire, and at Tam asleep in the loft with his boots still on because nobody had the heart to take them off.

"I promise," she said. And meant it, the way she'd meant the promise to Hollis — completely, sincerely, with no idea which way she'd break it.

* * *

She woke once in the night, in the bunk Sefu had given up for her, and heard voices below — Sefu and Mara, low, the fire burned down to coals. She should have gone down. She told herself she was too tired, and that was a lie; she stayed because she was listening.

"—paid him to forget it," Mara was saying. "And then paid him again to forget that he'd forgotten. Do you understand what that means? The navy didn't just bury the expedition. They buried the *ground* it stood on. They bought the geography."

"They bought the silence," Sefu said. "The geography they got for free."

"And you sold it twice."

"I sold it three times, if we're counting. There was a mining company in between." A dry sound that might have been a laugh. "The mountain doesn't care who owns my memory, girl. It keeps its own ledger."

Silence. Then Mara, very quietly: "Do you think the cut is passable? Honestly. Not the conscience version. The truth."

A long pause. Long enough that Ansel found she was holding her breath, the way Tam did.

"I honestly don't know," Sefu said. "That's the truth of it. Thirty years is a long time for rock. It might be the fastest road over that mountain, and it might be a grave with good views. What I know is this: the woman who built that route with me — my wife, before she was anything else — she cut the first switchback with her own hands, and she said, *Sefu, this road is for people in a hurry to live, not people in a hurry to die. Make sure you know the difference before you send anyone down it.*" The coals shifted. "I've had thirty years to know the difference. I think you're people in a hurry to live. But I've been wrong before, and the mountain keeps the receipts."

Ansel lay in the dark and did not sleep again, and in the morning they left at first light, with the flour-sack map in Mara's jacket and Sefu's fish in their packs and the old man standing in his doorway watching them go, not waving, just watching, the way you watch something you're not sure you'll ever see again.

The trail bore east below the tree line, and the snow of the night before was already melting off the branches, and somewhere behind them — Ansel couldn't have said how she knew, only that she'd spent enough years reading water to read men — somewhere behind them, on the cairn trail, something was moving.

She didn't look back. She'd learned that on the water: you don't look back at what's chasing you. You look at the wall ahead, and you count the hours, and you hope the mountain holds its breath a little longer than the men do.

Two hours east, they heard the stream.

"Fill everything," Ansel said. "After this, there's no water for a day."

They filled everything. And then the trees ended, all at once, and the wall went up on their left like a cathedral, and the talus went down on their right like a staircase, and the cut began.

It was exactly where Sefu said it would be.
