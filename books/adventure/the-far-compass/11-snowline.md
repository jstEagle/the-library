## 11. Snowline

The snow came at dusk, without warning, the way the worst things did.

Mara saw it first — a single flake drifting past the headlamp beam, hanging in the air like a question. Then another. Then the light itself seemed to thicken, and the world above the tree line went soft and white and wrong.

"It's too early," Tam said, holding out his palm as if he could catch an argument in it.

"Tell the mountain that." Mara crouched over the map case, shielding it with her body out of habit. The flakes landed on the nylon and didn't melt. That was the detail that mattered. They didn't melt.

They made camp fast, the three of them moving with the wordless efficiency of people who had been cold together long enough to stop apologizing for it. Ansel rigged the tarp between two boulders while Tam fed the stove its ration of fuel, and Mara spread the maps across her sleeping pad, weighting the corners with stones, because paper was still the only thing she trusted to hold a route.

The pass was four days away if the weather held. Two days if it didn't. And the weather, clearly, had stopped holding anything for anyone.

Ansel sat down beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched through two layers of fleece. She didn't move away. Neither did Mara. Somewhere in the last week that had stopped being an accident.

"You're doing the thing," Ansel said.

"What thing."

"The thing where you stare at the map like it owes you money."

Mara almost smiled. "It does owe me money. Six years of it."

Above them, the snow thickened into something with intent. The stove hissed. Tam had already curled up against his pack, one hand on the supply rope out of habit, and within minutes his breathing went slow and young.

Ansel pulled her knees up. "So," she said quietly. "Hollis. You knew him before the boat. Before he came to me. I've been waiting three weeks for you to tell me how."

Mara looked at the fire, then at the snow falling through the beam of her headlamp, and understood that the deadline had arrived — not the one on the calendar, but the other one, the one she'd been outrunning since a bar in the port town when she'd first put her hand on Ansel's map case and said *I know those mountains.*

"I was engaged," she said. "To the expedition's surveyor."

Ansel went very still beside her. Stillness, Mara had learned, was what Ansel did instead of flinching.

"His name was Daniel Okoye," she went on, because once the door opened there was no closing it halfway. "Everyone called him Danny. He was — God, he was the kind of man who drew contour lines in his sleep. We met at a mapping conference in Nairobi. He corrected my projection in front of forty people and then bought me dinner to apologize, and I decided before dessert that I was going to marry him."

The snow ticked softly against the tarp.

"He spent two years on this range. He'd send me sketches — not survey work, just drawings. A ptarmigan. His tent in fog. Little maps of nowhere, drawn for no reason except that he liked making them." Her voice caught and she let it. "The last letter I got from him was a hand-drawn map of our kitchen. He labeled the stove *hazardous terrain.*"

Ansel's hand found hers in the dark. No announcement, no hesitation. Just found it, the way you find a rope in whiteout conditions — by feel, by faith.

"The navy listed the expedition as lost to weather," Mara said. "Nine names, one line in a shipping register, done. But Danny's instruments came back. Someone returned them. Six years later, a crate arrives at my office with his theodolite and his field books, half water-damaged, no return address. And inside one of the field books, a page in someone else's handwriting — coordinates, and a note: *the truth is cached above the cloud line.*"

"Hollis."

"Hollis. Though I didn't know that then. I spent six years reconstructing the route from Danny's field notes. Every camp, every bearing. I got close enough that Hollis heard about me — a cartographer asking questions about a dead expedition makes noise, even in quiet places. He tracked me down a year ago. Told me he was the radio operator. Told me he'd walked out alone and had been walking ever since." She swallowed. "He wouldn't give me the map. Said I wanted it for the wrong reasons. Said I'd trade the truth for evidence, and evidence could be buried again, but the truth, once spoken —"

She stopped.

"Once spoken," Ansel finished quietly.

"I hated him for it. For six years I hated a dying man for withholding a piece of paper." Mara laughed, and it came out cracked. "And then I took your boat, and your promise, and here we are, and I still don't know which of us he trusted more."

"He trusted us both," Ansel said. "That's what scares me."

They sat with that. The snow kept falling, patient as arithmetic, and Mara did the calculation she'd been avoiding since the foothills: four days to the pass, twelve until it closed, Corvane somewhere behind them or ahead of them, and her own heart sitting in her chest like an unexploded shell she'd been carrying since Nairobi.

"Danny wanted to name a peak after me," she said. "After we were married. He had it all planned — some unnamed summit on the eastern ridge, he'd surveyed it himself, said it was waiting for a name." She turned her hand over so her palm pressed against Ansel's. "I don't want his bones, Ansel. I don't want the ledger, or the proof, or justice with a capital J. I want to know what happened to him. I want someone to say his name out loud where he died. That's all. That's the whole treasure."

Ansel was silent for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was rougher than the altitude accounted for.

"When we get there," she said, "whatever's in that cache — you read the names. Not me. Not Corvane. You."

"Why me?"

"Because you'll say them right." Ansel squeezed her hand. "Because you've been carrying them longer than anyone alive."

Mara turned toward her. In the stove-light, Ansel's face was all angles and exhaustion and something underneath both that Mara had been pretending not to see since the river — since Ansel had gone into glacial water after Tam without a second's thought, since she'd come up gasping with her shoulder torn open and laughed about it.

"You're staring," Ansel said.

"You're staring back."

"Yeah," Ansel said. "I know."

Neither of them moved. The snow fell. And Mara thought: *we have four days, maybe less, and a man with a gun behind us, and I am falling in love at eleven thousand feet in a snowstorm, and I would not trade a single hour of it.*

"We should sleep," she said, and made no move to sleep.

"In a minute," Ansel agreed.

* * *

Mara woke once in the black hours to the sound of Ansel breathing — awake, she could tell, the way you can tell weather coming. She shifted against her shoulder, and Ansel's arm came around her without either of them deciding it.

"Can't sleep?" Mara murmured.

"Shoulder's singing."

"Liar."

A pause. "Both true."

Mara tilted her head back to look at her. Snow-light filtered through the tarp, turning everything blue and grainy, and Ansel's eyes were open and fixed on the roof of the shelter as though reading something written there.

"What are you thinking?" Mara asked.

"That I've spent ten years underwater," Ansel said slowly, "because down there nothing can surprise you. You know exactly how deep you are, how much air you have, how long until you have to come up. Everything's measured." Her thumb moved absently along Mara's arm. "Then Hollis hands me a map and a promise, and suddenly I'm above the cloud line in a blizzard with a teenager and a woman who knows more about these mountains than the mountains do, and nothing — *nothing* — is measured anymore. And I keep thinking I should be terrified."

"Are you?"

"No." A breath of a laugh. "That's what terrifies me."

Mara rolled toward her. Up close, the snow-light caught the salt-scars on Ansel's hands, the diver's calluses, the small white line above her eyebrow she'd never explained and Mara had never asked about. There were whole wrecks in this woman, Mara thought. Whole sunken holds. And she was beginning to understand that she didn't want to salvage any of it. She just wanted to dive with her.

"Whatever happens at the cache," Mara said. "Whatever Corvane does, whatever's in those crates — I need you to know it doesn't change this. Whatever this is."

"This is a snow cave and bad decisions."

"This is the truest heading I've ever plotted."

Ansel looked at her for a long moment, and then she kissed her — carefully, the way you handle something recovered from deep water, something that has waited a long time to surface. Outside, the storm built its white silence, and the pass closed another day's worth, and neither of them moved to check the time.

* * *

Dawn came gray and windless, the world rebuilt overnight in white. Two feet of fresh snow had erased the trail behind them completely — and, Mara realized as she stood blinking at the slope ahead, most of the route markers Sefu had described.

Tam stood at the edge of camp, stamping his boots, breath streaming. "Good news," he called. "I found the latrine. Eventually. By falling in it."

"Bad news?" Ansel asked, working her shoulder in slow circles.

"Bad news is I dug out the food bags and we're down to five days' rations. Four if we eat like people who like each other."

Mara unfolded the map against her knee, then the backup, then Danny's old field book, cross-referencing bearings against landmarks that no longer existed under the snow. The col was visible from here — a notch in the ridge, sharp as a folded page — but the approach had gone featureless.

"There," she said finally, pointing with her chin. "We traverse east along the rock band, then cut up through the gully. If Sefu's shortcut was honest, we save a day. If it wasn't—"

"If it wasn't," Ansel said, "we're standing in a bowl with one exit and a navy lieutenant who reads terrain better than we do."

"Cheerful."

"I'm a salvage diver. We're professionally cheerful. It's how we stay alive."

They struck camp. And it was as Mara coiled the last of the rope that she saw it — a single set of boot prints in the fresh snow, maybe two hundred meters below their camp, running parallel to their route of yesterday. One person. Heavy tread. Military heel-strike, spaced like a metronome.

Not following them. *Leading* them. Moving toward the pass, hours ahead.

Mara said nothing. She folded the map, looked up at the col, and felt the deadline tighten around all of them like a rope coming taut.

Four days to the pass. Three, now, if the snow kept its pace.

And someone was already on the route.
