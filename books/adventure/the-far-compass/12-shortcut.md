## 12. Shortcut

Sefu's route began where the trail didn't.

That should have been the first warning. Every path we'd climbed since the foothills had been worn into the mountainside by generations of feet — traders, pilgrims, smugglers, the occasional lost navy patrol — but the track Sefu had described turned off the main line at a cairn no one had bothered to maintain and climbed through scree that no one had bothered to walk. Mara checked the bearing three times against her reconstructed route, and each time the pencil came up the same.

"It's shorter," she said. "Two days, maybe three. If the snow holds off, it puts us at the col before Corvane can flank us."

"If the snow holds off," Tam said, "and if the map's right, and if an eighty-year-old man who took navy money to forget a route remembered it correctly anyway."

"Optimism looks good on you, Tam."

"I'm fourteen. I'm contractually required to be suspicious."

I let them argue and watched the mountain. The route climbed a rib of gray rock between two hanging valleys, steep but honest-looking, with ledges wide enough for a loaded pack and no exposure you couldn't talk yourself across. After the river, after the crossing that had cost us two days of food and most of the use of my left shoulder for anything above chest height, honest-looking was worth more than easy. You can rest an honest mountain. It's the dishonest ones that kill you.

We climbed it.

For six hours it behaved. The ledges held. The scree was loose but predictable, the kind that slides a hand-width under each boot and then commits. Tam went first with the rope, the way he'd insisted on since the river — I think he needed to be the one holding the line, after the line had nearly taken him — and Mara moved like she'd been born on rock, which I was starting to suspect she had. Six years reconstructing a dead expedition's route will do that. You spend enough time in a man's footprints, you start walking like him.

I brought up the rear, because my shoulder couldn't be trusted with the rope and because someone had to watch the way we'd come. That was the job I'd given myself without saying it out loud: rear guard. Watching for blue.

The navy hadn't shown since the ridge above the hermit's hut. That was worse than if they had. Corvane was a chess player — I'd understood that much from our one conversation aboard my boat, the polite questions and the way he'd looked at Hollis's effects like he was reading a letter from home. Chess players don't chase. They position.

By late afternoon we'd gained the shoulder of the rib, and the route bent east into a hanging valley that Sefu had described with an odd tenderness — *the green stair, the easy way, the way I took the expedition's mules when their hearts were failing* — and I remember thinking, right up until the moment we rounded the last spur, that the old man had given us a gift.

Then we rounded the last spur, and the gift turned out to be a wall.

* * *

The rockfall filled the valley floor from wall to wall, two hundred feet of broken stone heaped forty feet high, and at first glance it looked like what it was pretending to be: a slide. Mountains shed. It happens. You climb over or you turn around.

But I'd spent eleven years diving wrecks, and a wreck teaches you to read the difference between something that broke and something that was broken.

Slides have a shape. They fan. They pour out of their source like water finding its level, big stones at the bottom, fines on top, a scoured chute above where the mass let go. This had no source. The slopes above the fall were intact — grass on them, still holding, the same late-season green we'd been walking through all day. No chute. No scar. Two hundred feet of mountain sitting in the valley floor with no explanation for how it had gotten there except that someone had put it there.

I climbed partway up the near face to be sure. Halfway up, wedged between two blocks, I found the proof, and my stomach went cold in a way the altitude couldn't account for.

The blocks were fitted.

Not masonry — nothing so neat — but placed. Set on edge, angled into each other, the gaps packed with smaller stone and then with earth so that rain would knit the whole thing together into something that looked, from below, like accident. And in the seam between two of the largest blocks, half-swallowed by forty years of lichen, a length of chain. Ship's chain, anchor stock, the links the size of my fist, rusted to a red-brown lace. Somebody had hauled chain up out of a hold, up a green valley, and buried it in a wall of rock to hold it together.

Nobody builds a wall like this to keep travelers out by accident. You build it to keep them out *and* to make them believe the mountain did it.

I climbed down and told the others, and I watched Mara's face do the arithmetic before I'd finished the sentence.

"The expedition," she said. "The original route. Hollis's journal put them through this valley — the mules, the green stair, all of it. If this wall was here when they came through—"

"They came through before the wall," I said. "Or the wall came up after them."

"After." She said it flat, the way you say a thing you've been dreading for six years. "Someone sealed the route behind them. Sealed it so no one could follow, and so no one could—" She stopped. Her hand had gone to the map tube at her chest, the way it did when she was thinking, and I saw her thumb press against the cap hard enough to whiten the knuckle. "So no one could bring them back."

Tam had climbed the fall's flank while we talked, because Tam couldn't not climb things, and he came back down at a speed that made my heart lurch before my brain caught up with the look on his face.

"There's a way through," he said. "Sort of. The wall's not solid — there's a gap on the north side where it meets the cliff, maybe a body-width, and it goes all the way through. But Ansel—" He glanced at Mara, then at me, and I watched him decide to say it anyway, which is the thing I liked most about him and the thing that was going to get him killed someday. "There's a cairn on the far side. A big one, stacked like Sefu's, like a guide's. And it's fresh. The stones under the lichen are clean."

Clean stones. Someone had stacked that cairn within the last few years. Within the last few seasons.

Sefu had taken navy money to forget a route. Sefu had also, with a hermit's careful kindness, given us a shortcut that ended at a wall someone had built to stop us — and a cairn someone had built to mark the way through it.

Kindness with a question mark. I'd written the phrase in my head at his hearth and dismissed it, because the old man had fed us and bandaged Tam's hands and talked about the expedition's mules like he'd loved them. Now the question mark had a wall around it and a chain through its heart.

"Either Sefu sold us," I said, "or Sefu was sold a long time ago and never knew it, and the people who built this wall have been waiting for someone dumb enough to come up the green stair."

"Or," Mara said quietly, "Sefu knew exactly what he was doing, and the cairn is his. His way of giving back what he was paid to bury." She looked at the fall for a long moment. "He told us the navy paid him to forget. He didn't say they paid him to forgive."

We went through the gap. Of course we went through the gap. Behind us was the river and the winter and Corvane's chessboard, and through was the col, and I had promised a dying man I'd carry his map over the range, and I had promised myself — without meaning to, without wanting to — that I'd get these two to the other side of it alive.

The gap was exactly as bad as Tam had understated it. Body-width meant my shoulders touched both walls, and the walls were the wall — fitted stone on one side, living cliff on the other — and forty feet of it, in the dark, with my bad shoulder leading. I went through with the map tube slung across my back and my breath held the whole way, and when I came out the far side into the last flat light of evening, the cairn was there, man-high, and the valley beyond it opened toward the col like a held breath finally let out.

Mara came through second, then Tam, and for about ninety seconds we stood there in the wind and let ourselves believe it. Two days to the col. The snow holding. The wall behind us and the navy behind the wall.

Then Tam, who had climbed the cairn the way he climbed everything, said, in a voice with no fourteen in it at all:

"Ansel. The ridge."

* * *

They were on the ridge above the hanging valley, on the skyline we'd have been silhouetted against if we'd come up the main route an hour later, and there were six of them where I'd have bet everything I didn't own there'd be three. Navy blue, but wrong for navy — no whites, no dress anything, just field gear dyed the color of deep water, and they moved like the wreck divers the navy doesn't admit to employing. Spread out. Spacing kept without signals. One of them already had a glass up.

The light was dying and they were above us and behind us, and the wall that had been our obstacle was now our fortification, and I did the math the way you do it in the water when the current turns: not *can I make it* but *what do I give up to make it*.

"Down the valley," Mara said. "They have to come around the fall or through the gap. The gap's one at a time — we could hold it—"

"With what? Tam has a wrench."

"I have a wrench and a bad attitude."

"Down," I said. "Now. While they're still deciding what we are."

Because that was the thing, the thing that kept my feet moving while my mind screamed the other direction: they hadn't moved. Six professionals on a skyline with a glass, and not one of them had started down. They'd seen us — you don't put a glass on three people crossing an open valley and then lose them — and they were *waiting*. Watching us run the way you watch a current, checking its set, its speed. Corvane hadn't chased us off the boat and he hadn't chased us out of the port town and he wasn't chasing us now. He was herding. He'd known about the green stair — maybe from Sefu, maybe from the wall's builders, maybe from a file older than all of us — and he had let us walk into a valley with one exit, and he was standing at the top of it timing our pace.

The col was two days. The snow was a coin flip. And somewhere behind us, moving at the speed of a man who has never once had to hurry, was the officer who'd asked me about Hollis with such terrible gentleness, letting us open the door he couldn't be seen opening himself.

I didn't know that yet, not for certain. What I knew was the chain in the wall, and the clean stones on the cairn, and six blue shapes on a ridge that didn't move, and the map tube against my spine, heavier than paper had any right to be.

We ran the valley in the last light, three small figures on a floor of green with the mountains closing in behind and ahead, and when I looked back one final time at the saddle where the ridge met the sky, the shapes were gone.

All six. Not following. Not yet.

Which meant they knew another way down — a way that would put them ahead of us, at the col, at the cache, waiting with the patience of men who had already decided how this ended and were only letting us confirm the details.

Mara caught my look and read it, because she read everything.

"They're not behind us anymore," she said.

"No."

"Then who built the wall?"

I put my hand against the map tube and thought about Hollis dying in my bunk with the storm coming in, about a radio operator who'd walked away from a mountain forty years ago and spent the rest of his life drawing the way back.

"Ask them at the col," I said. "I think they've been asking each other the same question since before we were born."

We climbed into the dark, and somewhere above us, above the cloud line, above the snow, the mountain kept its secret — and kept our appointment.
