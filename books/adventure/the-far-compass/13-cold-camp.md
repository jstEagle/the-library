## 13. Cold Camp

The storm found them an hour after they dropped into the crevasse field, and by the time Tam had the tarp strung between two ice screws, the world had gone white in every direction at once.

Not snow, exactly. Not wind. Something between — a churning nothing that erased the ridge they'd come down and the col they were supposed to climb toward and any idea of which way was up. He'd been in weather before. Port storms, monsoon rain that came down like the sky was being wrung out. But this was different. This wasn't water falling on you. This was the air itself deciding to stop existing.

"Get inside," Ansel shouted, or he thought she shouted. Her voice arrived flattened, stripped of direction, like it came from everywhere and nowhere.

The crevasse mouth was a blue slit in the glacier, maybe four feet wide, angling down into a chamber big enough for three people and their gear if nobody minded intimacy. Mara went first with the rope, then Tam shoved the packs through, then Ansel last, hauling the tarp line in behind her and anchoring it across the entrance with a boulder of ice she broke loose with her boot heel.

Then it was just the three of them and a candle.

Tam lit it because his hands needed something to do. The flame stood perfectly still in the dead air, which somehow made everything worse — outside the storm screamed and tore at the mountain, and in here the flame didn't even lean.

"Two days," Mara said. She'd already dug out the food bag and was counting with her lips moving. "Maybe two and a half if we stretch it."

"Three," Tam said. "If we stretch it."

Mara looked up at him. In the candlelight her face was all planes and shadow, and there was something in her expression he couldn't read — not pity exactly, but close to it. "You're sixteen," she said. "You burn fuel faster than we do."

"I'm crew," Tam said. "Crew eats same as captain."

Ansel made a sound that might have been a laugh. She'd wedged herself against the far wall, right arm cradled against her ribs where the shoulder sat wrong since the river. She hadn't let anyone look at it properly since. "He's got you there, cartographer."

"I'm not the captain either."

"You're the one with the map memorized."

"That's navigator," Mara said. "Different job." She tossed Tam a strip of dried fish, hard as a shingle. "Eat slow. Chewing is half the meal."

They ate in silence while the storm worked above them. Tam could feel it through the ice — a deep grinding vibration, like the mountain clearing its throat. Once, something massive slid somewhere overhead and the whole crevasse hummed like a struck wire, and all three of them froze until it passed.

* * *

Sleep came in shifts. Ansel took first watch by default because she said she couldn't sleep anyway with the shoulder, and Tam lay in his bag listening to the storm and pretending he wasn't doing math.

But he was always doing math. That was the thing about him nobody understood. They saw a kid who could strip an engine blindfolded and assumed the hands did all the work, when really it was the arithmetic underneath. Fuel to distance. Food to days. Wages to debt.

His mother's debt, originally. Then hers alone after the fever took her, because creditors didn't care whose name was on the paper once the signer stopped breathing. Fourteen years old and holding a ledger with numbers in it that would take a dockworker thirty years to clear. So he'd run. Run straight into the one port town where rumor said a salvage diver and a mad cartographer were hiring for a climb that would probably kill them, because the alternative was the debt collectors finding him first, and they had ways of collecting from minors that made dying on a mountain look reasonable.

And then, in Last Port, over bad coffee, Ansel had mentioned it casually, the way you'd mention weather: Corvane's office was posting a standing offer. Information leading to recovery of expedition property. Substantial reward. Enough, Tam had calculated in about four seconds, to erase his ledger twice over and buy passage anywhere in the world.

He hadn't done anything with the number. He wanted to be clear with himself about that. He hadn't *done* anything. He'd just held it, turned it over, felt its weight. The way you hold a knife you find on the ground. You don't stab anybody. You just notice how well it fits your hand.

Now, lying in the dark listening to Ansel's breathing change as her watch stretched on, he noticed the knife again.

* * *

It was deep in the second night — or what he guessed was night; time had gone soft in the whiteout — when the voices woke him.

Low. Careful. The specific register of people talking quietly because someone else might be asleep, which meant they thought he was.

"...if it comes to that," Ansel was saying. Her voice had that flatness she got when she was being honest, which was rare enough that Tam kept still and listened. "I want you to know now, so it's not a fight later."

"Mm." Mara, noncommittal.

"The map. If the pass seals, if we can't get it down, if it comes down to carrying it or ending it — I'll burn it. I need you to hear me say that so you're not surprised."

A long pause. Tam could hear the candle guttering, could picture the two of them sitting close together in the blue light at the far end of the chamber, shapes more than faces.

"You promised Hollis you'd carry it," Mara said finally.

"I promised him I'd carry it *or* burn it. Both options were mine to choose. He wrote it that way on purpose — I've read the promise a dozen times. 'Deliver it whole, or see it destroyed.' He gave me both doors." A breath. "I used to think that was him hedging. Now I think it was mercy. I think he knew what was in that cache, and he knew whoever carried his map would end up standing where I'm standing, and he wanted them to have a way out that wasn't treason."

"And the names?" Mara's voice had gone careful, the way it did around the edges of things that mattered most to her. "The list. If you burn the map, the coordinates go, the route goes — but the names are in the ledger too, back at the depot. Those we carry regardless."

"The names we carry." Quiet, certain. "But the map itself, Mara — if keeping it means Corvane's chain of command gets it, means it becomes evidence in some hearing where lawyers decide what the dead are worth — I don't know. Maybe some things should stay buried with the men who died in them."

Another silence, longer. When Mara spoke again her voice had dropped even lower, and Tam had to strain.

"You'd really do it? After everything? After the river?"

"The river isn't why I'm saying it." And here Ansel laughed softly, without much humor. "The river is why I know I could. I dove into that water after Tam without deciding to. My body decided. And afterward I kept thinking — that's the only kind of decision I trust anymore. The ones you make before you know you're making them." A pause. "Burning the map feels like that kind. Like if I wait until the moment, I'll talk myself into the clever choice, the choice that keeps everything. Some things you have to decide in advance or you never decide them at all."

"So it's decided."

"It's decided. If it comes to that."

Tam lay in his bag with his eyes open and his heart going hard and strange against his ribs.

She'd burn it.

The map — the route, the coordinates, the whole architecture of the thing Corvane's office was paying a fortune for — gone. Ash. And with it, every version of the future Tam had been quietly building in the back of his head since Last Port. The reward required information leading to recovery. No recovery, no reward. No reward, no ledger burned, no clean start, no boat south where nobody knew his mother's name.

He should say something. That was the thought that kept circling. He was awake, he'd heard, and crew told crew things — that was the whole point of crew, wasn't it, that was what Ansel had pulled him out of the river to prove. All he had to do was sit up and say, *before you decide that, you should know there's another door,* and show them the knife he'd been holding.

Instead he lay still and watched the candle flame stand straight in the dead air and did the math one more time, slower.

If the map burned: no reward. But also — and this was the part that kept snagging — also no Corvane. No navy crawling up the pass behind them, no sergeant with the eyes of a man counting ammunition, no hearings. Just three people walking down out of the mountains with a page of names and nothing else, owing nothing to anybody.

If the map survived: reward, maybe, if he could get word out. But Corvane would come for it, and Corvane had already shown at the high col that he could take whatever he wanted whenever he wanted it. The reward existed because the navy wanted the property *controlled*, not recovered. Tam had read the posting closely. Controlled. Which meant whoever handed it over got paid and then got watched forever, because you don't pay a man for a secret and then let him walk around owning it.

He turned the numbers over and over and they wouldn't settle. Every column balanced wrong. Because the problem wasn't arithmetic anymore, and that was the real discovery of the night, lying in the cold listening to two people plan a mercy he couldn't afford: somewhere between the river and this crevasse, the equation had changed. There were variables in it now that didn't reduce to coin. The weight of Ansel diving into glacial water without deciding to. Mara's voice saying *the names we carry* like a vow. The specific, unbearable fact that these two strangers had become, without paperwork or ceremony, the closest thing to family he'd had since his mother died — and family, he was learning, was just another kind of debt, except the only one where you wanted to keep paying.

So he said nothing.

He lay there and said nothing, and hated himself a little for it, and told himself it wasn't betrayal, it was just silence, silence wasn't a thing you did, it was a thing you failed to do — and knew even as he thought it that this was exactly the lie people told themselves right up until the moment silence became the loudest thing they'd ever done.

The candle burned down. Ansel's watch ended. Mara took over without a word passing between them, and Tam stared at the ceiling of blue ice and did not sleep at all.

* * *

Morning — if it was morning — brought no change in the light, because there was no light. The whiteout held like a held breath. They rationed breakfast to half portions and Tam volunteered to check the tarp line, mostly to have somewhere to put his hands and his face where nobody could study him.

Outside was apocalypse. The wind had sculpted the surface into blades and ridges of packed snow, and the visibility was maybe ten feet, maybe less. He worked the anchors, cleared the drift pressing against the tarp, and stood there a moment letting the storm hit him, because it felt like a fair punishment, though he couldn't have said for what.

When he crawled back in, Ansel was trying to rewrap her shoulder one-handed and losing the argument with the bandage. Without thinking about it — without letting himself think about it — Tam knelt and took over, winding the wrap tight the way he'd seen the port medic do it, firm and even.

"Good hands," Ansel said.

"Crew," he said.

Her eyes came up to his face and stayed there a beat too long, and Tam felt something cold slide through his stomach, because Ansel Vasquez missed nothing, ever, and he suddenly couldn't remember what his own face was doing.

"You sleep?" she asked.

"Some."

"Liar," she said, but gently, and let it go, and that gentleness was worse than an interrogation.

* * *

The storm broke on the third day, mid-morning by the candle-clock they'd stopped trusting, all at once, like a curtain torn down. Light poured into the crevasse mouth, blinding after the long dark, and outside the world had been remade — drifts reshaped, the route up toward the col buried under cornices that hadn't been there before, the whole mountainside gleaming and treacherous and new.

Mara stood at the entrance a long time, reading the slope the way she read charts. "We lost three days," she said finally. "Food says we have one and a half. The col says four, maybe five, the way that cornice is hanging."

"Then we move fast and eat thin," Ansel said.

"There's another option." Mara turned. Her face was doing something complicated. "Sefu's shortcut. The one that dead-ended at the rockfall. It doesn't dead-end if you go *over* the rockfall — I've been studying the contours since the shortcut. There's a chimney on the left side, maybe climbable, maybe not, that cuts two days off this route."

"A rockfall Sefu swore was natural," Ansel said, "that we both looked at and agreed was man-made."

"Yes."

"Someone sealed that path on purpose, and you want to climb over their seal."

"I want us to not starve," Mara said simply. "Sealed paths keep secrets. Starving people don't care about secrets."

Tam looked from one to the other, and the knife fit his hand again, heavier than ever — because a shortcut meant speed, speed meant the pass, the pass meant the moment of choosing arriving sooner, and every hour he stayed silent was an hour borrowed against a decision Ansel had already made without knowing he was holding the other half of it.

"Show me the chimney," Ansel said at last.

While they packed, Tam checked the rope coils, and his fingers found the little tin box sewn into his jacket lining where he kept the things that mattered — his mother's ring, a spare striker, and the scrap of paper from Last Port with the reward notice copied out in his careful hand, the number written on it big enough to live in.

He didn't throw it away. He wanted to be honest with himself about that too. He didn't throw it away.

But he didn't look at it either. He buttoned the lining shut over it, shouldered the pack, and followed the others up onto the bright killing slope, toward the sealed path, carrying the one secret the family couldn't afford.
