## 14. The Navy's Mountain

Corvane had learned one thing in twenty years of wearing the uniform: the mountain always told you who you were before it let you pass.

The high col sat at four thousand meters, a saddle of gray scree between two shoulders of black rock, and by the time his team reached it the wind had stripped every soft thought out of his head. He stood with his binoculars braced against the gusts and watched three figures crawl up the far side of the saddle like insects on a plate — one broad-shouldered, moving with a diver's economy; one tall and deliberate, testing each foothold like she was reading it; one small and quick, half-buried under a pack nearly his own size.

Vasquez. Okonkwo. The boy.

"They're going to make the col before dark," Sergeant Ibarra said beside him. She had her rifle slung and her hands free, which was how she preferred to travel — ready to work rather than ready to shoot. "Sir, we can be on them in twenty minutes. They're exhausted. Vasquez is favoring that shoulder. We take the map now, we turn them around, and this is over."

"And then what?"

Ibarra blinked against the wind. "Then it's over, sir."

Corvane lowered the binoculars. Below him, on the near side of the col, his six-person team was digging in for the night among the rocks, tarps snapping, stove flame guttering blue in the wind. Above him, the sky had gone the color of gunmetal, and somewhere past the western ridge a storm was assembling itself out of nothing, the way storms did up here — patient, total, indifferent to orders.

"Ibarra," he said, "what happens if we take the map now?"

"We destroy it. That's the order."

"That's the order I wrote." He handed her the binoculars. "Look at them. Tell me what you see."

She looked for a long moment. "Three people who shouldn't have made it this far."

"Wrong. You see three people who *did* make it this far. Through the river, through Sefu's shortcut, through weather that killed two of my porters last season. Do you know why they made it?" He didn't wait for her to answer. "Because they think they're chasing something. A cache. A fortune, maybe — the boy certainly thinks so. And people chasing something will walk through hell to reach it. People being chased will stop at the first hard ground and fight, or run, or die, and none of those outcomes gets me what I need."

"What you need," Ibarra said carefully, "is for that map to cease to exist."

"Yes." Corvane turned back to the ridge. The three figures had reached the saddle now, tiny against the sky, and he watched the small one — Tam — plant something in the scree. A cairn stone, maybe. Or a marker of their own. Children built monuments everywhere they went; it was one of the things he'd never managed to train out of himself either. "But a destroyed map proves nothing. A burned map is just ash. Ash doesn't answer questions, Ibarra. It creates them. Forty years from now some archivist finds the expedition file, finds my signature on the destruction order, and asks: what were you so afraid of?"

"Sir—"

"My father," Corvane said, "led eleven men into that range and brought back four. The official record says avalanche. The official record says weather. The official record says nobody could have done more." He heard his own voice go flat, the way it did when he was holding something down. "I have read that record two hundred times. I wrote half of it myself when they asked me to update the file after his death. And every time I signed my name under his, I wondered whether I was honoring him or burying him deeper."

The wind gusted. Somewhere below, a tarp tore loose and someone swore.

"So no," Corvane said. "We don't take the map tonight. We let them open the cache."

Ibarra stared at him. In eight years she had questioned maybe five of his orders, and he could count them all, because she did it the way she did everything — precisely, and only when it mattered. "You want them to find it."

"I want *someone* to find it. They've earned the door. I couldn't find it in six years of searching, and neither could the navy's survey teams, and neither could Hollis, apparently, until he stopped looking for it and started running from it." He watched the three figures descend the far side of the col, dropping out of sight toward the glacier basin where his maps said the coordinates lay. "They'll open it. Vasquez will open it because she promised a dying man she would. Okonkwo will open it because she's spent six years reconstructing that route and she needs to know if she got it right — that's not greed, Sergeant, that's a cartographer's religion. And the boy will open it because he wants to belong to something bigger than himself."

"And when they do? When it's documents instead of gold?"

"Then I'll be standing there," Corvane said, "when they learn what it is. And I'll decide, in that moment, with my own eyes, what the truth is worth." He pulled his collar up against the wind. "That's not something anyone can do for me. Not an order. Not a destruction directive. Not even you."

* * *

They came down off the col at first light, moving fast on the hard crust, and by midmorning Corvane could see the basin below them — a wide white bowl cradling the glacier's tongue, ringed by seracs that leaned over it like rotten teeth. His forward scout, Okafor, radioed back that the trio had camped at the basin's edge overnight and broken camp an hour ago, heading for a line of cairns along the eastern moraine.

"They're following the moraine like it's marked on a chart," Okafor reported. "Because it is, sir. Someone marked it. Stones stacked knee-high every fifty meters, fresh enough that the lichen hasn't taken."

"Hollis," Corvane said.

"You think he came back?" Ibarra asked.

"He came back once. Twenty years ago, alone, in summer, and turned around at the icefall. I found his turnaround note in the port authority archive in Kessel — he'd filed a route report nobody ever read." Corvane checked his watch. "He knew where the door was. He just couldn't open it alone. So he went looking for someone who could, and he found a salvage diver with debts and a promise-shaped hole in her life."

"You sound like you admire her."

"I sound like I've read her file." He started down the slope. "Ansel Vasquez. Two commendations for rescue diving, both declined. One disciplinary action for refusing a navy contract on a sealed site — she walked off a paid job rather than touch it. She owes money to men who break fingers, and she still wouldn't dive where they told her to. What does that tell you?"

Ibarra considered. "That she has a code."

"It tells me she'll keep a promise to a dead man all the way up a mountain. Which means she'll also keep the second half of it." He paused at the edge of the icefall, where the glacier cracked and groaned beneath them like something waking. "Hollis gave her two options, Ibarra. Carry it or burn it. Everyone assumes she'll carry. I'm not sure everyone's right."

"Which do you want her to do?"

Corvane looked at the basin, at the line of cairns marching along the moraine toward whatever the ice had kept for forty years, and found that he genuinely didn't know. That frightened him more than the storm did.

"I want to be there," he said. "Whatever she chooses. I want to see it made."

* * *

They gained the moraine by early afternoon, and that was when the weather turned.

It didn't arrive so much as assemble — a wall of cloud boiling over the western ridge, swallowing the sun in minutes, dropping the temperature ten degrees while Corvane watched. His radio crackled with the coast station's voice, thin and urgent: severe system inbound, all high-route parties advised to shelter, window closing.

"Window closing," Ibarra repeated, lowering the handset. "Sir, if that hits before they reach the coordinates—"

"It won't hit before they reach the coordinates." Corvane raised the binoculars again. Far ahead, perhaps two kilometers along the moraine, the three figures had stopped. Even at that distance he could read the shape of what was happening: the tall one — Okonkwo — bent over something in her hands, the map presumably, while Vasquez stood facing the ice. Then Vasquez pointed, and all three of them began to move with sudden purpose, leaving the moraine and cutting straight across the glacier's shoulder toward a cluster of stones at the base of the headwall.

A cairn. But bigger than the route markers. Bigger than any trail cairn had a right to be.

"There," Corvane said quietly. "There it is."

Okafor came up beside him, breathing hard, snow already starting to streak his sleeves. "Orders, sir? We can close the distance. Twenty minutes, maybe fifteen if we push."

Corvane did the arithmetic he'd been doing since the col. Fifteen minutes put his team arriving at the cache in full view, weapons visible, storm front five minutes behind them. It put Vasquez and her people with their backs to an ice wall, cornered, exhausted, and — critically — *unopened*. Whatever was inside those crates would stay inside them, sealed by fear or force or simply by the storm, and he would have traded the only chance he would ever get to stand in front of the truth for the satisfaction of control.

His father had controlled everything. That was the family disease. Control the route, control the rations, control the men — and when the mountain took the men anyway, control the story afterward. Corvane had spent his whole career perfecting the same instinct, and here, at four thousand meters, with the snow coming down sideways and the answer to forty years of questions sitting under a pile of stones two kilometers away, he finally understood that control was just another way of never finding out.

"No," he said. "We hold here."

"Sir—" Ibarra started.

"We hold." He turned to face her, and made himself say the rest of it plainly, because she deserved plain speech and because saying it aloud would bind him to it. "Sergeant, I am ordering this team to stand down and observe. We let them open the depot. We let them read what's in it. When the storm breaks, we approach — openly, unarmed if I can manage it — and we ask to see the ledger. If my father's name is in it, I will read it with my own eyes, and then we will decide together what leaves this mountain and what stays. If his name isn't in it—" His throat tightened. "If it isn't, then I owe that woman an apology and a safe escort home, and I'll give her both."

Ibarra studied him for a long moment, snow gathering on her shoulders, and Corvane realized she wasn't deciding whether to obey. She was deciding whether to believe him.

"And if the sergeant disagrees with the lieutenant," she said at last, "the sergeant notes her objection for the record."

"Noted."

"For the record, sir: I think letting them open it is a mistake. I think whatever's in there should have stayed buried, and I think you're gambling your career and possibly our lives on a salvage diver's sense of honor." She paused. "And I think you're right that we'd never forgive ourselves for burning it unread. So. Holding position."

"Thank you, Sergeant."

"Don't thank me yet." She nodded past him, toward the headwall. "Look."

Corvane turned. Through the thickening snow, at the base of the great stone cairn, the three figures had begun to dig — and even at two kilometers' distance, even through the murk, he could see that they'd found something. The boy was hauling at a slab of ice with a pry bar. Okonkwo had her hands inside a gap in the stones. And Vasquez stood slightly apart, one arm hanging at her side, the other pressed flat against the rock as if she were listening to it — listening for whatever the mountain had been keeping all these years, and whether it was ready to speak.

The storm swallowed the ridge behind them. The temperature fell. And Corvane, who had chased this mountain his entire adult life, stood in the falling snow two kilometers short of the answer and did the hardest thing he had ever done in uniform.

He waited.

* * *

By dusk the wind was screaming and visibility had dropped to fifty meters, and the camp became a small island of canvas and stove-light in a white void. Corvane sat in his tent with the expedition's original file open on his knees — the real one, the one he'd carried out of the archive himself, unauthorized, folded against his chest like contraband — and read his father's handwriting by lamplight for the hundredth time.

*Position untenable. Decision made at 0400. Details to follow.*

No details ever followed. Four survivors, eleven names on a memorial plaque, one sentence in an official record, and a son who had spent thirty years turning that single sentence over like a stone, looking for what lived underneath.

Outside, Okafor's voice rose above the wind, calling from the observation post they'd dug into the moraine. Corvane ducked out into the white roar and followed it.

Okafor was prone behind a windbreak of stones, binoculars useless now, watching by the faint glow of a light source far across the basin — a headlamp, maybe two, moving at the base of the headwall where the cairn stood.

"They're in, sir," Okafor shouted. "Or they're about to be. There's a gap in the ice. Big enough for a person."

Corvane knelt in the snow beside him and looked, and saw nothing but storm and distance and a smudge of light that might have been anything. But he knew. The way you knew a hatch was open before you heard it — pressure changing, the whole night shifting its weight.

Somewhere under that light, Ansel Vasquez was opening the navy's mountain. Somewhere under that light, his father's last decision was waiting in a crate, typed or handwritten, signed or unsigned, damning or clean.

"Keep the watch," Corvane said. "Wake me if the light moves."

He went back to his tent and lay down on top of his sleeping bag, boots on, file under his hand, and listened to the storm try to tear the mountain apart. Sleep didn't come. What came instead, sometime deep in the white hours, was a memory he hadn't visited in years: his father at the kitchen table, the night before the expedition shipped out, sharpening a pencil with a small knife and saying, *Records are just promises that outlive the people who make them, Elias. Make sure yours are the kind you'd want kept.*

In the morning, the storm would break or it wouldn't. The light across the basin would move or it wouldn't. And Lieutenant Elias Corvane would walk down onto that glacier carrying nothing but his name, and ask a debt-ridden salvage diver to show him what his father had done.

Across the basin, faint through the wind, he thought he heard the crack of old ice giving way.

It sounded almost like a door.
