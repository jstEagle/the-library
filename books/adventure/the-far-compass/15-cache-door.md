## 15. Cache Door

The coordinates put them on a shelf of black rock above the cloud line, and the cloud line, that morning, was a floor.

Ansel had lived her whole life under weather. Weather was a ceiling you bumped your head on, a wall you leaned into, a thing that came at you sideways with intent. She had never stood *on* it. Below their boots the cloud sea ran white to every horizon, moving in slow tectonic swells, and the peaks around them rose through it like the masts of drowned ships. The sun came off it hard enough to make her squint through salt-cracked lips. It was, she thought, what the surface must look like to a man going down — light without bottom, silence without mercy.

"Coordinates say forty meters northeast," Mara said. She had the map out despite the wind, weighted at four corners with stones, her gloved finger tracing the last leg Hollis had drawn sixty years ago. Her handwriting habit had transferred itself to everything; she'd annotated the margin in pencil, small and neat: *cairn?*

"You trust his numbers?" Tam asked. He was gray-lipped and hadn't eaten much at the cold camp, but he'd carried the rope coil all morning without complaint, which was more than Ansel could have managed two weeks ago.

"I trust that a dying man didn't have time to lie about arithmetic," Mara said. "Lies take space. He only had room for truth."

They found the cairn at forty-one meters.

It was bigger than Ansel expected — not a trail marker but a structure, waist-high and wide as a table, built from flat slabs fitted with a mason's patience. Lichen had gotten into the seams and dyed them orange, so the whole thing looked like something grown rather than stacked, like it had been here since before the mountain had an opinion about people. Someone had loved this work. Someone had wanted it found by the right person and missed by everyone else.

Nobody touched it right away. They stood around it in a loose triangle, breathing hard from the altitude, and Ansel felt the same stillness come over the three of them that used to come over a dive boat when the wreck first showed up on the sonar — that moment where the thing you've been chasing stops being a story and starts being a place.

"Well," Tam said. "Somebody's got to."

He looked at Ansel. Of course he did. She was the one who opened things for a living.

She knelt. The top slabs came away easier than they should have, and she understood why: they'd been designed to be moved, keyed so one person with numb fingers could lift them in order. Underneath, packed into a shaft lined with flat stones, was a plug of ice, blue-green and glassy, and down inside the ice —

Metal. A corner of it. A seam running straight as a surveyor's line.

"It's a door," Ansel said. "It's not a cache. It's a *building*."

* * *

Getting into it took the rest of the day, and it took Ansel's whole strange inheritance — every hour she'd spent learning how things seal and how they fail.

The depot wasn't buried; it had been *drowned*. Whoever built it had cut it into the rock below grade and roofed it, and over six decades the mountain's meltwater had found it, frozen, and entombed it. The ice plug filled the entry shaft like water fills a hold. You couldn't dig it out. You could only do what she'd done ten thousand times in warm water: read the pressure, find the flaw, let the material tell you where it wanted to break.

She worked with the pry bar and then with bare hands, chipping a channel along the shaft wall where the ice had pulled away from stone, and the cold went through her gloves like the gloves were a rumor. Tam kept a small fire going off to the side and fed her hot water sweetened with the last of the sugar, and she drank it because he made her, not because she wanted anything ever again. Twice her shoulder — the bad one, the river shoulder — locked up mid-stroke and she had to sit back on her heels and breathe through it while Mara held the injured arm and worked the joint with her thumbs, saying nothing, which was its own kind of talking.

By late afternoon the channel was deep enough. Ansel set the bar, said the thing she always said — "on three, nobody help me" — and leaned.

The ice let go all at once, the way ice does, with a crack that rolled off the peaks across the cloud sea and came back twice. Cold air fell out of the hole, old air, air that had been holding its breath since before Tam was born. It smelled of metal and kerosene and paper and, faintly, impossibly, of coffee — the ghost of coffee, sixty years stale, soaked into wood.

Then it smelled of nothing, because Ansel was already climbing down inside.

* * *

The depot was one room, maybe eight meters by five, cut square into the mountain. Her headlamp beam swept across it and her breath stopped in her throat, and she was glad no one could see her face, because what she felt wasn't awe exactly. It was recognition.

She knew this room. Every diver knows this room. It was a hold, sealed and intact, and everything in it was exactly where living hands had left it.

Crates, stenciled with the expedition crest — the same crest as the strongbox on the navy wreck, the wheel-and-star she'd been pulling mystery out of the sea for months. Bedrolls rolled tight. A cook stove, disassembled and greased against the cold, wrapped in oilcloth. Tins of fuel ranked like ammunition. And crates, dozens of them, and the nearest one had been opened and resealed, and the stencil on its side did not say AMMUNITION or RATIONS.

It said RECORDS.

"Mara," she called up the shaft, and her voice came out wrong, too loud in the dead air. "Get down here. Bring the light."

Mara came down the way she did everything, precisely, and stopped beside Ansel, and Ansel watched six years of reconstruction land on her face all at once. Her hand came up and hovered over the crate stencil without touching it, the way you hover over a relic in a museum, the way you hover over a sleeping person.

"This is the survey depot," Mara whispered. "From the route. I mapped this room. I mapped it from *nothing* — from a supply manifest and a guess. I drew it in my notebook when I was twenty-four." Her voice cracked on the number. "It's real. It was real the whole time."

"There's more," Ansel said, and hated how flat her voice sounded, and couldn't help it. Diver's discipline. You don't gasp at the pretty coral. You look for what's wrong.

Because something was wrong. Her beam kept snagging on details that her gut flagged before her mind caught up. The bedrolls — four of them, unrolled, slept in, never repacked. A mug on the stove ledge with a brown ring dried inside it. A deck of cards spread face-down across a crate lid, mid-game, abandoned. And against the far wall, the thing that made the cold walk up her spine on legs:

A radio. Field transceiver, crated, the crate nailed shut, shipping seals intact. Still packed. Never opened.

A radio operator's station, and the radio operator had never set it up.

"Hollis was the radio man," she said slowly. "That's what the journal said. So why is his radio still in a box?"

Mara turned in a slow circle, beam sweeping. "Because he never reached this room," she said. "These are the others' things. Four men. Four bedrolls." She stopped. "Ansel. Where's the fifth?"

The expedition had been six strong. They both knew it. They'd read the roster a dozen times between them.

Ansel pried the RECORDS crate open with the flat of the bar, gently, the way she'd open anything that might be rigged or rotten. Inside, wrapped in waxed paper, were bundles of documents — pay ledgers with the navy's own stamp, requisition forms, ration returns, a thick envelope of letters bound with string, envelopes addressed in a woman's hand to a Sergeant E. Corvane, and other names she didn't know yet. On top lay a single sheet, typed, dated eleven days after the depot's last supply entry:

*All personnel accounted for at this position. Request extraction. Weather window closing.*

Signed by the commanding officer. Corvane senior. Filed and never answered — or answered and never shown.

"They waited," Ansel heard herself say. "They got here. They sent word. And somebody left them here."

Above them, faint through the open shaft, Tam's voice cracked across the silence — high, scared, wrong:

"Ansel! Mara! Get up here — there's someone coming up the glacier. Navy blue. A lot of it."

Ansel looked at the ledger in her hands, at the dead men's cards still laid out for a game nobody finished, and felt the awe drain out of her like warmth out of wet cloth, leaving only the diver's oldest knowledge: *you are not alone down here, and the thing that shares the water with you has been patient a very long time.*

She tucked the ledger inside her jacket, next to her skin, and started up the shaft toward the light.
