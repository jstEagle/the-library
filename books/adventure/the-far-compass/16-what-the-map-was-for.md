## 16. What the Map Was For

The storm was still an hour out when Mara understood the map.

She'd had it spread across the floor of the ice depot's antechamber, weighted at the corners with four ration tins, her breath fogging the vellum in slow pulses. Outside, through the cairn's low entrance, the cloud sea rolled and broke against the col like something alive. Inside, the air was dead still, cold enough to make her fingers stupid, and she'd been staring at Hollis's marginalia for two hours without seeing it.

Then she saw it.

Not because the writing got clearer. Because she stopped reading it as a cartographer and started reading it as a woman who had spent six years reconstructing a dead man's route from fragments — and knew what it looked like when someone drew a map that wasn't meant to navigate anyone anywhere.

"Hollis," she said aloud, to no one, to him. "You clever, frightened old man."

* * *

The map itself was beautiful work — better than beautiful, better than anything Mara could have produced under pressure, which was the first thing that should have told her. Survey-grade contour lines. Bearings corrected for declination with a precision that took either years of local observation or instruments the expedition hadn't carried past the second camp. A dying man in a borrowed bunk on a salvage boat doesn't draw like that from memory alone. He draws like that because he's been drawing it for thirty years, over and over, refining it, until every line is load-bearing.

And the load it bore wasn't treasure.

Mara traced the route with a numb fingertip. The cache coordinates sat at the center, yes — but around them, in ink so faint she'd needed Tam's headlamp angled flat across the vellum to catch it, ran a second system of marks. Not waypoints. Tally strokes. Small clusters of them, scattered along the route like lichen, each cluster beside a place-name written in Hollis's cramped hand: *the moraine below Kettle Pass. The blue icefall. The shelf above the second lake.*

Thirty-one clusters. She'd counted twice.

Thirty-one men.

The expedition manifest — what survived of it, what Corvane's navy had let survive of it — listed thirty-two souls. Hollis, the radio operator, made thirty-two. Which meant the map marked every place along the route where they had found or left or lost one of the others. Every body. Every grave that wasn't a grave. Every spot where a man sat down in the snow and the expedition — what was left of it — walked on.

And beside each cluster, in that same faint hand, a few words. She copied them out now in her notebook because her hands were shaking too badly to trust herself with the original:

*Baker. Stayed at the icefall. Would not go on.*

*Dunmore. Fell. We could not dig.*

*Rivas. Gave his rations to the last three. Sat down after.*

On and on. Thirty-one small epitaphs in pencil, pressed so lightly into the vellum that only a certain angle of light would raise them — as if even the ink had been afraid of being read.

"It's not a treasure chart," Mara said, when Ansel came in from checking the lines. "It never was."

Ansel stamped snow off her boots and crouched beside her, shoulder stiff, face raw from wind. "Then what is it?"

"A deposition." Mara heard her own voice come out strange, formal, like she was reading someone else's finding. "He couldn't write it down as testimony — Corvane's people would have burned paper. But a map? A map goes in a drawer. A map looks like money." She touched the tally strokes, one cluster, then the next. "He drew the route so precisely that whoever carried it couldn't help but walk the same ground. Past every one of them. And when they got here—" she nodded toward the inner chamber, where the crates waited under their shroud of ice dust, "—they'd find the ledgers, and the letters, and they'd understand what the little marks meant. It's a confession he hid inside a navigation aid. He wanted someone to *walk it*. To say the names out loud over the ground where they lie."

Ansel was quiet for a long moment. Wind worried at the cairn's entrance, a low moan through stone.

"He made me promise to carry it or burn it," she said finally. "I thought those were two different things."

"They were the same thing," Mara said. "That's what I didn't understand either. Carrying it *was* the testimony. The walking was the reading." She looked up. "He wasn't asking you to deliver a document, Ansel. He was asking you to attend a funeral thirty years late."

Tam appeared in the entrance, cheeks whipped red, and froze when he saw their faces. "What?"

"Come here," Mara said, and showed him. She watched the boy count the clusters himself, lips moving, and watched him arrive at thirty-one and stop, and look up with an expression far too old for his face.

"That's why the navy buried it," he said.

"That's why the navy buried it," Mara agreed. "Not gold. Not weapons. Just the record of an order given and men abandoned above the snowline." She paused. "And one name missing from these marks. One man who walked down off this mountain while thirty-one others didn't."

Tam looked at her. Then, carefully, at the inner chamber door, where somewhere among the crates lay a pay ledger with a captain's signature on its final page.

"Corvane's father," Tam said.

"Corvane's father," Mara said.

Outside, the light had gone the flat pewter color that meant the storm was no longer coming. It was arriving.

* * *

They worked by headlamp after that, hauling the crates toward the antechamber — not to take everything, there wasn't time and Ansel's shoulder wouldn't allow it, but to open the critical ones and verify. Mara insisted on verification. Six years of reconstruction had taught her that hope was the least reliable instrument in her kit.

The third crate gave them the pay ledger. Canvas-bound, water-stained, every entry in a clerk's neat hand until the final week, where the handwriting changed and became hurried and became, on the last legible page, a single line:

*Provisions insufficient for full complement. Detachment to proceed to coast for relief. Remainder to hold high camp.*

Signed: *Capt. E. Corvane.*

No date of return planned. No rations allocated to the remainder. No relief ever dispatched — the navy's own inquiry, the one Mara had spent two years prying fragments of from sealed archives, recorded the expedition as lost to weather, all hands, regrettable, file closed.

"Detachment to proceed to coast," Ansel read over her shoulder. "How many went down with him?"

Mara had already done the arithmetic, days ago, from the fragmentary rosters. "Four. Plus the captain. Five walked out. Thirty-one held the high camp." She closed the ledger gently, the way you close a coffin lid. "And the man who signed it came home and raised a son and told him nothing, or told him lies, and the son grew up and joined the same navy and spent his career making sure nobody ever asked."

"You feel sorry for him," Ansel said. It wasn't quite an accusation.

"I feel sorry for everyone on this mountain," Mara said. "Including us."

Tam, at the entrance, said: "Someone's coming up the col."

* * *

Mara had imagined this moment perhaps forty times across six years — the confrontation, the truth surfacing, the guilty called to account. In none of those imaginings had she pictured Lieutenant Corvane climbing alone out of a whiteout with frost in his eyebrows and his service pistol held low and wrong, the way a man holds a weapon he's hoping he won't have to remember how to use.

But he wasn't quite alone. Behind him, half-swallowed by the blowing snow, moved the bulk of his sergeant, and behind the sergeant, shapes — three, maybe four more, fanned out with the unhurried competence of professionals closing a trap they'd already sprung.

"Inside," Corvane said. Not a request. His voice was stripped down to wire. "All of you. Now."

They retreated into the antechamber. He followed, weapon tracking between the three of them with a steadiness that contradicted everything else about him, and his eyes went immediately — Mara watched them go — to the open crate, the canvas-bound ledger, the page turned to that final line.

"Don't," Ansel said.

"Don't what?" Corvane's laugh had no humor in it at all. "Don't look? I've climbed nine days through country your map says can't be crossed before the snows. I've burned my career doing it. I'm going to look." He stepped forward, and Mara saw that his hands were shaking — the gun steady, the man shaking, some division running straight through the middle of him. "Read it to me."

"What?" Mara said.

"The ledger." He wasn't looking at her. He was looking at the book as if it were a well and he was standing at the edge. "You're the cartographer. You've read it. I watched you close it just now like it was — like it mattered how it was closed." His jaw worked. "I need to know if my father's name is in it. That's all. That is the only thing I have climbed this mountain to know."

"Sir," the sergeant said from the entrance, snow streaming off his shoulders, "we should secure the documents and—"

"In a minute, Sergeant." Corvane didn't turn. "Read it to me."

Mara looked at Ansel. Ansel looked back, and something passed between them that had no words in it yet — because they both understood, in the same instant, that they were holding the detonator to whatever was left of this man, and that setting it off might be mercy and might be catastrophe and there was no way to know which until after.

Wind slammed the cairn entrance. Somewhere above, the storm found its voice properly, a long descending roar off the summit fields, and the temperature in the antechamber dropped as if the mountain had opened a door.

Mara picked up the ledger.

She opened it to the final page. She held it where Corvane could see, and she watched his eyes move down the clerk-neat columns — names, rations, dates — and reach the changed handwriting, and stop.

"*Provisions insufficient for full complement,*" she read aloud, because that was what the map had always demanded of someone, in the end. Reading aloud. "*Detachment to proceed to coast for relief. Remainder to hold high camp.*"

She paused.

"Read the signature," Corvane said. Very quietly. The gun had drifted downward without his seeming to notice. "Please."

"*Capt. E. Corvane.*"

The lieutenant closed his eyes. For a moment he looked less like a pursuing officer than like a man standing at a graveside thirty years late, hearing for the first time the words that had actually been spoken over the hole. When he opened them again they were wet, and furious about being wet, and fixed on the middle distance where, presumably, a childhood was rearranging itself around a new fact.

"He wrote it down," Corvane said. "He *wrote it down.* He knew. He sat in that tent and he wrote it down and he signed it and he walked down the mountain and he—" His voice failed and restarted. "I used to shine his boots for inspection. I used to—"

"Sir." The sergeant again, closer now, and his voice had changed — flatter, harder, the voice of a man who has decided his officer is no longer reliable. "Sir, we need to secure the site. All materials are to be destroyed per standing order. Sir, I need you to step away from the civilians."

Corvane didn't move. Snow blew in past the sergeant's shoulders and the light outside was going from pewter to nothing, the whiteout swallowing the col whole, and Mara saw the sergeant's hand drift toward the flare pistol on his hip — not a weapon for shooting people, but very much a weapon for burning paper, and tents, and caches, and everything else.

"Lieutenant," the sergeant said.

Corvane opened his mouth —

— and the mountain answered first. The storm hit the cairn like a fist, a pressure change that popped Mara's ears and snuffed both headlamps and sent the ration-tin weights skittering off the map, and in the sudden absolute dark she heard Ansel's breath catch, and Tam shout, and the sergeant swear, and beneath it all, faint and rising, the sound of the high icefields letting go somewhere above them — a long white thunder rolling down toward the col.

The last thing Mara felt, before the dark took the room entirely, was the ledger being pulled gently out of her hands.
