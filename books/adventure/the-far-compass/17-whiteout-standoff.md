## 17. Whiteout Standoff

The storm did not arrive so much as fall on us — one minute the sky above the col was the color of dirty pewter, and the next it was gone entirely, replaced by a white that had no top and no bottom.

I'd been in weather before. I'd been in water you couldn't see your own hands in, in current that argued like a river, in a navy wreck at dawn with my air running low. But I had never stood inside a whiteout on a mountain with a gun drawn twenty feet away and a crate of dead men's letters open at my feet.

"Corvane." Mara's voice came thin through the wind. "The ledger. Look at the ledger."

He was already looking. Lieutenant Corvane knelt in the snow with his gloves off — I remember that detail because it seemed insane, bare hands in that cold — turning the pages of the pay ledger with a care that made my chest hurt to watch. The wind tore at his collar. He didn't notice. His sergeant, a slab of a man named Dreke whose face I'd only seen as a shape behind Corvane's shoulder since the pass, stood three paces back with his rifle slung and his hands flexing open and shut like he was warming them on something that wasn't there.

Tam crouched beside me at the depot entrance, half in, half out of the ice-sealed doorway, breath ragged. His eyes kept going from the rifle to the crates to me, and back to the rifle. He was sixteen years old and he was doing arithmetic with his life, and I could see every line of the sum on his face.

The wind screamed through the col and for a moment none of us existed except as noise.

Then Corvane spoke, and even bent over the ledger I heard him clearly, because his voice had changed. It had lost the polish. What was left underneath was younger and much worse.

"Vasquez Company," he read. "Third of September. Rations issued: full complement." A page turned. "Fifth of September. Rations issued: half complement. Note in margin — *senior officer party departed with reserve stores at 0400.*"

He stopped.

Mara didn't move toward him. She'd learned by then — we all had — that Corvane was a man you circled, never approached. She said, quietly, into the wind: "Keep reading."

"There are forty-one names on the muster roll." His voice was very flat now, the way water is flat right before it goes over the edge. "There are thirty-two names in the burial record. My father signed the departure order."

* * *

I want to say I felt sorry for him. Part of me did — the part that had spent thirty years underwater looking for things other people had sunk and wanted forgotten. But most of me was watching Dreke.

Because Dreke had stopped flexing his hands. He had gone still in the way men go still when they've finished deciding something, and his stillness was pointed at the crates.

"Sir," Dreke said. It wasn't quite loud enough for politeness. Corvane didn't hear him or chose not to. "Sir. We should secure the site."

"I'm reading, Sergeant."

"The storm's building. Command expects—"

"Command," Corvane said, without looking up, "expects a report that this cache does not exist. That has been the expectation for twenty-six years. You may note the irony that they sent the son of the man who—" He stopped himself. His jaw worked. "Keep reading, Lieutenant," he said to himself, bitterly, and turned another page.

Dreke's hand moved to the rifle sling.

I don't know how I knew what he was about to do before he knew it himself. Maybe it's a diver's thing — you spend enough time in black water and you develop an ear for the moment a situation turns, a pressure change in the room. Or maybe I'd just spent two weeks learning exactly how far a man will go to keep a mountain quiet, and Dreke had the look of a man who had been paid to keep it quiet for a long time.

"Sergeant," I said.

He looked at me. In the whiteout his face was just features floating in nothing, and that made it worse, not better.

"These documents go down the mountain," I said. "All of them. That's the whole point of them. You burn them up here and there's no proof they ever burned anything but paper."

"They're not going anywhere," Dreke said. "Not down. Not anywhere. Command's position hasn't changed in twenty-six years, ma'am, and it isn't going to change because a salvage diver found a box." He unslung the rifle. "Lieutenant. Step away from the materials."

And Corvane — God help me — Corvane finally looked up from the ledger, and I saw that whatever had broken in him had broken all the way through, and what was standing in his uniform now was not an officer of anything. "No," he said.

"Sir—"

"My father left them," Corvane said. Each word came out like a stone being set down. "He took the reserve stores and he walked out and he let thirty-two men freeze waiting for a relief column he'd already radioed would never come. And then the navy buried it, and then the navy gave *me* the job of keeping it buried, because that's what we do with our fathers, Sergeant. We carry them." He stood up. The ledger stayed open in the snow between us, pages lifting and falling in the wind like something breathing. "It goes down the mountain."

"It goes nowhere," Dreke said, and brought the rifle up.

* * *

Later — in the weeks after, when Tam told the story at anchorages where nobody believed him anyway — Tam always said he moved first. And he did move first, I'll give him that; I saw him launch out of the doorway like something thrown. But I was already moving too, because I'd watched Dreke's thumb find the selector switch, and there is a sound a rifle makes when it goes from safe to fire that is very small and very final, and I have heard it twice in my life and both times I was moving before I finished hearing it.

What happened next happened in pieces, the way violence does. Not a scene. Shards.

A shard: Tam hitting Dreke around the waist and both of them going down into the snow, the rifle's muzzle swinging skyward, a shot going off into the white that sounded less like a gunshot than like the mountain clearing its throat.

A shard: Mara dragging the ledger out of the snow, clutching it to her chest, screaming something I couldn't hear over the wind.

A shard: me, getting between Dreke and the crates, arms out, which was the single stupidest thing my body has ever done without consulting me, because Dreke was bigger than Tam, angrier than Tam, and trained, and I was none of those things.

A shard: his fist, or his elbow, or the stock of the rifle — I never did establish which — catching me across the bad shoulder. The one the river had given back to me at half price.

Here is what I know about shoulders: they hold more than arms. They hold the whole architecture of a person upright. When mine went, it went with a wet tearing feeling I felt in my teeth, and the world tilted, and I went down on one knee in the snow with my arm hanging wrong and the pain arriving a half-second late like thunder after lightning.

But I did not go down out of the way. That was the thing. I went down *between*. Between Dreke and the crates. Between him and the letters of thirty-two men who had waited in the cold for a rescue that their own commander had cancelled, and who deserved, at absolute minimum, to have someone stand — or kneel, or bleed — between them and one more erasure.

"You'll have to go through me," I said. It came out mostly as breath. The wind took most of it anyway.

Dreke stood over me with the rifle half-raised and snow streaming past him sideways, and for a long moment — three seconds, five, ten, I couldn't count them, time had come apart along with my shoulder — I genuinely believed he was going to do it. Shoot a kneeling woman in a whiteout on a mountain no map admitted to. And the terrible thing, the thing I still can't shake, is that his face didn't say *murderer*. It said *clerk*. It said: this is paperwork. This is what the job requires. Somebody has to initial the line.

Then Tam hit him again.

From behind, around the knees, with everything the boy had, which after two weeks of mountain rations wasn't much but was aimed true. Dreke staggered, turned, swung the stock down — and Corvane caught it.

I saw that clearly, even lying crooked in the snow with my arm folded under me like a dropped tool. I saw Corvane take the rifle stock in both bare hands, inches from my head, and hold it, and the two of them strained against each other in the roaring white, officer against sergeant, and Corvane's face was streaming — snowmelt or something else, impossible to tell in a whiteout, maybe deliberately impossible — and he said, in a voice the wind almost didn't manage to carry:

"They waited eleven days, Sergeant. Eleven days. Read the log. *Read the log.*"

And Dreke let go.

Not all at once. His hands opened finger by finger, like a man untying a knot he'd tied years ago and forgotten why, and the rifle came down, and he stepped back into the white and stood there with the storm pouring over him, breathing hard, staring at nothing.

Nobody moved. The shot's echo had died somewhere below us. The wind filled the whole world.

* * *

Afterward there was blood in the snow — mine, a surprising amount of it, bright and obscene against all that white — and there was the ledger, and there were four people and one boy standing in a circle around a hole in the ice full of dead men's testimony, and no one knew what came next, because no one had planned for the possibility that everyone would stop.

Mara got to me first. Her hands were on my shoulder before I could tell her not to, gentle and clinical at once, and her face was very close to mine and very controlled, which is how I knew she was frightened. "Dislocated?" she asked.

"Out," I agreed, through my teeth. "Put it back."

"In this cold?"

"If you wait it'll swell and you won't be able to. Do it."

She did it. I'm not going to describe it. There are things a person is entitled to leave out of the record, and that is one of mine. What I'll say is that Tam held my other shoulder steady and counted down from three and lied about the number, starting the push on *two*, which is the oldest trick in the world and it worked, and the joint went home with a sound like a hatch sealing, and I lay back in the snow and laughed until it hurt, which took about one second.

When I could sit up, Corvane was kneeling at the depot mouth again. Dreke stood apart, rifle slung, facing away from all of us into the featureless white, a man exiled to a country with no borders.

Corvane had the ledger open to the last page. The muster roll. Forty-one names. He was reading them the way you'd read a letter from someone you loved who died badly — slowly, moving his lips, giving each name its full weight, as if the reading itself were a duty that had been waiting twenty-six years for someone willing to perform it.

The storm was thickening. I could feel it in the way the light failed, the white going from bright to dim to a kind of gray blindness, the temperature dropping through the floor. Whatever window the col had offered us was closing, and closing fast.

"We can't stay here," Mara said. "Another hour in this and none of us walks down."

Corvane didn't answer. He'd reached a name near the bottom of the list — I couldn't see which — and his thumb had stopped on it and stayed there, pressing into the paper hard enough that I thought it might tear.

"Corvane." I got my feet under me, one-armed, Tam bracing me. "The pass seals tonight. Whatever you're deciding, decide it walking."

He looked up at me then. And whatever had been breaking in him had finished breaking, and what remained was quieter than the man who'd hunted us across the range, and stranger, and harder to predict — which frightened me more than the rifle had.

"The ledger comes down," he said. "Officer's word. All of it." Then his eyes went to the map — Hollis's map, rolled in its oilcloth tube inside my jacket, the thing I had promised a dying man I would carry over the range or burn — and stayed there a moment too long.

"And the rest?" I asked.

The wind answered first, a gust that shoved all of us a half-step downslope like a hand. Somewhere above us, muffled by the white, came a deep cracking boom — the cornice on the ridge letting go, or the mountain shifting its weight, or just the cold telling us its opinion.

Corvane closed the ledger and held it out to Mara.

"Get your people off my mountain," he said. "We'll talk about the rest at the bottom — if there is a bottom."

We started down into the blind white, four of us and a boy roped together with salvaged line, and behind us the storm swallowed the cache door whole, and I put my good hand on the map inside my jacket and understood, with total clarity, that the promise I'd made Hollis had one mile left to live.
