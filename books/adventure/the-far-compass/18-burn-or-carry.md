## 18. Burn or Carry

The storm came down the col like something with intent.

One minute the sky was the color of dirty wool and the next it was white — not snow falling so much as the mountain erasing itself around them, wind screaming through the notch where Corvane's sergeant had stood with a rifle an hour ago and where now there was nothing but blowing white and the sound of rock grinding against rock somewhere below.

Ansel got Mara by the collar and hauled her behind the cairn. Tam was already there, curled around the satchel like it was a sibling, his face gray under the frost in his eyebrows.

"Is he—" Mara started.

"Corvane's alive." Ansel had seen him go down when Tam hit the sergeant, seen him roll behind the ice ridge with the ledger clutched to his chest like a man saving a child from a fire. She'd seen the sergeant stagger back into the white with blood on his jaw and murder in his posture. "The other one I don't know."

"The sergeant will come back," Tam said. His voice cracked on *will*. He was sixteen and he'd tackled a soldier and he was shaking so hard his teeth chattered, but he'd done it anyway, and Ansel thought — not for the first time since Kettle Pass — that she would trust this kid with anything she had left.

"Then we move before he does." She looked at the depot mouth behind them, the ice-sealed crates, the radio still packed in its crate like a promise nobody had kept for thirty years. Everything they'd climbed for was sitting right there, and the storm was sealing the pass over it, and none of it mattered anymore because of one thing: the ledger was out of the ice and in Corvane's hands, and Corvane had read it, and Corvane had broken.

She'd watched him break. It hadn't looked like what she expected. It had looked like a man finding his own face in a mirror he'd been avoiding for decades.

"Ansel." Mara grabbed her wrist. Her lips were going blue. "We can't stay here."

"I know."

"We can't carry all of it. The documents, the ledgers — there's forty pounds of paper up that slope and we have maybe two hours of daylight and a man with a rifle—"

"I know."

"Then decide." Mara's eyes were fierce and terrified and absolutely clear, the way they always were when everything else was chaos. That was the thing about cartographers, Ansel was learning. They didn't panic about terrain. They read it. "You made Hollis a promise. Both halves of it. So decide which half you're keeping."

* * *

They got as far as the sheltered side of the cairn, into the lee where the wind couldn't strip the heat off them quite so fast, and Ansel spread the map on a flat stone and weighted its corners with rocks.

Hollis's map. Ink gone brown, paper soft as cloth from thirty years inside a coat pocket. The route drawn in a hand that started confident at the bottom edge and got shakier toward the top, because he'd drawn most of it after, from memory, the only survivor walking down out of these mountains with his dead friends' names in his ears. And in the margins — Mara had taught her to see them — the testimony. Little annotations. *Rations short here.* *Turned back without us.* *Said he'd send help.*

He never sent help.

And beside the cache coordinates, in letters pressed deep enough to score the stone beneath: *SO SOMEONE WILL READ IT ALOUD.*

Ansel knelt over the map with the storm howling above her and felt the weight of two promises land on her shoulders at once, and understood finally that Hollis had known exactly what he was doing when he made her swear both. *Carry it or burn it.* Not because he didn't care which. Because he knew she couldn't know which until she'd seen what was in the cache. He'd handed her the choice the way you hand someone a live coal and say: hold this until you understand why I'm letting go.

Carry it down. Forty pounds of proof through a sealed pass, past a navy lieutenant whose career died the moment that ledger was opened, into a chain of command that had buried this operation once already and could bury it again. Maybe justice. Maybe a courtroom where old men explained away old crimes while the families waited another thirty years. Maybe nothing but a file number in a drawer.

Or burn it. Let the dead rest unaccused. Let Corvane senior be remembered however his son needed to remember him. Let the mountain keep its secret the way it had kept everything else — the expedition, the men, the truth — under ice, clean, finished.

Her shoulder throbbed where the river had nearly taken Tam and her with it. Her hands wouldn't stop shaking, cold and adrenaline and something else, something that had been building since a dying man made her promise two opposite things on the deck of her own boat.

"Whatever you choose," Mara said quietly, kneeling beside her, "I'm with you. I told you at Snowline — I wanted the truth more than the evidence. But Ansel — the truth isn't the paper. The truth is what we know now. All three of us know it. We read it aloud already, in a way. Up there, in the depot. You read the names off the pay ledger yourself."

"And then it dies with us," Ansel said. "Three people who'll spend the rest of their lives knowing, and no one else ever will. Is that enough? For your surveyor?"

Mara was quiet a long moment. Wind screamed across the col. Somewhere above them, a rifle bolt — or just ice cracking; in a whiteout you couldn't tell, and that was its own kind of terror.

"Elias has been dead six years," Mara said finally. "I spent those six years thinking if I found the route, I'd find a reason. Something that made the losing of him mean something." She touched the map's margin, the shaky ink. "This is what makes it mean something. Not a court. This. Someone carrying it. Someone choosing."

Tam crouched on her other side, saying nothing, watching her with those hungry careful eyes. She knew what he saw. She'd heard herself say it in the crevasse camp — *if it comes to that, I'll burn it* — and she'd known he'd heard it too, and known what it cost him, because Tam needed the reward the way drowning men need air. He'd said nothing. He'd carried rope and food and fear up a mountain for a payday that was evaporating in front of him, and he'd said nothing, and that silence was worth more than any salvage she'd ever raised.

"Tam," she said. "There's no reward. You understand that. Whatever happens next, there's no money in this."

"Yeah," he said. "Figured that out around chapter twelve." A ghost of a grin. "You owe me a apprenticeship instead. On the boat. Real wages. Hot food."

"You hate boats."

"I hate being cold more. Boats have engines. Engines are warm." His voice wobbled on the last word and he turned his head away fast, and Ansel put her hand on the back of his neck the way she'd done on the rope over the green stair, without thinking, the way you do for family.

Then she looked at the map.

* * *

Here is what Ansel Vasquez knew about burning things: she'd done it once before, at nineteen, in a harbor town she'd rather forget — burned a letter from a man who'd promised her the world and delivered a debt. She'd stood on a pier and fed it to a brazier and felt nothing afterward except lighter, and she'd spent years telling herself that lightness was freedom when mostly it was just emptiness with good PR.

This would not feel like that. She knew that too.

Because this wasn't a letter from a liar. This was a dead man's last testament, drawn in a shaking hand so that someone — anyone — would finally climb high enough and care enough to open the ice and read what the navy had paid to forget. Burning it wasn't mercy for the dead. The dead didn't need mercy. They needed witnesses.

But witnesses to what, exactly? To names. To the fact that they had lived and been abandoned and frozen on a mountainside while their leader walked down and reported weather delays.

The ledger held the evidence. The map held the plea. And the list of names —

Corvane had the ledger. He'd rolled away with it clutched to his chest. Which meant the choice in front of her wasn't actually carry-or-burn the whole archive. The choice was smaller and sharper than that: the map itself, and whatever page of testimony she could take from the depot before the storm closed the door forever.

She stood up. The wind tried to fold her back down and she let it push her two steps and then planted her boots and leaned into it, the way she leaned into current at depth, patient, immovable, a body that had learned to argue with pressure since before she could legally drink.

"Mara. Tam. Depot. Two minutes. Grab the letters — the personal ones, the ones with addresses. Leave the ledgers."

Mara stared at her. "You're burning the rest?"

"I'm deciding what walks down this mountain and what stays." She met Mara's eyes. "You said the truth is what we know. Fine. Then we walk down knowing it, and we say it out loud, every headland, every port, until it stops being a secret. Paper can be confiscated. Paper can be filed. Voices can't."

Something moved through Mara's face — grief and relief and love, all of it arriving at once, badly wrapped. She kissed Ansel, quick and hard, tasting of ice and iron, and then they moved.

* * *

The depot again was like entering a throat of blue glass. Their headlamps carved cones through the ice. Ansel went straight to the crate she'd opened before — the letters, bundled in oilcloth, addressed in a dozen different hands to a dozen towns she'd never heard of. Last words home that never went home. She took the bundle. She took one more thing: a single loose page from the pay ledger, the muster roll, the names. Thirty-one names in a clerk's neat hand, and beside each one, in Hollis's later, shakier ink, small corrections. *Died 14th. Died 15th. Survived: 1.*

Survived: 1. Hollis had written himself onto the list last, smallest, like an apology.

She folded the page into her jacket, against her skin, where the map had lived for a hundred miles.

Then she came back out into the storm's edge, and Mara and Tam stood back from the flat stone, and the map lay there weighted by its four rocks, and the wind kept tearing at its corners like it wanted it.

Ansel struck the match.

It caught on the second try — her fingers were beyond feeling by then — and she held the flame to the bottom edge of the map and watched the brown ink curl and bloom orange. The route went first, the long line of their whole journey, the passes and rivers and the little X above the cloud line. Then the margins, the testimony, *rations short here*, *turned back without us*, the handwriting of a man who'd spent thirty years writing to no one. Fire ate it all with a sound like breathing.

She didn't feel lighter. She felt the exact weight of it, every year of it, passing through her hands and up into the white.

When it was ash she ground it into the stone with her boot, and the wind took even that, and then there was no map. There never had been a map. There was only a mountain that had swallowed an expedition and three people standing in its weather with a bundle of unsent letters and a single page of names.

"There," she said. Her voice came out wrecked. "Both promises, Hollis. Carried and burned. You absolute bastard. You knew. You knew I'd have to do both."

Tam made a sound that might have been a laugh or a sob. Mara put her arm through Ansel's arm and held on.

* * *

They found Corvane twenty minutes later, or he found them — it was impossible to say in a whiteout, he simply materialized out of the blowing white like a man stepping through a curtain, alone, unarmed, the ledger gone from his hands. His face was bare of everything. Not cold. Emptied. A man who had read his father's name in a column marked *abandoned* and come out the other side of the reading as someone new, and didn't yet know who.

"The sergeant?" Ansel said.

"Gone down the south side. I told him the mission was concluded." A pause. "He disagreed. Loudly. He'll report me. Good." He looked at the three of them, at the ash-stain on Ansel's gloves, and something almost like understanding crossed his emptied face. "You burned it."

"The map. The ledgers stay under the ice. The letters come with us." She pulled the muster page from her jacket and held it out to him, and watched him look at it and not take it. "Thirty-one names. Your father's is fourth from the top."

Corvane looked at the page for a long time. The storm screamed. When he finally spoke, his voice was quiet enough that she had to lean in to catch it.

"When we get down," he said, "there will be an inquiry. I'll be facing charges regardless — my sergeant will make sure of that. When they ask you what happened on this mountain, Ms. Vasquez, you can tell them anything you want. The truth. All of it. I won't contradict you." He exhaled, and it shook. "Just — the names. Read them somewhere. My father doesn't deserve to be on that list. But the other thirty do, and no one has said them out loud in thirty years."

"They will be," Ansel said. "Every headland between here and the north coast. I gave my word."

He nodded once, a soldier accepting orders, and turned into the white, and for a moment he was only a shape, and then not even that.

Mara's hand found hers in the dark. "Which way down?"

Ansel turned her back to the wind and felt for it — the compass sense divers have, the inner needle — and it steadied, pointing down and east, toward the coast, toward the monsoon, toward the boat.

"That way," she said. "All the way down. We've got a lot of names to read."
