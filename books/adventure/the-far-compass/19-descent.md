## 19. Descent

The snow began to fail before the sun did.

Mara noticed it first as sound — a soft, granular hiss along the ridge, like the mountain exhaling. Then the drifts started slumping where they'd stood hard for weeks, cornices sighing off the col in slow gray curtains, and the whole white world turned the color of wet paper. Thaw. Three days after the storm broke, the pass was already dying, and everything the mountain had sealed shut was opening at once.

They came down through it in single file, and the order of the line told the whole story of the last three days.

Tam walked point.

Nobody had discussed it. On the first morning out of the col, Ansel had simply checked the rope, handed Tam the lead, and said, *You've got the sharpest eyes for blue ice. Call it.* And Tam — who two weeks ago had been a runaway mechanic stealing diesel in a port town — had taken the front without a word and hadn't given it back since. He moved well, Mara thought. Careful in the way of someone who'd learned that carelessness was a luxury. He probed every snowbridge twice, flagged the crevasses with wands cut from their spare tent poles, and never once looked back to see if anyone was following, which was either confidence or a boy refusing to let them see his face.

Behind him came Corvane.

That was the part Mara still couldn't make sit flat in her mind. The lieutenant of navy intelligence — the man who'd boarded their boat with polite menace, who'd posted their descriptions in every port from here to the coast, who'd chased them up a mountain with a sergeant and a rifle squad — walked in the middle of their line with his weapon slung and his collar open and his father's letter in a waterproof case against his chest, and he said almost nothing. He moved when Tam moved. He stopped when Tam stopped. Three days ago he'd been the storm chasing them; now he was just another body in the rope team, and the strangeness of it hadn't worn off. It had only deepened.

Mara took the rear, because someone had to, and because the rear was where she could watch all of them at once — Ansel's broad back and the stiff set of her left shoulder, Tam's quick careful feet, Corvane's gray silhouette moving like a man carrying something heavier than a letter.

She'd spent six years reconstructing the lost expedition. Six years of archive dust and dead reckoning, of plotting a dead surveyor's course across maps that had never been printed. She'd thought she understood what happened on this mountain. Then she'd stood in the ice-sealed depot and watched Corvane read his father's name in a pay ledger — read the entry where Captain Edmond Corvane had drawn rations for eleven men on a day when only seven were alive to eat — and she'd learned that understanding and knowing were different countries, and she'd been living in the wrong one.

* * *

They camped that second night in the lee of a moraine, and the thaw turned the camp noisy. Meltwater ran everywhere, ticking off the stones, gurgling under the snow, the whole glacier muttering in its sleep. Mara got the stove going while Tam rigged the shelter, and Ansel sat apart, working her shoulder in slow circles, her face turned away.

Corvane sat on a rock at the edge of the firelight, not quite inside it, not quite out. He'd been doing that for three days — holding himself at the perimeter like a man waiting to be told whether he was a prisoner or a guest. Nobody had told him. Nobody had decided.

Mara poured two cups of tea and carried one to him.

He took it without looking up. "Thank you."

"You've said maybe forty words in three days."

"I'm counting them." He turned the cup in his hands. His knuckles were raw from the storm, split along two fingers where he'd held the ledger line against the sergeant. "In my line of work, words are ammunition. I've been trying to figure out which kind these are."

"And?"

He was quiet long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer. Down-slope, the glacier groaned — a long settling sound, ice shifting in the heat, the mountain rearranging its dead.

"When I was nine," he said finally, "my father came home for six weeks. The only time. He taught me knots. He'd sit across the table and tie them blindfolded, and I'd have to untie them and tie them back, and he'd say — " Corvane's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "He'd say, *A line is only as true as what it's holding.* I thought he meant rope."

"You didn't."

"I found out what he was holding the day I read that ledger." He looked into the fire. "Eleven rations. Seven men. He knew they were going to die up here and he drew their pay anyway, and the navy buried the file because a captain's pension was cheaper than a scandal. And I spent my whole career — " He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I spent my whole career becoming the kind of officer who buries files."

"You didn't know."

"That's not the comfort you think it is." He drank his tea. "The sergeant was right, you know. Back there. The clean solution was to burn the depot and report the expedition lost to weather. That's what I was sent to do. That's what I would have done, three months ago, without losing a minute of sleep." He turned the cup again. "Then I read my father's handwriting, and it turns out a man can lose sleep over paper."

Mara sat down on the rock beside him, close enough for the fire's warmth, far enough for the truth of it. "What are you going to do?"

"Report it. All of it. Myself included." He said it flat, like reading coordinates. "The names go to the families. The file goes to the admiralty. And I go in front of a board and tell them I found the cache, lost the sergeant in a whiteout scuffle, and let civilians walk out with the evidence because I judged it belonged to them." A pause. "Which is even true."

"Corvane." She kept her voice level. "The sergeant — you said *lost*. What happened to him?"

"He's alive. Broken collarbone, furious, and two days behind us with the rest of the team. I left him a written order to hold at the col until relieved." Something dry moved through his voice. "He'll hold. He hates me now, but he's regular navy. He'll hold."

"And the board. They'll believe you?"

"No." He said it simply. "Probably not. A Corvane testifying against a Corvane? They'll want a second explanation, a cleaner one. They'll want the file rewritten." He looked at her then, and for the first time in three days his eyes were steady. "Which is why the ledger matters more than my career. If it's destroyed, my father dies a hero and eleven families keep a beautiful lie. If it survives — if it gets read, aloud, in front of people — then at least the lie dies with witnesses."

Mara thought of Hollis then. Old Hollis in his bunk on Ansel's boat, trading a map for a promise with his last good breath — *drawn so someone would finally read it aloud.* The radio operator who'd walked down off this mountain alone and spent forty years carrying testimony nobody would take.

"You sound like him," she said.

"Who?"

"Hollis." She watched his face. "He was the expedition's radio operator. Your father's radio operator. He heard the last transmissions. He spent his whole life trying to get someone to listen." She let that land. "He gave us the map because he was dying and the mountain was closing, and he needed someone to carry the truth over the range one more time. You've been chasing his ghost for three weeks, Lieutenant. I think maybe you've been carrying it longer."

Corvane didn't answer. But he turned the cup in his hands one more time, and set it down, and for the first time since the col he looked — not at peace, nothing like peace — but *aimed*. Like a man who'd found his bearing.

Across the fire, Ansel was watching them. She'd been watching the whole time, Mara realized. She caught Mara's eye and held it for a moment, and something passed between them that needed no words — the look of two people who'd made a hard choice in a whiteout and were still, daily, choosing it.

* * *

On the fourth day the snow gave out entirely, and the mountain turned to water.

They descended into a country that was coming apart at the seams. Streams that had been solid crossings a week ago now ran brown and violent, tearing at their banks. The trail down the valley floor had become a chain of braided channels, and twice they had to climb the flank of the valley itself to get around water that hadn't been there when they came up.

Tam led them through all of it.

He'd stopped being the quiet kid somewhere on the descent — or maybe he'd stopped being quiet and Mara had only just started listening. He talked now, in a low running commentary, calling the route: *Rock bridge, good on the left. Water's rising — we cross now or not at all. Ice rotten, stay off the shade side.* He'd learned the mountain's grammar in two weeks flat, and he spoke it without bravado, the way Ansel spoke to the sea.

It was on the fifth day, at a crossing none of them liked, that he earned the rope.

The stream came down from a hanging valley in three channels split by gravel bars, running thigh-deep and fast enough to sing. Ansel tested the first channel with a pole and shook her head — too deep, too fast, her shoulder still a wreck from holding the line against the sergeant. Corvane went in to his waist and came back white-faced, having lost his footing twice in four steps.

"We go upstream," Ansel said. "Find the braid point."

"Or we cross here," Tam said.

They all looked at him. He was crouched at the edge, reading the water the way he read engines — not the surface but the structure underneath.

"See the bars? The channels braid back together thirty meters down, but here — " he pointed — "here they're shallow. Fast, but shallow. The deep channel's the middle one, and it's only two meters wide. We cross the edges easy and jump or bridge the middle. If we go upstream we lose the afternoon and camp on that scree slope in the rain." He glanced up, and there was something new in his face — not asking permission, exactly. Asking to be trusted. "I've crossed worse. In the harbor. On the hulls."

Ansel looked at the water for a long moment. Then at Tam. Mara watched her weigh it — the old instinct to carry everything herself against the newer, harder discipline of letting someone else hold the line.

"Okay," Ansel said. "You're on the rope. Call the moves."

Tam rigged the crossing fast and clean — rope anchored to a boulder, waist-deep belay on the far side, everyone clipped in. He took the middle channel first, jumping the deep slot with the rope singing, and came up the far gravel bar soaked to the chest and grinning like he'd never once in his life been a runaway.

Then he called them across, one at a time, and each of them went — Ansel with her bad shoulder, Corvane with his letter, Mara last — and each of them crossed because a sixteen-year-old told them it was safe, and each of them believed him.

On the far side, wringing out her sleeves, Mara caught Ansel watching Tam with an expression she'd never seen on her face before. Not pride exactly. Something older and more startled than pride — the look of a woman discovering she had a family the way you discover weather, already all around you, arrived while you were looking elsewhere.

"He's good," Ansel said quietly.

"He's us," Mara said. "Three weeks ago none of us could steer."

* * *

They came down out of the high country on the seventh day, into green.

It happened fast, the way spring does at altitude — one switchback the world was gray rock and dirty snow, the next it was grass and stunted pine and the smell of thawing earth, thick and biological and almost obscene after three weeks of white. The melt ran everywhere, feeding streams that fed rivers that fed, somewhere far below, the harbor where the monsoon was already stacking up over the sea.

They made camp that night under the first real trees any of them had seen since the foothills, and it was Corvane who broke the silence at supper.

"The coast," he said. "You'll make it before landfall?"

"Two days," Ansel said. "Maybe three, if the rivers argue."

"The monsoon makes landfall in two." He said it without drama, just the flat delivery of a man who'd spent a career reading weather reports. "When it hits, every harbor road on that coast goes under. And when it hits, I'll be reporting in — which means I can't be seen with you, and you can't be seen with me. After the coast, we're strangers."

"Wasn't planning to write," Ansel said.

But Mara was watching him, and she saw what he wasn't saying — that *strangers* was the price of what he was about to do, and he was paying it in advance, and he'd already decided it was fair. He'd walk into the admiralty alone, carrying a letter and a ledger's worth of truth, and the people he'd spent his life serving would decide whether he was a whistleblower or a traitor, and none of them would ever know there'd been a rope team once, a boy on point and a diver with a bad shoulder and a cartographer who'd loved a dead surveyor, all of them pulling the same line down off a mountain.

"The families," Mara said. "When the names are read. Will they publish them?"

"Full inquiry. Public, if I have anything left to bargain with." He looked into the fire. "Hollis will be on the list. Radio operator, first-class. He'll finally be read aloud." A pause. "I'd have liked to thank him."

"He knew," Mara said. "That's the thing about Hollis. He set the whole thing in motion forty years ago and he knew it would outlive him. He just needed to believe someone would carry it." She looked around the fire — at Ansel, at Tam, at the enemy who wasn't one anymore. "He bet on strangers. And we came down."

Corvane was quiet for a while. Then he took the waterproof case from inside his jacket, opened it, and — for the first time — read a passage of his father's letter aloud. Just a few lines. Something about knots, and a line being only as true as what it holds, and a boy across a table. His voice didn't break. But it went somewhere low and careful, like a man walking a snowbridge, and when he finished he closed the case and put it away and said, "Thank you," though Mara wasn't sure whether he meant her or the fire or the dead.

Tam, sprawled against his pack, was already half asleep. Ansel banked the stove. The trees around them dripped with melt, and far below, invisible, the sea waited with its weather.

Mara sat up through first watch and wrote in her notebook by headlamp — the route, the camps, the crossings, the whole descent in clean lines for the record she'd one day file. At the bottom of the page, after the coordinates, she wrote the crew list. Not the expedition's. Theirs.

*Vasquez, A. Okonkwo, M. Tam. Corvane, Lt. (provisional).*

She looked at the last entry for a long time. Then she left it in.

* * *

The last morning, the river valley opened onto the coastal plain, and they saw the sea.

It was wrong. That was Mara's first thought — the horizon had gone the color of bruised copper, and a long dark line lay along it like a seam, and the air had the heavy, charged stillness of a room before a door slams. The monsoon, stacking itself offshore, twenty-four hours out, maybe less.

They stood on the last ridge in the wind coming off the water, four of them, rope still coiled on Ansel's pack though there was nothing left to rope.

"Harbor road's under by tomorrow," Ansel said. "We take the boat out tonight or not at all."

"You'll make it?" Corvane asked.

"We'd better."

He nodded. Then he did something none of them expected — he unslung his pack, took out the ledger's surviving duplicate pages, the ones he'd copied by hand at the cold camp in case the originals were lost, and held them out to Mara.

"Insurance," he said. "If I don't come out of the inquiry clean, these need to exist somewhere the admiralty can't reach. A cartographer archives things, don't they?"

Mara took them. Her hands were steady. "You're trusting us."

"I'm trusting the rope team." He almost smiled. "It's the only institution I still believe in."

He shook Ansel's hand — she gripped with her good arm and didn't let go as fast as she meant to — and he put his hand on Tam's shoulder, and the boy stood straight under it like a mast stepping into its boat. Then he turned to Mara, and for a moment the lieutenant and the cartographer just looked at each other, two keepers of the same dead men, and he said, "Read them aloud. All of them. Every headland if you have to."

"Every one," she said.

And then he was walking down toward the coast road alone, gray figure against the copper sea, a man carrying a letter into a storm of his own — and Mara stood on the ridge watching him go until Ansel's hand found hers.

"Come on," Ansel said. "We've got a boat to outrun."

Below them the harbor road gleamed wet in the first fat drops of rain, and the sea was already standing up in long gray lines, and Tam was laughing, actually laughing, running ahead down the trail toward the water — and Mara, holding the duplicate pages against her chest where a letter had ridden against a lieutenant's, followed her family down toward the monsoon.
