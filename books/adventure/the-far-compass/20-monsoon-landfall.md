## 20. Monsoon Landfall

The sea told us before the sky did.

We came down out of the last green fold of the coast range in the grey half-light of morning, and I heard it — a long, low roar like a furnace door left open, rolling up the valley. Tam stopped on the trail ahead of me, one hand braced against a wet trunk, and turned his head the way a deer does when it scents something wrong.

"That's not wind," he said.

"No," Mara said behind me. "That's the ocean arguing with itself."

By noon we could see it. The monsoon had made landfall in the night, and it hadn't crept ashore the way the fishermen in Last Port had described, all gradual and gray. It had arrived like an army. The horizon was a bruised wall of cloud stacked to the top of the sky, and beneath it the sea was running white all the way out past the harbor mouth, waves standing up in ranks and throwing themselves at the coast like they were being paid to.

The rain hit us on the last mile of the harbor road. Not rain as I knew it — this was horizontal, warm, relentless, a solid thing you walked through rather than under. Within a hundred meters I was soaked to the skin, my boots full, my jacket a dead weight on shoulders that had been carrying too much for weeks. My right shoulder had never really forgiven the river. It sat in its socket now like something borrowed, aching with every jolt of the broken road, and I'd stopped complaining about it somewhere above the snowline because there was no one to complain to who could fix it.

Tam walked point. He'd been walking point since the descent began, since the morning he took the lead rope without asking and simply went, finding the trail through the melt-swollen streams and the slides with a certainty none of us questioned. Somewhere between the col and the coast he'd stopped being a runaway we'd collected and started being crew. I watched him now, sixteen years old and squinting into the deluge, and felt that unfamiliar ache again — the one that wasn't debt and wasn't grief and didn't have a name yet.

Corvane walked last.

He'd been walking with us for four days. Not under guard — we had nothing to guard him with, and anyway he was armed and we weren't — but with us, silent, carrying his father's letter inside his coat where the rain couldn't reach it. The sergeant was gone; after the standoff at the cache, Corvane had sent him down the north side with the rest of the patrol team and a set of orders I hadn't heard but could guess at. What remained was just a man in a navy greatcoat, soaked through like the rest of us, carrying a piece of paper that had undone him more thoroughly than any weapon could have.

He hadn't spoken much. When he did, it was directions, or warnings about the trail, or once — only once — a single sentence spoken to no one: "He signed the requisition for the extra rations two days before they turned back." As if saying it aloud made it weigh less. It didn't. I'd watched it not make it weigh less.

Now the harbor road ran out ahead of us along a low black spine of rock, and beyond it the town — Last Port, or what the storm had left of it. The road itself was already half gone. Waves were coming clean over the seawall, sweeping across the asphalt in brown sheets, dragging driftwood and fish crates and one entire rowboat with them, then receding grudgingly, sucking at the stones. Where the road dipped toward the harbor mouth, the water stood waist-deep and moving.

"We can't go around," Mara shouted over the roar. She'd come up beside me, hair plastered flat, eyes bright with something that wasn't fear exactly. Mara didn't do fear the way other people did. She did intensity. "The tide's coming in on top of the surge. In an hour that whole stretch is ten feet under."

"So we go through," I said.

She looked at me. Rain streamed off her chin. "Through."

"I've crossed worse water moving faster."

"You dove into a glacial river for a teenager."

"And I'd do it again," I said. "So let's keep the teenagers out of rivers."

Tam, twenty feet ahead, raised a fist. We stopped. Below the road, where the harbor opened into the town's one long street, boats were hauling at their moorings like animals scenting fire. A crowd was working along the waterfront in yellow oilskins, passing sandbags hand over hand, and even from here I could hear the bell of the church ringing — not the hour, just ringing, steady and mad, the way bells ring when someone has decided that sound is the last thing holding the town together.

And tied up at the quay, riding out the surge on lines doubled and tripled, her mast bare, her hull scarred and salt-stained and more beautiful than anything I had ever stolen —

My boat.

* * *

They'd brought her around while we were in the mountains. I don't know how — some arrangement Hollis must have made before he died, or maybe the harbormaster's sentiment, or maybe she simply refused to be anywhere else. But there she was, and seeing her hit me harder than the cold camp, harder than the rockfall, harder than Corvane's gun at the cache door. Because the boat meant the ledger was real. The boat meant the mountain had happened. The boat meant I owed thirty days to a creditor who thought I was chasing salvage, and instead I was coming back with a page of names and nothing else.

We went down to the quay in a chain, Corvane leading now because he knew the harbor and the harbor didn't know him, and the water came at us in pulses — surge, retreat, surge — and we moved on the retreats, three civilians and an officer of the navy that had sunk an expedition and buried its name, wading a drowned street together like the world's least likely boarding party.

A man in oilskins grabbed my arm at the foot of the quay. "You can't tie up here! Harbor's closing! Coast guard's pulling everyone behind the mole!"

"I'm not tying up," I said. "I'm taking her out."

"Out?" He stared at me like I'd suggested sailing to the moon. "In that?"

"In that," I said, and kept walking.

Because here was the arithmetic I'd been doing since the pass, quietly, the way I did all my worst arithmetic: the creditor wanted the boat in thirty days. Thirty days minus the mountains left me — what? Nine? Ten? And a boat sitting in a harbor that was about to be sealed behind a storm barrier for the duration of the monsoon, which could be weeks. If she stayed here, she was gone. If she went out, she might be gone too, but she'd be gone free.

Or — and this was the thought underneath the thought, the one I hadn't said aloud even to myself until I saw her riding at the quay — if she went out, and survived, and I sold the salvage rights to the wreck like I'd been turning over in my head since the ice depot, I could clear the debt and still have a boat. Or clear the debt and not have a boat. Either way, alive. Either way, owing nothing.

I'd spent six weeks learning what things cost. This was the final exchange rate.

Mara caught up to me at the gangway. "Tell me you're not thinking what you're thinking."

"I'm thinking I need to get to the wreck's registry office before the storm closes the port authority."

"Ansel."

"Ten minutes," I said. "Then we go."

"It's a force-nine surf out there."

"I've read worse water."

"You haven't *sailed* worse water."

I looked at her. Rain between us, roaring everywhere, the whole world reduced to noise and urgency, and I found that what I wanted to say was not about the boat at all. "Come with me," I said. "Both of you. After. Whatever happens with the debt, whatever happens next — come north with me."

Something moved across her face, quick as a fish under ice. "Ask me when we're not drowning," she said. "But yes. Obviously yes. Now go sign your papers before the sea eats the registry office."

* * *

The port authority building was a stone box on high ground, and getting to it meant crossing the town street, which was now a river with opinions. We went in a line, roped together with the supply rope Tam had nearly lost on the Long Green Stair a lifetime ago, and the water took us to mid-thigh and shoved, and somewhere in the middle of it Tam went down and I hauled him up by the collar without thinking, without deciding, the way I'd saved him on the rope, and understood distantly that this was what I was now: a person who reached.

The clerk in the registry office was a small woman with wet sleeves and the unimpressed expression of someone who had processed emergencies for forty years. She stamped my transfer of salvage rights while the windows flexed in their frames.

"The wreck you're claiming," she said. "The navy-sealed one."

"That's the one."

"You know the navy holds a lien on that site."

"I know," I said, "that the navy is going to have considerably larger problems than my paperwork by tomorrow." I glanced at Corvane, dripping in the corner, and he almost smiled. Almost. The first movement of his face in days that wasn't grief.

She stamped it. "Congratulations. You own a hole in the seafloor nobody wants."

"It's worth more than that," I said, and for the first time in months, it was true enough.

* * *

We got her out on the afternoon tide, which was less a tide than a suggestion the sea briefly entertained.

I won't pretend it was seamanship. It was stubbornness and luck and a hull that loved me back. We motored out through the harbor mouth with the swell lifting us and dropping us like a hand playing with a toy, Tam on the bow line, Mara at the chart table calling depths she was reading by feel and memory, Corvane in the cockpit with his hands folded, useless and silent, letting himself be cargo for once in his life. Behind us Last Port shrank into the rain, its bell still ringing, its seawall vanishing wave by wave, and ahead of us the open water stood up in grey hills and marched at us without end.

At some point in the third hour, with the boat groaning and the spray coming over in sheets, Corvane spoke.

"The names page," he said.

I had it in the chart drawer, wrapped in oilcloth. One page. The list of names from the ledger — every man the expedition lost, every man his father's orders had abandoned, copied out in my own hand because the original stayed in the ice with the rest of the evidence, sleeping, testifying, waiting for a court or a conscience to come dig it out someday. I'd kept the copy because burning the map had felt like mercy, but burning the names had felt like murder.

I looked at him across the pitching cockpit. Water ran off his cap brim. He looked older than he had on the patrol launch, back when he'd been polite menace in navy blue. He looked like a man who had carried something up a mountain and found it heavier on the way down.

"You could have taken it at the col," I said. "You could have taken it any time in the last four days."

"I know."

"So why ask now?"

He was quiet for a long time. The boat climbed a swell, hung, came down hard enough to knock us both against the rail.

"Because taking it would have been an order," he said finally. "This is a request. There's a difference. My father taught me that difference, ironically, by failing to understand it." He held out his hand. "I'm going to take it to the admiralty board. Not my chain of command — above them. I don't know if it will go anywhere. I don't know if they'll bury it deeper than the expedition was buried. I know that if I keep it, I become the second Vasquez—" 

"Vasquez?" Tam said from the bow.

"Corvane," he corrected, tiredly. "The second Corvane to choose the institution over the men. And I'd rather drown first." Another swell. He didn't brace. "Give me the page, Ansel. Please."

It was the *please* that did it. Not the rank, not the threat, not the logic. Six weeks ago this man had boarded my boat with polite menace and made my debt look like a rounding error, and now he stood in his soaked greatcoat asking please, and there was no absolution in it — not for him, not for the institution, not for the father whose letter he carried against his chest like a splint. Some things don't absolve. They just change shape.

I got the oilcloth packet from the drawer and put it in his hand.

He didn't open it. He held it a moment, weighing it, then buttoned it inside his coat next to the letter, where the rain couldn't reach it.

"There's a naval station at Northreach," he said. "Two days north of here. I'll request a transfer ashore when we make port." He looked at the wall of grey water ahead of us, and then, unexpectedly, at each of us in turn — Tam, Mara, me. "Whatever you three are, you did what an entire institution couldn't. You carried the truth over the range."

"We burned the map," I said.

"You kept the names."

The boat rose. Fell. Somewhere behind us the monsoon ate the coastline, and somewhere ahead of us lay Northreach, and the admiralty, and whatever justice or oblivion waited there — none of it ours anymore. Our part was done. The page was his burden now, and he took it the way he'd taken everything: alone, upright, into the weather.

When we sighted the Northreach light that night, standing tall and steady through the rain, Corvane asked for the tender.

"I'll row in," he said. "Alone. Less paperwork."

Nobody argued. At the ladder he paused, one hand on the rail, and for a moment he looked like he was going to say something else — something about his father, or about Hollis, or about the difference between orders and requests. Instead he just nodded, once, a salute too small for regulation and too honest for ceremony.

Then he was down the ladder, into the tender, into the rain, rowing toward the light with a page of names buttoned over his heart, promising nothing — and meaning, I think, everything he was capable of meaning.

We watched until the dark took him.

* * *

The three of us stood in the cockpit afterward, wet and empty-handed and strangely light, the way you feel after setting down something you've carried so long your spine has grown around it.

"He'll bury it," Tam said. "Or they'll bury him."

"Maybe," Mara said. "Maybe both. Maybe neither." She leaned against me, shoulder to shoulder, and the contact went through me like warmth through cold metal. "Doesn't matter tonight."

"What matters tonight?" Tam asked.

I looked at my boat — scarred, salt-bleached, riding the swells like she'd never doubted — and at the two people on her deck who had walked into a mountain as strangers and walked out as something I didn't have a word for yet. Somewhere south, a creditor was waiting for a payment I could now make. Somewhere north, the sea opened onto everything I'd never planned.

"Tomorrow," I said, "we figure out what we're keeping."

Mara's hand found mine in the dark. "And the day after?"

I watched the light of Northreach turn slow and white through the rain, and felt the compass in my chest swing, steadying, toward a heading I hadn't known I was reading for.

"The day after," I said, "we sail north."
