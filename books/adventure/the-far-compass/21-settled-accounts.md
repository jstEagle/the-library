## 21. Settled Accounts

The harbor office smelled of wet paper and old coffee, the way every harbor office in the world smells, and the clerk behind the desk had the face of a man who had been waiting all morning for something to go wrong.

"Salvage rights," he said, not looking up. "On which vessel?"

"The *Meridian Star*."

That got his eyes up. He was younger than his voice — mid-thirties, a jaw that hadn't decided yet whether to grow a beard or give up on it entirely. "That's a sealed site."

"It's a sealed site with a hole in its hull big enough to drive a launch through," I said. "I know. I drove one through it. Metaphorically."

He turned a page on his clipboard as if the page might contain an escape route. "Navy writ. Restricted salvage. I can't process—"

"You can process it." I set the envelope on the counter between us and left my hand on it. "Because the navy released it. Yesterday. In writing." Corvane's letter was inside, along with everything else: the survey exemption, the hazard abatement order, the whole paper architecture of a man unburying a thing he'd spent years helping to bury. It was signed twice — once by him, once by a commodore whose name meant nothing to me and clearly a great deal to the clerk, because when he read it his shoulders came down half an inch and his face went from *problem* to *paperwork*, which is the happiest transformation a bureaucrat ever undergoes.

"Well," he said. "Well. That's unusual."

"That's the word for it."

He processed it. There was a form for the rights themselves, a form for the assessment of remaining cargo value, a form declaring that no ordnance, classified materials, or human remains were known to be aboard — and I signed that last one and thought about Hollis, about the depot above the cloud line, about the list of names folded flat against my sternum where it had lived for three weeks now, and I signed it anyway, because none of that was aboard the *Meridian Star*, and because the truth has layers, and some of them are nobody's business but the dead's.

The number came out at noon. The clerk read it aloud like a man reading someone else's lottery ticket, apologetic and faintly envious. Enough to clear Vasko's debt with the interest and the late fees and the threat-fees the lender had invented along the way. Enough to buy the boat free and clear from under the lien. Not enough for new gear. Not enough for a season's fuel and food and insurance and the other hundred small hemorrhages that keep a working dive boat alive. Not enough to dive again, really. Enough to stop drowning, and nothing more.

I stood on the office steps with the receipt in my hand and watched the rain come off the harbor in gray sheets, and I felt exactly nothing, which after the mountain felt like a kind of luxury.

* * *

Vasko came to the boat himself, which surprised me. I'd expected a runner, or worse, a lawyer. Instead there he was at the end of the pier in a coat too good for the weather, holding an umbrella like a man who'd been told umbrellas were a sign of weakness and had compromised.

"You're paying," he said.

"I'm paying."

"All of it."

"All of it. Interest, fees, the fee you charged me for threatening to charge me fees. All of it. Count it if you want. I would, in your position."

He didn't count it. He stood at the rail of the *Cormorant* — my boat, only mine now, the lien stamped RELEASED in ink still tacky enough to smudge — and looked at her the way a man looks at a horse he'd bet against. "You went into the mountains," he said.

"I went into the mountains."

"There are stories. Port talk. A navy officer walking out of the high country with salvagers, a kid leading the descent through the melt." He shook his head slowly. "In thirty years of lending to people who work the water, I have never once heard a story like that come back true."

"Maybe it didn't," I said. "Maybe we all got frostbite and dreamed it."

He almost smiled. Then he took the money, counted it after all — of course he did; men like Vasko don't get old by trusting — and handed me back the mortgage papers, and for a moment we just stood there in the rain with the transaction finished and neither of us knowing what you're supposed to say next. He solved it by leaving, which is what he was best at. At the head of the pier he stopped, without turning around.

"The wreck," he said. "The sealed one. You sold the rights."

"This morning."

"To whom?"

"A buyer through an agent. Cash, no name." Which was true, and also a lie, depending on how you held it up to the light, because the agent had worn a navy overcoat and paid full asking price without blinking, and we both knew whose money that was and what it bought: silence, officially purchased, the last door closed. Corvane had offered it two days ago on the dock at Sorrow Bay, standing in the drizzle with his father's letter in an oilskin pouch against his chest, and he'd said, "It's the only clean thing I can do. The navy can't return what it took. But it can stop profiting from the hole." And I'd said yes, because the dead didn't need the hull anymore, they had the names, and because a debt is a debt even when it's paid in other currencies first.

Vasko nodded once, satisfied or pretending to be, and walked away into the rain, and I went below and put the kettle on and listened to the boat creak on her lines, free and clear, mine alone, worth less than she'd been worth in my head for years and worth more than anything else I owned.

* * *

Mara found me at dusk. She didn't knock — she never knocked, she'd learned the hatch code in the first week and treated that knowledge as a permanent easement — but she did stamp the rain off her boots on the top step, which from Mara was practically a courtesy.

"You look like a woman who's been staring at a wall," she said.

"I've been staring at a chart."

"Worse." She dropped onto the bench across from me, dripping, and helped herself to the tea I hadn't offered. Her hair was shorter than it had been on the mountain — Tam had taken the scissors to it in Kettle Pass with more enthusiasm than skill, and she'd kept the result out of what I suspected was politeness to Tam's feelings rather than any attachment to the style. "The portmaster says you sold the salvage rights."

"The portmaster says too much."

"He says a naval agent bought them at full price sight unseen." She blew on the tea. "Ansel. That's Corvane's money sealing Corvane's grave. You understand that's what happened."

"I understand that's what happened."

"And you're fine with it."

I turned the chart around so she could see it. The coast, north to south, the whole run of it, with my pencil marks on it going back six years — dive sites, fuel stops, wrecks I'd worked, wrecks I'd dreamed of working. The *Meridian Star* sat on it with a circle around it, drawn the week I'd first heard about her, when she'd been the score that would fix everything, the one big lift that would pull me out of the water forever. Six years of circling that spot. Three weeks ago I'd have sold a kidney for those rights.

"I'm not fine with it," I said. "I'm done with it. Those are different things."

She waited. She was good at waiting. On the mountain I'd learned that about her — that underneath the maps and the precision and the way she talked like every sentence had been peer-reviewed, she had a patience in her like deep water. Things could sink a long way before they hit bottom.

"I used to think the sea was the escape," I said. "You know? Boat's the getaway vehicle. Whatever's chasing you — debt, the navy, your own stupid decisions — you point the bow at open water and it all falls behind. That was the whole religion. Keep moving, stay wet, don't own anything the tide can't take." I tapped the circled wreck. "And then I finally get close to the big score, the one that ends it all, and it turns out the score is a graveyard, and the treasure is a list of dead men's names, and the only way out is through a blizzard carrying somebody else's promise. And somewhere in there—" I stopped. Started again, quieter. "Somewhere in there I stopped diving to get away from things. I dove to get *back*. To Tam, in the river. To you, at the cache door. Every dive since the pass has been toward something instead of away from it, and I don't know how to do the other kind anymore."

Mara set down her tea. "So sell the boat."

"No." The answer came out fast, faster than I'd expected it, and it surprised both of us. "No. That's the thing. Everyone assumes it's either/or. The boat or the sea. Settle down or sail off. Sell the *Cormorant*, take the money, become a person with a roof." I put my hand flat on the deck overhead, on the wood that flexed and groaned with the swell like something breathing. "But this isn't a thing I own. This is the first thing that ever stayed. Everything else — jobs, ports, plans — they all ended or left or sank. She didn't. She floated through all of it. And I'm not selling the only witness I've got."

"So what are you saying?"

"I'm saying I choose both, and both means less. No more deep salvage. No more sealed wrecks, no more navy sites, no more betting the boat on one big lift. She becomes what she was built to be — a coastal boat. Charters, deliveries, running the lighters when the weather allows. Small work. Honest work. Work that comes home at night." I laughed, short and rough. "Do you know how humiliating that sounds? Ansel Vasquez, the woman who dove the *Meridian Star* on a dare and a prayer, talking about *lighter runs*."

"It doesn't sound humiliating," Mara said. "It sounds like a woman who learned what things cost."

"The true price of survival," I said. "That's what this is. Nobody tells you it's not blood. It's ambition. You pay in ambition. You come up from the last dive and you owe nothing, and you find out that owing nothing means wanting nothing bigger than this—" I gestured at the cabin, the lamp, the rain on the glass, her. "—and the strange part is it feels like winning."

She reached across the table and took my hand, the bad shoulder's hand, and turned it over and traced the scar the rope burn had left across the palm. "Tam asked me today if we're staying," she said. "Through the winter, I mean. He's already claimed the engine room. He's drawn up a maintenance schedule. It's color-coded."

"Color-coded."

"He's very serious, Ansel. There's a legend."

I looked at her — at this woman who had crossed a mountain range for a dead man's testimony and a lost fiancé's truth and had walked out the other side with neither, only a letter and a list and me — and I felt the compass needle in my chest swing and settle, the way it had on the last headland, steady, pointing at nothing on any chart.

"Tell him the engine room's his," I said. "Tell him we're staying."

* * *

Later, when Mara had gone ashore to her room at the Anchor Line and the harbor had gone quiet except for rain, I sat at the table with the chart again and did the arithmetic one more time, though it never changed. Debt cleared. Rights sold. Account balance: enough for fuel, food, and a winter's mooring fees, and not one coin past that. By every measure I'd sailed by for ten years, I was broke.

I unfolded the names page instead. Just to look at it. Thirty-one names in Hollis's careful radio-operator's hand, each one with a date beside it, the day they stopped answering. Tomorrow we'd sail north, and at each headland we'd read them aloud, and that was the whole estate, the entire fortune, the sum total of what the great expedition of the *Meridian Star* had left behind.

I smoothed the page flat on the table, and that was when I saw it — the thing I'd missed three times before, because grief makes you skim. Under the last name, under Hollis's own signature line, there was a thirty-second entry. Smaller writing. Newer ink. A name and a date that hadn't happened yet when the list was made.

A date next month. A name I knew.

The rain ticked against the glass, and the *Cormorant* breathed beneath me, and I read it again, and again, and the needle in my chest swung hard off true.
