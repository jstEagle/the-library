## 22. True North

The compass had been Tam's idea.

He'd found it in a chandler's bin in the last port south — brass, dented, its glass clouded like an eye going blind — and paid for it with money he refused to explain. He mounted it on the bulkhead beside the helm with four screws driven at angles that would have made any shipwright weep, and then he stood back and looked at it the way he looked at engines he'd just coaxed back to life: proprietary, suspicious, fond.

"It wobbles," Ansel said, tapping the housing.

"It settles," Tam said. "You got to let it settle."

Three weeks north of the coast, I was beginning to think the boy was a philosopher.

We were running the outside of the headlands where the land fell into the sea in long green shoulders and the water went from silt-brown to a blue so deep it looked load-bearing. The *Kestrel* — Ansel had finally renamed her, scrubbing off the bank's stencil in a fit of ceremony that involved turpentine, profanity, and me holding the ladder — rode light without the salvage gear. No compressor. no lift bags, no dive flag curled in its locker like a sleeping snake. She was just a boat again, which is to say she was a promise with a hull around it.

I stood at the rail with the oilskin packet under my arm and did what I had done at every headland since we cleared the river bar: I opened it, and I read a name aloud.

"Vasquez," I said. "No relation. Petty Officer Second Class, radio's mate. He was twenty-four."

The wind took it out over the water. Somewhere past the third wave, it stopped belonging to us.

Ansel didn't look up from the wheel. She never did anymore. But her hand left the spokes for a moment and found my shoulder — the good one, always the good one now, as if my body were a chart and she'd memorized which parts were safe to touch — and then it went back to steering, and the boat held her line, and that was the whole liturgy. Name, wind, hand, heading.

It wasn't much. It was everything Hollis had asked for and never said so.

* * *

The packet held forty-one names. We knew because Tam had counted them twice, once out loud and once silently, moving his lips like a boy doing sums in church, and both times he got forty-one, and after the second time he'd gone quiet in a way that had nothing to do with sulking and everything to do with a kid learning that numbers can be graves.

Forty-one names, and one page, and one map burned to ash in a stove at ten thousand feet while a storm tried to take the mountain apart around us.

People would ask, eventually. People already had. In every port since the descent there was someone who'd heard the shape of the story — a navy expedition, a sealed wreck, a cache above the clouds — and wanted the part they could use. Was there gold? Was there salvage? Was there anything left to claim? Ansel had developed a way of answering that consisted entirely of pouring another drink and changing the subject, and I had developed a way of answering that consisted of telling the truth in a tone so flat people assumed I was lying. Between us we kept the thing intact by keeping it worthless.

But the names we gave away freely. That was the point. That had always been the point, though it had taken me six years and a burned map to understand what I'd actually been reconstructing.

I'd spent those six years the way I spent everything: alone, precise, and certain that if I could just get the route right — every bearing, every camp, every correction in Hollis's cramped radio-operator's hand — the truth would assemble itself like a survey. Draw the line accurately enough and the territory would confess. It's what cartographers believe. It's what I believed when I came aboard Ansel's boat with my copies and my arguments and my claim, and it's what I stopped believing somewhere on the Long Green Stair, when she caught Tam's rope without thinking and I watched her face do something it clearly hadn't authorized.

The truth hadn't been a territory. It had been forty-one people who went up a mountain and were abandoned by the man who led them, and one man who came down carrying their voices because nobody else survived to carry anything. Hollis hadn't drawn a treasure chart. He'd drawn testimony — bearings and elevations arranged so that anyone who followed them would arrive, eventually, at the place where the reading had to happen out loud. Not filed. Not sealed. Read. A voice per name, over open air, the way he'd been trained to send them and never allowed to.

The navy had buried the operation. Corvane had nearly buried it deeper. And in the end the ledger went down the chain of command anyway — Corvane carried it himself, walking silent among us through the melting snow like a man escorting his father's coffin — but the page of names stayed with us. Corvane had taken it at the harbor road in the rain, and given it back two days later, folded, without a word, and I understood then that some men can only carry a thing far enough to prove they can put it down.

So we read them at headlands. One per landmark, north along the coast, because Hollis's marginalia had specified a heading and not a destination, and because I am who I am, I honored the specification.

"Mara." Ansel's voice over the engine noise, low. "Squall behind the point. Ten minutes, maybe less. Can the next one wait?"

I checked the packet. "Name thirty-one can wait. Thirty-one's patient. He waited sixty years."

"Then batten whatever needs battening."

Tam was already moving, because Tam was always already moving, small and quick in the rigging, tying off things that didn't need tying off because standing still made him look over his shoulder for the portmaster's list or the sergeant's rifle or whichever door he'd slipped out of last. He'd gotten taller. He'd gotten louder. He'd started calling Ansel "skipper" and me "sir," which I'd corrected exactly once before deciding that being sirred by a teenage runaway mechanic was a rank I could live with.

The squall hit sideways, as squalls do, and for eleven minutes the world was white noise and green water and Ansel's hands making small constant corrections, and I braced in the companionway and watched her work and felt the thing I'd been refusing to name since Snowline rise in my chest like water finding its level.

She'd told me once, in the cold camp with the storm screaming and Tam pretending to sleep three feet away, that she'd burn the map if it came to that. I'd known she meant it. What I hadn't known — what I learned later, from Tam, in a confession he made staring very hard at an engine he was not working on — was that saying it out loud had cost her something, because the map was the only currency she had, and she'd offered to spend it on the truth rather than the debt. She'd chosen the dead over her own rescue before anyone asked her to.

And then at the top, with the storm sealing the pass and the choice finally real, she'd burned the map and kept the names, and I had stood there watching paper curl in a wind that wanted to eat it, and thought: *there*. There is the person I crossed a mountain range for. Not the diver. Not the debtor. The woman who understands that some things you carry and some things you set free, and the whole art of a life is knowing which is which.

The squall passed. The sun came back the way it does at sea, all at once, like a decision.

"Name thirty-one," Ansel said.

I read it into the wind.

* * *

We made the northern anchorage at dusk, a hook of sheltered water behind a headland the charts called nothing at all. Ansel dropped anchor with the economy of someone who has done ten thousand things wrong and finally done this one right, and Tam cooked, because Tam had appointed himself cook by unilateral decree and his decree was backed by the fact that he was the only one of us who could make rice without turning it into ballast.

We ate in the cockpit under the first stars. The compass on the bulkhead glowed faintly in the lamplight, needle trembling, settling, trembling.

"I've been thinking about the wobble," Tam said.

"Don't," Ansel said.

"No, listen. It's not broken. It's just — it points at magnetic north, right? But true north's somewhere else. Always somewhere else. You got to know the difference or your whole course is wrong by degrees until suddenly you're miles off." He gestured with his spoon. "The wobble is it figuring out which one you're asking for."

I looked at Ansel. Ansel looked at me. Neither of us said *that's the whole book*, because neither of us needed to.

After dinner Tam turned in, folding himself into the forepeak with the boneless efficiency of the young, and the cockpit went quiet except for the halyard ticking against the mast and the water doing what water does against a hull at rest — that sound I have loved longer than I have loved any person, the sound of being held afloat.

Ansel sat across from me with her bad shoulder propped against the shroud, and for a while neither of us spoke, because we had gotten good at that, too. Six weeks ago we were two strangers negotiating over a dying man's map. Now silence between us was a room we both knew how to be in.

"The bank letter came," she said finally. She said it the way she reported weather: facts, bearing, no drama. "Final settlement. Wreck rights sold, liens discharged. I'm clear."

"You're clear," I repeated, because I wanted to hear it exist in the air.

"Owe nothing." She turned her cup in her hands. "First time since I was nineteen. You'd think it'd feel different."

"How does it feel?"

"Like the bottom of a dive." She looked up. "Like when you've been down so long that up stops meaning anything, and then you break the surface and the air's just — sitting there. Waiting to see what you'll do with it."

"And what will you do with it?"

"That depends." She set the cup down. "There's a heading I've been wanting to run. North and east, past where the charts go vague. Tam says the fishing's unrecorded. I say that's cartographer talk for *unexploited*, and I want to see your face when you find out I'm right."

"Ansel Vasquez proposing a voyage of discovery."

"Proposing," she said, and the word landed between us with a weight that had nothing to do with voyages, and she let it sit there, steady, the way she'd held the line at the col with her shoulder tearing, the way she did everything — all in, no flinch. "That a problem, Okonkwo?"

I reached across the cockpit and took her hand, the callused one, the one that had saved a rope and a boy and a promise and, though she'd deny it, me.

"It's a heading," I said. "Set it."

She leaned over and kissed me, and the compass on the bulkhead trembled and settled, trembled and settled, working out which north we meant.

* * *

We weighed anchor at dawn, because that's what the *Kestrel* did now; dawn was a departure, not a deadline. Tam took the bow and called the depth like a chantey, invented rhymes and all. Ansel had the wheel. I stood at the rail with the packet, lighter by one name than it had been yesterday, heavier by none.

At the headland where the coast bent away toward the vague charts, I read the last name on the page.

"Hollis," I said. "Radio operator. He brought them home."

The wind took it north. Ahead of us the water ran clean and deep and unrecorded, and the needle steadied, pointing not at where we'd been, not at where we owed, but at the difference between magnetic and true — and we sailed on it.
