## 1. The Mood of Sandbars

Every sandbar on the Vell has a mood, and I've learned them the way other people learn their neighbors' faces. The Graveling Bar sulks when the wind comes out of the northeast and lies flat and honest under a southern blow. The Twins—two humped shoals just below the ferry cut—are twins in name only; one is patient, the other spiteful, and no chart I've ever bought or stolen tells you which is which on a given night. The chart says six feet over the northern Twin. The chart has said six feet for forty years. The chart, as far as I can tell, was drawn by a man who never once got his boots wet.

I know because I've measured. Not with instruments—instruments are for people who trust machines more than water—but with my own two hands off the bow of the *Marrow*, feeling the current's shoulder against my palm the way you'd test a horse before mounting it. The river talks. It talks all night, every night, and most people have trained themselves not to hear it. I never had that luxury. When your whole livelihood depends on knowing where the water is lying to you, you learn to listen.

That night it was talking about rain.

* * *

The *Marrow* is a ketch the way an old dog is a pedigree—technically, and with some explaining. Forty-one feet on deck, oak on oak, patched in places with timber that came from three different wrecks and at least two different centuries. She leaks. I want to be clear about that up front, because everything else follows from it: she leaks from the garboard seam on the port side, she leaks a little from around the stuffing box, and in a hard beat to windward she leaks from somewhere aft that I've never been able to find, like a secret she keeps just to remind me who's in charge. Every morning I pump her out. Every morning she forgives me, or pretends to.

The Consortium holds her paper. That's the short version. The long version involves a bad winter, a salvage contract gone sideways, and a loan agreement written in language so fine it might as well have been written in spider silk—you don't see it until you're wrapped in it. Four hundred crowns outstanding, interest compounding like regret, and a clause that lets them call the loan if I miss two consecutive payments. I've missed one. I was not going to miss another, which is why I was running salvage upriver at eleven o'clock at night instead of sleeping like a person with nothing to hide.

The cargo was ordinary. A drowned barge off Harker's Reach, sunk three years back with a load of brass fittings still lashed below, and an owner who wanted it raised quiet, without harbor authority, without insurance men, without questions. People come to me when they want things done without questions. It's not a reputation I courted exactly, but I never ran from it either. Questions cost money. Silence pays better.

I came up the Vell with the tide under me and the running light doused, because the owner hadn't paid enough for witnesses. The night was the thick kind, low cloud pressing down until the river felt like a corridor rather than a landscape, and the only world was what the lamp at the bow showed me: black water, the pale smear of the banks, the occasional red eye of a channel marker winking like something deciding whether to bite. I steered by feel mostly. You can do that on the Vell if you've spent thirty years learning where she keeps her bones.

There's a spot below the old rope-walk where the current splits around a sunken piling nobody ever bothered to mark. The chart shows clean water there. The chart is wrong. I felt the split coming through the tiller a half-mile before I reached it—that faint stutter, like a word someone starts and abandons—and eased east without thinking about it, the way you scratch an itch. Behind me the wake folded closed over the place where I'd have been, if I were the sort of captain who trusts charts.

This is the part I'm good at. I want that on the record too, since I'm being honest tonight. Whatever else I am—a debtor, a smuggler-adjacent, a woman the harbor office has never once managed to catch doing anything worth fining—I am the best reader of water between Saltmouth and the gorge, and possibly beyond it, though past the gorge nobody runs boats to compare. My father taught me the beginnings of it before he taught me letters. He'd hold my hand flat over the side and say, *What does it say now?* And I'd say, *It says we're over gravel,* or *it says there's a hole under us deep enough to drown a church,* and he'd laugh and say I was making it up, and then he'd sound the depth and find I was right, and the laughing would stop being laughter and become something quieter and stranger. Pride, maybe. Or fear. With my father those two wore each other's clothes so often you couldn't always tell them apart.

He died in the gorge. But that's a story for a different hour of the night, and this hour belonged to brass fittings and a leaking hull and four hundred crowns.

* * *

I found the barge by its absence. That sounds like mysticism but it isn't—it's arithmetic. The current above Harker's Reach runs hard along the western bank, and where a thing sits on the bottom, the water above it wrinkles. Not visibly. Feelably. There's a texture to moving water passing over an obstruction, a caught note in the song, and once you've heard it you can't unhear it. I brought the *Marrow* up slow, let her drift across the spot twice, and dropped anchor in the eddy behind the wreck where the water rests like a tired animal.

Then I went down.

Diving at night is a discipline in humility. You carry a lamp that shows you six feet of murk and nothing beyond it, and the rest of the world is your hands and your memory of where you tied off. The barge sat in eighteen feet, listed hard to starboard, her deck furred with weed soft as moss. The brass was where the owner said it would be, lashed under a tarp that had rotted to lace. I worked by touch, cutting lashings, filling the lift bags, and the whole time the river pressed its cold thumbs against my ears and murmured whatever rivers murmur to people who intrude on them after dark. Something brushed my leg—a log, probably. Probably. I didn't stay to interview it.

By three in the morning the bags were rigged and the first load was breaking surface, dripping and green and worth, if the owner's numbers held, enough to cover the next payment with a margin left over. Enough margin, if I was careful, to start eating the principal. I allowed myself, floating there in the black water beside the dripping bags, exactly ninety seconds of imagining the loan papers going into the stove. Then I climbed aboard and got back to work, because hope is a leak too, and if you don't pump it regularly it sinks you faster than seawater.

* * *

The trouble started at the dock, which is where trouble usually waits, patient as a bill collector.

I put into Saltmouth at dawn with the brass stowed and the *Marrow* riding heavy and honest, and the owner's man was there to meet me, counting crates with the joyless efficiency of a man whose bonus depends on the count. Payment changed hands. I watched him walk away, and I stood at the rail doing the arithmetic again—the payment, the interest, the principal, the gap—and the gap was smaller than last month. Smaller. Not closed. I remember thinking, with the particular clarity that comes at the end of a night without sleep, that I could do this forever. Run one job ahead of the debt, forever, like a cart horse walking toward a carrot it will never taste. And I remember noticing, right after that thought, that the thought didn't frighten me. That frightened me.

That's when I saw him.

He was standing at the far end of the dock, near the fish sheds, in city clothes. That alone marked him—Saltmouth dresses in oilskin and rubber boots, and anyone wearing a coat that fit at the shoulders was either lost or hunting. He wasn't doing anything. That was the second mark. Men who are lost look at things: the boats, the gulls, the water. This man was looking at me. Had been looking at me, by the set of him, for some time. He stood with his weight even on both feet and his hands loose at his sides, and he watched me the way Quist's collectors watch a debtor's boat—not with curiosity but with inventory. Counting what I had. Estimating what I owed.

I went about my business. Water cans filled, engine checked, the brass residue scrubbed off the deck so the *Marrow* looked like any working ketch that had spent the night doing nothing in particular. He didn't approach. He just stayed, and the staying was the message. I know how long a man can stand in one place before it becomes strange. He blew through strange and kept going.

When I cast off, he spoke.

"Captain." His voice carried across the water flat and clear, a voice used to rooms where people listen. "I need passage upriver."

"Harbor master keeps a list of boats for hire," I said, not stopping.

"He does. Your boat isn't on it."

"Then I'm not hiring out."

"I'll pay well."

Everyone says they'll pay well. The words mean nothing; they're the sound money makes while it's still hypothetical. I kept coiling the line, keeping my eyes on my hands, because a man who watches your face is reading it, and I didn't intend to be read.

"I don't take passengers," I said.

"No," he agreed, and something in his tone made me look up despite myself. "You don't. That's rather the point of you, isn't it?"

He was younger than I'd expected. Thirty, maybe a little more, with the kind of face that's handsome the way a knife handle is handsome—functional, and meant to be gripped. City-pale. Eyes that had already finished assessing me and were now simply waiting, the way you wait for a lock to turn.

"You've got me confused with someone interesting," I said.

"I don't think I do." He crouched, unhurried, and set something on the dock edge—a coin, weighted, deliberate. "For when you change your mind."

"I won't."

"You will. Or someone less polite will come asking, and you'll wish it had been me." He straightened, brushed nothing off his trousers, and stepped back from the edge. "The tide's going to do something extraordinary soon, Captain Coldharbour. When it does, I'd rather hear it from you than about you."

My hands went still on the coil. I hadn't given him a name. Nobody on this dock calls me Captain anything—they call me Petra, or they call me nothing, or they don't speak to me at all, which suits everyone. He watched the name land, watched it land exactly where he'd thrown it, and I understood that the coin on the dock wasn't payment. It was a receipt. Proof he'd made the offer, so that whatever came later, he'd done it politely first.

"The tide," I said, "does what tides do."

"Not this spring." He smiled, small and private, a man enjoying a joke with himself. "Not this one. Ask the old ones at Gullwatch. They'll tell you what drains away when the King's Purse opens." He turned, then paused, and added, almost gently: "Take care of the leak on your port garboard. It's worse than you think."

And he walked away up the dock, coat fitting at the shoulders, leaving the coin gleaming on the planks like a small gold accusation.

I stood at the rail a long time. The gulls argued over the fish sheds. The tide, ordinary and law-abiding, slid out toward the sea. I told myself the sensible things: he was a crank, a fortune-teller, a Consortium plant sent to rattle me, and none of it mattered because I had brass money in the strongbox and a payment due and no reason on this earth to listen to a stranger who knew my boat's seams better than I'd told anyone.

But the river was talking, the way it always talks, and under the gulls and the diesel and the morning noise of Saltmouth I could have sworn I heard it saying something new. Something low and pulling, out past the point, where the water goes over the cliffs at Gullwatch and has never once, in all my years of listening, had anything to say.

I picked up the coin. It was old. Older than any currency I knew, worn smooth, stamped with a cipher I didn't recognize—a crown, maybe, or something that had once been a crown before the sea got to it.

I should have thrown it in the water.

Instead I put it in my pocket, cast off, and turned the *Marrow*'s bow downriver, telling myself I was heading home, and knowing, in the place where I keep the things I refuse to know, that home was not where I was going.
