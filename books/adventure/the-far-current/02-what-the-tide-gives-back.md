## 2. What the Tide Gives Back

The tide went out farther than it had any right to.

I noticed it the way you notice a stranger standing too long outside your door—not all at once, but as an accumulation of wrongness. The gulls were wrong first. They'd moved their feeding line out past the second sandbar, which they never do, because the second sandbar is where the current runs cold and the fish go stupid. Then the smell was wrong: low tide on the Vell smells of mud and rot and old rope, but this morning it smelled of stone. Wet stone, deep stone, the mineral breath of places that haven't seen air in a hundred years.

I was sitting on the Marrow's rail with coffee I'd stretched three days past decency, watching the water fall away from Gullwatch Point like a curtain being drawn by somebody with no sense of occasion.

I've read that water my whole life. My father read it before me, and his mother before him, back to Coldharbours who piloted barges when the dynasty still had a dynasty to be deposed from. The Vell doesn't do surprises. It does moods—sandbars have them, channels have them, the whole river has a temperament like a drunk uncle—and if you pay attention for forty years you learn to feel a change coming in your teeth before your instruments agree. But this wasn't a mood. This was the river doing something no chart recorded, no logbook mentioned, no old man at Saltmouth had ever slurred about over cheap brandy.

The sea had withdrawn from the cliffs below Gullwatch by a good forty yards. Maybe fifty. And there, at the base of the cliff where every chart since the Survey said *solid rock*, was a mouth.

* * *

Let me be honest about what I did next, because honesty is cheaper now than it used to be.

I didn't report it. I didn't row ashore and knock on the harbormaster's door like a citizen. I sat on my rail and drank my terrible coffee and did arithmetic.

The Marrow owed money. Not to a bank—banks want collateral and paperwork and other inconveniences—but to the Consortium, which had financed my last refit through an agent whose name I made a point of not knowing, on terms I'd signed without reading twice because reading twice only tells you how badly you're being robbed. The hull leaked the way old boats leak, faithfully, daily, a slow confession of age. The engine needed work I couldn't afford. And the payment due at month's end was a number I could say out loud without flinching only if I said it very fast.

Forty years on the Vell and I owned nothing outright except my hands and my habit of being overlooked. That's not self-pity. Self-pity requires an audience, and I'd spent decades making sure I didn't have one.

So: a cave that shouldn't exist, exposed by a tide that shouldn't happen, on a morning when nobody else seemed to be looking. You can call what I felt opportunity. You can call it temptation. On the Vell we call it salvage, and salvage is honest work, or so I told myself while I fetched the dinghy, the lantern, the crowbar, and the canvas bag I keep for things that don't officially happen.

* * *

The walk across the exposed seabed was like walking on the floor of someone else's memory.

Anemones the size of dinner plates hung closed and glistening, stranded mid-hunt. A wrecked skiff lay half-buried in sand, its timbers gone soft as bread—I stepped on one and my boot went through. Barnacles crunched underfoot in sheets. The cliffs rose on my left, black and dripping, streaked white with centuries of birdlime, and the cave mouth grew as I approached until it stopped being a mouth and became a doorway: twenty feet high, its arch smoothed by water into something almost architectural, almost deliberate.

Locals called it the King's Purse. Every kid on this stretch of coast grows up on the story—the drowned treasury of the Verane dynasty, loaded onto barges the night the palace fell, sunk in the storm off Gullwatch rather than surrendered, guarded ever after by the current itself. The current kept the secret so well that generations of treasure-hunters drowned proving it, and eventually everyone sensible agreed the whole thing was a story, the way everyone sensible agrees the moon is made of cheese because it saves time.

The story was supposed to stay underwater. The tide had other opinions.

Inside, the air was cold and tasted of salt and iron. My lantern threw shadows that moved wrong, the way they do in big spaces your eyes can't map. The floor sloped down and inward, still puddled in places with trapped seawater, and the walls were carved.

Not naturally carved. Chiseled. Rows of niches, some empty, some holding shapes I didn't examine too closely at first—urns, mostly, sealed with lead. And along one wall, half-dissolved by time but unmistakable, a frieze: a line of figures in procession, carrying objects on their shoulders. Chests. Scepters. One figure carried what was clearly a crown, rendered with enough loving detail that whoever cut it had either been paid well or had opinions about royalty.

I stood there longer than was prudent, listening to the drip of water and my own pulse, doing the thing I do best, which is deciding how much trouble a situation is worth.

Here's the trouble with treasure: it's never just treasure. Treasure is a story people kill for, and the older the story, the duller the knife. I knew that. My father knew that; it's arguably what killed him, though the gorge gets the official credit. A sane woman looks at a cave full of dead kings' belongings and walks back out to her leaking boat and her impossible debt and keeps both.

I am sane. Ask anyone who's managed to notice me, which is approximately no one.

But I also did the arithmetic again, standing in the dark with the frieze watching me carry nothing on my shoulders. Month's end. The number. The Marrow.

* * *

I went deeper. Of course I went deeper. You don't come all this way to look at the lobby.

The passage narrowed, then opened into a chamber where the legend stopped being a legend and became inventory. Coins—thousands of them, tens of thousands—fused into drifts by coral and concretion, spilling from rotted chests like frozen rivers of metal. Gold mostly, some silver gone black, the faces on them worn smooth or eaten entirely. Candlesticks. A mirror the size of a door, its silvering survived in patches, reflecting my lantern back at me in pieces. And against the far wall, a chest of lead—lead, which no salvager uses for valuables unless he wants them to survive—banded in iron, stamped with a cipher I recognized from the tail end of every history book in Saltmouth: the double wave and star of the Veranes.

My hands were shaking. I'll admit that. Not from fear exactly. From the specific vertigo of standing inside something everyone agrees isn't real.

And here is where I'd like to tell you I took nothing. Here's where the better version of me—the one in the stories, the one with a code—sets down the lantern, tips an imaginary hat to the dead, and walks out clean.

What I actually did was stand there and think about interest rates.

Because that's the thing nobody says about poverty: it makes thieves of the careful. Not desperate, raving thieves—careful ones. Thieves who take exactly one item instead of ten, who calculate weight and resale and risk, who tell themselves stories about loans and fairness while their hand is already moving. I looked at that room of drowned wealth and I thought, *they're dead, it's been a century, no one living owns this, and the Consortium owns me, and between those two facts there is one ring.*

There was a niche near the frieze, smaller than the others, holding personal effects—a rotted velvet case, a hairbrush with a few strands of brown still clinging to it, and rings. Signet rings. I picked up the least grand of them: heavy gold, a band sized for a man's smallest finger, set with a seal I didn't study closely because studying it would make it real. Somewhere behind me the lead chest sat with its royal cipher, and I deliberately turned my back on it, because a woman who takes one ring is a scavenger, and a woman who takes a chest is a target, and I have survived this long by being neither.

I put it in my pocket. I told myself it was pragmatism. One ring, pawned quietly in a town far enough downriver that gossip couldn't swim that fast, covers the month's payment, buys the engine part, keeps the Marrow floating, keeps me invisible. That's not greed. That's maintenance. That's what the tide gave back, and I was only accepting delivery.

You can dress anything up as pragmatism if the lighting is bad enough. The lighting in that cave was excellent, actually—single lantern, hard shadows—and I dressed it up anyway.

* * *

On the way out I passed the frieze again, and this time I did look at the figures properly. The procession wasn't going toward the cave mouth. It was going deeper in. Whoever carved it had shown the treasure being carried *away* from the light, into the dark, and the faces of the bearers were worn smooth—by water, I assumed, though the rest of the carving was sharp enough to cut you.

Maybe it was just erosion picking favorites. But standing there with a dead man's ring in my pocket, I got the distinct feeling the frieze was less a decoration than an instruction, and that I was walking the wrong direction.

The tide had begun to turn. I could hear it—a low, patient sound at the cave mouth, the sea remembering its property. I'm fast on wet rock; forty years of decks will do that. I made the mouth with minutes to spare and stood outside watching the water come back across the seabed, filling in my own footprints, erasing the skiff, closing the anemones like fists.

By the time I reached the Marrow, the cave mouth was a dark suggestion beneath six inches of rising water, then four, then two, then nothing. The charts were accurate again. The world had tidied itself.

I sat on the rail with the ring in my pocket and my coffee gone cold and told myself the day was over and nothing had changed.

That lasted until I got the ring out under proper light to see what I'd actually stolen.

It wasn't a king's ring. Too small, too plain for that—no crown cipher, no double wave and star. Just a single letter engraved in the seal, old-fashioned script, worn nearly smooth: a C.

Coldharbour. Or close enough to chill me either way.

I sat there a long time, turning it over, while the Vell came back in around my anchor like it had never left, and somewhere downriver in Saltmouth a pawnbroker I hadn't met yet began, unknowingly, to matter very much.
