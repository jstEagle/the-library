## 3. The Pawnbroker's Arithmetic

The pawnbroker's shop in Saltmouth sat between a chandler's and a funeral parlor, which struck me as honest geography: everything you owned ended up in one of those three places eventually. Old Ferro had run the shop forty years, and I'd been pawning things to him since I was nineteen—a compass my father left me, twice; a wedding ring that wasn't mine by blood but was mine by stubbornness; once, in the worst winter of my life, a coat so threadbare Ferro gave it back to me out of pity and called it a failed appraisal.

He knew me. That was the problem.

I'd told myself all the way up the hill from the dock that I'd be smart about it. No story, no questions, just set the ring on his velvet pad like it was any other piece of drowned silver and let him make an offer. The King's Purse was full of legends, and legends meant nothing to a man who weighed metal for a living. A signet was a signet. Gold was gold. Nobody in Saltmouth would look at a heavy old ring and think *dynasty*.

I believed that right up until Ferro picked up his loupe.

He was a small man, Ferro, built like a knot in wood, with hands that trembled from age or tea or both. But when he turned the ring under the lamp and the light caught the cipher cut into the bezel—the crowned wave, the three stars beneath—his hands went still. Completely still. I'd never seen them still before. It was like watching a river stop.

"Where," he said, "did you get this."

"Fished it up." I kept my voice bored. Boredom was armor, same as competence. "Off Gullwatch. Tide's been strange."

"The tide's been strange." He said it flatly, not as a question, and he did not take his eye off the bezel. Through the loupe his pupil was a tiny black pinhole. "Petra Coldharbour. Do you know what you're holding?"

"A signet ring."

"I know whose signet ring." He set it down on the pad with exaggerated care, the way you'd put down something that might go off. Then he looked at me, and his face had gone the color of the paper his loan tickets were printed on. "And I know that men have been hanged—*hanged*, girl—for less than carrying it through a market square."

"That was two hundred years ago."

"Laws don't drown, Captain. They just get rewritten by whoever's holding the ledger." He pushed the ring back toward me across the glass. "Take it somewhere else."

"You won't buy it?"

"I wouldn't touch it with a barge pole. I wouldn't touch it with your barge pole." He was already moving toward the door, and here was the thing that made the back of my neck prickle: he wasn't shooing me out. He was checking the street. He pulled the curtain aside an inch, looked left, looked right, and let it fall. "There was a man in here yesterday asking after salvage. City clothes. Soft hands. Asking who ran night work on the Vell, who didn't file manifests, who could be bought quiet." His eyes came back to me and there was something in them I hadn't seen since I was a child watching grown men talk about my father—pity, the kind that comes right before someone decides you're already gone. "I told him nothing. Not because I'm loyal to you. Because I'm loyal to staying alive."

"Ferro—"

"Get off the water tonight, Petra. Tonight. Before the tide gives anything else back." He opened the door for me, and the bell above it rang bright and stupid, announcing me to the whole street. "And whatever you do, don't come back here with more of it."

I walked out into the afternoon light with the ring burning a hole through my pocket like a coal, and I did the arithmetic I should have done at the mouth of the cave, standing in air that hadn't moved in two centuries. Three hundred crowns. That was what the debt came to, plus interest, plus the Consortium's fees for the privilege of owing them. Three hundred crowns stood between me and losing the Marrow, and I'd held a treasury in my hand and taken one ring because one ring was enough, because taking more would have made me a thief instead of a salvor, because the line between those two things was the only inheritance my father had ever given me.

One ring. And even one ring was too much.

* * *

Saltmouth is a town that talks the way water moves—finding every crack, pooling in every low place, arriving everywhere eventually no matter how you dam it. By the time I'd walked the length of the fish market, I could feel it happening around me. Conversations dipping as I passed. A boy peeling shrimp who watched me with his knife forgotten in his hand. Two net-menders by the seawall who stopped talking mid-word, and one of them, an old woman named Brack who'd sold me bait for fifteen years, gave me a nod that wasn't friendly and wasn't hostile—it was the nod you give a marked boat.

Word travels faster than a ketch under full canvas. I'd been in the pawnshop eleven minutes.

I bought nothing. I stopped nowhere. I went down to the dock with my collar up and my stride long, telling myself the whole time that this was nothing, that towns gossip about weather and prices and other people's misfortunes the way gulls scream—constant, meaningless, aimed at nothing. The Marrow sat where I'd left her, riding her mooring lines with that patient, slightly wounded air she always had, and seeing her settled something in my chest the way she always did. Whatever else was true, she was mine. Whatever else they took, they couldn't take what I knew about water.

I was coiling the spring line when the launch came around the breakwater.

It was Consortium gray, of course. Everything about the Consortium was gray—their ledgers, their launches, their agents' coats, the particular shade of their patience. It came in slow and economical, no wake worth the name, and it did not head for the commercial berths. It headed for me.

Dame Sable Quist stood at the rail with her hands folded in front of her like a woman waiting for a hymn to end. She was not what people expected when they thought of Consortium collectors—I'd heard dockhands describe her as somebody's maiden aunt, somebody's schoolmistress—and that was exactly the point of her. She was sixty-odd, silver-haired, neat as a pinned chart, with reading glasses on a chain and a voice soft enough to apologize for itself. She had collected debts in the river towns for thirty years. Men had wept in front of her. One man, they said, had jumped off the Tarrow Bridge rather than hear her read out the terms a second time.

She stepped onto my deck without asking, which told me everything before she said a word. You don't board another captain's vessel uninvited unless you're certain she can't afford to throw you overboard.

"Captain Coldharbour." She smiled, and it reached her eyes, which somehow made it worse. "What a lovely boat. The *Marrow*, isn't it? Such an evocative name."

"It's a working deck, madam. State your business."

"My business." She produced a leather folio from under her arm and opened it on my own chart table without ceremony, squaring its corners to the edge the way she probably squared everything—her papers, her conscience, other people's lives. "Your business, rather. Or ours. They've become entangled, haven't they?" She turned the folio toward me. My loan papers, all of them, every signature I'd ever pressed into Consortium paper, arranged in chronological order like a biography written in ink. "Three hundred and twelve crowns outstanding, as of this morning's interest posting. Forgive the extra twelve. Time is never free."

"I know what I owe."

"I'm sure you do. You strike me as a woman who knows exactly what she owes." She took off her glasses, polished them on a handkerchief that appeared from nowhere, and put them back on. It was a ritual, I realized. She performed it while deciding things. "You also strike me as a woman who has spent a great deal of effort being difficult to find. No fixed berth. Night runs. Cash trades. It's almost admirable. Almost." She folded the handkerchief away. "But here is the difficulty, Captain. This morning, a pawnbroker sent a message. Not to us—to a certain gentleman who keeps rooms above the customs house and pays well for messages. The message concerned a signet ring bearing the cipher of the Aldemere line. And the pawnbroker, in his wisdom, declined the item. Which means it is still in circulation. Which means it is still, presumably, in your pocket."

My hand wanted to move toward that pocket. I kept it on the rail, knuckles down, and thanked every god I'd never prayed to that my father had taught me cards young.

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"No," Quist agreed pleasantly. "You wouldn't. Let me tell you what I know instead, and you can decide afterward whether to keep pretending. I know the tide at Gullwatch Point dropped four feet below any recorded low. I know the fishermen are calling it an omen and the priests are calling it a judgment and both of them are wrong—it's weather, Captain, weather far out past the shelf, though I grant you nobody living has seen its like. I know the cliffs drained and a cave mouth stood open that has stood shut since before the Consortium held charter on this coast. And I know that within two days of that tide, a riverboat captain with three hundred crowns of Consortium debt and a documented talent for finding things underwater visited a pawnbroker with a royal signet in her hand." She smiled again, gently, the way you'd smile at a child who'd lied about breaking a window while still holding the brick. "I am very good at arithmetic, Captain Coldharbour. It's most of the job."

The harbor sounds went on around us—gulls, rigging, a winch groaning two piers down—but on the Marrow's deck it had gone very quiet, the way water goes quiet over a deep spot.

"So," I said. "You want the ring."

"I don't care about the ring." And here she leaned forward slightly, and for the first time the pleasantness thinned and I saw the thing underneath it, the actual engine, and it was colder than any collector's greed. "A ring is a trinket. Rings can be buried, melted, thrown down a well. What I care about is context. If one ring came out of that cave, then the cave is open, and if the cave is open, then everything in it is coming out—sooner or later, in pieces, through hands like yours. Coins. Plate. Documents." Her pause on that last word was small and precise, a blade laid flat against a table. "The Consortium's charter on this coast rests on certain historical settlements. Settlements require that certain events remain settled. Salvage from the Aldemere treasury, surfacing now, in quantity, with provenance—" she spread her hands, a small elegant gesture of disaster—"would be *unsettling*."

"And if I say I found nothing? If I say the ring isn't mine?"

"Then I take the boat." She said it without heat, without emphasis, the way you'd report the time. "That was always the arrangement, Captain. You signed it. I'm merely offering you an alternative before I exercise it. Find what else the tide revealed. Bring it to me. All of it—every coin, every scrap, and especially anything with writing on it. Do that, and I'll tear these papers up on this deck in front of you, and you'll owe the Consortium nothing, and we'll never have met." She gathered her folio, squared it against her hip. "Refuse, and I take the *Marrow* at noon tomorrow, and you can swim after your independence."

I looked at her for a long moment. There was a question I shouldn't ask, and I asked it anyway, because my father's other lesson was that you always count the other player's chips.

"Why you?" I said. "The Consortium has collectors for every port. Why does the chief of them come herself, in person, for one leaky ketch and one pawned ring?"

Quist paused with one foot on her gunwale, and something moved behind her glasses—not anger, not quite. Recognition, maybe. The look of a woman noting that the debtor had counted correctly.

"Because," she said, "the last time the King's Purse opened, my grandmother's family lost everything they had. We are thorough in my family, Captain. It's the only thing we were left." And then she stepped down into her launch, and the gray boat drew away, economical as ever, leaving nothing behind but a little wake slapping against my hull like slow, polite applause.

* * *

I sat in the Marrow's cabin until dark with the ring on the chart table in front of me and the lantern turned low, doing sums.

Here is what the arithmetic said. Take Quist's bargain: give her the cave, walk away clean, keep the boat. Simple. Clean. Except that a woman who erases evidence doesn't leave witnesses standing in the doorway, and I'd seen her face when she talked about documents. Except that the cave held a dynasty's whole drowned fortune and I'd be handing it to the same gray machine that had been bleeding this coast dry for a hundred years, and my father had died poor and I'd grown up poor and I knew exactly whose pockets that fortune would line. Except, except, except.

Here is what the arithmetic also said. Keep the ring, run tonight, disappear upriver the way I'd disappeared my whole life. Except Ferro's city-clothed visitor had found me anyway, in a town of ten thousand, inside a day. Except Quist had found me faster. Except invisibility, I was beginning to understand, was a service other people provided to you only for as long as it suited them, and the bill always came due.

Three hundred and twelve crowns. One ring worth perhaps forty, if anyone dared buy it. One cave full of dead kings' money that everyone suddenly wanted, and everyone suddenly wanted *through me*, because I was the one who'd been fool enough to reach into the water.

I turned the ring over. The crowned wave caught the lamplight. Somewhere under all that gold, I supposed, was a man who'd worn it, and a fleet that served him, and a family named Coldharbour that had—what? Rowed for him? Betrayed him? The legends never said how the dynasty fell, only that it fell, and that the sea had been paid to keep its mouth shut about it.

There was a knock at the hull. Three knocks, spaced evenly, too polite for a harbormaster and too confident for a thief.

I blew out the lantern and slid the ring into my pocket and went up the companionway with a boathook in my hand, and standing at the rail in the dark, in city clothes gone salt-stiff at the cuffs, was the man from the dock at Gullwatch. The one who'd watched me too closely. The one I'd refused passage to.

"You're a hard woman to book passage with, Captain," he said. "So I've come to change the terms."
