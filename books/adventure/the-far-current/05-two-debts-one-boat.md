## 5. Two Debts, One Boat

I slept badly, which was nothing new, but I slept badly in a new way—horizontally, for once, flat on the bunk in the Marrow's cabin instead of propped against the wheel with one eye on the water. Sleep came in shallow spoonfuls, each one flavored with a different face: Quist's gray gloves resting on my loan papers like a cat's paws on a dead bird, and the stranger at the rail saying *the ring is mine by blood* with the particular calm of a man who has never once had to shout to be believed.

By the time the gulls started their argument over the fish market scraps, I had already been up an hour, drinking tea that tasted of rust and doing arithmetic I didn't like.

Here was the sum as it stood. Dame Sable Quist held paper on my boat worth more than the boat, and she wanted me to go back down into the King's Purse and bring up whatever else the tide had uncovered. She hadn't said what she'd do with it. Collectors never do. The word *find* had come out of her mouth smooth as ballast stone going over the side, and just as heavy.

The stranger—I still wouldn't call him Emeric Vale, because names were currency and I didn't spend on credit—wanted me to carry him to the same cave for triple whatever Quist was paying, except he hadn't said money exactly. He'd said *what you're owed*. Which is either the most generous offer in the history of the Vell or the oldest con since somebody first told somebody else there was gold at the bottom of a river.

And underneath both offers, patient as silt, sat the truth: whoever got to the King's Purse would get there in my boat, through water only I knew, and both of them seemed to think that made me the instrument rather than the author.

I set the cup down harder than necessary.

"No," I said to the empty cabin, and it agreed with me immediately, which is more than most people manage.

* * *

The morning market at Saltmouth runs on noise. Fish thrown, prices shouted, wives arguing about ice. I moved through it with my collar up and my eyes down, buying things a captain buys—tar, canvas, a sack of onions—and listening with the other ear, the one I keep tuned to my own name.

It came twice. Once from a crab-boat man who looked at me a beat too long before turning back to his scales. Once from two boys on the seawall who shut up when I passed, which meant they'd been talking about me, because boys don't shut up for anything else.

Word travels on the Vell faster than any boat I've ever owned. By noon everyone from Gullwatch to Saltmouth would know Petra Coldharbour had walked out of a drowned king's cave wearing his ring on her thumb. By tomorrow they'd have improved the story. By Friday I'd have found a crown.

The pawnbroker had gone pale—that was the detail that kept snagging in my mind like a hook in netting. Not greedy-pale. *Afraid*-pale. He'd handled that ring like it might handle him back, and then he'd named a price so low it was practically an apology, and I'd taken it because taking it was faster than asking questions. That was the mistake. That was always the mistake. Asking questions costs time, and time on the Vell is paid for in hull.

So no, I wasn't going back into that cave for Quist, and I wasn't ferrying Vale to it either. New plan, simple plan, the kind of plan I trust: I'd go alone. Tonight's tide would run nearly as low as the spring's—the moon hadn't given up on us yet—and I knew the approach water better than any chart dared guess. In and out before dawn, take what could be carried by one pair of hands, and let the two of them negotiate over an empty purse.

Alone. The word sat comfortably, the way old boots sit. Comfortable, and full of holes, and smelling faintly of something wrong.

* * *

The Marrow told me her troubles while I worked.

Every boat talks if you put your hands on her long enough. Mine was forty years of talk compressed into oak and iron, and lately she'd gotten chatty in a way I didn't care for. I went along her garboard strakes with a screwdriver and a lamp, tapping, listening, and she answered in the language of soft spots. Here a plank that should have rung like a bell and only muttered. There a seam where the caulking had dried to the consistency of old bread. And aft, near the turn of the bilge, a patch of planking that gave under my thumbnail like the flesh of a fruit bought too ripe.

"Weeping again," I told her. "You're a disgrace and I love you."

She'd wept since the day I bought her, honestly—every old boat leaks a little, takes on her allowance of the river, gives it back to the bilge pump on schedule. A cup an hour was normal. Two cups was character. But this morning the pump had cycled four times between breakfast and noon, and when I opened the bilge and looked in, the water staring back at me had risen past the mark I'd scratched there in March.

Not flooding. Not yet. Just... ambitious.

I spent three hours on that soft spot aft. Drove oakum into the seam, painted her with tar thinned hot enough to smoke, screwed a backing block over the worst plank and prayed to no particular saint that it would hold until I could haul her out properly. Properly cost money. Money was the thing I kept almost having.

And while I worked, the light changed.

You learn to read weather the way you read a face across a crowded room—not one feature but the whole arrangement of it. The light off the river had gone thin and white, bleached of its usual gold. The wind had swung around twice without settling anywhere. And the barometer on the bulkhead, an instrument older than me and more honest than most people, had begun its slow slide: not dropping, not yet, but leaning, the way a drunk leans against a doorframe before he commits to falling.

Monsoon season didn't start. It arrived the way a debt arrives—you know the date for months, and then suddenly it's today.

I wiped my hands and stood in the well deck looking south, toward the gorge, toward the open sea beyond it, and felt the season lean with the needle.

Two days, maybe three, of working weather left. After that, nobody went anywhere, and every harbor became a trap with a view.

* * *

He found me at dusk, which figured. Men like him operated at dusk.

I'd tied up at the fueling dock below the chandlery, running the pump hose into the Marrow's tank, and I heard his footsteps on the planks—city footsteps, even-paced, no sailor's roll—and I said, without turning around, "Dock's rented by the hour and I'm using all of it."

"I'll stand." He stopped at the rail anyway, close enough that I could smell rain coming off him, actual rain, the first spit of the season beading on his shoulders. "You've decided."

"Have I."

"You've been aboard since morning. You haven't gone near the pawnbroker, or the Consortium office, or any of the three people in this town who'd sell your course to Quist for a drink." His voice stayed level, conversational, a man discussing tides. "Which means you're going alone."

I finished pumping, coiled the hose, and turned around slowly. He looked better rested than I felt, which offended me on principle. City clothes gone, replaced by oilskin and rubber boots too new to trust—a man who dressed the part quickly when the part required it.

"And if I am?"

"Then you'll die in that cave or drown on the way home carrying more weight than one person can swim with." He shrugged. "Or you'll make it, and Quist will find you inside a week, because you can't outrun paper, Captain. Paper doesn't sleep, doesn't eat, and has friends in every port from here to the delta."

"Fond of speeches."

"Fond of being right. It's a flaw." He leaned his forearms on the rail, unhurried, and I hated how unhurried he was. Hurried men I could handle. "You need a deckhand. You need someone on the pump, someone on the line, someone whose hands you can trust in the dark under thirty feet of cliff. One pair of hands brought you out last time. Barely. I watched you come ashore at Gullwatch, Captain. You were bleeding from the forearm and you hid it for six hours."

That stung, mostly because it was true. Coral had kissed me on the way out of the cave mouth and I'd wrapped it in a rag and said nothing, because saying something invites questions, and questions invite people into the small country of yourself that I keep carefully unpopulated.

"Say your offer," I said.

"Passage to the cave. Equal share of whatever we carry out. And when it's done—" he paused, and for the first time something moved under the calm, some current deeper than the surface, "—when it's done, I'll see your paper with Quist bought and burned. All of it. Whatever it costs."

"That's not an offer, that's a fairy story."

"It's arithmetic," he said. "You think I want coins? Captain, I could buy this dock if half my claims were true, and I'm telling you all of them are. What I want is down there, and it weighs less than a hatbox. Everything else in that cave is yours."

Everything else. Fused coral, drowned coin, the sediment of a murdered dynasty. I looked at his hands resting on my rail—clean hands, scarred in places hands shouldn't be scarred if their owner had grown up in drawing rooms—and I thought about Quist's gloves, and the pawnbroker's face, and the bilge water climbing its mark like a slow tide with ambitions.

Here's what I knew for certain, and it was a short list. One: Quist wanted what was in that cave badly enough to threaten my boat, and the Consortium did not send Dame Sable Quist anywhere for pocket change. Two: this man wanted what was in that cave, or in it, badly enough to dress poor and wait on docks. Three: the weather was turning, my hull was talking in its sleep, and I could not make the run alone and come back whole. I'd known that since noon. I'd simply been refusing to say it aloud, because saying it aloud meant needing someone, and needing people was a habit I'd broken decades ago and had no intention of relapsing into.

But the sea doesn't care about my habits. Neither does monsoon.

"Deckhand," I said.

His eyebrows rose a fraction. "I offered passage—"

"Passage is for passengers, and passengers get opinions, and passengers' opinions get people drowned." I stepped closer, and I let him see all the way down, which I almost never do, because I needed him to understand the depth before he signed anything. "You sign articles. Deckhand wages, which is to say your share comes after the boat's expenses, after the fuel, after the pump parts I'm buying tonight. You work when I say work, steer when I say steer, and go quiet when I say quiet. You don't touch the wheel without my hand on your shoulder telling you why. You don't ask about the scar, the debt, or the name on my papers. And when I tell you to jump, you ask *how far*, not *why*. Those are the rules. There are no other rules. Break one and I put you ashore wherever we happen to be, swimming, and I won't look back. Nod if you're still interested."

He looked at me for a long moment. Something flickered across his face that might have been amusement, or might have been respect, or might have been the expression of a man realizing he'd boarded a smaller ship than he'd imagined.

"Nod," I repeated.

He nodded.

"Then welcome aboard the Marrow. Wages start now. There's a bucket and a scrub brush in the locker aft, and the deck hasn't been done since the last owner, who died lazy." I handed him the coil of hose. "Coil that properly. If you roll it like a city man I'll know everything I need to know about you."

He took it, and—he surprised me—he coiled it correctly, over-and-under, the way deckhands are taught and passengers never learn, and he did it without comment, and I filed that away in the drawer where I keep facts that complicate my theories.

* * *

We sailed on the midnight tide.

I took her out of Saltmouth on the ebb with the harbor lights smearing themselves across the black water, and Vale—if he was Vale; I'd caught him watching me write his name in the articles and I'd written it the way he'd spelled it, which proved nothing except that he could spell—Vale worked. That was the second surprise. No instructions wasted, no standing around waiting to be told twice. When I said *fenders*, fenders appeared. When I said *that line*, that line came to me already flaked and ready. Somewhere behind the city clothes and the heir's calm was a boy who had learned boats somewhere, and learned them properly, and I found myself wanting to know that story and hating myself for wanting to know it.

The barometer fell another point by the time we cleared the point. Out of the south the sky had organized itself into long gray shelves, stacked and patient, and the wind carried that smell I knew from my father's last season—the smell of a lot of water deciding where to land. The Marrow's seams sweated in sympathy. Below, the pump cycled. Waited. Cycled again. A little sooner each time.

I stood my watch and let him stand his, alternating through the small hours, and somewhere past three in the morning he came aft to relieve me and paused beside the wheel, looking south at the horizon where lightning was practicing, silent and far off, like a fist clenching behind a curtain.

"How bad will it get?" he asked.

"Worse than you've seen, if you grew up inland. Better than the worst one, if the odds hold." I checked the compass more out of ritual than need. "Question isn't how bad. Question's whether we're done before it starts."

"And if we're not?"

"Then we'll be having a very different conversation about your treasure."

He was quiet a moment. Then: "You could still turn back. Take Quist's terms. Debt gone, boat safe, no monsoon run, no cave, no me."

I laughed—short, involuntary, surprising us both. "You've met me for two days and you think debt forgiveness would buy me."

"It bought your silence at the pawnshop."

"My silence was cheap because I was selling something worthless." I handed him the wheel and stepped back, and the Marrow came into his hands the way she came into anyone's—reluctantly, testing. "What's down in that cave stopped being worthless the minute two powerful people started bidding on it. Now go quiet, deckhand. Your watch."

He went quiet. He actually went quiet.

I went below to check the bilge, because checking the bilge was the only honest prayer I knew. The water had climbed another finger past March's mark, and the soft spot aft was weeping a thin bright thread that ran down the planking like the boat crying in her sleep.

Three days of working weather. Maybe two and a half, the way that sky was stacking itself.

Ahead, past the dark water and the leaning needle and the stranger holding my wheel, Gullwatch Point waited with its purse closed, and somewhere behind us, I'd have bet the boat herself, a gray launch with a woman in gray gloves was reading the same falling glass and making the same arithmetic.

I put my palm flat against the wet planking, the way my father used to, and told her what I tell her every time the odds go long.

"Hold together, old girl. Just a little longer than seems reasonable."

The pump kicked on beneath my feet, right on schedule, faithful as a heartbeat—and running, I noticed, a little fast.
