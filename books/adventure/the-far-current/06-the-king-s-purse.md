## 6. The King's Purse

*Petra*

Slack water at the King's Purse lasted maybe forty minutes, and I'd promised myself we'd be out of the cave before it turned. That was the plan. I have always been a great believer in plans, mostly because watching them fail tells you so much about a person.

"Rules," I said, while Emeric stripped down to his shirt on the Marrow's foredeck. The ketch lay anchored off Gullwatch Point in water the color of strong tea, the cliffs above us bare as ribs. Two days earlier the sea had pulled back farther than any living soul could remember, and somewhere under that retreating gray lip was a hole in the world that kings had supposedly filled with gold. "Rule one: nobody touches anything until I say. Rule two: if the water starts moving, we move faster than it. Rule three—"

"You keep the ring," he said.

I hadn't told him about the ring. Not exactly. He'd deduced it, which was worse, because deduction is what clever men do instead of asking questions they don't want answered out loud.

"I keep everything," I said. "You're deckhand, not partner."

"Deckhands get shares."

"Deckhands get paid. Eventually. If there's anything left after the bilge pump eats diesel." I checked the anchor rode, checked it again, because checking things twice is another kind of armor. "In and out. Twenty minutes. You dive like you said you could?"

"I grew up on this coast." He said it lightly, but there was a weight under it, the way there's a weight under every sentence that man spoke, like furniture moved into an empty house. "Different coast. Same water."

That wasn't an answer, but the tide doesn't wait for answers. We went over the side.

The cold hit me like a hand pressed flat against my chest—the Vell never really warms, even in summer, even in the shallows—and then the light changed and I stopped thinking altogether for a few blessed seconds. Diving does that for me. It's the only place where being invisible is the natural condition of things. Down there nobody sees anybody. You're just breath and motion and the long green hush.

The cave mouth opened ahead of us like a held note. From outside it had looked like a crack, a seam of darker dark in the cliff base, but once you were through it widened, and the surge that had been shoving at our backs let go all at once, and we rose into air.

Air. Inside the rock. A chamber tall enough that our lamplight didn't reach the ceiling, the water below us flat and black and still as a held breath. The tide had gone out of it and left a floor.

Emeric surfaced beside me, spitting salt, and for a moment neither of us said anything, because there are rooms that make talking feel like vandalism.

"Well," he finally managed, and his voice cracked on it, actually cracked, and I thought: there goes the heir act, whatever's true underneath it. Nobody fakes that sound.

We waded ashore onto a shelf of stone that hadn't felt weather in three hundred years. The lamp showed me walls streaked with mineral bloom, white and green, like frost on a window. And along the far edge, where the shelf stepped down into the pool—

Coins.

Not scattered coins. Coins grown into coral, fused into colonies of them, whole reefs of money, silver gone black and gold still holding its dull fire under the silt. They'd been poured in here by the chestload, centuries back, and the sea had done what the sea does, which is take loose things and make them permanent. Somebody had tried to hide a treasury from history, and history had responded by turning it into geology.

"It's real," Emeric said. He'd gone to his knees in front of the nearest cluster without seeming to decide to. His fingers hovered over the coral-locked coins and didn't touch. Rule one, observed. Points to him. "My grandmother used to tell it as a story. The night the fleet burned, the loyalists rowed the treasury into the caves below Gullwatch, and the tide took the entrance back, and that was the end of the Aurelian line's money and nearly the end of the line itself." He laughed, short and disbelieving. "She told it like a lullaby. *Sleep, the gold is safe.*"

"Families tell themselves stories," I said. "Keeps them from asking where the gold came from."

He looked up at me then, and something crossed his face—not quite offense, more like a man hearing a door he knew existed but had never opened. "What do you mean?"

"Later," I said, because my eye had caught on something past him, set back in a niche in the wall where the coral hadn't reached.

A chest. Lead-sheathed, small, the size of a strongbox, crusted white with centuries but intact. And stamped into the lead, repeated around the lid like a border, a cipher: a wave beneath a crown.

I've salvaged a lot of things off the bottom of the Vell. I know what my hands do when they find something valuable—they go quiet. Steady. It's the rest of me that wants to shake. I put my palms flat on the lid and felt the cold come up through the lead like the box was breathing winter.

"This is what Quist wants," I said. "Whatever's in here. This is worth a boat, and a debt, and probably a life or two."

"Then we should know what we're holding before she takes it." Emeric came to stand beside me. He was breathing carefully, the way people breathe when they're trying not to hope too loudly. "There'll be a lock. Aurelian work. Three tumblers, and the combination isn't numbers—it's a story."

"A story."

"The old families sealed their papers with riddles so servants couldn't open them. My grandmother taught me hers. She said—" He stopped. Started again. "She said one day the sea would give it back, and whoever stood in front of the chest would need to know why the crown sits *above* the wave, and not beneath it."

"And why does it?"

"Because the dynasty believed a king rules the water," he said. "And everyone who ever worked that water knew better. But you say the words anyway. That's the price of the lock. You flatter a dead king to get at his paper."

I stepped back and gave him room, and I watched him work, and I want to be honest in this account, wherever else I've shaded things: part of me watched him the way you watch a card sharp. Because if this was theater—if he'd rehearsed the story, planted the chest, run the whole tide-and-treasury con on a gullible riverboat captain—then this was the moment the trick would show. Tricks show at the lock. Anywhere else can be luck.

His hands found the three studs along the lid's edge. He turned the first, and I heard, faintly, through three hundred years of corrosion, a click.

"The crown above the wave," he murmured, turning the second stud, "because the king commands the sea—"

Click.

"—and the third," he said, and here his voice dropped, went almost private, "the third is for the queen, who kept the ledgers, and without whom the crown would have starved. *The hand beneath the hand.*"

Click.

The lid came up on a breath of stale air that smelled of lead and time and, underneath that, faintly, impossibly, of ink.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth that had gone stiff as bark, was a book. Ledger-sized. Bound in leather that had survived because lead survives, because someone had loved it enough to seal it away from the water that got everything else.

Emeric lifted it out with both hands, the way you'd lift a sleeping child, and I remember thinking: *that's either the truest thing I've ever seen or the best performance of my lifetime,* and I genuinely could not tell which, and that frightened me more than the rising tide did.

"Open it," I said.

He unwrapped the cloth. Opened the cover. The lamp threw our shadows huge against the wall, two giants bent over a dead king's paperwork.

And I saw his face change.

Wonder curdles fast when it curdles. I've seen it happen to divers who find a wreck full of fine china and then find the bones. One moment the world is generous; the next it's just old, and old means cruel. Emeric read down the first page and the generosity went out of him like tide through a cut.

"What is it?"

"It's not an inventory." His thumb moved along the columns. "It's not a list of what they *had*. It's a list of what they *took*. Names. Every family the crown borrowed from and never repaid. Every ship seized. Every loyalty bought cheap and paid for in silence." He turned pages, faster now. "It's signed. The queen's own hand, the third tumbler's hand—she kept the record of her family's crimes. Why would she—"

"Because somebody in that palace had a conscience," I said, "and no power at all. Common arrangement."

He wasn't listening. He'd stopped on a page near the front, where the entries were older, the ink browner, and he read the name there aloud like it was a word in a language he'd forgotten he spoke.

"Coldharbour."

The name went through the chamber and came back off the walls.

"My great-grandfather's fleet carried the treasury the night it burned," I said. My voice sounded strange to me, flat, like someone else doing the telling. "That's the family story, anyway. Loyal to the last. We've been poor and proud about it for four generations." I held out my hand. "Let me see."

He gave me the book, and I read the entry, and the lamp flame leaned in the draft from the tunnel, and the water in the pool had begun, very softly, to move.

*Paid to R. Coldharbour, master of the ferry fleet, for silence regarding the disposition of the eastern vaults: nothing. Given freely, he said, and asked only that his name never appear in any account. Entered here so that it appears.*

Nothing. Paid in nothing. Sold the fleet's loyalty for the privilege of erasure, and the erasure was the payment, and my family had spent four generations calling the wound an honor.

The water was climbing the shelf now. Slack water dying, the tide turning, the sea coming home to collect its rent.

"We're leaving," I said, and closed the ledger, and did not look at Emeric's face, because I knew what would be on it—pity, or worse, understanding—and I could not afford either one just then.

* * *

*Emeric*

I have read accounts of the fall of the Aurelian dynasty. Everyone has; it's the founding story of half the institutions on this coast, including the Consortium, though the Consortium prefers a cleaner version in which brave merchants overthrew a tyrant rather than, as the ledger suggested with every turn of its page, bought his debts and foreclosed on his kingdom.

But reading history is different from standing inside it with wet boots.

Petra read her entry and shut the book, and I understood—I thought I understood—that I had just watched a woman learn her inheritance was a lie. Not the poverty. The poverty was real, four generations deep, I could see it in the patched planking of her boat and the mended seams of her gloves. What was a lie was the reason. Her family hadn't been rewarded for loyalty. They'd been *bought*, and cheaply, and the purchase had been recorded by the very queen whose seal I'd spent my childhood romanticizing.

"The hand beneath the hand," I said quietly, as we swam the ledger out through the tunnel, the book wrapped in oilcloth and strapped under my arm like a second heart. "She meant herself. She meant: I am the one who holds everything up, and no one looks down."

Petra didn't answer. She swam ahead of me through the green, and I watched the light from the cave mouth widen around her silhouette, and I thought about how I had come to this woman with a story about blood and crowns and rightful inheritance, and how the book in my arms said that inheritance was a debt ledger, and every name in it was owed.

Including mine. I found my own line before we reached the surface—Vale, entered under its older form, Valeran, exiled at age six, *dispossessed of all claims, compensated: nothing.* Nothing again. The dynasty's universal currency. We were all of us paid in nothing, and the gold sat under the wave growing coral, and the Consortium sat above the wave collecting interest on the theft.

We came up alongside the Marrow and hauled ourselves aboard, and Petra went straight to the anchor windlass without wringing out, without speaking, working the handle with a violence she probably thought was invisible.

"He sold you," I said. "Your great-grandfather. For anonymity."

"For *safety*," she snapped, and then heard herself, and stopped cranking. The anchor came free and the Marrow swung her head toward open water, toward Saltmouth, toward the pawnbroker and Dame Sable Quist and all the arithmetic waiting downriver. "Or that's what they told us. Loyalty and silence. We raised sons on it. My father died believing the Coldharbours were the honest ones in every room." She wiped her face with the back of her hand—salt water, or not, impossible to say. "One book. One book and four hundred years of family pride turns out to be a receipt."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be careful." She turned and looked at me across the deck, and the evening light was coming up gold behind her, and I saw for the first time how tired she was, how tired she had probably always been, in the way of people who carry things alone because trusting costs more than carrying. "Because here's what that book means, heir. It means every family on that coast with a founding legend has a page in there. Half the Consortium's charter families, I'd wager. You don't hold a treasure, Emeric. You hold a grudge the size of a coastline, and grudges that big don't stay in caves. People kill over smaller ones."

"That's rather the point."

"No." She pointed at the oilcloth bundle, dripping on her deck, staining her planks with three-hundred-year-old seawater. "*This* is the point. That book gets you killed and gets me killed and solves nothing unless you understand what you're actually carrying. So tell me. Now. Before the tide finishes turning and before anyone else asks. Why does the last heir of a drowned line want a ledger of debts? Kings want treasure. What do *you* want?"

And I opened my mouth to give her the answer I'd prepared—the restoration, the restitution, the noble return of what was stolen—and found, to my considerable surprise, that she'd already seen through it, or that I had, somewhere between the third tumbler and her great-grandfather's name, and that the truth had been sitting in my chest since the chamber, waiting for someone to ask plainly enough.

What I wanted was not the crown.

What I wanted was for the ledger to be *read*. Every page. Every name. In daylight, in front of everyone, with the Consortium's founders made to sit and hear their own founding crime recited aloud in their own charter hall. I didn't want the dynasty back. I wanted the lie buried properly this time, with witnesses.

I started to say all of this.

I got as far as "Petra" before she went rigid, her eyes fixed past my shoulder, south along the channel where the water lay long and pewter-colored in the failing light.

At the far edge of visibility, hull-down on the horizon, running lights burning in defiance of dusk, a launch was coming up the Vell against the current. Fast. White wake in a straight line, the way boats travel when the person aboard pays for speed and doesn't care who sees.

"She couldn't have followed us," Petra said. "Nobody knew."

"Somebody always knows," I said, and pulled the ledger tighter against my ribs, and the Marrow ran south into the dark with the grudge of a drowned kingdom dripping on her deck, and behind us the King's Purse swallowed itself back under the sea, keeping, as it had for three centuries, everything except the truth.
