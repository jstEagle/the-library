## 7. Ledger of the Drowned

The lead chest sat on the deck of the *Marrow* like a thing that had been waiting for us and resented the delay.

We'd hauled it up through the cave mouth on the last of the slack water, Emeric hauling until his knuckles went white and I hauling until my shoulders remembered every bad decision of my life, and now it lay between us in the afternoon light, streaming seawater, furred with forty years of green. The royal cipher was stamped deep in the lid—a crowned current, if you could believe the vanity of it, a dynasty that put its own river on its seal.

"You should do the honors," Emeric said.

"I've done my honors for one day." I nodded at him. "You opened the cave story. Open the box."

He knelt beside it. His hands were shaking—not from cold, though the water off Gullwatch Point could chill you to the marrow of your name, but from something else. He worked the seal with a marlinspike I lent him and pretended not to notice when he held it wrong, and then the lid came up with a groan like an old man sitting down, and we both leaned over the edge of history.

Coins, first. Rows of them, fused into a single gray mass by time and salt, worth a fortune or nothing depending entirely on who was doing the arithmetic. And beneath them, wrapped in oilcloth that had somehow, impossibly, kept its promise: books. Three of them. Two swollen with damp, their pages welded shut. One dry as a sermon.

Emeric lifted that one out with both hands, the way you'd lift a sleeping child, and I thought: *he believes this.* Whatever else he was—con man, fool, heir—he believed it all the way down.

"What is it?" I asked, though I could see what it was. A ledger. Bound in leather gone black, the spine cracked, the pages dense with a hand so fine it looked printed.

"The treasury accounts," he said quietly. "The last steward's records. Everything the crown moved, and where, and—" He stopped. Turned a page. Stopped again.

I'd spent enough years around men going quiet to know that quiet meant trouble. "And?"

He didn't answer. He turned the book toward me instead, and pointed.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about your family: they are people. Not the polished figures at the head of a table in the stories your father tells, not the names carved on stones you visit once a year out of obligation. People, who did small things for small reasons, and some of those things were rotten, and the rot doesn't care how much you loved them.

The entry was dated sixty-one years ago. The hand was the steward's, meticulous as tide tables. It recorded a payment made from the treasury reserve to one **Aldous Coldharbour, Master of the River Fleet**, and the payment was noted against a single line item:

*Loyalty, fleet's. Silence, subsequent.*

Paid in silence. My great-grandfather had been paid in silence, and the silence had held for three generations, right up until a woman who'd never heard the family stories took a ring off a dead king's shelf and tried to pawn it in Saltmouth.

"Read it again," I said.

Emeric read it aloud. His voice was gentle about it, which was worse than gloating would have been. "There's more. The whole last section—it's a list of settlements. Every family the crown bought off in the final year. Pilots who ran false channels for the loyalist gold. Harbor masters who lost manifests. The Coldharbours are entry nine of fourteen."

"Fourteen families," I said.

"Fourteen families who got paid to look away while the treasury was sunk and the line was hunted." He closed the ledger carefully, as if it might bruise. "Petra. Do you understand what this is? This isn't treasure. This is a confession with interest."

I walked to the rail. That's a thing I do—I want it on record that I know my own habits. When the world hands me something too large to hold, I go stand by the water, because the water has never once asked me a question I couldn't answer.

Sixty-one years ago, Aldous Coldharbour sold the loyalty of the river fleet for whatever sum a desperate steward could scrape together, and the fleet scattered, and the dynasty fell, and somewhere downstream a boy grew up hearing that his grandfather had been a hero of the old days, master of boats, king of the current—and none of it true, or none of it the truth that mattered. My father died running a gorge trying to prove something to a name that had already been bought and paid for before he was born.

"It's not mine," I said finally. "Whatever else is in that box. It's not mine."

"No," Emeric agreed. "It's yours and everyone's. That's rather the point."

* * *

We argued for most of an hour, there at anchor in the lee below Gullwatch, while the barometer needle crept leftward and neither of us mentioned it, because mentioning it would have made it real.

His position was simple and, I'll grant him, beautifully argued—the argument of a man who'd rehearsed it in exile, probably to himself, probably for years. The treasury belonged to the dispossessed. To the fourteen families bought off cheap, to the fishermen whose grounds the Consortium claimed under a charter that traced back to the same stolen gold, to every soul on the Vell who'd ever paid a toll to men whose grandfathers had robbed the till. The ledger wasn't just proof of the dynasty's fall. It was proof of the theft underneath everything that came after. The Consortium's founding money was this money. The whole rotten architecture of the river's ownership rested on a box at the bottom of a cave.

"And you're the one to carry that banner," I said. "Last of the line."

"I'm the one with the blood and the name and the standing to make a court listen." He spread his hands. "I don't want a throne, Petra. I want restitution. I want the charter broken open in front of witnesses."

"That's a lovely speech."

"It's a true one."

"They usually rhyme." I turned back to the chest. "But here's my problem with it. Nothing that deep stays buried without reason. Sixty years, that cave stayed shut. The tide never gave it back, not once, not in living memory—and the Vell gives things back all the time. Boats. Bodies. Cargo from wrecks older than Saltmouth. The river returns everything eventually. Except this." I tapped the lid. "Until now. You want to tell me that's accident?"

"What else would it be?"

"Warning." I said it flatly, because flatly was the only way I could say it. "Somebody sank that treasury to end a war. Bought off fourteen families to keep it ended. You start handing out pages of that ledger in a fish market, you're not breaking a charter. You're reopening a grave with everyone's grandparents still in it."

"Then let them climb out," Emeric said. "Let everyone see what they're standing on."

"You say that like you've never watched what happens when a river changes course. It doesn't ask permission. It takes the fields with it."

He looked at me for a long moment. "Is that what happened to your hand?"

The scar runs from the base of my thumb across the heel of it, white and old. I get asked sometimes. I always give the same answer: rope burn, young and stupid. It's even true, in the way that a map is true—it shows you the shape of the country without telling you anything about the weather.

"Rope burn," I said. "Young and stupid."

He didn't push. That was either respect or patience, and I hadn't yet learned which of those frightened me more.

* * *

What I didn't say, what I couldn't find a shape for, was this: the ledger had done something to me that no debt collector ever managed. Quist could take my boat. The Consortium could take my livelihood, my mooring, my name on their books. But a dead steward with a fine hand had reached up out of a box and taken the one thing I'd built my whole life on—the idea that the Coldharbours were simply unlucky. That we'd been poor and proud and drowned by circumstance, and circumstance was nobody's fault.

It had been somebody's fault. It had been our fault. We'd taken the money. Entry nine.

I stood at the rail and did the arithmetic I do instead of grieving, which is the same arithmetic I do instead of hoping, and came up with the same answer I always come up with: keep moving, stay shallow, stay unseen. Except the ledger was a list of names, and my name was on it, and you cannot be unseen inside your own name.

"We should move," I said at last. "Slack water's gone. Current's turning."

"Back into the cave?"

"God, no. Downriver. There's a creek past the second bend with water deep enough to hide in while I think." I was already coiling lines. Thinking, for me, has always been a thing done best in motion, preferably with something between me and the horizon.

Emeric wrapped the ledger in the oilcloth again, then in canvas, then—after watching me watch him—in the waterproof satchel I kept charts in, because he'd noticed, damn him, that I'd noticed the sky. "And the coins?"

"Leave them. They're ballast with delusions."

"They're evidence."

"They're bait," I said. "Everything in that box is bait, and we are currently sitting on the hook."

He frowned at me. "Who's fishing?"

* * *

I saw her launch at half past four, and I will spend the rest of my life being glad that I saw her myself, with my own eyes, before any instrument told me—because instruments only report what's there, and I have always needed to know what's coming.

She was hull-down on the eastern horizon, off the Gullwatch shoals, a white smudge with a wake behind it, running easy. Too big for a fisherman. Too fast for a barge. Too confident for anything on this river except one thing.

Consortium launches don't run that line unless they're looking for something, and there was exactly one something on the water that day worth a launch's fuel.

"Emeric." I said it quietly, and something in my voice must have carried, because he was at the rail beside me before I finished. "See the white smudge, two points off the starboard bow, just clearing the point?"

"Yes."

"That's Dame Sable Quist, or her ghost, and she doesn't believe in ghosts. She's found us."

He was silent a moment. Then, absurdly, he laughed—a short, disbelieving sound. "From Saltmouth? In a day and a half?"

"She didn't follow us. She predicted us. There are maybe four places on this coast a boat can lie comfortable below Gullwatch, and she owns the charts to all of them." I was already at the helm, thumbing the starter, listening to the old engine turn over with the reluctance of a creature that knows weather is coming. "Which means she also owns the charts to everywhere we can run."

"So we surrender the ledger?"

"We run." I brought the *Marrow* head to wind, feeling her answer the way she always answered, faithful and tired, my leaky, stubborn, mortgaged girl. Behind us the chest sat open on the deck, coins glinting dull as old teeth, and ahead of us the eastern sky had gone the color of a bruise deciding whether to bloom. "Question is where. Because she'll cover the deep channel south, and the estuary's a trap at low water, and every harbor mouth between here and Saltmouth has eyes on Consortium payroll."

Emeric came aft, one hand braced on the deckhouse, the ledger satchel strapped tight across his chest like a bandolier. "Then where?"

I looked downriver, toward the gorge country, where the cliffs pinched the Vell down to a throat and the water ran fast and black even on a kind day. Where my father's charts ended and his obsession began. Where the far current was supposed to live, according to a man who died looking for it.

"Not there," I heard myself say. "Anywhere but there."

But even as I said it, my hands were already turning the wheel, and the *Marrow*, who had never once in her long leaky life obeyed anyone quickly, came about toward the gorge country like she'd been waiting sixty years for someone to ask.
