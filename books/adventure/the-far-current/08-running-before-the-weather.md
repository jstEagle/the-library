## 8. Running Before the Weather

I have been thrown out of three countries and one very good hotel in Vellmouth, so I know something about leaving in a hurry. But I had never before left a place with a woman at the wheel of a leaking ketch, a dead king's ledger under my coat, and a Consortium launch coming upriver behind us like a debt collector with a weather system attached.

"Sit down," Petra said, "before I make you sit down."

I sat down. There is a particular tone a riverboat captain uses that makes aristocracy feel like ballast, and Petra Coldharbour had it down to an art. She was wedged against the wheel with one boot hooked on the rail, squinting into the west where the sky had gone the color of wet slate. The Marrow was laboring already—her old planks working in their seams like arthritic knuckles, weeping thin threads of river through every joint that had ever known a hard winter.

"She's talking to you," I offered.

"She's complaining. There's a difference." Petra adjusted the wheel a hand's width. "Complaining is fine. When she starts *arguing*, you'll know."

The first band of the monsoon caught us twenty minutes below Gullwatch Point. It did not arrive so much as fall on us—a wall of rain that turned the world into gray wool and made the cliffs vanish as though someone had wiped them off a slate. The wind came with it, quartering across the current, and the Marrow rolled her shoulders and picked up speed she hadn't been asked for.

Petra's whole body changed. I'd watched her for two days now—the careful economy of her movements, the way she seemed to fold herself smaller around strangers—and I saw all of it burn away in about four seconds. Her hands went to wheel and throttle like they'd grown there. Her chin dropped, her weight settled into her hips, and she began reading the water the way other people read letters: fast, greedy, unembarrassed.

"There's a snag off the left bank," she said, not to me exactly, but near me. "Old barge ribs. You see how the rain hits flat there? Water's lying over something."

"I see rain hitting water."

"You see nothing. That's the trouble with you city people. You think seeing is looking." She eased the bow right. "Watch the surface. Rain lies on top of slack water. Over moving water it gets chewed up. Over a submerged obstruction it goes glassy-smooth for about a boat's length, because the current can't get any faster there, so it stops arguing."

I looked. And after a moment—I'll be honest, mostly because I didn't want to be the kind of man she clearly thought I was—I saw it. A long slick tongue in the chaos, smooth as poured wax.

"There," I said.

"Mm." Which from Petra Coldharbour was roughly a standing ovation.

* * *

The second squall hit harder. The Marrow took a gust broadside and staggered, and something below decks shifted with a sound I did not care for—a deep wooden groan, like an old man asked to run.

"Bilge," Petra snapped. "Pump's aft of the engine. Handle it."

I went aft through rain that hit like thrown gravel and found the pump, a brass-and-iron thing older than either of us, and threw my weight on the handle. Water came up in the sight-glass, brown and determined. I pumped. My shoulders, which have historically been used for holding wine glasses and signing documents, discovered muscles they had only read about.

And here is the thing I have never told anyone who wasn't paid to keep it quiet: I liked it. Not the water in the bilge—the work. The simplicity of it. For eleven years I had been a name in hiding, a rumor with excellent posture, a man whose entire existence consisted of not being found. Every skill I'd kept sharp was a skill of concealment. But this—this was the opposite of concealment. You could not pump a bilge invisibly. Either the water went down or it didn't. The river did not care what name I was using.

The squall passed. The rain thinned to a steady hiss. I kept pumping anyway until Petra shouted, "That'll hold," and I came forward wringing wet, hands stinging, feeling absurdly, embarrassingly alive.

She glanced at me sidelong. "You've done this before."

"No."

"You're lying badly."

"I'm lying adequately. There's a difference." I leaned on the rail beside her, close enough to be heard over the wind without shouting. "My mother's family kept a house on the coast. Before. I spent summers on a fishing smack when I was supposed to be studying heraldry. The skipper thought a boy should earn his berth."

"What happened to the house?"

"The same thing that happens to everything my family touches. It burned." I said it lightly, which is the only way I know how to say it. "The skipper's name was Odo. He taught me three things: how to reef in a blow, how to gut a fish without gagging, and how to tell when a passenger is lying about their sea legs."

"And?"

"You've got none either. You just hide it better than most passengers."

Something happened at the corner of her mouth. On another face it might have been called a smile. On hers it was more like a concession.

* * *

We ran through the afternoon in bands—squall, lull, squall—each one a little harder than the last, the way a fight escalates when neither party will quit. The Marrow took it the way old soldiers take marching: without complaint and with visible cost. Below, the seams were weeping faster. I could hear the bilge gaining between pumps, a soft insistent lap like something patient counting.

At the lull around midafternoon, Petra killed the throttle to half and shook the rain off her face. Her left hand was on the wheel, and I noticed then what I'd noticed at the dock the first night and filed away: the scar. It ran from the base of her thumb across the heel of the palm, old and white, the kind of mark that comes from rope or blade or both.

"That's a workingman's scar," I said. "Or a workingwoman's."

"It's a scar."

"It's a story you're not telling."

"Everything about me is a story I'm not telling. You're the one who bought passage on that principle."

"Fair." I let it go for a full minute, which for me is heroic restraint. Then: "Odo had one like it. Rope burn, he said. He also said he'd gotten it rescuing a duchess, fighting pirates, and once, memorably, 'wrestling the river god,' so I learned early that scars are biographies people edit."

Petra was quiet long enough that I thought she wouldn't answer. The rain ticked on the deck. Somewhere behind us, miles back and closing or not closing, Quist's launch was doing whatever Consortium launches do.

"My father," she said finally. "Hauling line in a gale. It wrapped his fist and mine together when the winch kicked. Took the skin off both of us down to the meat." She flexed the hand on the wheel. "He always said we matched now. Like it was a gift."

"Was it?"

"He died two years later running the gorge at flood." Her voice didn't change at all, which is how I knew it mattered enormously. "So no. It wasn't a gift. It was a receipt."

I understood receipts. Half my life was receipts for things other people had bought with my family's blood—the treasury they drowned, the name they erased, the exile they issued with wax seals and good consciences. I looked at her scarred hand on the wheel and felt, unwelcome and inconvenient, something adjacent to kinship.

"The gorge," I said carefully. "The one north of Saltmouth?"

"Don't."

"I'm not asking you to run it."

"You're asking by existing. Everyone asks eventually." She put the throttle up a notch, ending the conversation the way captains do, with machinery.

* * *

Dusk came early under the storm, a bruised purple dark that fell like a lid. We were making poor time and both knew it. The Marrow was built for stealth and salvage, not for racing a monsoon downriver ahead of a funded pursuit, and every mile cost her something. Twice Petra took us into the lee of the bank to let a squall pass over rather than fight it, and twice I stood at the stern rail watching the gray water behind us for a shape that wasn't a wave.

The third time, I saw it. Or thought I did—a low black suggestion in the murk, too straight to be water, gone before I could swear to it.

"Petra."

"I see you staring aft. If it's a launch, say launch. If it's your imagination, sit down."

"...Possibly a launch."

She came to the rail beside me, and we both stared into the failing light until our eyes watered. Nothing. The river shrugged and rolled and gave back nothing but rain.

"Could've been a log," she said.

"Could've been."

"Could've been Quist."

"Could've been."

She exhaled through her nose. "Then we don't stop tonight. We don't anchor, we don't fuel, we don't sleep at a dock. We run dark and lie up in the reeds above Marsh Crossing, cold food, no lamp, and we're moving again before first light." She looked at me, and in the last gray light her eyes were flat and bright as coins. "Can you handle four hours on that pump?"

"I can handle anything if you tell me why it matters."

"It matters because Quist doesn't chase. She corners. She'll let us run ourselves ragged all night and be waiting somewhere ahead, rested, with tea, at whatever lock or narrows a tired boat has to use. So we don't get tired. We get clever." She turned back to the wheel. "It's the only advantage we've got. She has money. We have me."

There was no false modesty in it and no boast either. It was simply inventory, stated plainly, and I found I believed every word of it.

* * *

We lay up in the reeds as promised, engine cold, lamp dark, the Marrow tucked into a stand of cane so thick it whispered continuously against her hull like a crowd discussing us. I took the first watch at the pump because Petra had been awake thirty hours and would not admit it, and I simply started pumping until she stopped arguing and slept sitting up against the wheel housing, chin down, scarred hand curled loose in her lap.

I pumped and watched the reeds and thought about the ledger under the floorboards beneath my bunk, wrapped in oilcloth, full of names. Names of families paid off, silenced, drowned, disappeared—including, though she didn't yet know it, a line reading *Coldharbour, fleet loyalty purchased, remitted in silence, 61 years past*. I had read it three times since the cave. I had not told her. I told myself I was waiting for the right moment, which is what cowards call waiting.

The rain kept on. The bilge kept its slow patient count and I kept answering it, handle over handle, and somewhere in the fourth hour I realized my arms had stopped hurting and started simply being, and that I was humming—an old smack song of Odo's, tuneless, half-remembered—and that for the first time since I was nine years old and my name became a death warrant, I was not afraid.

Not of Quist. Not even of the storm. Afraid of exactly one thing, which was worse: that I was starting to need this boat, this bitter competent woman, this life where effort translated visibly into survival. Need is a thing that can be taken hostage. I had sworn off it along with my name.

Petra stirred. "You hum when you lie to yourself," she said, without opening her eyes. "Did you know that?"

"I don't hum."

"You're humming."

I stopped. The reeds whispered. Far downstream, faint as a thought, a sound carried up the black water that was not thunder and not wind—a low, regular pulse, mechanical, patient.

An engine. More than one.

Petra opened her eyes.
