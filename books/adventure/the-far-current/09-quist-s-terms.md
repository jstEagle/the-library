## 9. Quist's Terms

The fueling lock at Cinders Reach was the kind of place that existed for one purpose only: to remind you that the river owned you, and so did everyone who'd ever built a wall across it.

Two stone piers, a chain gate, a lock-keeper who hadn't smiled since the last dynasty fell—maybe before—and a queue of barges stacked up like coffins waiting on paperwork. The Marrow slid in behind a grain barge whose captain looked at my hull the way you look at a dog you suspect has fleas. Fair enough. The old girl was weeping at every seam by then, and I'd stopped pretending otherwise. Bilge pump running on a schedule of its own choosing, planks drinking river like a drunk at closing time.

I was watching the gauge when Emeric said, quietly, "We have company."

I didn't need to look. You can feel Dame Sable Quist the way you feel weather coming off open water—a pressure change, a flattening of the air. But I looked anyway, because there are things a captain does with her eyes even when her stomach is already writing its will.

She stood on the pier above us in a grey coat that probably cost more than the Marrow, hands folded, two men behind her wearing the particular blankness of people paid not to be interesting. She didn't wave. She didn't call down. She simply waited, the way a trap waits, and let me come to her.

"Captain Coldharbour," she said when I reached the pier. "You've been difficult to find."

"I've been easy to find," I said. "I've been right where I said I'd be. You just kept being somewhere else."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not a smile. Quist didn't smile; she approved, occasionally, and it looked much the same from the outside and felt considerably worse.

"You took on a passenger."

"Took on a deckhand," I corrected. "There's a difference. One of them works."

"And where is he now?"

"Below," I said truthfully, because he was—sitting on the engine housing with the ledger chest between his knees like a man guarding his own heart. "Bailing. He bails a lot. It's practically his whole personality."

Quist studied me for a long moment. She had the kind of face that made you aware of your own tells—the itch under your collar, the way your weight shifted to the good foot. I'd spent forty years learning to be unreadable, and she made me feel like a first-season deckhand.

"Captain," she said, "let's dispense with the theater. I hold paper on this vessel. Fourteen months past due, plus penalties, plus the salvage lien the Consortium filed against your cargo rights in March. By my arithmetic, I could take the Marrow tomorrow with a word to any harbormaster on the Vell."

"Then take her," I said. "And explain to the next owner why she lists to port and lies about her draft."

That corner of the mouth again. "Here is what I would prefer." She unfolded her hands. Behind her, one of the blank men produced a document case and held it out without being asked, which told me they'd rehearsed this, which told me she'd expected to find me here, which told me she'd known my course before I had. "Full forgiveness. Every page of debt, burned. A clean record in every registry from Saltmouth to the delta. Your name cleared of the Gullwatch salvage claim entirely—you were never there, nothing was taken, no ring exists."

The words landed soft as silt and just as suffocating.

"In exchange," she continued, "you deliver the man below decks to my launch at the delta locks. Unharmed, if it pleases you. Alive is the operative term. What happens after is Consortium business, not yours."

"And if I say no?"

"Then the arithmetic proceeds as written." She said it without heat, without threat, the way you'd read a tide table. That was the worst part. Quist never threatened anyone. She simply informed them of the future and let them live in it until they agreed with her.

I looked out over the lock basin. The grain barge was through the gate now, and the water in the chamber sat flat and green and patient. Somewhere below my feet, a man who claimed to be the last heir of a drowned dynasty was keeping my boat afloat with a bucket and his two hands.

"You'll want time to consider," Quist said, and there it was—that certainty, that terrible patience. "Take the night. The lock opens at dawn either way. Survival, Captain, is an excellent negotiator. By morning you'll have discovered you agree with me."

She turned to go, then paused, half-facing me, and added—almost gently—"Your father ran the gorge, didn't he? I read your file. There's very little in it, but there's that." She let that sit exactly long enough. "People who've lost someone to the water tend, eventually, to make sensible arrangements with it."

Then she walked away down the pier, grey coat moving like fog, and left me standing in the smell of diesel and wet rope with fourteen years of my life priced at one passenger.

* * *

Emeric knew. Of course he knew. He came up the companionway while I was still staring at the lock gate, and he'd heard enough—I could tell by the way he held himself, careful and still, like a man walking on ice he doesn't trust.

"Triple her price," he said. "That was my offer. I'll make it quadruple. Whatever she promised to forgive, I'll pay outright. Cash, coin from the chest, whatever form you—"

"Stop." I rubbed my eyes. "It's not a number, Vale. That's the whole point of her. She doesn't buy things. She buys *people*, and she pays in absolution. You can't outbid forgiveness. Believe me, I've spent forty years trying to earn some cheaply, and the market rate is ruinous."

He leaned against the doorframe. Up close, in the bad light of the lock basin, he looked younger than he claimed and older than he admitted, and tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "What did she offer?"

"Everything," I said. "Debt gone, record clean, the whole ledger of my sins torn up. All I have to do is hand you over at the delta."

He nodded slowly, absorbing it, and I watched him do the same arithmetic I was doing, and I noticed something else too—something I didn't like. His eyes went, just briefly, to the hatch below. To the chest.

"She'll get you anyway," he said. "If I'm not delivered, she takes the boat, and you're back where you started, except poorer and angrier."

"That's the general shape of it."

"So the rational choice—"

"The rational choice is none of your business." I said it harder than I meant to. "You're a deckhand. Deckhands don't get opinions on navigation."

He almost smiled at that. Almost. "Petra." First time he'd used my name. It landed strangely, warm and unwelcome, like sun through a hole in a storm cloud. "Whatever you decide—I won't make it harder for you. If you turn me over, I won't fight you. I know what the Marrow means. I've watched you with her."

"Don't," I said. "Don't do the noble speech. I've got limited tolerance and you're near the cap."

But he wasn't doing a speech. He was just looking at me with those steady eyes, and the trouble was that I believed him, and believing him made everything worse, because it's easy to sell out a liar and considerably harder to sell out a man who means what he says.

"Get below," I said finally. "Check the bilge. And Vale—if you touch that chest tonight for any reason other than bailing, we're going to have a conversation you won't enjoy."

Something flickered across his face—too fast to name, gone before I could price it. "Understood," he said, and went below.

I stood alone in the cockpit and watched the light die over Cinders Reach, and told myself the flicker had been nothing. Told myself I trusted him. Told myself a lot of things, the way you do, lying to yourself in a calm voice because panic is bad for the ship.

* * *

I didn't sleep. Captains don't, the night before a decision. I sat in the cockpit with a cup of tea going cold and the barometer falling in slow, deliberate ticks, each one a small knock on the door of the monsoon, and I thought about my father, and about sensible arrangements with water, and about how Quist had read my file and I hadn't read hers, and how that asymmetry was probably the whole story of my life.

Around midnight the rain started—first fat, scattered drops, the monsoon sending scouts ahead of the army. I went below to check the bilge, mostly as an excuse to check on him.

The cabin lamp was turned low. Emeric was asleep—or appeared asleep—sprawled against the engine housing, one arm flung out, the chest sitting closed beside him. Everything correct. Everything arranged.

Too arranged.

A sleeping man doesn't position the lamp so its shadow falls across the chest lid. A sleeping man doesn't lie with his boots on. And a sleeping man's breathing doesn't change rhythm—just slightly, just for a heartbeat—when the third step creaks under my weight.

I gave him ten full minutes. Then I said, conversationally, into the dark: "The ink's still wet on page eleven."

Silence. The particular quality of silence that follows a sentence like that.

Then he exhaled, long and slow, and sat up, and there was no point pretending anymore. In his lap, half-hidden by the blanket he'd pulled over it, lay three sheets of paper covered in his own careful handwriting—transcriptions, line for line, of the ledger's entries. Names. Sums. Dates. The families the drowned dynasty had bought and broken, copied out in a fair hand fit for a court or a gallows.

"Why?" I asked. It came out quieter than I intended.

"Insurance," he said. "If the chest is lost—"

"If the chest is lost, the copies float just as badly."

"If the chest is *taken*," he corrected, and his voice had an edge now, the polished heir-polish cracking to show something harder underneath. "If Quist boards us, if she seizes the evidence, if everything I am and everything I've claimed burns in one locked box—then the copies survive. In my coat. On my body. Petra, I'm not betraying you. I'm making sure that when you finally choose, you're choosing whether to save *me*, not whether to save the whole cause."

"And you couldn't have asked?"

"Would you have said yes?"

I opened my mouth. Closed it. Because the honest answer was no, and we both knew it, and he'd known it when he started copying by lamplight while pretending to sleep, and I'd known it when I crept down the steps pretending not to watch him.

"There it is," I said. "That's the trouble with trust, Vale. It's not that people lie to you. It's that they're all keeping their own books, and nobody shows you the entries."

He set the pages down carefully, squared their edges, and looked up at me. "And you? What entries are you keeping, Captain? Have you decided yet what I'm worth?"

Outside, the rain found its rhythm—steady now, committed, the monsoon arriving in earnest against the deck overhead. The bilge pump kicked on with its wet asthmatic cough. And I stood there in the low lamplight with a falling barometer, a holed hull, a debt worth one heir, and a man asking me to price him, and I realized I'd been stalling since the pier—not because I hadn't decided, but because I had, and I hadn't yet found the courage to say it out loud, even to myself.

"Dawn's in six hours," I said. "The lock opens at first light. Get some sleep."

"And your answer?"

I picked up his copied pages, read the top sheet by the lamp—names I half-knew, sums that explained half the river's oldest fortunes—and set them back in his lap.

"My answer," I said, "is that Quist thinks survival will negotiate for her. Let's see how she likes negotiating with something else."

His eyes narrowed. "Which is?"

I climbed the steps to the cockpit, where the rain was coming down hard and honest, and looked north toward the black water and the gorge beyond it, and said the thing I'd been circling for two days:

"Hold on to your hat, Vale. We're going to need a bigger current."
