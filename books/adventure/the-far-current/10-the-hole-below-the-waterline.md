## 10. The Hole Below the Waterline

**Petra**

The snag never showed itself. That's the insult of it. Forty years on the Vell and I can read a sandbar's mood from the color of the water over it, can tell you where a channel shifted three days ago by the way the gulls sit, but a drowned tree, waterlogged and rolling slow ten feet down in tea-dark flood, gives you nothing. It just waits, patient as a debt, until your keel finds it at four knots with the monsoon's first proper band chewing the river into gray wool.

The sound was wrong before I understood it. Not the crack of planking—that I'd know in my sleep, and have—but a long grinding shudder up through the deck plates, like the Marrow had swallowed something and regretted it.

"What was that?" Emeric called from under the dodger, where he'd been fighting the chart case shut against the rain.

"That," I said, "was the river introducing itself."

I brought her head up into the current and listened. Any captain will tell you the same: eyes are for charts, ears are for hulls. And the Marrow was talking. A new note under the engine drone, a wet whisper that hadn't been there an hour ago. Water coming aboard where water had no invitation.

I got the floorboards up amidships and looked into the bilge and felt my stomach do the arithmetic before my head agreed to it. Six inches. Rising. And when I swung the lamp low, I saw it—a seam aft of the keelson, opened like a split lip, weeping brown river in a thin steady line, and beside it two more seams flexing with every roll, the old caulking giving up after decades of being asked to hold opinions above its station.

"How bad?" Emeric asked. He'd come to stand behind me without my hearing him, which should have annoyed me more than it did.

"She's holed. Not fatally. Yet." I said it flat because flat is what keeps panic off a boat. "But she won't make open water in this, and she won't lie to a mooring overnight without me pumping every hour and praying between."

"So we—"

"We beach her. Deliberately, before the river decides for us." I pointed upstream-left, where the banks went soft and green and tangled—the mangrove country, all creeks and fingers and no witnesses. "There. If I take her in bow-first at half throttle, the mud'll hold her upright and we can patch her at leisure instead of drowning at speed."

He looked at the mangroves, then at the sky, which had gone the color of wet slate, then at me. "You've done this before."

"I've done everything before. That's the trouble with getting old on a river—you collect the whole catalog." I put the wheel over. "Bilge pump. Now. And if she starts shipping faster than you can sling it, say the word *now*, not politely."

I ran her in on the flood tide with the rain hammering sideways and the creek mouth showing itself only as a darker seam in the darker bank. Half throttle, then quarter, then the Marrow's bow slid into mud with a soft decisive *whump* that I felt in my back teeth, and she settled, listing gently to starboard, held like a toy boat set down by a careful child.

I killed the engine. The silence afterward was enormous. Just rain on the canopy, wind in the mangroves, and the tick of cooling metal.

"Well," Emeric said. "She's afloat in the sense that she's surrounded by water."

"Don't," I said, and he had the grace to look apologetic, or possibly just wet.

* * *

We worked through what remained of the light. He pumped; I crawled into the bilge with a lamp clamped in my teeth and packed the worst seam with oakum and tallow, working blind by feel, arms overhead, rain drumming the deck above like a landlord who wanted rent. The monsoon proper arrived around dusk—not the squalls we'd been dodging for two days but the real thing, a wall of it, rain so dense the creek disappeared and the world contracted to the length of the boat.

By full dark the pump had beaten the leak. Barely. The bilge sat at two inches and stayed there, which on the Marrow counted as victory.

We ate cold rice and smoked fish in the cabin, sitting on the floor because everything else was gear, and the lamp swung between us with the river's residual push against the hull. Emeric looked, for the first time since the dock at Saltmouth, like what he probably was underneath the city clothes and the careful voice: tired. Younger than he pretended. A man a long way from whatever he'd started from.

"You should sleep first," he said. "You've been in the bilge six hours."

"And you've been pumping them. We'll split the night. Two hours on, two hours off." I poured the last of the tea, cold, into two cups anyway, because ritual matters when everything else has gone to mud. "Pump till the bilge stops arguing with you, then listen to her. She'll tell you if she's lying."

"Do you talk to the boat?"

"Everyone talks to their boat. Boats are the only ones who don't pretend not to hear."

Something moved across his face—not quite a smile, but the place where one would go if he ever let one out. "My grandfather talked to his horses. Said they were better company than ministers."

"Was he right?"

"He was a king in exile who'd lost a war to men with ledgers. Everything was better company than ministers." He said it lightly, the way people say heavy things when they've practiced. Then, quieter: "I was seven when we left. I don't remember the palace. I remember a night crossing, and my mother's hand over my mouth because I was crying, and lights on the water behind us that she told me were fishing boats. They weren't fishing boats."

The lamp swung. Rain filled the pause for him, which was kind of it.

"They burned what they couldn't carry," he went on. "The estate, the records, the portraits. My father said it was so no one could prove anything about us ever again. He thought that was mercy. Erasure." His thumb moved along the rim of his cup, around and around. "I grew up in rented rooms in cities whose names I changed twice a year. I learned four languages and none of them well enough to dream in. And every year on the anniversary of the crossing, my mother would set one extra cup at the table and not explain it, and I understood eventually that she wasn't mourning a throne. She was mourning a kitchen. A specific kitchen. With a blue door."

I didn't say anything. There's a skill in salvage—knowing when a thing needs lifting and when it needs leaving where it lies—and grief is cargo of that second kind.

"The ring you took," he said. "It was hers. Her mother's. It came out of that cave the night they crossed, when the loyalists sank the treasury rather than let the Consortium have it. My mother wore it forty years and sold it the winter she was dying, to pay for—" He stopped. Started again. "To pay for a doctor who was useless. So. When I saw it in the pawnbroker's window, Captain Coldharbour, I wasn't chasing treasure. I was chasing a door. Blue, if you're wondering."

I looked at him a long moment. "Petra," I said. "If we're trading stories below the waterline, it's Petra."

* * *

**Emeric*

She gave me her name the way other people give blood—one unit, carefully measured, no more than strictly necessary. I found I was absurdly pleased by it.

Her turn came near midnight, during my watch, though I'm not sure she intended it as a trade. She climbed out of the bilge hatch where she'd gone to check my packing job—found it adequate, I could tell, by the fact that she didn't redo it—and stayed crouched there a while with the lamp off, listening to the rain.

"My father ran the gorge," she said, to the bilge, or to me, hard to say. "The Karst Gorge, upriver. Before the flood gates, before anyone charted the deep channel. Everyone said it couldn't be run at any water. He said there was a far current—a deep lane through the middle that the floods fed clean, if you could find it. He spent eleven years looking for it. Charts drawn in pencil, hidden under the cabin sole. My mother burned them after, so I only know the pieces I saw as a child."

"What happened?"

"The usual. He almost had it. Third season, high water, he took the Marrow in—I was nine, I was supposed to be ashore with my aunt, I stowed away because I was nine." Her voice did something complicated, flattened and roughened at once. "He made the entrance pools. Made the narrows. I remember thinking, *he's done it, he's actually done it.* And then the rockfall shelf, the one that isn't on any chart because no chart survives that gorge. He went left. The current wanted right." She was quiet a moment. "They found the boat two days later, twelve miles down. They found him a week after that. I swam. Nine years old, and the river spat me out like it couldn't be bothered."

"Emeric—" I started, and didn't know how to finish it.

"Here's the part I don't tell people." She finally turned the lamp on, and its light made her face look carved. "I've spent thirty years telling myself I stay off the big water because I'm sensible. Because small boat, small risks, keep your head down, live long. But that's not true, is it. I stay off it because the last time I was on it, it kept him and not me, and I have never once believed I deserved the arithmetic." She stood, joints cracking, and looked at me with something close to defiance. "So now you know. The great invisible Petra Coldharbour, hiding from a river."

"It sounds less like hiding from the river," I said, "than from the version of yourself that survived it."

For a heartbeat I thought she'd throw me out of my own watch. Then she huffed—almost a laugh, filed down to its smallest legal size. "City boy reads books. Fine. Sleep. Pump in two hours. And if you wake me saying the bilge is 'a little damp,' I will put you ashore in the mangroves and let the crabs negotiate with you."

* * *

**Petra**

Dawn came gray and featureless, the rain settled in for the long haul, and the Marrow sat in her mud berth listing like a drunk leaning on a fence. But the bilge was dry-ish, the patch was holding, and we were alive, which put us ahead of several people I could name.

We spent the day working. There's a mercy in work I've always trusted: it asks nothing of you but your hands. I re-caulked the secondary seams from inside while Emeric bailed, pumped, and—annoyingly—proved competent at it, learning the rhythm fast, learning to read the pump's note the way I'd told him. By afternoon he could tell me the bilge depth by sound alone. I hated how pleased that made me.

Toward evening the wind backed and freshened, and the mangroves began their long dry rattle, and I stood in the cockpit looking at the sky and did sums I didn't like. This band would blow through. The ones behind it wouldn't. And Quist's launch was somewhere downriver with a fueling lock, a radio, and patience, all three of which we lacked.

"We can't stay," Emeric said, appearing beside me. He'd started doing that—reading weather off my face instead of the sky. "How long can we hide here?"

"A creek this size, maybe two days before someone walks the bank. Less if they've a skiff." I watched a heron stalk the shallows like it had money owed to it too. "And the patch is a promise, not a repair. She'll want real work—careening, new caulking, a yard. None of which happens while a Consortium launch is combing the river for us."

"So we need distance."

"We need *invisibility*," I said, and heard the word come out of my mouth like an old coat, worn smooth from handling. "Except—" I stopped. Looked at the boat that had failed to be invisible all season. Looked at the heir standing on her deck because I'd chosen him over a clean ledger.

"Except?" he prompted.

"Except I've been buying invisibility my whole life and it has never once held," I said. "Maybe it's time I bought something else."

He didn't ask what. He just nodded slowly, and then his gaze went past me, downriver, and sharpened. "Petra. What's the flood window on this river?"

"Peak flood, another day, maybe two. Why?"

"Because I've been reading your father's pencil marks." He held up the oilskin packet we'd salvaged from under the cabin sole—charts I hadn't known were still aboard, drawn in a dead man's hand, lines running straight through the Karst Gorge. "And because I think I understand why you flinched at the name."

I took the packet from him. The paper was soft as cloth. My father's handwriting marched down the margins—soundings, times, a single repeated note in the corner of the last sheet, pressed so hard the pencil had nearly torn through:

*THE FAR CURRENT RUNS TRUE.*

The rain came harder. Somewhere downriver, faint under the wind, I could have sworn I heard an engine, though I knew better than to trust my ears in a blow.

Two days of peak flood. One failing patch. One gorge nobody survives.

"One condition," I said.

"Name it."

"When we run it, nobody goes left."
