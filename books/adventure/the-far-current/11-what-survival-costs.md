## 11. What Survival Costs

The scrap dealer's name was Ovid Brann, and he lived the way a hermit crab lives—inside something dead, wearing it as a home. His "yard" was a beached lighter, half-sunk in the creek mud, its hold stacked with brass fittings, propeller blades gone to lace, and the bones of engines that had outlived their boats. He came out of it wiping his hands on a rag that had once, by the smell of it, been an oilskin.

He looked at our coins the way a man looks at his daughter's suitor.

"Where'd you get these?" he asked.

"Off a dead man," I said.

"Which dead man?"

"One who won't be asking for them back."

Brann grunted. He turned a coin over in his palm, held it to the light that leaked through the lighter's rotted hatch cover, and bit it—not because anyone bites gold anymore, but because men like Brann do everything the old way when they want you to know they're thinking. He set it down on his workbench with a click that sounded final as a coffin nail.

"Coral-fused," he said. "You'll take melt value and like it."

"I'll take fair value," I said, "and you'll like giving it, because we both know what these are worth even melted, and we both know you've been waiting twenty years for someone desperate enough to bring you treasure at scrap prices. Today's your lucky day. Don't ruin it by being greedy about it."

That's the trick of haggling when you're desperate: never let them see the desperation. I'd learned it young. My father taught me cards before he taught me knots, and knots before he taught me anything about love, which was never, but the cards and the knots have kept me alive longer than love would have.

Brann counted out notes from a tin box bolted under his bench—the kind of box that says *I expect robbers*—and I counted them twice, and Emeric stood behind me saying nothing, which was the correct amount of things for him to say. The coins went into Brann's scale. The notes went into my oilskin. The treasure of a drowned dynasty became, in the space of ten minutes, boat parts: a sheet of copper, a bucket of bronze fastenings, two gallons of epoxy that Brann swore was fresh and I swore to test, and a hand pump that had last pumped something I chose not to think about.

* * *

Here is the arithmetic nobody teaches you about salvage: the treasure is only worth what it costs to carry. The Marrow was holed below the waterline, patched with canvas and prayer, and every hour she sat in that creek, the bilge ran and the monsoon sat on us like a debt collector. To save the treasure, we spent the treasure. By the time we rowed back up the creek to where she lay moored between the mangroves, I had exactly enough left to buy the copper, the fastenings, the glue, and nothing else. Not food beyond hardtack. Not fuel beyond what sat in her tanks. Not one coin toward the Consortium debt, which grew in the dark like mold.

Emeric carried the copper sheet on his shoulder, and I noticed—he was getting better at carrying things without complaint. Three days ago he'd have made a speech about it. Now he just walked, mud sucking at his city boots until I told him to take them off and go barefoot like a person.

"You could have kept three coins back," he said, as we came in sight of the Marrow's mast above the green tangle. "Hidden them. Brann would never have known."

"And then what? Buy bread with dynasty gold while Quist's launch sits at the creek mouth? Every coin that surfaces is a flare. You don't light flares you don't need."

"You needed some."

"I needed a boat." I said it harder than I meant to. The words came out with history behind them. "The boat is the only thing I've ever owned outright. Everything else—the name on my papers, the berth at Saltmouth, the air I breathe on this river—it's all mortgaged to somebody. The Marrow isn't. Wasn't. And now she's leaking through a hole the size of my fist, and I've just paid a king's ransom in scrap to keep her floating, and do you know what the funny part is?"

"What's the funny part?"

"There isn't one. That's the funny part."

* * *

We worked through the afternoon and into the evening, by lamplight swinging in the cabin because the rain had started again—that thin, patient monsoon rain that doesn't fall so much as settle, like it's moving in. I ground the hull seam clean while Emeric mixed epoxy, and I'll grant him this: he followed instructions exactly, which is more than most deckhands manage, and more than I expected from a man whose hands had clearly never done an honest day's work before this month. They were learning, though. There were blisters on them now, and blisters are a kind of education.

While the patch cured, we sat in the cabin with the lamp between us, and I did sums in the logbook's back pages, because writing numbers down makes them realer and therefore more bearable, or sometimes less bearable, which is also useful information.

"What are you counting?" Emeric asked.

"What everything costs."

"And what does everything cost?"

I turned the page so he could see it. Debt to the Consortium, principal and interest, compounded through eleven years of bad seasons and worse luck. Repairs, ongoing. Fuel. Food. The pawned ring, bought back at a loss I hadn't yet confessed to him. Against it: the notes from Brann, already spoken for. A column of zeros where savings should be.

"That's not everything," he said quietly. "That's just money."

"It's the kind of everything I've got."

He was quiet a moment, turning his cup in his hands. Then he said, "My mother used to say poverty is a room with no doors. She said the rich build the rooms and then sell you the walls."

"Your mother sounds like she'd have gotten along with mine. Mine said the river doesn't care if you drown, so don't give it reasons."

"They're the same advice."

"They usually are."

And there it was again, that thing that kept happening between us—the way our separate griefs kept turning out to be the same grief wearing different clothes. It would have been easier if he'd stayed a fool. Fools you can dismiss. But he bailed bilge without being asked, and he'd taken a beating protecting a ledger that wasn't his to protect except by blood, and when I caught myself almost telling him things—real things, the scar, my father—I reminded myself that Quist had probably offered him the same deal she'd offered me, and that need makes liars of better people than either of us.

Except. Except that he'd had the ledger in his hands for three days now, and the chest, and every chance to slip away down the creek at night and trade it for whatever Quist was paying heirs. And he was still here, barefoot in the mud, mixing epoxy by lamplight.

I didn't know what to do with a man like that. I'd built my whole life around knowing what to do with every other kind.

* * *

It was near midnight when he said it. We were on deck because the cabin smelled of epoxy fumes and the rain had thinned to mist, and the creek lay black and still around us, the mangroves holding the water the way old women hold gossip.

"The gorge," Emeric said.

"Don't finish that sentence."

"The flooded gorge. Monsoon peak. Two days of high water, maybe three, before it starts to drop."

"I heard you the first time. I'm choosing not to have heard it."

"Quist has the creek mouth watched. She has the lock watched. She has every harbor master from here to Saltmouth paid or frightened. Petra—" and he used my name, which he'd started doing only recently, and which I hated how much I didn't hate, "—she doesn't need to catch us. She just needs to wait. We can't outrun her on the open river. We can't hide forever; the Marrow drinks too much to lie low in the backwaters past a week. There's exactly one way off this stretch of water that she can't follow."

"The gorge killed my father."

"I know."

"You don't know. You know the shape of the story. You don't know that I was nine years old and standing on the bank at Vell's Neck watching for his mast to come through, and it never came through, and they found the boat three days later in the lower pools, and they never found him at all." The mist had gotten into my voice somehow. I cleared it. "So when I say don't finish that sentence, I mean it in the way that people mean things."

He didn't say anything for a long time. Below us, the bilge pump clicked and knocked, doing its slow work, keeping us alive hour by hour the way it had been since the snag opened us up—a sound like a tired heart.

Then he said, "I'm not asking you to do it for me."

"No. You're asking me to do it for the ledger. For the families in it. For whatever grand dissolution you're planning." I laughed, and it came out ugly. "You should hear yourself. You talk like a man buying something expensive with other people's lives."

"Yes," he said simply. "I do. Because that's what it takes, and I'd rather say it plainly than dress it up. I've spent my whole life being dressed up, Petra. It's the one thing I refuse to do anymore." He turned to face me fully, and in the dark his eyes were just shadows, but his voice wasn't. "But understand this. If you run the gorge, I'm not asking you to run it for the ledger, or for me, or for the dead. I'm asking you to run it because Quist will never stop. Not for the debt—you could pay her ten times over and she'd find a reason. She wants the ledger burned and me buried, and you're the boat that carries both. The day she gets what she wants, you're a loose end. You've seen what she does with loose ends."

I had. The fisherman at the lock, the one who'd talked to us—his skiff had been found empty, and nobody downstream would meet my eye anymore. That's how Quist worked. Not with knives, mostly. With weather. She made you feel like the whole river was against you, until hiding felt like the only honest option, and hiding was exactly what she wanted, because hidden people can't testify.

And there it was. That was the thought I'd been circling for days without letting myself land on it: *hidden people can't testify.* I had spent thirty years making myself invisible—running dark, no lights, no name given, no port called home too loudly, no debts acknowledged, no face remembered. I'd told myself it was freedom. Sitting in that creek with a holed boat and a drained purse, I finally did the sums I'd been avoiding my whole life.

Invisibility had a price too. I'd been paying it since I was nine years old, standing on the bank at Vell's Neck, learning that the world takes what it takes and remembers nothing. I'd paid it in loneliness disguised as independence, in silence disguised as prudence, in every dock I slipped away from before dawn so no one would ask my business. Thirty years of premiums, paid punctually, and what had the policy ever covered? Not my father. Not the Marrow. Not one night's sleep. The invisibility had never once held. All it had ever done was make sure that when the taking happened, there'd be no witnesses—and no one to help dig me out after.

Quist was counting on it. She'd read me right, probably. She'd looked at me and seen a woman who'd rather sink quietly than make a splash, and she'd priced her offer accordingly, and somewhere in a dry office she was sleeping well because of it.

Well.

"Two days of peak flood," I said.

"Maybe three."

"The channel through the gorge shifts every season. The charts are forty years old and wrong. Half the rockfall from the slide is still in there somewhere, moving around under the flood like dice in a cup." I was talking fast now, and I knew what that meant; it meant the decision had already been made somewhere below my voice, in the older, deeper part of me that reads water. "There's a current through the middle of it. My father used to say there was always a far current—the deep channel that runs true when everything on top is lying. He died looking for it. Everyone said he was mad to try, and everyone said the current didn't exist, and I've spent thirty years believing them because believing them was safer than hoping."

"And now?"

"Now I think he found it." I looked down at the black water, at our own faint reflection broken by rain. "I think he found it and the flood took him anyway, or I think he never found it and died mad—but either way, it's the only map I've got that Quist doesn't. She can buy every harbor master on the Vell. She can't buy my father's ghost."

Emeric was silent. Then, carefully, like a man stepping onto ice: "You don't have to decide tonight."

"I decided tonight. That's the problem with deciding tonight. Tomorrow I'll start finding reasons, and by the day after I'll have talked myself into surrendering the ledger and calling it wisdom." I stood up. My knees cracked; the damp gets into everything. "So we do it tomorrow. Or the day after, when the gauge at the Neck reads peak. We run the gorge at monsoon flood, in a holed boat, with a patch held on by epoxy and spite."

"It'll come apart," he said. Not arguing. Just stating, the way you'd state the tide table.

"Probably."

"We could die."

"Definitely, possibly. The odds are terrible either way. That's how you know it's the right choice—when every option costs everything, you might as well pick the one that's yours."

He looked at me for a long moment across the lamplit deck, and whatever he saw in my face seemed to settle something in his, because he nodded once, slow, like a man accepting terms.

"Then I'd better learn that pump properly," he said. "If I'm going to die, I'd rather it be at a task I've mastered."

"That," I told him, "is the first sensible thing you've said since you boarded."

Below us the bilge pump knocked on, faithful as a heartbeat, and upstream, far off toward the Neck, thunder rolled along the cliffs like the river clearing its throat to speak. Two days. Maybe three. Somewhere out in the dark, the flood was already gathering, and for the first time in thirty years, I wasn't going to run from it.

I was going to run *with* it.
