## 12. The Creek at High Water

I had been on the Marrow eleven days when I learned that the bilge pump has a voice, and that if you listen long enough it starts to sound like a person. Ours sounded like a tired aunt complaining about her knees. *Thump, wheeze, thump.* I'd named her Berthe by day three, and by day five I was apologizing to her when I forgot to oil her.

"You talk to that machine more than you talk to me," Petra said, not looking up from the chart she'd spread across the cabin table.

"Berthe listens better."

"She's also dying."

"So are we all," I said, "but some of us with more dignity."

Petra gave me the look she reserved for my city education — a kind of patient contempt, like watching a cat fall off a windowsill — and went back to the chart. We were anchored deep in a mangrove creek that didn't appear on any chart at all, which was rather the point of it. She'd found it the way she found everything: by reading water the way other people read letters. The creek was a green throat between roots, barely wide enough for the Marrow's beam, and the mangroves closed over us like a hand folding shut.

The monsoon proper had arrived two days earlier, and it did not arrive so much as move in, unpack, and begin rearranging the furniture. Rain came in sheets that turned the world into a gray smear. The air was wet enough to drink. Everything I owned — which, after the events of the last month, amounted to one satchel and a ledger wrapped in oilcloth — had gone soft at the edges.

And under all of it, the patch. Petra had driven oakum and pitch into the seams where the snag had opened her hull, and for a while it held, but the sea is a patient creditor and it collects in the end. Every hour the pump ran a little longer. Every hour Berthe complained a little louder.

"We have two days of flood left," Petra said that evening, tapping the gorge on the chart. "Peak water. After that the window closes until next season, and we sit in this creek growing moss while Quist decides how she wants us delivered."

"And if the window doesn't close? If we run it?"

She looked up then, and there was something in her face I'd learned to recognize — the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she hated. "Then we find out what my father found out."

She said nothing else, and I asked nothing else, because that was our arrangement. Half-truths traded at fair market value.

* * *

They came three nights later, and I will always believe it was the smoke that betrayed us.

You cannot run an engine cold in monsoon damp without drying things, and you cannot dry things without fire, and fire in a mangrove creek at night glows like a confession. Petra knew this. She rationed our cooking fires down to embers screened behind a sheet of tin, but even embers breathe light, and someone had been breathing along the coast looking for exactly that.

I heard the dogs first. Or what I took for dogs — a low, carrying baying that didn't belong to any animal I knew from the city, and then I understood it was men, calling to each other across the water in voices pitched to carry and not to be understood. Four notes, repeated. A language of their own.

Petra was awake before I finished sitting up. That was the thing about her; she slept like a knife in a drawer, all edge and readiness. She put one finger to her lips, and in the dark I saw her already dressed, already moving, killing the lamp with a squeeze of her fist as though she were strangling a small animal.

"Engine?" I breathed.

"Not yet. They'll hear it half a mile off." She pressed the ledger into my chest — the oilcloth bundle, heavier than its paper, heavy with every name the drowned dynasty had ever bought or broken, including mine, including hers. "You carry this. You don't fight. You swim if I say swim."

"I can fight."

"You can flail. There's a difference, and the difference is usually blood." She was already out the companionway, and I followed her into the rain.

The creek at night was a black corridor. Rain fell straight down with a hiss like sand pouring, and the mangrove roots stood around us in the shallows like the ribs of some drowned cathedral. I could see nothing. Petra could apparently see everything, because she caught my sleeve and pulled me flat against the hull just as a lantern bloomed through the leaves maybe thirty yards downstream.

Three boats. I counted them by their lights — a stern lamp, a bow lamp, a third swinging wild as its bearer climbed through the roots. Quist's men moved like professionals, which frightened me more than incompetence would have. Professionals don't shout. They fan out. They send one man ahead quiet, and the quiet man is the one who kills you.

The quiet man nearly got us. He came up the near bank through the mangroves with his lantern hooded, and he was ten feet away when Petra stepped out of the dark and put a boathook across his throat and walked him backward into the water with a courtesy I can only describe as maternal. He went under without a cry. When he came up, gasping, she had his own knife resting against his collarbone.

"Tell Dame Sable," she said pleasantly, "that the Coldharbour toll has gone up."

Then everything happened at once, because of course it did. A shout from the far boat. Lanterns converging. A whistle splitting the rain. And Petra, instead of running for the Marrow, ran *away* from it — out onto the mudflat, drawing them after her, giving me the creek and the boat and the ledger and the whole desperate arithmetic of her plan in a single gesture.

I should have stayed hidden. Any sensible man would have stayed hidden. But I watched three of them chase her into the open, and I watched her go down under a tackle that no single woman should have been able to survive, and I discovered something about myself that night that I have never entirely forgiven: I am not a sensible man.

I hit the first one from behind with a belaying pin. This is a sentence I had never expected to write, even in fiction, and I want it noted that I committed it with genuine feeling. He dropped. The second one turned, and I learned that professionals train for exactly this, because he didn't swing at me — he simply stepped inside my reach and put his shoulder into my chest, and the world became mud and rain and a knee finding my stomach twice, three times, with the patience of a metronome.

The ledger was under me. I understood that much through the pain — I had fallen on it, curled around it the way you curl around anything that matters — and so when they rolled me and struck me and rolled me again, I only had one job, which was to stay between the oilcloth and the world.

Someone kicked my head against a root. The night went white, then red, then briefly very interested in detail — I remember raindrops standing on a leaf above me, each one lit gold by a lantern, absurdly beautiful — and then hands were dragging the bundle out from under my arm and I made a sound I didn't know I owned, animal and furious, and grabbed the nearest ankle with both arms and held on.

Holding on is underrated. Everyone praises courage and cunning, but holding on requires neither intelligence nor bravery, only a refusal to be pried loose, and I had a lifetime of stubbornness banked and nowhere to spend it. They dragged me six feet through the mud. They kicked my fingers. One of them stamped on my wrist and I felt something in it go, a small bright collapse, and I still held on, screaming now, mostly in rage.

And then the second man went down, and the third turned toward the new sound, and Petra came out of the dark with a gaff hook and the expression of a woman settling accounts.

What happened next took perhaps four seconds and I missed most of it, being occupied with the mud. When I could see again, two of Quist's men were retreating to their boats at speed and the third was face-down and groaning, and Petra stood over him breathing hard, a long red line opened along her forearm where a blade had found her — a gash, ugly but shallow, the kind of wound that looks worse than it is and then doesn't heal right for a year.

"The ledger," she said.

"Here." I tried to lift it and discovered my left arm had resigned from service. "Mostly here. It's — I'm attached to it somewhat."

She crouched, tore the oilcloth back with her teeth, checked the pages inside with a quickness that told me everything about how much it mattered to her now, and then looked at me — at my swelling eye, my ruined wrist, the mud and blood of me — with an expression I couldn't read at all.

"You absolute idiot," she said, and it was the softest thing anyone had ever said to me.

* * *

We cast off within minutes, engine cold, drifting down the creek on the ebb with poles and paddles and the rain covering our noise. Petra bound her forearm by lamplight below decks — my lamplight, one-handed, because my left hand had become a decorative object — and I watched her tie the knot with her teeth and thought, with the strange clarity of the concussed, that I had known kings' counselors who could not manage adversity half so well as this woman managed a bandage.

"How bad?" she asked, nodding at my arm.

"I believe the technical term is 'cracked.'"

"Can you work a pump handle?"

"With the right one, yes."

"Then congratulations. You've been promoted to Berthe's assistant."

Berthe, that night, earned her keep. The patch was failing — I didn't need Petra's ear to know it; I could hear the sea coming aboard in a thin insistent whisper beneath the pump's complaint, water finding every seam we'd plugged, patient as a lawyer. We pumped in shifts through the small hours, me one-armed and half-blind, Petra with her forearm stitched by her own needle and thread, neither of us saying what both of us were calculating: how many hours of pumping remained before the arithmetic failed.

Dawn came gray and without distinction, the rain easing at last into a steady miserable drizzle. We reached the mouth of the creek and lay there in the shadow of the last mangroves, looking out at the open river, and that was where Petra finally said it.

"Gorge runs at peak flood. Two days of real water left — today and tomorrow. After that it drops and we're done." She flexed her bandaged hand, testing it, wincing. "Quist knows where we are now. She'll have boats at every landing between here and the gorge mouth. We can't run the river past them in this hull — we're taking water faster than Berthe can complain about it."

"So we run the gorge."

"So we run the gorge." She said it the way you'd say *so we file the paperwork*, and I wanted to shake her, and I wanted to laugh, and underneath both I was afraid in a way I hadn't let myself be since the cave. "There's a channel through it. My father mapped it with his life. I was nine years old when he drew it for me — not on paper. In my head."

"In your head."

"Charts can be seized," she said. "Memories have to be killed."

I looked at her — at the stitched arm, the salt-stiff hair, the eyes that had watched her father die in the water she now proposed to enter at its angriest — and I understood that I had misread her from the beginning. I had thought she was hiding from the Consortium, from debt, from Quist. She wasn't. She was hiding from this. From the current that had taken everything, waiting all her life for the moment she'd either run it or admit she never would.

"My wrist will heal wrong," I offered, "and I'll write my memoirs about you. The chapter titles alone will be devastating."

"Make me tall in it," she said, and pushed off from the mangroves, and turned the Marrow's broken bow toward the gorge.

Behind us, far down the creek, a lantern swung through the trees. They had found our anchorage empty, and they would be fast, and they would be angry, and the flood would peak tomorrow at dawn and not one hour after.

Two days. One channel drawn in fear. And a boat coming apart beneath us the whole way there.
