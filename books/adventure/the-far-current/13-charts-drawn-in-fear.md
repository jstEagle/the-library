## 13. Charts Drawn in Fear

I drew the channel on the underside of the Marrow's chart table with a carpenter's pencil worn down to a stub, because the paper charts had gone overboard in the skirmish and because some things you don't keep on paper anyway. Paper can be found. Paper can be read by people like Quist. A memory drawn in grease and graphite on a boat that might not survive the week—that was about as secure as anything got.

Emeric sat across from me with his ribs strapped and his eye still swelling shut from where one of Quist's men had introduced himself to it, and he watched my hand move without saying anything for a long time. He'd learned when to be quiet. It was one of the few things I liked about him.

"That's not a river," he said finally. "That's a rumor of a river."

"It's the gorge," I said. "It always looks like that."

I'll give you what I gave him. The Vell narrows at Grayfall to less than a quarter mile between walls of black stone, and for six miles it drops through a staircase of rapids that every hydrographer who ever surveyed it marked with the same notation: *impassable at flood*. The official charts show a portage road on the east bank, built eighty years ago, washed out forty years ago, never rebuilt. What the charts don't show—because no surveyor lived long enough to confirm it—is the far current.

My father used to talk about it the way other men talked about God. Not often. Only late, only after rum, only in the tone of a man confessing something he wasn't sure he believed anymore. The gorge has two currents, he said. The surface current, the loud one, the one that eats boats—that's the one everyone sees. But underneath it, hugging the north wall where the rock shears away into deep water, there's a second flow. Slower. Deeper. The flood funnels down through a drowned slot in the riverbed and comes back up along the wall, and if you could find it—if you could hold it—you'd ride through the worst water on the Vell like a cork through a bottle neck, while everything else got chewed.

He went looking for it the year I was nine. He took the Marrow—she was younger then, sounder—and he took two deckhands whose names nobody says in front of me, and he ran the gorge at three-quarter flood because he thought peak would be suicide but three-quarter would give him enough water over the slot to find it.

The slot is deeper than that. Or shallower. I don't know. Nobody knows. They found the Marrow's mast downstream, wedged in the rocks below the last fall, and they found one deckhand, and they did not find my father, and I have spent thirty-one years knowing exactly as much as that.

* * *

"You were there," Emeric said. "When he planned it."

"I was there when he drew it." I shaded in the standing waves with the side of the pencil stub. "Same table. Same pencil. He let me hold the lamp." I blew graphite dust off the black stone of the north wall. "He said the trick isn't courage. Courage just gets you into the gorge. The trick is knowing where the water wants to go and agreeing with it faster than it changes its mind."

"And do you?"

"Know where it wants to go?" I tapped the drawing. "I know what he knew, which is what his father knew, which is what some Coldharbour or other has known since before the dynasty drowned. The entry line off the split rock. The eddy behind the third pillar where you catch your breath. And then—" I drew a line hugging the north wall, thin and confident, a lie in graphite form—"the far current. Which I have never seen. Which may not exist. Which killed him."

Emeric was quiet again. Then: "You're drawing your father's map from a child's memory of watching him draw it. In a boat held together with scrap-dealer epoxy and prayer. At monsoon peak."

"When you say it like that it sounds insane."

"How else would you say it?"

I put the pencil down. My hands were steady, which surprised me; I'd expected them to shake, and their steadiness felt like a stranger's hands resting on mine. Outside, the rain came down in the long gray sheets that meant the monsoon had settled in for days, and the creek ran high and brown past the mangroves, and somewhere downriver Quist was regrouping, counting her men, checking her watch against the same flood window we were.

"The treasure," Emeric said carefully, "is in the bilge locker. The coins we didn't spend on hull patches. The ledger is in the lead chest. If we scuttled both right now, swam ashore, and walked away—"

"You'd lose the ledger."

"We could copy it. We have days of copying done already—I know you caught me, don't pretend otherwise. The chest survives water. We bury it, come back after the flood, dig it up. No gorge. No Quist catching us in open water. We live."

"And Quist finds the burial site, or finds the copies, or simply keeps hunting us until one of us makes a mistake, which will happen, because mistakes are the only cargo I've ever carried reliably." I leaned back. The cabin lamp swung with the boat's small restless motion against her lines. "You want to publish this thing. Dissolve the Consortium's charter. You think a ledger buried in a mangrove swamp survives until the hearings? She burned a light station's records once—don't look at me like that, I've heard the stories longer than I've known you. She doesn't leave evidence. She leaves absence."

"So we run the gorge instead. Where she definitely can't follow, and where we also probably die."

"There it is," I said. "The arithmetic."

* * *

Here's the thing I hadn't said out loud yet, maybe hadn't said even to myself, and the lamp was low and the rain was loud and Emeric had taken a beating for a book of dead men's debts, so I figured he'd earned the truth even if the truth made me look foolish.

"It was never about the treasure," I said.

He raised the eyebrow that still worked.

"The ring. The coins. Even the ledger, a little. I told myself it was pragmatism—tide gives you a gift, you pocket it, you pay your debt, you stay afloat. That's the story I've run my whole life. Stay small, stay useful, stay invisible, and nobody looks too hard at Petra Coldharbour and her leaky boat." I turned the pencil over in my fingers. "But invisibility isn't free. You pay for it every day. You pay in the fares you don't take and the harbors you avoid and the names you don't give. I paid my whole life, and here's what it bought me: a Consortium loan I couldn't refuse and a collector who thinks I'm furniture she can repossess. Thirty-one years of staying invisible, and the first time anyone looked straight at me, they tried to own me."

"So the gorge—"

"The gorge is the one door out of here that she can't buy, can't follow, can't wait outside of. Quist's launch draws too much to run it at flood and she knows it—she's a careful woman, careful women don't run gorges. For two days, that water is mine. Nobody else's." I looked at the channel I'd drawn, my father's line reborn under my hand. "She offered me a clean record, Emeric. Full forgiveness. Do you understand what that means to someone like me? Everything I've owed since I was twenty-two, gone, if I handed you over. And I lay awake all night doing the sums, and the sums said yes, and I still said no. That's how I knew."

"Knew what?"

"That it's not about the debt either. It's about not being owned. Not by the Consortium, not by the tide, not by fear, not by my father's ghost sitting at the bottom of that gorge waiting for me to either finish his work or prove him a fool." I stood, and the cabin felt suddenly smaller than it had all night. "Quist owns half the river because everyone on it would rather be safe than free. I've been safe my whole life. Look how that went."

* * *

Emeric didn't answer right away. He looked at the drawing a long time—the entry line, the eddy, the thin hopeful thread of the far current—and I watched him do the thing he does, which is take someone else's faith apart to see what it's riveted with.

"Your father died believing in a current nobody's ever confirmed," he said. "And now you'll risk both our lives on a child's memory of his confidence."

"Yes."

"That's not an argument."

"No," I agreed. "It's a tide table. The window closes tomorrow noon. Arguments are for people with time."

He almost smiled—the unswollen side of his mouth, anyway. "For what it's worth, I believe you about the current. Not because of the map. Because of how you read water. I've watched you for weeks. You hear things in it the rest of us need instruments for." He paused. "If it kills us—"

"It won't kill us."

"You can't know that."

"I can't," I said. "But I'll tell you what I do know. My father ran that gorge at three-quarter flood looking for a current he'd only been told about. I'm going to run it at peak, with the flood at its fullest, when the slot is scoured clean and the far current is running strongest. He went looking for a whisper. I'm going to chase a shout." I picked up the pencil stub again. "If there's any justice in hydraulics, the louder water is the easier find."

"And if there isn't any justice in hydraulics?"

"Then we'll have bigger complaints."

* * *

We worked through what was left of the evening. I drew the channel three more times from memory, each pass pulling up details I didn't know I carried: the white scar on the split rock where lightning had struck it the summer I was eight; the way the third pillar's eddy spun counterclockwise, which meant you entered it bow-left or you didn't enter it; the smell of the gorge in flood, which my father described once as *like the river clearing its throat*, and which I somehow had never forgotten though I'd never smelled it myself. Memory is a strange salvage. You haul up things you never knew you'd lost.

Emeric copied the final drawing onto oilcloth with a piece of charcoal, twice—one copy for the wheelhouse, one wrapped in waxed canvas and tucked inside his shirt, next to the ledger pages, so that if the Marrow ate us the map would float on without us. Practical. Grim. I approved.

Near midnight I sent him forward to sleep—he needed it, his ribs needed it, and one of us had to be able to work the pump tomorrow—and I stayed at the table with the lamp turned low and the rain hammering the deck above me, and I did the thing I'd been avoiding all night.

I talked to my father.

Not out loud. Out loud would have woken Emeric, and besides, I've never been sure the dead take requests. But I sat with the drawing and I told him, silently, in the shorthand of people who shared a boat and a bloodline: *You went for glory, old man. Everyone said so afterward, that you were chasing a legend to make your name. I never believed it. I think you went because the Consortium had liens on half your rig and the banks owned the other half, and the gorge was the one stretch of water where nobody's signature meant anything. I think you went to be free for six miles.* I traced the far current with my fingertip, not touching the graphite. *I'm going to finish it. Not for you. Well—not only for you.*

The lamp guttered. The creek roared. Somewhere south of us, I'd have bet the boat, Dame Sable Quist was doing her own arithmetic, and her numbers were better than mine, and she only needed to be right once.

Tomorrow at noon, the flood peaked. Tomorrow at noon, we cast off into the loudest water on the Vell, steering by a dead man's confidence and a living woman's stubbornness, with a king's stolen ledger riding in a lead chest and a hole in our hull patched with hope and scrap-dealer epoxy.

I blew out the lamp and went aft to check the engine one more time, because fear acknowledged is fear managed, and because the Marrow deserved to hear it from me first:

"Hold together one more day," I told her, "and I swear I'll get you a new keel."

She creaked. From her, that was practically a yes.
