## 14. The Last Safe Harbor

**Petra**

The light station at Crowsfoot Point had been dead longer than I'd been alive. Its lamp room stood open to the sky like a broken tooth, and the keeper's cottage leaned against the wind the way old men lean into weather they've stopped arguing with. It was the kind of place nobody charted anymore because nobody needed to—no shipping lane came close, no smuggler bothered, no Consortium collector wasted a launch's fuel on a ruin.

Which is exactly why I chose it.

I brought the *Marrow* in through the creek mouth on the last of the light, running shallow over a bar I remembered from a salvage job eight years back, and nosed her bow-first into the lee of the station's stone jetty. The patch below the waterline was holding the way a held breath holds—not comfortably, and not for long. Every hour since the mangrove creek, the bilge pump had been keeping a conversation going that I didn't want to have out loud.

Emeric hopped onto the jetty with the ledger wrapped in oilcloth against his chest, like a man carrying an infant through a crowd of strangers. He'd taken his beating two nights ago protecting that thing, and he still cradled it gentler than he cradled his own ribs.

"Home sweet home," he said, looking up at the cottage.

"Don't get attached."

"I never do." He said it lightly, but there was something under it, the way there always was with him—a stone dropped down a well that took too long to hit bottom.

I got the pump running on the deck battery, rigged the tarp over the cockpit, and did the arithmetic every captain does in a shelter: how long the fuel lasts, how long the food lasts, how long the patience lasts. Fuel: four days if I ran the engine only to charge batteries. Food: five, if Emeric kept pretending he wasn't hungry, which he would. Patience: unknown. Patience had been the variable all voyage.

The monsoon arrived proper around midnight. Not the leading bands we'd been outrunning for days—the real thing, a wall of rain that came off the sea like a curtain drawn across the world. Inside the cottage, with the shutters wedged and a driftwood fire going in the old hearth, the storm became almost companionable. Almost. There was a hole in the roof that turned the floor near the window into a mud garden, and the wind found every gap in the masonry and played them like a bad flute.

Emeric sat across the fire from me, drying his socks with the solemnity of a man performing surgery, and I realized I'd been waiting three days to ask him something.

"You said you wanted your family's name back," I said. "Back in Saltmouth, first night aboard. You said restoration. Restoration of what, exactly? A throne? A title? Because I've seen what's left of dynasties, Vale. They end up as gift-shop trinkets and arguments between historians."

He finished with the socks. Folded them. Set them on the hearthstone with care, buying himself the time, and I let him have it because I recognized the maneuver—I used it myself whenever anyone asked about my father.

"Restoration," he said finally, "was the story I told pawnbrokers and dockside gossips. It was even the story I told myself, for a while. It's easier to want a crown than to want what I actually want." He looked up. "I don't want anything restored, Captain. I want it *ended*."

"Ended."

"The Consortium's founding charter—the one that gives them their shipping monopoly, their banking rights, their little empire on the Vell—it traces back to a grant. A royal grant, signed by the last king of the drowned line, two years before the dynasty fell. In exchange for financing his armies, the merchant houses that became the Consortium received title to the river trade in perpetuity." He tapped the oilcloth bundle beside him. "That grant is the root of everything they own. And everything they own grew out of a treasury that was stolen from the people those same houses betrayed. The King's Purse isn't a legend, Petra. It's evidence."

I fed a piece of driftwood to the fire and watched it catch. Outside, the rain hammered the roof like it was trying to collect a debt of its own.

"Evidence of what?"

"That the founding claim was fraud from the first day. The ledger names the payments—the bribes, the silenced families, the fleet captains bought cheap. My ancestors sold the river to save their necks, and the Consortium has been collecting rent on that sale for six generations." His voice stayed level, but his hands didn't; he'd laced his fingers together and squeezed until the knuckles went white. "I'm not going to Saltmouth to be crowned. I'm going to publish that ledger. Every page. Every name. In the fish market, in the courts, nailed to every customs house door between here and the headwaters if that's what it takes. Let the charter stand or fall on what's written in it. I already know how it falls."

I sat with that for a while. The fire popped. Somewhere in the walls, wind moved through the chimney flue like a ghost with somewhere to be.

"You understand what you're describing," I said. "Quist isn't chasing you because the Consortium wants its heir back. She's chasing you because you're carrying a bomb with their name on the fuse."

"Now you see it."

"I see more than that." I looked at him across the fire and felt something cold settle in my stomach that had nothing to do with the drafty cottage. "I've been wondering why a woman like Quist spends this much on one salvager's debt. Debt collectors don't chase. They write letters. They seize boats. They don't run two launches and hire creek pirates and burn fuel through a monsoon for a ketch worth less than her wardrobe." I shook my head. "She was never collecting. She was erasing. Me, you, the ledger, the cave—everything that could point back to what's in that chest."

"And when she's done erasing," Emeric said quietly, "there won't be a trial, Captain. There won't be a hearing where you argue your side. People who know too much about the Consortium's foundations don't testify. They become part of the foundation."

The rain filled the silence. I thought about Dame Sable Quist standing on my dock in Saltmouth with my loan papers in her gloved hand, offering me a choice that was never a choice, smiling the way bankers smile—like the outcome was already filed.

"I ran my whole life on being invisible," I said. "Small boat, small jobs, no name on any manifest that mattered. I told myself it was freedom." I laughed, short and without much humor. "Turns out invisibility is just a service you provide to people who'd rather you weren't seen. And they don't pay for it. They just expect it."

"So stop providing it."

"It's not that simple."

"It never is. That's why it matters." He leaned forward, firelight moving on his face—the bruise along his jaw from the creek fight had gone yellow-green, healing, which felt like a metaphor I refused to examine. "Petra. When we come out of this—and we will come out of this—you could take the coins and disappear again. Deeper this time. Up some tributary no chart remembers. But Quist will still be out there, and the Consortium will still own the river, and someday someone will come up that tributary asking questions, and you'll be invisible in exactly the way they prefer."

"And the alternative?"

"The alternative is witnesses. Lots of them. That's the only armor that works against people like Quist—not hiding, but being seen by so many eyes that making you disappear becomes more expensive than letting you talk." He smiled, tired. "You've spent your life learning water no chart records. I'm asking you to help me put the Consortium on a chart."

I should have said something clever. Wry, deflecting, the usual armor. Instead I heard myself say:

"My father tried to warn people once. About the Consortium's toll practices, before they owned the locks outright. He wrote letters. Nobody published anything." I stared into the fire. "Then he stopped writing letters and started running the gorge, and we both know how that ended."

"Then finish what he started."

"That's a hell of a thing to ask a woman you've known three weeks."

"I know." He didn't look away. "I'm asking anyway."

* * *

**Emeric**

She slept in the chair by the fire, boots on, arms crossed, the way she slept everywhere—as though rest were a tactical position rather than a surrender. I sat by the window with the ledger open on my knees, reading pages I had memorized years ago and still couldn't stop reading, and watched the storm try to tear the sea off the earth.

I had lied to her twice on this voyage. Once at the dock in Saltmouth, under a false name, which hardly counted—everyone lies at docks. And once by omission, the night I told her I wanted my name back. Both lies were dead now, drowned somewhere between the King's Purse and the mangrove creek, and what remained felt stranger than either lie: the truth, sitting in the open between us, enormous and unglamorous.

A ledger. That was the whole inheritance. Six generations of exiles had guarded stories of crowns and scepters and secret vaults, and what the last king actually left his bloodline was an accounting of his own crimes. I'd raged about that as a boy in exile—my grandfather's cold rooms in a foreign city, the portraits of ancestors who'd sold a kingdom and gotten nothing but the bill. I'd wanted gold. I'd wanted the treasury to be real in the way treasure is real, heavy and redeeming.

It was real. It was just heavier than gold, and it redeemed nothing. It named names. Including, I'd learned in the cave, hers.

I glanced at Petra, asleep in the firelight with the scarred hand curled loose against her knee, and thought about the page I hadn't shown her yet. Not hidden—she'd read the whole ledger, or most of it—but not discussed. The Coldharbour entry. Her great-grandfather's signature, taking Consortium silver to hold the fleet's loyalty, paid in silence. She'd read it with the same face she wore reading depth soundings: nothing wasted, everything recorded.

I wondered what it cost her to keep that face. I wondered what it would cost her to drop it.

The storm eased toward dawn—not ended, monsoons don't end, they just change their mind about intensity—and in the grey half-light I heard her wake the way she did everything: instantly, completely, no transition. One moment asleep, the next upright, eyes on the window, listening.

"What?" I asked.

"Engine sounds wrong." She was already pulling her boots tighter. "Or maybe it's the pump. Or maybe it's the storm. Come on."

We went down to the jetty together through rain that had softened to a steady assault, and she knelt in the cockpit with her ear near the engine housing while I held the tarp against the wind, and after a long moment she sat back on her heels.

"Bearing's singing," she said. "Not dying. Singing. We've got days, maybe. If I can find grease in the stores, less singing."

"Days until what?"

"Until we make our move." She stood, wiped her hands on her trousers, and looked east along the coast, where the sky was the color of wet slate. "The gorge window opens day after tomorrow. Peak flood. Two days, maybe three, when the current runs deep enough and fast enough that Quist's launches can't follow and her charts are worthless." She turned to me. "You understand what I'm proposing. My father died in that gorge trying to find the far current. Half the river pilots think it's a myth. The other half think it's a grave."

"And you think it's real."

"I think my father believed it was real, and my father was wrong about many things but never about water." Her jaw set in that way I'd learned meant the conversation was over whether or not the words were. "But before we run it, there's something you need to decide, Vale. Because once we're in that gorge, there's no Quist to bargain with and no turning back. Everything you are—your name, your proof, your whole campaign—it goes through that gorge with us or it doesn't go at all."

"And if it doesn't survive?"

"Then you'll have traded a dynasty's revenge for a drowning." She met my eyes. Rain ran off the brim of her hat between us. "So before I risk my boat and both our lives on your ledger—tell me plainly. No heir's language, no talk of charters and claims. What happens if we win? What does the world look like the morning after you publish?"

I thought about the cold rooms of my childhood. About the portraits. About the fish market at Saltmouth, where I intended to nail the truth to a post and let the river read it.

"The morning after," I said, "the Consortium's charter gets challenged in every court on the Vell, and it loses, because the document it stands on was bought with stolen money and signed by a king selling things that were never his to sell. Tolls come down. The lock fees come down. Families that were paid in silence get paid in public, with interest, out of assets the courts unwind." I paused. "And a lot of very comfortable people become uncomfortable, and some of them will be angry, and some of them will be dangerous. I won't pretend otherwise. Revolutions that cost nothing aren't worth the paper they're printed on."

"And you?" she asked. "What do you get?"

"Nothing." I smiled. "That's rather the point. An heir who profits from the ledger is just another claimant. An heir who burns his own claim to light the document—that's a witness. I intend to be a witness."

She studied me for a long moment, rain streaming past us both, the wounded engine ticking as it cooled, the gorge waiting somewhere east under the slate sky.

"A witness," she repeated. Then, almost to herself: "My father would have liked you. He trusted documents too. It was people he gave up on."

"And you?"

She didn't answer. She turned and climbed back up the jetty toward the cottage, and I followed, and neither of us said anything else, because the answer was in the following—in the fact that we climbed toward the same ruined shelter, carrying the same forbidden book, planning the same impossible run.

But that night, packing the stores for the gorge, I found she'd done something without announcing it. She'd waterproofed the ledger herself—re-wrapped it, double-oiled, sealed it into the lead chest alongside the signet ring, and stowed the chest in the stern locker, the driest place on the *Marrow*, the place a captain puts the things she intends to save first.

She never mentioned it. I never thanked her.

Some agreements are made that way—signed in ballast and bilge, witnessed by storms, binding as any charter ever inked.

At dusk the next day, with the barometer falling like a stone and the gorge window opening, we cast off from Crowsfoot Point into the rising flood.

Behind us, far down the coast, a light blinked twice. Three times. Twice again.

Petra saw it too. She went very still at the wheel.

"That's a signal," she said. "Somebody's telling somebody we're here."

The gorge was a day's run east. Quist, wherever she was, now knew exactly where we'd sleep tonight.

"We leave now," Petra said, and pushed the throttle forward into the dark.
