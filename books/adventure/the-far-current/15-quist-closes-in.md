## 15. Quist Closes In

The light station had been dead for forty years and smelled like it—rust and gull droppings and the sweet rot of old paint—but it stood on a shelf of rock above a channel deep enough to float the Marrow, and after three days of monsoon I'd have kissed a rock for less.

I tied off at the old coal dock with two lines and a prayer, which is one more line than I usually trust to prayer. Emeric climbed the tower stairs to see if anything was left worth having and came back down with a lantern, half a tin of lamp oil, and an expression like a man who'd found his grandmother's ghost doing housework.

"There's a bed," he said. "Or what was a bed."

"I've slept on worse."

"You've slept on worse this week."

He wasn't wrong. The bilge pump had run so long my arms had their own weather system, and the patch below the waterline was holding the way a held breath holds—not forever, and not well. We'd beached her in that mangrove creek with the water rising around us like a slow accusation, and every hour since, the Marrow had been telling me in her own language—creaks, weeps, a new note in the planking that I didn't like at all—that she was coming apart faster than I could argue her back together.

So: light station. Shelter. A night to think.

I should have known better than to think thinking was on the menu.

* * *

Quist's launch came up the channel at dusk, running dark except for a single riding light, which was its own kind of message. You don't show a light unless you want to be seen. Dame Sable Quist did not arrive anywhere by accident, and she did not knock on doors she intended to leave unopened.

Emeric saw her first. He came down from the gallery where he'd been standing watch—I'd given him the watch because I wanted the deck to myself, and because a man who can't sleep is better used than wasted—and said, quietly, "Boat in the channel. No lights but one."

I looked. I knew the silhouette before I knew the boat. Consortium launches have a particular arrogance to their lines, all forward rake and gunwale like a raised chin.

"She'll anchor off," I said. "She won't come alongside without asking. That's the point."

"What point?"

"The polite point." I wiped my hands on a rag that was past helping. "Everything Quist does is polite right up until it isn't. She wants me to come to her. Or she wants me to watch her sit there all night knowing exactly how much sleep I'm not getting."

"Which do you think it is?"

"Both. She's efficient."

We stood at the rail while the launch dropped hook a hundred yards out, riding light swinging gentle against the last grey of the sky. Then a boat went over the side—a gig, four oars, one of them Quist herself, which told me something. Dame Sable Quist did not row for just anyone. She rowed for negotiations.

She came up the coal dock steps with water dripping off her oars and her coat immaculate, which should have been impossible and wasn't, because Quist treated the world as a thing that ought to accommodate her and mostly the world agreed. She stopped at a respectful distance from the rail. Behind her, the oarsmen stayed in the gig, faces blank as coins.

"Captain Coldharbour," she said.

"Dame Quist."

"You've been difficult to find."

"The river's been difficult to be found in. Timing's everything."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not a smile. Quist kept smiles the way other people kept savings—for emergencies only. "May I come aboard?"

"It's not my boat you want to talk about. Let's talk here."

"Here, then." She folded her hands. "You are, I believe, owed some numbers. So let me give them to you plainly, because I respect plainness and I suspect you're tired of arithmetic done in the dark. Your loan with the Consortium stands at nine hundred and forty marks principal, plus interest accrued, plus penalties for the late payments you made while fleeing my collectors through floodwater, which I will generously characterize as acts of God rather than acts of Petra Coldharbour."

"Generous of you."

"I'm capable of generosity. It's one of the things people fail to understand about me." Her eyes went past my shoulder, to Emeric, standing in the shadow of the wheelhouse with his arms crossed. "Good evening, Your Highness."

"He goes by Vale," I said.

"He goes by whatever keeps him alive another week. Names are clothing, Captain; you of all people know that." She turned back to me. "Here is my offer, and I will make it once, tonight, because dawn brings weather and weather brings complications and I dislike both. The debt is forgiven. All of it. The Marrow is yours free and clear, papers clean, no lien, no claim, nothing in any ledger anywhere with your name on it. Further: your record. Every port fine, every harbor dispute, every irregularity in your salvage filings going back fifteen years—struck. You would be, on paper, the most boring captain on the Vell."

Fifteen years of careful invisibility, erased with a stroke. Fifteen years of staying small enough that nobody looked twice, offered back to me like a gift.

"And in exchange," I said, because there was always an exchange, "you get the ledger and the heir."

"In exchange," Quist said, "you get the ledger and the heir delivered to my launch before dawn, along with your sworn statement that you never saw either of them. I don't need your help finding things anymore, Captain. I need your help forgetting them."

"And if I say no?"

"Then at dawn my launch weighs anchor, and you go on being exactly what you are now: a woman in a leaking boat with a dying engine, a failing patch, and a man aboard whose existence is a legal problem you cannot afford. The monsoon has another week in it. The gorge is at peak flood. I've read your father's file, Captain. I know what the gorge does to boats crewed by better men than either of us." She unfolded her hands. "I'm not threatening you. Threats are for people who need convincing. You already know every fact I could put in front of you. I'm simply declining to lie about what they add up to."

That was the worst of it, and I think she knew it. Quist never lied. Lying leaves fingerprints. She just arranged the truth until it looked like a wall.

"I'll need the night," I said.

"You have until first light. My coxswain will signal with a white lamp for yes, red for no. If there's no signal, I'll assume no, and act accordingly." She stepped back toward the stairs, then paused. "One more thing, Captain, offered freely. The ledger names families. Yours among them, I understand. Whatever you imagine you're protecting by keeping it—the Coldharbours were paid long ago, in silence, and silence is the one currency that never appreciates. You're not avenging anyone. You're just carrying someone else's shame in a wet boat."

She went down the stairs. The gig pulled away. The launch sat black on the black water with its single light burning, patient as a debt.

* * *

Emeric hadn't moved from the wheelhouse door. When I came in he said, "No."

"No what?"

"Whatever she offered. No. Don't even turn it over. She's lying about something—she's always lying about something—and the moment you hand me over, you're a witness who's outlived her usefulness. People who outlive their usefulness to the Consortium don't get clean records. They get closed files."

"I know how the arithmetic works."

"Do you?" He turned to face me fully, and in the lamplight his face had the raw look of a man who'd been doing his own arithmetic and didn't like the sum. "Because I've been watching you look at that launch for ten minutes, and it's the same way you look at a channel you're deciding whether to run. And I need to tell you something before you decide, so you're deciding with all of it. The ledger doesn't just dissolve the Consortium's charter claim. It names the men who signed the original transfer. One of them was a Coldharbour."

"I know. Chapter and verse. My great-grandfather sold the fleet's loyalty for a purse and a promise of quiet. I've read the page."

"And you still—" He stopped. Recalibrated. "You read it and you're still standing here?"

"That's exactly why I'm standing here." I picked up the lantern. "Go sleep, Emeric. I've got a patch to fix and a night to waste, and I do my worst decisions alone, same as everybody."

He didn't move. "Petra."

"Sleep. That's not negotiable. One of us needs to be able to pull an oar tomorrow, whichever tomorrow turns out to be."

He went, eventually, up into the keeper's quarters, and I heard him lie down on the ghost of a bed, and I heard him not sleep, which took him a long time, and then I heard him manage it anyway, the way exhausted people do—suddenly, completely, like falling off a ledge.

Which left me alone with the lamplight, the tools, and the hole in my boat.

* * *

There's a particular hour—it comes somewhere past midnight, when the tide turns and even the water seems to lose interest in you—when a decision stops being a decision and becomes a shape. You stop weighing options and start noticing which one you've already chosen, the way you notice a stone in your shoe only after the walking's done.

I spent the early part of the night under the Marrow's floorboards with a lamp hooked on a string, sewing canvas and marine glue into the patch, because whatever I decided, the patch needed to hold through it. That's the thing nobody tells you about choices: they all require the same boat afterward. Run or surrender, the Marrow was the only thing between me and swimming, and I have never trusted the Vell enough to swim it at night.

The patch was ugly work. The original seam had opened along a butt joint where some shipwright thirty years ago had economized on fastenings, and the surrounding planks were soft as bread. I drove in screws that bit sideways, slathered glue that stank like a chemistry lesson, and pressed the canvas home with my whole weight while the bilge ran and the rain came down on the deck above like applause from a crowd that wanted somebody else.

Somewhere in the middle of it my hands started remembering things I hadn't asked them to.

My father's hands, doing this exact job, the summer before the gorge. He patched the Marrow's hull on our beach with me holding the lamp, eight years old, and he talked the whole time—not to teach me, I understood later, but because talking kept the fear somewhere outside his chest. *A boat forgives everything but neglect*, he said. *You can run her aground, you can overload her, you can sail her into weather no sane person would look at. But if you let her rot while you're busy being clever, she'll remember, and she'll collect.* He laughed when he said it. He was always laughing near the end. I know now that was the sound fear makes when it gives up pretending.

And Quist's voice over the top of it, smooth as silt: *you're not avenging anyone.*

She was wrong, though. That was the thing I kept turning over while the glue cured. She thought the ledger was leverage, or evidence, or a problem to be erased, and Emeric thought it was justice, and both of them were partly right, and neither of them had read page forty-one the way I had—lying in a bunk at two in the morning with a lamp turned low, reading the entry where Aldous Coldharbour signed away eleven boats and two hundred crews and got, in exchange, a sum so small it was almost an insult, plus one line in the margin in the paymaster's hand: *Subject cooperative. Desires no further contact.*

Desires no further contact. My family's whole inheritance, in five words. Two hundred years of Coldharbours keeping their heads down, taking salvage jobs for unnamed clients, refusing passengers who watched too closely, teaching daughters to read water instead of faces—all of it, every generation, tracing straight back to a man who took money to disappear and taught his children the trick so well they forgot it was a trick.

Quist was offering to sell me the same product my great-grandfather bought. Silence, certified clean. And the price was a man asleep overhead who'd trusted me with his life on the honest grounds that I hadn't yet sold it.

I sat on the floorboards with my back against the engine housing and let myself, for exactly one minute, imagine saying yes.

It was a good minute. That's what I'll admit and no more. The Marrow mine outright, no collector's shadow on any dock I ever touched. My name scrubbed of every fine and flag, free to take work or refuse it, invisible again, safe again, small again. Nine hundred marks of fear lifted off my shoulders like wet oilskins.

And then the minute ended, and what was left underneath it was the truth, which is that safety bought with someone else's delivery isn't safety. It's just a longer rope. Quist would own me cleaner than the loan ever had. Every job I took after, I'd take knowing what I'd traded, and every night I'd hear the white lamp and the red lamp, and one day—maybe years out, maybe next month—she'd come back with a new offer, because that's what you do with a person who's sold once. You buy again.

My father died running the gorge trying to prove the river couldn't own him. He died stupidly, at peak flood, in a boat he'd neglected, chasing a channel he'd only half-charted. I had spent my whole life being the correction to his mistake: careful, patient, unseen. And it had gotten me a leaking ketch, a criminal debt, and a Consortium dame on my doorstep offering to purchase my soul at a fair market rate.

Careful hadn't worked. Invisible hadn't worked. There was no version of small that the world wouldn't eventually come looking for.

But there was one thing Quist had said that I kept coming back to, the way a tongue finds a broken tooth. *If there's no signal, I'll assume no, and act accordingly.* Act accordingly. At dawn. With a launch full of men and a channel behind her that the Marrow would have to pass to reach open water—or the gorge, which was suicide, or—

Or.

I got up. My knees cracked like rigging in a squall. I climbed to the gallery with the lamp and stood looking south, toward the place where the river narrowed and the cliffs drew in and the maps went vague, the way maps always do where the water is serious.

The gorge at peak flood. Everyone said it couldn't be run. My father had believed otherwise, and everyone had said the same thing about him, and the difference between a dead fool and a living legend was a current nobody had ever charted—the far current, the deep lane he swore existed past the standing waves, the one he died hunting. I'd spent twenty years refusing to think about it. Refusing was easier. Refusing was invisible.

But I had read that water my whole life. Every sandbar's mood, every eddy's temper, every place the Vell lied about its depth. And a child who holds the lamp while her father draws a channel in the dirt—over and over, every winter, till the lines wore grooves in her memory—remembers more than she admits.

Below me, the launch rode its single light. Patient. Certain.

I went back down, took out the chart case, and unrolled the old Admiralty sheet of the gorge reach, the one with the blank space in the middle where the surveyors had given up. Then I found a pencil.

Dawn was four hours off. The patch would hold. The lamp had oil.

Whatever I signaled at first light, I intended to be the one who decided what it meant.
