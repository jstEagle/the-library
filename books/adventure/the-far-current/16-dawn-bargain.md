## 16. Dawn Bargain

I slept maybe two hours, and I dreamed of my father the whole time, which was either a warning or a rehearsal.

In the dream he stood at the wheel of the Marrow with his back to me, the way I remembered him best, one hand on the spokes, the other holding a mug of tea gone cold because he never could drink it while there was water to watch. He didn't turn around. He just said, in that voice he used for things he'd already decided, *the channel's still there, Petra-girl. It doesn't care whether you believe in it.*

Then the dream flooded, as dreams do, and I woke on the deck of the derelict light station with salt-stiff hair and a lamp burned down to a stub, and the patch below the waterline holding by exactly the amount of prayer I'd put into it overnight, which is to say: not much.

Emeric was asleep against the mast step, one arm curled around the lead chest like a child around a dog. He had a bruise coming up along his jaw from the creek fight, purple going yellow at the edges, and he'd lost skin off both knuckles protecting pages written by men dead two hundred years. I looked at him for a long moment and felt the shape of the decision settle over me like wet canvas.

Here is what nobody tells you about choosing: it isn't dramatic. It's not a lightning strike or a choir. It's you, sitting alone in the dark with a cold lamp, doing arithmetic you've been avoiding for weeks, and then—done. The answer arrives quiet as bilge water. You just notice, one moment, that you've stopped arguing with yourself.

I had stopped arguing somewhere around three in the morning. The answer was no. Not Quist's no—the polite, negotiated, *yes ma'am, here's your ledger and your heir* no that would buy me a clean record and a debt-free boat and another twenty years of being nobody on this river. My no. The kind my father must have said to himself, once, standing at the mouth of the gorge with the flood building behind him.

I got up quietly, found the last of the coffee, and started getting the Marrow ready to betray somebody. I hadn't yet decided who.

* * *

Quist came at first light, because of course she did. Dame Sable Quist struck me as a woman who considered dawn less a time of day than an administrative courtesy—a deadline made visible.

Her launch came around the point at half tide, grey hull, grey awning, four grey men, and Quist herself standing at the rail with her hands folded like a woman attending her own funeral and finding the eulogy acceptable. She came aboard without being asked, which told me everything about how she expected this meeting to go, and I let her, because letting people underestimate you is the cheapest armor there is.

"Captain Coldharbour." She said my name the way you'd read it off a ledger. "You've had a productive night."

"I patched a boat."

"You repaired an asset." Her eyes went past me, cataloguing: the strained seams, the mangrove-scoured paint, Emeric's chest sitting fat and smug amidships under its tarp. "You'll forgive the observation, but the asset is dying. Another monsoon day on this hull and she's firewood."

"She's held worse."

"Has she?" Something almost gentle moved through her face, and it was worse than the cruelty would have been. "Petra. May I use your name? Thank you. I've read your file. All of it. Your father's debts, your grandfather's debts, the salvage licenses, the quiet jobs, the ones nobody wrote down. Do you know what I see, when I look at that file?"

"A boat worth repossessing."

"I see the most invisible woman on the Vell," she said, "who has spent thirty years making sure no one needed to know her name, and who is now standing in the open with a deposed dynasty's proof of theft and a man who wants to burn the Consortium to the waterline with it. You are visible now, Captain. That happened the moment you took that ring out of the King's Purse. Everything since has just been deciding what the world sees when it looks."

The flood was building. I could feel it in the way the Marrow tugged at her lines, restless, wanting the current. Behind Quist, out past the point, the gorge mouth showed as a brown seam of standing water between the cliffs, and even from here I could hear it—that low drumroll the rock makes when the whole river is trying to squeeze through a keyhole.

"So here is my final offer," Quist said. "The ledger. The heir. In exchange: the Marrow is yours free and clear. Every paper burned. Your record clean. No collector will ever trouble you again. You go back to being no one, safely, permanently, with your boat under you and your name off every list I keep." She smiled, thin as a knife laid flat. "It's a generous price for two items."

"And if I say no?"

"Then at some point in the next forty-eight hours, the Vell takes your boat, or my men do, and I get the ledger anyway, and you get nothing but wet." She unfolded her hands. "Survival does the negotiating, Captain. It always has. I'm simply giving you the dignity of agreeing before it does."

I looked at her for a long moment. And then I gave her the only thing I had left to trade.

"Yes," I said.

She didn't gloat. That was the frightening thing. She just nodded, as though a form had been signed, and held out her hand for the ledger.

"Not here," I said. "The chest is sealed—waterproofed, weighted. If I crack it on deck and we take a squall, two hundred years of evidence goes into the river and you've traded a clean slate for nothing. We do the transfer underway. I'll run us to your launch, calm water inside the point, and we hand it across proper. Ten minutes."

For the first time, something flickered behind her eyes—not suspicion exactly. Arithmetic. She was weighing ten minutes of my control against the inconvenience of forcing the issue, and I watched her decide that a woman who'd just said *yes* wasn't going to spend her last bargaining chip on theater.

"Underway, then," Quist said. "My coxswain will follow you in."

"Keep your distance. My patch doesn't like wakes."

She stepped back onto her launch, and I stood at the rail watching them fall away toward their own boat, and my heart was going like a pump in a flooding hold, steady and terrible, because I had just lied to the most dangerous woman on the river and she had believed me, and there was no version of the next hour that ended with everyone content.

Emeric was awake. He'd been awake for all of it—I could tell by the way he was very carefully looking at the horizon instead of at me.

"You said yes," he said.

"I said yes."

"To Quist."

"I find it helps, in negotiations, to begin with the word they're listening for." I pulled the tarp off the engine hatch. "Get her warm. Slow and easy—no smoke above idle until I give the word. When I raise my hand, bring her to full. Don't ask questions. Just don't flood her."

He looked at me, and I watched him understand—not the plan, because there wasn't much of one, but the shape of it. His jaw bruise tightened. "Where are we going?"

I nodded at the gorge mouth, where the brown water stood up in ridges and the drumroll had become something you felt in the soles of your feet.

Emeric went pale in a way that had nothing to do with his beating. "That's suicide."

"That's a channel," I said. "My father drew it for me when I was nine years old. He just never got to finish the lesson." I turned away before he could see what saying that cost. "Warm the engine, Vale."

* * *

We cast off at full flood slack-plus-twenty, which any sane pilot will tell you is madness, and which is precisely why no one was watching the gorge mouth except madwomen.

Quist's launch fell in behind us at two hundred yards, close enough to feel supervised. I brought the Marrow around slow, lazy, a tired boat limping toward calmer water and a peaceful transfer, and I kept her that way through the first quarter mile while Emeric fed the engine fuel by hand because the line was fouled and neither of us dared stop to fix it properly.

The chest sat amidships under its tarp. The ledger sat in the chest. And every hundred yards, the water changed under me—green going brown, brown going white at the edges—and the sound of the gorge climbed from drumroll into something with teeth.

At the last bend before the point, Quist's coxswain must have finally done his own arithmetic. I saw the launch heel as it came hard about, saw the wake stand up, heard—faint, then not faint—the flat cracks that meant someone aboard had decided talking was finished.

The first rounds hit the water short. The second didn't.

One slapped the transom an arm's length from Emeric's head and left a splintered star in the wood. Another went through the foresail furled on its boom like it was paper, which it was. I raised my hand.

Emeric opened the throttle.

The Marrow surged, and I felt the patch let go a little—not fail, not yet, but *announce itself*, a shudder through the keel like a joint popping. The bilge, which had been running all night, found new work. I put the wheel over and drove her straight at the gorge mouth with bullets stitching the water behind us and Quist's launch turning to chase and the whole grey sky leaning down on us like a lid.

"Petra!" Emeric shouted over the engine. "They're following!"

"They won't follow far."

"How do you know?"

"Because their boat draws six feet," I said, "and in ten minutes, nothing that draws six feet is going to survive in here."

Then the cliffs took us.

* * *

I have run bad water my whole life, and I want to be honest about what the gorge was, because honesty is the one thing I have left that Quist can't repossess.

It was the worst water I have ever seen, and I chose it.

The flood peak had turned the gorge into a single moving argument—standing waves taller than the rail, holes that opened and shut like breathing things, and everywhere the brown roar of the Vell insisting on its own way. Rockfall from the spring slides had narrowed the channel to a throat, and the guide waves were breaking clear across it, so that the entrance I needed showed itself for perhaps three seconds at a time, between sets, like something blinking.

But underneath all of it—underneath the chaos the surface insists on—my father's channel ran true. The far current. The deep lane that hugs the north wall where the old slide shelf kicks the main flow outward and leaves a tongue of smoother water, narrow as a cart track, running straight through the heart of the mess. He'd died believing in it. Every chart said it was a myth. I was about to find out whether my father had been a prophet or a fool, at a speed that permitted no second opinion.

"Emeric! Bilge!"

He was already there, both hands on the pump, working it like a man chopping wood, and I loved him a little for not asking why, for just doing the next thing, which is the entire secret of staying alive on water.

The first wave hit us abeam and threw the Marrow sideways like a dog shaking off rain. I corrected. The second hit us square and the world went white and green and the patch tore loose entirely—I felt it go, a long tearing shudder under the floorboards, and the bilge began to win. Water came over the bow in solid sheets. The lead chest broke free of its lashings and slid, and Emeric left the pump to catch it, which was the wrong choice and the right one, both at once.

"Leave it!" I screamed.

"It's the *ledger*—"

"The ledger's waterproof! *Pump!*"

He dropped the chest, scrambled back, and pumped, and I steered, and the gorge did its best to kill us, which was considerable.

There is a moment in every serious piece of water—I've known it since I was a girl hanging off my father's knee—when the boat stops being a thing you command and becomes a thing you *agree with*. You don't fight the river anymore. You negotiate. You put the hull exactly where the water was already willing to let it go, and you pray your memory is better than your fear.

I put the Marrow on the far current.

And it held.

Just like he said. A tongue of green water hugging the north wall, smooth as poured glass compared to everything around it, narrow and fast and *real*, running us through the throat of the gorge while the Vell raged on either side close enough to touch. I laughed. I couldn't help it—laughed out loud in the middle of the worst water of my life, because my father had been right, he'd been right all along, and he'd died without ever knowing it, and I was knowing it for him at fifteen knots with my boat coming apart beneath me.

The rockfall throat passed to starboard close enough to read the lichen. A hole yawned off the port bow and I walked the Marrow around its edge like a dancer. Behind us, small and receding, Quist's launch had stopped at the gorge mouth—hung there a moment, a grey speck against the white—and then turned away, because her coxswain was a professional and professionals know the difference between a risk and a grave.

Then the cliffs fell back, the water lay down, and the Marrow, having carried us through hell, quietly began to die.

* * *

She went by degrees, the way old boats do. First the list, gentle as a sleeping woman turning over. Then the deck coming up to meet our feet at an angle that stopped being charming. Emeric bailed; I steered for the shallows past the gorge tailrace where the fishing families' drying racks stood along the shingle like a row of patient birds. The Marrow settled onto the sand with a sigh I felt in my chest, and the river came in over her rails, gentle now, almost apologetic, taking her back the way the tide takes everything eventually.

I stood in waist-deep water beside my sinking boat and did not cry, mostly.

Emeric waded out to me, the lead chest held above the waterline, and for a moment neither of us said anything. Behind us, on the shingle, people were gathering—fisherfolk, dozens of them, drawn out of their huts by the impossible sight of a boat running the gorge at flood peak, and every one of them had watched us come through. Every one of them had seen the grey launch turn back at the mouth. Every one of them would be telling this story tonight, and tomorrow, and for years.

Witnesses. The one cargo Quist could never sink.

"Well," Emeric said at last, breathless, soaked, holding two centuries of stolen history over his head like a man carrying a baby out of a fire. "Your father's channel."

"Was real," I said.

"He'd have been proud."

"He'd have called me an idiot." I put my hand flat on the Marrow's settling hull, on the planking my grandfather had laid and my father had caulked and I had run through the gorge, and I let myself feel all of it at once—the grief and the triumph, in one breath, exactly as tangled as they'd always promised to be. "And then he'd have bought the drinks."

The water rose around us. The fishing families came down the shingle toward us, and for the first time in my life, I let them look.
