## 17. The Far Current

**Petra**

The gorge announced itself before we saw it—a sound like the Vell clearing its throat, deep and continuous, the kind of noise you feel in your back teeth. I'd been hearing it in my sleep for twenty years, ever since my father's boat came down that throat of water and didn't come out the other side.

"Tell me again," Emeric said from the engine well, both hands on the bilge pump handle like it was the last honest thing in the world.

"No."

"Petra—"

"You asked me to tell you again and I'm telling you no. If I say it out loud twice, it becomes a plan instead of a prayer, and I can't steer a prayer." I checked the patch one more time by feel, my palm flat against the hull below the waterline where the canvas and pitch were holding by stubbornness alone. It shuddered under my hand. Everything about the Marrow was shuddering now. She felt less like a boat than like an animal that knew what was being asked of her.

The flood had come up overnight the way monsoon floods do—not rising so much as arriving, as if the river had simply decided to be somewhere else. The gorge mouth ahead was a brown chaos of standing waves, water stacked on water, and above it the cliffs leaned in close enough that a gull crossing overhead would've had to file a flight plan. Somewhere in all that violence was a channel. My father had spent eleven years looking for it. He'd found it, I believed that, he'd found it—and then he'd died forty yards short of proving it to anyone who mattered.

I intended to prove it to everyone who mattered. Quist's launch was behind us, and behind us was the only direction she could follow.

"Emeric." I took the wheel. "When I say pump, you pump. When I say hold, you sit down and hold onto something that's bolted down, because nothing else will be. When I say jump—" I stopped. "Actually, there is no when I say jump. We're not jumping. Jumping is how people die politely."

"What's the polite way?"

"Drowning with your boots on."

He almost smiled. Under the grime and the bruise Quist's men had given him, he looked younger and older at once, the way people do when they've stopped pretending the odds are good. "My family ran this river once," he said. "Kings of it, if the ledger's true. I never learned to read water."

"The ledger's true," I said. "And the water doesn't care whose it used to be. Pump."

I put the Marrow's nose into the gorge.

* * *

**Emeric**

There is a moment—I have since learned every survivor of a gorge run knows this moment—when you understand that the thing carrying you has stopped being a vessel and become a projectile. The Marrow went up the first standing wave like something thrown, and for one full second we were airborne, Petra's knuckles white on the wheel, the bilge pump handle tearing loose from my grip, the whole drowned treasury of a kingdom sloshing around my ankles in the form of river water.

Then we came down and the world became noise.

I pumped. That was my entire universe: down, up, down, up. Water came over the bow in sheets, found every seam the old girl had, and made itself at home. The patch aft was groaning a note I didn't like, a low wooden complaint like a door in a house nobody should live in.

"Starboard!" Petra shouted, and I understood her not at all until the cliff wall arrived where starboard clearly meant *not*, and she threw the wheel over and we scraped past the rockfall with a sound like a giant filing its nails. Boulders the size of cottages sat in the flood where the cliffs had shed them, and between them the water did things I would have called impossible if I hadn't been inside them. It stood up. It turned corners without going anywhere. It opened holes.

"Left fork!" she yelled, mostly to herself. "He wrote left fork—he always said left fork—"

Her father. She'd told me about him in the mangrove creek, in the stripped-down hours when survival makes liars of no one: how he'd charted the gorge from a fishing skiff for a decade, how the Consortium had refused to insure the run, how he'd gone anyway because a man who reads water can't spend his whole life reading other men's faces. How Petra, nine years old, had memorized his hand-drawn channel off his cabin wall the way other children memorized prayers.

She was steering us through his memory. I kept pumping.

The second wave hit us broadside and the Marrow rolled—truly rolled, gunwale under, the lead chest breaking loose from its lashings and sliding across the deck to fetch up against my boot like a dog seeking shelter. For a heartbeat I looked straight down into brown roaring water and thought, with strange clarity: *so this is how the dynasty ends, twice.*

Then she came back. Petra hauled her back with the wheel and the throttle and what I can only describe as argument—the boat and the woman disagreeing violently about physics, and the woman winning on points.

"Pump!" she screamed, and I pumped, and somewhere in the next minute the patch gave up its long negotiation. I heard it go—a soft wet sigh beneath the roar, the sound of canvas surrendering—and the bilge began to gain on me in earnest.

"How long?" she shouted.

"If you mean how long can I keep her afloat—yes! If you mean how long can I keep her afloat—ask a different question!"

"That's what I thought!"

We came around the great bend where the cliffs pinched together, and I saw her face change. Not fear. Recognition. She was looking at the water the way I imagined she looked at old friends across a crowded dock—searching for someone in particular.

"There," she breathed. "Oh, Da. There you are."

* * *

**Petra**

It was just as he'd drawn it. A seam in the chaos, darker water, smooth as poured ink, running hard along the base of the north cliff where every sane pilot would swear the rockfall made it impassable. The far current. My father's whole life's arithmetic, summed in one line of black water that only showed itself at peak flood, when the river rose high enough to drown the rocks that guarded its deepest road.

Everyone ran the gorge wide, fighting the standing waves in the middle where the water was big but honest. Everyone died there too, sooner or later. My father had figured out that the safe-looking water was the killer and the impossible-looking seam was the way through—if you could hold your nerve for four hundred yards of cliff-shadowed current moving faster than anything deserves to move.

"Hold!" I shouted, and Emeric dropped onto the chest and wrapped his arms around it, and I put us into the seam.

The world went quiet. That's the part nobody believes. Inside the far current, with the walls of water standing on either side of you higher than the mast, the noise falls away and there's just speed—a smooth, terrible, gliding rush, the Marrow running like she'd never run in her life, spray whipping off the wave-walls, the cliffs flicking past close enough to count the strata. Forty feet of clear water ahead, forty behind, and everything else in the universe was trying to kill us except the one line we were riding.

I laughed. I couldn't help it. Twenty years of staying invisible, of keeping my head down and my wake small, and here I was at the loudest, most visible moment of my life, laughing like my father at the tiller.

The rocks came at the end. Of course they did. The seam narrowed where a slab had calved off the cliff face years ago, leaving a gap my father had marked with three question marks and the word *maybe*. I remembered tracing that word with my finger when I was small. *Maybe* was the whole inheritance he'd left me.

I held my breath and drove the Marrow into *maybe*.

The port side kissed stone—one grinding, splintering instant, planks giving up their arguments—and then we were through, spat out the far end of the gorge into the wide brown calm below, the river suddenly gentle as a lake, the rain falling soft and ordinary, as if none of it had happened.

Behind us the gorge roared on, indifferent. Ahead the water stretched out flat and gray-green to the delta country, and Quist's launch was nowhere, because nothing that floated could follow what we'd just done.

The Marrow was dying. I could feel it in the wheel, loose and vague, the rudder answering late. Emeric was bailing with the chest lid now, both hands, no rhythm left, just fury. Water stood thigh-deep in the well.

"She's going down," he said. Not a question.

"Not here." I steered her for the shingle bar on the south bank, easing her in the last hundred yards as gently as I'd ever handled anything, and ran her aground in eighteen inches of water with the dignity of a queen taking her throne. Then she settled, sighed, and lay over on her beam, and it was done.

I stood in the flooded cockpit with the rain coming down and put my hand flat on the deck one more time.

"Thank you," I told her. "You beautiful, leaking, impossible old girl."

Emeric waded over, hauling the lead chest, and set it on the high gravel beside me. He was bleeding from a cut on his forehead I hadn't noticed him get, and shaking, and grinning like a man who'd been let in on the best secret in the world.

"The far current," he said. "It's real. Your father found it."

"He found it." My voice cracked on the second word and I let it. "Forty years early, he found it. Took me twenty years and a sinking boat to catch up."

Downstream, maybe half a mile, smoke rose from cooking fires among the stilt houses of a fishing camp—people who would have heard the gorge, who on a morning like this would be standing on their walkways watching the flood, watching everything the river sent down.

Watching us.

Emeric followed my gaze. "Witnesses," he said quietly.

"Witnesses," I agreed. And for the first time in my life, the word didn't taste like a threat. I picked up my end of the chest. "Come on, heir or no heir. Help me carry your kingdom ashore."
