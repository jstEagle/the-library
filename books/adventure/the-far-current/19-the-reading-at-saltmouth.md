## 19. The Reading at Saltmouth

Saltmouth smelled the way it always did on a market morning—brine and diesel and fish guts going sweet in the sun—but that day there was something else under it, something I couldn't name until I was standing in the crowd with my arms crossed like a woman waiting for a bus she had no intention of boarding.

Paper. The smell of paper. A lot of it.

Emeric Vale had rented a fish stall.

Not a hall, not a courthouse steps, not any of the dignified venues his name would have bought him in the city. A fish stall. Number eleven, between a woman selling eels by the string and a man whose entire inventory appeared to be ice, which he sold in blocks the size of coffins. Emeric had paid for the stall with coins from a drowned treasury, and he'd set up a folding table on it, and on the table he'd stacked the ledger—the actual ledger, salt-stained, coral-freckled, smelling faintly of two hundred years of seawater—alongside three hundred printed copies of its pages.

He'd had them printed in Saltmouth. At the press behind the chandlery, where old Maren Oduya ran off tide tables and funeral notices. He'd paid her triple rate to work through the night, and she'd taken the money, and then—this was the part that got me—she'd refused to keep quiet about what she'd printed. He'd offered her silence as part of the fee. She'd said no. She'd said, and I heard her say it later, "I've been printing other people's secrets for forty years. Let me print one of mine."

So the whole town knew before dawn. And the whole town came.

* * *

I want to be honest about my first instinct, which was to be somewhere else entirely.

I'd tied the Marrow up at the far end of the commercial dock, past the fuel lock, in the spot nobody wanted because the fenders rubbed against a piling with a nail in it. I'd told myself I'd come ashore for coffee and rope wax and nothing else. I'd told myself a lot of things on the walk over, most of them variations on *you don't have to be there, this isn't your fight anymore, you delivered him, you're done.*

The trouble with telling yourself things is that eventually you hear yourself doing it, and then you have to decide whether you're a liar or a coward, and I've never much liked either job description.

So I stood at the back of the crowd with my hood up, because old habits drown hard, and I watched a deposed dynasty's last heir climb onto an ice block so the fishwives could see him, and begin to read.

* * *

He didn't start with the treasury. That surprised me. I'd expected the gold—I think we all expected the gold; crowds come for gold the way gulls come for chum. Instead he read names.

"The ledger opens," he said, holding the original up so the light caught the water damage, "with an accounting. Not of what the crown took. Of who it took it *from*." He turned a page with the care of a man handling a bird with a broken wing. "Entry one. The Vell ferry tolls, collected for sixty-one years from families who were promised the crossings would be free. Entry two. The deepwater mooring rights at Gullwatch, purchased from the Kestrel family for a sum of twelve crowns and a pardon."

A voice from the crowd—an old man's voice, cracked as river clay: "Kestrels are us. We're the Kestrels." And a murmur went through the gathering like current under still water, because half of Saltmouth was descended from somebody in that book and didn't know it yet.

Emeric kept reading. Ferry tolls. Mooring rights. Salvage claims—my stomach tightened at that one—salvage claims on the lower Vell, bought from working captains for fractions of their worth, backed by threats dressed as law. The dynasty hadn't just stolen treasure. It had built a machine for stealing, and when the dynasty drowned, the machine hadn't drowned with it. It had been sold. Inherited. Rechartered.

"Page forty," Emeric said, and here his voice changed, went quieter, the way water goes quiet before it drops. "The founding charter of the Consortium of the Vell, dated ninety-one years after the last king's death, grants its signatories 'all rights, claims, and revenues formerly held by the crown below the high water mark.' Below the high water mark. Do you understand what that means? The Consortium's entire legal claim to this river—to every salvage, every mooring, every debt contracted on these waters—rests on a document that transfers property the crown stole in the first place. They are collecting rent," he said, "on a theft."

The crowd made a sound I'd never heard a crowd make. Not a cheer. Something lower and more dangerous than a cheer. The sound of several hundred people doing arithmetic about their own lives all at once.

* * *

I should say something about Dame Sable Quist, because she was there.

Of course she was there. Quist never missed a fire she might yet control. I spotted her gray coat near the chandlery awning, standing very straight, hands folded, wearing the expression of a woman auditing a warehouse she already knew was empty. Two of her men flanked her, and both of them kept looking at the crowd the way you look at weather that's turning.

She'd had nine days since the gorge. Nine days to buy witnesses, burn copies, file injunctions, do whatever it is people like her do when the water rises. And what she'd actually done, as far as I could tell, was nothing—which told me more than anything she could have done. There are problems you can pay your way out of, and there are problems where paying makes it worse, and a public reading in a fish market with three hundred printed copies already loose in the town was the second kind. Every copy she seized would be a confession. Every witness she leaned on would lean back.

Quist understood arithmetic better than anyone I've ever met. That was always her gift. And the arithmetic had changed.

She stayed through the first hour. Through the ferry tolls and the mooring rights and the long recitation of families, some extinct, some not. She stayed until Emeric reached the entries about the salvage fleet—the Coldharbour fleet—and then, I noticed, she didn't stay. She turned and walked toward the harbor office with her spine straight and her pace unhurried, a woman leaving a meeting early because she'd already read the minutes.

I'd like to tell you I felt triumphant watching her go. What I actually felt was the specific emptiness of a debt collector leaving your house. The relief doesn't arrive until later, and even then it arrives sideways, and it brings questions with it.

* * *

Here is the part of the ledger I need to talk about, and I've been circling it for nine days without landing, so let me land.

Page fifty-one. The Coldharbour entry.

My great-grandfather, Aldous Coldharbour, held command of eleven vessels during the last year of the dynasty. When the capital fell and the royal fleet was ordered scuttled rather than surrendered, Aldous was the one who carried out the order—and the ledger records, in a clerk's careful hand, that he was paid for it. Not with gold. With silence. A grant of anonymity, sealed forever, for him and his line. The crown bought the erasure of its own crime, and my family's price for participating was to be erased along with it. No name on the salvage rolls. No share of anything recovered. No record at all, beyond one line in a drowned king's private book.

We spent four generations being nobody, and it turns out we were *paid* to be nobody. My father died running the gorge chasing a channel no chart recorded, trying to make a living on a river his own blood had helped strip, and he never knew why the Coldharbours had never risen anywhere, never owned anything, never been mentioned. He thought it was bad luck. It was a contract.

When Emeric first showed me that page, in the creek, with the bilge pump clacking away like a patient clock, I'd felt nothing for a full minute. Just white. Then I'd felt the thing underneath the nothing, which was fury—not at the dead king, kings are weather, you can't hate weather—but at the shape of it. The *mechanism*. Because I recognized the mechanism. I'd been living inside a smaller version of it my whole life. Stay invisible. Stay unrecorded. Take salvage for people who'd rather not be named, and never ask why they'd rather not be named, because asking is how you stop being useful. I thought I'd chosen that life. Some part of me had inherited it like eye color.

* * *

By midmorning the reading had stopped being a reading and become something else. People weren't listening anymore; they were talking. Clustered around the printed copies, reading aloud to each other, finding their own surnames in the entries and shouting them to relatives across the stall. A woman from the net-menders' row stood on a crate holding page thirty overhead like a lantern and read the section on the oyster leases twice, once slow and once fast, and the second time the crowd repeated the worst lines back at her, all together, like a congregation.

Maren Oduya's press was running again. I could hear it from the dock—a steady iron heartbeat coming from behind the chandlery. She'd decided on her own to print a second run. Then, someone told me, a third.

And the harbor master came out of his office around noon, which mattered, because the harbor master is the Consortium's man, has been for twenty years, takes his instructions from the same gray coats that used to visit my boat. He walked down to stall eleven with a document in his hand and everyone held their breath thinking it was an injunction, and instead he cleared his throat and announced—in the flat voice of a man reading words he did not write—that effective immediately, pending inquiry, all Consortium collection activity in Saltmouth waters was suspended, and that the harbor office would receive statements from any party with claims arising from the historical record.

"Statements," somebody said. "You mean complaints."

"I mean statements," the harbor master said, with dignity, and went back inside, and I swear the door closed softer than usual.

* * *

Emeric found me at the rail of the Marrow in the late afternoon, after the crowd had thinned to stragglers and the ice-seller had begun charging people to pose next to his remaining stock.

"You were there," he said. "Back left. Gray hood."

"You saw me?"

"I've watched you disappear in open water, Petra. A crowd is nothing." He leaned on the rail beside me, and for a while we both looked at the harbor—the masts, the gulls working the fish waste, the Consortium launch sitting empty and idle at its berth like a dog whose whistle had stopped working. "Quist's launch provisioned this morning. She's taking the outside passage north. Gone by tomorrow, Maren says. Before the inquiry commissioners arrive."

"Running."

"Departing," Emeric said, "strategically." And then, quieter: "It's over. Or it's beginning, which amounts to the same thing but with more paperwork."

I nodded at the harbor office. "They'll want testimony. The commissioners. You'll give them everything."

"All of it. Every page." He glanced at me sideways. "Including yours."

"I know."

"They'll want you specifically. You found the cave. You carried the ring into Saltmouth. You ran the gorge—" He shook his head, and there was something in his face I hadn't seen before, something almost shy. "Petra, half the town watched you take that boat through standing water at flood peak. There are fishermen drawing charts of your line right now. Bad charts. Charts with the wrong bends. But they're drawing them."

"Then they'll draw them wrong," I said. "That channel's mine."

"It's your father's."

"It's mine now. He never finished it. I did." I heard my own voice come out harder than I meant, and then, unexpectedly, not hard at all: "He'd have liked the fish stall. The venue. He always said the truth keeps better on ice."

Emeric laughed—a real laugh, startled out of him—and for a moment neither of us said anything, and the harbor did its harbor business around us, and I realized my hood was down. Had been down for hours. Nobody had looked at me twice, and nobody had failed to look at me at all, and both of those things had happened and I was still standing there.

* * *

The summons came at dusk, and it came for both of us, and it came publicly, which I understood was deliberate.

Two clerks from the inquiry commission—advance riders ahead of the commissioners themselves, young, sea-bitten, carrying document tubes—walked the length of the commercial dock posting notices on the pilings. One of them, a round-faced girl with ink on her fingers, read the notice aloud in a clear carrying voice, the way news gets distributed in a town that trusts voices more than paper:

"By order of the Commission of Inquiry into the Historical Claims of the Vell: Emeric Vale, styling himself heir of the drowned line, is requested to appear and produce evidence. And—" she squinted at the second notice, and I watched her find my name on it, and I watched her decide to say it loudly— "*Petra Coldharbour, master of the vessel Marrow,* is requested to appear as witness to discovery, recovery, and events subsequent. Testimony to commence upon the commissioners' arrival within the week."

Witness. Captain. Coldharbour. Three words, posted on a piling, nailed there where the tide tables usually went.

I stood there long enough that the round-faced clerk got self-conscious and asked if I was the Coldharbour named, and I said, "I'm the Coldharbour named," and she wrote something in her book, probably *confirmed*, and moved on down the dock, and I stayed.

Here's what I expected to feel: nothing good. Exposure has always cost me something—sleep, appetite, the easy slide through a crowded dock. Being named meant being counted, and being counted meant being owed to and owing, and I'd built forty years on the principle that the safest number to be is zero.

But the feeling that came up through the planking wasn't fear. It was closer to what I feel when the tide turns—when the current that's been pushing against you all day quietly reverses and starts carrying you the direction you were going anyway. Nothing dramatic. Just the water deciding, at last, to help.

Emeric came back down the dock at full dark, carrying two bowls of fish stew from the eel-woman, who had refused his money on the grounds that he was "the reading man" and therefore fed free for life. He handed me mine and leaned on the rail and looked at the notice nailed to the piling, lit yellow by the dock lamp.

"They'll ask you hard questions," he said. "About the ring. About why you took it. About whether you intended to sell it."

"They will."

"And what will you tell them?"

I ate some stew. It was good—too much pepper, the way I like it. Out past the breakwater the evening tide was making, smooth and sure, the whole harbor swinging gently on its anchors to face the incoming water, every boat in Saltmouth turning together like a field of flowers following the sun.

"The truth," I said. "All of it. Even the parts where I was small." I looked at him. "That's the whole point of a ledger, isn't it? Somebody finally writing down what actually happened."

Emeric smiled and said nothing, because there was nothing to say, and we ate our stew in the lamplight while the tide came in beneath us, lifting the Marrow inch by inch off her fenders, until the nail in the piling let go of her hull with a small wooden sigh and she floated free.
