## 20. Salvage Rights

They brought the Marrow up on a Tuesday, on a gray tide that couldn't decide whether it wanted to be high or low, much like the rest of my life.

I stood on the salvage barge with my arms crossed and my jaw set, watching the crane crew work the slings under her hull while the diver made his last check. Forty-one days she'd lain in the shallows off Widow's Reach where we'd abandoned her—no, where I'd *sunk* her, there's a difference and I've learned to own the difference—forty-one days of current combing through her open ports and silt settling into her bilge like a debt collector who knows you're not going anywhere.

"She'll come up or she won't," said the salvage master, a broad woman named Orla Fenk with forearms like mooring line. "No promises on a boat that's been down that long. You understand that."

"I understand boats," I said.

Orla looked at me for a long moment, the way people had been looking at me since the reading at Saltmouth—the way they look at something they'd previously walked past without seeing. Before, when I was just another lean captain with a leaky ketch and no history worth mentioning, nobody had looked at all. Now every dockhand in the Vell delta knew my name and half of them knew my business, and I will tell you honestly: being seen is overrated right up until the moment you can't get it back. Then you learn to live in it, the way you learn to live with a scar.

"Then you know what she's worth," Orla said. "And what raising her costs."

"I do." And I did, to the coin, because I'd spent three nights doing arithmetic by lamplight before I signed her contract. The last of the recovered coins sat in a canvas bag under the floorboards of the room I rented above the chandlery—not enough to buy a new boat, not even close, but enough to raise an old one and put her back together if the old one had anything left to say about it.

The sling lines came taut. The winch groaned. Water poured off the Marrow's hull in sheets, brown and streaming with weed, and then her keel broke the surface and the whole barge seemed to hold its breath along with me.

She came up crooked and dripping, listing hard to port, her starboard rail stove in from the grounding and the gorge both. Her name was still legible on the transom, though, because I'd painted it deep the year I bought her, two coats and a prayer: MARROW. The word for the part of you that makes blood. The part that keeps going.

"There she is," Orla said, and there was something almost gentle in it. "Ugliest thing I've hauled in ten years."

"She's beautiful," I said, "and I'll thank you to remember that in your invoice."

* * *

The yard at Saltmouth took her the same afternoon—a tidal gridiron behind Brannock's shed, all mud and rusted cradles and the smell of pitch and old salt. Brannock himself was seventy if he was a day, mostly deaf, entirely unimpressed by recent events, which after six weeks of being a minor historical figure I found so restful I nearly wept.

"Bottom planks are done," he told me, tapping along her port side with a mallet, listening to her the way a doctor listens to a chest. "Here, here, here. Garboard seam's opened like a bad marriage. Rudder stock's bent. You ran her through the gorge, they tell me."

"I did."

"Hm." He tapped again. "Your father tried that run. Twenty-two years back. Everyone said he was mad."

"He wasn't mad," I said. "He was right. He just didn't have anyone on the pump."

Brannock grunted, which from him was a eulogy. "Cost you more than she's worth, doing this proper."

"Everything worth doing costs more than it's worth. That's how you know it's worth doing."

"That's not wisdom, girl, that's poverty talking."

"It can be both," I said, and he laughed, a dry rattle like a hawser running out, and got to work.

I paid him the first installment from the canvas bag, counting the coins into his palm one at a time because I wanted to feel each one leave. That was the point. Every coin I'd pulled from the King's Purse had been somebody's grief once—families named in a drowned ledger, loyalty sold cheap, silence bought dear. I wasn't going to spend them lightly. But I'd thought long and hard about what Emeric had argued in the cave, and what I'd come to was this: the money itself was never cursed. It was only ever what people did to keep it hidden. Spend it in daylight, on honest work, on a boat that carried witnesses instead of secrets—that was as close to restitution as a coin could get.

Besides. Quist was gone, the Consortium's charter claim had collapsed in a heap of documented theft, and the debt that had ridden my shoulders for nine years had died with it. Nobody held paper on me anymore. Nobody owned a piece of the Marrow but me.

It should have felt lighter. For the first week it mostly felt strange, like walking with a limp that's suddenly healed—you keep bracing for the pain that isn't coming.

* * *

Emeric found me on the third morning, sitting in the cradle shadow with sandpaper and a block, fairing the first new plank into place. He came down the gridiron in city clothes gone soft from river travel, carrying two cups of coffee from the market, and handed me one without asking, which by then he knew he could do.

"You're really doing it," he said. "Rebuilding her. I half expected you to take the insurance money and vanish. Start over somewhere no one's heard of Coldharbour."

"No such place anymore," I said. "That's rather the point of everything we did."

"Mm." He crouched beside the hull, looking up at her exposed ribs. "There's a hearing in the capital. Restitution commission. They're calling families, calling witnesses, calling—" he paused, delicately, "—captains. My solicitors think the ledger alone won't be enough. They want living testimony. People who were there when Quist came hunting. People who can speak to what the Consortium's collection practices actually looked like from the water."

"You want me to testify."

"I want to ask you to consider testifying. There's a difference, and I promised myself I'd stop assuming your answers."

I sanded. The plank whispered under the block. Below us the tide slid in around the gridiron legs, patient as everything else on the Vell.

"What's in it for me?" I asked, because I'd earned the right to ask ugly questions.

"Nothing material. I won't insult you with an offer. What's in it for—" he gestured, taking in the boat, the mud, the river beyond the sea wall, "—all of this, maybe. A delta where the Consortium doesn't own the water rights. Salvage licenses that don't require knowing the right people. A harbor master who doesn't look the other way for a fee." He shrugged. "A world where a girl learning her father's channel doesn't have to do it in secret."

"That's a lot to hang on one witness."

"It's a lot to hang on a hundred witnesses. You'd be one of a hundred." He smiled, tired and real. "But you'd be the one they listen to. You ran the gorge. You're the story they'll tell their grandchildren."

"I'm the story they're already telling in every tavern between here and Gullwatch, and half of it's wrong."

"Then come correct the record."

I set down the sandpaper and looked at him properly. Six weeks ago I'd have said yes or no inside a minute, whichever answer kept me smallest. Now I let the question sit, turned it over, felt its weight. That was new. That was mine.

"Not yet," I said. "The Marrow comes first. She's not seaworthy, I'm not leaving Saltmouth, and I'm not testifying to anything until I can arrive under my own power, on my own deck, having paid my own way. If I stand up in front of that commission, I stand up owing nobody."

Emeric studied me for a moment, and then he nodded slowly, like a man confirming a chart against the coastline.

"You know," he said, "for someone who spent her whole life staying invisible, you have a remarkable instinct for how to be seen properly."

"It's called doing things in order. Exiles never learn it—you people always want the ending before the middle."

"We do," he agreed. "It's a family trait. We want the treasury before the war's even lost." He drained his coffee and stood. "The invitation stands. The hearings run through the season. And Petra—" he hesitated at the foot of the gridiron, and for a second he looked less like a claimant to a drowned throne and more like a man who'd spent a monsoon on a bilge pump, "—when the Marrow floats, there's a larger thing I'm building. Restitution isn't a hearing, it's a generation of work. Maps, licenses, schools for pilots who aren't born to the trade. I'll need people who read water better than any surveyor. I'd want it to be a partnership, not employment. Think about it. No deadline. I've learned my lesson about deadlines."

He left me with the coffee and the plank and the sound of the tide coming in.

Partnership. The word sat in my chest, warm and dangerous. A year ago it would have meant a rope around my neck with a friendly face holding the other end. Now it meant—what? Standing in front of rooms. Being counted. Being *needed*, which is its own kind of exposure, worse than debt sometimes, because you can pay off debt but you can't unsay yes.

I picked up the sandpaper again.

Not yet. But I didn't say no either, and we both knew it, and the door stayed open behind him like a draft from the sea.

* * *

By the end of the month the Marrow stood upright on the gridiron with new garboard strakes and a rudder stock straightened true, and Brannock let me caulk her seams myself, on the theory—and I agree with it—that a captain should know every inch of her hull with her hands, not just her eyes. I drove oakum until my palms blistered and poured hot pitch until my forearms were speckled with burns, and every evening I climbed the ladder to my rented room and counted what remained of the coins, fewer each week, each one converted into plank and fastening and paint.

The last coin went to Brannock on a bright cold morning, exchanged for a fresh topsail and the balance of his bill. He weighed it, bit it out of ancient habit, and squinted at me.

"Treasure money," he said. Not a question.

"Recovered property," I said. "Spent lawfully."

"On a boat."

"On *my* boat."

He pocketed the coin and jerked his chin at the gridiron, where the Marrow waited on the ways with fresh paint drying, white rail gleaming, her name renewed in letters deeper than before. "Launch her tomorrow, then. Tide turns at seven. I'll flood the ways."

I stood there a while after he'd gone, looking at her. Nine years I'd kept that boat alive on patches and luck and silence, and I'd told myself it was because I loved her, and some of that was even true. But the rest of the truth was that a patched boat keeps you humble, and a humble woman asks no questions, and a woman who asks no questions stays invisible, and invisible was the only safety I'd ever believed in.

The Marrow had nearly died of my invisibility twice—once in the mangrove creek, once in the gorge. Rebuilding her plank by plank with treasure money in full view of the whole harbor was the most visible act of my life, and it hadn't killed me yet. That was the lesson, if there was one. Visibility didn't sink you. Hiding did, slow, in shallow water, where nobody could even find the wreck.

Tomorrow, seven o'clock, the tide would turn and the ways would flood and the Marrow would take the river under her again.

And two days after that, according to the letter that had arrived with the morning's post—thick cream paper, a capital postmark, Emeric's precise hand—the restitution commission would sit for the first time, and the list of witnesses would be posted at the Saltmouth customs house, and my name would be on it whether I'd said yes yet or not.

I read the letter twice on the rail of the unfinished boat, the river running gold below me toward the sea.

Then I went to find Brannock, and asked him to move the launch up to dawn.
