## 21. High Water Mark

The tide at Gullwatch Point that morning was the most ordinary tide I'd ever been glad to see.

Ordinary. That's a word I used to distrust. Ordinary meant no surprises, and no surprises meant no salvage, and no salvage meant the Consortium's arithmetic closing another finger around my throat. For thirty years I'd prayed for extraordinary water the way other people prayed for rain in drought—desperately, guiltily, with one eye on what it might cost.

Now I stood at the Marrow's wheel and watched an unremarkable mid-morning tide lap against the cliffs below Gullwatch like it had never done anything remarkable in its life, and I felt something I didn't have a name for at first. It took me most of the channel from Saltmouth to work it out.

It was relief. The cave was sealed. The King's Purse was a purse with its drawstring pulled tight again, holding whatever it held, giving nothing back. The sea had made its one unprecedented gesture, taken its toll in nearly everything I owned, and gone back to being the sea.

"You're smiling," Emeric had said to me once, somewhere around chapter nine of our acquaintance, when we were both too tired and too scared to lie well. "I've been trying to picture it for weeks."

He'd seen it eventually. Mostly at inconvenient moments. But this morning there was nobody on deck to see it, so I let it spread all the way across my face, which is a luxury a captain forgets she has.

The rebuilt Marrow rode sweet. That's not sentiment—that's reportage. Salvors had raised her off the shingle below the gorge in three days flat, mostly because half the fishing families who'd watched us shoot the far current showed up with hawsers and opinions and wouldn't leave until she floated. Old Maren Tull, who'd hated my father for reasons neither of them ever explained to me, stood waist-deep in the surf guiding her stern onto the cradle and said, "She's got good bones, Coldharbour. Your father built her stubborn on purpose." Which was the closest thing to an apology anyone in that family had offered in two generations, and I took it the way you take a fish thrown at your head: gratefully, and watching your hands.

Six weeks of rebuilding. New planking below the waterline where the snag had opened her, new frames where the gorge had beaten her, a new bilge pump that Emeric had insisted on buying with his own money—"call it rent," he'd said, "six weeks of sleeping aboard while I pretended I was helping"—and a fresh coat of black over everything, so she looked less like a wreck that had learned to swim and more like a boat again.

I'd paid for it myself. Every plank, every nail, every hour of the shipwright's labor, out of the last of the coins from the lead chest. Not all of them—the ledger had first claim, and after it the restitution fund, and after that the families named in its pages, the ones still living, the ones who'd spent eighty years being quietly robbed by a charter claim built on stolen treasury. But what remained after all that was enough. Enough to raise the Marrow. Enough to rebuild her. Enough to pay off, coin by counted coin, every copper of what I owed—and to do it standing upright, in daylight, at the Consortium's own counting-house window, sliding the last purse across the counter to a clerk who kept looking at my face like he was trying to place me.

"Captain Coldharbour," he'd finally said, and written it down. Written *my* name down, in a ledger of his own, as though I were a fact worth recording.

I'd walked out of there lighter than I'd felt since I was nineteen years old, and I'd spent the whole walk back to the dock waiting for the feeling to curdle into something else, because good feelings always did, in my experience. They hadn't yet. Six weeks, and they hadn't. Maybe that was the real miracle of that season—not the low tide, not the cave, not even the far current. Maybe it was that the good thing held.

* * *

Gullwatch Point slid past to starboard, its cliffs brown-green with monsoon-fed grass, the gulls wheeling their usual arguments overhead. Below the tideline, invisible, the cave mouth waited under six fathoms of ordinary green water. I found myself looking anyway. You don't spend a lifetime reading water without developing some habits you can't break.

There was a new marker buoy on the point now—a survey boat had come through two weeks ago, official-looking, taking soundings, asking questions. The chart would be redrawn by winter. Somewhere in a hydrographic office, a cartographer was drawing in the King's Purse and the deep channel beyond it, and somewhere in the margin, if the fishing families had their way, a note about the far current: *local knowledge advised; channel confirmed by Captain P. Coldharbour, salvage.*

Confirmed by Captain P. Coldharbour. My father had died trying to put that channel on a chart. It had taken his daughter running it at monsoon peak in a holed ketch to finish the job, and even then only because half the delta was standing on the shore watching her do it. He'd have laughed himself sick. Or cried. With him it was usually both, which is why I'd inherited his habit of doing my crying alone, at night, at anchor, with the pump handle for company.

Not tonight, though. Tonight I had a grave to visit.

* * *

The churchyard above Saltmouth is a scruffy terrace of wind-bent stones facing the water, because the fishermen who sleep there wanted to keep an eye on their boats even after they'd stopped needing to. My father's stone sits near the seaward wall, third row, where you can hear the harbor bell on a clear day. I'd planted rosemary there years ago because it survives neglect, which seemed like the right flower for our family.

I brought a flask of tea and sat down on the grass beside him, the way I used to when I was small and he was working on the boat and I'd come to report on my day whether he wanted the report or not.

"Morning, Da," I said. "Or afternoon. You know how it is."

A gull screamed something unhelpful. I poured tea into the cap of the flask and set it on the stone, because he'd have wanted his share, and drank from the flask itself.

"So. The far current." I looked out at the harbor, at the Marrow riding at her mooring, black and stubborn and floating. "You were right. About all of it. The channel runs east of the standing wave, not west—your chart was right and everyone who called you a drunk was wrong, and I'm sorry it took me twenty years and nearly dying to prove it. The rockfall has shifted since your time, there's a fin of stone just under the surface at the second bend that isn't on anything you drew, but the deep water's where you said. Dead center of the gorge, hugging the far bank until the third bend, then hard to port. You could run it blind. I nearly did."

The tea was too hot and too sweet, the way he'd always made it, sugar being cheaper than milk in our house. I drank it anyway.

"I lost the boat doing it," I told him. "Well. Nearly lost. She came apart in the calm water past the gorge, which—if you're going to founder, Da, that's the place, I recommend it highly. And then I swam ashore in front of forty witnesses, which you'd have hated, because you taught me that the best thing a Coldharbour can be is hard to find. And here's the thing." I turned the flask in my hands. "Here's the thing I've been turning over for six weeks."

Forty witnesses. Quist couldn't disappear forty witnesses, and that was why we'd lived—but it was also why everything had changed. I'd spent my whole life believing that safety was the same thing as invisibility. Be small, be quiet, be nobody, and nobody comes for you. And then I'd spent one season discovering that being nobody had never actually protected me—it had just made me easier to squeeze, because nobody notices when you scream. The Consortium had financed my loan precisely because I was invisible. Quist had cornered me precisely because I was alone. Every year of staying unseen had bought me exactly nothing except the loneliness, and the loneliness had cost extra.

"It turns out," I told my father, "that the safest place on this river is standing in front of everybody with your name spelled right. Who knew. Certainly not you. Certainly not me. We could have saved ourselves thirty years."

The gull landed on the wall and regarded me with the frank appraisal of a creature that has never once worried about its reputation.

"Emeric sends his regards," I said. "He's in Saltmouth most days, at the hearings. They've got lawyers now, actual lawyers, men in coats reading the ledger aloud in rooms with windows, and every family the old dynasty cheated gets to stand up and say their piece. The Coldharbours are on the list. Did you know that? Great-grandfather Aldous sold the fleet's loyalty for a payment in silence, and the silence ran downhill for four generations until it pooled in me. Well. It's draining now. There'll be restitution—probably not much, probably late, probably wrapped in so much procedure it'll take years to untangle, but it'll come, and it'll come *openly*, which is the part that matters. Nobody's paying us to shut up anymore. Quite the opposite."

I finished the tea. Down in the harbor, the afternoon light was going long and gold across the water, and the Marrow swung gently on her mooring like a dog that knows it's nearly walk time.

"I'm keeping the boat," I said. "Obviously. Rebuilt her myself, mostly. Maren Tull helped, which you'd find funnier than I did. And I'm still running salvage—small jobs, honest ones, the kind where nobody asks me to lose anything I can't afford to lose. But I'm taking passengers now. Proper ones. Paying ones." I paused. "That's the other thing I came to tell you."

Because here was the truth underneath the truth: for thirty years I had refused passengers, and I'd told myself it was because passengers were trouble, and that was even partly true. But the deeper reason was that every passenger was a question I couldn't answer—who are you, who sent you, what do you want—and questions were how traps closed. So I'd checked everyone. Dock gossip, harbor records, the shape of their hands, the cut of their clothes. I'd turned away a man in city clothes once, at the start of all this, because he watched me too closely, and it turned out that man was Emeric Vale, and it turned out that refusing him had delayed the whole catastrophe by approximately one week.

Vigilance had its uses. Vigilance had also cost me thirty years of company.

"So I've made a rule," I informed the gravestone. "New rule. I check the weather, I check the water, I check the boat, and I check the money. Those I check. People—" I shrugged, and the gull shifted its weight, unimpressed. "People get the benefit of the doubt. One benefit. Doubt included free of charge."

My father said nothing, being dead, but the harbor bell rang twice, which I chose to take as agreement, because grief gives you certain licenses and I intended to use mine.

* * *

I went down to the dock as the light failed, and that's when it happened—the thing I'd been circling all day without quite saying aloud, even to a man who couldn't repeat it.

There was a woman waiting at the foot of the Marrow's gangplank. Fifties maybe, traveling coat, one bag, salt-gray hair pinned up in a style that said she'd once cared very much and had since made her peace. She wasn't watching me the way Emeric had watched me that first night—no hunger in it, no calculation. She was watching the boat, with an expression I recognized because I'd worn it myself at the rail of the light station: the look of someone seeing a thing they'd only ever heard described.

"Captain Coldharbour?" she said.

"That depends on the business."

"My name is Odile Ferran. I need passage upriver. To Vellmouth, and then the upper reaches, as far as you go." She named a price that was fair—generous, even, but not suspiciously so, which I noted and approved of. Then she reached into her coat and produced papers, laid them out on the bollard where I could read them: a trading license, letters of reference, a bill of lading for cargo already arranged. Everything in order. Everything true, as far as I could tell, and I've spent a lifetime telling how far I can tell.

I read the papers. I looked at her hands—working hands, honest calluses, no rings. I looked at the harbor behind her, empty of launches, empty of black sails, empty of Dame Sable Quist, who had departed Saltmouth rather than face inquiry and was, by every report, someone else's jurisdiction's problem now.

Old Petra would have asked who sent her. Old Petra would have walked the docks that evening, buying drinks and answers, checking the woman's name against three sources and her face against a fourth. Old Petra would have found some thread to pull, and pulled it, and discovered that everyone is hiding something, and refused the fare, and sailed alone, and called it safety.

I folded the papers and handed them back.

"Deckhand rules apply," I said. "Life vest in the locker. You break it, you bought it. We cast off at dawn."

Something flickered across her face—surprise, maybe, or relief, or the particular expression of a person who has heard stories about a captain who says no and is meeting the revised edition.

"That's it?" she said. "No questions?"

"Oh, I've got questions." I stepped aside to let her aboard. "I'll ask them over dinner, like a civilized person. But the papers are good, the money's good, and the tide's good. Rest is your own business until it becomes mine."

She smiled—tired, genuine, grateful—and picked up her bag, and as she crossed the gangplank I caught myself doing something I hadn't done in thirty years of command:

I held out my hand to help her over the rail.

We cast off at dawn, as promised, with the morning fog lifting off the Vell in long silver rags and the current running easy beneath us. Odile Ferran stood at the rail watching the water go by, and I stood at the wheel watching her watch it, and I thought: *this is what it costs, trusting. Nothing. It costs nothing. It was the cheapest thing on the river all along and I priced it like gold.*

Past Gullwatch Point the cliffs fell away astern, and beneath six fathoms of ordinary, honest, unremarkable tide, the King's Purse kept its silence. Let it. Some things belong underwater. What mattered was on the surface now—in a ledger being read aloud in lit rooms, in a channel drawn on charts with my name in the margin, in a rebuilt boat running easy with a stranger aboard whose secrets were, for once, none of my business until they became so.

The river bent north. I followed it.

And that was when I saw the letter waiting in the wheelhouse—slid under the door sometime in the night, Saltmouth postmark, Emeric's cramped handwriting on the front, and one line visible through the envelope's thin paper that stopped my breath for half a second before I'd even broken the seal:

*Petra—they've asked for you by name.*
