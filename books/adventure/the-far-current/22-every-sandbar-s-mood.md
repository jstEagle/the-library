## 22. Every Sandbar's Mood

The letter came on a Tuesday, which meant nothing on the Vell except that the fish market at Brackwater ran long and the channel was crowded with boats that didn't know how to read a falling tide. I took the Marrow out past the moorings anyway, because I'd learned something in the last few months about not hiding, and one of the things I hadn't learned yet was how to sit still in a harbor while people pointed.

Not that they pointed much anymore. That was the strange part. For thirty years I had built my life like a hull below the waterline—sound, necessary, and never once seen unless something went wrong. Now the fishwives of Brackwater nodded to me by name. The lock-keeper waved me through without the usual inspection. A boy of maybe ten asked me if it was true I'd run the gorge at flood peak, and when I said it was true enough, he looked at me the way I used to look at charts of places no one had ever charted.

I still wasn't sure I liked it. But I'd stopped flinching from it, and that was more than I'd ever managed before.

The letter itself was thick paper, city paper, with Emeric Vale's handwriting slanting across it like a man writing against a deadline—which he always was. *Petra,* it began, no title, no ceremony. He'd learned that much about me at least.

*The restitution hearings begin on the first of the month at Saltmouth. Quist has retained counsel—three firms, which tells you what she thinks is at stake. The Consortium's charter claim is being challenged on the strength of the ledger, but documents don't testify. People do.*

*I need you there. Not as a favor to me—I've stopped asking for those, as you may have noticed. As a witness. You found the King's Purse. You held the ledger. You saw what Quist offered and what she did to get it. There isn't another soul alive who can speak to all three, and the tribunal will want to hear it from someone who isn't me. They'll want to hear it from someone with nothing to gain.*

*I know what you're thinking. You have plenty to lose. Your name is attached now, whether you wanted it or not—the gorge run made sure of that. But here's the arithmetic I've learned since Saltmouth: invisibility was never free either. It cost you the Marrow once. It cost your father everything. It's costing you still, every morning you wake up and check the horizon out of habit.*

*Come testify. Come be seen on purpose this time.*

*There's a berth reserved at the customs quay. No one will board her without my say-so. And Petra—if you don't come, I'll understand. I'll even believe you when you tell me it's the tide's fault.*

*But I'll know better.*

*—E.V.*

I read it twice at the rail, then a third time with the paper flat on the chart table where the light was better, though the light had nothing to do with it. Outside, the Vell did what the Vell always does at slack water—went glassy and indifferent, holding its secrets the way it had held the dynasty's treasury for two hundred years, and my father for twenty-five, and every other thing the river takes and doesn't explain.

My father. I thought about him more lately than I had in years, and not the way I used to—not the flinch, not the old wound of a girl watching a boat go into the gorge and nothing come out. I thought about him steering. I thought about the channel he'd died trying to find, the far current, the deep clean lane through the flooded rockfall that every pilot swore didn't exist. I'd found it. I'd run it with my boat coming apart under me and a stranger pumping bilge behind me, and I'd come out the other side into calmer water and a different life.

He would have hated the hearings. He'd have told me to stay invisible, stay small, stay alive. He'd also have been wrong, and I think somewhere down in whatever deep water the dead keep, he knew it. He went into the gorge looking for a channel no chart recorded. That wasn't the act of a man who wanted to be unseen. That was the act of a man who wanted to find something true and put it on the map.

I folded the letter along its creases and put it in the drawer with the salvage log—the same drawer where the signet ring had sat for one bad night in Saltmouth, back when I still believed I could pocket history and pawn it without anyone noticing. The ring was gone now, returned to the tribunal as evidence, catalogued and photographed and entered into the record under my name. *Recovered by Petra Coldharbour, master of the ketch Marrow.* My name, in an official document, doing something honest. It had taken me thirty years and a drowned dynasty to manage it.

The tide had turned while I read. I could feel it before I could see it—a change in the way the Marrow lay against her mooring lines, a faint chatter of current along the hull, the first suggestion that the river had made up its mind about the afternoon. Every sandbar has its mood. My father taught me that before he taught me letters: the bar off Gullwatch that shows its teeth at ebb and goes gentle at flood; the snag field below the fueling lock that shifts after every big rain like a man turning over in his sleep; the long shallow reach above Brackwater where the water goes the color of pewter and lies to you about its depth. No chart records any of it properly. Charts are drawn by surveyors who visit a place once and write down what it was doing that day. I live with these waters. I know them the way you know a person—by their moods, their lies, the things they do when they think no one's watching.

For most of my life I'd assumed I was the same kind of creature as the water. Uncharted. Best known by staying unread.

Emeric's letter sat in the drawer like a lead chest with a cipher on it.

Here's what nobody tells you about being invisible: it works right up until it doesn't, and then it works against you. All those years of running dark, of taking salvage for people who paid cash and used no names, of keeping my head down and my papers clean—it bought me time, nothing else. When Quist came for me, she came with papers too, and mine were thinner than hers. When the flood took the Marrow, there was no one who knew where to look, because I'd spent three decades making sure no one knew where to look. Invisibility didn't save me. What saved me was witnesses—fishing families on the bank who watched a madwoman run the gorge and lived to describe it, dozens of eyes that Quist couldn't buy or drown or disappear. Safety turned out to be the opposite of what I'd practiced my whole life. Safety was being seen by many eyes.

And now Emeric wanted me to walk into a hearing room full of the largest collection of official eyes on the coast and open my mouth.

I went up on deck. The flood was making properly now, the current swinging the Marrow's bow toward the sea, toward Saltmouth, forty miles downriver and a lifetime away. The customs quay. The tribunal. Counsel for three firms, all of them paid to make people like me sound like liars or fools or worse, accomplices. I knew how those rooms worked without ever having been in one. Rooms like that are built to make you feel the way I'd felt my whole life anyway—small, watched, presumed guilty of wanting something.

But I'd run the gorge. I'd stood in a flooded cave with coins fused into coral and read my own great-grandfather's name in a ledger of the betrayed, and I'd come up from both and kept breathing. What was a hearing room, next to that? A room with worse lighting and drier feet.

Still, I stood at the rail a long while.

The thing about the Vell at flood tide is that it looks like it's going nowhere. The surface smooths out, the current runs deep and steady beneath, and a stranger would swear the river was at rest. Only a pilot reads the truth in it—that the whole mass of the water is moving, committed, carrying everything on it toward the sea whether the sea's ready or not. I used to think I was fighting that current, all those years. Running salvage against the grain, keeping myself apart, paying for my privacy coin by coin. Standing at the rail just then, I finally understood I'd been in the current the entire time. Everything moves. The only choice you get is whether you steer.

A gull came off the water and hung above the mast, screaming its idiot head off, and I took that for an omen only because I needed one and the river wasn't offering anything subtler.

"All right," I told it. "All right."

I cast off.

Not fleeing—I want that on the record, even if the record is just me and a gull. Every other time I'd pointed the Marrow's bow at Saltmouth, I'd been running. Running Quist, running the debt, running the truth of what I'd taken from the King's Purse. This time the tide was fair, the engine was warm, and the letter in the drawer was an invitation. An arrival. There's a difference, and you can feel it in the hull, the way she answers the helm when the helmsman isn't afraid. A boat knows. Mine had spent weeks under my hands being rebuilt plank by plank—her old bones salvaged off the mud where the gorge spat her out, her seams re-caulked, her engine rebuilt with the last of the recovered coins—and she knew the difference between a flight and a passage the way I knew the mood of a sandbar. She settled into the current like a woman sitting down in her own chair.

Brackwater fell astern. The pewter shallows gave us room. Off Gullwatch Point the cliffs stood bare and ordinary at high water, the King's Purse sealed beneath the sea again, keeping its emptied pockets to itself. I saluted it anyway, one hand off the wheel, because you honor the places that changed you even when they won't remember you back.

By midafternoon the customs quay at Saltmouth was in sight, and so was the crowd—or what passes for a crowd in a working port: stevedores pausing with crates on their shoulders, a knot of children on the bollards, a woman selling roasted chestnuts who pointed me out to her neighbor with her chin. News travels faster than boats on this coast. The hearings started tomorrow, and here came the captain who ran the gorge, arriving a day early, in daylight, at the busiest quay in the harbor.

Let them look. That was the whole lesson, wasn't it, purchased at the price of a boat, a father, thirty years of silence: let them look. Eyes that see you are eyes that can't unsee you, and a woman seen by many is a woman who can't be quietly erased.

I brought the Marrow alongside with the current, kissed the fenders against the stone, and made her fast—bow line, stern line, spring—neat as a Sunday. The quay-master came down with his book, and instead of the usual squinting suspicion he said, "Berth's reserved for you, Captain Coldharbour. Paid in advance." Paid by whom, I didn't ask. I knew whose money it was, and for once the debt didn't feel like a hook. It felt like a hand extended across a rail.

Emeric was waiting at the top of the stairs. City clothes gone, replaced by salt-stained canvas that fit him better than his tailoring ever had—he'd learned something on the Marrow too, apparently, besides bilge pumps and fear. He didn't come down to help with the lines, which I appreciated. He knew I didn't need help with lines. He just stood there in the evening light with the ledger's consequences hanging off him like rigging, and when I reached the top step he said:

"You came."

"Tide was fair," I said.

He smiled, because he knew better, and I let him know better, and that was new too—being known, and standing still for it.

"They'll call you tomorrow," he said. "First witness after the documentary evidence. Quist's counsel will try to tear you apart. They'll say you're a smuggler, a thief, a woman who sold her testimony."

"They'd be half right," I said. "About the smuggling, anyway. The rest I gave back."

"Will you be all right in there?"

I looked past him, down the quay, at the Marrow riding easy on the flood, at the water beyond her running steady and deep toward the open sea, carrying everything on it, going somewhere whether the world was ready or not. Somewhere in that water was a channel no chart had ever recorded, found by a dead man's daughter and named for no one. Tomorrow I'd stand in a bright room and say my name out loud—Coldharbour, master of the Marrow, witness—and the saying of it wouldn't shrink me. That was the trick my father never got to learn, and the one thing I could give him now, thirty years late, from the far side of the gorge.

"I've been torn apart by better than lawyers," I said. "Ask the river."

Below us, the tide turned, and the whole harbor swung gently to meet it—every hull, every mooring, every boat in Saltmouth coming round together like a fleet deciding. I felt the Marrow tug at her lines, patient, ready, reading the water the way I'd taught her, or she'd taught me; by then I honestly couldn't say which.

Tomorrow they'd ask me who I was.

For the first time in my life, I had an answer I wasn't afraid to give.
