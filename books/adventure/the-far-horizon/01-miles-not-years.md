## 1. Miles, Not Years

The bridge had been dead fifteen years, and it creaked like something alive when Tamsin Ashgrove walked its spine.

She moved the way she always did on old iron—weight low, hands loose, listening through her boots. Below her, the Ashen River ran brown and slow over the pilings, carrying silt from the high country down toward the coast. Above her, the sky was doing that thing autumn skies did in the corridor: going silver at the edges, like tin left out in the rain. The Merrow Line's trestle stretched away north into the hills, rails long since stripped for scrap, ties rotted to soft black lace. Somewhere past those hills, forty miles as the gull flew and a hundred as the tunnel wound, lay the Far Horizon pass.

Tamsin did not look north. She looked at the rivet heads under her fingers, counting them without meaning to, an old habit from the days when counting rivets meant knowing whether a span would hold a train or fold beneath one.

"Tamsin!" Pell's voice came up the ladder well, bright as a struck bell. "Brannigan says if you don't come eat while it's hot, he's giving your portion to the gulls."

"Tell him the gulls are welcome to it," she called back, though she was already moving toward the ladder, because Brannigan's threats were never empty and his stew was never worth missing.

* * *

The crew yard sat on a shelf of flat ground where the river bent, half boatyard and half junkyard, smelling of tar and woodsmoke and the iron-and-weed smell of standing water. The *Meridian* rode at her moorings off the near bank—a squat pump barge with a crane arm folded like a sleeping heron, her hull patched in so many places she looked quilted. Their other gear lay scattered across the yard: diving helmets hung on pegs like strange brass heads waiting for bodies, air hoses coiled in baskets, survey instruments wrapped in oilcloth against the damp.

Brannigan had set the stew pot on two stones near the fire ring, because he claimed food tasted better outdoors and refused all evidence to the contrary. He was a big man gone soft at the middle, forearms still hard as dock rope, with grey coming into his beard in patches that made him look weathered rather than old. He ladled without being asked, which was how he said most things.

"Bridge is sound?" he asked.

"Sound enough for another fifty years of nobody using it." Tamsin took the bowl. "The footings on the east bank are cracked, but they've been cracked since before I was born. The river's done all the moving it means to do there."

"That's what you said about the Kelder viaduct."

"The Kelder viaduct fell down."

"After you signed the survey saying it wouldn't."

"It fell down *after*, didn't it?" Pell said, grinning around a mouthful of stew. "That's the whole trick of it. Nothing ever falls while Tam's watching it."

Tamsin let that pass, the way she let most things about herself pass. Pell was twenty-two, all elbows and questions, with a diver's shoulders already building under a boy's frame and a shock of hair he cut himself with a knife, badly. He'd come to them three years ago off a herring boat, half-starved and full of talk, and Brannigan had fed him once and that had been that—the way a stray dog decides on a house. Now he could run a surface-supplied dive better than men twice his age, and he asked questions the way other people breathed.

"Why do you think they pulled the rails?" he asked now, gesturing with his spoon back up the line. "The Merrow, I mean. The bridges are good. You just said so yourself."

"Steel was worth money after the war."

"But the tunnels—" Pell shook his head. "A tunnel's worth more than steel. You can't melt a tunnel down. Somebody spent a fortune cutting through the Far Horizon range, and then everybody just... agreed to forget it existed. That doesn't make sense."

"Pell." Brannigan's voice, mild but with weight behind it, the voice he used to stop arguments at the table.

"What? It's a fair question."

"It's a question with no answer worth having," Tamsin said, and kept her tone level and bored, which took practice, because it wasn't either of those things. "Railroads close. Companies fail. Men move on. That's the whole story."

Pell looked at her with those open brown eyes, and she saw him decide—not for the first time—to believe her. That was the thing about Pell: he wanted the people around him to be honest so badly that he accepted honesty on credit. Someday someone would hand him counterfeit truth and he'd take it to the bank without checking the serial numbers. Tamsin hoped it wouldn't be her handing it.

It would be. She already knew that, in the place where she kept such knowledge.

"Eat," she said. "We're on the tide tomorrow."

* * *

Evening came down the valley like water filling a bowl. Tamsin took the ledger book into the wheelhouse of the *Meridian*—her office, her refuge, the only room anywhere with her back to a wall and a view of every approach—and worked the day's figures by lamplight. The Farbridge survey had paid thin; the county council drove hard bargains with anyone willing to work the closed corridor, reasoning, not unreasonably, that only desperate crews would go there.

Desperate was a strong word. Comfortable, Tamsin thought, was closer. Invisible, closest of all. A crew that surveyed dead bridges and dredged small harbors drew no eyes. Nobody asked where they'd come from. Nobody asked why Tamsin Ashgrove could read a tidal chart the way priests read scripture, or why she went quiet—really quiet, a silence with a floor that dropped out of it—whenever anyone mentioned the pass upcountry, or the railway, or the word *Merrow* spoken plain instead of as the name of a fish.

Fifteen years of miles, and not one of them a year she could count honestly. That was the arithmetic of her life. Distance instead of time. She had measured everything since the collapse in miles put between herself and the Far Horizon, and the terrible joke of it—the joke she never laughed at—was that the work she was best at in the world kept bringing her back to water. Water over stone. Water over things that had been built and then drowned. As if some part of her were circling the wreck of her own history the way a diver circles a hull, tapping, listening for hollow places.

The knock came at the wheelhouse door just after full dark.

Courier boys didn't come up the river track at night. Tamsin marked her page, blew out nothing—she left the lamp burning—and slid the survey knife from the drawer beside her knee before she opened the door. Habit. Fifteen years of habit.

He was young, mud to the knees, holding a document case out at arm's length like something that might bite. "Delivery for the crew of the *Meridian*. Signature required."

"Who sent it?"

"Hale Consolidated Salvage, ma'am. I'm to wait for the mark."

Hale.

The name went through her like cold water through a wetsuit. Corwin Hale owned more salvage tonnage than any three companies on the coast, and he had built it the way barnacles build—by being everywhere first, fastening on hard, and never letting go. She had met him exactly once, seven years ago at a harbor commission dinner she'd attended for the fee, and he'd shaken her hand and held it a half-second too long and said, *Ashgrove. Any relation to the Merrow Ashgroves?* and she had smiled and said *no relation* and left early, and had thought about it for a month.

Now he knew exactly where she was. Which meant he had known for a while.

She signed. The boy touched his cap and was gone into the dark, and she stood a moment on the deck plates listening to his footsteps fade down the plank road, feeling the crack come into her invisibility the way ice cracks—one fine white line, silent, spreading.

Inside the case: a contract, heavy paper, wax seal. Hale Consolidated Salvage retained the services of the crew of the barge *Meridian* for salvage and recovery operations at the site of the former Merrow Line, Far Horizon pass, section eleven, flooded galleries. Compensation generous. Terms confidential. Duration six tides from commencement.

Six tides. Someone had done their hydrology. Six tides was the window before the spring equinox swells pushed the pass past working depth—any competent surveyor knew it, and Corwin Hale employed the best surveyors money could buy. But the window also meant urgency. It meant he wanted divers down in those drowned tunnels within the week, and out again with whatever he was after before the sea closed the door.

And there was only one thing in the Far Horizon worth a fleet's mobilization. Everyone knew the story, even if everyone pretended they didn't: the Merrow pay-train, caught in section eleven when the mountain came down, sealed in the flood with a season's payroll aboard. Gold and banknotes and the company's strongboxes, sunk in the dark for fifteen years. Salvage men toasted it the way sailors toast mermaids—something beautiful, something impossible, something that had drowned better men than them.

Tamsin read the contract twice by the lamp. It was clean. Too clean. No mention of the collapse itself, no clause touching liability or inquiry, nothing that smelled of courts or coroners. Just the wreck, the gold, the window, the money. Justice, Hale had told a newspaper once, talking about another matter, *is what happens when the right man holds the ledger.* She wondered which man Hale considered right, and what page of whose ledger this contract was torn from.

Because it was torn from a ledger. She could feel it. A man like Hale didn't spend coin on a drowned payroll out of sentiment for the drowned. He wanted the gold before anyone else reached it—or he wanted something else entirely, and the gold was the bait, and she was the fish he'd chosen, and he had chosen her for reasons written in ink she hadn't seen yet.

She carried the contract up onto the open deck. The night was clear and cold; the stars over the northern hills were hard and close. Somewhere beyond them, forty miles of dead rail and a hundred miles of drowned tunnel away, the Far Horizon pass held its black water over its black secret, and her mentor's bones, and the last conversation they'd ever had—which had been an argument, and which Tamsin would give ten years of her life to have over again.

Brannigan found her there. He came up quietly, the way big men can when they decide to, and stood beside her looking at the paper in her hands.

"Hale," he said, reading the letterhead upside down. He was quiet a moment. "You going to tell me what you're thinking?"

"No."

"Didn't think so." He scratched under his beard. "Pell will want to know what it says."

"Pell wants to know what everything says."

"Aye. And someday that'll get him drowned or rich." Brannigan looked north, at the hills, and Tamsin watched his jaw work like a man chewing words before swallowing them. "You want my counsel, girl, and you don't, but here it is anyway. That pass has been calling your name for fifteen years. I've watched you walk away from it in four different counties. Paper like that isn't a job offer. It's a hook. And hooks don't care whether the fish bites today or next year. Only whether it bites."

"I know," Tamsin said.

She held the contract over the brazier by the rail, the coals banked but still red-hearted, and fed the corner of the page to them. The heavy paper curled and browned and caught. She watched Corwin Hale's seal blister and go black, watched the generous compensation and the confidential terms and the six-tide window curl away into ash that lifted on the river wind and scattered out over the dark water, and she felt—for exactly the length of time it took the last corner to burn—like a woman who had won.

Then she felt the other thing. The hook hadn't been in the paper. The paper had only shown her the shape of it, the way a shadow shows the shape of the thing that casts it. Hale knew who she was. Hale knew where she was. And a man like that did not send a courier sixty miles up a dead river at night merely to be refused.

The ash drifted north, toward the hills, toward the pass.

Tamsin stood a long time after the fire had eaten everything there was to eat, and Brannigan stood with her, saying nothing, because that was the kind of family they were. Behind them in the yard, Pell's lamp still burned in the bunkhouse, and she could hear him faintly through the wall, asking the empty room one of his endless questions, the way he always did before sleep took him.

*Tam,* he'd asked her once, weeks ago, watching her chart a tide table by lamplight. *If you could go anywhere you wanted—anywhere at all—where would you go?*

Anywhere at all, she had almost answered. Anywhere but north.

The lamp in the bunkhouse went out. The river ran on toward the sea, patient as everything else that intended to arrive eventually.

Three days later, Corwin Hale's steam launch came up the Ashen River with its paddles turning slow and sure, and Corwin Hale stepped off onto the *Meridian*'s deck carrying not money, but a single page of an old ledger, and the past stopped being avoidable.
