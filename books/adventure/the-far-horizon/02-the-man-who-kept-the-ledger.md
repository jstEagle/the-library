## 2. The Man Who Kept the Ledger

The courier came at dawn, and Corwin Hale came with the tide.

Tamsin knew the second man was coming before she saw him. She was on the yard's seawall with her tea going cold in her hands, watching a black-hulled steam launch cut the grey water of the harbor with the kind of arrogant precision that belonged to only one fleet on this coast. Behind her, the yard was waking the way it always did—Brannigan's chimney breathing woodsmoke into the cold air, Pell's hammer ringing off the dive tender's hull where he was already working, though the sun had barely cleared the headland.

She had burned Hale's contract two nights ago. Fed it page by page into Brannigan's stove and watched the ink curl and go, and felt nothing but relief—or what she told herself was relief. A refusal should have ended it. Men like Corwin Hale did not travel six hours by sea to hear *no* twice, but she had built fifteen years of quiet on the assumption that they could be made to.

"Captain." Pell appeared at her elbow, wiping rust from his hands onto his trousers, his eyes going past her to the launch. "That's—"

"I know what that is."

"He's got a flag up. Is that a salvage pennant? I've never seen one up close. Do you think he'd let me look at his pumps? They say his fleet runs the new Merrow-pattern regulators, the ones with the—"

"Pell." She said it gently. The boy had an engine for a heart; everything excited him, even things that ought to frighten him. "Go help Brannigan with breakfast."

"But—"

"*Help Brannigan.*"

He went, shoulders dropping, and Tamsin stayed on the wall and watched the launch come in. There was no use pretending otherwise: she wanted to see him. Not because she had anything to say to Corwin Hale, but because a man who kept coming was a man who knew something, and she needed to know how much.

* * *

Hale came up the yard ramp alone, which surprised her. She had expected muscle, lawyers, a retinue. Instead there was just a broad-shouldered man of fifty-odd in a coat too fine for salt air, carrying a leather folio under one arm like a clerk. His hair was iron-grey and cropped short, his face weathered but soft around the eyes, and when he smiled it was the smile of someone who had practiced it in mirrors until it stopped meaning anything.

"Captain Ashgrove." He extended a hand. She looked at it until he withdrew it. "You burned my contract."

"You heard."

"I pay people to read my mail. It's the whole secret of my success." He glanced around the yard with frank appraisal—the slipway, the rusting crane, the Meridian moored along the quay with her pump housings tarred against the winter. "Modest operation. Honest, though. That's rarer than money these days."

"Why are you here, Mr. Hale?"

"To show you something." He opened the folio. "May we sit? What I have to show you is better sitting down. Old habit. I spent twenty years reading ledgers to men who didn't want to hear them."

They sat under the awning by the cookhouse, where Brannigan's table stood scarred by thirty years of knives and arguments. Brannigan himself filled the doorway behind it, arms crossed, a ladle still in his fist as if it might serve as a weapon. Pell hovered at the edge of the awning, ostensibly coiling rope, actually listening with his whole body.

Hale laid a single sheet of paper on the table. Heavy stock, old, foxed at the edges with brown blooms of damp. A ledger page, torn cleanly from its binding—and Tamsin's stomach dropped before she even read it, because she recognized the format. Merrow Line company stationery. The double-ruled columns. The stamp of the Far Horizon district office.

She hadn't seen that letterhead in fifteen years. She had made sure of it.

"Read it," Hale said quietly.

She read it. Her eyes went first to the date—four days before the collapse—and then down the column of figures, and then to the authorization line at the bottom, where a signature sat in faded iron-gall ink, slanted and sure of itself:

**E. Ashgrove, District Works Director.**

Her father's name. Her father's hand. She would have known that handwriting anywhere; he had written her letters from every posting on the line, tall confident loops, the *g*s like hooks. *Thinking of you at the pass, Tam. One day you'll survey it better than I ever did.*

"This is a forgery," she said.

"It isn't."

"People forge my father's signature all the time," she said, and heard her own voice come out flat and strange. "He was famous. Everyone wanted a piece of the Merrow after it fell."

"No one has forged anything." Hale turned the page toward himself, tracing the signature with one blunt finger without quite touching it. "I bought the Merrow Liquidation Trust's remaining records eight years ago. Every order, every requisition, every payroll entry from the last three years of the line. This is requisition 4471-C. Blasting powder, forty cases, drawn from the Kestrel magazine four days before the collapse. Signed out for 'tunnel stabilization works, Far Horizon pass.'" He looked up at her. "There were no stabilization works ordered at Far Horizon. I've checked the district schedules. There were no crews assigned, no permits filed, no follow-up reports. Forty cases of blasting powder walked into that mountain and never walked out. Four days later, the pass came down and took eleven men with it."

The wind moved the awning. Somewhere down the yard, a gull screamed and was answered.

"My father signed supply orders every week of his life," Tamsin said. "It proves nothing except that he did his job."

"Perhaps." Hale folded his hands on the table. "Or perhaps it proves that the collapse wasn't geology. Perhaps it proves somebody ordered that mountain brought down on purpose, and your father's pen was on the order. Either way, Captain—" and here his voice dropped into something almost gentle, which was worse than cruelty, "—you've spent fifteen years telling everyone you don't talk about the Merrow. I'm offering you the chance to find out whether you've been protecting your father's memory or his crime."

Brannigan's ladle creaked in his grip. Pell had stopped pretending to coil rope entirely.

"And what do you want?" Tamsin asked. "If you've had this page for eight years, why now?"

"Because the wreck won't wait." Hale leaned forward. "The pay-train's still down there, Captain. Sealed in the flooded galleries with eleven dead men and a strongbox car full of railway gold—wages owed to workers who never lived to collect them. My divers can reach it inside a tidal window that opens in nine days and closes forever after. I want your crew because you're the best on this coast, and because you know those tunnels better than any living soul. And I want—" he tapped the ledger page once, softly, "—justice. For the eleven. For the families who were paid off and silenced. For a girl who lost her mentor in the dark and was blamed for it by every newspaper on the coast."

The word *mentor* landed like a hook beneath her ribs. She had never said Ilsa Vane's name to Corwin Hale. She had never said it to anyone.

"How do you know about Vane?" she asked, very quietly.

Something flickered across Hale's face—not guilt exactly, but the careful blankness of a man stepping over ground he knows is mined. "I keep ledgers, Captain. People, too. It's what I do." He closed the folio. "Six tides of work. Full hazard rates, triple shares on recovery, and my personal guarantee that whatever you find down there about how that pass fell gets published, not buried. I'll put my own press behind it. You have my word."

"Your word," Brannigan said from the doorway, the first thing he'd said. "Worth about what your timeline's worth, I'd wager."

Hale smiled his mirror-smile. "I like your cook, Captain. Keep him." He rose, settled his coat, and left the ledger page lying on the table between them. "Keep that, too. It's yours more than mine. I'll give you until tomorrow's tide. If the answer's no, I'll sail for Far Horizon with the fleet I have, and you'll never see me or that page again—and neither will anyone else."

He went down the ramp to his launch without looking back. Tamsin watched the black hull recede toward the harbor mouth and found that her tea had gone entirely cold, and that her hands, wrapped around the cup, were shaking.

* * *

They voted that evening, because that was how the crew did things—everything over Brannigan's table, everything argued to exhaustion, everything decided together or not at all.

Brannigan voted no first, fastest, and loudest. "Nothing that man says is straight. Nine days becomes six tides becomes whatever suits him. We take his coin, we take his leash."

Pell voted yes before Brannigan finished speaking, and then had the grace to look ashamed of his eagerness. But when Tamsin asked him why—really why—he went quiet in a way she had rarely seen, turning his cup in his hands.

"It's my mum," he said finally. "The doctors in Port Sallow. The treatments. It's... it's more than I make in five years, Captain. Triple shares." He couldn't look at her. "I know it's a bad idea. I'd vote no if it was anybody else's mother."

Old Marta, their tender-hand, said she'd follow Tamsin into hell or away from it, whichever the captain chose, which was Marta's way of voting yes while claiming neutrality. Young Sorrel, their pump engineer, wanted the money for a boat of her own and said so without apology.

Which left Tamsin.

She sat with the ledger page in front of her through all of it, and no one touched it, and no one asked her about it, though she could feel their questions pressing against the walls of her silence like water against a hull. Her father's handwriting stared up at her. *E. Ashgrove.* The man who taught her to read charts before she could read words. The man whose compass she still carried and had never once used since the pass fell.

If the page was true, then either her father had signed a murder order, or someone had used his hand to sign one, and both possibilities were unbearable, and only one of them could be survived by finding out.

"The answer's yes," she heard herself say.

Brannigan slammed his palm flat on the table. "Tam—"

"We take the work, we take his coin, and we watch him like hawks the whole way." She looked around the table, at the faces of the only family she had chosen since the other one broke. "But we do it our way. We set the terms. And if he lies to us—even once—we walk, and he can explain to his fleet why the best crew on the coast quit mid-job."

Pell was grinning. Marta nodded slowly. Sorrel raised her cup. Brannigan held out longest, jaw working, and then muttered, "Somebody's got to keep you all alive," which from Brannigan was a yes.

Tamsin picked up the ledger page and folded it into her breast pocket, over her heart, where it sat like a coal.

Outside, the tide was turning. In nine days, the window at Far Horizon would open—and somewhere out on the grey water, Corwin Hale's fleet was already moving, and she could not shake the feeling, cold as the harbor, that the six tides he'd promised her were a lie measured to leave her exactly enough rope.
