## 3. Six Tides

Pell had never seen the Meridian move before. In his two years with the crew, the old pump barge had sat at her moorings like a beached whale, rust bleeding down her flanks in long orange tears, gulls colonizing her wheelhouse roof until Brannigan threatened them with a broom and lost. Now she was awake—boilers lit, deck thronged, lines cast off—and she was carrying Pell out toward the Far Horizon pass at a speed that made the water hiss against her hull like something eager.

He stood at the bow rail and let the spray hit him in the face, because it felt like the kind of thing a real salvage diver would do.

"Stop grinning," said Sefa, coiling rope beside him without looking up. "You'll crack your face."

"I'm not grinning."

"You've been grinning since dawn."

Pell wiped his mouth with the back of his hand, which was not a denial, and went to help her coil. That was the thing about the crew: there was no standing around on the Meridian. Brannigan ran the galley and the boilers both, because he claimed hunger and cold were the same problem solved the same way. Sefa handled rigging and could tie a bowline blindfolded, upside down, in a gale, and had once done so to win a bet that paid for everyone's winter boots. Old Wren kept the charts and the tide tables and spoke maybe forty words a day, each of them load-bearing. And Tamsin Ashgrove—

Tamsin was everywhere at once. Pell watched her cross the deck with the contract's terms already translated into action: dive gear broken out of its crates and inspected link by link, air hoses pressure-tested twice, the Meridian's pumps torn down by Brannigan and reassembled singing. She touched everything once, confirmed it was right, and moved on, and the whole barge seemed to organize itself around her passage like iron filings around a lodestone.

She'd signed Hale's contract at dawn. Pell had seen her do it—the pen scratching, her jaw set like a woman signing something other than paper. Then she'd turned to the crew, all five of them assembled in the yard with the ink still wet, and said only: "Six tides. We work fast or we don't work at all." No speech. No explanation of why six tides mattered, or why Hale's fleet was already at sea when the man himself had stood in their yard talking like they had weeks.

Wren knew why. Wren always knew why. He'd spread the charts across the mess table an hour after they cast off, weighting the corners with mugs of Brannigan's coffee, and now he tapped one blunt finger on the narrow throat of water where the mountains came down to meet the sea.

"The pass floods," he said. "Spring tides, this time of year. Six tides from tomorrow's dawn, the water over the wreck goes past eighty feet. Past safe diving depth for hard-hat work. After that, whatever's down there stays down there till autumn."

"And Hale's fleet?" Sefa asked.

Wren's finger slid east along the chart, past the headlands, out into open water where a small penciled cross marked a position no one had explained. "Three days out. Maybe four if the weather turns. He told us we had time. We don't."

"He lied," Pell said, and felt the word land strangely in his chest—he'd never accused anyone of anything bigger than stealing apples.

"Hale lies the way other men breathe," Brannigan rumbled from the galley doorway, a pot in one hand. "Question is what he's lying *for*."

Tamsin stood at the chart table's edge, arms folded, studying the penciled cross as though she could sink it by staring. "It doesn't matter what he's lying for. It matters that we have six tides and he thinks we have twelve. That's the only advantage anyone's given us, and I intend to spend every hour of it." She looked up, and her eyes found each of them in turn. "First descent is mine. Outer gallery, survey run, mark the train's position. Second tide we bring up anything light. Third tide onward, we cut the strongbox car free and lift it. Questions?"

Pell had about forty questions, but he asked the one that mattered most to him. "I'm diving with you, right? Second tide?"

Something crossed her face then—quick as a fish under a boat, gone before he could name it. "Second tide," she said. "If the first run comes back clean."

* * *

The first night out, Pell couldn't sleep. The Meridian lay anchored just short of the pass, riding a gentle swell, and through the porthole beside his bunk he could see the black shape of the Far Horizon headlands against the stars—a jagged gap in the ridge like a door left ajar. Somewhere beyond it, under all that dark water, a train had been dying for fifteen years.

He gave up on sleep and went topside, meaning to sit in the wheelhouse, but the deck lamp was already lit and Tamsin was there, alone, going through the dive gear again. Checking links she'd checked that morning. Testing valves that had tested true at noon.

"You'll wear it out before the water gets a chance," Pell said.

She didn't startle—that was the thing about her; nothing startled her—but she did look up, and in the lamplight her face looked older than it ever did in daylight. "Can't sleep either?"

"No, ma'am."

"Don't call me ma'am. Makes me feel like a magistrate." She held up a helmet, turning it so the brass caught the lamplight. "You know how old this rig is?"

"Older than me."

"Older than the wreck." She set it down gently, the way you'd set down something sleeping. "Ilsa Vane wore one like it. My mentor. She taught me to dive in water colder than this and half as forgiving." A pause, and then, as if she'd said more than she meant to: "Get some rest, Pell. Tomorrow we work."

He should have gone below. Instead he heard himself say, "What happened to her? To Ilsa Vane?"

The lamplight hissed. Somewhere below, Brannigan turned over in his bunk with a sound like a landslide. Tamsin looked at him for a long moment, and Pell understood suddenly that he was seeing a door close—not slammed, not locked, just quietly shut, the way you shut a door on a room you intend never to enter again.

"That's not tonight's work," she said.

"Yes, ma'am," Pell said, because he didn't know what else to say, and went below feeling like a boy who'd peeked at something he shouldn't have—and feeling, underneath that, a small cold splinter of a thought he didn't want: *she checks the gear three times because she's not checking the gear.*

* * *

Dawn brought the first tide, and with it the first sight of the Far Horizon pass from the water, and Pell forgot the splinter entirely for a while, because the pass was beautiful in the way that dangerous things are beautiful. The mountains leaned together overhead, leaving a slot of sky; the sea ran green-black between them, smooth as poured glass, hiding everything. Fifteen years ago a tunnel had run beneath that water. Now the water ran where the tunnel had been, and the train lay somewhere in the drowned dark like a coin at the bottom of a well.

They worked the anchor winches in silence. Wren called depths off his lead line. Brannigan fired the air compressor and the whole barge shuddered like a waking animal. And Tamsin dressed for the dive in the old hard-hat rig—canvas suit, weighted boots, the brass helmet with its little round windows—while Sefa laced her in and Pell handed her each piece as she needed it, trying to memorize everything, because someday that would be him in the suit and he wanted every step written into his hands.

"Air's good," Brannigan reported, tapping the gauge. "Line's clear. Rope signal's as agreed—one pull attention, two come up, three come up fast and pray."

Tamsin nodded. At the last moment, before the helmet went on, she looked out at the pass—not at the crew, not at the water directly ahead, but at the headlands themselves, as if checking whether they remembered her.

Then the helmet closed, and she stepped off the ladder, and the green water took her.

For two hours the Meridian was nothing but sound and waiting: the compressor's heartbeat, the air line paying out inch by inch, Wren timing slack, Sefa watching bubbles, Pell holding the rope taut and feeling every tremor in it like a pulse. Twice the line pulled once—attention—and both times his heart tried to climb out through his ribs before the second signal failed to come.

When she finally surfaced, streaming, helmet coming off in a cloud of steam and salt, her face was gray beneath the tan. Pell saw it before anyone. He was closest; he took the helmet from her hands.

"Well?" Sefa asked. "Is she down there?"

"She's down there." Tamsin's voice was rough, as if the water had gotten into it. "Lantern car's intact. Sealed. Whole train's sitting proud of the silt, better preserved than any wreck I've seen at this depth." She pulled off her gloves finger by finger. "We can work her. Second tide, Pell and I go down together, start rigging the light cars for lifting."

There was cheering—Sefa whooped, Brannigan banged the pot he happened to be holding—and Pell cheered too, loud as any of them. But he was still holding her helmet, and he had been raised by a mother whose hands shook, and he had learned young to read faces the way other people read weather, and what he saw on Tamsin Ashgrove's face was not triumph.

It was the look of someone who has found a thing in the dark that she very much wishes she hadn't found—and who has decided, already, in the space of a single breath, exactly how much of it she is going to tell.

"Better preserved than expected," Wren said quietly, watching her. Not a question. Wren's words were load-bearing, and this one bore a great deal.

"Better than expected," Tamsin agreed, and reached for the coffee Brannigan offered, and drank it looking out at the pass instead of at any of them.

That night, anchored in the shadow of the headlands, Pell lay in his bunk doing arithmetic he didn't want to do. Six tides. One spent. Five left, and Hale's fleet three days out and closing, and the strongest diver he'd ever known lying about something—or not lying, maybe, just not telling, which was different, wasn't it? Wasn't it?

He thought of her checking the gear three times. He thought of the door closing quietly. He thought of the way she'd looked at the headlands before the helmet went on, like a woman greeting someone she owed money to.

Above him, through the deck planks, he heard footsteps cross to the stern and stop. He got up without deciding to and climbed the ladder, and found Tamsin at the rail with a chart unrolled against the wind, weighted by a knife, studying the outer gallery by lamplight—and marking something on it. Marking it small, in pencil, in a corner where no one else would look first.

"Pell," she said, without turning around. "Second tide. You'll be on my shoulder the whole way down. Anything I point at, you look at. Nothing else."

"Aye," Pell said.

And then, because the splinter in his chest had grown teeth: "Anything you want me to see? Or just what you point at?"

The lamplight flickered. Out in the dark, the Far Horizon pass waited with all its water, keeping everything it had ever been given.

Tamsin rolled up the chart. "Both," she said.
