## 4. What the Water Keeps

The Meridian sat at anchor off the pass like a patient animal, her pumps ticking in the cold morning air, and Tamsin stood at the rail watching the Far Horizon swallow the dawn.

Fifteen years had not changed the shape of it. The headland still rose black against the grey water, its tunnel mouths hidden beneath the tideline, its cliffs streaked with the mineral weep of the drowned galleries inside. From the deck it looked almost peaceful—a sleeping thing, a wound that had scabbed over with salt. She knew better. She had helped draw the maps of what lay under there, back when the maps were drawn by lamplight instead of memory.

"You're up early." Brannigan's voice came from the galley hatch, rough as his stew. "Or you never went down."

"Never went down."

He grunted, which from Brannigan was a whole conversation. He came out onto the deck with two tin mugs and handed her one without ceremony, and they stood together while the tide turned below them, the water around the Meridian beginning its slow clockwise crawl toward the seaward mouth of the old line.

"First dive's at slack," he said. "Pell's been up since four checking the regulators. Boy polishes them like they're relics."

"They are relics. Half this gear's older than he is."

"That's not funny, Tam."

"I wasn't joking." She drank. The tea was terrible and hot and exactly right. "I'm taking the outer gallery myself."

Brannigan set his mug down on the rail with a click that meant trouble. "No."

"It's a survey dive. Shallow end of the tunnel, forty feet at most, good visibility on the ebb—"

"It's the *Merrow*, Tam." He said the name the way other men said the names of graveyards. "You haven't been under that rock since the collapse. Nobody has. That's why Hale's paying us what he's paying us—because every diver who knows these tunnels is either dead or too sensible to go back."

"I'm going back because I'm the only one who knows them," she said. "Pell doesn't have the experience for a first descent into unknown structure. You're needed topside running the pump. And I'm not sending anyone into that dark to do something I won't do myself."

"That's not courage, that's pride wearing courage's coat."

Tamsin looked at him. Brannigan was sixty if he was a day, built like the bollards he'd spent his life tying ships to, with a face that had stopped negotiating years ago. He had cooked for her crew through three wrecks and two near-mutinies, and he had never once asked about the Merrow Line, and she had never once offered.

"I need to see it first," she said quietly. "Before anyone else does. If it's bad—if it's worse than bad—I want to be the one who decides whether we tell them."

He studied her for a long moment. The tide ticked against the hull.

"One hour," he said finally. "Line tendered by Pell, who will be told you're doing a routine structural survey of the outer gallery, which is true as far as it goes. And you come up at the first tug on the line, no arguments, no heroics."

"Agreed."

"And Tam." He picked up his mug again, but he didn't drink; he just held it, steam curling past his knuckles. "Whatever's down there. It's been keeping fifteen years. It'll keep another hour after you surface. Don't let it keep *you*."

She almost smiled. "That's the plan."

* * *

The water at the seaward mouth was green-black and cold enough to ache through the suit, and Tamsin hung on the descent line for a moment before committing, letting her body take the weight, letting the silence close over her ears like a door.

Below her, the tunnel mouth opened out of the cliff face like a held breath.

She had expected the dark to feel like an ending. Instead it felt like a room she'd left mid-sentence. Her lamp swept across stone dressed with the railway's old cut marks—chisel scars in neat rows, the work of men who'd believed they were carving a future—and then across the first of the debris field: a wheel truck lying on its side, furred white with fifteen years of silt-bloom; a section of railing bent outward, away from the blast, though the official report had called it an inward failure of the crown.

She swam on. The line paid out behind her, Pell's steady tension a thin thread of the living world. Forty feet. Fifty. The gallery widened, and her light found the walls rising on either side—the grand approach, they'd called it, tiled once in cream ceramic so the lamplight would carry, and even now, under the silt, she could see the tiles' ghost-glitter where the current kept them washed.

Her heart was doing something complicated. She made herself breathe slow, count the rhythm, do the work. Survey the structure. Mark the hazards. Come home.

Then her light caught glass.

It was a lantern pane, and it was clean.

Not merely intact—clean, polished by some current that shouldn't exist this deep, catching her beam and throwing it back in a long gold smear across the gallery floor. Tamsin hung motionless in the water, her breath loud in her own ears, and followed the gleam down.

The lantern car sat upright on the tunnel bed sixty yards inside the mouth, exactly where the trackbed ran, couplings still shrouded in their canvas covers. Sealed. Whole. Its brass fittings ungreened, its windows unbroken, its roof bearing—not eroded, not collapsed upon, not crushed by the tons of rock that the official survey swore had come down in this very section—bearing, in flaking but legible paint, the number 7.

Car seven. The pay-train's lantern car. The car that led the train, that rode ahead of the strongbox cars to light the way.

The car that, according to the survey Tamsin herself had helped file fifteen years ago, was buried under ninety feet of collapsed crown rock half a mile further in.

She drifted closer, and the wrongness of it got into her bones like the cold did. There was no collapse here. No fall-in, no rubble choke, none of the devastation the reports described. The gallery overhead was sound—she swept her lamp across it twice, three times, looking for the fracture lines, the fresh-cut faces of broken stone. Nothing. The rock was old-failed in places, yes, minor spalling, the ordinary shedding of a tunnel under stress. But the catastrophic collapse, the one that killed eleven men including Ilsa Vane, the one that sealed the line and drowned the truth—

There had been no collapse here.

Which meant either the survey was wrong—and she had walked those galleries herself, she had *measured* the choke point—or the collapse had happened somewhere else entirely, and someone had moved the story to fit the wreck. Or moved the wreck to fit the story. A lantern car didn't walk sixty yards uphill through solid rock. Someone had put it here. Someone had wanted the pay-train found *here*, in the shallow gallery, where any salvage crew working from the official charts would tear this section apart looking for gold that was never buried in it.

A decoy. Fifteen years old and still working.

And if car seven was a decoy, then the real train—the real bodies, the real gold, the real reason for all of it—lay further in, past the false choke, in the deep galleries where the water ran black and the tide tables turned hostile. Where Ilsa Vane had died. Where Ilsa Vane might have—

Tamsin's chest went tight around the thought, tighter than the pressure at fifty feet had any right to make it. She checked her gauge. Eleven minutes down. The hour Brannigan had given her stretched ahead of her like a corridor, and at the far end of it, past the silt and the dark, the deep tunnels waited with whatever Ilsa had been carrying when the water took her.

The line tugged. Once. Pell, dutiful, punctual, asking nothing.

Tamsin looked at the lantern car one last time—at the little painted 7, at the clean glass throwing gold across the dark—and turned for the surface.

* * *

She came up the ladder into grey daylight and Pell's face, bright with worry and admiration in equal measure, and she felt the lie assemble itself in her mouth before she'd decided to speak it.

"Well?" he said, unwinding the line from the capstan, hands quick and careful. "How's the structure?"

"Bad," Tamsin said. She pulled off her hood, and the wind hit her wet skin and she was grateful for the excuse to flinch. "Crown failure through the first three hundred yards, just like the old charts said. Heavy debris field. The lantern car's there—car seven—but it's crushed flat, half-buried. Nothing salvageable on it."

Pell nodded, writing it on his slate in his careful boyish hand. "So the strongbox cars'll be deeper in. Past the choke."

"Past the choke." Her voice held. She was almost proud of it. "We'll need the second tide to run the deep line. I want the pump at full pressure and nobody diving the inner galleries without me. Understood?"

"Aye." He hesitated, slate against his chest. "Tam—you went down there yourself. First one in. That took nerve."

"It took seniority," she said, and managed something that passed for a smile, and went aft to strip her gear before he could look at her too closely.

Brannigan was waiting by the galley with a towel and a look that missed nothing. He didn't ask. He just watched her dry her hair with hands that were not quite steady, and when she finished he said, low, "One hour. What did the water keep?"

Tamsin folded the towel. Set it down. Straightened it.

"Debris," she said. "Rubble. A crushed lantern car. It's as bad as the charts say."

Brannigan held her eyes for a long, long moment—the same eyes that had watched her cook's fire for six years, that had seen every kind of weather a person could make—and then he nodded slowly, and took the towel, and said, "Stew's on in twenty," and walked away.

She stood alone at the stern rail while the tide turned, and the Far Horizon sat black against the fading light, and somewhere inside it a lantern car with clean glass threw its small gold light across a lie fifteen years deep.

She had lied to Pell. She had lied to Brannigan. The words had come out smooth and easy and that was the part that frightened her—that the skill hadn't rusted, that she could still do it, still seal a truth behind her teeth the way the founders had sealed theirs behind stone.

Tomorrow was the second tide. Tomorrow they would run the deep line, and Pell would swim into galleries she now knew were not what anyone had been told, chasing gold that someone had gone to great lengths to make findable.

And somewhere in the dark beyond the false choke, Ilsa Vane's last survey waited—with, Tamsin was suddenly certain, the same certainty she'd once trusted her own instruments—the names of everyone who had built the lie she'd just helped maintain.

She gripped the rail and watched the water, and did not go below until the stars were out.
