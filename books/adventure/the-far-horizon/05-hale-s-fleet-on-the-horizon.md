## 5. Hale's Fleet on the Horizon

Pell saw the sails first because he was the only one doing nothing useful.

Brannigan had him on the Meridian's deck at low tide, scrubbing the rust bloom off the winch housing with a wire brush that had long since given up its argument with the metal. But the brush was down and Pell was leaning on the rail with a mug of chicory going cold in his hand, watching the horizon past the Far Horizon's shoulder—the way he'd learned to watch it these last two days, the way a man learns to watch a door when he suspects someone's coming through it.

Three masts. Then four. A fifth, low and heavy, riding deep.

They were hull-down when he first caught them, gray shapes against gray water, but Pell had grown up on this coast counting other people's ships the way other boys counted stars, and he knew what salvage steamers looked like. He knew the rake of their funnels, the crane arms folded along their spines like sleeping birds. He knew them, and he knew they weren't supposed to be here for another day and a half.

"Ho," he said, and his voice came out smaller than he meant. He tried again. "HO. Sail!"

The yard went still the way a body goes still—everything listening at once. Brannigan's head came up from the pump manifold. Quill set down her calipers mid-measurement, which Pell had never once seen her do. And Tamsin Ashgrove came out of the chart house like weather coming over a ridge, quiet and total.

She didn't ask what he'd seen. She took his shoulders, turned him by degrees until his sightline was hers, and looked.

Pell felt her go rigid under his hands. Not much. Just enough that he understood something new about her: that Tamsin Ashgrove could be afraid without showing it anywhere except in the muscles of a stranger's shoulders.

"Count again," she said.

"Five. Maybe six behind the headland." He swallowed. "Salvage steamers. Deep-riding."

"How fast?"

"With this wind? They'll have the pass by tomorrow's morning tide."

Tamsin was silent for the space of three breaths. Then she said, in a voice pitched to carry across the whole yard, "Everyone stop working. Come here. All of you."

* * *

They gathered in the chart house because it was the only room with walls, and walls made a thing feel containable even when it wasn't. Tamsin spread the coastal chart and marked the fleet's position with a grease pencil—a small X that somehow made everything worse, the way writing a thing down makes it real.

"Hale said six tides," Quill said. She was the crew's surveyor, dry-voiced and exact, and she'd been the one who'd argued hardest *for* the contract. "Six tides before his fleet arrives to take custody of whatever we raise. That was the term."

"That was the lie," Tamsin said.

"You're sure it's his?"

"The lead ship is the *Cormorant*. I'd know her funnel anywhere—I helped fit her pumps before I left the trade." Tamsin's finger rested beside the X without touching it. "He gave us six tides so we'd work fast and shallow and hand him an easy wreck. He never meant to share anything. We were never partners. We were the men who went into the dark first."

Pell watched the faces around the chart table and tried to read them the way he'd been trying, all week, to read Tamsin. Brannigan's face was stone. Quill's was arithmetic—she was already calculating, you could see it, tide heights and cable loads and odds. Old Sef, their rigger, who mostly communicated through grunts and perfect knots, was looking at Tamsin with an expression Pell couldn't name.

"So we cut and run," Quill said. It wasn't cowardice; it was bookkeeping. "We've got the barge, we've got our gear, we've got a contract Hale will now be motivated never to pay. We weigh anchor tonight and be in Corvess Harbor before his fleet clears the point. No harm done except to our pride."

"No harm done," Sef repeated, in the tone of a man testing whether the words weighed anything.

Silence. Pell realized everyone was looking at Tamsin, and that Tamsin was looking at the chart—at the pass itself, at the little penciled depth soundings that climbed toward the drowned tunnels like a staircase going down into the page.

And then she did something Pell had never seen her do.

She sat down.

Tamsin Ashgrove stood through arguments and storms and the whole fifteen-year weight of her history; standing was simply what she did, the way water was wet. But she sat down at the chart table, put both hands flat on the paper, and said, "Before anyone votes, there's something I owe you. Something I should have said two days ago, on the first dive, and didn't."

Pell's stomach dropped. He thought of her surfacing that first descent, gasping on the ladder, eyes too wide. He thought of her report—*outer gallery flooded to ceiling, no sign of the train*—and how smoothly she'd said it. How easily he'd believed her.

"I found the lantern car," Tamsin said. "First dive. Intact. Sealed. Sitting in the outer gallery where the official Merrow survey swore nothing could be, because that section of tunnel collapsed inward and buried everything under forty feet of rock." She drew a breath. "It isn't buried. The tunnel isn't collapsed. The records are false. Someone falsified the official survey after the disaster—and the only reason anyone falsifies a survey of a dead tunnel is if the truth of how those people died is worth hiding."

The chart house was very quiet. Outside, the wind worried the rigging, a thin persistent note like someone whistling to keep brave.

"Why?" Quill asked quietly. "Why would the railway falsify its own survey?"

"Because of what killed them," Tamsin said. "Because the collapse wasn't a collapse."

Pell heard it land in the room like a stone into still water. Fifteen years ago, the Far Horizon tunnels had gone down with thirty-one souls aboard the pay-train and the maintenance crews working the gallery. Everyone on this coast knew the story. Everyone had grown up in its shadow, the way Pell had—his own village had lost two men to the Merrow Line, and his mother had forbidden him, *forbidden* him, ever to speak their names in the house because his grandmother still lit candles.

"It was engineered," Tamsin said. "Deliberately. And I found the proof of it lying in the dark, and I surfaced, and I lied to you." Her jaw worked. "I told myself it was strategy. That if Hale suspected what was really down there, he'd change the terms, or bring more men, or bury us with it. And some of that was true. But mostly—" her voice cracked, just once, and Pell would remember that crack for the rest of his life— "mostly I've spent fifteen years not looking at this. And I let that habit spend your trust for me. I'm sorry."

Nobody moved. Pell found his own hands had gone white on the table edge.

Then Brannigan spoke.

"My brother was on the Merrow."

Every head turned. Brannigan was the crew's cook and its ballast, a big slow-moving man whose anger took days to arrive and years to leave, and in four years aboard the Meridian Pell had heard him talk about himself perhaps a dozen times, and never once about family.

"Davin Brannigan. Maintenance second-class, gallery four." His voice was flat as slate. "They sent my mother a letter with a seal on it and eleven crowns compensation, and a line about tragic geological instability. Eleven crowns." He looked at Tamsin, and Pell saw that the stone of his face had fault lines running all through it. "You knew. You dove down there and found the proof, and you kept it in your chest for two days while I cooked your supper."

"Yes," Tamsin said. "I did."

"And you're telling me now why?"

"Because you deserve to hear it from me before you hear it from Hale. Because if we run, the truth stays down there with his fleet sitting on top of it, and no one will ever dig it out. And because—" she stopped, started again— "because I can't ask you to go back into that tunnel unless you know exactly what's down there besides gold."

Another silence, longer. The wind rose outside. Somewhere below, the old pump engine ticked as it cooled, patient as a heart.

Brannigan pushed back from the table. He stood, and he filled half the chart house, and Pell braced himself—for the leaving, or the blow, or whatever came next.

Brannigan picked up the coffee pot and refilled everyone's mugs, one by one, ending with Tamsin's.

"We don't run," he said.

Quill stared. "Brannigan—"

"He lied to us. So we owe him nothing—not the contract, not the courtesy, not the six-tide schedule he invented." Brannigan set the pot down with a click. "My brother's been down there fifteen years under a lie with a government seal on it. Hale wants to put his cranes over that tunnel and drag out the gold and sink every other question forever. You want me to sail away from that?" He shook his head slowly. "I've been cooking for this crew for four years because it was the first place that ever felt like it held together. Davin deserved a crew like this. He never got one. So we stay, and we finish it, and we take the whole rotten truth up into daylight where it belongs."

Sef grunted—which from Sef was a speech. Quill exhaled through her nose, pulled the chart toward herself, and began marking tide lines, which from Quill was also a speech. One by one, without ceremony, the vote happened the way votes happen among people who live in each other's pockets: not with raised hands but with stayed bodies.

All of them stayed.

Pell realized he was crying and didn't care, or cared very little, and wiped his face on his sleeve and said, "So what do we do?"

Tamsin looked around the chart table at her crew—her impossible, unforgiven, un-departed crew—and Pell watched something come back into her face that had been missing since the day she'd surfaced from the outer gallery. Not relief. Resolve. The difference between them being that relief looks backward.

She pulled the chart of the inner tunnels from beneath the coastal sheet and smoothed it flat.

"Hale thinks we're shallow divers chasing a strongbox on his schedule," she said. "Good. Let him. Quill, I want the tide tables for the next three days and every sounding we've taken of the seaward galleries. Sef, the Meridian's pumps—we get all four barrels running by nightfall, no excuses. Brannigan—"

"Aye."

"—make sure we eat. All of us. It's a long way down and we're going to need our legs." She straightened, and her eyes found Pell's, and held them, and there was something in them that was either apology or request or both. "Pell. You're with me. First dive of the morning tide. There's a survey log in that wreck—my mentor kept one, waterproofed, chained to her kit. If it survived the water, it survived everything else. And it's time somebody read what's written in it."

Above them, through the chart house glass, the five gray shapes crawled nearer along the horizon, patient as debt.

The sixth tide, Pell thought, was coming early.

But so were they.
