## 6. The Survey Log

The third tide came in grey and patient, the way grief does.

Tamsin stood on the deck of the *Meridian* while the pump barge rolled under her boots, watching the water of the Far Horizon pass swallow the light of the morning whole. Two tides down. Two descents survived. Her shoulders ached in a way that had nothing to do with the weight of the gear, and her left ear still rang from the squeeze of the first dive, a thin silver note she'd carry up from the tunnels whether she wanted to or not. The pass did that to you. It gave you something small and wrong and let you keep it.

"Third tide's the deep one," Pell said beside her, already suited, already bright-eyed, because the boy had never once in his life met a morning he didn't trust. He was checking her harness straps without being asked, the way he did, tugging each buckle twice as though the sea could be argued with by diligence. "Engine car's past the second gallery, you said. Past the squeeze."

"Past the squeeze," Tamsin agreed.

"And you're sure it's there. The log."

She wasn't. That was the truth she'd been carrying since the lantern car—sealed, intact, sitting forty yards from where the official survey swore the tunnel had folded shut like a fist. *Someone falsified the records.* She'd surfaced with that thought lodged in her chest and told Brannigan the gallery was empty, told Pell the lantern car was a rusted shell, and the lie had sat in her mouth for a day and a half now like a stone she couldn't spit out. Every time Pell grinned at her, the stone grew heavier.

"Ilsa Vane kept her survey logs in waxed canvas," Tamsin said instead. "Waterproof. She used to say paper outlives pride. If the engine car is where the lantern car says it should be—"

"Then the log's with it." Pell finished the buckle at her shoulder and stepped back, and for a moment his grin faded into something older. "You knew her. Your mentor. Ilsa Vane."

"I knew her."

"What was she like?"

Tamsin looked out at the pass. The water lay flat as poured pewter, and somewhere beneath it was a woman who had taught her to read a tunnel the way other people read faces, and who had gone down into the dark on the worst day of Tamsin's life and not come back up.

"She was like someone who wrote things down," Tamsin said.

* * *

The descent through the outer gallery was familiar now, and that was its own kind of horror—how quickly the drowned world became ordinary. The guide line ran out ahead of her lamp, taut and pale, and the tunnel walls rose on either side, furred with fifteen years of silt and pale growth, the old riveted iron of the Merrow Line showing through in patches like bone through skin. Her breath rasped in her ears. Pell's lamp glowed behind and below her, steady, obedient, keeping the spacing she'd drilled into him.

The squeeze came at the bend past the second gallery—a collapse of rock and twisted rail where the passage narrowed to little more than a body's width. Tamsin went through it the way Ilsa had taught her: exhale, turn the shoulders, become water pretending to be a woman. The rock scraped her tank. For three heartbeats the tunnel held her, patient and enormous, and then she was through, into the inner gallery where the water went absolutely black beyond the reach of her lamp.

The engine car was there.

It lay on its side across the old rail bed, half-buried in silt, its boiler plate furred white, its great driving wheels still linked by their rods as if the train had been caught mid-stride and frozen there. Her lamp found the number plate—*MERROW 7*—and Tamsin had to put her gloved hand against the cold iron and hold on, because for a moment the water was not water, it was fifteen years ago, it was the sound of the far collapse rolling down the tunnel like the voice of God clearing his throat, it was Ilsa's lamp going away from her into the dark.

*Steady.* She breathed. Counted. The regulator hissed. Pell's light swung into the gallery behind her, and the present came back.

They worked the cab together. The silt came away in slow clouds, and Tamsin's hands—her father's hands, though she hadn't let herself think of them that way in years—found the cab's rear locker by memory and by Ilsa's own teaching. Survey crews always stowed the log aft, low, away from the firebox. *Paper outlives pride, Ashgrove. Put it where the fire can't argue.*

The locker door was jammed with silt and rust. Pell worked a pry bar into the seam while she held the lamp close, and the boy's face behind his mask was all concentration, all that terrible faith of his, and Tamsin thought: *I lied to him. I lied to all of them, and he's down here holding my light.*

The locker gave with a groan of rust. Inside, wrapped in waxed canvas gone grey and soft as old skin, was a bundle the size of a hymnal, tied with a cord.

Tamsin knew the knot. Ilsa had tied that knot ten thousand times—survey crews' cord, the double loop with the tail tucked, the knot she'd taught Tamsin to tie when Tamsin was nineteen and all elbows and hunger. Her fingers knew it before her mind did. They were untying it before she had decided to.

The log came free. Heavy. Intact. The waxed canvas had done its work; beneath it, the oilskin-wrapped pages were stiff but whole. Ilsa Vane's name was stenciled on the cover in her own hand, and beneath it: *FAR HORIZON SURVEY — FINAL.*

*Final.* Tamsin held the word in the lamplight and felt the tunnel lean in around her.

Pell touched her arm and pointed up—his reserve gauge, half-spent, the question in his eyes. She shook her head and pointed to a shallow alcove in the tunnel wall above the engine car, a pocket of trapped air from some old fissure. Not enough to breathe. Enough to talk.

They rose into it, masks off, heads in the cold dark pocket, the tunnel roaring its small silence around them. The air tasted of iron and old stone.

"We're going back," Tamsin said. Her voice came out strange, flattened by the rock. "Get your mask loose. I need two minutes."

Pell didn't argue. That was the thing about Pell—his faith was so total it looked like obedience. He pulled his mask aside and waited, breathing the bad air, trusting her with his lungs.

Tamsin unwrapped the last fold of oilskin and opened the log to the final entries, holding her lamp close, shielding it from the water with her body.

Ilsa's handwriting was as she remembered it—small, level, unhurried, the hand of a woman who had never once written anything she couldn't stand behind. Survey figures. Depth soundings. Dates. And then, on the third page from the end, the level hand changed. The letters got smaller. Pressed harder. As if she were writing not to be read but to be *found*.

*They tell the crews it's ground shift. It is not ground shift. I have run the levels three times and the charges were set where the rock is sound. The order came from above—Ashgrove is only the pen. The instruction originated with the founders themselves: Verrin, Calloway, and the elder Merrow. They want the pass closed and the crews' claims voided with it. A 'controlled' collapse, they call it. Fourteen men underground. Controlled.*

Tamsin read it twice. The water in the alcove lapped at her chin. Somewhere below, the engine car sat in its grave, and somewhere above, the sky was doing whatever the sky did, and none of it reached her.

*Ashgrove is only the pen.*

Her father's name. Not as author—as instrument. And beneath it, on the last page, the signature itself, reproduced in Ilsa's careful hand beside the date: the order for the final charge, signed *E. Ashgrove, Director of Works.* Her father had signed the paper that put fourteen men into the ground and sealed the pass over them. Her father, whose compass she still carried, whose hands were her hands, whose voice she could still hear saying *the Merrow is a promise, Tammy, we keep it or we're nothing*—

The lamp trembled. She steadied it with both hands.

Pell made a sound—small, questioning, the bad air burning in him now, his eyes wide in the lamplight. Tamsin looked at her mentor's last words, at the names that could pull a railway's founders out of their graves and hang the truth around their necks, at her father's signature sitting at the bottom of it all like a stone at the bottom of a well.

She could not surface with this. Not yet. Not with the lie about the lantern car still unconfessed, not with Pell breathing bad air and waiting on her word, not with her own hands shaking so hard the pages whispered. She wrapped the log back into its oilskin, tied Ilsa's knot with her father's hands, and sealed it into the dry box at her belt, against her ribs, where it lay like a second heart. A diseased one.

"Mask on," she said, and her voice was steady, and it was the hardest thing she had done in fifteen years.

They dropped out of the alcove into the black water, and Tamsin led the way back through the gallery, past the engine car, through the squeeze, along the guide line, following her own bubbles up toward a light she no longer trusted herself to stand in. Behind her the tunnel held its dead and its truth and its patience.

The last thing her lamp caught, before the inner gallery swallowed it, was MERROW 7's driving wheels, still linked, still frozen mid-stride—fourteen men who had been told they were building a promise.

* * *

She came up the ladder into grey afternoon and Brannigan's hands, and she let him haul her onto the deck because her arms wanted an excuse. Pell surfaced behind her, coughing, alive, and Brannigan cuffed the boy's helmet gently, the closest thing to tenderness the old cook allowed himself in daylight.

"Third tide done," Brannigan said. "What'd you find?"

Tamsin's hand went to the dry box at her ribs. Through her palm she could feel the log's weight, Ilsa's knot, her father's name.

"Rust and silt," she said. "The engine car's stripped. Nothing worth the air."

Brannigan looked at her a moment too long. Fifteen years of reading her, the same way she read tunnels. But he only grunted and turned to Pell, and Tamsin stood dripping on the deck with the truth pressed against her heart and the second lie of the week sitting in her mouth, heavier than the first.

Then Pell pointed south, where the sea met the sky, and said, "What's that smoke?"

Three funnels. Black smoke. Running before the wind with no salvage fleet's patience in them at all.

Hale's ships were a full day early.
