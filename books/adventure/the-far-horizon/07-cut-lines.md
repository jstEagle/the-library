## 7. Cut Lines

Pell had learned to read the Meridian's sounds the way other men read weather. The pump barge spoke in a language of knocks and sighs—the diesel heart thudding below decks, the compressor coughing its wet rhythm into the air lines that ran down through the flooded gallery like umbilical cords. When everything was right, the barge hummed a note he could feel in his back teeth. He'd been standing at the rail with a mug of Brannigan's tea, listening for that note, when it changed.

It was the third tide of six, and Tamsin and Pell were due down in twenty minutes.

The change was small. A stutter in the compressor's rhythm, half a beat where there should have been none. Then another. Pell set the mug down on the rail and listened harder, the way Tamsin had taught him—*don't just hear it, ask what it means*—and what it meant was pressure loss somewhere in the manifold, slow but steady, the kind of leak that didn't announce itself until it had already eaten your margin.

"Brannigan," he called toward the deckhouse. "Compressor's off its rhythm."

Brannigan emerged wiping his hands on his apron, and for once the big cook didn't argue or joke. He put one palm flat against the pump housing the way a doctor touches a fevered brow, and his face did something complicated.

"That's not wear," he said quietly. "That's a cut."

* * *

They found it at the junction block where the main air line split toward the descent points: a slice through the rubberized hose, clean as a surgeon's work, made by someone who'd known exactly where to cut so the failure would look like chafe. The line hung limp over the barge's side, hissing its last breath into the grey water of the pass.

"Chafe doesn't cut square," Brannigan said. He crouched at the rail, turning the severed end in his huge hands. "And chafe doesn't wait for the tide window when both our divers are topside."

Pell looked up and the horizon answered before he could speak. Far out past the headland, where the sea went from green to the deep bruised blue of open water, three shapes sat low against the light. Salvage hulls. Crane masts. Hale's fleet, a day early, running dark and patient, and now close enough that their divers could swim the seaward approach to the tunnels without anyone on the Meridian seeing them come.

"He never meant to share it," Pell said. His voice came out smaller than he wanted. "Not the wreck. Not the gold. Not us."

"No," said a voice behind them, and Tamsin was there, already pulling her dive knife, already reading the cut line with an expression Pell had only seen once before—in the tunnel, over the lantern car she'd sworn she hadn't found. "He meant to take it. And he meant for us to be at the bottom of the pass when he did."

She turned, and Pell watched her do the arithmetic every diver does and hates: two lines down, one cut. The spare reel would take an hour to splice and pressure-test, and the tide wouldn't wait, and neither would Hale's men, who were even now swimming through the drowned galleries toward a pay-train full of gold with no one standing between them and it except a cook, a boy, and a woman who'd spent fifteen years pretending this water didn't exist.

"We go down anyway," Tamsin said. "One line. Shared air if we have to."

"Tamsin—" Brannigan started.

"It's my name on the contract, Brannigan. And it's my father's name on whatever's waiting down there." She was already stepping into her harness. "Splice the line while we're under. We'll burn reserve slower than Hale burns his luck."

Pell checked his gauges twice, then a third time because his hands were shaking, and followed her over the rail into the cold.

* * *

Down was a country Pell was still learning to navigate. The flooded gallery swallowed the last of the surface light ten meters in, and after that they moved by lamp-beam through corridors the railway had carved and the sea had claimed—tile walls furred with white bacterial bloom, a luggage trolley standing on its side like a dead animal, the ghost-glow of their lights catching old enamel signs whose letters the water had softened into suggestion. Tamsin swam ahead on the shared line, economical as ever, and Pell matched her stroke for stroke, counting his breaths the way she'd drilled him. *Four in. Six out. The sea takes the panicked first.*

They reached the engine car on the fourth gallery turn, and that was where the dark changed texture.

Another lamp. Seaward side, moving fast, cutting through the silt haze toward the strongbox car—and behind it more beams, three, four, five, a constellation of them converging through the flooded corridor like fish that had learned to carry knives.

Hale's divers.

What happened next happened in the language of the deep, which is mostly silence punctuated by violence. One of Hale's men swam straight at Tamsin—not aggressive, exactly, just *insistent*, shoulder-checking her away from the engine car's broken window while his partner slipped inside. Tamsin caught the man's harness strap and spun him into the tile wall hard enough to raise a cloud of rust. Somewhere beyond the lamplight a regulator free-flowed, a shriek of bubbles like a kettle left on the fire. Pell grabbed the survey log's recovery bag off the engine car's floor where they'd cached it on the second tide, hugged it to his chest, and felt a hand close on his ankle.

He kicked. The hand held. In the beam-light he saw a face behind a plate glass mask—young, maybe older than Pell himself, eyes wide with the same fear Pell felt—and understood with terrible clarity that the man wasn't a monster. He was a hired hand doing arithmetic, same as all of them, and Hale had done the sums for everyone: gold divided five ways is worth more than gold divided by nine.

Then the water *moved*.

It came as a pressure wave first—a shove against the eardrums, a flutter in the gut—before the sound arrived, rolling up the tunnel from the direction of the pass mouth: a boom, deep and structural, the kind of sound stone makes when it gives up an argument it's been losing for fifteen years. Silt bloomed across the gallery like ink dropped in milk. Through it, Pell saw Hale's divers break off—all of them, at once, swimming hard for the seaward breach, because they knew something the Meridian's crew didn't yet know.

They hadn't come for the gold. Not first. They'd come to make sure no one else could reach it.

The second boom was closer, and this time the ceiling shed grit that stung Pell's cheeks through the water, and Tamsin's hand closed on his arm and hauled him not toward the surface but deeper along the gallery, away from the falling debris, into the old first-class waiting room where the roof was thickest and the tile still clung to the walls.

They crouched in the drowned dark among the ghosts of benches, sharing one lamp, and Pell got out his slate and wrote: *Line?*

Tamsin checked. Her shoulders told him the answer before her slate did.

*Cut. Seaward side. Same blade.*

* * *

On the surface, Brannigan was fighting a war on two fronts with a crew of one and a half.

The Meridian's remaining air line had survived—he'd spliced the cut main in forty minutes flat, swearing at the crimps with a fluency that would've embarrassed a dockworker—but the compressor intake was fouled with silt from the collapse, starved and overheating, and the gauge on the down-line was bleeding pressure in a way that meant either a new rupture or the worst kind of slow death. And out past the headland, Hale's nearest hull had weighed anchor and was making directly for the pass, crane booms swinging out like arms opening for an embrace.

Brannigan stood at the rail with the deckhouse phone pressed to his ear, listening to the young voice of the yard's night watchman, and made the only call there was to make.

"Get me the coast pilot out of Kellwick harbor," he said. "Any hull with a pump. Tell them there's divers trapped in the Far Horizon pass and a salvage baron about to make widows. Tell them Brannigan sent you, and tell them I'll pay in stew for the rest of their natural lives."

He hung up, rolled his sleeves to the elbow, and went to war with the compressor.

* * *

Below, the arithmetic was simpler and crueler.

One line. Two divers. The gauge on Pell's wrist said forty minutes of air if he breathed alone, and Tamsin's said thirty-one, and the nearest route to the surface was blocked by a tunnel section that was, at intervals, deciding whether to exist.

Tamsin clipped them together at the harness, shoulder to shoulder, and took the single working regulator off Pell's tank. She breathed, held up one finger—*one breath each, alternate*—and handed it back. It was the oldest trick in salvage diving and the most hated, because it worked only if both divers stayed calm, and calm is a currency that spends faster than air.

*Four in,* Pell told himself. *Six out.*

They moved through the settling gallery in a slow rhythm, passing the regulator mouth to mouth like a sacrament, Tamsin leading by touch along the guide rope she'd rigged on the first tide—her rope, her knots, her fifteen-year-old memory of these tunnels guiding them home. Once, when the silt thickened and the lamp died to a brown glow, Pell felt panic rise in him like floodwater, and Tamsin's hand found his chest through the harness and pressed, steady, twice, and he understood: *here. Now. Breathe.* He breathed.

At the third rest stop, in the black behind the collapsed ticket hall, Tamsin pulled the recovery bag from between them and pushed it into Pell's arms. Then she took his wrist and pressed his fingers to the bag, and to the shape inside it—Ilsa Vane's survey log, waterproofed, irreplaceable, the whole buried truth of the Merrow Line in a canvas sleeve—and pointed at him. At his chest. At the bag.

*You carry it. You keep it.*

Pell shook his head in the dark. *You're the diver. You're the—*

Her grip tightened on his wrist, and in the dying lamplight he saw her face behind the mask, and it wasn't the face of a rival or a captain or a liar. It was the face of someone handing over the heaviest thing she owned and trusting the weight to hold.

*I can't be trusted with it down here,* her eyes said. *I'll trade it. I'll bury it. I don't know yet. But you—you'll bring it up. That's what you do. You bring things up.*

Pell lashed the bag to his chest with shaking hands, double-knotted it the way she'd taught him, and gave her a thumbs-up that he hoped looked steadier than it felt.

Above them, faint through forty feet of grey water, came a sound he'd know anywhere: the Meridian's compressor, coughing, stuttering—and then catching, finding its rhythm, humming the note he could feel in his back teeth. Brannigan. Brannigan had won his fight.

They rose toward the sound together, sharing breath, sharing weight, the truth of the Merrow Line strapped to a nineteen-year-old's chest—and broke the surface into rain and lantern-light and the sight of four strange fishing hulls anchored in a ring around the barge, and beyond them, closing fast through the murk, the black bow of Corwin Hale's flagship, crane already lowering, net already open, coming to swallow the pass whole.
