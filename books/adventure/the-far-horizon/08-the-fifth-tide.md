## 8. The Fifth Tide

Tamsin woke to the taste of salt and the sound of Brannigan arguing with the sea.

She was on the deck of the *Meridian*, wrapped in a blanket she didn't remember being given, her hands still trembling in that fine, maddening way they did after a line went slack and the world narrowed to a single breath shared between two people. Pell lay three feet away, gray-lipped and sleeping, his chest rising in shallow, stubborn increments. Between them, coiled like something dead, hung the severed end of an air hose—cut clean, cut deliberate, cut by hands that had wanted them drowned in the dark.

Brannigan stood at the rail with his back to her, shouting down at the pump barge's engine well where young Oskar and the twins were wrestling the auxiliary compressor back to life. His voice carried the particular fury of a man who had spent four hours terrified and would rather die than admit it.

"—and if you flood that line again I'll drown you myself and save the water the trouble!"

"Brannigan." Her voice came out as gravel. He turned. For one unguarded moment she saw everything in his face—the fear, the relief, the fifteen years of loyalty he'd never once asked her to justify—and then it shuttered behind the cook's mask he wore like armor. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me." He crossed the deck and crouched beside her, pressing a tin mug into her hands. Broth, scalding, over-salted. Perfect. "Thank yourself for teaching the boy to share air calm instead of fast. Another minute of panic and I'd be hauling up two bodies instead of one diver and half a diver."

"He held," Tamsin said. It wasn't a question. She had been there, in the black, with the log pressed against her ribs and her reserve bleeding out through Hale's knife-work, and Pell had breathed when she told him to breathe and waited when she told him to wait, and somewhere in that drowning dark she had understood that the boy she'd fished off the docks five years ago had become the kind of diver she'd have followed anywhere. "He held better than I did, my first bad air."

"Everyone holds better than you did your first bad air. You bit through your own regulator." Brannigan's mouth twitched, then flattened. "Tamsin. The lines."

"I know."

"They didn't just cut ours. They anchored theirs into our mooring points. When we pulled free we nearly rolled the barge. That wasn't salvage practice. That was a man who wants us gone."

"I know." She drank the broth and let its heat settle into the hollow place inside her, the place where the survey log sat even now—wrapped in oilcloth, strapped under her bunk, heavier than any gold. Ilsa Vane's last entries. The names of the men who had ordered the Far Horizon collapse. And on the final page, in ink that hadn't faded because it had never seen light: *A. Ashgrove, countersigned.*

Her father. Her father's hand.

* * *

The crew gathered at dusk, which was not a meeting so much as a surrender to the inevitable. They crowded into the *Meridian*'s cramped galley around Brannigan's scarred table, six of them where eight should have been—Oskar still below with the compressor, and old Wren topside keeping watch on the seaward approach, because everyone understood now that watching was no longer paranoia.

Pell came last, moving carefully, favoring the ear that had taken the worst of the pressure. He wouldn't go below to rest. Tamsin hadn't ordered him to. Some debts you let a man pay standing up.

Hale's fleet was visible from the galley's porthole now, or would be by morning: three lights on the horizon where last night there had been none, riding at anchor beyond the pass like patient sharks. A day early. A full day early, when his contract had promised six tides of clear water and his word had promised a fair split. Corwin Hale had lied about the timeline, which meant he had lied about everything, which meant the only thing left to decide was what they intended to do about it.

"So we run," said Sorrel, the eldest of the twins, arms crossed, practical as a cleaver. "We've got the log. That's what matters. We take it to a magistrate, a newspaper, anyone with a conscience, and we let Hale have his gold. Gold doesn't convict anybody."

"The log convicts nobody either," Brannigan said quietly. "Not without the wreck to anchor it. A page naming founders means nothing against men who own the magistrates and half the newspapers. But a page *and* a pay-train raised from a tunnel the railway swore was empty—that's a story no one can buy quiet. Evidence needs weight, Sorrel. The wreck is the weight."

"And we can't raise the wreck before the sixth tide," Sorrel said. "Not with our compressor coughing blood and their fleet sitting on our exit lane. So we run, or we die stupid."

The silence that followed had teeth in it. Tamsin let it sit. She had learned long ago—from Ilsa Vane, though she'd hated admitting even that much—that a crew speaks truest in the pause after the argument, when everyone has said the safe thing and is waiting to see who says the real one.

It was Pell who said it.

"Why does he want us gone?" The boy's voice was hoarse from the salt air, but steady. "I keep turning it over. If Hale just wanted the gold, he could've brought his whole fleet from the start and frozen us out. Instead he hires us, gives us charts, gives us *time*—and then cuts our lines the moment we get close to something. He doesn't want us dead for the gold. He wants us dead for whatever else is down there." He looked at Tamsin, and there was no hero worship in his eyes anymore, only the harder thing that comes after it. "You knew. On the first dive. You came up shaking and told us the gallery was empty, and you knew."

The galley went very still. Brannigan set down his mug with exaggerated care.

"You're right," Tamsin said. "I lied."

She laid it out plainly, because they deserved plain: the lantern car, sealed and intact, exactly where the official survey said the collapse had crushed everything flat. Records falsified. A disaster engineered on paper before it was ever engineered in stone. And beneath it all, the order her father had signed—a name she had not yet spoken aloud, because speaking it would make it real, and she had spent fifteen years building a life on top of the place where it was buried.

"My father signed the collapse order," she said. "Ilsa Vane proved it before she died proving it. And I found her proof yesterday, forty meters down, and I couldn't bring myself to tell you, because telling you meant burying him twice."

Nobody moved. Then Brannigan reached across the table—not to touch her, but to turn his mug handle toward her, the way he'd done every morning for fifteen years, the small dumb ritual that meant *you're still one of us*. "My brother rode the Merrow," he said. "Third car. You knew that?"

Tamsin nodded. She had known for years and never once said it, and neither had he, and the silence between them had been its own kind of grave.

"He always said the tunnels sang wrong before a collapse. Said the Merrow's bones were rotten from the day they poured them." Brannigan's jaw worked. "He was right. They weren't rotten. They were *murdered*. So no, Sorrel—we don't run. I didn't come back to these waters to swim away from them."

Sorrel looked around the table, found no allies, and exhaled through her nose. "Then say what we're actually doing, Captain. Because 'raise the pay-train before six tides' was impossible this morning, and nothing's improved since."

Tamsin stood. Her body ached from the dive, from the near-drowning, from fifteen years of carrying a weight she'd mistaken for survival. But underneath the ache was something new, or something very old wearing new clothes—the feeling she used to get at the start of a survey, pencil sharp, chart blank, the whole unknown world waiting to be measured.

"Hale thinks he's running a salvage operation," she said. "He thinks the prize is gold, and that we're a loose end. He's wrong on both counts. The gold is real—I've seen the strongbox car, and it's worth more than his entire fleet. But the *wreck* is the prize. The wreck is the confession. Every rivet, every falsified manifest, every inch of tunnel that collapsed in a pattern no accident makes. Hale wants to raise the strongbox and blow the rest to rubble so no one ever asks why it fell. So we're going to do his work for him."

"We're going to raise it," Pell breathed.

"All of it. The strongbox car, the lantern car, the engine—and Ilsa's log with it, logged, witnessed, and signed by every soul on this crew, so that when it surfaces it surfaces as *ours*, not his. Six tides was always a lie; the real window closes tomorrow night when the spring tide floods the pass past diving depth. Which means we don't have a tide to spare and we don't have a man to waste." She met each of their eyes in turn. "We rig the lift bags tonight. We run the cables through the gallery while his divers are sleeping off their murder attempt. And at the fifth tide, we take everything—his gold, my father's crime, and Ilsa's truth—and we bring it up into the daylight where it belongs."

"And if the tunnels come down again?" Sorrel asked. "They came down once. Water remembers."

"Then we'll be faster than the water." Tamsin picked up the chalk and began sketching the gallery on the tabletop, quick and sure, the lines coming back to her the way they always did—as if the Far Horizon had been drawn on her heart all along and she'd only now agreed to look at it. "Wren takes the seaward watch. Oskar keeps the compressor alive if he has to feed it his boots. Twins on the cable winches. Pell—" she paused, and the boy straightened as if pulled by wire, "—you're with me. You know the gallery now. Nobody knows it better except me, and I need someone whose ears still work."

Pell grinned, gray-faced and exhausted and utterly alight. "Fifth tide," he said.

"Fifth tide," the galley echoed.

Outside, the sea was turning over toward the ebb, and far out on the dark water, Hale's three lights burned like a verdict waiting to be read. Let them burn, Tamsin thought. By tomorrow night, Corwin Hale would arrive at the Far Horizon expecting to bury a secret and find instead that the sea had given up its dead—all of them, every name, every signature, every lie—into hands he had made the mistake of calling expendable.

She chalked one last mark on the table: the strongbox car, and beside it, small and square, the shape of a survey log.

Survivors ran. Claimants stayed.

At the fifth tide, she intended to claim everything.
