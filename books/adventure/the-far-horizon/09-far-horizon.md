## 9. Far Horizon

The sixth tide came in grey and hungry.

Pell felt it before he saw it—a change in the pressure of the air inside the Meridian's wheelhouse, a low ache in his back teeth, the way the water in the bilge sloshed against the hull like something impatient. Brannigan had taught him to read tides with his body as much as his instruments. *The sea tells you when it's coming for you,* he'd said on Pell's first week aboard. *Most men just don't listen.*

Pell was listening now. The sea was saying: *last chance.*

"Line's rigged!" he called down through the deck hatch, voice cracking on the second word. He cleared his throat and tried again, deeper, steadier. "Main cable's rigged! Two hundred fathoms, shackled at the strongbox car!"

Below him, Tamsin Ashgrove stood knee-deep in the pump well, her hands moving over the valve wheels like she'd been born to them, and maybe she had—maybe everyone on this barge had been born to something they'd spent years pretending not to be. She looked up at him through the hatch, and in the lamplight her face was pale and set and absolutely certain.

"Then we pull," she said.

* * *

They had worked through the fifth tide without sleeping, and the sixth tide without stopping. The Meridian's pumps thundered day and night, driving the flooded gallery below the pass down by inches—precious, agonizing inches—while Pell and Vess ran dive lines into the dark and Brannigan kept the old barge's heart beating with wrenches, wire, and what he called *aggressive encouragement.* The plan was Tamsin's, drawn in charcoal on the underside of a chart table: no salvage fleet could work the tunnels fast enough to beat the collapse she knew was coming, so they wouldn't try. They would raise only what mattered. The survey log, already recovered. And the strongbox car of the Merrow pay-train—the gold that had lain fifteen years at the bottom of the Far Horizon pass while men lied about how it got there.

Hale's fleet had been sighted at dawn, three hulls standing in from the open water, black funnels smoking against the grey. Six hours out, maybe less. Hale had lied about his timeline once already; nobody aboard the Meridian believed the six hours would be six.

"He'll run his divers straight up the seaward gallery," Vess said, coiling line with quick, angry hands. "Cut us off from behind like last time."

"Let him come," Brannigan rumbled. He sat on an ammunition crate they'd repurposed as a stool, mending the air hose Tamsin and Pell had shared two nights ago—the one with Hale's knife-mark in it. His big hands moved gently over the scarred rubber. "This time we're not down there when he does."

Pell understood then, watching them all move around each other in the cramped wheelhouse, that this was what a crew was. Not a payroll. Not a contract. A knot of people who had every reason to scatter and didn't. He'd joined the Meridian for the pay and stayed for the stories; somewhere between the third tide and the fifth, the stories had stopped being about other people's courage and started being about theirs.

Tamsin climbed up onto the deck, dripping, and put her hand flat against the main cable where it ran taut off the bow winch and down into the green-black water of the pass. Pell had seen divers do that—touch the line like a pulse, feeling for the tremor of the load below.

"She's holding," Tamsin said. "Winch her slow. Brannigan, give me everything the pumps can spare and nothing more—we drop the gallery level another foot and we risk bringing the roof down on the car itself."

"And if we don't?" Pell asked.

She looked at him, and for a moment he saw past the captain to the woman underneath—the one who had walked away from this place fifteen years ago and carried its weight ever since.

"If we don't," she said quietly, "the tide does it for us. Winch."

* * *

The strongbox car came up out of the dark like a resurrection.

Pell stood at the bow rail and watched the cable sing, watched the water below the pass boil and swirl as forty tons of iron and sealed gold rose through the drowned gallery, inch by groaning inch. The Meridian listed hard to port under the strain, her old timbers popping like distant gunfire. Brannigan threw his whole body against the pump throttle. Vess fed the winch. And Tamsin stood at the rail with the water-light playing across her face, counting under her breath—not fathoms, Pell realized, but years.

Fifteen feet down. Ten. Five.

The car broke the surface at slack water, streaming weed and rust, its lantern-glass windows starred with silt but unbroken, and even fouled with fifteen years of the sea it looked like what it was: a coffin someone had finally bothered to open. Pell whooped—couldn't help it—and Vess laughed out loud, and even Brannigan let go a long breath that sounded almost like a prayer.

Then the mountain answered.

It came up through the keel first, a deep grinding shudder, and then the sound itself rolled out of the pass like a giant clearing its throat—the groan of stone shifting after fifteen years of false quiet. The surface of the water dimpled and danced. Silt bloomed up from below in great brown clouds.

"The roof," Vess whispered. "It's going."

"It's been going since the sabotage," Tamsin said. Her voice was calm in the way Pell had learned meant the opposite. "That's why the founders used explosives instead of patience—it was never stable. Get lines on the car. We tow it clear before the pass closes."

They were fastening the tow bridle—Pell hooking shackles with numb fingers, Vess hauling, Brannigan cursing the rusted rings with inventive fluency—when Tamsin went still.

Pell saw it happen. Saw her head come up like a deer scenting weather. Saw her look at the water, then at the tunnel mouth below the pass, where the outer gallery ran dark and drowning.

"The log," she said.

"It's aboard," Pell said. "You gave it to me—I've got it in the—" He patted the oilskin wallet inside his coat, and his blood went cold. The wallet was there. The log was not. In the chaos of the fifth-tide rescue, the sharing of air, the cutting free of the fouled line—he'd stowed it in the dive locker. The dive locker on the submerged staging platform. The platform that was going under with the tide.

"Ilsa's log is in the locker," he said, and hated how young his voice sounded.

Tamsin was already pulling on her fins.

"No—" Pell grabbed her arm. "Captain, the roof's coming down, you can't—"

"Pell." She put her hand over his, and her grip was gentle and immovable. "That log is the only true thing anyone ever wrote about this place. It names the dead properly. I am not leaving it in the dark with the liars." She met his eyes. "You've shared my air once already. You don't have to do it twice. Stay on the line."

But he didn't stay on the line. Of course he didn't. He followed her down.

* * *

The gallery was dying around them.

Pell swam the flooded tunnel with Tamsin's fin-strokes pulsing ahead of him, their lamp-beams sweeping across stone that was visibly, audibly coming apart—cracks racing along the ceiling like lightning frozen mid-strike, chunks of rock the size of carts sloughing away and vanishing into the brown murk below. The water was thick with silt; he navigated by the guide line under one hand and Tamsin's shape ahead of him and nothing else in the world.

The staging platform loomed out of the cloud. The dive locker hung open, its contents scattered by the current—and there, snagged against the ladder rungs, the oilskin roll of Ilsa Vane's last survey log, fluttering like a drowned bird.

Tamsin took it. Wrapped it against her chest with both arms. Looked at Pell and pointed up, and he nodded, and that was when the ceiling came down between them and the exit.

He heard it more than saw it—a concussion through the water that hit his chest like a fist, and then the guide line snapped taut and went slack, cut clean, and the tunnel behind them filled with falling stone. The way out was gone. The way out was gone, and his gauge needle was kissing the red, and Tamsin Ashgrove was looking at him across four feet of black water with the same expression she'd worn at the rail: certain, and calm, and lying about it.

She grabbed his wrist. Pressed his hand to her chest—no, to the regulator hose at her hip, to the emergency bottle, the reserve, the last breaths either of them would get. She held up fingers: *one breath each. Then we swim.*

They swam.

Pell had no memory afterward of the distance, only of moments: the burn in his lungs becoming a fire becoming a strange white calm; Tamsin's grip on his collar, towing him when his legs quit; the silt parting ahead of them into a wedge of grey-green light that might have been the surface or might have been heaven and not caring which. His vision narrowed to a coin. The coin shrank.

And then hands—huge, impossible hands—closed on his harness and hauled, and the world tore open into wind and rain and Brannigan's voice roaring like the whole storm given words, and Pell was vomiting seawater across the Meridian's deck with the strongbox car lashed beside him and the Far Horizon pass sealing itself behind them in a rising wall of foam and falling stone.

Brannigan had gone in after them. Off the tow line, on a single breath, into a collapsing tunnel, because that was who Brannigan was—that was who they all were, apparently, now, whether they'd planned it or not. He knelt on the deck getting his own breath back, one huge hand still fisted in Pell's harness, and when he could speak he said, "Never. Again. Either of you," and pulled them both into a crushing embrace that cracked ribs and meant everything.

Behind them, the pass closed with a sound like a door. Fifteen years of secrets, folded shut under sixty feet of new stone.

Ahead of them, out on the grey water, three black-funneled hulls came standing in—Corwin Hale's fleet, right on schedule, arriving to claim a wreck that no longer existed, in a tunnel no diver would ever enter again.

Vess appeared at the rail beside Pell, soaked and shaking and grinning like a maniac. "Well," she said. "What do we tell them?"

Tamsin stood at the stern with Ilsa Vane's log safe inside her coat and her hand resting on the lashed cable of the strongbox car, watching Hale's flagship grow larger against the rain. When she turned to Pell, she wasn't smiling exactly—but something in her face had surfaced along with everything else, something that had been underwater a long time.

"We tell them the truth," she said. "For once, we tell everyone the truth. Signal the flagship. Tell Corwin Hale the Merrow Line has new owners—and that we'd be obliged if he'd step aside while the workers' cooperative takes delivery of its gold."

Pell stared at her. "We don't own anything yet."

"Not yet," Tamsin agreed, and turned back toward the incoming fleet, toward the fortune at her back and the proof against her chest, toward whatever came next. "Signal him anyway, Pell. Let's go introduce ourselves."
