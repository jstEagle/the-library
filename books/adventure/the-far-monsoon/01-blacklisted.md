## 1. Blacklisted

The rain in Port Ammerly came down like an apology nobody meant — thin, persistent, and too late to do anyone any good. It fell on the tin roofs of the fish market and ran in brown ropes down the gutters, and it fell on Kestrel Ishikawa as he stood outside the Guide Registry Office with his hands in his pockets and his collar up, watching the clerk through the glass.

He had been watching for twenty minutes. Long enough to memorize the man's habits: the way he straightened the license plaques on the wall every quarter hour, the way he drank his tea in three long swallows and then stared into the cup as though it owed him money. Long enough for the rain to find the seam at Kestrel's left shoulder and begin its slow work through wool and shirt and skin.

The registry was open. That was the whole point of coming here instead of sending a letter. A man could refuse a letter. A man could not so easily refuse another man standing in front of him, wet and sober and holding his papers flat on the counter.

Kestrel pushed through the door.

Inside smelled of ink and damp paper and the particular staleness of rooms where decisions about other people's lives were made by men who never went outdoors. The clerk looked up from his ledger, took in the height of him, the set of the shoulders, the weathered face beneath the wet hair — and Kestrel watched recognition arrive like a lamp being lit behind the eyes.

"No," the clerk said.

"I haven't asked for anything yet."

"You're Ishikawa. The mountain guide." He said *mountain guide* the way other men said *counterfeiter*. "You're on the list."

"I'm aware I'm on the list." Kestrel set his papers on the counter anyway: certification, references, the old expedition logs he'd carried across two borders because they were the only things he owned that proved he could do the one thing he knew how to do. "I'm asking you to look at them anyway. Read the log from the Karst Traverse. Fourteen days above the snowline, no losses. You won't find a better—"

"The list is the list." The clerk was already standing, already reaching beneath the counter for something — a whistle, a stick, whatever small authority port towns issued to small men. "Two countries bar you. Two. You think I'm going to be the one who lets you sign my book?"

"Neither country ever told me why."

"They don't have to tell you why. That's what makes it a blacklist and not a conversation." The clerk came around the counter, and two dockhands who had been warming themselves by the stove rose without being asked, the way dogs rise when their master shifts in a chair. "Out. And if you come back I'll have the harbor watch walk you to the ferry myself."

One of the dockhands put a hand on Kestrel's arm — not hard, almost apologetic, the touch of a man who'd guided timber crews once himself and knew what he was laying hands on. Kestrel looked at it. He did not shake it off, because shaking it off would have been the beginning of something longer, and he had learned — expensively, publicly, in a hearing room in another country with rain on different windows — that the world did not reward a blacklisted man for being right. It only rewarded him for being quiet.

He picked up his papers. He tucked them inside his coat, against his chest, where they wouldn't get wet. He walked out into the rain.

Behind him, faint through the door before it swung shut, he heard the clerk say to the dockhands, "Ishikawa. His grandfather was the surveyor, you know. The one who—" and then the door closed and the rain took the rest.

* * *

Port Ammerly in low season was a town holding its breath between storms. The fishing fleet sat at anchor like tired animals, tarps lashed over their gear, masts nodding. The taverns were half-empty and priced accordingly. Kestrel had a room above the Salt & Anchor that cost him four coppers a night and came with a window that didn't close and a view of the harbor wall, and he had been there eleven days, which was ten days longer than his money had planned.

He ate that evening in the tavern's common room because the fire was there and his room had no fire, only the window and its draft. Stew, bread, one beer. He counted what remained after: enough for six more nights at four coppers, or five if he wanted to keep eating, or three if he wanted to keep eating and buy lamp oil, and after that —

After that was a country he didn't let himself look at directly. After that was selling the climbing irons. His grandfather's compass he would sell last, or never, but the irons were steel and steel had weight in any market.

He was halfway through the stew when the woman came in out of the rain.

She was wrong for the room immediately, the way a heron is wrong for a chicken coop. Grey traveling coat, government cut, boots that had seen actual mud rather than the mud of fashionable streets. She stood in the doorway a moment, letting her eyes adjust, and Kestrel saw her do what he himself did in every new room: read the exits, count the people, mark the ones who mattered. Then she crossed the floor directly to his table, which told him she had marked him from the door.

"Kestrel Ishikawa."

Not a question. He put down his spoon. "You've got me at a disadvantage, which I assume is deliberate."

"Corporal Sef Anhari. Office of Territorial Survey, Ministry of Interior." She didn't offer to sit. Government people never offered; they waited until the shape of the room made sitting inevitable. "You were at the registry office this afternoon."

"You have someone watching the registry office."

"We have someone watching everyone worth watching. In low season that's a short list, and you're on it twice — once under your own name and once under your grandfather's." She said it without cruelty, the way a doctor says a diagnosis. "May I?"

She sat anyway, pulling the stool over, and set a leather folio on the table between them. Rain ticked against the windows. Somewhere behind the bar a man was arguing cheerfully about the price of eels.

"I'll save us both time," Anhari said. "You're going to tell me to go to hell, because that's what pride requires, and you're a proud man — it's practically all that's left of your reputation. So while you're composing that, I'm going to talk."

She opened the folio. Maps. Photographs. A manifest written in a hand so precise it looked printed.

"Three weeks ago, a government survey team entered the Sareth Valley. Four people. Team leader Oren Vasquez, twenty years' service. Engineer Mara Quill. Cartographer Den Fell. Junior surveyor Tomas Brandt. Their assignment was routine boundary verification in the highlands — flood-country mapping, tedious work, the kind of work nobody fights over." She turned a map toward him. The Sareth Valley lay along it like a green vein, the river braiding and rebraiding through country that even on paper looked difficult. "Eleven days ago they went silent. Scheduled check-in, no signal. We assumed equipment failure. Then a supply flight over the lower valley spotted their base camp."

"And?"

"And it was intact. Tents staked. Food cached under stone. Instruments packed for travel — packed, Mr. Ishikawa, the way you pack instruments when you intend to carry them somewhere, not when you intend to abandon them. No sign of struggle. No sign of illness. No tracks leading out." She looked up. "None. In ground soft enough to hold a print from a week-old bird."

Kestrel found he had stopped eating. He made himself take another bite, mostly to prove to both of them that he wasn't interested. The stew had gone cold and tasted of it.

"People leave," he said. "Teams fracture. Someone finds religion, someone finds a lover, someone finds a boat south."

"Four people, together, leaving no tracks, taking their instruments, and not reporting in for eleven days? Through country controlled by the Halvard Trust, which has denied our liaison requests twice?" Anhari closed the folio halfway, keeping one photograph visible — the camp, shot from the air, small and pale as a bone dropped in green water. "We sent a constable patrol up the valley road. They were turned back at the Trust's boundary markers. Politely. With rifles they didn't bother hiding."

"So ask the Trust."

"We did. Edmund Halvard expressed his deep concern and offered the Ministry the services of his own guides." Her mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "His own guides, Mr. Ishikawa. Handpicked, loyal, and answerable to the man we'd be investigating. The Ministry declined. And now the Ministry is out of options, because every licensed tracker in this country either works for the Trust, fears the Trust, or isn't good enough — and the one tracker both governments agree is the best in the region can't legally tie his own bootlaces inside a registry office."

The rain thickened against the glass. Kestrel looked at the photograph again despite himself. He knew that kind of camp. He could read it the way Anhari presumably read her manifests: the windward placement of the tents, the cache built to survive scavengers, the careful geometry of people who expected to come back.

"What makes you think I'd say yes?" he said. "You just watched me get thrown out of a registry office. My name is worth less than the paper it's slandered on. If I go up that valley and something happens to your team, I'm not a rescuer, I'm a suspect. Blacklisted man, last seen entering restricted territory. They'd hang me with the same rope they used to ruin my grandfather."

Anhari didn't flinch at the name. He noted that. Most people flinched.

"That's true," she said. "It's also true that you'll say yes, and we both know why, and it isn't the money."

"It's always partly the money."

"It's partly the money." She slid a folded paper across the table. He didn't touch it. "Contract fee, hazard supplement, paid regardless of outcome. Enough to keep you fed through two winters. But here's the rest of it, and I'll say it plainly because you strike me as a man who's had enough of plainness withheld." She leaned forward. "Your grandfather mapped that valley. Your family's name is buried somewhere under those boundary stones, and whatever happened to that survey team happened in the shadow of the same machine that ruined him and then got around to ruining you. You can sit in this tavern counting coppers until the monsoon comes, or you can go up there and find out what the water's hiding. One of those choices leaves you a guide. The other leaves you a man who used to be one."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. Behind the bar the argument about eels had resolved itself into laughter. The fire popped. Outside, the harbor lights smeared themselves across the wet dark, and far inland — so far he couldn't possibly have seen it, and saw it anyway, the way you see a scar through a sleeve — the mountains stood up against the sky where the Sareth began.

"My grandfather didn't talk about the valley," Kestrel said finally. "Twenty years in that house and he never once said the word *Sareth*. He'd go quiet when the maps came on the radio. Like a man listening for footsteps." He turned the contract paper over with one finger, not picking it up. "What exactly am I agreeing to? Find them alive?"

"Find them. Alive preferred. Evidence of what happened required." Anhari stood, buttoning her coat, business concluded in her mind whether he'd signed or not. "You leave Thursday. There's a pilot who runs the lower river — Voss, out of the old salt docks. Unlicensed herself, as it happens, so the two of you should get along famously. She knows the channels. She doesn't ask questions. I'd recommend you extend her the same courtesy."

"And if I say no?"

"Then Thursday I hire someone worse, and in a month I write letters to four families, and you go on counting coppers." She paused at the edge of the firelight, rain-wet and certain. "But you won't say no. Pride brought you to that registry office today, Mr. Ishikawa. Pride's a fine thing for getting a man to the door. It's purpose that carries him up the mountain."

She left the contract on the table and went out into the rain without looking back, which was, he thought, exactly what he would have done.

* * *

He read the contract three times that night by the light of a lamp he couldn't afford, sitting on the bed in the room with the broken window while the draft moved the flame around like a nervous animal. It was clean, as government contracts went. Clean language, clean terms, a clause about discretion that he read twice and understood completely: whatever he found in the Sareth Valley belonged first to the Ministry, and only afterward to the truth.

He thought about refusing. He actually lay back and constructed the refusal, sentence by sentence, the speech he'd give Anhari's memory — *I don't work for the people who blacklisted me, I don't dance when the ministry whistles* — and it was a good speech, and it would have felt wonderful for approximately one evening, right up until the fourth copper night became the fifth.

Then he took out the compass.

It was a small thing, brass-cased, the lid dented where a rock had caught it forty years ago on a ridge whose name his grandfather had never spoken. Inside, the needle still swung true, settling north with a small patient insistence, as if it had spent all these decades waiting for someone to ask it a real question. On the inner lid, scratched in a surveyor's cramped hand, were coordinates. He'd copied them out as a boy, tracing each numeral, and his grandfather had caught him at it and gone white as chalk and taken the compass away for a month — and never explained, and never erased the numbers either.

Kestrel held the compass over the contract and compared the scratched coordinates, number by number, with the survey team's assigned grid.

They matched. Not approximately. Exactly. To the digit.

The needle trembled against its glass, pointing north, pointing inland, pointing up the drowned green vein of the Sareth toward whatever had waited forty years for somebody to come asking questions. Kestrel Ishikawa sat in a rented room with rain coming through a broken window, a disgraced name, and eleven days of money, and he laughed once — short, bitter, surprised out of him like a cough.

"Well," he said to the compass, to the valley, to the dead man who had never once said the word. "I suppose it's Thursday, then."

He signed the contract. Outside, far off over the rooftops, thunder rolled up from the sea — early, the fishermen would say later, weeks early — and the first true breath of the monsoon moved through Port Ammerly like a rumor of everything to come.
