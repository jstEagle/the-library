## 2. The Leaking Boat

The boat was called the *Sparrowhawk*, which struck Kestrel as optimistic in the way that most names on the Sareth were — a wish spoken over water that had heard too many wishes and drowned them all.

She sat low at her moorings in the grey dawn light of Tarn's Landing, a river pilot's boat built for cargo and patched for survival: forty feet of riveted hull and salt-scoured decking, a wheelhouse the size of a coffin standing upright, crates lashed amidships under oilcloth gone stiff with age. Her paint had once been green. Now she was the color of the river itself, as if she'd been dipped in it so long the water had claimed her by degrees and only lent her back for work.

The woman scrubbing her gunwale looked up when his boots hit the dock planks.

"You're staring," she said, without pausing in her scrubbing. "Either you want passage or you want to buy scrap. Which is it?"

"Passage. Upstream. As far as the old survey cut above Kellin Bend."

That stopped her hand. She straightened, and Kestrel got his first proper look at Nadya Voss: mid-thirties maybe, dark hair hacked off short at the jaw in the practical way of people who worked around machinery that liked to grab things, a face browned and wind-scored, eyes the flat grey-green of river water over silt. There was a scar through one eyebrow that had been stitched badly by someone in a hurry.

"The survey cut." She said it slowly, tasting it. "Nobody goes up there. The Trust closed that stretch two seasons ago."

"I know."

"And yet here you are, wanting to go there."

"I'm paid to."

She leaned on her scrub brush and looked him over with the frank appraisal of someone pricing livestock. "You've got government-issue boots and a civilian coat, a pack packed like a man who expects to sleep rough, and the look of somebody who hasn't slept well in a month. You're either a surveyor or you're hunting one." Her eyes narrowed. "Or I know you from somewhere."

Kestrel said nothing. The river said something instead — a long, low groan of current against the pilings, the sound the Sareth made when it was thinking about people.

"You're Ishikawa," she said suddenly, snapping her fingers as if swatting a fly. "Kestrel Ishikawa. The mountain guide. The one they pulled off the registries in Vhalen *and* Corisande. Two countries. That's not easy to manage — you have to really work at getting thrown out of two countries' registries." Something shifted in her face, not quite a smile. "I read about the Karst Ridge thing. Four clients. Two dead."

"One dead," Kestrel said. His voice came out level, which cost him more than shouting would have. "And the inquiry found no fault with my decisions. The blacklist was administrative."

"Administrative." She rolled the word around like a stone in her mouth. "Sure. Mine was administrative too, if you want to trade stories, but mine's cheaper, so I'll keep it." She turned back to her scrubbing. "No."

"No?"

"No. I don't run strangers into restricted water, and I especially don't run blacklisted guides into restricted water, because when the Trust asks me why — and they will ask — 'he seemed honest' is not going to hold up at the toll office." Scrub, scrub, scrub. "Find another boat."

"There is no other boat. I checked every mooring from the customs house down. Half of them won't take the upper river this season, and the other half are Trust-contracted, which means they'll report me before I'm out of sight of the dock." He kept his voice reasonable, though reasonableness felt like swallowing glass. "You're the only independent hull on this stretch."

"Lucky me." But she'd paused again, and he watched her weigh something — the same arithmetic he'd done himself all night in the doorway of the registry office, pride on one side of the scale and an empty wallet on the other.

"Triple the going rate," Kestrel said. "Half now."

Now she did turn fully around, and the look she gave him was long and measuring. "How much is half?"

He told her. Her eyebrows went up despite herself, then came back down hard, as if she were annoyed at them for it.

"That's desperate money," she said. "That's *I'm-going-up-that-river-or-die-trying* money. What's up there, Ishikawa? Bodies?"

"Maybe. A missing survey team. Government contract. Recovery and assessment."

"Recovery." She snorted. "You mean find out if they're floating." She looked upstream, where the Sareth bent away north between walls of dripping green, already dark with the season's first swelling. The monsoon was weeks out still, but the high country had started its slow exhale of meltwater, and everyone who lived on the river could feel the change coming the way animals feel weather.

"Three days up to Kellin Bend, two more to the cut, if the channels behave," she said at last. "They won't. They never do." She stuck out a hand, calloused palm first. "Half now, half on return, and you haul your own gear, cook your own food, and stay out of my wheelhouse unless I ask. And if the Trust stops us, you're my hired deckhand and you don't talk. Your voice sounds like trouble. Deckhands don't have voices."

Kestrel shook her hand. Her grip was strong and dry and brief. "Deal."

"Don't thank me," Nadya Voss said, turning back to her gunwale. "I already regret it."

* * *

They cast off within the hour, and within two hours Kestrel understood the true shape of his bargain.

It began as a sound. Under the diesel clatter and the river's constant commentary there was another noise, fainter, rhythmic — a sort of liquid knocking, like a knuckle rapping the inside of the hull. He noticed it while coiling line in the bow, dismissed it, noticed it again. Knock. Pause. Knock-knock. Water finding its way in and being thrown back out by the motion of the boat, or water settling into some cavity it wasn't meant to occupy.

He made his way aft along the narrow deck, ducking under the stays, and found the bilge pump running. That wasn't strange in itself; every working boat ran its pump. What was strange was the small glass inspection port set into the deck beside it, and what he could see through it: water standing four inches deep in the bilge, brown and patient, rising visibly against the struts even as the pump chewed at it.

He knocked on the wheelhouse door frame. Nadya stood at the wheel with one hand, chart case open on the shelf beside her, steering by eye and memory through a reach where the channel braided itself into three silver threads between gravel bars.

"Bilge has four inches in it," he said.

"It always has four inches in it."

"It had three an hour ago. I checked when we loaded."

Her jaw tightened. In the wheelhouse mirror he saw her glance down, quick, involuntary, toward the deck plates — checking whether he'd actually looked, deciding how much of the truth the situation required. He'd seen that exact calculation on clients' faces a hundred times, usually right before they lied about their experience on ice.

"She takes a little water," Nadya said. "Old hull. Riveted construction, forty years of service. The pump handles it."

"The pump is handling it the way a man with a bucket handles a sinking ship — adequately, until the weather changes."

"You've been aboard six hours. You don't get an opinion yet."

"Four inches of opinion," Kestrel said. "Rising."

For a moment he thought she was going to put him off at the next gravel bar, consequences be damned. Then something in her expression gave way — not softening exactly, but a kind of tired honesty, the look of a woman setting down a weight she'd been carrying alone.

"There's a seam aft of the engine bed," she admitted. "Opened up last spring when I grounded on the Maren shoals. I welded it. It held through the dry season." She shrugged, elaborate and unconvincing. "Welds and cold water have opinions about each other."

"How bad?"

"If I keep her out of weather and don't push her hard against current? Manageable." She met his eyes in the mirror. "If we get a squall on the upper reaches, it gets interesting."

"*Interesting* is not a word I want attached to the hull."

"Then you picked the wrong river, Ishikawa. Everything on the Sareth is interesting. That's why they pay triple rates." She flicked the chart closed. "There's a spare bucket under the thwart. Consider it your opinion, officially registered."

He found the bucket. It was dented, patched at the handle, and stained with the history of many previous opinions. He stowed it where he could reach it fast, and settled into the rhythm of the river, and tried not to listen to the knocking.

* * *

The friction found its full voice that evening, when they tied up against a rotting timber landing below an abandoned toll station and the question of watches came up.

"I take first and last," Nadya said, handing him a tin plate of rice and dried fish that was, he had to admit, better than he'd expected. "You take middle. Middle watch is nothing anyway — the channel's straight for eight miles and there's no traffic after dark."

"I'll take first," Kestrel said. "You've been on the wheel since dawn."

"I've been on the wheel since dawn because it's *my wheel*. Watch rotation isn't charity, it's scheduling." She sat down across the tiny galley table from him, and the lantern between them threw her shadow huge against the bulkhead. "Besides, I don't sleep well the first night with a stranger aboard. You'd be watching a woman lie awake listening to you breathe. Nobody wants that."

"What I want," Kestrel said, and heard his own voice come out sharper than he intended, "is to be treated like a professional. I've spent eleven years reading rivers and ridgelines. I've run white water in three ranges. I am not a passenger."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "You're a famous passenger. That's different and worse." She pointed her spoon at him. "Karst Ridge, Ishikawa. Walk me through it. Because I've read the inquiry summary — the public one, the one they let newspapers print — and it reads like a man took four paying clients above the snowline in a window every guide in the province knew was closing. Two of them didn't come down. And somehow the paperwork says nobody's fault, which on any river I've ever worked means somebody paid somebody, and the somebody doing the paying wasn't you, because you're eating dried fish on a leaking boat with a blacklisted pilot instead of drinking brandy in Vhalen."

The lantern hissed. Somewhere out in the dark the river moved against the pilings, patient as erosion.

"The storm came early," Kestrel said. Each word was placed like a foot on questionable ice. "The forecast said eighteen hours. We had six. I made the call to push for the ridge saddle instead of retreating, because retreat meant two days in the open drainage and I judged the saddle reachable. I was wrong about the timing. One client died in the slide. The inquiry cleared me because the facts cleared me. The blacklist happened afterward, quietly, because the family had connections and grief needs somewhere to go." He set down his spoon. "You can believe that or not. It doesn't change what I'm paid to do."

Nadya studied him across the lantern for a long moment, and whatever she saw there made her look away first, out the little wheelhouse window at the black water.

"Grief needs somewhere to go," she repeated softly. "Yeah. It does." She stood, collected the plates, and said, without turning around: "First watch is yours. Wake me at midnight. Don't wander past the bow line — there's a snag field just downstream of the landing and the markers are all rotted out."

She disappeared into the stern cabin, and Kestrel sat alone with the lantern and the knocking of the water in the hull, feeling the strange double weight of having defended himself and given nothing away.

* * *

The squall came at two in the morning, out of the northwest, with no warning worth the name.

Kestrel felt it first as a pressure change, a tightening behind the ears that eleven years of mountains had wired into him deeper than thought. Then the river surface went rough-textured under the moon's dying light, then the rain arrived — not gradually but all at once, a solid cold wall of it, and the wind behind it flattened the water into moving hills.

He had the engine throttled down and the bow turned into the chop before Nadya stumbled out of her cabin in an oilskin thrown over bare shoulders, hair plastered flat, eyes wide awake and furious.

"Why didn't you wake me—"

"No time. Bilge."

She didn't argue. That was the thing he would remember later — that there was no argument in her at all once the situation became real. She dropped down the engine hatch, hauled the secondary pump out of its bracket, and when its worn impeller chattered and seized on the first pull, she swore once, briefly, with real artistry, and reached for the manual handle.

Kestrel already had the bucket.

What followed was ninety minutes of the purest work he had ever done. No words. None needed. She pumped, he bailed, they swapped without discussion when his arms burned and hers did, passing the handle and the bucket through the hatch like a ritual older than both of them. The *Sparrowhawk* bucked and wallowed, shipping green water over the rails, and below decks the seam aft of the engine bed sang its thin high note of admission, and the bilge rose and fell and rose, four inches, five, six — and then, as the squall spent its first fury and settled into steady hammering rain, five, four, holding.

Lightning walked along the far ridge, printing the valley in negative: black trees, black water, the white stutter of the falls above Kellin Bend miles upstream. Thunder rolled down the gorge like furniture being moved in a house the size of the sky.

At some point — he couldn't have said when — they stopped swapping and simply worked together, her pumping while he braced the hatch cover that was rattling loose, him bailing while she wedged a wedge of scrap wood against the worst-working seam. Their hands overlapped on things. Neither of them remarked on it.

When the wind finally backed off toward grey dawn, the *Sparrowhawk* rode battered but alive, and the bilge stood at three inches, and the rain fell straight and gentle as if apologizing.

They sat on the engine housing side by side, soaked through, sharing a thermos of tea that tasted of rust and cardamom. The river slid past, high and brown and powerful, carrying whole trees southward like matchsticks.

"Well," Nadya said at last.

"Well," Kestrel said.

She looked at him sideways. Rain dripped off her chin. "You bail like a man who's done it before."

"Once or twice."

"Karst Ridge?"

"Karst Ridge." He wrapped both hands around the thermos cup. "We bailed for nine hours. It didn't matter. Some boats you can't save." He hadn't meant to say it aloud. It came out anyway, quiet, into the rain.

Nadya was silent for a while. When she spoke, her voice had lost its edge entirely, and that was somehow more disarming than anything sharp she'd said.

"My license," she said. "Since we're trading. I ran cargo for men I shouldn't have. Not once — for two years. Good money, quiet docks, no questions. And then one night the cargo was people, and I found out halfway across the Maren flats, and I brought them in anyway because turning back meant the men, and delivering them meant the law. I chose the law." She turned the cup in her hands. "They took the license anyway. Complicity, they called it. Administrative."

The rain ticked on the wheelhouse roof. Down in the bilge, the water knocked its patient knuckle against the hull.

"So when I say I know what a blacklist is," Nadya said, "I'm not guessing at yours. I'm telling you mine fits right alongside it." She drained her tea and stood, joints cracking, and looked down at him with those flat river-water eyes, and something in them had changed — not warmth, not yet, but a kind of truce, the ceasefire signed between people who have bailed the same boat.

"Get some sleep, Ishikawa. We make Kellin Bend by tomorrow night if the channels behave." She paused at the hatch. "They won't. But if they did."

Kestrel lay in the narrow bunk listening to the rain ease and the engine knock and the water knock back, and found, to his surprise, that he slept.

He woke past noon to the smell of frying fish and the boat running smooth and strong, and came forward to find Nadya at the wheel — and stopped in the wheelhouse doorway, because her manner had changed. The sharp tongue was still there in the set of her mouth, but her eyes had gone distant and careful, fixed on the water ahead with an attention that was more than navigation.

She had the chart open on the shelf, and her finger was resting on it, very lightly, on a stretch of the upper river marked with faded contour lines and a notation in old ink: *restricted — Trust survey zone.*

"We'll be entering the closed water tomorrow morning," she said, without looking at him. "And there's something about the route you're following, Ishikawa. Something I should probably tell you before we get there." Her finger tapped the chart once, twice. "Because I've run this stretch before. And last time I did, I was carrying cargo for the Halvard Trust — and what they had me hauling upriver wasn't supplies."

She finally turned to look at him, and the river-grey eyes were troubled.

"It was chain. Survey chain, and brass boundary markers. Enough of them to redraw the map of the whole valley."
