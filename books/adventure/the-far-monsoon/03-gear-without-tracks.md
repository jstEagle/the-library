## 3. Gear Without Tracks

The Sareth Valley did not welcome them so much as swallow them.

For two days the river had narrowed and darkened, folding itself between walls of rain-forest that leaned over the water like conspirators sharing a secret. The air was thick enough to chew — wet earth, rotting fruit, the green metallic smell of leaves crushed under their own weight. Somewhere above the canopy, thunder grumbled without conviction, a storm that had been threatening for hours and couldn't be bothered to arrive.

Kestrel stood in the bow with the survey coordinates copied onto oilcloth in his pocket and watched the banks for anything that shouldn't be there.

"Your camp should be past this bend," Nadya called from the tiller. She had to raise her voice over the engine's laboring cough. "If your government friends gave you honest numbers."

"They gave me numbers," Kestrel said. "Whether they're honest is the question I've been asking since they handed me the contract."

"You always this cheerful?"

"I'm not being paid to be cheerful."

"No," she said, and there was something almost like sympathy in it, which from Nadya Voss was more unsettling than an insult. "You're being paid to find four people who walked into a valley that eats surveyors."

The bend came, and the river widened into a drowned meadow where the current slowed and spread itself thin across a field of half-submerged grass. And there, on the eastern bank, on the one shelf of dry ground the flood hadn't yet claimed — canvas.

White canvas, staked taut, catching what little light filtered through the canopy.

Kestrel felt his pulse change before he understood why. It wasn't relief. Relief would come later, maybe, if the tents held living men. What he felt first was the tracker's old cold attention, the part of him that had made his name in three ranges and unmade it in two, waking up like a dog that hears a sound its owner can't.

"Nadya. That bank, thirty meters past the big fig. There's a gravel shelf."

"I see it." She swung the boat wide, reading the current the way he read ground — instinctively, continuously, without seeming to think about it at all. "Water's shallow over the shelf but the approach is soft mud. You'll want to jump, not step."

"Noted."

"And Kestrel." She killed the engine and let the boat drift, her voice dropping to match the hush of the flooded meadow. "I've run this stretch six times. I have never once seen anyone camp here. Not traders, not poachers, not smugglers, and I've carried cargo for all three."

"Why not?"

She nodded upstream, toward the gorge whose roar was just beginning to make itself heard beneath the birdsong. "Because everything below that gorge is Trust land, and everything above it belongs to the river. This shelf is in neither country. Nobody camps in a place that belongs to nobody. Nobody camps in a place where no one will hear you scream."

* * *

He jumped the gap as she'd told him to, boots hitting gravel, the boat swinging away behind him with Nadya's hand raised in a lazy salute that didn't quite hide her tension. He'd see her again when he'd read the camp. If the camp could be read.

It could. That was the first wrong thing.

A camp that has been abandoned in panic tells you everything: scattered gear, torn ground, the archaeology of fear. A camp where men died tells you other things, worse ones, written in flies and silence. But this camp —

This camp was *tidy*.

Four dome tents, staked with proper tension, guy-lines dressed and coiled. The flysheets were still on. Rain had pooled in the sag of one vestibule, and mosquito larvae wriggled in the standing water, which meant the tents had been sealed for at least five days, probably seven. Seven days of monsoon season, and no one had come back to shake the water off.

Kestrel crouched at the edge of the camp and did not enter it. First rule of reading ground: you don't put your own print on the page until you've finished the sentence someone else started.

He worked the perimeter clockwise, slow, bent low, hands clasped behind his back like a man inspecting a garden. The leaf litter told him the story in fragments.

Here: boot prints, four distinct tread patterns, coming and going along a beaten path between the tents and the riverbank. Worn deep. A camp lived in for weeks, not days. The prints were government-issue mountain pattern, the kind issued by the Survey Directorate — he knew the sole because he'd worn it for nine years, until the day the same Directorate had struck his name from the registry with a rubber stamp and a sentence he'd never been allowed to contest.

Here, at the fire pit: ash raked flat and cold, stones stacked neatly beside it. Not kicked apart. *Stacked.* Men who expected to return, or men who wanted whoever came next to know they'd left in order.

Here, against the largest tent: a cache. Oilcloth over a crate, rope lashed in a proper trucker's hitch, the whole thing raised on stones above the damp. He circled it twice before he touched it, and even then he lifted only one corner of the oilcloth.

Rice in sealed tins. Salt pork. A medical kit, unopened, the wax seal intact. A signal mirror wrapped in cloth. Enough food for four people for ten days, sitting untouched in a valley where four people had vanished.

His mouth had gone dry somewhere in the last few minutes. He noticed it the way you notice a change in wind — belatedly, and all at once.

*They left deliberately,* he thought. *They packed for travel and they left deliberately.*

That was the second wrong thing. Because deliberate departure meant choice, and choice meant they'd known something, and if a government survey team had learned something worth abandoning a stocked camp for — walking out into monsoon country without waiting for extraction — then whatever they'd learned was either worth a fortune or worth a grave.

* * *

Inside the main tent, the instruments confirmed it.

The theodolite was packed in its case, straps buckled, tripod collapsed and bound. The barometers were cased. The field notebooks were gone — every one of them, he checked three times, going through the map case, the document wallet, the waterproof pouches hanging from the ridge line. Empty. A survey team that had died or fled in terror might leave its notebooks; paper weighs nothing and panicking men drop everything. But a team that took its notebooks took its *work*. They had intended those pages to survive even if they didn't.

One thing remained on the folding table, weighted down with a stone: a tin cup, upside down, and beneath it a scrap of pencil-written note that the damp had blurred to ghosts. He angled it toward the light seeping through the canvas. He could make out perhaps a third of the words.

*...markers are wrong... source... not what the Trust...*

Then water damage, then nothing.

Kestrel sat down slowly on the camp stool, which groaned under him, and stared at the fragment for a long time. Outside, the thunder finally committed to something and rolled down the valley like a barrel of stones.

Boundary markers. The Halvard Trust's claims ran on boundary markers — stone pillars and brass plates set into the earth by surveyors forty years ago, the legal skeleton of an empire that owned, by paper, nearly everything the eye could see from the estate's tower. His grandfather had drawn maps of this valley once. Before. Before the discrediting, the blacklisting, the long quiet ruin that Kestrel had inherited along with the family name and nothing else.

He put the thought away. It was too early, and thinking about the Trust while your judgment mattered was how you started seeing enemies in shadows.

Discipline. Read the ground.

* * *

He spent the afternoon on it, working outward from the camp in concentric sweeps, and by the time the light went bronze and thick among the trees, he had the shape of the story — except for the ending, which was missing, and the beginning, which was worse than missing.

The team had been here six weeks minimum. Firewood cut in increasing radius from the pit. A latrine trench sited properly downslope, filled and moved once. A washing spot at the creek with soap-scummed stones. They'd been comfortable. Competent. Four people — he'd counted the sleeping places, the cups, the ration tins — doing patient work.

On the final morning, they had cooked a full meal. He found the evidence at the fire pit: fish bones, charred rice crusted to a pot that had been washed and left to dry, the dregs of tea in the pot's lid. Nobody ate a last breakfast like that if they were running. You eat like that when you're leaving on purpose, fed and organized, with a plan.

Their outbound trail led south from the camp, toward the high ground — and here was the third wrong thing, the one that had kept him circling all afternoon with his heart beating too fast.

There were no tracks leading out.

Not faded tracks. Not washed-out tracks. *No* tracks. The soft ground south of the camp, ground that preserved the prints of their daily traffic perfectly, showed their comings and goings for weeks and then simply stopped, as cleanly as a sentence cut off mid-word. No exit trail. No drag marks. No sign of a struggle anywhere within two hundred meters.

Men did not walk out of soft ground without leaving prints. Men did not fly.

Which left the possibilities he didn't like, arranged in a row like knives: they'd been taken by water — but the river hadn't spiked in six weeks, Nadya's charts said so, and four bodies would have left signs along the banks that even monsoon growth couldn't fully erase. They'd been taken by men who erased tracks — possible, but erasing four sets of prints across open ground required time and manpower and a reason. Or they'd walked out on ground that didn't hold prints — the limestone, the bare rock shelves up toward the gorge rim, the karst country where a good tracker could follow a party for a kilometer and then lose them to stone.

Or — and this possibility sat in his chest like swallowed ice — they had never left on foot at all.

He was kneeling at the southern edge of the cleared ground, forehead nearly touching the leaf litter, letting his eyes go unfocused the way his grandfather had taught him — *don't look for the track, boy, look for the absence of track, the ground remembers being stepped around* — when he found it.

A print. Single, partial, pressed into the clay at the lip of the drainage channel where the ground stayed soft year-round. He measured it against his own hand without touching it.

Too large for any of the team. Too large for most men. And the tread —

Kestrel went very still.

Government-issue. But not the Survey Directorate pattern the team wore. This was the newer sole, the one rolled out three years ago to the ministries and the marshals' service — deeper lugs, a distinctive heel brake, the kind of boot issued to men whose job involved standing in the rain giving orders. He'd seen those boots on the dockside in Port Ammerly, on the liaison's escort, on the men who guarded the registry offices that had thrown him out of two countries.

Someone from the government had come to this camp *after* the team left. After the tents were sealed, after the larvae hatched in the pooled rainwater — because this print was pressed into clay that had been churned by later rain, and it sat *on top of* the silting, crisp-edged, recent. Days old. Maybe less.

And it pointed not toward the camp, and not toward the river.

It pointed south. Toward the high ground. Toward the karst.

Toward wherever four careful people had gone without leaving a single mark on the earth.

Kestrel knelt there in the failing light with the rain starting again, fine and cold through the canopy, and felt the unease in his gut finish its slow hardening into something with edges. He had taken this contract believing it was a rescue. Lost surveyors, worried ministry, standard grief-work with a paycheck attached. He had told himself the blacklist didn't matter, that finding people was finding people.

But lost men don't take their notebooks. Dead men don't rake their ashes. And government boots don't walk into an abandoned camp in a valley owned by the richest inheritance empire in the province unless someone very powerful wants to know exactly what those four people knew — and exactly where they'd gone.

He rose, wiped his hands on his trousers, and looked south, where the gorge roared and the limestone shoulders of the high country stood black against the bruised sky.

"Nadya," he said quietly, though she was fifty meters away and the rain was falling between them. "We need to talk about who else is looking for these people."

Behind him, in the sealed tent, the water in the vestibule rippled with larvae that would never become mosquitoes, and the fragment of note under the stone cup waited with its ghost words — *not what the Trust* — for someone to finish the sentence.
