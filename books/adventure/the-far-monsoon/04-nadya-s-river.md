## 4. Nadya's River

The Sareth did not forgive. That was the first thing they taught you on the pilot exams, back when Nadya Voss still had a license to lose: *the river does not forgive, it only forgets — and it forgets nothing you can afford to rely on.*

She stood at the wheel with one hand on the brass and one eye on the depth gauge, and she thought about forgetting. About how much of this river was already forgotten by everyone but her.

"Sandbar off the left bank," Kestrel said from the bow.

"I see it."

"You're heading straight for it."

"I'm heading straight for *past* it." She eased the throttle down a quarter and let the current take them, the old boat yawing lazy as a drunk leaving a funeral. The bar showed itself a moment later, a pale tongue of silt just under the surface, and the *Maren* slid over its shoulder with six inches to spare. "That bar wasn't there two years ago. Two years before that, there was no bar at all — there was a church steeple."

Kestrel turned around. He had that look he got when he was deciding whether she was lying to him, which was most of his looks. "A church."

"The village of Ostenmere. Drowned in the Trust's first impoundment, forty-one years ago. Steeple still stands. On a low year you can see the weathervane." She tapped the gauge. "Every chart of this river above the gorge is a lie. The Trust redraws them whenever the water rises. Officially, none of this exists. Unofficially, I've run cargo through it for eleven years, so I know where the roofs are."

"And the cargo?"

She looked at him. Rain beaded on the canvas behind him; the light came through greenish, river-colored, and made him look like something dredged up.

"You read my file," she said. "You tell me."

"I read what the registry would give me. Which is a page of redactions and a revocation date."

"Then we'll both keep our mysteries a while longer." She turned back to the wheel because turning back to the wheel was easier than the rest of it, and because the channel narrowed ahead into the drowned orchard country, where dead apple trees stood in three feet of water like a congregation waiting for a sermon that had been forty years coming.

* * *

The license had been real once. Laminated, stamped, photographed — Nadya Voss, Class Three River Pilot, certified for the Sareth system and its tributaries. She'd been twenty-four and insufferable about it. Her father had framed a copy and hung it in the chandlery where he sold rope and diesel and small kindnesses to people who couldn't always pay, and customers would ask *is that your girl?* and he'd say *that's my girl* in a voice like a struck bell.

Her father had also owed money. That was the part the file didn't say, or maybe it said it in the language of numbers that nobody reads: debts to men whose names were never written down, only remembered, the way you remember weather.

Daven Corr had come into the chandlery on a Tuesday in the wet season, wearing dry boots in a month when nobody's boots were dry, which told her everything before he opened his mouth. He bought a coil of line he didn't need and mentioned, casually, the way you mention rain, that her father's account had been passed along to him, and that he was a reasonable man, and that reasonable men preferred pilots who knew the upper Sareth.

"Nobody runs the upper Sareth," she said. "It's restricted. Trust water."

"Restricted," Corr agreed, smiling. "Not flooded. There's a difference, and the difference is channels. Old ones. Underneath." He set a folded chart on the counter, hand-drawn, beautiful, wrong in every way a document could be wrong and right in every way that mattered. "You know these waters better than any map. So do I. Let's not pretend otherwise to each other."

She should have said no. She knew that then, too — that's the obscenity of it, that complicity is almost never a surprise. You walk into it with your eyes open because your father's hands shake when he counts coins now, because the alternative has teeth, because you tell yourself it's one run, just one, and the cargo is crates and crates is crates and what's in them is not your business.

It was one run. Then it was four. Then it was a season.

The cargo changed. She noticed — of course she noticed; you don't spend your life on water without learning to read weight — but noticing is a currency you spend to buy sleep, and she spent it freely. Crates became drums. Drums became things wrapped in oilcloth that sat wrong in the hull and made the boat ride heavy on one side, and once, something that breathed. She didn't ask. Asking converts knowledge into guilt, and guilt is ballast you can't pump out.

The last run was in the spring of her twenty-sixth year, up a drowned channel she knew by heart, carrying six drums and a passenger. The channel was called the Serpent's Neck by the old boatmen, a loop of pre-flood river that the Trust's engineers had sealed at both ends and left full, a mile of black water lying over what used to be the town of Harrow's Bend. It saved half a day against going around, if you knew the entry markers, and only three people alive knew the entry markers, and Corr had made sure Nadya was one of them because a secret shared with a debtor is a debt renewed.

Halfway down the Neck, the patrol boat came around the point with its running lights off, and Nadya understood, with the terrible clarity of cold water, that she had been sold. Not even sold — spent. A pilot with a record was worth more to Corr than a pilot without one. The drums went over the side. The passenger went over the side after them, swimming, and she never learned whether he made the far shore or whether the Neck kept him, and the not-knowing lived in her chest from that day forward like a swallowed stone.

They took her license at a hearing that lasted eleven minutes. The magistrate asked if she had anything to say, and she looked at Corr, sitting comfortable in the gallery in his dry boots, and said, "No, sir," because her father was in the gallery too, and some cargoes you carry to the end.

* * *

"Nadya."

She came back to the present the way you surface from deep water — too fast, ears popping. Kestrel was crouched at the bow rail, looking back at her, rain streaming off his hood.

"You went somewhere."

"Nowhere you want to visit." She checked the gauge, the sky, the water. All of it lied less than her face probably did. "We're coming up on the Narrows. I'll want your eyes."

He came aft without argument, which she was learning was his way — he argued with words and surrendered with his body, doing the work before the discussion was finished. He braced beside the wheel and studied the water ahead, and she watched him read it the way she read it, and hated how much she liked having someone aboard who could.

The Narrows were where the old highway had gone under. You could still see it on clear days in a drought year: the guardrail marching down into the green, stone posts standing in a row like mourners, and past them the ghost of a bridge, its deck gone, its piers holding up nothing but memory and current. The Trust's charts showed open lake here. The lake was a lie laid over a road laid over farms laid over graves, and every foot of it was Halvard water, Halvard paper, Halvard law.

Kestrel pointed. "There. The eddy line off the second pier — it's pulling harder than it should."

"It should pull hard. There's a culvert under the old embankment. Trust built it to drain the impoundment when they were raising the level, then abandoned it. It's been eating boats ever since." She fed the wheel left, felt the *Maren* lean into the turn, heard the engine note change as the prop found cleaner water. "Stay wide of the boil line and we pass clean."

"You've run this a lot."

"I've run everything a lot. That's the problem with me."

He didn't laugh. He watched the boil line instead, tracking it with those tracker's eyes, and after a while he said, quietly, not looking at her: "Whoever drew the original survey for this stretch — the one underneath the Trust's version — knew the drowned channels. Marked the culvert. Marked the Serpent's Neck, if it goes that far north."

The wheel went light in her hands. Just for a second — a hitch in her grip, a stutter in the rudder, nothing the river would notice but he would.

"How do you know about the Serpent's Neck?"

"I don't. The name. From the survey notes I was given." He turned. Rain ran off his jaw. "You know it."

Everyone knew the Neck existed the way everyone knew the sea had a bottom. Nobody knew the entry markers. Nobody who would admit it.

"I ran cargo up here," she said. "Before. For men I shouldn't have. That's the whole confession, and it cost me my license, and I'm not giving you the tour."

"I wasn't asking for a tour."

"Everybody asks eventually. They ask casual, the way you just did, and then they ask again, and then they're asking me to take them places the Trust doesn't want visited, and then I'm the one in front of the magistrate again, except next time there won't be a gallery for anyone to sit in." She heard her own voice climb and hauled it down. "I know why you're really here, Ishikawa. It's not the government contract. Government contracts don't hire blacklisted guides. Somebody wants the Sareth opened up, and somebody wants a man with nothing to lose to do the opening, and I'm the boat that gets you there. Fine. But when it goes wrong — and it goes wrong, it always goes wrong — I'm the one who loses the second thing. There's only one left."

He took that without flinching, which was worse than arguing. "What's the one thing?"

"My father's boat. My name on the papers, even if the papers aren't worth much." She looked at the water rather than at him. "And the knowing. That I'm not that woman anymore. It's not much of an inheritance, but it's mine."

The *Maren* cleared the Narrows with the pier sliding by an oar's length off the starboard side, close enough that she could see the flood-line stains on the old stone, high-water marks stacked like tree rings, each one a decision made in an office she'd never seen by men whose boots never got wet. The culvert gurgled behind them, patient as a debt.

* * *

They moored that night in the lee of a drowned mill, its wheel long since stripped for scrap, its walls rising black out of the shallows. Kestrel cooked rice with too much salt and they ate in the cockpit under the tarp, listening to the rain work the canvas, and neither of them talked, and the silence was different from the first night's silence. The first night's silence had been two strangers guarding their borders. This one had furniture in it.

She slept badly, as she always did on the upper Sareth, and woke before dawn to the sound of Kestrel moving, quiet as a man checking a snare line. She joined him at the bow with the coffee pot and found him studying the route ahead by the gray light, a pencil hovering over the government chart without touching it.

"We cut west here," he said. "Through the flooded valley, following the old boundary line. The survey team's last camp is beyond the ridge of the submerged hills."

Nadya looked where the pencil hovered, and the stone she had carried since the Serpent's Neck rolled over in her chest.

Because she knew that line. Not from any chart. From Corr's folded map, the beautiful wrong one, the one that had shown her the drowned channels and the culverts and the sealed roads — and one more thing, marked in Corr's own hand with a little annotation she had never understood until this moment, until a disgraced guide's pencil hovered over the same bend of nowhere: *Halvard keeps his instruments here.*

The survey team hadn't wandered. They'd been aimed — at the one place in the valley where the Trust hid the originals, the plates, the proof, whatever it was that made a man like Edmund Halvard flood a valley to keep it dark. And whoever had hired Kestrel knew it, which meant the government contract was either a rescue or a retrieval, and she had been on enough runs to know which way retrievals ended.

"Change of plan," she heard herself say, and her voice came out flat and steady, the voice she'd used on the Neck with the patrol boat bearing down. "We go the long way. Around the ridge, down the eastern channel."

"That adds two days."

"Two days beats whatever's waiting on the short one."

Kestrel looked at her for a long moment, rain dripping off his hood, and she watched him decide — not whether to trust her, but how much of himself to spend on it. "You know something about that route."

"I know that route is how I lost everything," she said. "And I know that whoever sent you up this river knows exactly where they're sending you, and it isn't toward survivors."

He didn't answer right away. Downstream, far off across the drowned fields, a sound reached them across the water — faint, rhythmic, wrong. Not thunder. Thunder didn't keep time.

A gate, opening. Steel rolling on steel, somewhere upstream in the dark, and the water beneath the hull beginning, very gently, to move the wrong way.
