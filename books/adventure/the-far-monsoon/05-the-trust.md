## 5. The Trust

The rain began at four o'clock, as it always did in the first week of the monsoon, and Edmund Halvard watched it arrive from the long window of his study with the satisfaction of a man observing a schedule kept.

Below him the Sareth Valley unrolled in the grey light — terraced fields gone dark with water, the river swollen to a brown ribbon threading through green, and beyond it all the drowned country, the lowlands the Trust had purchased over three generations and that the flood each year made briefly, beautifully whole. From this height the water looked like order. It looked like intention. It looked like a thing drawn by a careful hand.

That was the trick of elevation, Edmund had learned early. Down in the valley, water was chaos; it ruined crops and carried off fences and drowned children who wandered too near the channels. But from the estate, from the house his grandfather had built on the shoulder of the mountain with its back to the weather, the flood was architecture. Every channel cut, every levee raised, every sluice gate hung on its iron hinges was a sentence in a document written across forty miles of country. The valley read it whether it wished to or not.

He turned from the window to the desk, where the flood-control schedules lay in their leather folder, and he read them again, though he knew them by heart.

The upper gates at Vell's Reach: scheduled opening, the ninth day of the monsoon, half-draw. The middle gates above the gorge: eleventh day, quarter-draw, rising to full on the seventeenth. The lower weirs: eighteenth day, managed release into the old spillway — *no*, not the old spillway. He struck the line with his pen and rewrote it. The lower weirs would release into the main channel only. The old spillway was to remain sealed. It had been sealed for eleven years, since his father's time, and there was no reason on earth to open it now, whatever the engineers said about sediment load and pressure differentials. Some doors stayed shut because what they opened onto was inconvenient.

He initialed the amendment, blotted it, and set the pages square.

The study smelled of beeswax and cold stone and, faintly, of the cedar oil the housekeepers used on the map cabinets along the far wall. Forty-two cabinets. Edmund had counted them as a boy, running his fingers along the brass fittings while his father's surveyors argued in the next room about contour lines and legal metes. He had not been allowed to open the cabinets then. He was allowed now, of course. He was allowed anything now. That was what inheritance meant, when you came down to it: permission without end, and no one left alive to tell you what the permissions were for.

His father had died in this room, in that chair, with a glass of brandy going warm beside the schedules. Edmund had found him at dawn. There had been a single sheet of paper in the dead man's hand, covered in figures, and Edmund had read it standing in the rain-grey light while the household slept, and then he had fed it to the fire page by page, because whatever else his father had been, he had not been a man who deserved to be remembered by arithmetic done too late.

The rain thickened against the glass. Somewhere below, in the estate yard, a groom shouted at a horse, and the sound rose thin and was swallowed.

* * *

Corren came at the fifth hour, which was correct; Corren was always correct. His steward entered without knocking because knocking was a formality between equals and they were not equals, and stood with his hands folded and the rain still dripping off the shoulders of his oilskin in two neat lines, like punctuation.

"The riders are back from the lower valley," Corren said. "And there is a matter."

"There usually is." Edmund did not look up from the schedules. "Give me the riders first."

"Two of the boundary cairns on the north ridge have been disturbed. Not moved. Disturbed — stones lifted and replaced, badly. And the marker post at Kiln Bend has fresh paint on it where someone scraped the Trust's number off." Corren's voice never changed register; it moved along a single flat line the way the river moved along its bed, carrying whatever it carried. "Also, the ferryman at Tarn's Landing sold passage upriver three days ago to a woman with a boat. He says she asked after the drowned channels."

"Asked how?"

"By name. She named them. The old names."

Edmund set down his pen. That was a small thing and a large thing at once, the way small things were. The drowned channels had official designations now, letters and numbers issued by the Water Authority, clean and bloodless. The old names — Marrow Cut, the Widow's Run, Saint Ilse's Channel — those belonged to the villages that had held title before the Trust bought their deeds at famine prices, before the gates went in, before the water came up over the doorstones one managed autumn and never fully went down again. Nobody under forty used the old names. Nobody who used the old names wished the Trust well.

"And the matter," Edmund said.

"A guide." For the first time Corren hesitated, a fractional pause, a hitch in the flat line. "A licensed guide entered the restricted zone yesterday morning, above the gorge. The watchpost logged him and let him through — his papers were in order, government contract work. Survey recovery. A team went silent up there ten days ago and the Ministry sent someone to find them."

"Name?"

"Ishikawa."

The rain filled the silence. Edmund sat quite still and looked at the middle distance, where the schedules lay, and felt something move in him that was not surprise. Surprise was for things you had not already feared.

"Kestrel Ishikawa," he said.

"You know him?"

"I know the name." Everyone in the valley country knew the name, if they were old enough, if they had been paying attention the way Edmund had been trained since childhood to pay attention. There had been an Ishikawa once who drew maps — the finest maps, people said, though Edmund's father had called them forgeries and seditions and had burned the ones he could get and ruined the man who made the rest. And there had been a grandson, a mountain guide of real gift, blacklisted out of two countries inside eighteen months for reasons that varied with the telling but always traced back, if you pulled the thread far enough, to men who did not care to be mapped. Edmund had signed none of the orders himself. He had merely not objected to them, which in the economy of power amounted to the same signature.

"A disgraced guide," Edmund said, "on a government contract, walking into my restricted zone, asking — I assume — nothing, saying nothing, being entirely ordinary."

"He filed a route plan. Standard. Up the east bank to the last survey camp, then assessment."

"Which means he'll find the camp empty."

"The camp is empty," Corren agreed. "It has been empty for some days. We've known where the survey team went since the second week. They're in the limestone caves past the Marrow Cut, all four of them, and our friend inside keeps them fed and keeps us informed. They can't come down. The paths are watched."

"Then the guide will find nothing at the camp and report nothing, and the Ministry will write a memorandum, and that will be that." Edmund said it mildly, testing the shape of it, the way he tested everything — held up to the light, turned, weighed. It did not sit right. Nothing about Ishikawa sat right. A man did not claw his way back from two blacklists to walk a routine recovery route in the rain. Desperate men took desperate contracts, yes. But desperation had a smell, and this had a different one. This smelled like the north ridge cairns disturbed and replaced badly. Like old paint scraped off a post. Like the old names spoken aloud at Tarn's Landing.

Things were surfacing. That was the trouble with water, in the end; everyone believed it concealed, and it did, for a season, for a year, for a generation — and then some drought or some flood or some fool with a shovel, and up came the foundations, the doorstones, the bones.

"Where is he now?" Edmund asked.

"Our watcher put him at the abandoned watchtower above the gorge last night. Sheltering from the rise."

"The rise." Edmund glanced at the window. "The river's ahead of forecast."

"Ten days ahead at the middle gauges. The hydrologists can't account for it. They're blaming the glacier melt."

The hydrologists could blame whatever they liked. Edmund had signed the order himself, nine days ago, quietly, through the Water Authority liaison who understood that certain adjustments were made at the Trust's discretion and that discretion was rewarded. The upper gates at Vell's Reach had opened early and half-drawn, feeding the middle river, swelling it past every natural mark — not enough to drown anyone, not yet, nothing so crude. Just enough to make the high country hostile. Just enough to strand a man above the gorge with the paths washed out behind him, so that whatever he found, he would find it slowly, and wetly, and with his exit closing.

It had seemed sufficient at the time. Now, sitting with the name Ishikawa in the room like a draft under a door, Edmund reconsidered.

* * *

He rose and walked the length of the map cabinets, and Corren waited, because Corren always waited; patience was the first duty of service and the second was silence. Edmund stopped at cabinet thirty-one, the one holding the valley surveys of the third commission — the commission his grandfather had claimed, in the founding documents, to have ordered and paid for, the commission whose plates established the boundaries the Trust defended in court to this day. He did not open it. He simply rested two fingers on the brass fitting, feeling the cold of it, and thought about plates.

There were plates in the high country. He was nearly sure of it. The survey team had gone up asking questions no recovery mission needed to ask — Corren's reports made that plain — tracing markers to their sources, walking the north ridge, disturbing cairns. Looking for origins. And origins, in this valley, were the one commodity more dangerous than dynamite.

If the original plates existed — the true ones, the first ones, the ones drawn before his grandfather's hand had redrafted the boundaries inward — then somewhere in a cave or a cache or a dead man's strongbox lay the difference between the Trust and theft. Edmund had spent his whole life being told the distinction was academic. His father had told him so with brandy on his breath: *Every great holding is built on something somebody regrets, Edmund. The regret dies. The holding doesn't.* He had believed it the way one believes the floor. You do not test it daily. You simply stand on it.

But lately — and he could not have said when it began, whether it was his father dying with figures on a page, or the rain coming earlier every year, or the simple accumulating weight of forty-two locked cabinets — the floor had begun to feel thin.

It didn't matter. That was the discipline. He had learned the discipline young, kneeling in this room while his father explained why the widow at Marrow Cut had to be bought out at a third of her price, and why the schoolteacher who complained had to be discredited, and why the surveyor Ishikawa had to be destroyed root and branch — *not punished, Edmund, destroyed; punishment creates martyrs, ruin creates caution* — and the discipline was this: you did the necessary thing cleanly, without heat, and you slept, and in the morning the schedules were still the schedules.

"What does the Ministry know of his progress?" Edmund asked.

"Nothing yet. His check-ins are weekly, by relay pigeon from the Tarn's Landing post. First bird isn't due for four days."

Four days. Edmund turned the interval over. Four days was enough for a good tracker to read an empty camp the way other men read letters — to see that a team had left deliberately, packed instruments, cached food. To see, perhaps, what direction deliberate departure pointed. And the caves past the Marrow Cut were not hard to find, if you knew the old names, if you had grown up on maps drawn by a hand that knew every fold of this country better than any Halvard ever had.

He could send riders. Six men, quiet, up the east bank. They would find the guide on some rain-slick traverse and there would be an accident — the valley was full of accidents; it was practically a factory of them — and the Ministry would receive a memorandum and a body, and the body would be regrettable, and the regret would die, and the holding wouldn't.

But riders left tracks, and tracks were testimony, and somewhere behind this particular tracker came a government contract that would eventually be missed. No. Crude instruments were for crude problems. Edmund's father had taught him that too, though he'd taught it mostly by failing at it, by leaving bruises everywhere, by building a reputation for menace that required constant menace to maintain. Edmund preferred the other way. The way of the water itself: patient, total, and deniable. Nothing in this valley was more inevitable than the monsoon. If a man drowned in the monsoon, who had killed him? The sky?

"Corren," he said. "The flood-control schedules. Bring me the current gauge readings from Vell's Reach and the middle gates."

Corren fetched the folio without question. Edmund read the figures twice. The middle river stood at seven point two meters and climbing, a meter above seasonal norm, and the forecast rains had not even properly begun. The valley people would be moving stock to high ground already, grumbling, resigned; the monsoon was unpredictable, everyone knew that. Everyone had always known that. It was the most useful truth in the district.

"If the guide continues upstream," Edmund said, "he will be above the gorge when the next draw comes. The paths down will be gone. He'll be trapped in the high country with the survey team — wherever they are — until the water drops." He paused, pen hovering over the schedule. "Unless someone opens the gates wide."

Corren said nothing. Corren understood that certain sentences were completed by their hearer or not at all.

"The monsoon is unpredictable," Edmund said. "The engineers have warned me for years that the upper catchment is volatile, that a sudden volume could force an emergency release regardless of schedule. If that happened — if the gates at Vell's Reach opened fully, tonight, ahead of any forecast — no reasonable person could call it anything but nature." He signed the order in the margin, small and neat, initials and date, the same initials that appeared on leases and school donations and his mother's grave marker. "Half the high trails will be under water by morning. Whatever the guide has found, he'll keep it. Whatever he is, he'll answer to the river, and the river answers to no one."

"And the survey team?" Corren asked. Flat. Unjudging. Asking only because the record required the question.

"Are in the high country during an unprecedented flood," Edmund said. "Tragic. The Ministry will want to know why they left their camp, and we will want to know it too, and neither of us will ever learn. Grief closes many files." He handed the amended schedule back. "See it done. Quietly. And Corren — the friend inside. Tell him to move the survivors' stores deeper into the caves and then come down. His usefulness ends when the water rises. I won't have a paid man's testimony floating to the surface later, tied to mine."

"Yes, sir."

Corren went. Edmund heard the outer door, the rain taking the sound of boots on gravel, and then nothing but the weather.

* * *

Alone, he returned to the window.

The valley had gone the color of pewter. Rain walked across the terraces in visible sheets, and the river below the estate grounds had climbed its banks and taken the alder meadow, and far off, at the head of the valley, the clouds sat on the peaks like a lid on a pot beginning, very softly, to rattle.

Somewhere up there, a man named Ishikawa was sheltering in a broken tower, reading the ground, reading the water, reading — with that famous inherited eye — the whole valley like a text written by hands the Halvards had spent sixty years trying to burn. Edmund found that he did not hate the man. Hatred was heat, and heat was for lesser men; his father had run hot and died cold. What Edmund felt was closer to recognition, almost to respect: here was another person who read this country truly. That was precisely what made him intolerable. The valley could hold only so much truth, and the Trust had claimed the entire capacity generations ago.

The first really heavy rain of the season arrived while he stood there, slamming the glass in gusts, and the lights of the estate came up one by one below him, small and golden and fragile-looking, lamps against a darkness that paid them no attention whatsoever.

Upstream, in the dark, the gates at Vell's Reach would begin to lift within the hour. Edmund imagined the sound — he had heard it once as a boy, taken to see the works, the groan of iron and the white roar beneath — and he imagined the brown water shouldering down the middle channel toward the gorge, patient as arithmetic, blind as justice was supposed to be.

"The monsoon is unpredictable," he said aloud to the empty room, trying the words on, and they fit so well he almost smiled.

In the morning the river would be higher than any gauge had promised. In the morning there would be a drowned trail, a sealed cave country, a silenced question. And if a certain guide's name surfaced someday anyway — names had a way of doing that, in this family's experience — well.

The water would have killed him, not the Trust.

The water answered to no one.

Except, of course, to whoever held the keys.
