## 6. Rising Water

The river told them first.

Kestrel heard it before he saw it — a change in the voice of the water, a lowening of pitch, the way a choir drops when the basses lean in. He was on the foredeck checking the mooring line when the sound reached him through the soles of his boots, and by the time he looked up at the banks, he already knew what he would find: the Sareth had climbed its walls in the night.

The mudline on the stone embankment below the old ferry landing stood a full hand higher than it had yesterday evening. Nadya's boat, tied to a dead fig tree with two wraps and a slip knot, sat lower in the current than she should have, the water licking at her gunwale like something tasting.

"Nadya," Kestrel called. "Your river's lying to you."

She came up from the cabin rubbing sleep out of one eye, coffee tin in her fist, and stopped dead when she saw the bank. For a long moment she said nothing at all, which from Nadya Voss was louder than shouting.

"That's wrong," she said finally. "That is *wrong*. The gauge at Tarn Ford said we had six days before the first pulse came down from the high snowfields. Six days. I've run this stretch eleven seasons. The Sareth doesn't lie about its schedule."

"Then somebody changed the schedule."

She turned and looked at him, and he watched the thought arrive in her face the way weather arrives over a ridge — first the shadow, then the wind, then the rain. She didn't argue. That frightened him more than anything else that morning.

* * *

They ran upstream against it for half a day because there was nowhere else to go — the gorge lay below them, a slot of white chaos that no sane pilot would enter at any level, let alone this one, and above them the valley narrowed toward the survey country where four men and women had walked out of existence three weeks ago. Going back meant admitting the contract was dead. Going forward meant climbing. So they pushed the boat up a side slough Nadya knew — "a smuggler's cut, don't ask how I know" — and winched her into the willows with the anchor line and a great deal of swearing, and then the river took the argument out of their hands entirely.

By noon the slough was gone. By mid-afternoon the willows were gone. They stood on a shelf of rock above what had been their campsite and watched brown water move past with the calm, unhurried confidence of something that intended to be here for a very long time. Whole trees went by, traveling upright, roots first, like drowned men walking home.

"The forecast pulse," Nadya said. She had her arms crossed tight, her knuckles white against her own sleeves. "It hasn't come yet. This isn't meltwater. Meltwater is cold and it's *green*, Kestrel, it comes down milky with glacier flour. Look at it."

He looked. The water was warm-brown, the color of steeped tea, the color of lowland rain and released reservoirs. It smelled of drowned fields and turned earth and, faintly, of the iron tang that came off the sluice works at Halvard's upper dams — he'd smelled it once before, years ago, as a boy, when his grandfather had taken him up to see the gates and been turned away by men in grey coats who spoke softly and carried documents instead of guns.

"It's coming from behind the gates," Kestrel said. "Somebody opened them early."

"Nobody opens the gates early. There are schedules. There are treaties about the schedules — the lowland rice districts have *treaties*—"

"And who holds the other end of those treaties?" He didn't wait for her to answer. "The Trust. Edmund Halvard's Trust. The monsoon is 'unpredictable,' Nadya. That's what they'll say. That's what they always say. And while everyone downstream is sandbagging their doorways, nobody will be asking why the survey team vanished, or why the boundary stones in the high valley keep getting moved, or why a government team that found something inconvenient happened to disappear two weeks before an early flood could wash all the evidence into the sea."

Nadya was quiet for a long time. A log the size of a man went past, turning slowly, barnacled along one flank where it had lain submerged long enough to grow a garden.

"You talk like a man who's read the ending," she said at last.

"I talk like a man whose family wrote the beginning." He hadn't meant to say it aloud. It came out anyway, flat and hard, and he turned away from her before she could look at him too closely. "Come on. We need height. If they're managing the water, they'll manage more of it tonight."

* * *

The watchtower had been built, according to the rotted plaque beside its footings, in some governor's brief enthusiasm for border security, and abandoned shortly after by every government that followed. It stood on a granite shoulder above the flood line, three stories of mortared stone with one wall fallen outward into a slope of tumbled blocks, and it was the only dry ground for miles that wasn't a goat track. Kestrel got them up to it in the last grey light, hauling their packs and the boat's canvas cover and Nadya's precious tin of lamp oil, and by full dark they had a fire going in the lee of the collapsed wall, small and shielded, and rain beginning to come down in the long diagonal sheets that meant the true monsoon was thinking about arriving.

Nadya cooked. Rice and dried fish and a scandalous amount of chili oil, eaten off tin plates with the rain roaring on the canvas they'd strung between two standing walls. Neither of them talked much. Fear does that; it fills the mouth like water fills a bilge, silently, from below.

It was Kestrel who noticed the wall.

He'd gone to check the fire draft, crouching where the fallen blocks met the surviving stonework, and his lantern caught something that shouldn't have been there: mortar that didn't match. In a wall of rough, grey, hasty masonry, one course of stones had been set with pale lime mortar, careful, deliberate, the work of someone with time and a reason. Someone had rebuilt a section of this wall from the inside.

"Nadya. Bring the light."

She came, held the lantern high, and frowned. "Repair work?"

"Or hiding work." He worked his fingers into the joint between two stones. The mortar crumbled — it had never fully cured, or the damp of decades had done the curing for him — and the block shifted with a grinding whisper. Behind it: darkness, and a smell of paper and mildew and old iron.

They pulled three stones loose in twenty minutes, working fast, glancing at the rain. Inside the cavity, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, was a bundle. Kestrel drew it out into the lantern light with both hands, the way you lift a wounded bird.

A tin box, army pattern, latch rusted but holding. Inside, wrapped in a shirt that had once been government survey grey: a brass instrument case, empty. A water flask. And a book — a proper survey logbook, bound in oiled canvas, its pages swollen and rippled where water had gotten in past the wrapping and then, mercifully, mostly dried again.

"They were here," Nadya breathed. "The survey team. They *came through here*."

Kestrel turned pages with a care that made his fingertips ache. Ink had run in places, blooming into grey flowers, but much of it survived — the neat, disciplined hand of a trained field officer, columns of figures, sketch maps, elevation notes. Dates. Three weeks ago and further back. The last entries were the most damaged, the ink smeared as though the book had been closed on wet pages in a hurry, or in fear.

And on the inside back cover, written vertically in a different hand — smaller, faster, pressed hard enough to score the paper — a single line that the water had spared:

*If we don't make it down, the proof is where the old channel sleeps. H. knows.*

Kestrel read it twice. Then a third time, his lips moving without sound.

"Halvard," Nadya said quietly. She'd read it too. Of course she had. "H. knows."

"No." Kestrel shook his head slowly. His heart was doing something strange, beating too hard and too slow at once. "*Halvard* doesn't know. That's the point. Whoever wrote this — that's a warning *to* the others, not a message to us. 'H. knows' — H. is the one they're hiding it *from*. The proof is hidden somewhere Halvard doesn't know to look. Somewhere connected to—" he tapped the page, "—the old channel."

Outside, the rain thickened. Down in the dark, the river climbed another inch, another inch, patient as arithmetic.

Nadya sat back on her heels. "So the team left the tower. Deliberately. They cached what they couldn't carry and they went down toward—" she gestured vaguely southward, toward the drowned country, the flooded channels the maps marked in flat blue with no names inside them, "—there. Into the one place on this river even I won't run in the dark."

"And then they vanished," Kestrel said. "Between here and the lowlands. With the whole valley flooding around them." He looked at the swollen pages, at the careful dead handwriting, at the line scored into the cover by someone writing fast in the dark. Dread had been walking beside him since the mudline that morning; now it stopped walking and stood still, and he felt it take his measure.

But underneath the dread, something else was waking. Something older. It felt disgracefully like hope.

"We rest till first light," he said. "Then we find the old channel."

Nadya looked at him — at the book in his hands, at whatever was on his face — and for once she didn't argue, didn't needle, didn't name a price. She just banked the fire, and said, "You'd better pray my boat floats better than your plans do."

Above them the rain came down like a verdict. Below them the river rose, managed and merciless, carrying the Trust's secrets toward the sea one drowned inch at a time — and in Kestrel's hands, half-ruined and half-alive, the survey team's logbook waited to be read.
