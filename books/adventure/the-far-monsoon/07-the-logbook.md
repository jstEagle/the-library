## 7. The Logbook

The rain did not stop. It changed its mind every few hours — hammering the watchtower's broken roof like a fist on a door, then easing to a hiss that filled the dark with the sound of a thousand small rivers running down the hillside — but it never stopped. Kestrel had learned to sleep inside weather the way other men slept inside houses, and he slept anyway, curled against Nadya's pack with the oilskin over his head, listening to the tower groan around them like an old ship settling into its own bones.

He woke because the lamp was burning.

That was wrong. He had blown it out before sleeping; lamp oil was worth more than blood this far upriver, and Nadya rationed it the way misers rationed gold. But there it was, a soft yellow bloom of light in the corner where she sat cross-legged with her back against the fallen lintel, turning pages.

Not just any pages. The logbook.

She had found it again while he slept. He'd wedged it into the wall cavity himself, wrapped in the survey team's own waxed groundsheet, and he'd told himself he would not open it until morning — some tracker's superstition about reading dead men's mail by daylight, when you could see their faces in your imagination properly lit. Nadya had no such scruples. She sat with the book open across her knees, her hair still damp from the river crossing, her lips moving faintly as she read, and the lamplight made her look younger and more frightened than anything she'd said since they'd met.

"You're wasting oil," Kestrel said.

"I'm wasting your oil," she said, without looking up. "You bought the last can at Ferrow's Landing. Which makes it yours." She turned a page, careful as a surgeon. "Come here, Ishikawa. You need to see this."

* * *

The tower had been a Trust structure once — he could tell from the ironwork, the same scroll-and-spear motif that stamped the sluice gates and the boundary markers and, if the stories were true, half the magistrates in two provinces. It had been abandoned long enough for the hill to begin reclaiming it: ferns pushing up through the flagstones, a strangler fig splitting the eastern wall, bats in the rafters that muttered and shifted whenever the wind gusted. Someone had built a fire here recently — days old, maybe a week, the ash still holding the ghost of its shape — and Kestrel had spent part of the evening reading that fire the way he read everything else. Three people. One of them favoring the left side. They'd burned wet wood and green fern, which meant they hadn't dared show smoke or light, which meant they were hiding from someone who could see.

The logbook fit that story. It was a standard government field journal, canvas-bound, the kind issued by the survey office with a serial number stamped in the spine. Water had gotten to it despite the groundsheet — the first third was a brick of fused paper, every page welded to its neighbor into something like slate. But whoever had kept it had known what they were doing near the end. The last forty pages were written in pencil, pressed hard, the letters deep enough to survive the water that had soaked everything else. Pencil doesn't drown. Ink does. Some surveyor with more foresight than luck had switched to graphite somewhere in the middle of this expedition, and that single decision was the only reason any of it had survived.

Kestrel knelt beside Nadya and took the book when she offered it. The pages were cold and swollen, soft as skin after a long bath. He turned them with the edge of his thumbnail, one at a time, drying each with the corner of his shirt before he let himself read.

The handwriting changed three times. He noticed it the way he noticed everything now — automatically, the way his eyes went to broken twigs and scuffed stone without being asked. A small, precise hand that numbered everything and drew margin diagrams. A looser hand that slanted hard to the right and underlined nothing. And a third, cramped and hurried, that appeared only in the final entries, as though the last writer had taken the book out of necessity rather than habit.

"Read it aloud," Nadya said. "I want to hear how it sounds."

"It's not a story."

"Everything's a story. Read."

So he read.

* * *

The early entries were routine — the kind of bureaucratic poetry that filled a hundred thousand government journals in a hundred thousand offices. Elevations. Barometric readings. Notes on vegetation density. The precise hand recorded water levels at fixed stations along the Sareth's middle reach, day after day, and Kestrel understood immediately why the numbers mattered: they were baseline figures, pre-monsoon, the measurements against which any future flood could be judged. The team had been mapping the valley's hydrology. Not its borders. Its water.

"They weren't a boundary commission," Nadya said slowly. "This is drainage work. Flood control."

"The Trust controls the flood gates." Kestrel turned another page. "If you wanted to know whether someone was managing the river — moving water where it shouldn't go, drowning land that should stay dry — this is how you'd find out. You'd measure everything before the season started, so no one could tell you afterward that the flood was natural."

Nadya was quiet for a moment. Outside, the rain thickened, and somewhere down the slope a section of hillside let go with a long wet sigh, earth sliding toward the river.

"Keep reading," she said.

The middle entries were where the routine began to crack. The loose-slanting hand took over — a second voice, more impatient, more alive — and the notes stopped being purely about water. There were sketches. Boundary markers, drawn in cross-section: stone posts sunk into the earth, each stamped with the Halvard spear-and-scroll, each set on a concrete footing. And beside the sketches, dates. The dates the markers claimed to have been placed, chiseled into the stone itself in the Trust's official record. And next to those, in a different column, the dates the stones actually showed — the ones you could only read if you knew where to look, on the buried face of the footing, where the casting mold had left its maker's stamp.

Kestrel read the column twice. Then a third time, tracing the numbers with his finger as though touch might make them behave.

"That's impossible," Nadya said. She had leaned in close enough that he could feel her breath on the page. "Those footings. Say it again."

"The mold stamps are dated thirty-one years before the commission date." His voice came out flat, which was the only way he could keep it steady. "Every marker they checked. The stones say they were poured decades before the Trust claims to have built them."

"Concrete molds have serials?"

"Government castings do. Foundry batch, year, inspector's mark. It's anti-corruption standard — so nobody can swap footings later and claim the originals failed." He heard himself explaining it and realized, distantly, that he was explaining it to himself too, making the shape of the thing solid by saying it aloud. "The Trust filed its land claims on a survey it says it commissioned forty years ago. These markers are supposed to be forty years old. The stamps underneath say seventy-one."

The lamp flame leaned in a draft and straightened. In the silence, the rain sounded very far away, and very close.

"So the markers were moved," Nadya said. "Or recast. Or—" She stopped. He watched her get there, watched it arrive in her face the way bad news always arrived, all at once, after a long time traveling. "Or the whole claim is forged. The whole thing. Every acre."

"Every acre," Kestrel said. "From the source to the gorge."

* * *

They worked through the ruined middle section together, and it was slow, and it was the best work Kestrel had done in six years.

The fused pages could be separated with steam. Nadya rigged it from the lamp itself — a tin cup balanced over the glass chimney, a strip of the groundsheet cut and folded into a shallow trough, the damaged block held above the rising vapor until the paper softened enough to tease apart with a knife tip. It was delicate, maddening work. Pages came free torn, or came free blank where the ink had bled into its neighbor, or came free with two entries printed mirror-wise on top of each other, legible only in fragments. But fragments were his trade. He had spent his life reading country that didn't want to be read — sign pressed into mud by rain, tracks hours old, direction divined from the angle of a snapped branch. Paper was just another kind of ground. You learned what the marks weighed, and which way they pointed.

What emerged, entry by steamed-open entry, was the shape of the survey team's last weeks.

They had followed the markers upstream. That much was clear from the elevations and the sketch maps — a line of Halvard stones marching up the valley's western flank, each one dutifully recorded, each one carrying its impossible secret beneath the soil. The precise hand had grown more excited as they went, the margins filling with calculations, with comparisons against the official Trust survey plates reproduced in the public registry. The lines didn't match. Not slightly — not the way honest surveys drift, by chains and links accumulated over rough country. They matched the way a lie matches the truth: everywhere except where it mattered. The Trust's boundaries ran fat through the good bottomland and thin through the high scrub, generous to the estate and stingy to everyone else, and the discrepancy wasn't error. Error scatters. Design doesn't.

Then, eleven days ago by the journal's reckoning, the handwriting changed for the last time.

*We found the source,* the cramped hand wrote. *Not the marker source. The plate source.*

Kestrel read the entry twice before he trusted himself to speak.

"There's a cache," he said. "Up past the old weir, in the limestone. The original survey plates — the glass negatives, the field originals, the whole run. Dated and signed." He looked up. "Dated and signed by the surveyor who made them. Seventy-one years ago."

"Seventy-one years." Nadya sat back on her heels. "Before the Trust existed."

"Before the Trust *claims* to exist. Before the founding survey it says it commissioned." He turned the page carefully. The next entry was longer, and the pencil had been pressed so hard it had nearly cut through the paper, as though the writer had needed the words to hold by force alone. "Listen to this."

He read it aloud, and the cramped letters became a voice in the dripping dark:

*"The plates are genuine. I've checked them against everything — paper stock, plate chemistry, the registrar's counterstamps. Three independent series, all consistent. The boundaries in these plates follow the old village lines. Every settlement in the lower valley held title under these surveys. The Trust's founding documents describe a survey commissioned forty years ago that never happened. What happened instead is that someone destroyed the originals, redrew the map, and drowned the villages that could argue with it. The flooded channel. God. The flooded channel isn't flood damage. It's evidence management."*

Below that, in fresher pencil, a shorter line:

*"Mara says we should take the plates out ourselves. Renshaw says we should leave them and report. We compromised. We photographed everything and hid the plates again and split the film between four packs. If anything happens to us, the film will surface somewhere."*

And below that, the last entry. Undated. The pencil shaking now, the letters barely legible:

*"A man came to camp tonight. Well dressed, dry shoes, out here in the wet season — which tells you more than his name did. He said he represented interests that valued our discretion. He used the word 'consulting.' He used the word 'pension.' He laid out figures on my own table. Halvard's man offered us money.*

*We said no."*

* * *

For a long time neither of them moved.

The lamp burned with its small stubborn flame. Water ran somewhere in the walls of the tower, finding its way down through seventy years of cracked mortar, patient as everything else in this valley. Kestrel became aware, distantly, that his hands were trembling, and he closed them into fists and waited for them to stop, and they didn't quite.

"We said no," Nadya repeated softly. "Four people, alone, a week's travel from anywhere, and a man walks into their camp offering more money than they'd earn in twenty years of government wages." She shook her head slowly. "And they said no."

"People do that," Kestrel said. "More often than you'd think. It just doesn't make good stories, because nobody writes songs about the ones who refuse and then vanish anyway."

"You think they're dead."

He thought about the fire pit outside. Three people, one limping. He thought about the camp they'd found — tents staked, food cached, instruments packed for travel, a departure planned and orderly and utterly trackless. He thought about government-issue boot prints that belonged to no member of the team, arriving after they'd gone.

"No," he said. "I think they're alive. I think they ran, and they're hiding, and they're waiting for the monsoon to peak because nobody travels in peak monsoon — not even the men hunting them." He tapped the journal's last page, gently, the way you'd touch a wound. "And I think that's why the gates opened early."

Nadya went very still.

"The forecast put the freshet nine days out," Kestrel said. "The river came up four days ago. Mara — the engineer, the one whose handwriting this is — she'd know what that means better than either of us. Somebody is managing the water. Somebody looked at a rising river and a missing survey team and decided the solution to both problems was the same solution." He heard his own voice drop, go quiet, the way it did when he was certain of something terrible. "You don't hunt four people through flooded karst country. You drown the karst. Everything in the caves comes up, or it doesn't come up at all. And afterward there's no camp, no trail, no bodies, no questions. Just a bad season. Just the monsoon being unpredictable."

The word landed between them like a dropped tool. Unpredictable. He'd heard it already, hadn't he — or something shaped like it — in the mouths of Trust men, in the comfortable language of people who controlled the water and called it weather.

Nadya reached out and turned the lamp down to a bead of flame. Her face went half to shadow.

"My brother fished the drowned channel," she said. "Before it was drowned. Our family held a mooring license there — a real one, stamped, paid every year. When the water came up, the license office told us the land had reverted to the Trust under a claim older than ours." She laughed, short and bitter, with no humor in it anywhere. "Older than theirs, too, it turns out. Older than everybody's. There's a village under that channel, Ishikawa. My mother was born in it. I used to think the flood was an act of God."

"Now?"

"Now I think God keeps better records."

Kestrel looked down at the logbook in his lap. Four names were written inside the front cover, in the neat block capitals required by the survey office: the team roster, signed at muster. E. VANCE, party leader. M. OKAFOR. T. RENSHAW. D. SILVA. Four signatures, four small acts of faith in ink that had, unlike everything else in this book, mostly survived the water.

He had taken this contract to find missing people. That had been the shape of it in his mind for days — a rescue, a recovery, a job with a clean moral geometry: locate the lost, bring them home, collect the fee, disappear. Even after the empty camp, even after the wrong boots, he had kept that shape. It was a comfortable shape. It asked nothing of him except competence.

But competence wasn't what the book in his hands was asking for.

The stakes had shifted under him the way a riverbank shifts — silently, completely, so that the ground you stood on was no longer the ground you'd stood on. This wasn't a search anymore. It was a delivery. Four people had carried proof of the largest fraud in the valley's history into the wilderness, and the wilderness was being arranged to swallow them, and the only people who knew what they carried were sitting in a ruined tower with a dying lamp and a leaking boat tied below in the flooded willows.

If they died, the plates stayed hidden. If the plates stayed hidden, the flood would come and go, and the valley would be scoured clean of markers and memory together, and the Trust's map would stand as the only map, forever. The monsoon would do what the money couldn't.

Unless somebody got to the team first. Unless somebody got them — and the film, and the coordinates of the cache — out ahead of the water.

"I need your boat," Kestrel said.

Nadya looked at him for a long moment. Rain drummed the broken roof. Down in the willows, something shifted against the hull of the *Vela* — the current tugging at her lines, impatient, already pulling toward the gorge.

"You need more than my boat," Nadya said. "You need the drowned channel. The plates are past the old weir — that's limestone country, and the only navigable approach runs through channel the Trust flooded and charts as open water. No pilot runs it. No chart shows what's under it." She held out her hand for the logbook, and he gave it to her, and she opened it to the sketch maps in the back with the lamp turned up again, studying the penciled contours of a riverbed that officially did not exist. "But I ran cargo up there twice, before my license died. I know where the old millrace lies. I know which bend has the church steeple just under the surface." Her jaw tightened. "I know it because I helped drown it. So yes, Ishikawa. You'll have your boat."

She was folding the relevant pages flat against her knee, committing them to memory, when she stopped. Her head came up the way an animal's comes up — no thought in it, only attention.

"What?" Kestrel whispered.

She killed the lamp.

In the sudden blackness the tower reassembled itself around them in shades of grey, and he heard it too: voices, far downslope, carried up on the wind off the water. More than one. Unhurried. Men talking quietly among themselves, the way men talk when they believe no one is listening — and beneath the voices, rhythmic and unmistakable, the knock of a boathook against a hull.

Against a hull.

The *Vela* was tied in the willows below, and someone was boarding her.
