## 8. Found Family, Found Trouble

The storm did not announce itself. That was the thing about the Sareth in late monsoon — it gathered in the high country like a debt being tallied, and then it came down all at once, and you paid.

Nadya Voss felt it before she saw it: a pressure behind her ears, a flatness in the air, the river going the color of wet slate. She had been reading water since she was nine years old, standing at her father's elbow on the tiller of a boat no bigger than this one, and the water was telling her now, in the old language of eddies and slack current, *get off me or I'll take you.*

"Kestrel," she said.

He was at the bow, doing what he always did — watching the banks, reading the ground even when the ground was underwater, tracking a team of ghosts who had left nothing behind but tents staked against weather that never came. He turned at her voice. He had learned, in the week they'd been on the river, that she didn't raise it twice.

"Storm's coming down the western feeder," she said. "Big one. We don't make the gorge anchorage before it hits, and I'm not running the narrows blind in a downpour with a boat that—" She stopped. She had been about to say *that takes on water faster than I admit,* which was a thing she had not admitted, and which he almost certainly knew anyway, because the bilge pump ran more than it slept and he wasn't a fool.

"With a boat that has opinions," she finished.

Kestrel looked at the sky, then at the banks, then at her. "Options?"

"Two." She pointed left, upstream of the bend, where the forest came down dark and dripping to the waterline. "We tie off to trees and ride it out in the open, and we probably lose the canopy and maybe the boat." She pointed right, where the current bent around a gravel point and a scatter of rooftops showed gray through the rain-haze, smoke rising thin from a chimney or two. "Or we put in at Sarnholt."

"Sarnholt."

"You know it?"

"I know the name from the charts. Fishing hamlet. No registry, no Trust presence marked."

"No Trust presence *marked*," Nadya said. "No. But I know it another way." And that was true, and the knowing of it sat in her chest like a stone she'd swallowed years ago and never passed, and she looked at the storm coming down the valley and decided, the way you decide things on water — fast, and then you live inside the decision — "Put in at Sarnholt. I've moored there before."

Kestrel's eyes stayed on her a moment longer than the sentence deserved. He had a way of looking at her like that — not suspicious, exactly. Inventorying. As if he were reading her the way she read the river, noting the snags, the slack water, the places where the surface lied about the depth.

"All right," he said. "Take us in."

* * *

The storm caught them in the last hundred meters, because storms always do. Rain came down like the sky had been holding its breath for a season and let it all out at once, and the wind flattened the river into a chaos of white and drove the boat sideways toward the gravel point, and Nadya worked the tiller with both hands and her whole back and every trick her father had ever taught her, and Kestrel was over the side to his waist in the freezing shallows, hauling the bow around by main strength, and between the two of them and the river's grudging mercy they came in hard, scraped, and alive, and the *Magpie* — Nadya's boat, her father's boat before her, leaking the way old boats leak, honestly, like a confession — ground to rest on the shingle below the hamlet.

Sarnholt was eleven houses, a drying rack for nets, a shrine to the river with coins green in its cracks, and a long covered shed where the boats wintered. It was also, Nadya saw as she stood dripping on the shingle with her heart still slamming, exactly as she had left it four years ago. That was the cruelty of small places. The river changed every hour, but Sarnholt didn't change at all, and it remembered everything.

The first face she saw under the shed's awning was old Maret, the net-mender, who had taught her to splice line when she was nineteen and running her first honest cargo, and Maret looked up from her work, and her hands went still, and she said, in the flat tone of a woman setting a hook:

"Well. Nadya Voss. The river brings back what it wants, I suppose."

"Maret."

"Four years." Maret went back to her net. The shuttle clicked. "Four years, and you come up the Sareth in monsoon, with a man I don't know, in a boat that's taking water even as it sits on gravel. I can hear it from here. That pump of yours has been talking since you rounded the point."

Nadya felt her face do something she didn't authorize. "It's an old boat."

"It's an honest boat. That's more than I can say for its pilot, the last time she passed through." Maret looked up again, and her eyes were not unkind, which was somehow worse. "You'll be wanting the shed. Get her out of the weather. And you'll be wanting food, and dry clothes, and you'll get them, because Sarnholt doesn't let anyone go hungry in a storm, not even liars. But you'll eat at my table, Nadya Voss, and you'll tell me what you're really doing on this river, because the last cargo you ran through my hamlet nearly burned us all."

Kestrel, beside her, said nothing. But she felt him listening the way a keel listens to the bottom.

* * *

They got the boat under the shed. The hamlet turned out for the storm the way small places do — not because anyone was ordered to, but because a boat on the shingle in a squall is everyone's problem and therefore everyone's work. Two fishermen whose names Nadya half-remembered, Dorin and his brother, helped her haul the *Magpie* up on rollers. Kestrel worked without being asked, without talking, finding the heavy end of every job and taking it, and Nadya watched the villagers notice that, and she saw something shift in how they looked at him. Strangers were suspect in Sarnholt. Men who hauled without complaint got a longer look.

It was old Sefa — the boatwright, ninety if she was a day, hands like driftwood — who knelt at the hull, ran her thumb along the seam below the waterline, and made a sound like a hen finding a snake.

"This," Sefa said. "This is your worst one. How long has she been weeping here?"

"Since before I had her," Nadya said.

"Since before you had her." Sefa spat, ceremonially, into the rain. "And you've been running her up the Sareth in flood season. Nadya Voss. You are either brave or you are trying to drown yourself, and I have known you since you were small enough to fit in a net basket, and you have never once been brave. You've been stubborn, and lucky, and too proud to fix things, but not brave."

Nadya laughed despite herself, and the laugh cracked something loose in her chest. "Fix it, then, if you're going to insult me the whole time."

"I'll fix it. The girl will help." Sefa nodded at Tam, Maret's granddaughter, a wiry fourteen-year-old who had been circling the boat with the frank hunger of a kid who wanted to be on the water and was never allowed. "And you'll tell me where you got the crack in the garboard, because that's impact damage, that's a rock or a piling, and you hit it hard enough to open a seam four years of caulk should have kept shut."

"Rocks," Nadya said. "There were rocks."

Sefa looked at her for a long moment. "There usually are," she said, "when a person is running at night with no lights."

* * *

They ate at Maret's table, because Maret said they would, and in Sarnholt Maret's word had the weight of law. The house was low and warm and smelled of woodsmoke and fish stew and the particular green smell of the valley that gets into everything in monsoon — wet fern, wet earth, wet stone. The stew was carp and river greens and potatoes and a handful of dried chili that made Kestrel's eyes water on the second spoonful, which Nadya enjoyed more than she could reasonably say. The man was a mountain guide, famous once, blacklisted in two countries, and he could read a slope like scripture, but he could not eat chili.

"Your grandfather's people were river folk, weren't they," Maret said, watching him suffer with something close to approval. "Ishikawa. I remember the name from the old days. Surveyors."

Kestrel set down his spoon. "You knew my grandfather?"

"I knew *of* him. Everyone on this river knew of him. He walked the whole valley when the Trust said it couldn't be walked. My father drank with him twice." Maret ladled more stew into Kestrel's bowl without asking, the way she'd been doing all evening, an act of feeding that was also, Nadya understood, an act of judgment: *you will sit here and be fed until you are one of us or you break.* "That was before. Before the Trust decided the valley belonged to it, and the maps stopped agreeing with the water."

Nadya saw Kestrel go still in the way he went still — completely, like an animal that has heard something under the wind. "The maps stopped agreeing with the water," he repeated.

"Boundaries moved," Maret said, shrugging, and the shrug was forty years old and tired. "Paper moved. The water stayed where it was. And then the Trust flooded the low villages, and the water was told where to go after all." She waved a hand, dismissing a history too large for a kitchen. "Eat. It's storm food."

* * *

Afterward, the storm still hammering the roof like a fist on a door, the hamlet gathered in Maret's long room because there was nowhere else to be and no reason to be anywhere else. Someone brought out a deck of cards soft as cloth from years of handling. Dorin produced a bottle of plum spirit that tasted like it had been distilled from lightning and regret. Tam, the granddaughter, produced a fiddle that was missing a string and played anyway, and the music was thin and wild and exactly right for the weather.

And for a few hours — Nadya kept track of them, the way you keep track of anything precious, counting — it was warm.

She sat at the table with cards in her hand and Kestrel across from her, and Dorin and his brother, and old Sefa who cheated magnificently and openly and dared anyone to object. Kestrel played like he did everything: carefully, watching everyone's hands, losing small on purpose for the first three hands until Sefa told him to stop insulting the table and play to win. Then he won, quietly, twice, and apologized with his eyes, and Dorin laughed and said he'd known a guide like that once, up at the high lakes, and the whole table went briefly quiet in the way tables go quiet around a blacklist, and then Tam played something faster on the broken fiddle and the quiet passed.

Nadya found herself laughing. Actually laughing, at Sefa's cheating, at Kestrel's face when Dorin's brother told a story about a mule and a wedding that Nadya didn't fully follow and didn't need to. She felt the warmth of the stove on her back and the spirit in her blood and the storm on the roof, and she thought: *this is the thing I lost.* Not the license. Not the name. This — a room full of people who feed you without asking what you're worth, who mend your boat while insulting you, who make room at the table because there is room at the table.

She had run cargo for men she shouldn't have, after they took her license. She had told herself it was just money, just runs, just the river. But what she had actually lost, the night the inspectors boarded her boat at Kessarin and read her name off a list, was this: the room, the table, the people who mend what you break. She had spent four years telling herself she didn't miss it. Sitting in Maret's long room with cards in her hand and a storm outside, Nadya Voss admitted, privately, in the one court where her testimony still counted, that she had missed it every single day.

Kestrel caught her looking at him across the table. He didn't smile — the man rationed smiles like water in a drought — but something in his face eased, and he raised his cup to her, an inch, a formality, and she raised hers back.

*You too,* she thought. *You lost this too. I can see it. That's why you watch everyone's hands.*

* * *

It was late — the fire down to coals, Tam asleep under a blanket on the bench, the storm down from a fist to a steady drumming — when Maret sat down across from Nadya with two cups of tea and the expression of a woman about to say a thing she had decided to say.

"Your man," Maret said. "The guide. You're running him up the Sareth in flood season, past the old markers, toward the high valley. And you came up the river like a woman being followed."

Nadya's hands went still around her cup. "We're not—"

"Don't." Maret said it gently. "I'm not your inspector, girl. I'm not the registry. I'm an old woman who mends nets and has watched this river longer than the Trust has owned it on paper, and I'm telling you what I know because Sarnholt fed you tonight and that creates an obligation in both directions." She pushed one of the cups toward Nadya. "Riders came through nine days ago. Six of them. Trust colors, but plain tack, plain weapons — the way they dress when they don't want to be remembered. Asking after strangers on the river. Asking at every landing from Kessarin up. A man and a woman, they said. A pilot with a record and a guide with a blacklisted name."

The fire popped. Somewhere in the walls, the house ticked and settled.

"They had a description of you," Maret said. "Of *you*, Nadya. Not of a pilot. Of Nadya Voss, and the boat, and the leak in her hull that they knew about." She let that sit. "Somebody upstream is reporting your movements, and they know your boat well enough to know its sins."

Nadya looked at the tea she wasn't drinking. Her mind was already working the river — the landings above, the channels, the places a boat could be watched and the places it couldn't. "The survey team," she said quietly. "The government team that went missing. Kestrel's contract. The Trust doesn't want them found."

"Or doesn't want what they found *found*," Maret said. "Same thing, worse." She drank her own tea. "There's more. The riders said they'd come back through. They said the monsoon was running early this year and the Trust had *maintenance* scheduled on the upper gates." Maret's voice did not change at all, which was how Nadya knew how much it cost her. "Maintenance. On the upper gates. In the heaviest monsoon in a decade."

Nadya felt it then — the cold under the warmth, the snag under the smooth water. The stew in her stomach turned to river stone.

"You should go at first light," Maret said. "Before the storm fully clears. Take the eastern channel past the shrine — it's shallow but the riders won't expect it, and your draft is light enough if you're careful. And Nadya." The old woman's hand covered hers on the table, dry and strong and rough with fifty years of line. "Whatever you're carrying up this river — whatever you did four years ago that you think put you past forgiveness — this hamlet doesn't hold it against you. But don't you dare hold it against yourself so hard you steer wrong now. I've seen people do that. The guilt takes the tiller. And then the river takes everything."

Nadya couldn't speak for a moment. She nodded.

Maret squeezed her hand once and rose, and began banking the fire, and that was that, the way things were *that* in Sarnholt — said once, meant forever, no ceremony.

* * *

Nadya went out into the shed where the boat lay on its rollers, patched and caulked, its worst seam closed for the first time in years, smelling of pitch and lamp oil. Sefa's work. The old woman had stayed up half the night on it, cursing Nadya's pride with every stroke of the caulking iron, and the seam was better now than it had ever been, and Nadya ran her hand along it in the dark and thought about her father, and about the room full of people sleeping in Maret's long room, and about the words *maintenance on the upper gates* sitting in her chest like the stone she'd swallowed, only heavier now.

She heard footsteps and didn't startle. She'd known he'd come. He always came to the boat at night; she'd watched him do it, checking the lines, checking the hull, a man who trusted nothing to hold without inspection.

"Storm's easing," Kestrel said from the doorway. "We could make the eastern channel by mid-morning."

"Maret told you about the riders."

"She told me." He came to stand beside her at the gunwale. In the lamplight his face was tired and hard and honest, and she thought, with a strange lurch, that she was beginning to know all his faces, and that this was a dangerous thing to know about a man. "She told me they had your description. Nadya — if someone on this river is feeding them our position—"

"Then we were already being followed before Sarnholt," Nadya said. "And every choice I've made since Kessarin has been watched." She looked down the dark length of the valley, where the river ran invisible under the last of the rain, toward the drowned channels and the high gates and whatever the survey team had found that was worth a flood. "I know these waters, Kestrel. Better than the Trust does. Better than their charts. And I know what *maintenance on the upper gates* means in a monsoon like this one."

He was quiet a moment. "What does it mean?"

She looked at him. "It means at first light we run the eastern channel," she said, "and we don't stop again until we're above the gorge."

Behind them, far up the black valley, so faint it might have been thunder settling — but it wasn't thunder, and they both knew it wasn't thunder — something deep in the mountains boomed once, like a door being opened.
