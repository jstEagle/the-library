## 9. Riders

Kestrel woke to the sound of hooves on wet stone.

It was not the sound itself that pulled him from sleep — hamlets like Sarn's Ferry had mules and dray horses moving at all hours — but the *quality* of it. Four horses, moving in step, unhurried, shod heavier than farm stock. Iron on stone with a rhythm too disciplined for drovers. He had spent eleven years listening to country, and his ears had never once lied to him, even when his mouth had cost him everything else.

The loft above the fish-drying shed was dark except for a seam of grey light along the eaves. Rain had stopped sometime before dawn; the thatch dripped in slow, irregular counts. Below him, the village still slept. Somewhere across the lane, Nadya was curled in the boat under an oilskin, one hand on the bilge pump out of pure habit, and the thought of her sleeping while iron-shod horses came down the ferry road made something cold settle behind his ribs.

He rolled to the gap between the shutters and looked out.

Four riders. Grey cloaks gone dark with river mist, riding in a line abreast rather than single file, which no honest traveler did on a narrow lane. They wore no livery — of course they didn't; the Halvard Trust had learned long ago that its name looked better on deeds than on saddles — but their tack was matched, oiled, expensive, and each man carried a carbine slung across his back in the manner of soldiers rather than hunters. The lead rider raised a closed fist as they reached the well at the center of the square, and all four halted together, silent, while the first light came up the valley behind them and turned the flood-swollen river below into a long ribbon of tarnished pewter.

They were not asking directions. Men asking directions dismounted.

Kestrel dressed without light, boots in hand, and went down the ladder rung by rung, testing each one. Old Maren's shed smelled of brine and smoke and years of herring, and he moved through it the way he'd once moved through high camps — weight on the outside edges of his feet, breath shallow, nothing rattling. At the door he paused and listened again.

The riders had split. Two remained by the well. Two were walking their horses toward the mooring path.

Toward the boat.

* * *

He crossed the lane in three strides and put his knuckles against the hull twice, soft, the old river-code knock that every pilot learned before they learned to read charts. Inside the oilskin he heard the immediate, total stillness of a woman coming awake — not a stir, not a rustle, just the sudden absence of sleep.

"Riders," he breathed through the gap in the tarpaulin. "Four. Two coming down the mooring path. You have maybe two minutes."

Nadya's eyes appeared in the dark, already fully lit, already furious about being awake. "Of course I do," she whispered. "Because why would anything ever be simple."

"Can you take her out quiet?"

"She's taking water through a seam I patched with fish glue and prayer, and you want me to ghost her past a picket line in fog." But she was already moving under the tarp, hands finding the mooring lines, the sweep oar, the bail bucket she kept wedged by the sternboard. "Where are you going?"

"To make sure those two minutes are still two minutes when you need them."

Her hand shot out and caught his wrist. Her grip was stronger than her size suggested, calloused from the sweep oar, and for a moment her face in the grey light was stripped of all its armor — young, and afraid, and trying very hard not to show either.

"Ishikawa." She used his whole name the way other people threw a rope. "Don't be stupid in a way I can't fix."

"That's the only kind of stupid I know," he said, "but I'll try for a small one."

Then he was up the bank and into the terraces, because there was no time left for anything better.

* * *

The hamlet clung to the hillside above the river in a series of stone-walled fields stepped upward like the rungs of some drowned ladder — rice paddies in the wet season, grazing terraces now, each wall shin-high and slick with moss and nine hundred years of careful mud. Kestrel knew this country the way he knew the inside of his own hands. Not this exact hillside, but its thousand cousins: the way guide-routes stitched through working land invisible to anyone who'd only ever read maps. There was a shepherd's cut behind the third terrace wall that would let him come out above the mooring path. There was a drainage channel under the fourth that a man could walk in if he didn't mind water to his knees and leeches to his waist.

He minded both. He took them anyway.

From the shadow of the terrace wall he watched the two riders reach the mooring. They sat their horses at the top of the slipway, looking down at the boat, and even at forty paces Kestrel could see the lead one's posture change — the small forward lean of a man who has found what he was sent to find. One of them unslung his carbine and laid it across his thighs, casual as a farmer leaning on a fence, and the gesture told Kestrel more than any shouted challenge could have. These were not men under orders to arrest. These were men under orders to tidy.

Which meant Edmund Halvard had stopped pretending the survey team had simply wandered off.

Kestrel picked up a loose piece of terrace stone, hefted it, and threw it high and hard into the brush on the far side of the mooring path, uphill, away from the water.

The crack of it through the wet branches brought both riders around, carbines up, horses sidling. And Kestrel stood up out of the terrace shadow with his empty hands visible and his voice pitched to carry — the voice he'd used on ledges and in whiteouts, the voice that made frightened people believe someone competent was in charge.

"Ferry office is the other direction, gentlemen," he called. "You've missed it."

The lead rider turned, and Kestrel saw enough of his face beneath the hood to know he'd been right about the type: paid, patient, and not inclined to conversation. "Name," the man said.

"You first."

"We're looking for a pilot and her passenger. Boat with a patched hull." The rider's eyes went past him, up the terraces, cataloguing escape routes with a professionalism Kestrel noted and disliked. "The Trust pays better than whatever they're paying you."

"The Trust pays in other people's valleys," Kestrel said. "I've seen the exchange rate."

The second rider laughed — one short bark, quickly smothered — and Kestrel understood, in the way he understood rockfall before it came, that the moment had a shape now and only one exit. He turned and ran.

* * *

The first shot took a chip off the terrace wall a hand's width from his shoulder, and the sound of it went through the morning like a hammer through ice. Birds exploded out of the alder scrub. Somewhere below, a dog began to scream, and doors began to open, and Kestrel ran the contour line with his head low, boots finding the worn footholds in the stone walls the way his grandfather's plates had found boundaries — precisely, stubbornly, where nobody wanted them.

Behind him, hooves. Not on the path — the riders wouldn't risk horses on the terraces — but swinging wide, two going for the upper track that looped around the hill toward the shrine clearing, two holding below to cut him off at the ford. He gave them credit for competence and hated them for it. Competent men, following forged paper, ready to drown a valley full of families so that a ledger somewhere would balance prettily.

He dropped into the drainage channel.

The water hit him like a fist — snowmelt-cold even now, monsoon-fed, running fast over smooth stones — and he went down it bent double, hands out, letting the current do half his work while the walls of the channel hid him from the upper track. Leeches found his wrists within ten steps. He counted his breathing instead: four in, four out, the rhythm that kept panic on the far side of a door. Ahead, the channel dropped through a culvert under the shrine path, and beyond the culvert the hill fell away into bamboo, and beyond the bamboo was the river bend where—

Where Nadya should already be gone.

He came out of the culvert spitting water, slid down a bank of black loam, and burst through the bamboo screen onto a shelf of rock above the river just in time to see the boat.

She was moving. Barely. The patched seam had opened again — he could see the dark ribbon of the leak trailing from her stern like a wound that wouldn't close — and she was fighting the flooded current with the sweep oar alone, engine cold, because an engine heard at dawn would have brought every rider in the valley down on her. She stood at the stern with her whole body in the work, oilskin streaming, and the boat crawled along the bank at the speed of a slow walk while the current leaned on her like a crowd pushing through a door.

And on the upper track, clear of the trees, two riders had seen her too.

Kestrel didn't decide anything. Later, when he tried to reconstruct it, there was no decision anywhere in the memory — just the shelf of rock, the drop, the water, and himself inside all of it, plunging thirty feet of wet granite into the pool below the bend.

The cold closed over him like a book.

* * *

He surfaced downstream of the boat, caught the gunwale, and hauled himself half aboard with his lungs full of broken glass. Nadya looked down at him with an expression he would remember for the rest of his life: fury and relief fused into a single unbearable thing.

"You absolute—" She couldn't finish it. She hauled on the oar instead. "Get in. Get *in*, you lunatic—"

"Riders on the track," he gasped, dragging himself over the transom. "Two. Coming along the bank. They've seen us."

"I *know* they've seen us, they're shooting at us!"

The third shot cracked off the sternboard and sent splinters spinning across the bilge. Kestrel got the engine cover off with shaking hands, said a wordless apology to the engine, and got her coughing on the second pull. The boat surged, wallowed, shipped a gallon of gray water over the transom, and finally began — grudgingly, leakingly, gloriously — to move.

The river took them the rest of the way. It was at flood stage, brown and muscular, carrying whole trees, and it carried them around the bend and out of the riders' sightline faster than any horse could follow along the tangled bank. Behind them the shots stopped. Ahead the gorge mouth opened like a held breath, mist pouring off the water, and the noise of the rapids rose to meet them, and for a long time neither of them said anything at all.

* * *

They ran the easy water until the adrenaline burned off, then tied up in a side slough choked with reeds, where the current forgot about them and the rain started again, soft and businesslike. Nadya bailed. Kestrel wrung out his shirt and picked four leeches off his arms with his thumbnail, flicking them into the river one by one, and the silence between them thickened until it had weight.

She spoke first, and she didn't look up from the bailing bucket.

"You should have let them find the boat empty."

"They weren't going to find it empty. They were going to find it and burn it, with or without you in it."

"So your plan was to stand in the open and announce yourself to armed men." Now she looked up, and her eyes were bright and hard as river ice. "One man. No weapon. On ground they own. That was the plan."

"My plan was to buy you two minutes."

"And it worked, congratulations, you bought two minutes and nearly died in a drainage ditch, and meanwhile I sat in this boat listening to gunfire over my head doing *nothing*, because you told me to go, and I went." The bucket hit the bilge boards. Water slopped over the rim. "Do you understand how that felt? Do you have any idea—"

"Yes," Kestrel said quietly. "As it happens, I do."

That stopped her. Because she did know — she'd been the one left behind before, in whatever story she hadn't told him yet, in whatever cargo run had cost her license and left that particular scar behind her eyes. He watched her recognize that he knew, and watch her decide to be angry anyway, because anger was easier than the other thing.

"You should have waited for me," she said, but the edge had gone out of it.

"If I'd waited, we'd both be dead, or worse — taken, and then Halvard knows exactly who's on the river and what we've read." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and made himself hold her gaze. "Nadya. If it were reversed — if you were the one on the bank and I was in the boat — what would you have done?"

She stared at him. The rain ticked on the oilskin. Somewhere in the reeds a rail bird called, three notes, like a question asked twice.

"I hate," she said finally, "that I would have done exactly what you did."

"There it is."

"It's a terrible way to live. It's how people get killed. Somebody always has to be the one in the boat, and somebody always has to be the idiot on the bank, and one day the idiot doesn't come out of the ditch." She dragged the bucket through the bilge again, harder than the water required. "So here's the new rule. We don't split unless there's no other choice. And if there's no other choice, the one in the boat waits as long as waiting doesn't kill her. Agreed?"

"Agreed."

"And Ishikawa?" She pointed the bailing scoop at him like a magistrate's gavel. "Next time you jump off a cliff, yell something first. So I know whether to plan a rescue or a funeral."

"Noted."

Something loosened in the air between them then — not forgiveness exactly, because there was nothing to forgive, but the settling of two loads into balance. She fished the last of Maren's flatbread out of the provision sack and broke it in half without asking, and they ate in the reeds while the rain came down and the river went on talking to itself about everything it had seen and drowned.

It was Kestrel who noticed it first: the water level in the slough, marked against a reed stalk he'd tied a strip of cloth to when they tied up. He watched it for the length of eating half a flatbread.

"Nadya."

"Don't say it."

"The river's still rising. Fast. Faster than yesterday." He stood up in the rocking boat, looking north toward the gorge, where the mist had thickened into something that wasn't weather. "This isn't the monsoon yet. The monsoon's a week out. This is upstream."

She followed his eyes and went very still, the way she had on the day she'd recognized the route he was following and stopped talking about it.

"Somebody's opened gates," she said.

Above the gorge, faint through the rain, came a sound neither of them could mistake — a long, deep, structural groan, like the valley itself shifting its weight. The water in the slough shivered. The cloth marker on the reed stalk climbed another finger-width while they watched.

Kestrel thought of the logbook hidden in the watchtower wall, drying page by page under his shirt against his skin. He thought of boundary stones under brown water, and villages with legal title, and a young heir reviewing flood schedules with cold precision.

"He's not chasing us anymore," Kestrel said. "He's drowning the evidence. And everyone in the lower valley with it."

Nadya was already untying the painter.
