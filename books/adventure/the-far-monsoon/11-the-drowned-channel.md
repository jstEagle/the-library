## 11. The Drowned Channel

The coordinates in the logbook did not lead anywhere. That was the first thing I noticed, and the last thing I understood for a long time after.

They led to a blank space.

I sat in the stern of Nadya's boat with the waterlogged pages spread across my knees, drying by degrees in a thin sun that kept sliding behind cloud, and I traced the final plotted line with my finger until it simply stopped — not at a village marker, not at a river fork, but in the middle of what every map in my satchel called open water. The Sareth's western channel. Flooded, according to the Trust's surveys, since before anyone now living had held title to anything.

"Dead end?" Nadya asked from the tiller. She didn't look over. She'd learned to read me sideways, the way she read the river — out of the corner of her eye, while pretending to watch something else.

"Worse," I said. "A blank."

"Blanks are never blank." She said it the way other people say *good morning*. "Blanks are where somebody paid to have something erased."

* * *

We entered the channel at first light on the third day, when the mist still lay on the water like a sheet drawn over furniture in a house whose owners had gone away and were not coming back.

It was wrong from the very beginning, though it took my eyes a mile or two to say so. The current was too slow. A channel this wide, this far into the highlands, should have run hard with snowmelt even in dry season, but the water moved with the sluggish patience of a reservoir, not a river. Held back. Managed. Somewhere downstream, gates stood closed against it, keeping this water deep and quiet and exactly where the Trust wanted it.

And the trees. That was the thing that finally made the back of my neck prickle. They stood too far apart, and they stood in rows.

Nobody plants trees in rows unless they're orchards, and nobody leaves orchards standing in the middle of a flood channel unless the flooding was an afterthought — or an apology that never got made. Their crowns rose through the surface here and there, black and skeletal, dead these many years, and between them the water lay so clear and still that I could see down thirty feet to where pale shapes waited.

Nadya cut the engine and let us drift. Even she had gone quiet, and Nadya's silences were always louder than her talk.

"Stones," she said finally. "Look at the stones."

I looked. Beneath the hull, beneath the keel, beneath twenty feet of deliberately impounded water, the shapes resolved as we drifted over them: foundation walls. Lintels. The stub of a chimney. A wellhead with its cover rotted through, opening downward into darkness like a pupil. Street lines — actual streets, laid out on a grid some surveyor had once walked off with a chain and a plumb bob, streets with names, streets where children had been taught to walk on the left side going up the hill.

Villages. More than one. The drowned foundations ran along both banks and out across what had been the valley floor, whole communities submerged under a lake that appeared on no honest chart because it was not a lake at all. It was a drowning, dressed up in the passive grammar of hydrology.

"They had title," I said. My voice came out strange, flattened, as if the water had gotten into it. "Mara said it — the villages in the western channel held legal title. Deeds older than the Trust. You can't flood land you don't own unless you make the owning disappear."

"So you drown the deed-holders' houses," Nadya said, "and call it flood control." She spat over the side, which was as close as she came to prayer. "My grandfather used to say the rich don't steal things. They steal the word for the thing."

* * *

I went down twice.

Not far — the cold saw to that, and the clarity of the water was a lie told by depth; ten feet down everything turned green-black and close. But I had to touch it. Some part of me would not believe the evidence floating under our hull until my hands confirmed it.

The first dive took me to the street. I came down through a shaft of grey light past a dead tree whose branches reached upward like a man wading toward air he'd never reach, and I landed knee-deep in silt on something flat and fitted. Cobblestones. I knelt there in the cold and pressed my palm against them, and they were smooth — worn smooth by feet, by cart wheels, by decades of ordinary living, long before the water came. You cannot fake that wear. It is the one signature no forger can counterfeit.

The second dive I followed the street to its end, where a doorway stood open in a fallen wall, and inside, half-buried, I found an iron hinge the size of my forearm and, wedged in the silt beside it, a ceramic tile glazed blue, unbroken, with a pattern of fish around its rim. Somebody's kitchen. Somebody had eaten breakfast off that pattern while the rain came down outside, in a house on a street with a name, on land with a deed, and then one day men with government paper had come and the water had come after, and the fish on the tile swam on regardless, circling forever around a rim nobody would ever set a plate on again.

I came up gasping, more from the chest-tightness than the cold, and Nadya hauled me over the gunwale without a word and put a blanket around my shoulders like she was angry about having to do it.

"You look like a man who found a grave," she said.

"I found a kitchen."

"That's worse." She handed me tea gone lukewarm in the thermos and watched me drink it, her eyes narrow. "Kestrel. Why does this channel bother you more than the gorge bothered you? You ran the gorge like it was a Sunday. This is making you quiet, and your quiet is bad quiet."

Because I knew the hand. That was why. And I wasn't ready to say it yet, because saying it would make it true, and it had been true for three days already, growing truer with every page of the logbook I deciphered.

* * *

That night we moored against a dead tree whose trunk rose clean out of the floodwater, and I built a fire in the boat's little iron brazier and brought out the logbook and my grandfather's maps together for the first time.

I had carried those maps for eleven years. Folded into oilcloth, wrapped in oilskin, moved from pack to pack to the lining of a coat, through two countries and every cheap room and colder camp I'd slept in since the registries struck my name. I told myself I carried them out of sentiment. Grandfather's things, grandfather's ghost, the usual soft excuses a man makes for the weight he chooses not to put down.

I unfolded them now across my knees, and the firelight fell on the linework, and my stomach dropped the way it drops on a ridge when the ground falls away faster than your eyes expected.

The style. God help me, the *style*.

Every surveyor has a hand, the way every writer does. My grandfather's was unmistakable once you'd seen it: contour lines drawn with a slight thickening on the north-facing slopes, as if his pen hesitated there out of respect for the shade; spot elevations written in a small, severe numeration slanted forward like a runner leaning into wind; and his boundary markers — his signature above all signatures — marked not with the standard triangle but with a small open circle pierced by a single stroke, a symbol he told me once he'd taken from an old bridge-builder's mark, *because a boundary should be a crossing, Kestrel, not a wall.*

There it was. On the logbook's sketch plates, copied from originals the survey team had photographed in whatever archive or strongroom they'd found: the north-slope thickening. The runner-slanted figures. And on the oldest plate, the boundary stones ringing the drowned villages below our moored boat — every one of them marked with the open circle and the single stroke.

A crossing, not a wall.

"These aren't Trust surveys," I said. I could barely get volume into it. "These were never Trust surveys. The Trust didn't commission them. They *took* them." I turned the pages toward Nadya, my finger shaking, and I hated that it shook. "This is my grandfather's work. This is his hand — see the contours, see the markers? He surveyed this valley forty years ago. These villages, these boundaries, these channels — he mapped every stone of it. The plates the team found, the ones that prove the Trust's claims are forged — they're Ishikawa plates. The original surveys of the Sareth were done by an Ishikawa."

Nadya looked at the pages for a long time. Then at me. Then out at the black water, where the drowned streets lay beneath us like a sentence someone had tried to strike through and failed.

"The Trust's founding claim," she said slowly, "the one they've been waving in courts and capitals for thirty years — it's built on your family's maps."

"On maps they stole after they ruined him." The words came out of me like something splintering. "He wouldn't sell. I know that story the way I know my own heartbeat — the honorable surveyor who refused the money, who got discredited, blacklisted, driven out. I grew up on that story. I thought it was a fable. A family legend to explain why we were poor and bitter." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "It wasn't a fable. It was a *deed abstract*. Everything the Trust owns, they own because an Ishikawa measured it first and an Ishikawa said no."

The fire cracked. Somewhere out in the flooded channel, a dead branch settled beneath the water with a sound like a door closing softly.

"And you," Nadya said. Not a question. She'd already assembled it; I could see it in her face, the pilot's habit of reading currents before they show on the surface. "Your blacklist. Two countries, no hearing, no appeal, no reason ever given. You thought it was your disaster on the Karst traverse."

"It was their machine." My hands had gone cold around the tin cup. "The same machine that drowned these villages. You don't ruin a man with a hearing — hearings leave records. You ruin him quietly, the way you flood a valley. Open the gates, wait, and by the time anyone asks questions, the landscape has changed and everyone's forgotten there was ever a road through it." I looked down at the maps, at the open circles and single strokes marching along boundaries that legally, officially, had never existed. "They drowned him first. Then they drowned the proof of him. And then they drowned the villages that proved *that*, and called the water maintenance."

Nadya was quiet a moment. When she spoke, her voice had lost its edge entirely.

"My cargo run," she said. "The one that cost me my license. The manifests I didn't ask enough about — they were Trust freight. Equipment. Gate machinery." She turned her cup in her hands. "I helped build the lock on the door of this room, Kestrel. I just never saw the room."

I reached over and put my hand over hers, and neither of us said anything else for a while, because there was nothing to say that the water below us hadn't already said better.

* * *

Near midnight the clouds tore open and the moon came through, and the drowned channel turned to hammered silver, and I saw it clearly for the first time: the shape of the whole crime, laid out beneath our keel like a body under a shroud.

The villages had held title. The title rested on surveys. The surveys were Ishikawa work — accurate, sworn, filed. So the Trust had taken the surveyor, taken his reputation, taken his plates, refiled them under its own name with dates forged backward, and then removed the witnesses. Not with guns. With water. A controlled release, decades ago, timed to peak melt, written up afterward as a natural flood event. The families scattered. The deeds drowned. The boundaries vanished under twenty feet of managed, patient, deliberate stillness.

Except that water forgets nothing. Water keeps everything. Water is the worst possible accomplice for a crime like this, because water holds the shape of what you give it, forever, waiting for someone to come and read it.

And someone had come. The survey team had come, with their instruments and their government boots, and they had read it, and they had written down the coordinates that led to this blank space on the map — because they understood what I understood only now, sitting in a leaking boat above a drowned town with my grandfather's ghost in my lap:

The blank space was the archive. The flood was the vault. And the vault had never been locked — only dark.

"Mara's coordinates," I said suddenly. "The final entry. It doesn't point at the ruins. It points *through* them." I spread the logbook flat and held the lamp low. "Look — the bearing runs straight down the old high street. There's a structure at the terminus. Something they wanted found by whoever came next. Something they couldn't carry out."

Nadya leaned over my shoulder. Below us, silvered by moonlight, the drowned street ran away into the dark like a road into sleep.

"A records house," she murmured. "Every old town has one. Deeds, registers, the parish rolls." Her eyes came up and met mine, and I saw the same thing kindling in them that was burning in my chest — that terrible, wonderful, dangerous feeling of a trail going hot. "You're telling me the original deeds might still be down there. Sealed. Filed. In a building full of paper that's been underwater for thirty years."

"I'm telling me that Edmund Halvard thinks they're down there," I said. "Which is why the sluice schedules matter. Which is why he'll open every gate in the valley rather than let this channel drop one foot." I folded the maps along their old creases, careful as handling a wound. "The flood isn't just to hide the ruins anymore, Nadya. It's a race. Every day this water stays high, the paper dies a little more. And somewhere upstream, a young man with a cold, precise mind is watching gauges and deciding how much longer the vault stays sealed."

Nadya looked out at the silvered water, at the dead orchards standing in their drowned rows, at the wellhead gaping its small black eye at the moon.

"Then we'd better find it before he finds his nerve," she said, and reached for the tiller.

Above us the monsoon clouds were gathering again, far off to the south, patient as everything else in this valley. And beneath us, in the dark, at the end of a street with no name on any living map, a records house waited with its doors shut and its paper sleeping — holding, if my grandfather's hand had not lied to me, the only inheritance that had ever truly been mine.
