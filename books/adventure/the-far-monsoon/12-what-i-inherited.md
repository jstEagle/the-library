## 12. What I Inherited

The rain came down in ropes that night, and the boat was tied up in a drowned alder grove where the channel widened into something that had once been a lake and was now only a rumor of one. Nadya rigged the tarp over the cockpit and lit the little spirit stove under the canvas, and the flame made a small orange world against all that black water.

Kestrel sat with the logbook wrapped in oilcloth on his knees and could not stop looking at it.

He had told himself, since the watchtower, that he would wait. That he'd read it again in daylight, cross-check the coordinates, be methodical about it — that was what a good guide did, what a careful man did. But the firelight kept catching the water-stained pages through the cloth as if they were glowing, and every time he looked at them he saw his grandfather's hand.

Not a resemblance. Not a style he recognized from study. He *knew* that hand. He had grown up watching it move across butcher paper on a kitchen table in a house that smelled of ink and pipe smoke, drawing contour lines with a ruling pen held at exactly forty-five degrees, the hachures falling away from ridge crests like rain off an eave.

The surveyor who had drawn the plates the team found — the surveyor whose initials were pressed into the brass corner-mounts of the original boundary markers — signed himself *T. Ishikawa*.

Kestrel's grandfather's name was Tomas Ishikawa.

"You've gone somewhere," Nadya said. She was mending the second worst seam with a sail needle and waxed twine, working by feel more than sight, her hands moving in the dark like they had their own opinions. "You've been gone somewhere for two days. Where?"

Kestrel didn't answer right away. Out on the water, the rain made a sound like static, like a radio tuned between stations, and somewhere far upstream — too far to matter tonight, but not far enough — the sluice gates stood open in the dark, doing whatever Edmund Halvard had ordered them to do.

"My grandfather," Kestrel said, "was a surveyor."

Nadya's needle paused. "I know. You said. Once. Drunk, in Port Merren, the night I agreed to take you upriver." She went back to the seam. "You said it like an apology."

"It is one."

"Then make it a longer one. We're not going anywhere until this rain breaks, and I hate talking about myself." A pause. The needle bit through canvas. "So talk."

* * *

The house Kestrel grew up in stood above the terraced fields on the south rim of the Sareth Valley, and when he was small he believed it was the finest house in the world because of what was inside it: maps. Maps everywhere. Rolled in tubes along the walls, pinned flat across tables, hanging from beams like strange pale laundry. His grandfather had surveyed half the highlands in his prime, and the other half in his stubbornness, and the whole house was the evidence.

"He taught me to read ground before he taught me to read words," Kestrel said. "I'd sit on his knee and he'd put my finger on a contour line and say, *what does the land do here, boy?* And if I got it wrong he wouldn't tell me. He'd just wait. He said the land always tells you the truth eventually; the trick is being patient enough to hear it."

Nadya snorted softly. "He never met a river. Rivers lie."

"He'd have liked you." Kestrel turned the oilcloth package over in his hands. "He worked the Sareth for eleven years. Boundary surveys, mostly. The valley families hired him — the fishing people, the terrace farmers, the hamlets up the drowned channels. They couldn't afford government surveyors, so they pooled what they had, season after season, and he did it for cost because he loved the work and because—" Kestrel stopped. Swallowed. "Because he believed a map was a promise. That if you drew a line honestly, the line would protect people. That paper could hold back water. Paper could hold back anything, if it was true."

The stove hissed. Rain drummed the tarp.

"The Halvard Trust came to him in his last year of fieldwork," Kestrel said. "This was — thirty years ago now. More. The Trust was young then. Old Halvard was still alive, and he was buying up claims all through the middle valley, and there was a problem: the old boundary markers didn't agree with the Trust's deeds. The Trust's documents said their holdings ran to the high-water line of the old floodplain. The markers on the ground said otherwise. Markers said the valley families' title ran deeper — right down into what's underwater now."

"And your grandfather had surveyed those markers."

"He'd *set* half of them." Kestrel heard his own voice go flat and hard, and didn't soften it. "They offered him money first. Then a position — chief surveyor of the Trust, a house in the capital, his name on everything. Then they offered his family security, which was a threat wearing a coat. He refused all of it. He said—" Kestrel closed his eyes. "He said, *a man who sells a line sells everyone standing behind it.*"

"What happened?"

"You can guess. It took two years. First his credentials were questioned — discrepancies found in old filings, clerical errors that somehow all ran one direction. Then an inquiry. Then the inquiry found irregularities, and the irregularities became findings, and the findings became a blacklist. Two countries. Same machine, same hand on both registries — you'd know how that works better than most." He glanced at her; she didn't flinch, just kept sewing. "He lost the license. Then the clients, who couldn't hire a discredited surveyor. Then the house. My mother tried to fight it and it broke her marriage and nearly broke her. He died when I was nineteen, in a rented room, with his instruments in pawn and his plates in a government archive nobody could access without Trust countersignature."

"That's why you were blacklisted," Nadya said quietly. "It wasn't just the Karst Traverse."

It was Kestrel's turn to be silent.

Because yes — there was the official story, the one he'd told a hundred times in registry offices and port bars with the particular bitterness of a man who believes his ruin was his own fault: the Karst Traverse, the client who died, the route he shouldn't have pushed in rising water. And that story was true. He had pushed. A man named Ovid Renner had died below him on an ice-glazed pitch, and Kestrel had carried the weight of it ever since, and he did not ask anyone to take that weight from him.

But the inquiry afterward — the one that stripped his license in both countries — had been chaired by a man whose brother-in-law sat on the Halvard Trust's board. And the finding had leaned, delicately, on a pattern: *judgment consistent with the subject's family history of professional misconduct.* As if the blacklist were hereditary. As if the Ishikawa name itself were a defect to be managed.

"I thought it was coincidence for years," Kestrel said. "Then I thought it was paranoia. Now I'm sitting in a flooded valley holding my grandfather's handwriting in a dead man's logbook, and I don't believe in coincidence anymore."

* * *

He unwrapped the logbook and opened it to the page he'd marked, angling it toward the stove light.

The team's lead surveyor — a careful, cramped hand, nothing like Tomas's — had traced the boundary markers upstream to their source, exactly as the coordinates suggested, and had documented each one: brass mounts, stamped serials, and beneath the stamps, the surveyor's touchmark. A small device pressed into soft metal to sign work, the way a smith signs a blade.

Tomas Ishikawa's touchmark was a mountain with three lines of water running from its foot.

It was on marker after marker after marker. And beside the last entry, the team's surveyor had pasted a rubbing — graphite on tissue, lifted straight from the brass — and written: *Plates predate Trust commission by 22 years. Originals verified against archive seals. The Trust's deeds cite a survey that did not exist when these boundaries were fixed. The claims are forged. T.I.'s discrediting was the forgery's foundation.*

And below that, the final entry, the one Kestrel had read aloud in the watchtower with his voice cracking on the last word:

*Halvard's man offered us money. We said no.*

"They said no," Kestrel repeated now, uselessly, to the fire. "Four of them, alone, in the wet, with the Trust's riders asking questions in every hamlet. They said no."

Nadya set down the needle. She looked at the rubbing for a long moment — the three lines of water running from the mountain's foot — and then at Kestrel.

"You knew," she said. Not accusing. Testing. "When you took the contract. Some part of you knew."

"No." And then, because lying to her by firelight felt worse than lying to the rain: "Maybe. I knew the Sareth was restricted territory. I knew the Trust held it. I knew my grandfather had worked the valley and been ruined, and I knew the two facts had to be connected, and I never once let myself finish the thought. Twenty years I didn't finish the thought. Because finishing it meant—" He gestured at the black water beyond the tarp, at the drowned channels, at all of it. "Meant this. Meant that the thing that ate my grandfather's life is still alive and still eating, and I spent my whole career running the other direction, taking contracts in karst and ice, anywhere but a river valley, anywhere the Trust's shadow fell."

"So you ran to mountains."

"Mountains don't drown people on purpose."

"Rivers do," Nadya said, "when someone opens the gates." She picked the logbook out of his hands gently, the way you take something fragile from someone shaking, and turned pages with her thumb, careful of the damp. "My turn, since we're confessing. The cargo I ran, the year I lost my license — half of it was Trust freight. Sealed crates, no manifests, moved at night through channels the charts don't show. I told myself it was just cargo. Crates are crates." Her jaw tightened. "Third run, one crate shifted and broke open. Survey instruments. New ones, government issue, still in factory grease. And papers with the Trust's seal. I understood then that I wasn't hauling freight. I was hauling the machinery that un-makes people. And I finished the run anyway, because I was paid, and because the men I was running for don't forgive debts." She handed the book back. "That's the complicity I carry. So don't apologize to me for running. Apologize to the valley."

"I am," Kestrel said. "That's what this is."

* * *

The rain eased near midnight, from ropes to threads, and the fire settled to coals, and Nadya slept in the bow with her boots on the way boat people sleep — ready to move. Kestrel stayed up with the logbook and his grandfather's ghost.

He thought about the day he'd found the compass.

After the funeral, going through the rented room's few effects, he'd found a brass compass in a sock drawer, wrapped in a page torn from a survey field book. On the page, in that unmistakable hand, was a single line: *For the boy. The needle doesn't lie even when the men do.* Kestrel had carried it on every contract since, and had told himself it was sentiment, a keepsake, a dead man's trinket. He had never once considered that it might be an instruction.

*The needle doesn't lie even when the men do.*

His grandfather hadn't left him a grudge. He'd left him a tool and a standard. And Kestrel had spent twenty years treating the inheritance like a wound instead of a duty — hiding the name when he could, flinching when he couldn't, letting the blacklist define the shape of his life the way a fence defines the shape of a field.

But fences, his grandfather used to say, tapping a boundary line on a map, are only as honest as the hands that drew them. Someone had drawn a fence around Kestrel's life with dishonest hands. And fences drawn in lies could be redrawn in truth — that was the whole of surveying, the whole of the law, the whole of the stubborn faith that had gotten Tomas Ishikawa ruined: that truth, properly measured and properly recorded, was a force. That paper could hold back water.

The team had proved it. Four people with a logbook and a set of rubbings, saying *no* to the most powerful inheritance empire in two countries. They had done their part. They had measured the truth and recorded it.

What remained was to deliver it. To carry it downstream, past the gates, past the riders, past the elegant cold-eyed heir who would rather drown a valley than be diminished by it — and put it in front of eyes that mattered, before the monsoon peaked and the water erased everything the way it had erased the villages, the channels, the names.

Kestrel looked at the sleeping boat, at the patched seams and the scarred gunwales and the woman in the bow who had lost her license to the same machine and finished the run anyway and hated herself for it ever since. He looked at the coals, and the rain, and the black patient water.

"We're going to need to run the Middle Gorge," he said aloud, very quietly, to no one. "At flood stage. With this boat."

From the bow, without opening her eyes: "I heard that."

"You were supposed to."

"I know." She rolled over. "Sleep, Ishikawa. Tomorrow I'll tell you all the reasons it's impossible, and then we'll do it anyway, and I'll hate every minute, and you'll owe me a new hull."

Kestrel wrapped the logbook back in its oilcloth and tucked it inside his jacket, against his chest, where it rode like a second heartbeat. For the first time since the watchtower, since the gorge, since maybe since Ovid Renner fell past him into the white — he slept without dreaming of falling.

He dreamed instead of a kitchen table, and a ruling pen at forty-five degrees, and an old man's voice saying: *what does the land do here, boy?*

And in the dream, for the first time, Kestrel answered correctly: *it waits. It waits for someone honest enough to draw the line.*

Dawn came gray and low over the drowned alders, and with it, far downstream, faint on the wind, came the sound Kestrel had been dreading and expecting in equal measure — engines. Trust launches, working their way up the channel, beating the water for strangers.

Nadya was already at the tiller, untying the line. "Gorge," she said. "Now. Before they close the top of it."
