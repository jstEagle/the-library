## 13. The Middle Gorge

**Kestrel**

The Middle Gorge announced itself before they saw it — a low, continuous thunder rolling up the valley like a stone being dragged across the floor of the world.

Kestrel stood at the bow with the surveyors' logbook wrapped in oilcloth against his chest and watched the river narrow ahead into a corridor of black rock. The flood had turned the Sareth into something that no longer resembled water. It was brown and muscular, carrying whole trees on its back, and it moved with the confidence of something that had never once been argued with.

"That's the entrance," Nadya called from the helm, her voice pitched to carry over the roar without wasting a syllable on fear. "Half a kilometer of nothing you want to be in. After that, the narrows."

Behind him in the boat, Mara clutched the gunwale with both hands. The other two survivors — Ovid and the quiet one, Sena — had wedged themselves among the cargo with the practiced calm of people who had already decided how they would die if it came to that. Kestrel had seen that look before, on climbers waiting out a storm at altitude. It wasn't resignation. It was bookkeeping.

"How fast?" he shouted back.

"Faster than we should be going. Slower than they are." She jerked her chin upstream, where the bend still hid their pursuers but not the sound of them — engines, more than one, running hard against the current. Trust riders with a patrol skiff and, if Kestrel's ears were right, something bigger behind it. "They'll have us in the open water below the narrows unless we put this gorge between us. So we put the gorge between us."

"You've run it?"

"In this boat? No." Her grin was quick and crooked and entirely unconvincing. "In anything? Once, at half this level, ten years ago, in a boat I liked better than this one." She spat over the side, an old pilot's habit that Kestrel had come to understand as a kind of prayer. "The channel runs left off the tongue, then hard right under the overhang. If the flood hasn't moved the rocks — and the flood has definitely moved the rocks — we thread it and come out the far side rattled but floating."

"And if it has?"

"Then we find out what's downstream of the narrows," she said, "which nobody knows, which is my favorite kind of geography."

He almost laughed. That was the thing about Nadya Voss that three weeks on her leaking boat had taught him: she joked hardest when the arithmetic was worst. He'd stopped mistaking it for carelessness somewhere around the third night of bailing side by side in the rain. It was load-bearing. Take it out of her and the whole structure might come down.

"Kestrel." Her voice changed. The joke drained out of it like water through a seam. "If we hole out in there, there's no eddy for six kilometers. No bank worth the name. You understand what I'm telling you."

"I understand."

"I need you to say it."

"If we go in," he said, "we don't stop until we come out."

She held his eyes for one long second — the same look she'd given him the first night, when she'd known his name and his blacklist and taken his money anyway — and then she turned to the wheel and brought the boat around to face the throat of the gorge.

The last thing Kestrel saw of the open valley was the sky, high and bruise-colored, and the first thing he saw after that was the wall of spray rising off the tongue of the rapid, white as a held breath.

* * *

**Nadya**

Her father had taught her to read water the way other fathers taught scripture: *the river always tells you where it doesn't want you to go.* Ten years of running cargo for men whose names she'd been smart enough not to ask had refined the lesson into instinct. Instinct was what kept her alive now, because there was no time for thought.

The tongue hit them at full flood and the bow rose, hung, and slammed down into a trough deep enough to swallow the mast-light. Green water came over the gunwales in a single cold slap. Somewhere behind her Mara screamed — or maybe it was the river; at this volume the two were indistinguishable.

*Left off the tongue.* The channel was there, exactly where her memory said it would be, except the memory had drawn it two meters narrower and the flood had filled the difference with standing waves the size of oxen. She fed the wheel left, felt the current take the stern and swing it, corrected too much, corrected again — the old dance, the only dance, the one where the partner never forgives a missed step.

"Hold!" she roared, and heard Kestrel relay it down the boat, his voice stripped to its bones.

The overhang came up faster than anything had a right to move. Black rock ceilinged the channel, dripping, close enough that she could have reached up and touched the stone teeth hanging from it. Right. Hard right. She threw the wheel and the boat answered with a groan of timber that she felt in her wrists, and then they were through — through, *through*, the narrows peeling away behind them and the river opening into the long churning mile below —

— and that was when she saw the tree.

It stood in the middle of the current like a sentinel that had died at its post: a full fir, root-ball upstream, trunk angled down into the flood, branches combing the surface. A strainer. The word every river runner learned once and never forgot, because a strainer let the water through and held everything else — boats, bodies, the people inside them — pinned under the flood while it went on without them.

There was no channel around it. There was barely a channel at all anymore; the flood had rearranged the lower mile while she'd been busy surviving the upper one, and the tree sat squarely across the only line the water offered.

"Nadya—" Kestrel was beside her now, one hand locked on the rail, reading her face instead of the river because he couldn't read the river. "Tell me you see a way."

She didn't answer. Answering cost breath and breath cost steering. She took the boat at the gap between the root-ball and the near bank — a gap the width of the boat and a handspan more — and for one suspended moment it looked like it would hold, it actually looked like—

The flood shifted.

She felt it before she saw it: a change in the pull on the hull, the current shrugging like a sleeper turning over. An eddy line rolled off the strainer's downstream shoulder and caught their stern sideways, and the boat — her boat, her leaking, stubborn, beloved ruin of a boat — swung broadside into the current like a door coming off its hinges.

The world tilted. Water came aboard in tons. She heard timber crack somewhere below her feet, felt the deck lurch and soften under her boots, and knew with a pilot's cold certainty that they'd found a rock with the hull and the rock had won the exchange.

Then the rail hit her hip, the sky swung past, and Nadya Voss went into the Sareth at full flood.

* * *

**Kestrel**

The cold was total. It wasn't temperature so much as erasure — the river closed over him and deleted everything he was, every thought replaced by a single animal command: *up.*

He surfaced gasping into chaos. The boat was thirty meters downstream and sideways, shipping water, three heads visible clinging to it — Mara, Ovid, Sena, all accounted for. Nadya was not with them.

He found her by the white of her jacket, fifty meters off, being carried downriver at the speed of a galloping horse. And beyond her, directly in her line, the strainer waited with its branches combed flat by the current, patient as a trap that had never once needed resetting.

Everything else fell away. The contract, the logbook against his chest, the blacklist, the Trust, Edmund Halvard and his elegant sluice schedules — all of it dropped off him like a coat in summer. What remained was older and simpler: a man who had spent his life reading dangerous ground, watching someone he — 

*Someone,* his mind supplied, and refused to finish the sentence, because finishing it would take time and time was the one thing the river wasn't selling.

He kicked free of his pack. The logbook — he hesitated one half-second, one unforgivable half-second, and then tore it out of the oilcloth and shoved the bundle at the boat as a wave carried him alongside. Mara's hands closed on it. Good. Let the paper survive even if he didn't; paper was the whole point of all of this.

Then he swam.

Not toward her — toward the eddy line above her, the way a tracker cuts the angle to intercept a trail rather than chase it. His grandfather had taught him that on a mountain, of all places: *never follow, always meet.* The water tried to peel him downstream and he fought it stroke by stroke, lungs burning, the roar of the gorge filling his skull until thought was impossible and only the body's dumb arithmetic remained: angle, distance, strength remaining. Not enough. Not nearly enough.

But rivers are honest about one thing: they deliver everything to the same place eventually. The strainer's eddy folded back on itself, and Nadya's line of drift curved, and Kestrel's curve met hers at the edge of the boil where the drowned branches sucked the surface down into a brown whirlpool stitched with needles of broken wood.

His hand closed on her collar.

She fought him. Of course she did — drowning people fight everything, and she was half-drowned already, blue-lipped, eyes wide and fixed on nothing. He got an arm across her chest from behind, the way the rescue manuals said, the way he'd learned and never used, and locked his grip and turned his back to the strainer and gave himself to the current.

The river took them both.

* * *

**Nadya**

Later — hours later, years later — she would try to reconstruct those minutes and fail. Memory gave her fragments only: the taste of silt. The pressure of an arm across her ribs, iron and human and impossibly stubborn. Rock scraping her back. A moment of air, stolen between waves, and Kestrel's voice against her ear, raw as torn canvas:

"*Breathe when I breathe.*"

So she did. She matched her lungs to his, breath for breath, the way oarsmen match strokes, and it was the strangest intimacy of her life — closer than any lover, closer than family — this stranger's rhythm holding hers together when her own body wanted nothing but surrender. The river hammered them against stone, pulled them under, gave them back. Each time they surfaced he found air for them both, found the seam in the current, found the next rock to put between them and the strainer's hungry comb.

And then, all at once, the river set them down.

An eddy behind a shelf of granite, a pocket of slack water no bigger than a room, and the flood simply — released them, the way a dog releases a toy it has grown bored of. Kestrel hauled her onto the shelf and collapsed beside her, and for a long time neither of them moved. The gorge thundered past close enough to touch. Spray drifted down like rain that couldn't commit.

Nadya lay on her back on wet stone and laughed.

It came out of her cracked and ragged and unstoppable, laughter with river water in it, and after a moment Kestrel started too, lying beside her, both of them laughing at the gray sky like a pair of lunatics, because the alternative was feeling what had almost happened, and neither of them was ready for that yet.

"You cut the angle," she managed at last. "Above me. Nobody does that. Nobody *knows* to do that."

"My grandfather taught me. On a glacier, of all the stupid places." He was still breathing hard. "Never follow. Always meet."

"He sounds like he'd have made a hell of a pilot."

"He'd have hated boats." Kestrel turned his head to look at her. His face was scraped raw along one cheekbone, and there was silt in the creases around his eyes, and he looked at her the way she imagined a man looks at a fire in winter. "You're bleeding."

"So are you."

"Yes. But I asked about you first."

Something in her chest went loose and warm and terrifying. She sat up before it could become a sentence, before either of them could say whatever was queuing up behind their teeth, and scanned the river.

The boat was gone from where they'd last seen it. For three full seconds the loss landed in her like a second drowning — and then she spotted it, wedged against the far bank two hundred meters down, listing badly, three figures waving something pale and frantic. Alive. All three. And the boat, God help her, still floating.

"She's holed," Kestrel said, following her gaze.

"She's *patchable.*" Nadya got her feet under her, tested her legs, found them obedient if rude about it. "There's a shingle bar below the shelf. We can swim the eddy chain down to it. And Kestrel—" She stopped. Looked at him. The words she'd been holding since the water let them go rose up and refused to be held any longer. "You came in after me. You gave up the pack, you gave up the dry clothes, you gave up — you jumped into full flood for a woman you've known three weeks, and I need to know why, because I've spent ten years making sure nobody has a reason to do that for *me*, and I don't know what to do with it."

Kestrel was quiet for a moment. The gorge roared. Somewhere upstream, faint beneath the water's voice, the engines of their pursuers were still hunting.

"Because the crew is the point," he said finally. "I spent six years thinking the work was the point — the summits, the routes, the name in the registry. Then they took the name and I found out I'd already lost the work, and what was left was nothing. Just a man with a reputation and no one to share a fire with." He shook his head, water flying. "Out here, with you, bailing your rotten boat and running your rotten gorges — this is the most myself I've felt since before the blacklist. You're not cargo I'm protecting, Nadya. You're the reason the trip exists."

Nadya Voss had run flooded rivers, outrun revenue patrols, lied to customs officers with a straight face and a steady pulse. None of it had prepared her for that.

So she did the only thing her body knew how to do with a feeling too large for words: she grabbed the front of his soaked shirt and kissed him, hard and brief and tasting of silt, and then shoved him toward the water's edge before either of them could make it complicated.

"Eddy chain," she said, her voice not entirely steady. "Three pools down to the shingle bar. Stay off the green water. And Kestrel—"

"Yeah?"

"When we get to the boat, you're sleeping in the dry spot tonight. Non-negotiable."

* * *

**Kestrel**

They reached the boat as the light failed.

She lay heeled over on the shingle bar like a wounded animal, and she was worse than she'd looked from the shelf: a rock had opened the hull below the waterline just forward of the keel, a gash as long as a forearm, plugged for now by a shirt jammed into it and a plank lashed over the wound. The cargo was soaked. The engine had swallowed half the Sareth and refused, with mechanical dignity, to turn over. Ovid had a gash across his scalp that Mara was cleaning with the last of their antiseptic, and Sena had gotten a fire going out of driftwood and desperation, because some skills transcend circumstance.

But they were five. Five alive, on a bar of gravel in the middle of the Middle Gorge, with the flood roaring past and the Trust's engines somewhere upstream in the dark, unable to follow them through water that had nearly killed professionals.

"We can't run the rest of the gorge in this," Nadya said, crouched over the gash in the hull, her fingers reading the damage the way Kestrel read ground. "Not patched, not loaded, not at night. We patch properly, we wait for the level to drop, we run it at dawn."

"The riders will have found the wreck site by then," Mara said quietly. "They'll know we went in. They'll be waiting below."

"Then we don't go below." Kestrel spread the salvaged map on a dry crate, weighted the corners with stones, and put his finger on the gorge's southern wall. "There's a guide route. Old one — pre-Trust, grandfather's era. It climbs out of the gorge here, crosses the shoulder, and drops back to the river below the narrows' end. Two days on foot, one if the weather breaks our way. We carry what matters, cache the rest, and meet the boat on the far side."

"And the boat?"

"Stays with whoever's steadiest." He looked up. Found Nadya already shaking her head.

"No," she said. "You're not splitting us. I've seen what happens to crews that split."

"Mara's the engineer. She can't climb that route on that ankle. Someone stays."

"Then *I* stay. It's my boat."

"It's my route," he said, "and I'm not leaving you on a gravel bar with Trust riders upstream and a hole in your floor."

For a moment the firelight held them both, exhausted and scraped and unwilling to yield, and Kestrel saw the argument for what it really was — not about the boat at all, but about the new, frightening, unspoken thing between them, and how quickly it could be taken away. Around the fire, the others watched with the careful neutrality of people witnessing something they'd already understood before the two principals had.

Sena broke it. She was the youngest of the survivors and had spoken perhaps forty words since the cave, and now she looked up from the flames and said, in her small clear voice:

"The logbook survived. Mara has it dry. Doesn't that mean we should keep moving? All of us. Together."

Silence. Then Nadya laughed — tired, real, defeated — and put out her hand, and Kestrel took it, and that was the settlement: nobody split. They'd haul the boat around the worst of it by rope-line along the bank, all of them, slow and ugly and together.

Later, when the others slept, Kestrel took the middle watch and found Nadya awake beside him, mending the torn shirt she'd worn into the river. Neither spoke for a long time. The gorge thundered. The stars wheeled behind the racing clouds.

"Three weeks ago I hired you because you were cheap and desperate," she said at last, not looking up from her needle.

"I know."

"I want you to know that's not why I'd hire you now."

He smiled in the dark, where she couldn't see it, and said nothing, because some things survive being said and some don't, and he'd finally learned the difference.

It was Mara, waking near dawn, who ended the quiet — sitting bolt upright, staring downriver at the eastern sky, where a smudge of smoke rose thin and deliberate against the sunrise. Not a campfire. Too straight. Too maintained.

A signal fire. At the gorge's exit. Exactly where anyone running the rapids would come out.

"They're not chasing us anymore," Mara said. "They're waiting."
