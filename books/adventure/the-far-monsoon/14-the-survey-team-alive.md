## 14. The Survey Team Alive

The cave mouth breathed at us before we saw it.

We'd been climbing for two hours through the karst above the drowned channel, rain coming down in gray sheets that turned the limestone slick as oiled glass, when the air changed. A cold exhalation from the hillside itself, carrying the mineral smell of deep stone and — fainter, underneath, so faint I almost missed it — woodsmoke.

I held up a fist and the column behind me stopped. Mara, the engineer, had gone rigid as a plumb line. Her eyes were fixed on a seam in the cliff face ahead, half-hidden behind a curtain of hanging ferns that grew straight out of the rock like green hair.

"There," she whispered. "That's where we—"

She didn't finish. Her voice cracked on the word *we*, and I understood: she was looking at the door out of which she'd run, or been driven, or from which she'd watched others leave and not come back. Eleven days in these hills since the survey camp went silent. Eleven days of whatever had happened between four people who went in as colleagues.

"Nobody moves," I said quietly. "Nadya, watch our back trail. Mara — how many came in and out of that mouth?"

"Two." Her jaw worked. "Two came in. We were four."

"And you?"

"I got out." The words came out flat, stripped of everything. "Eleven days ago. I climbed down the waterfall side. They don't know I'm alive either."

*They don't know I'm alive.* I filed it away. Whatever waited inside that cave had spent eleven days believing its guard duty was complete — or its prison secure.

The seam was wider than it looked, maybe chest-high at the entrance, narrowing into blackness. Cold air poured out of it steady as a river current. And now that I was close, the woodsmoke was unmistakable, along with something else — tallow, rendered fat, the smell of people living hard in one place too long.

"Mara," I said. "Inside. Slowly. Call out names first. If they have anything sharp pointed at the dark, I want them pointing it at someone they know."

She looked at me for a long moment. Rain ran off her chin.

"They'll think I'm a ghost," she said.

"Then be a loud ghost."

* * *

She went in calling "Dov! Dov, it's Mara!" in a voice that started steady and then broke apart entirely somewhere around the third repetition, and the darkness answered with a sound I will hear until I die — a man's voice, cracked and disbelieving, shouting her name back, and then another voice joining it, thinner, a woman's, and then the scrape and clatter of someone dropping something metal and running.

By the time I eased myself through the entrance behind her, my lantern raised, the scene resolved out of the gloom in pieces: a chamber tall enough to stand in, walls furred with pale flowstone that caught the light like wet bone; a fire pit ringed with stones, smoke crawling up some unseen chimney crack toward the world above; two figures stumbling across the floor — a big man with a beard gone to wire, a woman so thin her collarbones stood out like tent poles — and Mara meeting them halfway, the three of them collapsing into each other, arms and shoulders and grief, making sounds that weren't quite words.

A fourth figure did not move.

He sat against the far wall, apart, in the angle of shadow beyond the fire's reach. Younger than the others — mid-thirties, neat even in ruin, hair trimmed, a government-issue field jacket buttoned to the throat despite the heat of the chamber. His hands rested open on his knees. His face wore an expression I recognized from registry offices and tribunal rooms: pleasant, composed, and absolutely unreadable.

"That's Oskar Venn," the bearded man said over Mara's shoulder, seeing my gaze. Voice hoarse. "Our team lead. He—" A cough took him. "Kestrel Ishikawa. I know your name. Everyone in the service knows your name."

"Dov Sarn," Mara supplied, wiping her face. "And Ilka Bren. Hydrologist. Dov's our geologist."

"Surveyor," I corrected, because old habits die hard, and Dov almost smiled.

"Surveyor. Sorry. Eleven days underground does things to vocabulary."

Ilka hadn't let go of Mara. She was saying something low and rapid into her hair, over and over, and I looked away from it. Some doors you don't stand in front of.

Oskar Venn still hadn't moved. He was watching me with those pleasant eyes, and when he finally spoke, his voice was mild as milk.

"Kestrel Ishikawa. The blacklist guide. They sent the blacklist guide." A small smile. "How many of you are there?"

"A boat crew. Four of us counting the survivors here." I kept my tone level. "Your liaison in the port town hired me when you went silent. I found your camp. Packed instruments. Staked tents. No tracks leading out."

"Good camp discipline," Venn said. Something in it was almost warm. Almost proud.

"Who left the camp, Mr. Venn? You or them?"

The question dropped into the chamber like a stone into a well. Dov stopped talking. Ilka lifted her head from Mara's shoulder. The fire popped.

Venn's smile didn't waver. "Does it matter? You found us. That's what matters. The Trust's riders have been combing these hills for a week." He gestured vaguely upward, toward the hidden chimney crack, toward the sky. "You should get everyone moving soon. Downstream. Before they widen their search."

Something about the phrasing snagged on me. Not *before they find you*. Before *they widen their search* — as if he knew the shape and timing of the search. As if it were a thing being conducted on a schedule he could read.

"How do you know the riders' pattern?" I asked.

"The ridgeline gives good sightlines," he said easily. "I've been watching."

"You've been watching," I repeated. "For eleven days. From inside a cave."

"Somebody has to keep watch while the others rest." He spread his hands, reasonable, wounded. "Somebody has to keep the fire small. Ration the food. It's not a glamorous job, Ishikawa, but somebody—"

"Where's the food?" I asked.

Silence.

I turned slowly, letting the lantern light sweep the chamber. Flowstone and shadow. A water drip feeding a basin worn smooth into the stone. Bedrolls — three of them, clustered near the fire. Three.

Three bedrolls. Four men and women had gone up into this cave, by Mara's count. Two came out of hiding to greet her. One sat apart, clean-jacketed, composed. And there were three bedrolls for five living people, because Mara had just come home.

But eleven days ago there would have been four bedrolls. Four people eating. And I'd seen no cache. No stores. Nothing but the fire pit and the dripping water basin and the thinness hanging off Ilka Bren's frame like a garment.

"What have you been eating?" I asked Ilka gently.

She looked at me with eyes too large in her starved face. "Whatever Dov catches at the entrance at night. Snails. Water from the drip." A pause, and something moved behind her exhaustion, slow and cold as a fish turning in deep water. "There was more food. At the start. But..."

"But?" Dov prompted.

Ilka's gaze slid, very slowly, to Oskar Venn.

"He ate first," she said. "Every day. He said the team lead eats first. He said—" Her voice trembled, and steadied. "He said it was protocol."

* * *

What happened next happened fast, and mostly in the dark, and I want to set it down honestly because honesty is all I have left of a career.

Dov came off the wall like a landslide. I don't know what eleven days of hunger and fear and watching Venn eat first had built in the man, but it detonated all at once, and he crossed the chamber in three strides with his fists rising, and I got an arm across his chest and took his momentum into the flowstone wall with a crack that rattled my teeth.

"Dov! *Dov.* Not yet. Look at his boots."

It took him a moment. Hunger makes thought viscous. But he looked, because Mara grabbed his sleeve and pointed, and the whole room looked, and there they were in the lantern light: Oskar Venn's boots, dry-stained, mud-caked, government-issue, with the distinctive cleated sole of the Survey Directorate's field pattern.

The same boot print I'd read in the mud outside the abandoned camp, arriving after the team had left. The print that had no business existing, because the whole team had supposedly gone up the karst together.

"You walked out," I said. "After they hid. You walked down to the valley and back. More than once — the gait pattern changes between visits. Heavier tread going down, lighter coming back. You were carrying something down." I crouched, keeping my eyes on Venn. "Reports. Locations. A schedule of where your own team meant to hide next."

"You can't know that," Venn said. Still pleasant. Still smiling. But the smile had gone thin as paper over a blade.

"The mud knows. Mud doesn't lie, Mr. Venn. It's the one witness nobody can bribe."

"You're a blacklist guide reading boot prints in the rain," he said, and now the milk curdled, the contempt showing through. "Half the services in two countries revoked your ticket. Do you imagine anyone will believe a word you—"

"The logbook," Mara said.

Venn went very still.

"We hid it," she said, watching him with a terrible new clarity dawning in her face. "Before we split up. You told us to hide it — you said the safest place was with you, that you'd memorized the route and could retrieve it later. And then the riders started finding us. Every hollow. Every fallback point. Within hours of us choosing it." Her voice rose. "*Within hours,* Oskar."

"I scouted well," he said. "I chose bad points. Bad luck."

"Bad luck," Ilka echoed, from the floor, in a whisper with iron in it.

Dove Sarn pulled himself upright against the wall, swaying, and looked at his team lead the way a man looks at a structure he has just realized is load-bearing and rotten through.

"The gorge crossing," Dov said slowly. "We lost Tam because the crossing was watched. You picked that crossing."

Venn stood up. It was the first time he'd moved, and the movement had nothing mild in it anymore — coiled, controlled, a man who had rehearsed this exit. He was between us and the entrance seam, though whether by design or accident I couldn't tell, and his hand had drifted, casual as a man adjusting his jacket, toward the long knife at his belt.

"Think about what you're doing," he said. "All of you. Halvard pays better than the government ever did. Better than pensions. Better than funerals." His eyes found mine. "And you, Ishikawa. Edmund Halvard knows exactly what you are. He made inquiries. He could unmake the blacklist tomorrow. Restore your license. Your name. Your grandfather's—"

"Don't," I said.

"—good name," Venn finished softly, "restored to the registers. All you have to do is lose the boat crew's memory somewhere in the river. Accidents happen on flood-stage water. Ask anyone. Ask *her*." A flick of his eyes toward Nadya's silhouette in the entrance behind me — she must have come forward when the shouting started; I'd half-forgotten she was there. "Pilot licenses are easy to revoke. Easier than reputations."

I felt Nadya go tense at my back, and I felt something else too — the strange calm that used to come over me on bad mountains, when the route goes wrong and the only thing left is the next ten meters.

Here's what I knew in that moment, the way you know the load-bearing hold under your hand: Venn was faster than any of us. Hungry as the others were, he'd eaten first for eleven days, and it showed in his legs, his balance, the way he stood. If he went for the knife, someone in this chamber was likely to die, and it wouldn't be him.

So I did the thing the blacklist never trained out of me. I gave him the truth, plainly, without theater.

"No," I said.

He blinked. "No?"

"No bargain. No deal. You're going to walk out of this cave past five people who know what you are, and every rider in this valley is going to learn your name is worth nothing to the Trust the moment you stop being useful. Edmund Halvard burns his tools, Venn. Ask the sluice gates. Ask the villages under the channel. You're not a partner. You're a maintenance cost."

For a heartbeat — one full heartbeat — something moved behind his pleasant mask. Not doubt. Something worse. Recognition. The look of a man hearing his own eulogy read accurately.

Then the knife came out and he moved.

* * *

I'd like to say I fought him clean. What actually happened was that Dov Sarn, half-starved and furious, threw the fire.

Not the whole fire — a handful of burning sticks from the pit, flung wide, and Venn flinched from the sparks, one arm up, and that flinch was the tenth of a second I needed. I hit him low and hard, driving him back into the chamber wall, and the knife scraped along my forearm opening a hot line before I pinned the wrist against the flowstone. Behind us Ilka was screaming and Mara had the lantern, and Nadya appeared out of the dark like weather, boat hook in hand — she'd carried it up the whole climb, stubborn as sin — and hooked Venn's collar and hauled, and between the three of us we put him down and kept him down, breathing hard, blood dripping off my forearm onto the pale stone where it looked obscene, bright red on wet bone.

"Get it off me," Venn snarled, all the milk gone from his voice now, just a man underneath, mean and scared.

"The knife first," Nadya said, and kicked it skittering into the dark.

We tied him with his own bootlaces and the cord from the bedrolls, not gently, and stood him against the wall, and for a long moment nobody spoke except the fire and the water dripping into its basin.

"Kill him," Dov said. It wasn't a question, and his hands were shaking. "He got Tam killed. He sold us. Kill him and be done."

"No," I said again, and my forearm burned, and I was tired all the way down to the bedrock of myself. "He walks. Because if we kill him, we're exactly what the Trust says we are — outlaws in the hills. And because he's evidence." I looked at Venn. "You heard everything in this chamber. You'll testify to all of it, eventually, in a courtroom with lawyers. Or you'll spend the rest of your life knowing Edmund Halvard's riders have orders about loose ends, and the only people in the valley who *don't* want you dead are standing right here."

"You think Halvard won't reach me?" Venn laughed, ragged. "You think anywhere in this valley—"

"I think," Nadya said, stepping forward, boat hook resting on the stone with a click, "that I know every channel, every cut, every drowned farmstead in this river system, and I know which ones a running man can cross and which ones he can't. And I think you're going to run, right now, downstream toward the estate lands, because that's where your money is. And I think the water between here and there is going to be very interesting tonight."

She smiled at him. It was not a comforting smile.

Venn looked at her. Then at me. Then at the five of us — six, counting the boat hook — and something calculated its odds and found them poor.

He ran. Laces untied, wrists raw, jacket torn, he squeezed through the entrance seam and was gone into the rain, and we heard his footsteps slipping and sliding down the karst toward the valley, toward the estate, toward the man who owned him.

"Follow him?" Dov asked hoarsely.

"With what?" I gestured at the three of them — Mara strong, but Dov and Ilka swaying on their feet, eleven days of snails and drip-water written on their bodies. "We carry what matters and we move. Can you walk?"

"Try stopping us," Ilka said, and there was the iron again, all the way through now.

* * *

We took twenty minutes to eat, because starving people make fatal mistakes and I've buried enough of them. There was a little food in the packs we'd hauled up the climb — dried fish, hard bread — and we made them eat slowly, in thirds, drinking small sips, while Mara told it all in a flat exhausted voice: the logbook, the plates, the boundary markers traced to their source, the final entry. *Halvard's man offered us money. We said no.*

"Tam said no loudest," she finished. "That's why it was Tam at the crossing."

Nobody spoke for a while. The fire had burned down to embers, and the cave breathed its cold breath around us.

"The logbook," I said. "Venn never found it?"

"It wasn't with us to find. I hid it separately. In the watchtower." She looked at me. "You found the tower."

"We found the tower."

"Then you already have it." She managed something that was almost a laugh. "Eleven days I've been alive purely to make sure that book reached someone. And it turns out I was carrying nothing at all. It was waiting for you the whole time."

"Welcome to the Sareth," Nadya said. "Nothing here waits where you expect it."

We doused the fire, and I led them out into the rain.

* * *

The descent was the worst climb I've ever done, and I include the ones I did bleeding. Dov went down the waterfall route on a rope with his eyes shut and his teeth gritted; Ilka shook so badly on the exposed slabs that Nadya clipped herself to her and climbed as her anchor, talking the whole way in a low steady stream — about the boat, about the river, about the hamlet where they mended seams and cheated at cards, painting the downstream world so vividly that Ilka's feet kept moving toward it. Watching them, I understood something about Nadya Voss that I hadn't fully understood on the water: she doesn't rescue people. She *recruits* them. She makes survival sound like a place you're being invited to.

My forearm throbbed where Venn's knife had opened it. Mara bound it without comment, competent hands, and when she finished she said, quietly, "You could have let Dov kill him."

"I know."

"Why didn't you?"

"Because my grandfather spent the last twenty years of his life being called a liar by men with clean boots," I said. "And the only thing that cleared his name was paper. Testimony. Records. I'm not going to win this valley with a body in a cave. I'm going to win it with witnesses."

Mara considered that. "Venn will warn Halvard we're coming."

"Venn already warned him every day for eleven days. What's changed is that now Halvard knows *when*." I looked down through the rain at the river, far below, swollen and brown and roaring through the gorge, running high and angry toward the lowlands. "Which means we stop sneaking. Sneaking is done. From here we race."

The boat was where we'd left it, moored in the flooded willows, riding low and taking water the way she always did — faithfully, like breathing. Getting five weakened survivors and two soaked guides aboard in flood current was forty minutes of the ugliest seamanship of Nadya's life, and she swore at the boat, the river, the Trust, the rain, and me in roughly equal measure, and somehow we all lived.

Then we were moving, downstream, the gorge walls closing around us, the engine pushing against a current that wanted to drag us backward into the dark.

Nadya stood at the wheel beside me, rain streaming off her, and nodded once at my bandaged arm. "Stitches when we moor."

"If we moor."

"When." She said it like a fact of geography. Then, quieter, eyes on the white water ahead: "He's going to open the gates, Kestrel. Venn will tell him we have the survivors, and Halvard will stop managing the flood and start weaponizing it. Everything he's been holding back—" She lifted her chin toward the roar ahead. "That's what's coming down on us."

Ahead, the gorge narrowed to its throat, and the river stood up in ridges, and the last light died behind us, and we ran into the dark at full flood with three ghosts of a vanished survey team in the hold and a traitor's warning racing ahead of us through the rain.

I checked the compass. My grandfather's compass. Its needle swung wild near the iron-rich rock, steadied, and pointed downstream — the same direction the water was going, the same direction the truth was going, whether anyone willed it or not.

"Hold on," I said, and gave Nadya the channel.
