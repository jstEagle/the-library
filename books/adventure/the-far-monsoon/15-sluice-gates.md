## 15. Sluice Gates

The cave breathed water.

Mara Venn had spent eleven years thinking in hydraulics — head pressure, flow rates, the patient arithmetic of water finding its level — and so she was perhaps the only person in that dripping limestone dark who understood, before anyone said it aloud, what the sound outside meant. Not rain on rock. Rain *through* rock. The whole massif above them drinking the monsoon and passing it down through a thousand throats, and somewhere upstream, gates that were not supposed to be open yet.

She sat with her back against the cave wall where the stone was driest, her ruined left boot propped on a flat rock, and watched Kestrel Ishikawa feed sticks to their small fire as though he'd been doing it his whole life. Maybe he had. The other two survivors — Osei and little Priya from hydrographics — were asleep in the deeper chamber, wrapped in the tarps Nadya Voss had sacrificed from her boat's stores. The boat itself lay half-sunk in the flooded channel below, holed in the gorge run, and every hour they delayed was an hour the river rose.

"You're doing the math," Ishikawa said, without looking up from the fire.

"I'm always doing the math."

"Tell me the bad version."

Mara pulled the survey logbook toward her across the wet stone. It was hers again now — she'd written most of it, after all, before the team scattered into this cave like coins dropped down a well. The pages had swollen and dried and swollen again, ink feathering at the edges of every entry, but the numbers were still legible if you knew how to read past the damage. She'd been reading numbers past damage since she was nineteen years old and an inspector told her a dam she'd signed off on was built on falsified core samples, and she'd learned then what she was about to say now: the paperwork always tells you, eventually, what the men who wrote it hoped no one would find.

"The bad version," she said, "is that we have maybe thirty-six hours."

Ishikawa's hands went still over the fire.

* * *

She made him get Osei up first. This wasn't cruelty; it was engineering discipline. A plan explained twice is a plan explained wrong once, and once was a luxury none of them had. So she waited while Ishikawa woke the others, while Priya came stumbling out of the deep chamber blinking at the firelight, while Nadya Voss wrung out her hair and said nothing but arranged herself against the opposite wall with the particular stillness of a woman who intended to miss nothing.

Then Mara opened the logbook to the last clean page and began.

"Three weeks ago," she said, "we found the original survey plates. You know that part. What you don't know — what we didn't understand until we got into the Trust's flood schedules — is why Edmund Halvard cares about a valley this much. It isn't the land. Land is cheap up here. It's the *claims*. Every legal boundary in the Sareth Valley traces back to markers set by the Halvard Trust's own surveys. If those markers are proven forged, the claims collapse. Not just the Trust's holdings. The mortgages, the leases, the water rights sold downstream — everything built on top of the forgery comes down with it."

"So he drowns the evidence," Nadya said.

"He *maintains* the evidence." Mara let the word sit there. "That's the elegance of it. There's a maintenance release scheduled for every major sluice gate in the upper valley — I've seen the filings, they're real, they're dated weeks ago. Controlled drawdowns, scouring flows, standard practice before peak monsoon. On paper it's textbook. An engineer signs off, the gates open in sequence, and a flood moves through the valley floor at exactly the volume needed to strip topsoil off the old village sites and take every remaining boundary stone down to bedrock. When the water drops, there's nothing left to measure. No markers, no foundations, no corner posts. Just a scoured channel and a filing cabinet full of legitimate-looking maintenance records."

Priya had gone very pale. "The villages under the drowned channel—"

"Won't exist anymore. Not even as ruins. Scouring flow at that volume doesn't leave foundations. It leaves gravel." Mara turned a page, though she didn't need to; the schedule lived behind her eyes now, had lived there since the night she'd copied it out of the Trust liaison's satchel with her heart hammering like a trip-hammer. "And here's the part that matters. The releases are timed. Staggered gate by gate, upstream to downstream, each one feeding the next. It's beautiful work, honestly. Whoever designed it understood the basin better than our office does. The final gate — the master control at the upper gatehouse — opens last, and when it does, everything upstream of it comes through at once. Peak monsoon plus controlled release. The valley won't flood. Floods recede. The valley will be *regraded*. There's a difference, and the difference is measured in meters of removed earth."

Silence, except for the cave breathing its slow wet breath around them.

Osei spoke first, his voice rough from three weeks of cold and fear. "You're saying it's already moving."

"Some of it. The lower gates may already be open — that would explain the river coming up ahead of forecast, the surge you two rode down through the gorge." She nodded at Ishikawa and Voss. "But the master release can't be triggered remotely. That's not sentiment, that's physics — a gate that size needs manual confirmation at the gatehouse itself, two operators, physical keys. Halvard will be there, or his people will. And the gatehouse sits above the reservoir, a day's hard climb from here, up routes that—" she glanced at Ishikawa, "—that I'm told you know how to read."

Ishikawa was looking into the fire. "Thirty-six hours."

"Twenty-nine, if the rain holds at this intensity. I'm giving you the generous number because I want you to move fast, not because I believe in it."

* * *

They argued about it the way small groups argue when the stakes have outgrown them — in fragments, in interruptions, everyone talking over the fire instead of each other.

Voss wanted the river. "My boat's holed but she'll float patched, and the gorge run took us downstream in hours, not days. We put in tonight, we reach the lower settlements before the release, we warn them—"

"And then what?" Mara asked. "You warn them of what? 'Move uphill'? Half those families have nowhere uphill that isn't Trust land. And even if they clear out — the valley still dies. You save the people and lose the proof, and in ten years the Trust owns everything anyway, legally, cleanly, with the only contrary evidence buried under four meters of new gravel." She shook her head. "I've spent my career watching men lose arguments to paperwork. I'm not watching it happen to a whole valley."

"Then we climb," Priya said simply. She was the youngest of them, twenty-three maybe, and she'd held the team's maps through three weeks of hiding, and she said it the way she did everything, without decoration. "We climb to the gatehouse and we close the gate."

"It's not a door," Mara said gently. "It's a hundred-ton radial gate with a manual override designed to be operated by trained staff with the right keys. Closing it mid-release without the hydraulic system—" She stopped. Made herself finish. "Without the hydraulics, it means hand-cranking the emergency winch. Which takes six people working in shifts, and which the designers assumed would never be attempted during active discharge."

"How long?" Ishikawa asked.

"At full crew? Fourteen hours against the mechanism. Assuming the winch housing hasn't corroded. Assuming the access gallery isn't flooded. Assuming nobody's shooting at you while you do it."

"And your honest estimate?"

Mara met his eyes across the fire. He had the kind of face that didn't flinch from numbers, and she found, to her surprise, that she liked him for it. Most men wanted hope served first. This one wanted the load calculated before he agreed to carry it.

"My honest estimate is that four of us can't do it in time. Six might. Seven, if one of them knows mountain ground in weather like this, because the climb itself will kill us before the gate gets the chance." She looked at him steadily. "Which is why you're here, Mr. Ishikawa. The Trust blacklisted you because you were the best route-finder in two countries. I read your file. All of it. Including the parts that weren't in the file."

Something moved behind his eyes — old damage, briefly surfaced — and then he nodded, once, and looked at the fire again, and said, "Show me the approach."

* * *

She spread the schedule sheets on the flattest stone she could find, weighting the corners with pebbles, and drew the gatehouse approach from memory: the service road drowned years ago, the switchback trail washed out in the slide of two winters back, the ridge line above the intake canal where the rock was sound but exposed. Voss leaned over her shoulder, and Priya, and Osei, and Ishikawa stood apart with his arms crossed, studying the drawing the way she imagined he studied ground — not the lines themselves but what the lines were hiding.

"There," he said finally, and touched a point west of the ridge line, where she'd drawn nothing at all. "What's here?"

"Nothing. Cliff band. Maybe forty meters of it."

"Forty meters of cliff is a staircase if you know where the breaks are. Limestone like this sheds in slabs — there'll be a gully system, probably vegetated, probably invisible from below. If it goes all the way through the band, it cuts the approach to eight hours instead of fourteen." He looked up. "Has anyone climbed it?"

"No one's climbed it since the Trust closed the valley."

"Good," Ishikawa said, and something in his voice made Voss laugh — a short, sharp bark of a laugh, surprised out of her.

"You enjoy this," she accused him.

"I enjoy being useful," he said. "It's been a while."

Mara watched the two of them look at each other across the firelight and thought, with the detached clarity of exhaustion, that she was watching something being built — some structure with no name yet, made of trust and friction and shared weight, going up between these strangers plank by plank the way she'd once watched her father build a barn, without plans, without hurry, each piece tested before the next was laid. She had lost a team to this valley. She had not expected to find the beginnings of another one in a cave that smelled of bat guano and river rot.

"We'll need rope," Osei said, practical as ever. "Real rope. Anchors. The boat's rigging?"

"Take it all," Voss said. "She's sunk anyway. Might as well die useful."

"And lights," Priya added. "The access gallery inside the dam — if we have to work in there—"

"One lamp," Mara said. "Maybe two. We'll work in shifts, by feel if we have to." She paused, and then said the thing she had been holding back, because holding it back any longer felt like a lie of omission, and she had promised herself in the dark weeks of hiding that she would never again manage information the way the Trust managed it. "There's one more thing. About the gatehouse."

Ishikawa looked up.

"If Halvard is there himself — and he will be, because a man who builds a machine this elegant wants to throw the final switch with his own hands — then closing that gate isn't just an engineering problem. It's a confrontation. And I need you all to understand what we're walking into. He won't see villains. He'll see trespassers interfering with lawful maintenance. He has filings. He has permits. He has, God help us, signatures. Everything we carry — the logbook, my copies of the plates' documentation — none of it is admissible until it reaches the capital, and it doesn't reach the capital unless we stop that gate and live long enough to carry it out." She pressed her palms flat against the cold stone. "We are not rescuers anymore. We're evidence. All of us. And evidence has to survive to be worth anything."

The fire had burned low. Outside, the rain kept up its conversation with the mountain, patient, immense, indifferent to schedules and filings and the small ambitions of the living.

Ishikawa stood. He moved like a man whose body had finally been given permission to do the thing it was made for, and Mara saw the blacklist fall away from him — not erased, never erased, but irrelevant, the way a chain is irrelevant to a man who has remembered he can climb.

"Then we go now," he said. "In the dark, in the rain, while every hour is still ours. Voss — rigging. Osei — food and the lamp oil. Priya, you're on the logbook; it stays dry or it stays home, and it doesn't stay home. Mara—" he glanced at her, and for the first time since she'd met him there was something almost like warmth in it, "—you're my second. You know the dam. I know the mountain. Between us we might actually get seven people up a cliff nobody's climbed."

"Six," Mara said, standing, testing her ruined boot against the stone and finding it would hold. "I'm counting me."

Above them, far off through the rain and the dark, there came a sound she felt before she heard it — a deep, subterranean groan rolling down the valley's bones, metal against stone, vast and deliberate.

Everyone froze. Even the fire seemed to lean away from it.

"That's a gate," Mara said quietly. "Another one's opening."

Ishikawa shouldered the pack. "Then we've got less time than you thought. Move."

* * *

They climbed out of the cave mouth into the teeth of the monsoon, six small figures on a mountainside that had swallowed better-prepared parties than theirs, and behind them, far below in the drowned channel, the river rose in the dark to meet them — rising, rising, keeping its own count of the hours they had left.
