## 16. The Climb

The rain came down like a verdict.

Kestrel stood at the base of the escarpment and let it hit him — a cold, ceaseless drumming on his shoulders, on the hood of oilskin that had stopped being waterproof two days ago, on the slate slopes above where the water ran in silver threads that would become torrents by midmorning. Behind him, the others waited. He could feel them waiting, the way you feel a rope go taut.

Mara crouched over her map case, shielding it with her body, her engineer's hands tracing the contour lines she'd drawn from memory. The other two survivors — Ovid, the quiet one with the broken wrist splinted to a paddle shaft, and Sef, who hadn't slept since the cave — stood with Nadya at the treeline. The boat was hidden below in the willow scrub, lashed down against the rising river. Everything they had left in the world was either on their backs or in that hull.

"One route," Kestrel said. "There's always one route."

He said it more for himself than for them. He turned to face the mountain.

The escarpment rose six hundred meters of wet black rock, seamed and buttressed, its upper ramparts lost in cloud. Somewhere up there was the upper gatehouse — the manual override, the one thing standing between Edmund Halvard's schedule and the drowning of everything below. Mara's calculations gave them until tomorrow night. The climb itself, in good weather, with a fresh team: half a day. In this weather, with these bodies, carrying what they carried—

"Half a day," he said aloud, because saying it made it a number instead of a fear. "Maybe eight hours if the rain holds off. It won't hold off. So we move like the rain isn't there."

"It's there," Sef said flatly.

"Then we move like it's part of us." Kestrel pulled his hood back and let the water run through his hair, down his neck, into his collar. Let them see him do it. Fear is a thing you can hand to people or take away from them, and most of the handing is done by example. "The mountain doesn't care about the rain either. We're all soaked together, us and the rock. That makes us fair."

Nadya snorted. "That's not wisdom, that's misery with rhetoric."

"Most wisdom is." He looked at her, and something passed between them that had no words in it — the gorge was still in both their bodies, the strainer, the white roar, her hand and his hand closing on each other out of the flood. She'd stopped arguing with his routes after that. Not because he'd been right. Because they'd learned what each other's competence looked like from the inside.

He turned to the wall of the mountain and began to read it.

* * *

This was the thing they'd taken his license for. Not negligence, not the death on the icefield — that had been the pretext, the lever, the convenient tragedy. What they'd actually punished him for was being better at reading country than anyone they'd ever tested, and for being willing to say so. A man who can read terrain becomes a threat to every map ever drawn by someone with money at stake. His grandfather had learned that lesson with survey plates. Kestrel had learned it with a blacklist stamped by two governments.

And now here he was, doing the forbidden thing, being the dangerous thing, and it felt like putting on a coat that still fit.

The route wasn't a trail. Trails were for people who needed to be told where to go. The route was a sentence written in the mountain's own grammar, and Kestrel read it aloud as he climbed, calling back over his shoulder in the clipped cadence of a guide who has done this ten thousand times:

"Slab — friction, trust your feet, don't look for holds. Chimney on the left past the dead pine — body it, don't arm it. Above the chimney the slope eases but the drainage crosses it — we cross the drainage *fast*, all together, before the sun warms the snowmelt. There is no snowmelt today, but there's rain, which is worse. Nobody stops in the drainage. Nobody."

They climbed.

Mara went second, because she was the strongest and because she'd spent three years doing fieldwork in ranges worse than this; she moved like someone who understood load paths, placing her feet where the rock wanted weight. Ovid came third with his splinted arm strapped across his chest, and Kestrel had spent an hour the night before re-rigging that splint with boat cordage so the man could climb one-armed without it swinging — Ovid had watched him do it with an expression Kestrel chose not to name. Sef went fourth, close behind Mara, needing proximity to courage. Nadya took the rear, and she climbed the way she piloted — economically, contemptuously, as if the mountain were a slow barge cutting her off in a channel.

The first hour was trees and wet grass and the long diagonal traverse beneath the first cliff band, and it was almost easy, and Kestrel distrusted it completely. Easy ground kills more climbers than hard ground, because easy ground lets you stop paying attention. He kept them moving, kept them spaced, kept calling the route in that flat guide's voice that sounds bored and is anything but.

At the dead pine they roped.

It wasn't strictly necessary yet. But the slab above was polished by centuries of runoff, and the rain had turned it to glass, and Kestrel had watched enough good people die on glass to know that the rope is less about physics than about permission — permission to commit, because someone else is holding the other end. He set the anchor himself, testing the pine's roots with his whole body weight, then twice more, then a fourth time because the rain argued with him.

"Nadya. You're my anchor reader. If I go stupid, you say so."

"You go stupid hourly," she said, clipping in. "But I'll say when it's new stupid."

* * *

The chimney ate forty minutes and gave them nothing but bruised hips and a view straight down through falling water. Halfway up, wedged in the dark with the rain funneling past his boots, Kestrel heard Sef below him start to breathe wrong — the fast shallow panting of a person whose body has decided the situation is fatal regardless of facts.

"Sef." He didn't shout. Shouting in a chimney is cruelty. He pitched his voice low and level, the way you talk to a spooked animal or a panicking climber, which are the same problem. "Look at your feet. Just your feet. Tell me what color the rock is."

"Black," Sef gasped.

"Black rock. Good. Now tell me what your right foot is standing on."

"A—a ledge. Small."

"That ledge held me and it held Mara and it'll hold you. Press into it. Feel it take you?" A pause. The breathing slowed by a fraction. "Now move the left foot. Only the left foot. The mountain does the rest."

Foot by foot, word by word, he talked Sef up the chimney, and when the man finally hauled out onto the ledge above, shaking, Kestrel gripped the back of his collar and held him there a moment, forehead nearly touching his, rain streaming between their faces.

"There," Kestrel said. "You're still here. Remember this. The next wall, you'll be the one talking somebody up it."

Sef stared at him. Something shifted in the man's face — not gratitude exactly, something more structural, like a keel being laid. "Why do you know how to do that?"

"Because someone did it for me once, and I've been paying it forward ever since, badly, in the wrong currency." He released the collar. "Move."

* * *

Above the chimney the mountain changed its mind about them.

The drainage crossing was exactly as bad as he'd promised — a steep gully running with brown water deep enough to push against a man's shins, the bed of it loose cobbles that rolled and clattered downstream with every step, the whole chute a throat that would become a waterfall's tongue if the rain intensified. Kestrel stood at its lip and read it for a full minute while the others sheltered under the overhang behind him.

Not a waterfall yet. The water was heavy but organized, moving in channels rather than sheets. The cobbles were rolling but not avalanching. And the far side — he could see it through the gray curtain — the far side was the ramp he remembered from his grandfather's field notes, the long grassy shoulder the old man had called *the stairway*, marked in that precise antique hand with a note Kestrel had memorized as a boy: *Good going in dry weather. Do not attempt in flood.*

His grandfather had never met Edmund Halvard.

"We cross in pairs," Kestrel called back. "Rope stays on. Mara first with me, then Nadya brings Sef and Ovid. Count your steps — thirty-two steps to the far bank, no more, no less. If you fall, don't fight the water, roll with it and grab the left wall — there are root pockets on the left. Nobody fights the water. The water always wins the fight. We just decline the invitation."

Mara crossed beside him, hip-deep at the worst of it, her jaw set, her eyes never leaving the far bank. Thirty-two steps. He counted them aloud and she matched him stride for stride, and when they hauled out on the grass of the ramp she turned and threw the rope end back with a snap, and for one second, through the rain, she grinned at him — fierce, exhausted, alive — and Kestrel felt something in his chest unlock that had been locked since the registry office threw him into the street.

They got the others across. Ovid crossed one-handed with Nadya hauling on his rope harness, cursing the river in three languages, and when he came out on the far bank he sat down hard in the grass and laughed — actually laughed, a cracked, disbelieving sound — and Nadya stood over him dripping and said, "Yes, yes, you're magnificent, get up, we're not done," and Kestrel heard his own voice in hers and thought: *this is what a crew sounds like. This is what I've been walking alone outside of for eleven years.*

* * *

The stairway ramp gained them four hundred meters through the afternoon, and the rain never let up, and neither did Kestrel.

He counted heads every twenty minutes. He forced water and dried fruit on them at intervals whether they wanted it or not. When Ovid's splint chafed raw, Kestrel repadded it with moss and strips of his own spare shirt without breaking stride. When Sef stumbled on the scree, Kestrel caught his pack strap and turned the stumble into a step without either of them remarking on it. When Mara began to flag — the engineer's endurance finally meeting its ledger — Kestrel walked beside her and made her narrate the gatehouse mechanisms she knew, because a mind busy explaining sluice gates cannot simultaneously be busy despairing, and by the time she'd finished describing the drum-and-cable assembly she'd forgotten to be tired for a quarter hour.

This was the craft. Not the rope work, not the route-finding. The craft was people. A guide is a shepherd of human fragility, and the wilderness tests it constantly, and Kestrel Ishikawa had been told for eleven years that he was unfit to practice it.

By late afternoon they reached the shelf below the final headwall, and Kestrel called the halt because he had to — because bodies have limits that pride doesn't get to vote on. They crowded into the lee of a leaning flake of granite, and Nadya got the small stove hissing under a rock overhang, and they drank scalding sweet tea from a shared cup while the cloud below them filled the entire valley like milk poured into a bowl.

From here they could see none of the valley. That was mercy. They all knew what was down there — villages, hamlets, the fishing families who'd mended the boat, the drowned channel with its buried boundary stones — all of it waiting under tomorrow night's release like a debt waiting for collection.

"The gatehouse," Mara said, staring up at the headwall. The last pitch rose sheer above them, two hundred meters of stepped rock veined with water. "Is there a route?"

"There's always one route." Kestrel studied the wall, letting his eyes do the old work — reading the seams, the ramps, the places where the rock leaned back kindly and the places where it offered nothing but ambition. And there, threading up through the middle of the face like a signature: a crack system, a series of ledges, a vegetated ramp, a final corner. His grandfather's notes had called it *the ladder*. Of course they had. The old man had climbed this face sixty years ago with hemp rope and hobnails and a compass in his pocket, mapping the very watershed Edmund Halvard meant to weaponize.

Kestrel touched the compass through his shirt. It was warm, or he imagined it was.

"Yes," he said. "There's a route. My family drew it before the Trust existed."

* * *

They climbed the headwall at first light, because Kestrel refused to climb it wet-dark and refused to wait longer than he had to. The rain had thinned to a fine driven mist, which was worse in some ways — it hid the holds, greased the cracks, and turned the world into a gray room with no floor.

He led the whole pitch without a single rest stance, placing protection from a rack that was mostly imagination and boat hardware, and the others followed like a chain being drawn up out of the sea. At the crux corner — a widening crack that forced him to bridge his body across a void slicked with running water — he hung there for three heartbeats with nothing solid beneath either boot and the valley's breath rising cold around him, and he thought, with strange calm: *if I fall now, they fall after me, and the valley drowns, and it will be my route that failed them.*

So he didn't fail. He bridged, and stemmed, and pressed his palms into wet granite and asked it for one more favor, and the granite, which owes nobody anything, gave it anyway. He mantled onto the summit ledge and lay there a moment on his back in the streaming grass, laughing silently at the sky.

Then he brought them up, one by one, talking each of them over the crux the way he'd talked Sef up the chimney. Nadya came over the lip last, rolled onto the ledge beside him, and lay looking at the ceiling of cloud.

"You enjoyed that," she accused.

"No," Kestrel said. "I belonged to it. Different thing."

She turned her head and looked at him, rain beading in her lashes, and whatever she saw in his face made her nod slowly, once, as if confirming a figure in a ledger she'd been keeping since the port town.

Ahead of them, built into the saddle of the ridge with the plainness of all truly powerful things, stood the upper gatehouse — stone walls, iron roof, the great sluice mechanism visible even from here as a dark geometry against the sky. Smoke rose thin and straight from its chimney.

Somebody was home.

Kestrel got to his knees, then his feet, and checked his crew with a glance — Mara flexing her hands, Ovid grimly resplendent with his splinted arm, Sef steady now, steadier than yesterday, Nadya already counting windows.

"Listen," he said quietly. "Smoke means a stove, and a stove means men, and men mean riders, and riders mean Edmund knows exactly where we are and has known since the hamlet. This was never a race to beat the schedule. It's a negotiation, and he's arranged the venue." He rolled his shoulders, felt the climb living in every muscle, and found — to his own surprise — that he was not afraid. Eleven years of exile had finally burned off, and underneath was just the work, and the work was good.

"So we walk in the front," he said. "Wet, tired, and honest. And when he offers me my name back—"

"He will," Nadya said.

"—we'll see what it costs." He started down toward the gatehouse, and his crew fell in behind him, five sets of boots finding the same path, one unit, one rope, one purpose, and the rain came down on all of them equally, like the mountain's own indifferent blessing.

The gatehouse door opened before they reached it.
