## 18. The Release

*Edmund*

The gatehouse smelled of wet iron and lamp oil, and Edmund Halvard stood before the release wheel as though it were an altar.

He had dressed for it. That was the absurdity he would admit to no one — that he had put on his good coat, the grey one with the Halvard clasp at the collar, because some part of him understood this was a ceremony. His father had opened sluices too, though never by hand; his father had done it through engineers and schedules and the soft vocabulary of maintenance. Edmund had learned at his father's knee that power was most elegant when it left no fingerprints.

His hands were on the wheel now. They were leaving plenty of prints.

Below, through the rain-latticed window, the valley lay green and drowned-lighted under the storm, the river swollen to a brown rope twisting between the terraces. Somewhere down there were boundary stones his grandfather's engineers had set in concrete meant to last two hundred years. Somewhere down there was Kestrel Ishikawa, who had looked at him across the gatehouse floor an hour ago with eyes like his own grandfather's photographs — steady, unimpressed, unbought — and refused everything.

*Restoration of your license,* Edmund had offered. *Your name cleared. A seat on the Trust council, if you want it.*

*Ishikawas don't want seats at your table,* the guide had said. *We drew the table.*

And then he had turned away and begun climbing toward the override housing, and Edmund had understood that the moment for elegance was over.

He turned the wheel.

It resisted — old grease gone to glue, decades of careful disuse — and then gave all at once, so suddenly that he staggered, and somewhere beneath his feet the mountain exhaled. He felt it in his boots first: a shudder through the bedrock, deep and tectonic, the sound of gates older than himself swinging wide. Through the window the upper channel began to boil. White water stood up out of the brown like something being born.

"Sir—" His steward, Pellam, gray-faced in the doorway, rain streaming off his hat. "The volume — the forecast has it peaking tonight, sir, if you wait until—"

"I'm not waiting." Edmund watched the water rise in the spill race. His voice sounded very calm to him, very far away. "Waiting is how my father lost the survey plates."

"Sir, the manual override—"

"The override will hold." He said it the way he said everything, precisely, coldly, and heard even as he spoke it the small crack beneath the words where doubt lived. He had stood in the archive three nights ago with the original plates in his gloved hands. He had measured them against the Trust copies. He had confirmed, alone, in silence, what Ishikawa's team had died — nearly died — to prove.

He burned nothing. He told no one. And now he flooded the proof instead.

The gatehouse groaned around them. Water hammered the lower windows. Pellam was still talking — about the structural tolerances, about the surveyors' sworn testimony already moving downriver, about *sir, there may be another way* — and Edmund found he could not look at him. There was always another way. That was what decent men believed, right up until the water took the choice from their hands.

"Get to the horses," Edmund said.

* * *

*Kestrel*

Kestrel felt the release before anyone told him — a tremor through the cliff they were climbing, a change in the rain's sound, the whole gorge taking a breath.

"Down!" he roared, and the team dropped as one against the rock face, six bodies pressed flat while the first tongue of the surge went past below them, a brown wall taller than the boat, carrying whole trees end over end like matchsticks.

Mara was shouting over the roar, her engineer's voice pitched to cut through anything: "That's the main race! He's dumped the full head — Kestrel, do you hear me? The override won't hold against peak volume, it'll shear — but the old spillway!"

"Where?"

She grabbed his collar and hauled her mouth to his ear. Her breath was ragged. "Ninety years ago they built a secondary channel for exactly this — flood relief into the western ravine. It was sealed when the Trust consolidated the dams. The plug is stone and timber, it's rotten, but someone has to break it from the *inside*, and the access gallery floods first—"

"How long?"

"At this flow? Minutes! Less!"

Kestrel looked at his team. Rain needled sideways. Below them the valley was already beginning to drink the flood — he could see the river climbing its banks in the flashes of lightning, swallowing the low terraces, the fishing stages, everything the displaced families had rebuilt. Everything the logbook had named. Everything his grandfather had mapped in a hand Kestrel knew better than his own.

There was no decision. There was only the next move, and the one after that.

"Nadya takes the survivors down the eastern chute, high water line, she knows it better than any chart. Mara, you're with me — you're the only one who can read the gallery. Everyone else: down to the boats, get every soul above the ravine line, knock on doors, drag them if you have to." He met each of their eyes in turn. "Go."

They went. In the worst hour of his life, watching them go — Mara's grim nod, young Ovid's white face setting hard, the surveyor Tam half-carrying the injured one down the switchbacks — Kestrel felt the strangest thing underneath the fear. Not peace. Nothing so kind. But belonging, sudden and total, the way a keystone belongs in an arch.

Then he turned and climbed into the throat of the flood.

* * *

*Nadya*

The eastern chute was suicide in daylight and Nadya Vosse ran it anyway, because the alternative was worse.

The *Sparrowhawk* took the standing waves bow-first, her patched seams singing, the rescued surveyors bailing with anything that held water while Nadya stood at the tiller reading the flood the way other people read scripture. The Sareth at flood stage was not a river anymore. It was a herd. It came down the gorge in surging ranks, and her job was not to fight it — nothing fought it — but to be, for a few crucial seconds at a time, part of the herd.

"Hard left!" she screamed, and the boat swung, and a house-sized eddy tried to eat them and spat them back. Someone behind her was praying. Someone else was laughing, high and wild, and Nadya realized distantly it was her.

The hamlets appeared through the rain — lights in windows, figures on the banks staring up at the brown sky of water coming down the valley. She grounded the *Sparrowhawk* on the first gravel bar and didn't bother anchoring; the crew leapt into thigh-deep torrent and started hauling people uphill. An old man fighting her, insisting on his nets. A woman with twins strapped to her chest, silent, running. A boy who wouldn't let go of a dog. Nadya lifted the dog.

Twice more she ran the chute. By the third run her arms shook and the boat sat so low the river came over the gunwales in sheets, and the eastern ravine was filling, and she knew — the way pilots know things they cannot prove — that the fourth run would be the one the river won.

She made the fourth run anyway, because Kestrel and Mara were still up there, and because there were lights still burning in the lowest terrace of the far hamlet, and because she had spent ten years running cargo for men who taught her exactly what happened to people who stopped going back for the ones downstream.

She was loading the last family — the nets man finally aboard, clutching his nets like a drowning man's plank — when the override failed.

She heard it before she saw it. A crack like the world's spine breaking, rolling down the gorge, and then the sound changed. All of it. The whole roaring world dropped into a deeper register, a subterranean boom she felt in her sternum, and the river *stood*. For one impossible second the entire surface of the flood rose, a swell traveling downstream faster than anything alive should move, trees riding its crest like riders.

"The spillway," Nadya whispered. "Kestrel."

Then she put the tiller over and drove the *Sparrowhawk* into the wave.

* * *

*Kestrel*

The access gallery was a black throat breathing water.

They went in with lanterns and hand spikes, Mara calling the turns from memory of drawings ninety years dead — left at the junction, down the iron ladder, mind the gap where the floor plate had rusted through. The water was at their knees, then their thighs, cold enough to stop thought, and above them they could hear the main race hammering the gallery roof like a fist on a door.

"Here!" Mara's light swung onto a wall of mortared stone, and set into it, a timber plug the size of a wagon door. "The spillway's behind it — break the seal and the pressure does the rest, but you have to get the whole plug clear or it'll wedge—"

Kestrel didn't answer. He set his shoulder against the timber and pushed.

Nothing. The wood was swollen solid, forty years of seep gluing it home. He braced and drove again, boots slipping on wet stone, lungs burning, and felt something give — a crack, a shift, a thread of white water spitting through the seam and hitting the far wall like a rifle shot.

"Mara — pry bar — *now*—"

They worked together without speaking, spike ends jammed into the seam, hauling in rhythm while the water climbed their waists. Once, twice, three times. On the third pull the plug groaned, tilted — and the mountain took it. The timber tore free and vanished into the dark, and the old spillway opened with a sound Kestrel would hear for the rest of his life: not an explosion, not a roar, but a vast inhale, the flood finding the channel cut for it by dead engineers, the pressure of the whole storm funneling west into the empty ravine.

Which meant the gallery was draining. Which meant the water around their legs was now moving *out*, fast, dragging at them like hands.

"Swim with it!" Kestrel caught Mara's arm as the current snatched her feet from under her. "Don't fight it — ride it to the shaft—"

They rode the drain like flotsam, slammed along the flooded corridor, and shot out into the shaft chamber just as the gallery behind them collapsed — a thunderclap of stone, the roof giving way under the scouring flood, the way they'd come in ceasing to exist. The water in the chamber dropped away beneath them, and Kestrel found himself clinging to the iron ladder rungs with Mara gasping beside him, both of them staring down at the receding torrent, listening to the gatehouse above them begin, slowly, structurally, to die.

It did not fall all at once. It shrugged, settled, shed its roof tiles into the race like a man shedding a coat — and then the undermined foundation let go entirely, and ninety years of Halvard masonry slid into the flood and was gone, and the western ravine swallowed the surge in a white plume visible from the far shore.

Mara laughed. It was half sob. "Maintenance," she said. "On paper."

Kestrel put his forehead against the cold iron and let himself shake.

* * *

*Nadya*

The wave hit them broadside half a mile above the confluence.

Nadya had time to scream *down — flat — hold!* and then the world was brown and total, the boat rolled, the river had her, and she was tumbling in a churning dark lit only by lightning strobing through water. She had been swept before — everyone on this river had been swept — and her body did what her body knew: ball up, protect the skull, count, wait for the tumble to slow, kick for the surface.

She surfaced inside an eddy behind a half-submerged terrace wall, gasping, and hauled herself onto the stones. The *Sparrowhawk* was fifty yards downstream, upside down, wedged against a strainer of flood-snapped alders — and clinging to her hull were three heads, four, five, the nets man, the twins' mother with one twin shrieking, the boy, the dog paddling in circles and barking at the sky.

Counting. Always counting. Nadya counted and came up two short.

She went back into the water.

She found the old man snagged in a fence line, blue-lipped, still gripping his nets, and got an arm across his chest and swam the eddy lines with a strength she did not know she had left, kicking off fences, off drowned carts, off the top of a submerged chimney, until her boots found gravel and hands pulled them both up. The second missing one — the injured surveyor, Tam — was already out. Already breathing, coughing river water onto the bank, surrounded by the others.

And standing over him, soaked to the skin, bleeding from a gash over one eyebrow, mud to the hips, was Kestrel Ishikawa.

"You," Nadya said. Her voice came out in pieces. "You're supposed to be *up* the mountain."

"Mountain's finished with us." He dropped to his knees beside her in the shallows. Behind him, down-valley, the flood was visibly dying — the western spillway drinking the worst of it, the main channel dropping inch by inch, the brown rage settling toward ordinary brown. The lower terraces stood above the waterline. Beaten, flooded, half-drowned — but standing. "Is everyone—"

"Everyone." She counted them again anyway, aloud, pointing at each one, because after a night like this you trusted numbers more than eyes. Then she looked at him properly — the blood, the shaking hands he was trying to hide by closing them into fists, the look on his face of a man standing in the ruins of the thing he'd spent his whole life paying for. "Kestrel. The gatehouse?"

"Gone." He said it steadily. "Edmund was inside when I went up the ladder. I don't know if he got out."

Lightning walked the ridgeline. Downstream, in the graying edge before dawn, the river ran on — lower now, calmer, carrying its wreckage east toward the capital, where in a few days' time the water would deliver more than debris.

Because tangled in the driftwood, snagged on the drowned fence lines, scattered along miles of shoreline, the flood had done something no testimony could do. It had scoured the old channel. It had torn the silt off ninety years of burial.

All along the exposed flats, pale in the lightning-flash, the foundations of the drowned villages were showing. And standing in rows among them, washed clean, unmistakable, the old boundary stones — each one carved with the mark Kestrel had known since childhood, the mark on his grandfather's compass: the surveyor's cross of the Ishikawa plates.

Nadya watched Kestrel see them. Watched him go very still, the way men go still at gravesides.

"They're going to say we planned it," she said quietly.

"No." He wiped the blood out of his eye and looked east, where the sky was turning the color of pewter and the far monsoon clouds were massing over the horizon, unhurried, immense. "They're going to say the water told the truth. And for once—" he almost smiled, "—they'll be right."

Then the shouting started upstream, and they both turned, and saw figures on the far bank waving, and heard through the thinning rain a voice crying that there was a man alive in the wreck of the gatehouse — barely alive — and that he was asking, over and over, for Kestrel Ishikawa.
