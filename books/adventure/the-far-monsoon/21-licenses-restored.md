## 21. Licenses Restored

The letter came on paper so thick it felt like money.

Nadya turned it over twice before she opened it, standing in the doorway of the harbormaster's office in the capital with river mud still dried in the creases of her knuckles. The envelope was cream-colored, sealed with wax, stamped with the crest of the Central Guide Registry — the same institution that had struck her name from its rolls six years ago with a form letter and a refund of her annual dues. Prorated, no less. They had refunded her eleven coppers.

She broke the seal with her thumbnail and read standing up, because if she sat down she might do something foolish like cry.

*The Registry hereby formally restores the license of Nadya Voss, pilot, first class, with all privileges appertaining thereto. The Board regrets the circumstances of your original suspension and acknowledges that the evidence then relied upon has been shown to be compromised by parties now under investigation.*

*The Board further invites you to consider a permanent posting at our Harborside station: senior examiner, river certification division. Salary attached. Housing provided.*

Senior examiner. River certification. A desk with a window overlooking clean water, a chair that didn't rock, a roof that didn't leak. She read the salary figure twice and felt the number land somewhere under her ribs like ballast — heavy, steadying, and not entirely welcome.

Behind her, the harbormaster cleared his throat. He was an old man with a face like driftwood and he had known her since she was sixteen and stealing rides on coal barges.

"Well?" he said. "They don't write those letters for just anybody."

"They wrote me a different one once," Nadya said. "Thinner paper."

"Times change." He nodded at the letter. "You'd be a fool to refuse."

Nadya folded the letter along its expensive crease and put it in her coat pocket, where it lay against her hip like a stone she hadn't decided whether to throw or keep.

"I've been called worse than a fool," she said, "by better people," and she walked out into the rain.

* * *

Kestrel's letter had come three days earlier, delivered by a clerk who clearly expected to be invited in, offered tea, congratulated. He had found Kestrel instead on the dock beside the *Vespera*, knee-deep in the bilge with a caulking iron in his fist, arguing with the boat about a seam below the waterline that had been leaking since before the gorge and intended, apparently, to go on leaking out of principle.

The clerk had stood on the dock in his good shoes while the river slapped the pilings and the rain came down in that fine, patient capital drizzle that could soak a man through in twenty minutes without ever once committing to a proper storm. He had held out the envelope. Kestrel had taken it, read it, and handed it back.

"Tell them thank you," he'd said. "And tell them I already have work."

"You haven't even read the terms—"

"I read the part where they offered me my license back." Kestrel had gone back to the seam. "Six years ago they took it. Last month they were still calling me a liar in their bulletins. Now there's a flood in the valley, a dead empire, and suddenly my name is clean enough to print." He drove the caulking home with a short, vicious tap. "I know how rivers work, friend. Water rises fastest right before it finds its level. So does apology."

The clerk had retreated. The story reached Nadya by evening, the way all stories reached her by evening, through the dockside telegraph of gossips and tea sellers and one very well-informed oyster woman.

She found him still working when she came down at dusk, the lantern hung off the boom, the *Vespera*'s hull open to the sky like a patient on a table. She sat on the gunwale with her boots dangling and watched him work for a while before she said anything.

"You refused them."

"I refused them." Tap. Tap. "You?"

"I haven't answered yet."

He looked up at that, and in the lantern light his face was thinner than it had been two months ago, scarred new across one brow where a strainer limb had caught him, but the eyes were the same — those flat grey eyes that read water the way priests read scripture.

"Nadya Voss," he said, "turning down a dry office."

"It's not the office." She pulled her knees up. "It's what the office is *for*. Senior examiner. I'd spend my days deciding which kids get licenses and which ones get the thin paper. And I'd be good at it. That's the problem. I'd be fair, and careful, and I'd hand out the same kind of mercy nobody handed me, and in ten years I'd have a pension and a portrait and nothing I ever touched would smell like a river."

Kestrel set down the iron. "What did they offer you?"

"Harborside station. Certification division." She laughed, short and unamused. "I'd be the woman who failed me, if I'd stayed honest the first time around."

"And if you'd stayed honest," Kestrel said quietly, "you'd have starved honest, and the Sareth channels would have drowned another pilot who knew them, and I'd have gone up that valley alone and died in the gorge. Whatever you ran, and whoever you ran it for — it bought the only map of those waters that mattered." He picked the iron back up. "I'm not going to thank you for smuggling, Nadya. But I'm not going to pretend the registry's ledger is the only one that counts."

The rain ticked against the tarp they'd rigged over the bow. Somewhere down the dock a ship's bell rang the half hour, muffled and sweet.

"They'll keep offering," she said. "Men like that hate leaving a debt unpaid. It makes them feel like the debt owns *them*."

"Then we'll keep refusing until they believe us." He said it lightly, but she heard the steel under it, the same tone he used reading rapids — this is the line, this is where we go, no argument. "Unless you want the desk."

Nadya looked at him for a long moment. Then she looked at the boat — the patched hull, the scarred rails, the mast step he'd rebuilt with scavenged timber after the gatehouse took the old one, the whole battered, stubborn, impossible vessel sitting in the gray evening light like something that had died twice and gotten bored of it.

"No," she said. "I don't want the desk."

* * *

They refit the *Vespera* through the last cold weeks of the year, and it was the strangest winter either of them could remember, because for the first time in years neither of them was running from anything.

Mara came first. The engineer arrived at the dock with a single duffel bag and the expression of a woman who had spent four months in a limestone cave and found ordinary weather mildly insulting. She inspected the hull, pronounced it "structurally sentimental," and moved into the fo'c'sle the same afternoon. Within a week she had re-plumbed the bilge pump, redesigned the cargo lashings, and drawn up — on the back of a survey chart, because Mara drew everything on the backs of survey charts — a plan for converting the hold into passenger seating that violated at least three maritime regulations and every one of them for good reason.

Oyel and Senn came next, the two youngest surveyors, still carrying the cave-hollow look in their faces that food and daylight were slowly filling in. Oyel had been a cartographer before the valley; Senn had been a climber. Neither of them had anywhere to go back to — the Trust had employed them both, and the Trust's name was now worth less than wet paper — and when Kestrel asked, awkwardly, whether they wanted paying work or charity, Oyel had said, "Pay. Charity makes me feel like evidence," and that had settled it.

The money came from strange places. Half the valley families whose boundary stones had surfaced after the flood sent what they could — small sums, wrapped in oilcloth, carried downriver by anyone heading to the capital. The surveyors' institute, scrambling to rehabilitate its reputation, funded the refit as a matter of public contrition. And Edmund Halvard's seized estate, in the slow grinding way of courts, had begun disgorging compensation, though none of that had arrived yet and none of them planned around it.

Nadya did most of the negotiating and all of the provisioning. Kestrel did the work nobody saw — the seams, the rigging, the thousand small repairs that kept a boat alive — and he did it with a concentration she recognized and didn't comment on. She had watched men come back from ruin before. Some of them drank. Some of them raged. Kestrel caulked. He put his whole ruined history into making one small vessel watertight, and if the metaphor occurred to him he never once let it show on his face.

The leak never fully died. They chased it for weeks — the seam below the waterline, then a butt joint near the transom, then a place aft where the planking had flexed so many times under strain that the wood itself seemed to have learned the habit. Mara finally declared it "within tolerance." Nadya named it Gerald, because a leak you've named stops being an enemy and starts being crew.

On the last day of the refit, they launched.

It wasn't much of a ceremony. The tide was high, the rain had stopped for the first time in nine days, and the five of them walked the *Vespera* down the slip on rollers while the oyster woman — who had appointed herself witness to the entire enterprise — beat time on an upturned bucket. The boat took the water with a sound Nadya felt in her teeth, that long deep sigh of a hull remembering what it was for, and Gerald began to leak immediately, a thin bright thread of river finding its way in through the transom joint exactly as it always had.

"There she is," Mara said. "Flawed, functional, and floating."

"That's more than any of us can claim," said Senn.

Nadya took the tiller. Kestrel cast off the lines. And the *Vespera*, patched and scarred and taking on perhaps a bucket of water an hour, went out onto the broad gray harbor with the winter sun breaking through behind her like something forgiven.

* * *

The registries did not give up gracefully. That was the thing about institutions, Nadya thought — they treated refusal as a negotiation position, never as an answer.

The second round of offers arrived by courier in the spring. The Central Guide Registry raised its figure. The Coastal Authority offered Kestrel a directorship of search and rescue, which was almost funny, given that search and rescue was the discipline they'd blacklisted him out of. A private firm with government contracts offered Nadya a captaincy on a mail run — regular routes, safe water, a pension. Comfortable places, all of them. Warm rooms. Dry floors. Careers shaped like armchairs.

She answered them all with the same letter, which she drafted herself and made Kestrel approve:

*I am engaged. My vessel carries passengers and freight on the Sareth and its tributaries. We serve the valley settlements; our rates are posted at the Hamlet of Tern and our schedule is governed by the river. If the Registry wishes to book passage, the fare is the same as everyone else's.*

Kestrel read it twice and grinned — actually grinned, that rare crooked thing that transformed his whole face from something closed into something almost young.

"The fare is the same as everyone else's," he repeated. "They're going to hate that."

"They're going to pay it," Nadya said. "Watch. Bureaucrats can't resist a fixed price. It's the only honesty they understand."

She was right. By midsummer the Registry was booking quarterly inspection passages aboard the *Vespera* at posted rates, and the clerks who came aboard in their good shoes got the same bench seats, the same stew, and the same view of Nadya's wake as the farmers and the schoolteachers and the old women going upriver to visit graves that had spent thirty years underwater.

Because that was the trade, in the end. That was what they'd built. Not a company — Kestrel refused the word, said companies had letterheads — but a service, a promise, a route. The valley people needed to move: goods down, supplies up, children to school in Tern, the sick to the clinic at Ferrow's Landing, and always, always, people who wanted simply to see what the water had left and what was growing back. No one else knew those channels. No one else would take the boats into the flooded woods in high water, or thread the old drowned streets where the church spire still stood above the surface like a warning that had outlived its flood.

On the first anniversary of the release — the day the valley had nearly died and hadn't — they ran a free crossing for anyone who wanted to visit the boundary stones. Nadya made eleven trips. The last one went out at dusk, loaded with old men and flowers, and she held the *Vespera* steady against the current while they leaned over the rail and dropped wreaths into the channel where the villages used to be, and nobody spoke, and the river carried the wreaths slowly downstream toward the sea, and Gerald leaked his quiet faithful leak beneath their feet the whole while.

That night, moored at Tern, Kestrel sat beside her on the cabin top with the compass in his hands — the old brass Ishikawa compass, the one thing he'd kept — turning it over and over without opening the lid.

"Mara got an offer today," he said. "Institute position. Field director. Real funding, real staff."

"What did she say?"

"She said she'd think about it." He smiled faintly. "Which means she said no. She just wants to enjoy saying it properly."

"And the boys?"

"Senn's staying. Oyel's talking about maps — proper ones, of the restored valley. There's talk of a new survey. An honest one, this time. He'd lead it." Kestrel tilted his head back and looked at the stars coming out over the ridge line, the same ridges his grandfather had drawn a lifetime ago in ink that had outlasted empires. "Everyone keeps getting offered doors, Nadya. Good doors. And everyone keeps staying on the boat."

"Is that a complaint?"

"It's an observation." He opened the compass at last, checked the needle out of pure habit, and closed it again. North hadn't moved. "My grandfather lost everything because he told the truth about this valley. I spent six years thinking the lesson was *don't get caught telling truths*. Turns out the lesson was simpler. You find your people, you find your water, and you stay useful."

Nadya looked at him — this man she'd hired out of pity and a full fuel tank two monsoons ago, this blacklisted, stubborn, impossible man who had gone into a strainer after her without a second's hesitation and come up bleeding and grinning — and she felt the truth of it settle over her like fair-weather cloud.

"The registries will offer again next year," she said.

"Let them." Kestrel stood, stretched, and swung down into the cockpit, where the lamplight was warm and Mara was arguing with Senn about lashing knots and Oyel was drawing maps on the backs of charts. "We're booked solid through the rains."

Nadya stayed on the cabin top a moment longer, listening to the river move past the hull in the dark — the Sareth, her river, wide and slow and carrying the whole green valley on its back toward the sea. Then she went below, took the tiller-lines off the cleats, and began writing tomorrow's manifest by lamplight while the best crew she'd ever sailed with argued cheerfully around her and the water rose, gently, patiently, in the bilge.

Gerald was leaking again.

She let him.
