## 22. The Far Monsoon

The valley had learned to be green again.

Kestrel felt it before he saw it — the way the air changed at the mouth of the Sareth, thickening with the smell of wet silt and new leaf, the particular sweetness of a river running clean over gravel instead of brown over ruin. A year ago this stretch had been a wound: banks slumped like broken ribs, water the color of old tea, the drowned channel's secrets still hidden under its skin of flood. Now the willows had taken hold along the cutbanks in pale green ranks, and the terraces above the hamlet were stitched with young rice, and kingfishers — actual kingfishers, blue as struck flint — worked the shallows where the current slowed.

He stood in the bow of the *Far Monsoon* — Nadya had named her that the day they refit her, and no one had dared suggest anything else — and let the morning do what mornings here did now: arrive gently.

"Guide," Nadya said from the helm, not turning. "You're doing it again."

"Doing what?"

"Standing there looking like a man composing a speech."

"I'm looking at the river."

"You're looking at the river *meaningfully*. There's a difference, and my clients can smell it." She adjusted the throttle with two fingers, and the old boat answered the way she always did now — a shudder, a grumble, then steady. The patch over the worst seam, the one sewn on in the fishing hamlet by lamplight while villagers fed them stew and dealt cards and laughed, still wept a thin thread of water into the bilge. They'd fixed everything else. That seam they'd left, by unspoken agreement. A boat should keep some memory of what it survived.

Behind Kestrel, the client party was waking up in stages. There were six of them this trip — a botanist from the capital university, two of her students, an elderly couple who had farmed the middle terraces before the Trust flooded them out and who had paid for passage upstream not to see scenery but to see, specifically, a bend in the river where their house had been, and a quiet man named Oskar Vell who had booked under a false name and been recognized within an hour anyway, because everyone in the Sareth Valley knew Oskar Vell's face from the hearings. He'd been the junior clerk who leaked the sluice schedules to Mara's team. He kept to the stern and watched the water like a man counting his debts.

"Tea's on," called Mara from under the canopy, and the smell of it — cardamom, black leaf, condensed milk — rolled forward and made the whole boat feel briefly like a kitchen.

Kestrel turned away from the bow. This was the job now. Not the desperate tracking, not the running through gunfire across terraced fields with his lungs full of blood and rain. Just this: six strangers, one river, tea at dawn. He'd thought, in the first weeks, that he would find it small. He hadn't. He found it almost unbearably large.

* * *

They moored at midmorning below the ridge where the boundary stones stood.

The climb was nothing by Kestrel's standards — an hour of switchbacks through regrown alder, the trail soft underfoot from last night's rain, water dripping off every leaf in bright fat drops. But he set the pace slow, because of the old couple, Tomas and Ilse Brandt, who climbed stubbornly hand in hand and refused all offers of help except once, near the top, when Ilse took Kestrel's forearm without asking and gripped it hard for three steps and let go without a word of thanks, which was its own kind of thanks, and he understood.

Then the trees opened, and there they were.

The restored boundary stones ran along the ridgeline in a long procession, waist-high, granite, each one re-cut with the original survey marks and numbered in the old system. They caught the light and held it. Below them the whole valley opened out — the river making its silver S through green, the hamlet's tin roofs winking, the terraces climbing the far slopes in steps of impossible brightness, and beyond all of it, hazy blue, the high country where the survey team had vanished and where Kestrel Ishikawa had gone to find them and found himself instead, though he would have bitten through his own tongue before saying it that way aloud.

The botanist's students went straight to the stones, crouching, sketching, reading the inscriptions. Oskar Vell hung back, hands in pockets, looking at the valley with the expression of a man checking whether a wound has closed.

Tomas and Ilse Brandt walked the line of stones slowly, touching each one. At the fourth stone Ilse stopped. She didn't say anything. Tomas put his arm around her shoulders, and the three of them — the two old farmers and the stone — stood together in the wind.

"That's the corner marker," Tomas said finally, to nobody, to everybody. "Our north fence post sat right there. Forty years. My father set it."

"The lawyers used to call this line 'the Halvard western claim,'" the botanist said gently. "It's in all the old filings."

"It was never theirs," Ilse said. Not angry. Just certain, the way water is certain about downhill. "We told them that. Nobody asked us twice."

Kestrel let them have the moment and walked the line alone, the way he always did, checking each stone with a hand flat on the granite the way another man might check a horse's flank. The restoration crews had done honest work. His grandfather's plates had specified depths and footings; the new stones sat true. Somewhere under the grass, the originals — the ones the flood had scoured bare, the ones that had ended everything — were preserved behind rope lines and signage, pilgrimaged to by law students and journalists and, last month, a delegation from two countries' land registries who had spent an entire afternoon photographing moss.

He reached the end of the line, where the last stone stood slightly apart, and stopped.

This one was different. It had been re-cut too, but the crews had left the old damage on its face — the gouge where a Trust rider's axe had bitten deep forty years ago, when the first Ishikawa markers were being erased. The restoration charter, which Kestrel had helped draft at about four in the morning after too much tea, specified that this stone would keep its scar. *So no one forgets what was tried,* the clause read. Ilse Brandt had insisted on the wording.

He stood there longer than he meant to.

A year ago he had stood in a capital office while lawyers laid the plates out on a table under good light, and one of them — a dry woman with ink-stained fingers — had looked up at him over her spectacles and said, *Mr. Ishikawa, do you understand what you're holding? These boundaries make you heir to claims covering nearly a fifth of the valley. The reconstituted holdings, the compensation funds, the water rights. It's yours to file.* And Edmund Halvard's empire, already cracking, had waited on the other side of that table like a coat offered to a cold man.

He remembered how heavy the compass had felt in his pocket that day. How light the pen had felt. He had signed the papers transferring the plates — all of them, every plate, every claim — into trust for the displaced families of the Sareth Valley, administered by a board that included Tomas and Ilse Brandt and Mara and two village heads, and the ink-stained lawyer had watched him sign and then, unexpectedly, smiled and said, *Your grandfather would have done exactly that,* and Kestrel had walked out into the capital's grey drizzle carrying nothing but a brass compass and feeling richer than any man he'd ever guided for money.

He took the compass out now. Habit. He checked it against the ridgeline, against the stones, against the sun — and laughed at himself, out loud, because the needle swung lazy and true and told him absolutely nothing he didn't already know. He'd been navigating by feel since before dawn. The compass wasn't for finding his way anymore. It never really had been, not since the gatehouse, not since the flood. It was for remembering whose footsteps he was walking in.

His grandfather had been ruined by these mountains. Discredited, blacklisted, driven out of the profession for refusing to sell the truth of his own survey. And the same machine had come for Kestrel twenty-five years later, patient as water, wearing different faces. Two generations of Ishikawas erased from the registries of the very valleys they knew best.

And now look at it. Look at the green coming back. Look at the stones standing straight in the wind.

*Forgiving,* he thought. Not forgetting — the scarred stone beside him wouldn't allow that, and shouldn't. But forgiving. The wilderness had never held the grudge. Only men had.

* * *

They ate lunch on the ridge, and it was during lunch that Nadya appeared beside him, silent as she only was when she wanted him to notice something.

She nodded downstream, toward the southern horizon.

Kestrel followed her gaze and felt his breath go quiet.

The sky down there had changed. Not darkened — *deepened*, the way deep water is deeper than shallow, a bruised indigo wall building up along the far edge of the world, soft-edged and enormous, with the first gray veils of rain hanging beneath it like curtains not yet drawn. Lightning flickered inside it, unhurried, almost polite. The monsoon. The real one, the season-turning one, arriving on schedule now that the schedules belonged to the valley again and not to a man with sluice gates and a ledger.

"There it is," Nadya said.

"A day early," Kestrel said.

"A day early means it's honest." She leaned on the rail beside him, close enough that their shoulders touched, which was as much affection as either of them ever announced. "You remember what you said to me, the first night? On your boat, in the squall, both of us bailing?"

"I said a lot of things that night. Most of them unkind."

"You said, 'This boat is going to sink and I'm going to drown next to a stranger.'" She smiled at the horizon. "Look at us now."

Kestrel looked. Downriver, the *Far Monsoon* rode at anchor below them, patched and scarred and leaking her one faithful thread, deck crowded with drying laundry and Mara's instrument cases and the botanist's specimen presses. Upstream, the high country stood clear and open, no restrictions, no riders, no sealed zone — just trails, and people on them, and children from the hamlet who waved at every passing boat like the river belonged to them, because now it did.

"We're going to get wet," he said. "All the way down. That front will catch us by tomorrow noon."

"Obviously."

"And the gorge at flood stage—"

"—is a Tuesday, Kestrel. I've run it drunker than this." She pushed off the rail. "Get your clients moving. I want us anchored above the hamlet before the wind turns."

He watched her go, quick and sure down the switchbacks, and stayed one more minute.

The rain-veils on the horizon had thickened. The lightning flickered again, and this time, faint and low, came the first thunder — a long rolling sound, less threat than announcement, the way a host clears his throat before opening the door. The wind had begun to move, cool and mineral-smelling, pushing up the valley ahead of the weather like a scout.

Kestrel checked the compass one last time. North. Home. The needle barely trembled.

He laughed again — softly, at himself, at the year, at the improbable mercy of it all — pocketed the brass, and turned to lead his people down off the ridge, ahead of the far monsoon, into the green.
