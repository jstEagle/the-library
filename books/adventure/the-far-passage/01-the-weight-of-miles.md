## 1. The Weight of Miles

The pass was at 5,040 meters and my client was at the end of his rope, which is a phrase I use both literally and with affection.

"Tell me again how far," Gerald wheezed. He was fifty-eight, a retired dentist from Manchester with a chest like a beer keg and the stubbornness of a man who'd spent thirty years listening to people tell him it couldn't be done.

"Two point one kilometers to the summit cairn," I said. "Average gradient eleven percent. At your current pace, forty minutes."

"And how do you feel about that?"

I considered the question. It was the kind of thing clients asked when they wanted you to say something human — *I'm excited*, or *it's beautiful up there*, or some other line from the brochure of the soul. What I actually felt was arithmetic. Two point one kilometers. Forty minutes. Oxygen at sixty-one percent of sea level, adjusted for the barometric dip of a low-pressure system sitting over the range like an unwelcome guest. Gerald's pulse, which I'd taken that morning, sat at 104 resting, which meant I'd be watching him like a hawk with a clipboard.

"I feel it's forty minutes," I said.

He laughed, which cost him, and kept walking. That's why I liked Gerald. He didn't need me to feel things on his behalf. He had plenty of his own; he just needed someone to make sure those feelings didn't kill him above the snowline.

My name is Mara Okafor, and for twenty years I have been the best high-altitude guide nobody has heard of. This is not false modesty. It is a business model. The famous guides have names you can put on a poster, and posters attract clients who want the poster more than the mountain. My clients come by word of mouth — dentists, engineers, a Japanese ceramicist who cried at 4,800 meters and then came back the next year to cry in a different spot — and they come because I get them up and down, and because I never once made them feel like scenery in somebody else's adventure story.

The trade-off is that nobody knows my name, which suits me fine. Names are heavy. I carry enough weight already.

We crested the pass at 11:20, forty-two minutes by my watch, and Gerald stood under the prayer flags with his hands on his knees doing the great heaving breaths of the recently victorious. Below us the valley fell away in terraces of barley gone gold, and beyond that the white shoulders of the Serrano Range stood in their usual silence, pretending not to notice us.

"There she is," Gerald said, meaning the view, meaning all of it.

"There she is," I agreed, and checked his lips for cyanosis while he wasn't looking. Pink. Good.

Tsering, my senior porter and the only person alive allowed to call me by my childhood nickname, was already boiling water behind a wind wall. He handed me tea without being asked, which is the highest form of conversation between us.

"Gerald will want to stay an hour," he said.

"Gerald will stay forty minutes. His oxygen sats will drop below eighty-eight and he'll agree with me."

"He will argue."

"He'll argue for ten minutes and then agree." I drank my tea. "It's a known route."

Tsering smiled into his cup. Twenty years we've been doing this dance, and neither of us has ever once said anything as sentimental as *you're my best friend* or *I couldn't do this without you*. Instead we say things like *it's a known route* and *he will argue*, and somehow this contains everything else.

Gerald stayed fifty-five minutes, argued for twelve, and agreed. We were down to the high camp by four, and down to the roadhead two days later, where a jeep took him back toward Leh and whatever dental triumphs awaited him in Manchester. He shook my hand at the jeep door with both of his, the way men do when they want to convey something larger than a handshake but lack the vocabulary.

"You're wasted out here," he said. "You should write a book. Do lectures. People would love you."

"People would ask me how I felt," I said. "I'd have to lie."

He laughed all the way down the valley. I watched the dust of the jeep settle and felt the particular emptiness that arrives after a trip ends — not sadness exactly, more like a room after the furniture's been carried out. The shape of the next thing hadn't arrived yet.

This is the part nobody tells you about guiding: the miles are easy. Miles are the honest part. You count them, you walk them, they accumulate behind you like receipts. It's the hours between trips that are hard, the ones with no gradient and no destination, when your body finally stands still and your mind starts wandering off-route into country you've spent twenty years fencing off.

I knew the signs. Restlessness first: re-packing gear that didn't need repacking. Then the maps. Then the dreams.

So when the radio crackled that evening at the roadhead lodge, I was almost grateful.

* * *

The lodge keeper, a woman named Dolma who had fed me dal bhat for fifteen years and never once asked about my personal life (a quality I valued above most friendships), flagged me down in the courtyard.

"Satellite phone," she said. "Leh. A man asking for Okafor Guides. He says it's about a debt."

"A debt?"

"That's what he said." She shrugged with her whole body, a gesture that could mean *who knows* or *the world is strange* or *your dal is getting cold*, depending on context.

The phone was warm from the afternoon sun. The voice on the other end belonged to a Dr. Namgyal, whom I did not know, calling from the Sonam Norboo Memorial Hospital in Leh, which I also did not know, though I knew its kind. Hospice wards in mountain towns are quiet places where the mountains come to collect.

"Are you the Mara Okafor?" he asked.

"There's only one."

"Not according to the mountaineering registries. According to them there are several, none of any note."

I liked him immediately. "What debt?"

"A patient of mine. Tomas Reyes. He's dying — weeks, perhaps days, and he knows it better than I do, which is saying something, since knowing things better than doctors is the chief hobby of the dying. He's been asking for you by name for three days. He says you owe him."

"I don't owe anyone named Reyes anything."

"So I told him. He said—" here the doctor paused, and I heard paper moving, the sound of a man consulting notes he'd already memorized, "—he said, 'She doesn't know yet. Tell her it's about Elias Okafor, and she'll come.'"

The courtyard went very still around me. Dolma was chopping onions. A mule complained somewhere below the walls. The light did its evening trick on the mountains, turning them briefly the color of old brass, and I stood there holding a satellite phone like it might detonate.

Elias Okafor has been dead for twenty years. He has also, in that time, been a fraud, a cautionary tale, a chapter in three separate histories of Karakor exploration, and — to me, privately, in the locked room where I keep the things I don't look at — a pair of hands brushing dirt from pottery shards, patient as water.

"Doctor," I said, and my voice came out level, which I counted as a professional achievement, "when does the next bus leave for Leh?"

* * *

It left at five the next morning, which gave me nine hours to sleep badly and pack well. Packing is my liturgy. I know the weight of every item I own the way priests know their psalms, and I laid them out in the lodge courtyard by headlamp: rope, rack, stove, medical kit, the spare radio. I did not know what I was walking into, so I packed for everything, which is another way of saying I packed so my hands would be busy.

Tsering found me at four-thirty, before the bus, standing in the dark with my duffel like a woman waiting for a train in a war she hasn't been told started.

"The hospice?" he said. He'd heard, of course. Tsering hears everything within forty miles; I suspect he has arrangements with the wind.

"It's nothing. An old man with a story."

"You slept four hours."

"I slept fine."

"You rearranged your medical kit twice. When you sleep fine, you sleep six hours and the kit stays arranged." He picked up one end of my duffel without waiting for permission, which is Tsering's way of ending conversations he considers beneath us both. "I'll mind the office."

"We don't have an office."

"The shed, then. I'll mind the shed."

The bus was full of monks going down for a festival and smelled of butter tea and diesel, and I rode it through the dawn with my forehead against the glass watching the Indus valley open up below, brown and green and ancient, and I did not think about my father. I thought about distances. Leh was 214 kilometers away. The bus would take eleven hours. Tomas Reyes was dying at a rate I could calculate if I wanted to, and I did not want to, so I calculated the road instead: switchbacks per kilometer, elevation loss per hour, the exact moment the poplars would start, the exact moment the river would turn from gray to green.

This is what I do instead of feeling. I measure. It's not cowardice — or it is, but it's cowardice with excellent navigation. My father taught me the measuring, though he'd have hated to hear it called that. He called it *reading the ground*. He could look at a hillside and tell you what was buried in it, what had walked across it, what it had been a thousand years ago. He read the world like a ledger, and I inherited the habit and pointed it at different terrain. He measured the past. I measure the way ahead. Between us we covered everything except each other.

Twenty years ago he went up into the Serrano Range with eleven companions and a partner named Reyes, and something happened above the icefall, and only one man came down, and the story he told made my father the most despised name in the history of the range. Eleven men abandoned. Left without shelter, without rope, without their leader, while Elias Okafor supposedly descended alone with the expedition's best supplies and his own skin intact. He died two years later, before he could answer anyone, in a rockfall on a different mountain entirely — even the mountain declined to give him a dignified exit. And I was nineteen, and I changed my surname on my guide registrations, and I went to work in ranges where nobody knew the name, and I built a career out of competence the way other people build houses out of flood debris.

I have told myself for twenty years that I don't care what the histories say. I have told myself this so many times that it has worn smooth, like a stone in a riverbed, and smooth stones are the easiest ones to trip over.

* * *

Leh announced itself the way it always does: first the smell of the poplars, then the gompa on its ridge catching the last light like a lamp being lit, then the traffic, which has opinions. I got off the bus stiff and dusty and walked to the hospital because I didn't trust my legs to sit still in a taxi.

Dr. Namgyal turned out to be a small, precise man with reading glasses pushed up into gray hair and the unhurried manner of someone who has watched enough people die to stop being impressed by urgency.

"She came," he said, looking me over. "He'll be pleased. He bet me fifty rupees you wouldn't."

"Why wouldn't I?"

"He said because you'd have learned to run from that name by now." He led me down a corridor that smelled of antiseptic and, faintly, of the juniper someone was burning somewhere out of sight, the old medicine and the new sharing a building the way they share everything in this country. "He's lucid in the mornings. Fades after noon. Don't waste the morning arguing with him — he's better at it than you, and he's had less practice lately."

The room was small and clean and had a window with a view of the Stok Kangri massif, which struck me as either a kindness or a cruelty, depending on the patient. Tomas Reyes lay in the bed like a man who had been folded there by someone else — gaunt, yellowed, with the particular translucent skin of the nearly finished. But his eyes, when they found me in the doorway, were sharp as a prospector's pick.

"Well," he said. "She runs to the mountains after all."

"You have the wrong Okafor," I said. "I'm nobody."

"You're the image of him, is what you are. Same jaw. Same way of standing like the floor might charge you for it." He coughed, a long terrible business, and waved off the nurse who materialized. "Sit down, girl. I've got maybe four good conversations left in this body and I'm not spending one of them watching you hover in a doorway."

I sat. Against every plan I'd made on the bus, against the whole architecture of twenty careful years, I sat.

"Tomas Reyes," he said, and extended a hand that weighed nothing. "Your father's partner. The one who came down."

I knew that. Of course I knew that. Every child of the Serrano valleys knew that name the way they knew the names of storms. Reyes, the survivor. Reyes, the witness. Reyes, whose testimony buried Elias Okafor so deep that even his daughter stopped digging.

"I know who you are," I said.

"Do you." Something moved in his face — not quite a smile, more the place where a smile had been excavated and filled in. "Then you know I'm the man who ended him. And yet here you sit. Curious girl."

"The doctor said you owed me a debt. Or that I owed you. The message was unclear."

"The message was bait. You're here, aren't you?" He closed his eyes for a moment, gathering himself, and when he opened them they had gone somewhere else — up, I thought, up toward the icefall, up toward whatever he'd been carrying for twenty years. "I haven't much time, so listen. There's a cache. High in the Serrano, past the moraine, above the icefall. Your father built it. Sealed it. Everything the expedition carried that mattered, he sealed inside, and he marked it with his initials, because he was a vain romantic who believed the truth would outlive him." His hand trembled on the blanket. "He was right. It has. It's still there. And I am the last living soul who knows the route to it, and I am leaving this world in a matter of days, and I will not let that knowledge die in a hospital bed in Leh."

"Why me?" My voice sounded far away, like someone else's radio. "Why not your own family? Why not the archives? Why hand it to the fraud's daughter?"

"Because the map isn't for the world. It's for whoever deserves it. That was his condition and mine both." He fixed me with those pick-sharp eyes. "And I've watched you for years, Mara Okafor, even if you never watched me. The guides talk. They say there's a woman works the high passes who reads ground like her father used to, who gets everyone down alive, who takes no credit and tells no stories. They say she's the best climber nobody's heard of." He paused. "They say she measures everything and feels nothing. Which sounds to me like a woman carrying something too heavy to set down."

Outside the window, Stok Kangri caught the last of the sun and held it.

"I have a map," Tomas Reyes said. "Hand-drawn. It's yours on one condition, and the condition is not negotiable, and you can hate me all you like for the rest of my short life — please, hate me, I've earned it — but the condition stands. You carry what's in that cache across the range before the snows close the passes. And you deliver it to whoever deserves it. Not to yourself. Not to me. To whoever deserves it." He swallowed, and for the first time the sharpness in his eyes cracked, and underneath it I saw something that looked, unbelievably, like fear. "Do you understand what I'm asking?"

"No," I said honestly. "But I think I understand what you're offering."

"And?"

And there it was. The whole rest of my life balanced on a hospital chair in Leh, and I heard myself answer before the careful part of me could raise an objection — the part that measures, the part that counts switchbacks instead of counting the years since I'd spoken my father's name aloud.

"Yes," I said.

Reyes exhaled, long and slow, like a man setting down a load at the end of a very long portage. Then he reached beneath his pillow, and brought out a packet wrapped in oilcloth, and held it out to me with both hands, the way you hand someone something that weighs far more than it appears to.

"Come back at dawn," he said. "I'll teach you the route. All of it. Even the parts that will make you hate me worse."

I took the oilcloth packet. It was lighter than it should have been. The heaviest things always are.

That night I didn't sleep at all, and in the morning the nurse met me at the ward door with her hands folded and her eyes already apologizing, and told me that Tomas Reyes had died at 3:40 a.m., peacefully, in his sleep — and that he'd left instructions, and a letter, and that I was mentioned in both.
