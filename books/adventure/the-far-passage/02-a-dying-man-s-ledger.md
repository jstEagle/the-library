## 2. A Dying Man's Ledger

The radio call came in at 4,980 meters, which is to say it came in as a crackle and a prayer, half-swallowed by wind on the col. I was roped to a client named Whitfield who had paid me an obscene sum to stand where he was standing and pretend the altitude didn't bother me, and I confess I was doing exactly that — calculating our descent time instead of feeling my own fingers.

"Mara." The voice belonged to Jigmet, my dispatcher in Leh, and he sounded wrong. Not emergency-wrong. Something quieter and worse. "There's a man here asking for you by name. Says you owe him."

"Nobody up here owes anybody," I said. "That's the whole point of altitude."

"He says his name is Reyes."

I looked at Whitfield, who was admiring the view with the serene entitlement of a man whose oxygen debt would be someone else's problem tomorrow. Below us the valley went down in folds of brown and green, terraced like the inside of a shell, and somewhere down there was Leh, and somewhere in Leh was a name I hadn't heard spoken aloud in twenty years except by people who used it as a synonym for *liar*.

"Tell him I'm working," I said.

"He's dying, Mara. He says tell you that too. He seems to think it will change your answer."

It did not change my answer. It changed something else, something lower and less negotiable, the way a stone changes the course of water without asking permission. I told Jigmet I'd be down in four days. He said the man might not have four days. I said then he'd better spend them well, and signed off, and spent the rest of the descent not thinking about it, which is a skill I've honed to a professional edge.

* * *

I got Whitfield down, fed him, invoiced him, and watched him drive off toward the airport with the particular emptiness that follows every trip's end — the sudden absence of purpose dressed as freedom. Then I sat on the wall outside my guesthouse for a long time, watching the poplars do their patient work, before I walked to the hospice.

It was a converted house near the old mosque, all cool shadows and the smell of carbolic fighting a losing war against incense. A nun — Ladakhi, efficient, unimpressed by me on sight, which earned my immediate respect — led me down a corridor to a room at the end where the afternoon light came in low and gold through a window that needed cleaning.

Tomas Reyes was a ruin. There's no kinder word for it. He'd been a big man once; you could see the architecture of him under the blanket, the shoulders that had carried loads over passes most people see only in photographs. What remained was a frame with a fire guttering inside it, skin gone the color of old paper, hands resting on the coverlet like tools set down for the last time. His eyes, though. His eyes were still working. They found me in the doorway and fixed on me with an appetite that had nothing to do with food.

"Okafor," he said. "You look like him."

"Everyone says that." I stayed by the door. "I'm told I owe you a debt. I don't remember it."

"You don't." He coughed, a long wet business that seemed to take minutes off some clock only he could hear. "Sit down anyway. Debts don't need to be remembered to be real. That's what makes them debts."

I sat. The chair was wooden and honest and I gripped its edge like a man holding onto a ledge.

"My father has been dead twenty years," I said. "And disgraced for twenty-one. Whatever you knew of Elias Okafor, I promise you I've heard worse versions of it from better-informed people."

"I knew him better than anyone living," Reyes said. "Which is why I know the versions are wrong. All of them." He turned his head on the pillow with great effort, and for a moment I saw the mountaineer under the invalid, the man who had once judged weather and rockface for a living. "You guide now. High altitude. They say you're good."

"They say a lot of things."

"They say you never talk about your family. They say you take jobs nobody else will and finish them early. They say you measure everything." His mouth did something that on a healthier face would have been a smile. "He measured everything too. Did you know that? Before every climb he'd sit up all night with his notebooks, calculating rations, rope lengths, the weight of a man's fear in grams. I used to laugh at him. I said, Elias, the mountain doesn't read your figures. And he said, no, but I do, and I'm the one going up it."

My throat did something I chose to ignore. "What do you want, Mr. Reyes?"

"Tomas." He closed his eyes. Opened them. "I want to make a trade. But first you have to understand what you're trading for, or you'll refuse, and I haven't got the strength left to convince you twice."

* * *

He talked for two hours, with rests. The nun brought tea and looked at me once, briefly, with an expression I couldn't parse and have thought about since.

In 1986, Tomas Reyes and Elias Okafor had mounted a joint expedition into the Serrano Range — not the tourist side, not the mapped cols, but the deep interior, following a rumor. Reyes had been a prospector all his life, chasing mineral shows across three ranges, and he'd found something in an old shepherd's account: a high valley where the rock ran wrong, where the stones themselves seemed out of place, carried and deposited by ice from a formation that existed nowhere else in the district. Elias, who was between grants and hungry for a discovery that would silence the men who called him a dilettante, had put up half the money and all of the science.

They went in with fourteen men. Eleven came back.

I knew this story. Everyone in the climbing world of a certain age knew this story. It had been told at dinners I wasn't invited to, written into route histories with the flat cruelty of official record: *the Okafor expedition, 1986, eleven dead above the icefall; Okafor descended alone.* The inquiry had cleared him of legal fault — weather, they said, an early storm, bad luck — but clearing a man legally is not the same as believing him, and the climbing world had made its judgment in the older court, the one that never adjourns. Elias Okafor had come down off that mountain alive when eleven men hadn't, and no explanation he ever gave had been good enough to survive the weight of that arithmetic. He'd spent his last years teaching surveying in a town that barely tolerated him, and he'd died of a heart attack in a rented room with his notes boxed and unlabeled, and I had spent twenty years making sure no one connected me to any of it.

"That's the story," I said, when Reyes paused. "I've heard it. I've heard it with more detail and less kindness."

"The story is true," Reyes said. "Eleven men died above the icefall. Elias came down alone. Every word of it stands." He lifted one hand off the blanket — an enormous effort, I could see, a hand that had hauled sledges now trembling with the weight of being raised — and laid it flat. "But a story can be true the way a map is true. Accurate. Incomplete. Useless for getting anywhere that matters."

"And your version gets somewhere?"

"My version has coordinates." He said it simply, and the simplicity was the worst part. This was not a man performing. This was a man emptying his pockets because there was no one left to pick them.

He'd gone back, he said. Twice. Once in '89, alone, turned back by the icefall itself — a serac collapse had rearranged the route so thoroughly that his memory of it was worthless. And once in 2003, older, slower, with two hired porters who quit at the moraine field because they said the place was haunted, and maybe it was, he wasn't in a position to argue. But in 2003 he'd gotten high enough. High enough to find the cairn. Not the memorial cairn the rescue parties built — the other one, the one Elias had built, hidden in a crevice above the icefall, sealed with stones and marked with chiseled initials that Reyes had recognized even through twenty years of frost-scour.

E.O.

"He built a cache," Reyes said. "Above where they died. Above the icefall, past anything a rescue party would search, past anything a man descending alone would bother with. You understand what that means?"

I understood. I understood it the way you understand a load shifting on a porter's back — not with the mind but with the spine. A cache above the disaster site meant Elias had gone *up*, after. Had climbed past the place where eleven men lay, carrying supplies, and buried them in the ice for reasons he'd taken with him into whatever grave history assigned him.

"It means he wasn't running," Reyes said. "A guilty man descends. He ascended. With a hundred pounds of tinned food and his journals, if I know Elias, and I knew Elias." His breath caught and held. "I tried to open it in '03. My hands were already going — the cold took them years ago, you can see. I couldn't shift the capstone. I told myself I'd come back with proper gear. Then my wife got sick, then my son, then this—" he gestured at himself, the room, the whole diminishing country of a dying body, "—and now I'm coming back nowhere. Except through you."

"Why me?" I asked, and my voice came out flatter than I intended. "You have a grandson. I've seen the name. Devan. He runs salvage work out of Manali. Why not hand it to your own blood?"

Something moved behind his eyes — regret, or its fossil. "Because Devan believes the story," he said quietly. "The whole story. He grew up on it. His grandfather survived the mountain, and the boy built a cathedral around that fact, and I let him, because telling him the rest would have meant telling him what I did or didn't do up there, and I—" The cough took him again, longer this time, and the nun appeared in the doorway with the unhurried authority of a woman who had watched many men approach this exact border. When it passed, Reyes lay gray and small against the pillows, and when he spoke again his voice had dropped to almost nothing.

"I'm not going to tell you what happened above the icefall, Mara. Not because I'm cruel. Because I don't trust my own memory anymore, and because what's in that cache is Elias's testimony, not mine, and testimony should arrive intact or not at all. I'm giving you the location. That's all I have. What's in the tin belongs to whoever deserves it — and I've spent thirty years deciding that isn't me."

* * *

He made me wait while he slept. I stood at the window and watched the light go amber and then copper over the rooftops, and I told myself I could still walk out. That was the thing about doors: they worked in both directions until you committed to one.

When he woke, the nurse adjusted his oxygen and he waved her off with the imperiousness of the very ill, and he nodded toward the steel trunk at the foot of the bed. Inside, under wool blankets that smelled of camphor and decades, was a oilcloth packet, and inside that, a sheet of paper folded in quarters.

It was a map. Hand-drawn, ink on drafting film, the linework fine and confident — my father's linework. I knew it the way you know a voice on the phone after twenty years. The contour intervals, the little hooked symbols he used for seracs, the north arrow drawn as a stylized ibis because he'd once spent a season in Egypt and never got over it. It covered the Serrano interior from the last village to the high valley, annotated in his small upright hand: distances, estimated times, water sources, one line in red ink along the final approach that read, in English, *DO NOT TRUST THE ICEFALL AFTER NOON.*

Below the icefall, in a box of ink so faint I had to tilt the page to the window to catch it, were two initials and a date. E.O. And a date three days after the expedition's recorded end.

Three days. The record said Elias Okafor came down alone within forty hours of the storm. The map said he'd been up there three days later, drawing lines, marking a cache. The record and the map could not both be true, and one of them had been repeated at enough dinner tables to become the truth, and the other one was in my hands, cold as a stone pulled from a river.

"There's a second annotation," Reyes said, watching me. "Under the flap. Don't look yet. Look when you're on the mountain, if you go. Some things weigh less at altitude." Which was nonsense, of course — weight is weight — but dying men are permitted their physics, and I folded the map along his creases and said nothing.

"The trade," he said. "Here it is. I give you the map. You give me your word: you carry it across before the snows seal the passes, you open the cache, and you deliver what's inside to whoever deserves it. Not to yourself. To whoever deserves it. Those aren't the same person, Mara. I need you to say you understand that."

"Do you know what's in it?"

"No. I know what he told me he was putting in it, the night before we went up, and I know what kind of man wrote his rations to the gram. But no. I don't *know*. Nobody knows. That's the whole disease of it — thirty years of everyone knowing, and nobody knowing."

Outside, the call to prayer started up from the mosque, thin and silver in the evening air. Reyes listened to it with his eyes closed, and when he opened them again they were wet, and he didn't wipe them, and neither of us acknowledged it, because we were both professionals.

"Say it," he said.

I should have asked for time. I should have gone back to my guesthouse, sat with the map for a week, checked his story against the archives, weighed it the way I weigh everything. That's what the woman I'd built would have done — the guide who measures, who calculates descent times instead of feeling her fingers, who hasn't said her father's name aloud in six years and has arranged her entire life so she never has to.

Instead I heard myself say: "I give you my word. Before the snows. Whoever deserves it."

Reyes exhaled, a long rattling sound, and something in his face unclenched that had been clenched so long it had become the face. "Good," he whispered. "Good. He'd have liked you. He'd have hated that you guide rich tourists, but he'd have liked you." His eyes drifted toward the window, toward the mountains just visible above the rooftops, going violet with dusk. "He talked about you, you know. The year before. Said his daughter had his head for numbers and none of his patience, and that she'd be better off for it."

"He never—" I stopped. Recalibrated. "We didn't speak, those last years."

"I know." Gently. Terribly. "He knew it too. He kept a photograph of you in his survey kit. Little girl on a dig site, holding a trowel wrong. He showed it to the porters like a medal."

I stood there holding a dead man's map while a dying man gave me back pieces of another dead man, one at a time, like coins counted out of a purse, and I could not for the life of me calculate the exchange rate.

At the door I paused. "Why now, Tomas? Why me, why this month? The passes close in weeks. You must have known I'd say yes faster if you'd asked in spring."

He smiled, and for a second the ruin of him was young, was strong, was standing on a glacier arguing about rope lengths with a stubborn archaeologist. "Because the monsoon's coming early," he said. "And because I wanted to watch your face when you saw his handwriting. And because—" the smile went out like a lamp, "—because I'm not the only one who knows the cache exists, Mara. And the others won't trade. They'll just take."

I stood very still. "Who else knows?"

"Ask your questions in town," he said, settling back into the pillows, suddenly exhausted, suddenly finished with me. "You're a guide. You're good at reading tracks. Read the ones in Leh."

I walked back through the blue evening with the map against my chest like a second heartbeat, and I did not sleep, and at dawn the hospice called my guesthouse to tell me that Tomas Reyes had died peacefully in the night, and that he'd left one more instruction, which was this:

*Tell her to check the outfitter's ledger. Tell her somebody bought rope yesterday. Somebody who isn't her.*
