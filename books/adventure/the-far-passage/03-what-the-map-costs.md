## 3. What the Map Costs

Tomas Reyes died at 4:17 in the morning, though I only learned the time later, from a nurse who seemed to find the precision comforting. People want death to have a timestamp the way they want mountains to have summits — some fixed point you can name and then stop thinking about.

I was sitting in the vinyl chair beside his bed when it happened, and I will tell you honestly that I did not notice right away. His breathing had been doing its work all night — long laboring pulls with pauses between them that stretched like rope under load, until I'd started timing them out of professional habit, the way I time gaps between lightning and thunder to guess how far off the storm is. Four seconds. Seven. Eleven. At eleven seconds you start thinking about what you'll do if the storm arrives.

At some point the count simply didn't resolve. I looked up from my hands and he was gone, and the oxygen concentrator kept up its soft mechanical breathing beside him, which felt like a cruelty — the machine carrying on the rhythm its owner had set down.

I sat there for a while. I'm not going to perform grief for you here. What I felt was less like sorrow than like a door closing somewhere in a distant part of a large house — a pressure change, a draft, the sense that something had shifted in a room I wasn't in. I'd known the man for nine hours. He'd known my father for twenty years, or claimed to, and he'd handed me a folded piece of paper like a man passing a live coal, and now he was finished with all of it, including me.

The nurse came in on her rounds and checked him with the unhurried competence of someone who has done this many times, then looked at me with an expression I recognized. It's the look people give guides when something goes wrong above base camp — sympathy braided with relief that it isn't them.

"You were with him," she said. Not a question. Hospices keep records of these things.

"I was with him."

"Did he say anything?"

"He said a lot of things." I stood, and my knees cracked, and I thought absurdly about altitude, about how everything creaks more up high. "Most of it was about my father."

She nodded as if this were normal, and maybe it was. Dying men hand their obsessions to whoever's in the chair.

* * *

I walked back to my guesthouse through streets still dark and cold, Leh at that hour being all dogs and frost and the smell of woodsmoke from the bakeries that start before anyone's awake to buy bread. The map was in my jacket's inner pocket, against my chest, where I'd put it without deciding to. Some things you carry close to the body automatically, the way you carry a knife or a photograph.

In my room I boiled water, made tea I didn't drink, and unfolded the paper on the bed under my headlamp.

It was drawn in pencil on the back of what appeared to be a supply ledger page — columns of numbers showing faintly through, tins and kerosene and rope, prices in rupees from a currency era I couldn't place. The map itself was beautiful in the way old field maps are beautiful: not accurate, but *true*, every line drawn by someone who had actually stood where the line began. Reyes had walked this ground. You could see it in the confidence of the ridgelines, the way he'd hatched the steep faces, the little annotations in a cramped engineer's hand — *bad rock after monsoon*, *water at 4,100 reliable*, *goat track, use it*.

The route ran west and north from the last roadhead into the Serrano Range proper, up a valley I knew by reputation and nothing else, past a frozen lake marked with a careful cross and the word *BLACK ICE — TEST*, over a col that looked, even in pencil, like a bad idea, and then along the flank of a mountain that had no name on the map, just a triangle and an elevation figure that made me sit back on my heels.

Above the icefall. Of course. Everything in this story lived above the icefall.

And there, in a side crevice near the col's eastern shoulder, a small square drawn with a ruler's care, and inside the square two letters:

**E.O.**

My father's initials. Chiseled, according to Reyes, into the stone beside the cache itself. Elias Okafor, who dug where others wouldn't. Who had walked into the Serrano Range forty years ago with eleven companions and come out — no. Hadn't come out at all. That was the shape of the story history told: Elias Okafor led an expedition into the range, the expedition broke apart above the icefall, eleven men died, and Okafor descended alone with a story nobody believed and a reputation that curdled from brilliant to fraudulent within a season. He spent the rest of his life writing letters to journals and societies that stopped answering, and then he died, and I spent twenty years making sure no one connected the disgraced archaeologist to the guide who could get you over a five-thousand-meter pass in weather that turns other guides' clients around.

Competence as camouflage. It works better than you'd think. Nobody looks hard at a woman who is merely very good.

I traced the route again with one finger, not touching the pencil lines. Reyes had said the cache held supplies — expedition stores, sealed tins, things meant to sustain survivors who never arrived. And something else. He hadn't said what else. He'd said *deliver what's in it to whoever deserves it*, and he'd put his hand flat on the ledger page and looked at me with eyes gone yellow at the edges, and I had said yes, because dying men get to collect on debts, and because — I'll be honest, since I'm apparently being honest tonight — because the letters were mine. E.O. Two letters, and my whole chest went tight around them like a fist closing.

Here is what I did instead of feeling anything about that: I got out my own maps.

This is what I do. This is what I've always done. When the world hands me something too large to hold, I break it into distances and elevations and load weights, and I make lists, and the lists hold me together while the rest of me does whatever it does privately, later, alone, if ever. My father used to do the same thing, I realized — sitting there with his compass and his notebooks, converting grief into stratigraphy. Maybe inheritance skips the parts you'd choose.

I spread three sheets on the bed and cross-referenced Reyes's pencil against surveyed contours. The valley approach was real. The goat track was real — I'd seen it from a distance two seasons ago and dismissed it as not worth the detour. The col existed, though the map put it fifty meters lower than Reyes had, which meant either his altimeter was drunk or the glacier below it had wasted away and left the col higher relative to everything. Probably the latter. Glaciers are the only honest historians in the range; they record everything and flatter no one.

Distances: four days to the lake if the river crossing behaved, six to the icefall's base, eight to the col with loads. Nine days up, margin for weather, five down if nothing broke. Call it fifteen days, sixteen with a rest day. Which meant departure inside a week, because the monsoon was already sulking north over the plains, and the Serrano passes in early monsoon rain are a different animal than the ones you read about in guidebooks. Snow line drops. Rivers become arguments you lose.

I made the list. Rope, two hundred meters minimum. Fuel beyond what I'd carry for a commercial trip — melting snow for water at that col burns white gas like a furnace eats coal. Food for five, twenty days, heavy on the fats because cold at altitude is a tax paid in calories. Tent poles, repair kit, the good stove, not the loaner. Medical kit expanded. And porters — I couldn't carry this alone, and pride is a luxury item I stopped buying years ago.

Which meant Tsering, and Tsering would ask questions, and I would have to decide what lies to pack alongside the food.

I wrote all of this down in my notebook in my small square handwriting, and when I finished the list I looked at it and understood that I had just planned a fifteen-day expedition into a range that killed my father, starting within seven days, in the shoulder season, based on a dead stranger's pencil map — and that I had experienced the entire decision as a series of logistics, calm as a customs form.

That's the trick, if you're wondering. That's always been the trick. Grief is a load, and loads can be distributed. You just have to be the one carrying the manifest.

* * *

The light was coming up gray over the rooftops when I finally slept, two hours maybe, dreamless and dense as a stone dropped in a lake. I woke at ten with the tea beside the bed gone cold and the map still spread out, and I lay there for a moment in the particular disorientation of having your life rearranged overnight, before memory arrived and set everything back in its new configuration.

Then I went shopping.

Leh's main bazaar street in late morning is organized chaos with excellent produce. I worked it the way I always work a resupply — systematically, list in hand, comparing prices across three shops before committing, haggling on principle even for things I'd pay double for elsewhere, because the haggling is a relationship and relationships matter when you need a favor in October. Kerosene at the hardware shop near the mosque. Rope at the mountaineering co-op, where the owner knows me and raised an eyebrow at the quantity.

"Big trip," he said. Not a question either. Everyone in this town asks questions that aren't questions.

"Long one," I said, and gave him nothing, and he gave me a discount anyway, because I'd sent him clients for six years and commerce remembers kindness longer than friendship does.

It was at the third shop — the general provisioner at the top of the bazaar, where I was loading cases of dal and ghee and the dense brick-like biscuits that constitute expedition happiness — that I saw it.

Or rather, I heard it. A voice at the counter ahead of me, clipped, lowland-accented, giving an order: *"Twenty days for five. Same ration scale as the Serrano standard, but add the chocolate — the real stuff, not the local compound. And we'll take both the primus stoves you have."*

Twenty days for five. Serrano standard rations. Both stoves.

I went very still over my crate of dal, in the way that I imagine a heron goes still over water, and listened to the rest of it. The voice belonged to a young man, thirty maybe, well-dressed for town in a way that read as money rather than sense, and he was ordering for a crew that was, per the shopkeeper's end of the conversation, staying at the guesthouse by the bus stand. They'd bought rope yesterday. Kerosene the day before. A large order of high-altitude tents, the four-season kind, the expensive kind.

Serrano rations. Twenty days. Five people.

Nobody runs a twenty-day Serrano provisioning plan as a tourist. Nobody buys four-season tents and double stoves for a trek. This was an expedition manifest, and it pointed in exactly one direction, and the direction was the same direction as the pencil lines currently folded against my ribs.

Reyes had said someone might come asking. He hadn't said who. He'd said, in fact, very little about it — just a tightening around his mouth when I asked why he'd waited so long, why the map had sat unclaimed for however many years it had sat, and a sentence I'd turned over all night: *"I wasn't the only one who knew it existed. I was only the one who knew where it was. Those aren't the same thing, girl, and the difference has kept me alive longer than I deserved."*

Kept him alive. Past tense, as of 4:17 this morning.

I finished my transaction slowly, watching the young man in the mirror behind the counter — the provisioner kept a mirror for security, and I kept using it for other purposes. He was lean, impatient, with his grandfather's jaw, if the dying man in the hospice bed had been any indication, though twenty years and yellowed skin make such comparisons unreliable. He signed the shop's register without reading it. He argued one price, won it, and didn't enjoy winning it, which told me the argument was habit rather than hunger.

When he left, I asked the shopkeeper, casually, who was buying for the guesthouse party.

"Outfitting a trek, madam. Big group." He shrugged. "Foreign-returned fellow. Grandfather was some kind of sahib explorer, they say. He shows everyone the old photographs."

Some kind of sahib explorer. I felt the floor tilt a degree and corrected, the way you correct on scree — weight low, move slow, pretend nothing happened.

Because here was the arithmetic, and I did it standing in that shop with a crate of dal in my arms while my pulse did something unprofessional: Tomas Reyes had a family. Families contain grandchildren. Grandchildren inherit maps — or halves of stories, or grievances, or the conviction that a disgraced man's cache belongs to the bloodline that survived. If Reyes senior had spent forty years knowing where Elias Okafor's last camp lay sealed under stone, then forty years is a long time to keep a secret from your own dinner table.

Someone else had the same destination. Someone else was buying both the stoves.

I carried my purchases out into the bright brutal morning and stood on the bazaar step and let myself feel, for exactly as long as it took to drink half a glass of salted butter tea from the stall across the way, the full weight of what I was actually doing. Then I folded the feeling up small, put it in the same inner pocket as the map, and started planning.

Five days of margin. A rival crew a day or two behind whatever head start I could arrange. A route neither of us had walked in decades, through country that ate expeditions for punctuation. And at the end of it, a chiseled E.O., and whatever my father had thought worth sealing behind stone with his own hands.

I needed Tsering. I needed him today, and I needed him to say yes to a trip I couldn't yet explain, which meant I needed to decide, walking back down the bazaar with the sun hammering the whitewashed walls, exactly how much of the truth a man could carry at those altitudes before it became its own kind of load.

Behind me, at the guesthouse by the bus stand, someone was packing both the stoves.

I walked faster.
