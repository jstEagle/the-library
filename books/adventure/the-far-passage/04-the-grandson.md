## 4. The Grandson

The outfitter's shop on Fort Road smelled of kerosene, canvas, and the particular ambition of men who buy gear for mountains they haven't met yet. I'd come in for rope — two hundred meters of static line, because the map showed a couloir crossing and couloirs eat dynamic cord like it was candy — and I was halfway through testing the sheath of a coil when the bell over the door rang and the temperature of my afternoon dropped four degrees.

I knew him before he said his name. Not from a photograph — Reyes had shown me none — but from the way he moved through the shop. He had the same economy as his grandfather, that same habit of touching things once, decisively, as if the world were an inventory he'd already memorized. He was maybe thirty, tall in the way that makes porters nervous until they learn you can carry your own weight, with a sunburn just healing at the cheekbones and a week of beard grown deliberately, the beard of a man who wants to look like he belongs here more than he does.

He bought carabiners. Six of them. Locking. The kind you buy when someone has told you about a fixed anchor.

I stood there holding my rope and did the arithmetic I'd been doing since dawn: the rival crew's purchases — the dehydrated milk, the high-altitude fuel, the crevasse bars bought in quantities that suggested a party of six planning to stay up high past the safe window. Someone else had the same destination. And now here was the someone, three feet away, choosing between steel and steel.

"You're Mara Okafor," he said.

Not a question. That told me he'd asked around, which meant he'd known who I was before I'd known he existed. In my experience, being researched is never a compliment.

"And you're spending money like the monsoon is a rumor," I said.

Something flickered across his face — surprise, then appreciation. He extended a hand. "Devan Reyes."

"I know." I didn't take the hand immediately. I made him hold it out there for a second, hanging in the dust motes, and I'm not proud of how good that felt. Then I took it, because my mother raised me better than my father's reputation deserved, and his grip was warm and dry and entirely without apology.

"Your grandfather died Tuesday," I said. "I'm sorry."

"Are you?" He smiled when he said it, and it was a good smile, the kind that gets strangers to tell you their seat numbers on trains. "Because from what I hear, you sat with him for six hours and left with something."

The shop went very quiet around us. The proprietor found urgent business with a box of crampon straps near the back wall. I set my rope down on the counter, slowly, giving myself time to decide whether to lie. I decided against it, mostly because lying to this one would be like lying to a mirror that had learned to talk back.

"He gave me a map," I said. "Did he give you one?"

"He gave me stories." Devan picked up one of his carabiners, turned it over, set it down again. "Thirty years of them. Every winter, the same ones, worn smooth like river stones. And every story ended the same way — with Elias Okafor walking away from eleven men on a mountain while they froze." He looked at me then, directly, and his eyes were his grandfather's eyes, and I understood that I was going to hate this man and possibly need him, in whichever order the mountain decided. "So no. He didn't leave me a map. He left me a debt."

"Eleven men," I repeated.

"Ten men and one woman." He said it flatly, but I saw the effort it cost him, the way you see the strain in a rope before it parts. "My grandfather carried the woman's name hardest. He'd say her name in his sleep sometimes, near the end. *Amara.* Amara Okafor."

The floor did not move. The shelves did not fall. I stood in an outfitter's shop in Leh with a stranger's hand still warm in mine and felt absolutely nothing, which is how I know now that everything had been hit at once — the way an avalanche buries you so completely that the first thing you feel is not grief but silence.

"That's my mother's name," I said.

"I know whose name it is." His voice gentled by a degree, and somehow that was worse than cruelty. "That's why I came to find you instead of waiting for you to find me."

* * *

We took our tea at a place down the road where the momos are honest and the walls are papered with decades of expedition posters, faded climbers grinning above captions promising glory. We took the corner table because Devan asked for it, and I noted that too — a man who controls sightlines is a man expecting trouble.

He ordered for both of us without asking what I wanted, and got it right, and I filed that away under *threats* rather than *charms*, though I'll admit the filing system wobbled.

"My grandfather spent his last month talking," he began. "Morphine loosens more than pain, apparently. He talked about the Serrano Range. About an icefall he called the Staircase. About a whiteout that lasted four days." Devan wrapped his hands around his glass though the tea was still hot enough to scald; I watched him do it and thought: this is a man who punishes himself in small ways, methodically. "He talked about Elias Okafor more than anyone. Sometimes with hatred. Once or twice—" he paused, choosing, "—with something else. Something that sounded almost like grief."

"Almost," I said.

"Almost." He nodded. "And here's what I know, Ms. Okafor, and I'll lay it all out because I think we're going to be more useful to each other honest than clever. My grandfather came down off that mountain alone. Eleven people stayed up there. The world believed his version — that your father turned back at the icefall, ordered everyone to continue, and walked out to save himself while the others waited for a rescue that never organized. My grandfather testified to it. Twice. There are transcripts."

"There are also graves," I said. "Or there aren't. Nobody ever recovered the bodies. Isn't that convenient for a sole survivor?"

"It's convenient for everyone," Devan agreed pleasantly. "Grief prefers a villain with a face. But my grandfather wasn't lying. I'd stake my life on it. He wept telling it. Men don't weep over lies they invented — they weep over lies they survived."

"Men weep over exactly those lies," I said. "It's the invention part that takes practice."

Our food arrived. Neither of us touched it. The posters grinned down at us, all those dead expeditions smiling their confidence.

"So here's my proposal," Devan said. He laid his hands flat on the table, palms down, a gesture so deliberately open it closed something in me instead. "You have a map. I have thirty years of detail — campsites, altitudes, the shape of the route as my grandfather walked it twice, once up and once down. Alone." He let that sit. "Neither of us finds anything without the other. So: we share the route. We travel separately, or together if you prefer, but we share information. Waypoints, conditions, hazards. And whatever we find at the end—"

"The prize," I said.

"—we split honestly. Whatever it is. Documents, effects, remains." His jaw tightened on that last word and released. "Half each. Our grandfathers' names cleared together or buried together."

"And if what we find clears one name and hangs the other?"

The question landed between us like a dropped tool. Devan looked at it for a long moment, and to his credit he didn't flinch away from it.

"Then we carry it down together," he said, "and we let it weigh what it weighs."

It was a good answer. It was such a good answer that I distrusted it instantly, the way you distrust a perfect foothold in rotten rock — the better it looks, the more you test it. Because here is what I knew that he didn't, what I'd been carrying since Tomas Reyes pressed that oilcloth packet into my hands with his dying fingers: the map had a second annotation, hidden in the margin fold, in my father's handwriting. A date. Three days after the abandonment. Which meant Elias Okafor had been alive up there for three days after eleven people supposedly froze, doing something, writing something, being somewhere — and neither of these men's stories accounted for it.

I could have told him. Some part of me wanted to, wanted to watch his certainty crack and see what was underneath. But a date is a lever, and levers are only useful if you're the one holding them, and twenty years of guiding had taught me that information shared is information surrendered.

"Share the route," I said. "Not the prize."

His eyebrows rose. "That's not quite what I proposed."

"No. You proposed splitting whatever we find fifty-fifty, which sounds fair and isn't, because fairness assumes both parties brought equal things to the mountain. You're bringing your grandfather's memory. I'm bringing mine. One of us is bringing a map." I held his gaze. "Route-sharing. Waypoints. Warnings. If either team gets in trouble, the other comes running — that part I'll sign in blood if you want. But whatever's in that cache belongs to whoever it belongs to, and we don't know yet who that is. Maybe it's both of us. Maybe it's neither. Maybe it's eleven families who've spent thirty years believing a lie."

"Whose lie?" he asked softly.

"That's what the mountain's going to tell us," I said. "Not you. Not me."

He sat back. He turned his glass a quarter-turn, studying me, and I watched him arrive at the same conclusion I'd arrived at watching him buy carabiners: this person cannot be managed, only negotiated with.

"All right," he said finally. "Route-sharing. Separate teams. And Okafor—" he stood, dropping rupees on the table with the same economy his grandfather had touched everything, "—when we're up there, and one of us learns something the other doesn't want to know? Remember that I offered you honesty first. Whatever you're carrying in that pocket of yours, it's going to get heavier before it gets lighter."

He put out his hand again. This time I took it without making him wait, because whatever else he was, he was right, and rightness deserves acknowledgment even when you resent its source.

"Three days behind you," he said at the door. "That's my crew's pace. I'll keep it. If you see us closer than that, it means we found something you need to know about."

"If I see you closer than that," I said, "it means I'm moving too slow."

He laughed — a real laugh, brief and surprised out of him — and then the bell rang and he was gone into the street noise, and I sat alone with two untouched plates of momos and the poster-grin of dead climbers and the weight in my pocket that he'd named without seeing it.

* * *

That night I spread the map on my hostel bed and weighted the corners with everything heavy I owned, and I looked at the second annotation for a long time. Three days after. My father alive three days after eleven people died around him. Doing what? Burying them? Digging for them? Writing?

I thought about Devan's face when he said *Amara*. He hadn't known. He'd carried the name as his grandfather's wound without knowing it was mine, and there had been nothing false in him when he offered honesty first. That was the trap of him, I decided. Distrust wearing a friendly face — and the friendly face might even be real, which made the distrust harder, not easier.

Outside, the first pre-monsoon clouds were stacking up over the Ladakh range like unpaid debts, and somewhere south of them a mountain held a tin box with my father's last words in it, and a man who loved his grandfather was packing to follow me there.

I folded the map along its old creases and began counting rope lengths.

Two hundred meters wouldn't be enough. It never is.
