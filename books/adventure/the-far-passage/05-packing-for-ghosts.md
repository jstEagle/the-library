## 5. Packing for Ghosts

Grief, I've learned, looks a lot like a spreadsheet.

By morning I had the expedition costed to the rupee: rations by calorie and gram, fuel by burn rate at altitude, rope by the meter. I did this in the back room of Aunty Dolma's guesthouse while her grandson watched cricket with the sound off and kept glancing at me like I might explode. Maybe I looked like someone about to explode. There's a particular kind of arithmetic you do when you don't want to feel anything, and I am very good at it.

The map lay under my notebook, face down, because every time I looked at it the numbers went strange in my head. Reyes's hand-drawn contour lines. The crease worn soft as cloth. My father's initials waiting for me somewhere past the icefall like a debt collector who'd finally found my address.

Twenty years of not thinking about Elias Okafor, undone by a dying man and a piece of paper.

So: logistics. Logistics were clean. Logistics didn't ask what I intended to say to a dead man's handwriting.

* * *

I spent two days provisioning, and if you've never provisioned obsessively, let me describe the condition. You buy rice twice because the first lot was the wrong grain — short-grain holds better at altitude, doesn't go to paste in the pot, and paste is demoralizing in ways that are hard to quantify but real. You weigh every item on a fish scale borrowed from a butcher who asks no questions because he's seen worse. You repack the duffels three times, shifting weight from left shoulder to right, from high in the pack to low against the spine, until the load sits on your body like it grew there.

Tsering would have called this prayer. Tsering says everything is prayer if you do it carefully enough. He once told me that about cleaning a kerosene stove, and I laughed, and he didn't, and now I can't clean a stove without thinking about it.

I bought too much sugar. I knew I was buying too much sugar. Sugar is morale, and morale was going to be the scarce commodity on this trip, mine more than anyone's. I bought dried apricots from the old woman near the mosque who always gives me the bruised ones free because she's decided I'm too thin, which is her theory about everyone she likes. I bought a second stove because a single point of failure is how expeditions die, and also because buying things felt like doing something.

What I was actually doing, of course, was not opening the map again. Not looking at the second annotation I'd found the night before, tucked into the margin where the contour lines crowded together — small letters, cramped, in a hand I hadn't seen since I was fourteen. But we'll get to that. Or rather, we won't, because I put the map away and bought more rope instead.

A man in the bazaar sold me a pressure cooker with a dented lid and swore it had crossed the Saser La four times. "Fifth time now," I said, and he grinned like we'd shared a joke, and maybe we had. Everything in Leh has crossed something. That's why people come here — to be at the edge of all the places you can't easily go, and to sell each other the equipment for failing gracefully.

* * *

The storage locker was behind the trekking agency, down an alley that smelled of juniper smoke and diesel, in a building whose ground floor had been a stable before it was anything else. I rented the locker the year after my father died. I paid for it for twenty years, which if you do the math — and I'm good at math when I'm avoiding things — comes to roughly the price of a small car, spent storing boxes I refused to open.

That's the thing nobody tells you about leaving. You don't leave clean. You leave a locker.

I went there on the third day, ostensibly to get my high-altitude tent, which was genuinely in there, along with two decades of gear I'd sworn off and somehow never discarded. The lock stuck the way it always stuck. You have to lift the door while turning the key, a trick my hands remembered even though the rest of me had been trying to forget the whole building.

Inside: dust with a geological history. Crates labeled in my younger handwriting, which was rounder, more hopeful. A coil of rope gone stiff as wire. My mother's sewing kit, which I'd packed in a fury at twenty-four and never touched since, because touching it would mean deciding what to feel about her, and I'd filed that decision under *later* so long ago that *later* had become a country I held citizenship in but never visited.

The tent was where I remembered it. I pulled it out, checked the poles, found one cracked and replaced it from the spares tube. Competence again. Armor again. I was getting dressed in it layer by layer, same as any climb.

And then I saw the tin.

It sat on the top shelf, back corner, behind a box of maps I'd meant to donate to the mountaineering library in 2009. A biscuit tin, the paint worn to bare metal along the edges, with a picture of a sailing ship still faintly visible on the lid. I knew it before I touched it. Some objects you know through your palms before your eyes confirm them, the way you know a stair that isn't where your foot expects.

My father's compass.

I had sold that compass. I was certain I had sold it. The summer after the funeral — after the inquest, after the newspapers, after the woman from the expedition committee said *these things happen on mountains, Mr. Okafor's judgment was* — I'd taken a box of his things to the dealer near the main gate and gotten, I remember precisely, four hundred rupees and a feeling of having committed a crime I couldn't name. The compass had been in that box. Brass case, hinged lid, the needle that wobbled slightly east of true and my father's voice saying *she lies a little, but only to strangers.*

Except here it was. Either the dealer had returned it — impossible; I'd never gone back, wouldn't have known him now — or I had never sold it at all, and the memory of selling it was itself one of the things I'd packed away in this room, mislabeled, the way grief mislabels everything. Twenty years of paying rent on a lie.

I stood there in the dust with the tin in both hands for longer than I want to admit. Long enough that the light through the single high window moved from one wall to the other. Long enough that somewhere below, the alley filled and emptied with voices twice.

Then I opened it.

The compass sat in its bed of cotton gone yellow, brass greened at the seams, the hinge stiff. Inside the lid, engraved in a script too fine for the wear it had survived:

**To E.O., who digs where others won't.**

From my mother. It had to be. She'd had it done in Delhi the winter before the expedition, hiding it from him like a child, and he'd carried it every season after and pretended not to be pleased and shown it to everyone. I remembered that. I remembered being nine and watching him show it to a visiting professor, tilting the lid so the lamplight caught the letters, and the professor laughing, and my father not quite managing to hide that he was lit up inside like a butter lamp.

I had sworn I'd sold this. I had built twenty years on the foundation of having sold it, the way you build a house on a rock you tell yourself is solid.

I closed the lid. I put it in the top of my pack, against the frame, where it wouldn't shift.

I did not decide to bring it. Let's be clear about that. Deciding implies a moment of choice, a fork, a weighing. What happened was simpler and worse: my hands put it in the pack the way they'd lifted the door for the key, on memory alone, while the rest of me stood in the dust pretending to be a person who had options.

Inheritance doesn't ask permission. It just arrives, usually against your will, usually in a tin.

* * *

Tsering came to the guesthouse that evening without being summoned, which was his way. He appeared in the courtyard carrying two glasses of salt tea and the expression of a man who has already made up his mind and is only waiting for you to catch up.

"You need porters," he said. Not a question. Tsering Dorje — no relation to my Dorje, the mountains are full of Dorjes, half of Ladakh answers to it — had been my lead porter for eleven years. He was fifty-one, built like the root system of something that survives winters, and he had carried loads over passes that made experienced guides turn around and write books about their feelings instead.

"Late season," I said.

"Yes."

"Monsoon's early. Everyone in town says so."

"Everyone in town says many things." He handed me a glass of tea and sat on the step across from me, unhurried, the way water is unhurried. "You bought too much sugar."

"I know."

"Two stoves."

"A single point of failure—"

"—is how expeditions die. Yes." He drank his tea. Somewhere beyond the wall, the call to prayer braided itself into the sound of a motorbike. "Who else have you asked?"

"Nobody yet."

"Nobody yet," he repeated, tasting the phrase. "Mara-ji. Eleven years I work for you. In eleven years you have never once needed to ask me twice, and you have never once asked me to carry something you would not carry yourself. So I will tell you what I see, and you can tell me I'm wrong." He set the glass down. "You are going somewhere you have been before. Not with your feet. With something older than feet. And you did not sleep last night, or the night before, and you bought sugar like a woman feeding an army of ghosts."

I looked at my tea. Salt tea gives you nothing to hide behind; it's too honest a drink.

"The fee's good," I said. "Hazard rate. Full partnership split on the bonus."

"Mm," said Tsering, which in his language meant *you're lying, and I'm letting you, and we both know what that costs.* He let the silence sit between us until it had weight, then picked up his glass again. "When do we leave?"

"Day after tomorrow. Four porters besides you. I'll interview—"

"Dorje. The young one, with the bad knee he pretends he doesn't have. And Pema, if his wife allows it — speak to her yourself, she trusts you more than she trusts him, which is correct. Namgyal for cook. That's three plus me. You wanted five?"

"Four."

"Three is enough. Five is a crowd above the moraine." He stood, joints announcing themselves the way honest joints do, and collected his glass. At the doorway he stopped, the way he always stopped, with his back to me, delivering the sentence he'd actually come to deliver.

"Whatever you are carrying in that pack besides rope," he said, "decide soon whether it is cargo or ballast. Cargo you land with. Ballast you throw overboard when the river rises." Then he was gone into the blue evening, and I sat there understanding that he had seen the shape of the tin through the canvas, or simply through me, and that lying to Tsering was like lying to a barometer.

* * *

That night I repacked everything a fourth time, and when I got to the tin I took it out and set it on the floor beside my sleeping bag and told myself I was checking its weight.

It weighed almost nothing. That was the unbearable part. Twenty years of rent, a sold-or-not-sold memory, a childhood rearranged around a disgrace — and the object at the center of it all weighed less than a mug.

I opened the lid one more time. The needle swung, settled, wobbled slightly east of true. *She lies a little,* my father used to say, tapping the glass, *but only to strangers.* I'd thought it was a joke when I was nine. Sitting on the floor of a guesthouse room at thirty-eight, holding the thing, I finally heard it properly. Compasses don't lie. They just point where the iron is. All that wobbling east — some buried seam of metal in the casing, some flaw in the making, some small dishonesty baked into the instrument at birth that it could never stop confessing to anyone patient enough to watch.

I thought about Reyes's map, face-down in my notebook. I thought about the cramped marginal note in a hand I'd know anywhere, the one I was not going to look at again until I absolutely had to. I thought about a man who dug where others wouldn't, and what the world had decided that made him, and whether the digging had been the crime or the courage, and whether those had ever really been different things.

The needle trembled. Pointing, as always, at whatever iron was nearest.

I closed the tin, packed it deep against the frame, and lay down in the dark listening to Leh settle around me — dogs, diesel, the last motorbikes, a wind coming off the mountains that already smelled faintly of rain, weeks too soon.

Day after tomorrow, then. Three porters and a barometer who could read me through canvas, heading into a range that had eaten my family once already, carrying a compass that lied to strangers and a map I couldn't stop not looking at.

Sleep came eventually, the way weather does. And just before it did, I caught myself doing the thing I'd sworn off for twenty years: measuring the distance to the pass, not in miles, but in days of my father's life I'd never accounted for.

The math didn't balance. It never had. That was the whole problem, and I was walking straight into it with too much sugar and a dead man's brass heart beating quietly against my spine.
