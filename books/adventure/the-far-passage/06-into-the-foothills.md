## 6. Into the Foothills

There is a particular hour on the first morning of an expedition when everyone still believes the trip will go exactly as planned. The porters are fresh, the rope coils are symmetrical, and nobody has yet discovered which of their blisters will become a defining feature of their personality. I live for that hour. It's the last honest one.

We left the roadhead at six, five of us: me, Tsering, Dorje, Namgyal, and young Pemba, who had signed on because Tsering vouched for him and because Pemba would have signed on to carry freight across a lava field if Tsering asked him to. Behind us, a day back in Leh or thereabouts, Devan Reyes was doing whatever it is rival salvage crews do — buying more rope than they need, arguing about satellite phones, telling themselves stories about why they're really here.

I told myself stories too. Mine were better produced.

The route out of the valley climbed through terraces the color of old coins — barley going gold at the edges, poplars dropping their first yellow leaves into irrigation channels that have been running since before anyone thought to write down who dug them. A woman winnowing grain watched us pass with the flat professional interest of someone who sees expeditions leave every autumn and knows, without needing to be told, which ones come back thin.

"Good morning," I said in Ladakhi.

"Where are you going?" she asked, which is both a greeting and an audit.

"Up," I said.

She looked at our loads, at the late light on the peaks, and gave me the look that woman has probably given a hundred guides over a hundred years, the one that says *the mountain doesn't care about your schedule*. Then she went back to her grain. There's no arguing with that kind of wisdom, so I didn't try.

* * *

By midday we'd cleared the terraces and entered the country of scree and juniper stumps where the trail stops being a trail and becomes a series of suggestions. I set the pace deliberately slow. First days are for lying to your body gently — convincing it this is all quite reasonable, nothing dramatic, just a walk with luggage.

Tsering walked beside me, which he only does when he wants something or when he's worried. With Tsering those two categories overlap almost completely.

"You checked the weather report three times before breakfast," he said.

"I like to be thorough."

"You checked it twice more at the tea stall."

"The tea stall has better signal."

"Mara." He used my name the way other people use a crowbar. "What is different about this trip?"

The honest answer was *everything*: the map sewn into the lining of my pack, the dead man's promise folded around it, the name Okafor sitting in my mouth like a stone I couldn't spit out. What I said was, "Late season. Monsoon's unpredictable. I want options."

Tsering nodded slowly, the way a man nods when he's filing something away rather than accepting it. He's been my lead porter for eleven years. He has carried my tent, my rope, my broken wrist off a face in Zanskar, and my silences, and he has opinions about all four. He let it go, which from Tsering is not letting it go at all — it's choosing the terrain on which he'll bring it back up.

We made camp at 3,800 meters beside a stream the color of glacier milk. Dorje got the stove going with the efficiency of a man who considers fire a personal responsibility. Pemba fetched water and came back with his sleeves soaked and a grin suggesting he'd fallen in on purpose, which knowing Pemba was possible. Namgyal, our quietest load-carrier, put up the kitchen tent without being asked, as he does every trip, as he has done so consistently for so long that I genuinely could not tell you what his voice sounds like at volume.

I ate my dal and did what I always do after eating: ran the ledger. Distances, altitudes, load weights, calories, the burn rate of fuel against the days I'd budgeted. It's a form of prayer, arithmetic. You ask the numbers a question and they answer honestly, which is more than most things do.

The numbers said we'd reach the moraine field in nine days if the weather held. The numbers also said the weather was not going to hold, because the monsoon was already two weeks early down south and climbing north like it had somewhere to be. I wrote *options* in my notebook, underlined it twice, and understood that I was underlining a lie.

* * *

On the second day we crossed the first ridge and dropped into a valley so green it looked apologetic about it. Willows along the river. A shepherd's hut with its door propped open by a stone, empty until August's end. Chortens at the valley mouth, whitewashed, their painted eyes watching us pass with the mild patience of things that have seen every kind of leaving.

That's what this country is, I've come to understand. It's the geography of departure. Every route out of every village here points up and away, toward passes that take people places and sometimes don't give them back. The villages sit at the bottom of it all like punctuation at the end of sentences nobody finished.

I grew up in a different range entirely, but it was the same sentence. My father used to say mountains were libraries, and I used to think that was the kind of thing archaeologists say instead of saying anything true. Now I know he meant it literally: everything gets recorded up there, in ice and scree and cairn-stone, whether you want it recorded or not. The mountain keeps minutes of all meetings.

We passed a cairn at the valley fork, old ones, stacked hip-high by hands long gone. Pemba added a stone, as you do. I added one too, and hated myself a little for how much the gesture cost me, because adding a stone to a cairn is a small thing unless the last time you stood at a cairn you were twenty-four years old and furious at a dead man, and you threw your stone down in the dirt instead, and you have been walking ever since as though the walking were the point.

"Day behind us?" Tsering asked quietly, meaning Devan.

"A day, maybe less. They're carrying more fuel. They'll move slower on the climb but faster on the flats."

"And when we meet them?"

"We won't. Not above the snowline."

He looked at me for a long moment. "You say that like a plan. It sounds like a wish wearing a plan's coat."

I laughed despite myself. Eleven years, and he still finds new ways to tell me I'm full of it. "Eat your rice, Tsering."

"I am eating my rice," he said, eating his rice, "and I am also right."

* * *

The third day we gained the shoulder below the first real pass, and the terraced world fell away beneath us — the barley fields shrinking to postage stamps, the river becoming a bright thread someone had pulled loose from the landscape. From up here you can see the whole architecture of leaving: the trails braiding out of each village, converging, splitting again, all of them pointed at the white horizon like iron filings answering a magnet.

Devan's crew was visible once, mid-afternoon, as a line of dots on the opposite slope, moving with the deliberate rhythm of people who know they're being watched. Through binoculars I counted six. One of them stopped and raised something — binoculars of his own, probably, or a camera, or possibly just a hand. Hard to say at four kilometers. I lowered mine before I could find out.

"They're not trying to hide," Dorje observed, coming up beside me.

"No."

"That's either confidence or a message."

"It's both. Confidence usually is."

Dorje grunted. He's a man of few observations and strong ones. He'd taken one look at my route plan on day one and said, "You've built in no rest days," and when I said the schedule wouldn't allow it, he'd said, "Schedules never do. Legs do," and then shouldered his load anyway, because Dorje's philosophy is that complaints are free and obedience is cheap, and between the two of them a man can get most places.

We camped that night at 4,300 meters, high enough that the stars came down close enough to lean on. I took first watch, which nobody argues with; taking first watch is a guide's prerogative and also her way of avoiding sleep, which is a skill I've honed to a professional standard over twenty years.

The camp slept. The wind moved through the junipers downslope with a sound like pages turning. And somewhere in the middle of that watch, or later, or in the shallow fold of sleep that comes after — I dreamed.

* * *

In the dream I was small again, kneeling on the floor of a dig house in a country whose name changes depending on which decade you consult the map. The floor was packed earth, cool through my knees. In front of me, my father knelt too, and in front of him lay a pot — a cooking vessel, third millennium, its rim chipped, its belly whole.

He wasn't using brushes. That's what the dream got exactly right, the detail no photograph ever captured. Everyone imagines archaeologists with little soft brushes, dusting delicately. But Elias Okafor in the final inch above a find used his fingers. Bare fingertips, moving in short strokes, flicking the soil away grain by grain, reading the pot through his skin. He said you could feel where the clay changed temper, feel the ghost of the potter's thumbprint, feel whether the vessel had been mended in antiquity — a crack sealed with resin, someone's economy, someone's love of a thing too useful to throw away.

"Slow," he said in the dream, not looking up. "The ground gives it back at its own speed. You can't hurry it. If you hurry, you don't get the pot. You get pieces."

"What if there isn't time?" I asked, and my voice in the dream was my adult voice, twenty years older than my knees.

"There's always time," he said. "There's just less of it than you planned for."

His hands kept moving. Soil falling away in fine dark lines. And underneath, emerging not gradually but suddenly, the way truth does — the whole curve of the pot, unbroken, holding its shape after four thousand years underground, holding the shape of the hands that made it.

I woke with cold feet and the taste of earth in my mouth, and sat up in the dark tent with my heart going like a trekking pole on loose rock.

Outside, the wind had died. The mountains stood against the stars, black on blacker, and I sat there a long time doing the thing I do instead of feeling: calculating. Nine days to the moraine. Fourteen to the cache, if the annotation was accurate. Sixteen to eighteen before the passes closed, if the monsoon stayed polite, which it had shown no sign of doing.

Somewhere below and behind us, Devan Reyes was asleep with half a story in his pocket. Somewhere ahead, under forty years of stone, my father's hands had stopped moving mid-gesture, and the ground had never given him back.

*Not gradually,* I thought. *Suddenly. The way truth does.*

I woke Tsering for his watch and told him nothing, and he looked at my face and asked nothing, and we were both lying by mutual agreement, which is the closest thing to trust some partnerships ever achieve.

* * *

The fourth morning brought the river.

It had been a stream on the way up in my planning — a knee-deep braid across gravel flats, an inconvenience, fifteen minutes. Overnight it had become a brown argument, swollen with melt off a peak I couldn't see, running fast enough to hum. I stood on the bank and watched a boulder the size of a washing machine shift its weight downstream and understood that the mountain had opened negotiations ahead of schedule.

"Too fast to ford loaded," Tsering said. He'd arrived at my shoulder without making a sound, as usual.

"Too fast to ford unloaded."

"So we go up. Find the braid."

"Adds three hours."

"Adds dry legs," said Dorje, "and keeps the food barrels attached to living porters."

We went up. The valley narrowed, the banks steepened, and after an hour of scrambling we found a section where the river split around a midstream island and reassembled itself with slightly less conviction. I crossed first with the rope, anchored it, and ferried the loads across on a tension line while the others waded the shallower channel in relays, linked arm to arm, leaning into the current at the angle you learn young and never forget.

Pemba went down to his waist at the deepest point and came up laughing, which is either courage or a wiring fault. Namgyal crossed without a drop above his knees, somehow, and declined to explain himself. Dorje carried the heaviest barrel and swore at the river continuously in three languages, none of them fluently, all of them effectively.

And then, at the far bank, Tsering did the count and came up one barrel short.

We found it fifty meters downstream, wedged against a boulder, half-submerged — the food barrel with the rice and half the lentils. By the time Dorje and I reached it, working our way along the bank on a rope, the water had gotten inside. Rice swells. Lentils swell. We hauled it out, drained it, spread what we could save on tarps in the weak sun, and did the arithmetic standing over the wet wreckage like mourners at a very small funeral.

"We lose two days of margin," I said. "We eat tighter. It's manageable."

Nobody argued, which is its own kind of data. Teams argue when they trust the plan. Silence means they're trusting you instead, and there's a difference, and the difference has a shelf life.

As we repacked, I looked back upstream the way we'd come, and there — far off, on the trail we'd abandoned that morning, on the far bank where the original crossing was — a line of figures had appeared. Six dots, patient, watching our detour the way you watch another player's move across a board.

A day behind. Maybe less now. The river was slowing us down, and it was slowing them down equally, and neither of those facts was going to stay true forever.

Tsering appeared beside me and followed my gaze. He didn't say anything. He just stood there with me in the wind off the water, two men and a river and a race, and then he picked up his load and started walking, and I followed him up into the country where the leaving becomes the whole landscape — higher, colder, closer every day to the place where the ground remembers my family better than I do.

That night I didn't dream of pottery. I dreamed of a date written in pencil on the corner of a map, three days after eleven people stopped writing anything down, and I woke before dawn with my notebook already open, checking the arithmetic again, as if the numbers might have changed their minds overnight.

They hadn't. They never do. That's what I love about them, and it's getting harder, lately, to say what I love about anything else.
