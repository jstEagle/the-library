## 7. Tsering's Question

Tsering waited until the fire was down to coals and the porters were asleep to ask me the question I'd been dreading since Leh.

We were camped in a stand of stunted birch above the second valley, the kind of campsite that guidebooks describe as "sheltered" and guides describe as "adequate." The terraced fields of the last village were a day behind us, gold-green squares stitched up the hillside like a quilt made by someone with more patience than I would ever have. Ahead, the Serrano Range had begun showing its teeth — white summits over grey shoulders, the kind of mountains that don't smile first.

Tsering sat across the fire from me, mending a strap with a needle he kept threaded at all times, the way some men keep a knife. He'd been mending that same strap for two days. I suspected the strap was fine and the mending was for his hands, something to do while he watched me.

"You paid us well," he said.

"I pay everyone well. It's my one virtue."

"Too well." He pulled the thread through, unhurried. "Dorje says you doubled the season rate. Pemba says you didn't haggle. You always haggle. Last year you haggled with me over the price of tea."

"That tea was terrible."

"The tea was fine. You just enjoy it." He looked up. His face in the firelight was all planes and patience, a face built for waiting out weather. "So. Why are we really here?"

I had prepared answers for this question the way I prepare rope anchors: several options, each tested, none load-bearing alone. There was the fee story — a rich client, a salvage contract, a map bought from a dying man's estate, nothing personal. There was the science story — an archaeological survey, my father's old routes, academic interest. There was even, held in reserve for emergencies, a version of the truth trimmed so small it was practically a lie anyway.

"Tomas Reyes," I said. "The prospector who died in Leh. He left a map. A client wants what's on it, and the client pays for discretion as well as competence."

It came out smooth. That was the problem with lying well — you could hear yourself doing it, the way you can hear your own heartbeat when you're scared.

Tsering threaded the needle again. He did not say *you're lying*. He did not say *Reyes's estate sold nothing; I asked around in Leh too*. He said, "Ah," which is the Sherpa equivalent of a door closing quietly in your face.

He let the lie stand there between us, upright and unsupported, like a cairn someone built as a joke.

I want to be clear about what that costs. If he'd called it — if he'd said, *Mara, I have carried your rope for eleven years, tell me the truth* — I could have deflected, laughed, given him a piece of something real and called it enough. But he didn't call it. He simply accepted my answer with the courtesy you extend to a stranger, and the courtesy itself was the accusation. Intimacy, I've learned, is not people knowing things about you. It's people being able to ask, and you being able to answer, and both of those doors staying open. Tsering had just closed his half of the door very gently, so as not to wake the porters.

"Sleep," he said, folding the strap away. "River tomorrow. Early."

* * *

The river had been a knee-deep riffle when I scouted this route three years ago, in September, when the glaciers were polite. Now it was the last week of June and the melt was running weeks hot, and the river had opinions.

We heard it before we saw it — a low continuous roar, like a crowd that never stops talking. Then the birch thinned and there it was: a grey-brown torrent the color of wet concrete, maybe forty meters across, carrying boulders along its bed with a grinding sound I felt through my boots. Glacial flour suspended in the water gave it an opacity you could pour. You could not see ten centimeters down into it. You could not see the bottom at all.

"Charming," said Dorje.

"It gets worse every year," Pemba said. "My uncle says these rivers used to sleep later in the morning. Now they're awake by dawn, shouting."

Everything about the water said *wait*. Every instinct I had, honed across two decades of high-altitude work, said wait — wait for the cold hours before dawn when meltwater drops, cross then or not at all. But we'd lost time in Leh, and Devan Reyes's crew was somewhere behind us, and the monsoon forecasts were already using words like *early* and *aggressive*, and waiting meant giving the mountain another night to change its mind about us.

"We cross now," I said. "Rope traverse. Fastest swimmer goes first with the light line, we rig the heavy rope, loads go across clipped to the pulley. Nobody touches the water without a harness. Questions?"

"How deep is it?" Dorje asked.

"Deeper than your courage and shallower than your mouth. Let's go."

Tsering took the first line without being asked, because Tsering always took the first anything without being asked. He stripped to his base layer, tied into the light line, and stood at the bank reading the current the way other men read scripture — lips moving slightly, finding the verse that applied. Then he went in.

I will not pretend it looked easy. The river hit him like a debt collector. He swam ferociously, diagonally, letting the current carry him downstream toward the eddy behind the mid-channel boulder I'd picked, and for thirty seconds he was only a head in brown water, and then a hand, and then he was hauling himself onto the far shingle, gasping, alive, already coiling the line.

"Good?" I called.

"Wet!" he called back, which from Tsering was a full comedy routine.

We worked. This is the part of guiding I actually love, the part nobody puts in brochures: four people becoming one machine. Pemba anchored, Dorje fed rope, I rigged the pulley and ran loads. Packs went across dry. The stove went across dry. For ninety minutes the system sang, and I let myself think, briefly, that I might get through a chapter of this journey without the mountain taking a tax.

Then the food barrel.

It was the big aluminum one, sixty liters, packed with the bulk of our rations because I'm a professional and professionals consolidate their failure points. We slung it into the pulley system, and Dorje and Pemba pushed it off the bank, and it swung out over the water —

— and the current, which had been rising all morning the way glacial rivers do, invisible and remorseless, chose that moment to take the boulder.

Not the whole boulder. Just the top of it, the part that had been making our eddy. I saw the boil behind the rock flatten and spread and knew, with the sick clarity you get maybe twice a decade, exactly what was going to happen about four seconds before it happened.

The barrel swung wide, the line went slack as the eddy died, and the river took it. Not dramatically. That was the awful thing — no crash, no splash you could point to. The water simply folded around the barrel and it was gone, downstream, forty kilos of rice, dal, oil, and tsampa, traveling at the speed of regret.

Nobody spoke. The rope hummed in the current. Somewhere upstream, a serac calved with a sound like a door slamming in a very large house.

"Pemba," I said finally. "Inventory. What else is food?"

Pemba inventoried. Pemba is the kind of man who counts everything twice and writes it down once, and the news was what I expected: we had lost perhaps forty percent of our calories. Enough to continue if we moved fast and ate thin. Not enough to continue if anything else went wrong, and something else always goes wrong, because that is the arrangement mountains insist upon.

"We resupply at the next village," I said. "Two days ahead. We eat carefully until then."

Dorje looked at the river. Tsering, dripping on the far bank, looked at me across it, and even at forty meters of roaring water I could feel him not asking the question again. *Why are we really here? Is whatever you're chasing worth forty percent of our food?*

"Barrels float," Dorje said gloomily. "Somewhere downriver there is a very well-fed bear."

"Let him have it," Pemba said. "Better him than the monsoon."

We got the rest of the loads across, then the men, then me — last, always last, a rule I've never broken and once nearly drowned keeping. The water was colder than the altitude suggested, snowmelt straight from the glacier's throat, and it tried to fold me the way it had folded the barrel, and I fought it diagonally toward the dead eddy and came out on the shingle coughing and shaking and profoundly grateful to be holding a rope instead of being cargo.

Tsering handed me a towel without comment. That's the thing about him — he keeps a towel ready the way he keeps a needle threaded. Preparedness as a form of love.

We walked. The trail climbed away from the river through juniper scrub, and the roar faded behind us until it was just a rumor, and then not even that. By late afternoon we'd gained the shoulder of the valley and could see the country ahead: a long grey rampart of moraine, and beyond it, faint in the haze, the white wall of the range proper, and somewhere in the folds below it, a village where an old woman might still remember a name I wished she didn't.

I hadn't told them that either. I hadn't told them that the route Reyes drew passed within a day's walk of the place where eleven men — twelve, counting my father — had stopped coming home. I hadn't told them that I grew up on stories about this exact stretch of ice, told by a man who wasn't in most of them anymore.

At camp, Tsering cooked thin. He made the portions look generous, which is a skill, and he gave me the largest bowl, which is a message. I ate it and watched him watch the fire.

"The barrel," he said, not looking up. "In eleven years, you've lost a barrel?"

"Once. A yak stepped on it."

"And today?"

"Today the river changed its mind."

"Mm." He stirred the pot. "Rivers do that. People also." He ladled himself a portion, small, leaving the rest for the porters, and sat back on his heels. "In Leh, I asked about Tomas Reyes. Everyone knew him. Old prospector, no family they knew of, dying slow in the hospice by the mosque. Strange man to leave a map to a client."

"He was strange. Dying men often are."

"Yes." He ate a spoonful. Chewed. The pause stretched long enough to have its own weather. "Your client must trust you very much. To send you into the Serrano in a short season, with a rival crew behind you, over a route nobody has done in twenty years."

"My client trusts my competence."

"Of course." He nodded slowly, accepting this the way he'd accepted the lie the night before — completely, and therefore not at all. "Competence. That is why you check your father's compass every morning before anyone else is awake. For competence."

I hadn't known he'd seen that. The compass rides in the top lid of my pack, wrapped in a sock, and I take it out at dawn because twenty years of habit is stronger than twenty years of resolve, and apparently I am not as alone at dawn as I believed.

"Tsering—"

"No." He raised a hand, gentle. "You don't have to tell me. I only want you to know that I know that you haven't. There is a difference, and the difference matters, up there." He nodded toward the white wall, invisible now in the dark but present the way a debt is present. "Above base camp, secrets get heavy. Heavier than barrels. And we have already lost one barrel."

He banked the fire and went to his tent, and I sat alone with the coals and the compass in its sock and the honest arithmetic of the thing: I had lied to the one man whose respect I couldn't afford to lose, and he had forgiven me in advance, which is somehow harder to carry than anger.

Ahead lay the village, and the old woman, and whatever she would spit at me or hand me. Behind lay a river that had taken its tax and a crew a day back who wanted the same door I wanted. Above lay the icefall, and under the icefall, sealed in a crevice with my father's initials chiseled beside it, the truth about who abandoned whom.

I checked the compass one more time, though the needle had nowhere to point but north and I had nowhere to go but up.

Tomorrow, the village that remembers.
