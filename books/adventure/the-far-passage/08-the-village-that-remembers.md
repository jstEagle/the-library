## 8. The Village That Remembers

The village announced itself the way high villages do: first as a rumor of smoke against the scree, then as a smell — juniper and dung fires and the particular sour-sweetness of barley drying on rooftops — and finally as a scatter of flat-roofed houses stacked into the hillside like stones a giant had been too tired to finish arranging.

"Rinchenling," Tsering said, and spat, not from contempt but from habit. High altitude dries the mouth; spitting is just geography made audible. "Two hours. We should buy barley flour if they have it to sell. And butter."

"We have butter."

"We have your butter," Dorje said from behind me, which I chose to interpret as a compliment to my provisioning rather than an insult to my taste.

We came down out of the moraine's shoulder in the gold light of late afternoon, five of us and eleven mules' worth of gear between two borrowed ponies, and the village received us the way villages receive strangers who arrive with money and no kinship ties: politely, watchfully, and with an immediate recalculation of prices. A woman selling apricots looked at our boots, our ropes, the satellite phone clipped to my pack strap like a tumor of the future, and raised her apricot price by a third before we'd said a word. I respected that. In her position I'd have done the same.

The headman's name was Norbu, or he introduced himself as Norbu, which may have been his name or simply the local equivalent of *yes, I am the one you negotiate with*. He was perhaps sixty, built like the terrain itself — all angles and endurance — and he installed us in a guest room above the animal byre with the efficiency of a man who had done this many times and enjoyed none of it.

"You go where?" he asked, while his daughter laid out tea that could have stripped rust.

I gave him the honest version, which is to say the vague one. "Up-valley. Toward the old glacier. Survey work."

He nodded at the word *survey* the way people nod at weather reports for a country they'll never visit. Then his eyes went to Tsering, found a countryman, and softened by a degree. They spoke for a few minutes in rapid Ladakhi, and I caught enough — I always catch enough, it's the occupational hazard of being the only outsider in a tent — to know that Tsering was vouching for me and that Norbu was accepting the voucher the way you accept a check from a stranger: hoping it clears.

Dinner was tsampa and more tea. The room smelled of juniper smoke and the animals below, and I slept better than I had any right to, which should have warned me.

* * *

Morning brought the errand I'd been putting off since Reyes put the map in my hands in that Leh hospice that smelled of camphor and dying.

The map needed water, meat, and local knowledge, and local knowledge in these valleys lives in old women. Not because men don't hold it, but because men die younger and talk less, and because the women are the ones who bury everyone and therefore remember where everything is. My father taught me that. One of the few things he taught me that didn't come wrapped in ambition.

So I took the map — folded now inside a plastic sleeve, though I still hadn't shown it to anyone — and climbed the crooked lane to the house Norbu's daughter pointed me toward, where lived an old woman named, I was told, Ashi Dolma, who had been a girl when the survey parties used to come through, and who remembered everything.

The house was two rooms and a roof terrace crowded with drying chilies. She was small and upright, wrapped in a padded coat even indoors, sitting by the window where the light came in sideways, doing something patient with wool. Her eyes were the pale brown that some high-altitude elders get, the color of weak tea, and they went over me the way a customs officer goes over luggage.

"Tashi delek," I said, and offered the kata scarf I'd bought in Leh for exactly this purpose, white silk, ceremonial, the universal currency of respect.

She took it. That was something. She motioned me onto the bench across from her and poured tea without asking whether I wanted any, which in this country is not rudeness but law.

"You are the guide," she said. Her Hindi was accented but fluent. "Tsering's party."

"I am. Mara Okafor."

And there it is. Twenty years I've carried that name up and down mountains, and mostly it means nothing to anyone — Okafor is a Nigerian name, and the Serrano Range is about as far from Nigeria as a name can get without falling off the planet. Mostly people hear it, file it under *foreign*, and move on to the business of haggling.

Ashi Dolma did not move on.

Her hands stopped in the wool. Her face did something complicated and slow, like ice shifting on a lake — no single crack, just a general settling of weight — and then she looked at me again, properly this time, the customs officer finding the false bottom in the suitcase.

"Okafor," she said. "The archaeologist."

"My father," I said, because lying to old women in their own houses is a debt you can't pay back.

She spat.

Not dramatically. Not with performance. Just turned her head slightly toward the door and spat onto the packed earth floor, the way you'd spit after tasting something spoiled, and then went back to her wool as if I were already leaving.

I sat there. The tea steamed between us. Somewhere outside a donkey expressed opinions about its life.

"My grandfather," she said at last, to the wool rather than to me, "was Sonam Paljor. He was a porter. On the expedition." Another turn of the wool. "You say that name in this valley, you will eat alone."

I want to tell you I said something wise. I want to report that I met her grief with grace, that I asked the right questions in the right order, that twenty years of guiding other people through hard places had taught me how to sit inside someone else's pain without fidgeting.

What I actually did was nothing. I sat on the bench in a stranger's kitchen wearing my expensive boots and my dead father's surname, and let the silence do what silence does, which is make everything true.

"He was thirty-one," she said. "My grandmother was pregnant with my mother. He went up with the sahibs to carry loads past the icefall, and he did not come down, and nobody came to tell us anything. We learned it from other porters' families. Word travels faster than apology." She set the wool down. Her hands, I noticed, were perfectly steady. It was her voice that had gone thin. "The expedition said your father abandoned them. Eleven men. Left them above the icefall and walked down with the ones who could keep up. That is the story. That has been the story for fifty years. When the sahibs' books were printed, it was in the books."

"I know the story," I said.

"Do you." It wasn't a question. It was a wall.

"Ashi Dolma-ji—"

"Don't." The word came out flat and final, a stone laid on a grave. "You will not *ji* me. You drink my tea and wear your father's face and come asking about my grandfather's mountain, and you think I don't know why you're here?"

I hadn't known why I was here. Or rather, I'd known, and I'd filed it under logistics, the way I file everything — route, resupply, permits, weather — because filing things under logistics keeps them from bleeding. But looking at her face, at the pale eyes that had watched this valley for eighty years, I understood that I had come here precisely because of her. Because somewhere under the maps and the mileage I'd known that the route to my father ran through the people he'd broken, and I'd wanted — God help me — their blessing. Their absolution, pre-purchased, like the kata scarf.

"The cache," I said quietly. "There's supposed to be a supply cache above the icefall. My father's. With documents."

Something moved behind her eyes. Not softening. Recalculation.

"The depot," she said. "The men talked about a depot. Food and fuel, left for the return. My grandfather's load was kerosene tins." Her jaw worked. "They never reached it. That is what the story says. They never reached it, because the man who knew where it was left them to die."

"And where—" My voice cracked on the second word and I had to start over. "Where did it happen? The abandonment. Do you know?"

She looked at me for a long time. Outside, the donkey had found peace or exhaustion. A child laughed somewhere down the lane, that particular unguarded laughter that only exists in places too high for irony.

"Not far," she said finally. "A day ahead of you. Maybe two, the way you walk." She picked the wool back up, and I understood I was being dismissed by someone who had been dismissing important men her whole life. "The mountain is right there, guide. You can't miss it. Nobody who went up it ever missed anything. That was the problem."

I stood. Put a fold of rupees under the tea bowl, which she ignored so completely that I took it back, because some transactions aren't available.

At the door I stopped. "For what it's worth — and I know what it's worth, which is nothing — I'm not here to defend him. I'm here to find out what's true."

"The truth," Ashi Dolma said, not looking up, "is also buried up there. You dig it out carefully, guide. It cuts going down."

* * *

Tsering found me an hour later sitting on the roof terrace among the chilies, doing an excellent impression of a person studying route notes and a poor impression of a person whose entire inheritance had just been spat on.

He sat down across from me without asking, which is one of the ten thousand reasons I'd hire him forever. He waited. Tsering's silences are load-bearing structures; you can build a whole conversation on them.

"She knew the name," I said eventually.

"Mm."

"Her grandfather was one of the eleven."

"Mm," Tsering said again, and then, because he is capable of mercy: "In 1974 my uncle carried for a German expedition. Two sahibs died. The Germans wrote a book saying the porters panicked and caused it. My uncle read the book. He died still angry about it." He poured himself tea from the thermos. "Books are written by the ones who come down. You know this."

"I know this."

"So." He drank. "Now you climb toward a place where your name is a wound, carrying papers that might be a knife or might be medicine, and you won't tell me which."

"It's not that I won't—"

"Mara." He said my name the way he says *crevasse*: once, clearly, meaning it. "The team crossed the river yesterday because you told them the fee was good. Dorje has a daughter in school. Pemba is saving for a truck. They believe you. I let them believe you, because I believed you." He set down the cup. "Old woman spit on your family name today. Something in you jumped. I saw it. Whatever you're carrying up this valley, it isn't cargo."

Below us, the village went about its evening — smoke rising straight in the windless cold, children being called home in escalating volumes, a radio playing film songs to an audience of yaks. Life, conducted at five thousand meters, indifferent to my archaeology of shame.

"My father's journals are supposed to be in the cache," I said. It came out smaller than I intended. "Reyes — the dying man who gave me the map — he was on the expedition. He says the story everyone knows isn't the story that happened. He says the proof is up there."

Tsering considered this with the seriousness he gives to weather. "And if the proof says the story is true?"

I opened my mouth. Closed it. Looked at the mountain, which had come out from behind its afternoon clouds the way evidence comes out from under subpoena — huge, white, and refusing to testify.

"Then I'll carry that down too," I said.

"That is not an answer."

"No," I agreed. "It's not."

He nodded slowly, and I braced for him to quit, to gather Dorje and Pemba and young Namgyal and walk back down to the world where fees are fees and clients don't bleed. Instead he stood, brushed chili dust off his trousers, and said:

"Tomorrow we buy flour. And you will tell me the rest on the glacier, where lies freeze faster." At the ladder he paused. "Mara. The old woman. What she said about eating alone — in this valley, that is not a threat. It is a description. Be careful which table you ask to sit at."

* * *

That night I dreamed of my father's hands again.

Not the pottery this time. These were different hands — cracked, black-rimmed with frostbite, digging. Digging snow, digging a hollow in a slope, the way he'd taught me to cut emergency shelters, patiently, block by block, telling me *the mountain doesn't kill you, Mara, it just presents the bill*. In the dream I couldn't see who the shelter was for. There were voices around me in the white, eleven of them maybe, or one repeated eleven times, and every voice was asking the same question in a language I almost understood, and my father kept digging, and the snow kept coming down, and I woke at three in the morning with my heart going like a trekking pole on scree and the certain knowledge that the dream was not memory, could not be memory, I had never been on that mountain —

— except that I had. Everyone from that valley had been on that mountain for fifty years. Grief doesn't need witnesses present; it files its claim through the blood.

I lay awake listening to the byre animals shift below, and did the thing I do instead of praying: arithmetic. Two days to the moraine field. Three to the icefall, if the season held. Six days of food after resupply here, seven if we stretched. The monsoon was early; everyone from the chai-wallah in Leh to the clouds themselves had said so. Behind us, somewhere down-valley, Devan Reyes was moving with his crew and his half of the story, and ahead of us was a crevice sealed under a cairn with my father's initials chiseled beside it, holding either my vindication or my condemnation, and quite possibly both, because the truth is rarely considerate enough to pick a side.

Above the village, the mountain stood against the stars, black on blacker, the pass a notch of missing sky.

*A day ahead of you,* the old woman had said. *Maybe two.*

*It cuts going down.*

I got up in the dark, boiled water, and checked the map by headlamp for the hundredth time, and this time I didn't look at the route line or the cache mark. I looked at the little annotation in Reyes's shaking hand near the icefall — the date, three days after the eleven were stranded, a date that meant somebody had gone back up, or stayed, or something else entirely that I couldn't make myself imagine yet — and I folded it away, and I lay back down, and I did not sleep again.

Shame, I decided somewhere in that long grey hour before dawn, is exactly like altitude sickness. It doesn't care how fit you are. It doesn't care how many times you've been up. It waits until you've committed — until turning back costs more than continuing — and then it sits on your chest and asks what you were thinking, coming up here, at your age, with your history.

And the only treatment anyone has ever found for either one is the same.

You descend, or you acclimatize.

By the time the light came up over Rinchenling's rooftops, I had chosen. Tsering found me packing, and read my face, and said only, "Flour first," and we went down into the lane together to bargain with a village that remembered my name for all the wrong reasons, while two days' walk above us, under fifty years of ice, my father waited with the bill.
