## 9. Devan's Version

**Mara**

I smelled the woodsmoke before I saw the camp — juniper and dung, the smell of every shepherd shelter between here and the end of the world — and I knew before Tsering said anything that we weren't alone.

"Visitors," he said, in the tone he used for weather that was about to turn.

The shepherd camp sat in a fold of the valley where two streams met, three low stone huts with sod roofs, a corral of stacked brush, and forty-odd sheep doing their best impression of scattered gravel. Two shepherds stood near the largest hut, watching us with the polite blankness of men who had seen everything and intended to admit to none of it. And outside the second hut, sitting on a stone like a man who'd been waiting long enough to get comfortable with waiting, was Devan Reyes.

He'd made tea. That was the part that got under my skin first. There were four cups on the flat rock beside him, steaming, arranged with a neatness that looked almost apologetic.

"You're a day behind," I said. "You were supposed to be a day behind."

"I'm efficient when I'm angry." He stood, brushed off his trousers out of habit rather than need — they were already beyond saving — and gestured at the cups. "Your porter told my cook you'd come through by midday. Tsering has a big mouth."

"Tsering has an excellent memory for people who buy too much rope in Leh," I said. "It's a professional skill."

Tsering, behind me, said nothing with great volume.

Dorje and Pemba moved off toward the animals with the instinct of men who wanted no part of whatever this was, and I couldn't blame them. I took the cup Devan offered because refusing it would have cost more than accepting it. The tea was terrible — over-steeped, made with water that had been boiled twice — and I drank it anyway, because that was the etiquette of the mountains: you drank what was put in front of you, even when it was an ambush.

"You followed our track," I said.

"Your track is easy to follow. You walk like someone reading a book." He sat back down on his stone. "Left foot always testing the ground first. My grandfather did the same thing, according to my mother. She said he never trusted a step he hadn't interrogated."

Something in my chest turned over at *my grandfather* delivered so casually, so warmly, as if the man hadn't spent twenty years as the villain of my family's private liturgy.

"We're not sharing the route," I said.

"I know. We agreed."

"Then why are you here?"

Devan looked into his cup for a while. Around us the streams ran loud and white with melt, and one of the shepherds began singing something tuneless to his sheep, which ignored him with the contempt only sheep can manage.

"Because," he said finally, "I've been walking for four days with a question I can't shake loose, and you're the only other person alive who might have the same one."

* * *

**Devan**

Here is what nobody tells you about inheriting a story: you don't inherit the truth of it. You inherit the shape of it, worn smooth by repetition, the way a river stone is smooth — all the edges knocked off by everyone who ever picked it up.

My grandfather, Tomás Reyes, came down off the Serrano Range in October of thirty years ago with frostbitten fingers and a story that fit in a sentence: *Elias Okafor took the supplies and abandoned us above the icefall.* Eleven men dead. One survivor. The survivor was my grandfather, and for the rest of his life — forty-one years, most of them in a house in Huaraz that smelled of diesel and dried meat — he never added a single word to that sentence.

Not once. Not drunk, not dying, not when my grandmother begged him, not when my father, furious and grieving for men he'd never met, stood in the kitchen and demanded details. My grandfather would set down whatever he was holding, look at the wall, and say: *Okafor left us. That's all there is.*

As a boy I thought that silence was grief. Everyone thought it was grief. Grief was the acceptable explanation, the generous one, and we wore it like a coat because the alternative — that he was hiding something — didn't bear thinking about.

Then he died last spring, and among his things we found the map. Half a map. The western half, drawn in his own cramped hand, with the cache marked and a note in Spanish: *the rest belongs to Okafor's daughter, if she'll take it.*

My father wanted to burn it. My mother wanted to frame it. I wanted to know what was on the missing half badly enough to fly to Leh and start asking questions in outfitter shops until a guide named Mara Okafor walked in and stopped breathing for exactly one second when she saw me.

So now I sat on a rock at a shepherd camp at four thousand meters, drinking bad tea across from that same woman, and I asked her the question that had been chewing on me since Huaraz.

"When your father came down," I said, "what did he say happened?"

Mara's face did something complicated. It's hard to describe unless you've watched someone hold a door shut against wind.

"He didn't come down," she said.

"The expedition—"

"There was no descent party. No survivors' report. No radio contact after day nineteen. The mountain ate the whole expedition above the icefall — eleven porters and climbers, my mother, my father — and the only reason anyone knew there'd been an expedition at all was that Reyes came down alone, three weeks later, half-dead, and told the rescue commission my father had taken the supply cache and walked away from the others." She wrapped both hands around her cup. "That was the entire historical record. One sentence, from one man. Everything else was inference. Newspapers are very good at inference."

"That's not—" I started, and stopped, because I could feel the floor of my certainty going soft.

"What?" she said. Not cruelly. Almost gently, which was worse.

"My grandfather never talked about it. Forty years, and he never talked about it. We assumed—" I heard how it sounded as I said it. "We assumed the silence was shame. Guilt. Survivor's guilt, maybe, or complicity. He'd watched Okafor do it and lived with having done nothing."

"And instead?"

"And instead I'm standing on a glacier moraine in a country I've never worked, chasing a dead man's map, and I realize I don't actually know what happened up there." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "Nobody knows what happened up there. My grandfather's version is one sentence. Your father's version doesn't exist, because your father never came down. So the whole thing — the disgrace, the fraud, the name your family has been carrying for twenty years — rests entirely on what one frostbitten man said to a rescue commission in 1994."

Mara was very still. Stillness, I was learning, was her native language; she spoke motion the way other people speak a second language, carefully and with visible effort.

"You're saying he might have lied," she said.

"I'm saying I don't know. That's the point. I don't know, and neither do you, and we're both willing to cross this range on the strength of a story neither of us can verify." I set down the cup. "That's either faith or madness, and my grandfather used to say you can't tell them apart at altitude."

* * *

**Mara**

Twenty years I had built my life on top of a story I'd never examined, the way you build a house on a cliff without checking the rock.

I was nine when the news came. I remember the kitchen, the yellow light, my aunt's hands going still over the dishes. *They're saying Elias abandoned his team.* Even then, even at nine, some part of me had noticed the strangeness of it — that the whole catastrophe had arrived as a single sentence from a single mouth, and that everyone, everywhere, had simply agreed to believe it. But children don't audit the stories adults hand them. Children just move into whichever house the story builds.

And then I'd grown up and become a guide, and I'd told myself the distance I kept from archaeology, from expeditions, from my father's whole world, was independence. It wasn't. It was exile. I had left the country of my father's name the way you leave a country after a war, and I had never once gone back to check whether the war had been real.

Now a stranger with my father's compass-shaped absence in his life was telling me the war might have been fiction.

"Tell me about him," I said. "Your grandfather. Before the mountain."

Devan blinked. I think he'd expected defense, attack, the usual choreography of families defending their dead. Instead I'd asked him for the man himself, and it disarmed him more thoroughly than any argument would have.

"He was quiet," Devan said. "Funny, but late — the joke would arrive ten minutes after the moment for it. He fixed radios. He could fix anything, really. He'd take apart clocks just to watch them work." A pause. "He slept with the window open even in winter. My grandmother said he couldn't stand closed rooms after '94. Said they reminded him of—" He stopped. "Of the tent, I think. Whatever tent they had up there."

"He dug," I said. "Before the expedition. My father wrote to me about his partner — I have two letters, I was eight and nine, and in both of them there's a Reyes who makes him laugh. *Tomás says the mountains keep their secrets the way drunks keep their wallets, badly and with great drama.*" I heard my own voice do something embarrassing around the words and let it. "That was the man you knew? Clocks and jokes and open windows?"

"Yes." Devan frowned. "And also a man who, twice a year, on the same two dates, would walk up into the hills above Huaraz alone and come back silent. My grandmother called them his audits."

"Audits."

"Her word. She said he was checking his accounts with God." He looked at me. "What dates?"

"I don't know. Do you?"

"No. They died with her."

Above us the clouds had begun to build along the ridge — not monsoon yet, just the afternoon's usual congregation — and the light went pewter and strange. One of the shepherds started pulling his flock toward the huts, and the sound of it, bells and complaint, filled the pause that opened between us.

"So here's where we actually are," I said. "You have half a map and a sentence. I have the other half of the map, a promise to a dying man, and a sentence I've hated for twenty years. And the only place the sentences can be checked is above the icefall, in a cache that may not exist, in a season that's already too late."

"Yes."

"And if we get there and find proof your grandfather told the truth — that my father did abandon those men — what then?"

Devan didn't answer right away. I'll give him this: he didn't reach for the easy answer.

"Then I carry it home," he said, "and I put it on my father's grave, and I tell him the family story was true, and I live with it. Because I'd rather have the true wound than the comfortable one. My grandfather spent forty years bleeding quietly into a sentence, and it killed him slower than the mountain would have." He met my eyes. "And if we find the opposite?"

I looked at my hands. They were steady. They were always steady; that was the armor, that was the whole trick of me.

"Then I stop being the daughter of a fraud," I said. "And I don't know who I am instead. I've been her daughter-the-guide for so long I don't have another self in inventory."

"That's not—"

"It is, though. Don't be kind about it. Kindness at altitude is a waste of breath."

* * *

**Devan**

She was lying, of course. Or not lying — performing. There was a version of herself she'd rehearsed so long she'd mistaken it for fact: the woman who needed nothing, who measured distances instead of feeling them, whose hands never shook. I recognized the construction because I'd built one too. Mine was the loyal grandson, the patient heir, the man who asks respectful questions and waits his turn. We were both wearing costumes sewn by our dead.

The shepherds brought us food we hadn't asked for — tsampa and curd, flat bread — because that's what happens in these valleys; you feed whoever the mountain sends you, and you don't ask them their grievances. We ate cross-legged on the flat rock like children at a picnic, two armed salvage crews reduced to passing bread, and something in the absurdity of it loosened us both.

"My mother keeps his clocks running," I said. "All fourteen of them. She winds them every Sunday. She says it's the closest she can get to hearing his voice."

Mara looked at me over her bread. "My father's compass is in my pack. I swore I sold it years ago. I told myself a whole story about selling it." She shook her head slowly. "Turns out I just moved it from locker to locker for twenty years like a body I couldn't bury."

"Two heirs," I said. "One wound."

"Don't make it poetry. Poetry is how lies survive."

But she almost smiled when she said it, and I counted that as progress.

We talked until the light failed — about routes, about the icefall ahead, about Tsering, whom she clearly trusted more than she trusted oxygen, and about my cook Nima, who had quit my crew that morning in a mutiny I was pretending not to bleed from. We did not talk about the cache. We did not talk about the journals we each privately believed were waiting inside it. There are negotiations that happen best by not happening, and this was one.

When I rose to go — my camp was two hundred meters downstream, close enough to hear, far enough to claim independence — she stopped me.

"Reyes. Your grandfather's audits. Twice a year." Her face had gone careful again, but underneath the care something was moving fast. "If you ever learn the dates — write to me. Leh general delivery. It might matter."

"Why would dates matter?"

"Because my mother's birthday was in October," she said, "and my father never once missed writing to me on it, except the year he died. And I've spent twenty years assuming he was somewhere below the icefall when the storm hit." She looked up at the ridge, where the clouds were organizing themselves into something with intent. "But if the abandonment happened earlier than the official record says — if there were days up there that nobody accounted for —"

She didn't finish. She didn't have to. We both felt it land: the possibility that the timeline itself was wrong, that the sentence we'd each inherited had been built on a foundation of dates nobody had ever checked.

"Get there first if you can," I said. "But if you find the cache — read it before you decide anything. All of it. Promise me that much."

"I don't make promises to rivals."

"Then make it to the eleven," I said. "They're the only ones with a claim on any of this, and neither of us is one of them."

For a moment she looked at me as if I'd spoken a language she'd forgotten she knew. Then she nodded, once, and turned to her tents, and I walked down through the blue dusk to my own fire, carrying the strangest cargo of the expedition so far, which was doubt — light to lift, and heavier than rope.

Behind me I heard her voice, low, giving Tsering the watch order, and then the shepherd's song starting up again, and above it all the mountain, keeping its secrets the way drunks keep their wallets.

Badly, I hoped. With great drama.

And soon.
