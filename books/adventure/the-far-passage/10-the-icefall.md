## 10. The Icefall

The icefall announced itself the way bad news does — not all at once, but in pieces you pretend not to hear. First a groan from somewhere up-valley that Dorje swore was his stomach. Then a crack like a rifle shot that made even Tsering flinch, and Tsering doesn't flinch at anything except my cooking. By midmorning we stood at its base looking up into a river of ice frozen mid-cascade, blue and green and rotten-looking, blocks the size of houses stacked by a drunk god.

"Beautiful," said Pemba, who was twenty-three and had never watched anything kill anyone.

"It's a staircase," I said. "A staircase that occasionally rearranges itself while you're standing on it. We go through early, before the sun warms it. Nobody stops. Nobody photographs. Pemba, I mean it."

He put his camera away with the wounded dignity of a man being asked to choose between oxygen and art.

I'd crossed icefalls before — dozens of them, on peaks whose names never made it into magazines because the clients were oil executives and retired dentists rather than anyone worth quoting. That was my whole career: the best high-altitude guide nobody's heard of. I used to say it as a joke. Lately it had started to sound like an epitaph.

The map said the route went through the left side of the fall, along a shelf Reyes had marked with two small crosses and a word in Spanish I didn't trust: *fácil*. Nothing under four thousand meters is ever *fácil*, and nothing above it is either, no matter what language you write it in.

There was also the second annotation. The date. Three days after the abandonment. I had looked at it eleven times since Leh and each time it had looked back at me with the same question: *what happened in those three days, Mara? What did he do for three days?*

I hadn't told the team. I told myself it was because they didn't need the weight of it. I told myself it was because the date might mean nothing — a supply calculation, a moon phase, anything. I told myself a lot of things before breakfast. It's how I keep my appetite.

* * *

We roped up at first light with the mountain still gray and cold enough to hold its temper. Me on point, then Tsering, then Dorje and Pemba, Sonam anchoring. Five ropes, five heartbeats, one route.

For the first hour it was almost kind. The shelf Reyes marked held true, a terrace of compacted snow threading between seracs like a path through sleeping animals. I moved fast and light, reading the ice the way my father must have read it forty years ago — probing with the axe, listening with my boots, trusting the small vibrations that say *solid* and the ones that say *not today*.

It's strange, the things you inherit. I spent twenty years making sure I inherited nothing from Elias Okafor. No name, no profession — well, no *his* profession; mine was respectable, paid, anonymous. And then you get above five thousand meters and your hands start doing things you never taught them, and your ear starts hearing things, and you realize the inheritance came anyway, smuggled in through the blood like contraband.

"Guide." Tsering's voice, low, behind me. "Left side is weeping."

He was right. A ribbon of meltwater ran down the face of a serac the size of a chapel, darkening the snow beneath it. Water means warmth. Warmth means movement. Movement means the chapel has opinions about visitors.

"We cross below it fast," I said. "One at a time after me. Tsering, you call the intervals."

He nodded. That was the whole conversation. Twenty years of working together compressed into six words and a nod. If marriage worked like this, half my clients wouldn't be divorced.

I crossed. The ice held. I waved them across one by one, counting them over like sheep, like children, like everything I refused to admit they were to me.

Dorje last, because Dorje always argued about going last and this way the argument was already lost. He stepped onto the crossing point just as the sun cleared the ridge to the east and poured down into the icefall like something spilled.

The mountain woke up.

* * *

I heard it before I understood it — a sound like a giant clearing its throat, deep and wet and wrong. Above us, maybe eighty meters up, the base of a serac shrugged loose a block the size a truck. Then another. Then the whole lower lip of the thing decided it had been holding on long enough.

"Tsering!" I screamed it without thinking, without grammar, just his name thrown upward like a rope.

He was still on the slope below the crossing, ten meters short of my position, and I watched him do the arithmetic every mountaineer does in the half-second when the sky starts falling: *up, down, or still.* Up meant running toward me through the open ground. Down meant retreat toward shelter he'd already passed. Still meant prayer, and Tsering had told me once, cheerfully, that gods and he maintained a professional distance.

He chose up.

The block hit where he'd been standing and exploded into shrapnel and powder. The shockwave alone — I felt it through my boots, a punch from the inside of the mountain. Ice chunks the size of suitcases bounded past him, one clipping his shoulder and spinning him sideways. He stumbled, caught himself on his axe, kept moving with that terrible unhurried urgency of a man who knows hurrying kills more people than falling does.

And then the shelf under him cracked.

Not dramatically. Just a line, quick as a pen stroke, running from his boot to the edge of the terrace, and then the terrace — the *fácil*, the blessed shelf old Reyes had trusted with two little crosses — tilted its shoulder and began to shrug him off.

I don't remember deciding. I want to tell you I calculated angles and loads and made a professional judgment. What actually happened is that my body was already moving, already driving my axe pick into the ice at the shelf's edge and slamming my boot in beside it, and my voice was already saying, "Tsering. Look at me. Not the ice. Me."

He looked at me. Forty years of mountains in that face, and in that moment none of them mattered; only the next three seconds did.

"Rope's tight," I said. "Step where I step. Now."

The shelf gave way under his back foot as he lunged forward. He came off the edge into space and the rope snapped taut and the load hit my harness like a train arriving, and I heard myself make a sound I've never made before or since — not a scream, something lower, animal, the sound of a body refusing.

My axe held. My boot held. The ice around my pick fractured in a white starburst but the starburst stopped, and Tsering swung once against the face below me, penduluming on my anchor and his own momentum, and then got his axes into the wall and his boots onto something solid and climbed.

That was it. That was the whole miracle. He climbed, and I hauled, and thirty seconds later he lay on the terrace beside me breathing like a bellows, powdered white, a welt already rising on his shoulder, alive.

Pemba was crying. Dorje was swearing in three languages, which for Dorje constituted a religious experience. Sonam checked the ropes twice and then a third time, because checking things is how Sonam prays after all.

Tsering sat up, spat out a mouthful of snow, and looked at me.

"Well," he said. "Your route-finding is adequate."

Coming from Tsering, it was a sonnet.

* * *

We moved out of the icefall without stopping, because the mountain had introduced itself and I saw no reason to linger over introductions. An hour later we reached the flat ground beyond it, a gravel plain stitched with meltwater streams, and I called a halt that wasn't scheduled, because sometimes leadership is knowing when the schedule can go to hell.

Tsering's shoulder was badly bruised but nothing broken. He let me check it with the grim patience of a man being fussed over against his will, and when I finished he caught my wrist — not hard, just enough to stop me.

"You knew that shelf was tired," he said.

"I suspected."

"You suspected and you sent us across it anyway, one at a time, spaced, roped, with an escape plan." He released my wrist. "In my village we would say you fed us to the river but built the bridge first. I am deciding whether this is wisdom or arrogance."

"And?"

"I have not decided." He picked up his pack. "But I noticed you did not hesitate. Whatever else is in your head this season, Okafor, your hands are honest."

If he only knew what was in my head. Or rather — if he knew exactly what was in my head, the map folded in my jacket with its quiet little date, three days after eleven men were left above the icefall, three days during which someone, somewhere up there, had been doing *something* — writing, digging, waiting, dying — and I had read that date and said nothing to the people whose lives I was spending to reach it.

Your hands are honest. I looked at mine, scarred and steady, and thought about how much dishonesty they could carry while staying steady.

* * *

Camp went up in the lee of a moraine ridge, tents angled into the wind, the icefall glittering behind us like a wound dressed in jewels. We ate. The food tasted the way food tastes after adrenaline — like proof of being alive, slightly over-salted.

Pemba came to my tent in the evening, uncharacteristically solemn, and asked if I'd been frightened.

"No," I lied.

"Liar," he said, with the freedom of the young, and grinned, and left. The young believe fear is a confession. The old know it's a currency. You spend it on other people and hope you've budgeted correctly.

Later, when the others slept, I took out the map and unfolded it by headlamp, careful to angle the light away from the tent walls. Reyes's pencil lines. The crosses marking the shelf — *fácil*, you lying old man, though I suppose he crossed it too, and maybe it was fresh then, maybe mountains age faster than maps.

And there, tucked into the margin of the cache route, small as a secret: the date. Three days after the abandonment.

Three days. Eleven men left above the icefall — left by someone, abandoned, according to every account history cared to keep — and then three days more. Supplies cached. Journals written. Something done up there that took three days and was important enough for a dying prospector to mark it, and shameful enough that the world remembered only the leaving.

My father's initials were chiseled beside the cache door, according to the map. Elias Okafor, who dug where others wouldn't. The compass in my pack seemed to warm against my hip like a sleeping animal, and I hated it a little.

Here is what I understood, lying in the dark with my team breathing around me and my heart still recovering from the weight of Tsering on the rope: I had nearly watched a man die today for my secret. Not for the truth — for my *secret*, the difference between those being everything. If the shelf had taken him, the date would have been carved on his grave too, and I would have been the only one who knew what it cost.

Tomorrow or the day after, Tsering would ask again why we were really here. He'd earned the asking twice over now. And the lie I'd brought from Leh — the fee, the client, the professional errand — was getting heavier than any barrel of supplies.

Above the icefall, the wind turned and carried down the smell of high snow, clean and ancient and patient. Somewhere up there, three days' worth of something waited under stone.

I zipped the map away, and lay listening to the mountain settle, and did not sleep until well past the hour when honesty should have arrived — and didn't.
