## 11. Camp Above Clouds

Rest days are a lie we tell our bodies. The body believes it for about an hour, right up until it starts cataloguing everything that hurts and presenting the invoice with interest. Mine came due at dawn, when I rolled over in the tent and my shoulders filed a formal complaint about the icefall, my knees countersued on behalf of the moraine, and some deep bureaucratic layer of me stamped the whole thing *approved, payable in sleep*.

We'd earned the halt. Camp Above Clouds — Tsering's name for it, because he names things the way other men breathe, without effort and always correctly — sat on a broad shelf at five thousand four hundred meters, a terrace of old rock and wind-packed snow that hung above an ocean of cloud. Below us, the valleys we'd climbed through had drowned overnight. The white sea stretched to the horizon, unbroken except for a few dark islands where the highest ridges still stood with their heads up, looking embarrassed to be caught above water.

Above us: the icefall's upper gates, then the long gray ramp of the glacier, then whatever waited past that. My father's route. His initials chiseled into stone somewhere ahead of us like a signature on a letter twenty years late.

"Rest means rest," Tsering said, handing out tea. He said it the way he says most things — flatly, as a statement of physical law. "No sorting gear. No checking ropes. No standing there looking at the mountain like it owes you money."

"It might," I said.

"Everything owes you money. That is your problem." He settled onto his pack with the contentment of a man who has made peace with his own ambitions, which are few and reasonable and mostly involve tea. Dorje was already asleep sitting up, which is a skill porters develop and never lose. Pemba was mending a strap. Lobsang was doing nothing so aggressively that it counted as meditation.

I drank my tea and looked at the clouds below us and did not sort gear, which is to say I sorted gear in my head, where no one could stop me.

* * *

The compass came out of my pack around midmorning, when the light turned silver and the cold loosened its grip just enough to let you think about your hands.

I'd found it in Leh, in the storage locker I rent from a man who charges by the month and remembers nothing, which is why I rent from him. I keep almost nothing there. A duffel of summer clothes. A box of papers I've never reread. And at the back, wrapped in a wool sock inside a tin inside a plastic bag, the brass compass my father carried on every expedition of his life, including the last one.

I swore I'd sold it years ago. That's what I told people, anyway — told myself, more accurately, because nobody else ever asked. *Sold it in Delhi when I was nineteen, needed the money.* It was a good story. It had the shape of a good story: the daughter liquidating the estate of the fraud, converting his relics into bus fare. Clean. Final. The kind of story you can live inside.

The truth was that I'd taken it off a shelf the night before the estate sale, put it in my pocket, and then been unable to do anything with it for twenty years. Not use it — I have three perfectly good compasses, modern ones, liquid-damped, accurate to the degree. Not sell it. Not throw it away. Not even look at it, really. It lived in its wrappings like something under a curse, moving with me from town to town, from locker to locker, a small dense weight of unfinished business.

That morning I unwrapped it for the third time since Leh.

Brass, worn bright at the edges where a thumb had worried it for decades. The needle swung lazily, settling toward north with the unhurried confidence of an instrument built before anyone thought to rush anything. The glass had a scratch across one quadrant, old and shallow. The lid hinge was loose. I should oil that, I thought, and knew I wouldn't, because the looseness was his.

And there, engraved on the inner face of the lid, in letters small enough that you had to tilt it against the light:

**TO E.O., WHO DIGS WHERE OTHERS WON'T.**

No name of the giver. No date. Just that — a joke or a tribute or both, the kind of thing expedition partners engrave on each other's equipment after enough shared misery turns into affection. Reyes, I thought. It had to be Reyes senior. Who else would have had the intimacy and the wit? *Who digs where others won't.* At the time, presumably, it had meant something fond: Elias Okafor, the man who'd crawl into a crevice everyone else called a dead end and come out holding pottery.

Now it read differently. Now it read like an epitaph with a punchline missing.

I sat there with the compass open in my palm, watching the needle breathe, and let myself think the thing I'd been not-thinking since the hospice in Leh.

I didn't want treasure.

Oh, I'd dressed it up as treasure — the cache, the journals, the salvage value, the historical significance, all the vocabulary of recovery. And there was real work in it; I'm not pretending otherwise. Documents rot. Testimony decays. Someone had to go get those tins before another monsoon ate them, and if not me, then Devan Reyes, whose claim to them was built on a lie I now suspected ran the other direction entirely. There were reasons. Good ones. I'd stacked them up like ballast.

But underneath the ballast, in the hold where I kept the things I didn't inspect, there was this: I wanted the world to be wrong.

Not corrected. Not nuanced. *Wrong.* I wanted to stand in front of the record — the obituaries, the inquiry findings, the way men at outfitters' shops still lowered their voices when they said *Okafor*, the way they'd said it in my hearing my entire childhood, that particular pitying hush reserved for the families of the disgraced — and I wanted to watch it break. I wanted Elias Okafor's name to come down off the gallows it had hung on for twenty years, and I wanted to be the one who cut the rope, and yes, I wanted people to know it was me, the daughter nobody had heard of, the guide who'd spent two decades being excellent in places too high for reputations to matter.

Vindication. That was the word, and I made myself say it inwardly, plainly, without varnish, because Tsering wasn't there to catch the lie and lying to yourself at altitude is how people die.

*I want vindication more than I want the truth.*

They're supposed to be the same thing. They're not. Truth is a thing you find and hand over, whole, regardless of what it does to you. Vindication is a thing you want, and wanting bends the finding. I knew this. Every archaeologist's child knows this — it's the first lesson of the dig, the one taught before trowels: *the site will give you what you're looking for whether or not it's there, if you let your hands do the deciding.*

My father had known it too. Whatever else he'd been, whatever the mountain had or hadn't done to him, he'd been rigorous. He'd taught me to log finds before hopes, to photograph in situ, to write down what I saw and not what I expected. Somewhere in the journals ahead of us — if they existed, if the map was honest, if Reyes hadn't sent me chasing a dying man's fever dream — somewhere up there was his own account, written in his own hand, and I realized with a lurch that I couldn't trust myself near it.

Because if the journals cleared him, I'd want to shout it from the pass. And if they damned him —

If they damned him, what would I do?

I watched the needle settle north and understood that I didn't know. Twenty years of competence, of clean routes and safe clients and a reputation built on never letting wanting override judgment — and I did not know what I'd do if the ground itself testified against him. Bury the tins? I flinched from the thought and then made myself look at it, the way you look at a crevasse bridge before crossing: *yes, it could hold that thought, barely, don't linger.*

The compass didn't care. That's the thing about instruments. They point where they point. It was only ever the people holding them who lied.

* * *

Lunch was tsampa and cheese and the last of the fresh apricots from the foothill villages, eaten in a silence so complete you could hear the glacier thinking. Up here the air carries sound strangely — a shout travels forever, a whisper dies instantly, and between the two there's a band of ordinary noise that simply doesn't exist. We lived in that band. Five people chewing. Wind doing its endless clerical work on the ridge above.

Dorje woke from his seated sleep, looked at the view, and delivered his assessment: "Clouds are lower than yesterday."

"They're clouds," Pemba said. "Lowering is the whole job."

"These are lowering wrong."

I looked. He was right, in the way Dorje is usually right about weather and usually wrong about everything else. The cloud deck below us hadn't risen, but the sky above had changed texture — the deep altitude blue gone milky at the zenith, a veil thinning out of the northwest. Cirrus, but thickening, organized, arriving with intent rather than drifting through on its way to somewhere better.

Out of season. That was the phrase my mind reached for and I pushed it back down. Monsoon season was weeks out. The forecasts had said weeks. But forecasts are written for valleys, and we were living above the part of the sky they make predictions about.

"Snow tonight," Tsering said quietly, beside me. He'd followed my eyes up. He always did. "Maybe. Small."

"Small," I agreed.

"And tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow we move." I said it faster than I needed to. He noticed that too. Tsering notices everything and files it in whatever ledger he keeps behind his face, the one I'd been running a tab with since Leh.

He poured me more tea without being asked, which was either kindness or surveillance.

* * *

The afternoon went soft and golden, the kind of light that makes you forgive mountains everything. I spent it the way I pretend I don't spend rest days: reading the route. Not the walking of it — the reading. I spread the map on my knees inside the tent vestibule, weighted at the corners with stones, and traced the line Reyes had drawn in his dying hand.

His draftsmanship was better than it had any right to be. Old prospectors draw like engineers; the tremor in his fingers had cost him neatness but not accuracy. The route climbed past our camp, through the icefall's upper gates, along the true left of the glacier to a lateral moraine he'd marked with a symbol I recognized from his other annotations — a triangle with a dot, his shorthand for *searched, nothing*. Then the ramp steepened, crossed a rib of gray rock he'd labeled in Spanish, *la costilla*, the rib, and beyond that the contour lines squeezed together around a hanging valley the map showed as blank.

Blank except for two marks.

The first was the cache: a small square with **E.O.** beside it in careful block letters, and beneath it, in Reyes's shakier hand, the word *enterrado*. Buried.

The second mark was smaller. Fainter. Pencil rather than ink, pressed so lightly I'd missed it twice in Leh, and only found it the night before we left, by candlelight, tilting the paper the way you tilt the compass to catch the engraving.

It was a date.

Three days after the date history gave for the abandonment. Three days after the eleven men — twelve, counting my mother, though the histories never counted her, never counted any of them properly — were supposed to have died alone above the icefall while Elias Okafor walked down to save himself.

Three days. Which meant someone had been alive up there for three days. Writing. Digging, maybe. *Digging where others won't.* Sealing tins into a crevice and chiseling initials beside it, which takes hours, which takes hands that work, which takes a person who believes someone will come back for it.

Or it meant nothing. A supply note. A survey datum. Dates mean nothing until context makes them scream.

I folded the map along its old creases and put it away, and put the compass away, and sat in the vestibule watching the light die off the western ridges, and admitted the rest of it, the part underneath the vindication, the part I'd been carrying since the hospice:

I wanted him back. Not the name rehabilitated, not the record set straight — him. The man whose hands I remembered brushing dirt from pottery shards with a delicacy he never showed anything else alive. The man who smelled of pipe smoke and limestone dust. I wanted the journals to be a door and I wanted to walk through it and find him on the other side, mid-sentence, saying something precise and warm about stratigraphy, and I wanted twenty years to undo themselves.

That's not scholarship. That's not salvage. That's a daughter kneeling at a locked door hoping her father is on the other side of it, and knowing — the way you know at altitude, with the clarity the thin air gives you, cruel and total — that what's on the other side of that door is paper. Paper and rusted tin and whatever truth the mountain kept, indifferent to what any of us wanted it to say.

The first flakes came down at dusk. Fat, slow, deliberate, falling out of a sky that had no business producing them. Pemba caught one on his sleeve and frowned at it like it had broken a contract. Dorje said nothing, which from Dorje was a speech.

Tsering crouched next to me at the vestibule, watching the snow thicken from flurry toward something with intent.

"You checked the map again today," he said. Not a question. "Twice."

"Route-finding."

"Mm." He looked at the snow. "In Leh, you check the map once, maybe twice, whole trip. Here, twice in one afternoon." He stood, brushed off his knees. "Whatever is written on that map besides the route, Mara-jii — you will tell us before it costs us. Not after."

He ducked into his tent before I could answer, which was its own kind of answer. The snow fell harder. Above the cloud-sea, the last light went copper, then gray, and somewhere up the valley in the dark, three days past a date the world had already closed the books on, something waited under stone with my father's initials on it — and I lay awake doing the arithmetic I'd promised myself I wouldn't do.

Three days. Twelve people. One shelter dug by frozen hands.

The math only worked one way, and I didn't sleep.
