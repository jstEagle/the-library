## 12. The Second Annotation

The morning after the rest day, the snow had stopped and the world had been scoured to a brightness that felt less like weather than like judgment. Every flaw in a snowfield shows itself in that light — every hollow, every wind-slab lip, every place where the mountain is lying to you. I stood outside my tent with tea going cold in my hands and thought about how well light like that would serve an interrogation.

I didn't know yet that it was going to get one.

Tsering was already up, of course. Tsering was always already up, as if sleep were a courtesy he extended to his body only when it threatened to file a complaint. He was at the cook tent, and he was not cooking. He was sitting on the fuel crate with something flat and pale in his hands, and even from ten meters away I knew what it was, because I'd slept with it under my head for eleven nights like a guilty woman sleeping on a letter she should have burned.

My map. Reyes's map. The one I'd told the team was a salvage commission, coordinates and a cache, nothing personal.

He looked up when I came near. He didn't hold it out or wave it around. He just let me see that he had it, the way you'd let someone see you holding their passport.

"Where did you get that?"

"Your tent flap doesn't close all the way," he said. "It hasn't since Darcha. You fold it over and tuck it, and when the wind comes from the north it lifts." He turned the map over in his hands, gently, the way he handled everything — rope, ice screws, other people's reputations. "You sleep on your left side. You put it under the corner of your mat. Last night the wind came from the north."

Dorje and Pemba were emerging from their tents, drawn by the particular gravity of a conversation happening without them. Namgyal hung back by the cook fire, ladle in hand, wearing the expression of a man who has decided the porridge requires his complete attention.

"Tsering—"

"There's a date." He said it quietly, which was worse than shouting. Shouting is weather; quiet is climate. "Folded into the corner, where the crease hides it. Ink, not pencil. Someone wrote it after the map was drawn." He read it out anyway, though we both knew he'd memorized it: "*Three days after.* That's what it says. Three days after what, Mara?"

The mountain took that moment to calve somewhere far above us, a long dry boom rolling down the valley like a door closing in another house.

"Sit down," I said. "Have some tea."

"I don't want tea." But he sat, because Tsering's discipline was a thing he applied to himself before anyone else, and I have always suspected that's why it cut so clean. "I want to know what we're carrying. I've asked you twice on this trip why you're really here, and both times you gave me a price. Fees don't make a person sleep on a map like it's a child."

Dorje settled onto a rock with the theatrical patience of a man preparing to enjoy himself. Pemba crouched beside him. The audience assembled itself the way audiences do in the mountains — nowhere else to go, and the drama better than the view.

"It's a prospector's map," I said. "The man who drew it died in Leh. There's a cache up-valley, expedition stores, maybe instruments. Salvage rights are murky and I wanted them clean before anyone got ideas. That's all."

"That's all." Tsering nodded slowly, and I watched him decide something behind his eyes, some small internal gate swinging shut. "Twenty years I've worked with you. Twenty years, and you have never once lied to me badly. You lie well or not at all. This is you not at all."

Pemba made a sound that might have been a cough or might have been applause.

"The date," Dorje said, helpfully, because Dorje's gift in life was throwing stones into wells just to hear how deep they went. "Three days after what?"

"After the Okafor expedition," Tsering said, not taking his eyes off me. "The name is on the map too. In the corner. E.O. I thought it was a survey mark until last night, when I couldn't sleep, which is rare, so I had time to think." He folded the map along its old creases, precise, reverent, furious. "Elias Okafor. The fraud. The man who left eleven people on the mountain. My uncle carried loads for the rescue party that found nothing. He came home thin and silent and never went above five thousand meters again as long as he lived."

The porridge, I noticed distantly, was burning. Namgyal noticed too and did nothing about it, which told me exactly whose side the kitchen had taken.

"You knew," Tsering said. "Before Leh. Before Reyes. You've known this route your whole life, haven't you? It's in your feet. I watched you find the line through the moraine at Camp Two like you were walking home in the dark." He held the map out now, arm straight, an offering or an accusation. "What are we doing here, Mara? What is three days after?"

And here is the thing I could have done. I could have told them. Right there, with the porridge burning and the light merciless and four men watching me across twenty years of shared rope, I could have said: *my father drew this map's destination, my mother may be on this mountain, and I brought you all up here to dig through the wreckage of a story that ruined my family, and I didn't tell you because telling you makes it real.*

Instead I heard myself say, in the level voice I use with frightened clients: "It's a date relative to the abandonment. Reyes wasn't sure of the calendar. It's context for the cache — whether anything perishable survived. Nothing more."

Nothing more. Three words, and I felt each one land on the team like a load shifted onto the wrong shoulder.

Tsering took the map back. He looked at it for a long time. Then he stood, tucked it into my tent — my tent, not his hands, a deliberate returning — and said, "When you're ready to tell us the truth, we'll be ready to hear it. Until then, I lead the rope today. You navigate. We keep those jobs separate."

It was, I understood later, the gentlest mutiny in the history of high-altitude mountaineering. It was also a mutiny. On a rope team, trust isn't a feeling; it's a mechanical fact, like a bolt in rock. And I had just stripped the threads out of mine in front of everyone.

* * *

We moved that day the way families move through a house after a bad dinner — competently, silently, with great care taken not to touch anything. Tsering led. I navigated, which meant I walked second and stared at the ground and felt the specific loneliness of a leader who has been demoted by consent rather than coup.

The terrain did its best to distract us. We climbed out of the shadowed bowl below the serac field and onto a shoulder of grey scree where the wind came off the ice in long flat shoves that made conversation impossible, which suited everyone fine. Pemba sang once, two lines of some harvest song, and stopped when nobody joined him. Even the mountain seemed embarrassed.

By early afternoon we'd gained the shelf where I'd planned the next camp — a broad terrace of old lateral moraine, sheltered on three sides, with a streamlet still running under thin ice. Good ground. I'd have praised it, in another life, to men who wanted to hear me praise things.

We were pitching tents when Pemba, who had climbed a boulder to shake out the flysheet, said, "People coming down."

I got my binoculars up fast enough to hurt myself with the strap.

They were perhaps two kilometers down-valley and descending in our direction along the true right bank — six figures in matching red shells, moving in the tight economical rhythm of a crew that knows its business. Even at that distance I knew the walk of the man in front. Devan Reyes moved uphill like a man apologizing to it and downhill like a man leaving a funeral.

"Reyes," I said.

Dorje was beside me instantly, all earlier grievance forgotten in the pure animal pleasure of watching something happen to somebody else. "Six of them. They're coming right past us."

"They'll stop," I said. "He'll want to compare notes. See if we've found anything."

But they didn't stop. That was the first wrong note. As they closed the distance I picked out details that assembled themselves into a story I didn't like: no loads worth mentioning, the porters' packs riding light and high, the rope coils strapped but unused, frost on gear that hadn't been warm in days. A crew coming *down*. Not retreating — there was no shame-posture in their stride — but finished. Emptied. Men walking out of a story they no longer believed in.

They passed below our camp at a distance of perhaps two hundred meters. Close enough to shout. Close enough to see faces.

Devan saw me. I'm sure of it, because his head turned and held, and for three or four steps our eyes met across the cold air, and then he faced forward again and kept walking. No wave. No hail. Not hostility exactly — something more tired than that. The look of a man who has run out of reasons to climb toward something and is conserving what's left of him for the walk down.

His porters glanced up at us, curious, indifferent. One of them — the big one, the cook, I'd seen him at the outfitter's in Leh weighing dhal on a spring scale — gave our camp a single long look, and there was something in it I couldn't read then and can read now. Something almost apologetic.

Then they were below us, dropping away along the moraine crest, six red dots shrinking into the grey, and the valley swallowed them, and I stood there with the binoculars hanging from my neck feeling like a woman who has just watched the last train leave a station she wasn't sure she wanted to be at anyway.

"They turned back," Dorje said, stating it with relish. "Empty-handed. Ha! So the treasure is ours alone, eh, Memsaab?" He elbowed Pemba. "Now there's no one to share with."

No one laughed. Not even Dorje, really — the laugh died in him when he heard the silence it landed in.

Tsering stood apart, coiling a guyline with unnecessary precision. He didn't look at the departing crew. He looked at me, briefly, with an expression I couldn't file — not anger, not quite pity. Assessment. The look of a man recalculating a load.

That night the wind turned and came from the north again, and I lay awake with my map under the corner of my mat, flap tucked, and thought about how a secret is a weight like any other, except that it gets heavier the higher you carry it, and unlike everything else in a pack, you cannot divide it among the team.

* * *

Cohesion, in a rope team, is not a mood. It's a set of small behaviors: who checks whose buckle, who takes first watch without being asked, whether the porridge gets stirred for the man on sweep or just served. I began noticing, over the next day's march to the moraine camps, the small ways the machine was running rough.

Namgyal stopped making the extra tea. Small thing. Tea is never a small thing.

Pemba, who joked, stopped joking near me specifically, and resumed joking with the others, which was somehow lonelier than silence.

Dorje asked Tsering, within my hearing, whether the insurance covered "expeditions where the sahib's family is involved," and Tsering answered, also within my hearing, "Ask her," and neither of them asked me, and the question just lay there on the trail between us like a dropped carabiner nobody picked up.

And Tsering — Tsering did his job perfectly. That was the worst of it. Perfect leading, perfect anchors, perfect pitch of voice in the white sections. He had simply removed me from the category of people he worried about and filed me under terrain: present, must be crossed, cannot be trusted to hold.

On the third evening we reached the edge of the great moraine field — the one I'd been walking toward in my head for twenty years without admitting it — and made camp in the lee of a boulder the size of a chapel. The light went gold and then violet and then gone, and the temperature fell through the floor of the world, and we ate in the cook tent in a silence with edges on it.

It was Dorje, of all people, who broke it. Dorje, who could smell a buried grievance the way his cousins' dogs smelled truffles.

"So," he said, stirring his pot with the calm of a man lighting a fuse. "Eleven men died up there. The famous ones. And our Memsaab has a map with dates on it, and a name, and a face like a closed door. And the other sahib — the grandson — he turns around and goes home with nothing." He looked up, mild as milk. "I am only saying what everyone is thinking, which is: what happens when *we* turn around and go home with nothing? Because I have been on expeditions that found nothing, and they were sad. But I have never been on one where the boss knew beforehand it was nothing, and paid us anyway to carry her grief up a mountain."

"Dorje," Tsering said.

"No, no. Let me finish, it is almost finished." He pointed the spoon at me, gently, terribly. "Memsaab. You saved my friend Tsering's life in the icefall. I do not forget this. My family does not forget this. So I will say the ugly thing, because someone must, and it might as well be the man with the ugliest mouth." He set the spoon down. "Whatever is up there — whatever you're looking for — it isn't stores. Stores don't make a person's eyes look like yours do when you look up-valley. Tell us, or don't. But know that we are climbing on your secret now, and secrets, at altitude, are heavier than oxygen debt."

Nobody spoke. The stove hissed. Somewhere above us the mountain ticked and settled, cooling, keeping its own counsel.

I opened my mouth to say — I genuinely do not know what. Another deflection, probably. Another well-made, badly-fitting sentence.

And that was when Pemba, on evening sweep around the perimeter, called out from the dark: "There's a tent below us. One tent. Someone's climbed up behind us."

We came out with headlamps. Far down the moraine, a single point of light bobbed where no light should be — too high for the valley, too low for the passes, moving with the slow stubborn cadence of a man traveling alone.

One man. Alone. Coming up.

Tsering found the binoculars before I did. He watched for a long moment, then handed them to me without a word, and I steadied them against the boulder and found the walker in the blue-black murk: tall, deliberate, unroped, carrying a pack that rode heavy and low.

Devan Reyes. Empty-handed two days ago. Climbing alone now, in the dark, toward the cache — or toward us.

"He followed our track," Tsering said quietly. "From the moment they passed. He's been reading our footprints in the snow like a book."

I lowered the glasses. My breath hung in front of me, and beyond it the mountain stood black against the stars, holding whatever it held, three days after and twenty years deep.

"He'll reach us by tomorrow night," I said. And no one asked me what I intended to do about it, which was its own kind of answer.
