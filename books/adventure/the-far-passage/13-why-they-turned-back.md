## 13. Why They Turned Back

My grandfather used to say that a man learns who he is at altitude, because there's nothing up there to hide behind. He said it the way men say things they've never actually tested — with a whiskey in his hand and both feet firmly on the ground floor of a house in Shimla. I believed him anyway. I built a whole life on the belief. Which is how I came to be standing alone in an abandoned cook tent at 4,700 meters, reading my own obituary written in pencil on the back of a ration list.

But let me back up, because the abandonment didn't happen all at once. Mutiny never does. It happens the way frost heaves a road — invisibly, patiently, until one morning the surface you trusted is broken and you're driving over rubble pretending it's still pavement.

* * *

The first crack showed at the shepherd camp, three days up. My sirdar, Pemba, had worked for me four seasons and had never once questioned an order out loud. That was his value, frankly. I paid him well for it. But when I laid out the route on the map that evening — the high traverse past the icefall, the moraine field, the line climbing toward the unnamed col where my grandfather's half of the story ended — Pemba looked at it for a long time and then looked at me, and what was in his face wasn't doubt exactly. It was pity. Which is worse.

"Sahib," he said, "the mules won't go above the moraine this late."

"The mules don't have to. We carry from there."

"The snow," he said, "is wrong." And then, because he was Pemba and not a man given to drama: "It is falling like October falls. Not like June."

I told him the forecast said otherwise. The forecast, relayed through a radio set older than two of my porters, said six days of clear weather. I repeated this the way you repeat anything you need to believe more than verify.

Pemba nodded and went to check the loads, and I sat by the fire feeling the particular loneliness of a man who has just been contradicted politely and knows the politeness was the insult.

* * *

Here's what I hadn't told anyone, including myself, in quite so many words: I wasn't chasing treasure. The salvage crews that work these ranges call it salvage, but mostly it's grave-robbing with permits. There were rumors — there are always rumors — of an expedition cache somewhere in the high Serrano, tins and instruments and documents from the Okafor-Reyes expedition twenty years ago, the one that killed eleven people and made my grandfather famous and made Elias Okafor infamous. If there were journals in that cache, they'd be worth money. That was the story I'd sold my crew. It was even the story I'd sold myself, right up until the night I couldn't sleep and admitted the truth to the inside of my own tent:

I wanted to know what happened above the icefall.

My grandfather, Arjun Reyes, came down off that mountain alive when eleven men did not, and he never spoke of it. Not once, not ever, not even at the end, when the stroke had loosened whatever held his tongue together and he babbled about rivers and train stations and my grandmother's cooking. He talked around that mountain the way water talks around a stone. And a boy grows up in a house with a hole shaped like a mountain in the middle of it, and eventually the boy becomes a man who goes looking for the shape of the hole. That was me. That was the whole expedition, honestly. Eleven dead men and one silent survivor, and me with a copy of a map I'd bought from a dying prospector's estate broker, standing in the wreckage of my grandfather's silence, calling it archaeology.

* * *

The second crack came the next morning, when Dorje-equivalent number three — my cook, Norbu, a cheerful man whose optimism I had always mistaken for loyalty — asked me directly, over porridge, why we were really going up.

"For the cache," I said.

"Everyone says there is no cache," Norbu said, cheerfully, stirring the pot. "Old men say it. Guides say it. Even the woman Okafor's guide says it, and she is the best guide in the range, everyone says so."

"You've been talking to Tsering's crew?"

"We shared a fire with their porters two nights ago," Norbu said. "They are good men. They say she is going for her father. They say it quietly, but porters hear everything, sahib. Porters are the radio of the mountains." He smiled at me with real warmth. "So. Are you going for your grandfather?"

And there it was, out in the open, in the thin cold morning air, spoken by a man holding a ladle. I could have lied to him. I'd been lying to him for weeks. But something about the altitude, or the porridge, or the fact that Mara Okafor's crew apparently already knew my business better than I did — something made me tell the truth.

"My grandfather was on that expedition," I said. "He was the only one who came down."

Norbu stopped stirring. The other porters had gone quiet in the way porters do, which is to say completely, instantly, and with their ears enormous.

"He never said what happened?" Norbu asked.

"No."

"And you think the mountain will say it for him?"

"I think there might be papers," I said. "Records. Something."

Norbu considered this with the seriousness he usually reserved for spice ratios. Then he said, "Then we should hurry. Because the mountain is closing its mouth." And he pointed his ladle at the sky, where the clouds had begun doing something I didn't like — building from the south, stacked and patient, the way weather builds before it commits.

* * *

The third crack was the radio. On the fourth day up, at the last camp below the moraine, I raised the outfitter in Leh and got a voice I didn't recognize, breathless with the excitement of bad news. The monsoon surge had shifted north. The models — such as they were, in that office, which was mostly a man named Kalsang squinting at satellite prints — now gave the high passes ten days, maybe eight. Maybe less. The rain line was going to hit the foothills within the week, and after that, everything above would fill with cloud and snow and stay filled until September.

I thanked him and sat outside the tent and looked south, and the clouds looked back, and I understood that I had run out of time to be careful. I had a choice, the kind of choice that looks simple from a warm office and isn't: turn back now, lose the season, come again next year with better forecasts and a calmer heart. Or push for the moraine and the icefall beyond it in a shrinking window, with a crew whose faith in me was eroding like a riverbank.

I chose the window. Of course I chose the window. You don't cross an ocean to stand on the beach.

That night I told the crew. I laid it out straight: eight days, hard marching, the moraine in three, the cache if it existed, and down before the sky closed. I offered double pay for the push. I said all the things a leader says, and I watched their faces while I said them, and I saw — finally, clearly, too late — that I was not leading men anymore. I was proposing a gamble to creditors.

Pemba spoke for all of them, because that was his job and he was good at it.

"Sahib. We have carried for you four seasons. We would carry for you into the storm itself, if you asked." A pause, delicately managed. "But you are asking us to chase a ghost. The old men in the villages say there is nothing up there. The guides say it. Now the sky says it. And a ghost, sahib—" he spread his hands, apologetic, final— "a ghost does not pay double."

"We'll vote in the morning," I said, with a dignity I did not feel.

There was no vote. There was a note.

* * *

Which brings me back to the cook tent, and the pencil, and the ration list. In the morning the camp was struck, the loads distributed, the men formed up and heading down before I'd finished lacing my boots. Pemba stood apart, waiting for me, wearing the expression of a man returning a borrowed tool he's accidentally broken.

"The vote went badly," I said.

"The vote went honestly," he said. "It is not the same thing, but it is what we had." He pressed a folded paper into my hand. "Norbu wrote it. He wanted you to find it warm, not cold. There is tea in the flask. Do not die, sahib. Your grandfather survived this mountain; it would be rude for you to do worse."

And then they were gone, five men and their loads descending the trail in a neat, sad line, and I was alone at 4,700 meters with a flask of tea and a piece of paper.

I waited until they were out of sight to read it. Some humiliations you take standing; others require sitting down. This one required both.

*Sahib,* it began, in Norbu's round, cheerful handwriting, the same hand that had written *Happy Birthday* on my cake in April.

*We are sorry. We are not sorry enough to stay, but we are sorry. The monsoon is coming weeks early, everyone below says so, and the old men in the village say the mountain ate eleven men once and does not share. We think you are chasing a ghost, sahib, and ghosts are heavy, and our knees are fifty years old between us.*

*Also we think you are not chasing treasure at all. We think you are chasing your grandfather, and we cannot carry him for you. That load only you can lift. Please forgive us. Please come down. The tea is ginger.*

*Your men, who liked you very much.*

I read it twice. Then I laughed — actually laughed, alone in the wind at 4,700 meters, the sound coming out of me cracked and strange — because it was true, every word of it, and because Norbu had managed to fire me, break my heart, and comfort me in under a hundred words, which is more than most eulogists manage.

Then I sat down on a rock and drank the ginger tea, which was still warm, and let myself feel the full weight of what I'd done. I had spent my inheritance — not money; credibility — on a rumor and a wound. Four seasons of trust, spent in nine days. And the worst part, the part I kept circling like a tongue finding a broken tooth: I would have done it again. Given the same choice, same window, same hole in my grandfather's silence, I would spend it all again. Whatever was wrong with me, it was structural.

* * *

The question, once the tea was gone, was what a fired expedition leader does with himself at 4,700 meters with eight days of weather and no crew.

The honest answer was: go down. Follow my men, drink ginger tea at lower altitudes, grieve properly, come back next year as a wiser man with a new sirdar and a better story for the bankers. Every sensible cell in my body voted for this. I held the vote. It was unanimous.

I started packing upward instead.

Because here is the thing about pride, which I know now and knew dimly then: it isn't vanity. Vanity is wanting to look good. Pride is needing to be right — needing it so badly that you'll trade warmth, company, safety, and sense for the chance. My grandfather had needed to be right his whole life, and it had made him rich and respected and silent, and I was his grandson down to the bone. Somewhere above me was a crevice or a cairn with an answer in it, and either I found it or I became him: a man who came down and never spoke of the mountain again, except with his feet planted safely on the ground floor of some house, telling boys things he'd never tested.

No. Better to freeze on it than to inherit the silence.

* * *

I caught Mara Okafor's camp that afternoon, which surprised me, because I'd assumed they were a day ahead and moving fast. They weren't moving at all. They were pinned at the edge of the moraine field, tents up, and as I came over the rise I saw why: the weather to the south had organized itself into something with intent, and even I — a salvage man, not a meteorologist — could read the sentence it was writing across the sky.

Tsering saw me first. He was coiling rope outside the cook tent, and he watched me approach with the expression of a man watching weather he'd already forecast.

"Mister Reyes," he said. "You are alone."

"My crew voted," I said. "Democracy is brutal at altitude."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth that in a less disciplined man would have been a smile. "You want tea."

"I want," I said, "to talk to your boss."

Mara emerged from her tent zipping a jacket, and up close she had the specific tiredness of someone who'd been making hard decisions since dawn. She looked at me, then past me at the empty trail, then back, and I watched her do the arithmetic — one man, no porters, no loads, no leverage — and arrive at the sum.

"They turned back," she said. Not a question.

"This morning. Monsoon's coming weeks early. Their words, not mine — though the sky seems to agree." I nodded south. "You've seen it."

"We've seen it." She crossed her arms. "What do you want, Reyes?"

And here is where I'd like to report that I delivered a speech, something dignified about shared history and common purpose. What I actually said was simpler and cost more.

"Take me on your rope. As a climber. I pull my weight, I eat my own rations, I follow your calls without argument. I know the lower routes off this side of the range better than any map you're carrying — my grandfather drew half of them." I paused, because the next part had to be said plainly or not at all. "I'm asking because alone up here I'm not an expedition, I'm a corpse with a deadline. And because whatever's up there, I think you're the only other person alive who needs to see it."

She was quiet long enough that the wind filled the space. Behind her, Tsering coiled rope and pretended magnificently not to listen, which is a porter art form.

"No," Mara said.

"Mara—"

"It's Ms. Okafor on the mountain. And no." Her voice wasn't cruel. That was the terrible part — it was reasonable, measured, the voice of a professional declining a liability. "You've got no acclimatization margin left, no partner, no backup, and from what I can see, a crew that trusted you four days ago doesn't anymore. That's not a judgment. It's a diagnosis. I have three men whose families expect them home, and I don't add variables above the icefall."

"I know those routes."

"You know routes your grandfather drew twenty years ago, of a glacier that's moved since. Everyone's grandfather drew maps. The glacier didn't read them." She glanced at the southern sky again, and I saw her recalculating, the way she must recalculate constantly, distances against daylight, ambition against weather. "Go down, Reyes. Take the trail east of the river — it's faster than the way you came up. There's a hut at the confluence. Wait out the first blow and walk out clean."

"And if I don't?"

"Then I'll be having this conversation with your ghost," she said, "and I'm told ghosts are heavy."

She turned back toward her tent, and that should have been the end of it — pride choosing solitude, the beat playing out exactly as written, me standing in the wind with my hat in my hands like a suitor dismissed. But at the tent flap she stopped, half-turned, and said something I didn't expect.

"Why does it matter to you? Really. Not the salvage answer. The real one."

And because I'd learned something from a cook with a ladle, I gave her the real one.

"Because my grandfather came down that mountain and never said one word about it for the rest of his life, and I grew up inside that silence, and I am not going to become it." My voice came out rougher than I intended. "Whatever happened up there — whatever he did, whatever Okafor did — somebody has to say it out loud, and it might as well be the two of us, since we're the ones carrying the names."

For a moment — just a moment — something in her face opened, the way ice opens over a stream, showing dark water underneath. She knew exactly what I meant. I could see that she knew. Two heirs, one wound; we were more alike than either of us would admit at gunpoint.

Then it closed.

"East trail," she said. "Confluence hut." And she went inside.

* * *

I didn't take the east trail.

Let the record show I considered it. I stood at the edge of their camp for a long time after her tent flap fell shut, weighing obedience against obsession, and I want it noted for posterity that the sensible option was right there, marked, recommended by the best guide in the range, and I watched it sit there like a lifeboat while I decided to swim.

Instead I walked the perimeter of the moraine's edge until dark, found a boulder with a wind shadow behind it, and made cold camp with my remaining rations and my grandfather's half of the map. Above me the icefall glowed faintly in the last light, seracs stacked like teeth, and somewhere beyond it — if the old prospector's ink could be believed, if the mountain kept its promises, which it famously did not — was a cache with my family's name on part of it and hers on the rest.

The clouds came down overnight. By morning I couldn't see the icefall at all, just white on white, and the temperature had dropped hard enough that my water bottle slushed when I shook it. Early monsoon. Weeks early. Norbu's ladle had pointed true.

I should have gone down. Any competent version of me would have gone down.

Instead I broke camp and started up the moraine alone, following a line of cairns twenty years old, and within the hour I found the first thing the mountain had kept: a rusted plate, half-buried in scree, stamped with an expedition mark I recognized from photographs — the mark of the Okafor-Reyes expedition, the one that killed eleven men and broke two families and sent my grandfather home to his silence.

I knelt in the wind and brushed the dirt away from it with my fingers, the way an archaeologist would, and understood that I had just crossed some line I couldn't uncross.

Behind me, far below, I heard voices on the trail — Mara's team, moving up. Moving toward me. Toward whatever came next.
