## 14. The Moraine Field

The moraine announced itself the way old grief does — not all at once, but in increments you pretend not to notice until you're standing inside it.

We'd climbed since first light through country that had stopped pretending to be hospitable. The glacier below us was a gray tongue laced with meltwater channels, and above it the lateral moraine ran like a spine of broken teeth: boulders the size of prayer wheels, slabs split by frost, gravel that shifted underfoot with a sound like whispered gossip. Tsering walked point without being asked to. Dorje's arm was strapped tight against his chest, and he'd stopped complaining about it somewhere around dawn, which worried me more than the complaining ever had.

"Big rocks," Dorje observed, gesturing at the moraine with his good hand.

"Very big," I agreed.

"Many years of big rocks."

"That's generally how moraines work."

He grunted, satisfied. Conversation on this team had reached the stage where we communicated mostly in observations about geology, which I found preferable to questions about why we were here.

Because here — according to a dead man's map and my own unwilling memory of Reyes's dying instructions — was where the eleven had last been seen alive.

I'd known it was coming for days. I'd felt it approaching the way you feel weather behind a ridge: pressure, a change in the light, an animal unease with no name attached. But knowing a thing is coming and standing in it are different countries, and I have spent most of my life confusing the two.

* * *

We made camp at noon in the lee of a boulder the size of a truck, because the wind up there had opinions and expressed them constantly. Pemba melted snow and made tea with the grim efficiency of a man who has decided the expedition is a form of punishment but intends to be punished well-fed. Namgyal checked the radio out of habit; the static up here sounded like the mountain chewing.

I didn't tell them what this place was. Not yet. I told them we'd rest the afternoon and search the moraine in the morning, which was true as far as it went, and lies by omission are still the only kind I'm good at.

Tsering looked at me over his cup for a long moment. He has a face built for long moments — broad, weathered, patient as a cliff face — and when he deploys it at me I feel like a specimen under glass.

"You know something about these rocks," he said.

"I know a lot about rocks. It's my profession."

"You know something *sad* about these rocks."

"Tsering—"

"You will tell us when you are ready." He drank his tea. "Or you will not, and we will dig anyway, because you are paying us and because the map points here. Both are acceptable."

I wanted to be angry at him for that. I couldn't locate the anger anywhere. It was like searching a room for your keys while holding them.

* * *

I started walking the moraine alone in the afternoon, telling myself it was reconnaissance.

That's the thing about archaeology — it's mourning with a methodology. You learn this early or you don't survive the profession. My father taught me that, actually, though he'd have phrased it better. *Every excavation is a conversation with someone who can't answer,* he said once, crouched over a potsherd in some sun-blasted trench, brushing dirt away with a badger-hair brush he treated more gently than he treated most people. *Your job is to ask good questions and then shut up and listen.*

So I asked good questions of the moraine and tried to shut up and listen.

The field itself was maybe four hundred meters of lateral chaos, a rampart of stone dumped along the glacier's edge by centuries of indifferent arithmetic. Anything left here fifty years ago would be scattered, buried, crushed, or carried. Fifty years of freeze-thaw turns a camp into a rumor. I knew the odds. I've quoted those odds to clients to talk them out of foolish searches.

I searched anyway.

The wind did most of the talking. It came down off the icefall in long gusts, worrying at my hood, and between gusts there was a silence so complete I could hear my own pulse in my ears, which at 5,400 meters is less a sound than a reminder. I walked the line of the old camp — I could feel where it must have been, a shelf of relatively flat ground behind a windbreak boulder, the kind of site any competent expedition leader would choose, and my father was nothing if not competent, whatever else he was supposed to have been.

I found the stove plate two hours in.

It was half-buried under a slab, face-down, and I nearly stepped on it. Something about its shape snagged my eye — too regular for rock, too angular for bone. I knelt. The wind gusted. I dug with my fingers, then with the trowel I carry the way other people carry cigarettes, and worked it free of the gravel.

Rusted almost through, but the stamp survived. A circle, a stylized peak, and letters worn shallow: **S.E. — '74**. Serrano Expedition. The year my father's party went up and eleven people stopped coming down.

I sat back on my heels and held it in both hands and the altitude pressed down on me and the wind pressed down on me and the plate weighed maybe three hundred grams and it was the heaviest thing I have ever lifted.

This was their kitchen. Somebody cooked tea on this. Somebody — my mother's hands, maybe, she'd have been the one, she never trusted anyone else's stoves — somebody lit this burner at 5,400 meters and watched the blue flame and thought about home. And then the storm came, and the rope was cut, or wasn't cut, depending on whose story you believe, and the stove went cold, and the mountain took the rest.

I don't remember deciding to sit down. I was sitting. The plate was in my lap. I was doing the thing I do instead of crying, which is calculating: mass of the artifact, probable alloy, corrosion rate at altitude, decades of exposure. Numbers are a roof I put up when the weather turns.

It wasn't working very well.

* * *

I heard him before I saw him — gravel shifting, that particular careful footfall. Tsering came around the boulder with his hands in his pockets and no expression at all, which from Tsering is a whole speech.

"The tea is cold," he said.

"I'm busy."

"Yes." He looked at the stove plate. He read the stamp. I watched him read it, watched understanding arrive in his face the way dawn arrives on a peak — slowly, then all at once. "Eleven," he said quietly.

"You knew."

"I suspected since the village. The old woman spat at your name, and you let her. A guide who lets a grandmother spit at her without explanation is carrying something heavy." He crouched beside me, unhurried, joints cracking. "Now I know."

"They say my father left them here." My voice came out flat, informational, a briefing. "Abandoned them above the icefall and walked down alone. That's the story. That's the whole story everyone tells."

"And you came to find out if it's true."

"I came because a dying man gave me a map and I owed him." Which was also true. Both things were true, and neither one was the truest thing, and the truest thing was that I needed to know if the man whose hands I remembered brushing dirt from pottery had really walked past this spot and down the mountain while eleven people froze.

Tsering nodded slowly, absorbing this, and then he did the thing that undid me more thoroughly than any confrontation could have.

He stood up, walked ten meters down the moraine, and started looking.

No speech. No permission requested. He just began moving stones with the same unhurried competence he brought to everything — checking hollows, reading wind-scour lines, working the ground the way a man works ground he's hunted for thirty years. After a minute, Dorje appeared, drawn by whatever silent signal passes between old partners, took one look at what Tsering was doing, and started helping with his good arm. Then Pemba. Then Namgyal, radio still slung on his shoulder like he expected the mountain to call in.

Nobody asked me anything. That was the mercy of it. They simply decided, collectively and without discussion, that whatever I was mourning, they would help me mourn it properly, and mourning done properly means digging.

I stayed where I was for another minute, holding the stove plate, and then I got up and joined them, because grief is a poor foreman and someone had to keep the search systematic.

* * *

We worked until the light went bronze and then gray. I set a grid — of course I set a grid, my father's daughter to the marrow — pacing transects across the shelf and marking finds with red tape. Most of what we found was the mountain's own debris: frost-shattered rock, glacial till, a marmot skull polished by wind. But the mountain gives things back, grudgingly, the way a miser drops coins.

A boot eyelet, green with corrosion. A fragment of tent pole, aluminum gone chalky. A button — horn, hand-carved, the kind sold in every bazaar from Leh to Kathmandu, the kind my mother sewed on my father's shirts because she said brass buttons were for soldiers and liars.

Each item went into a labeled bag. Each label was written in my neatest hand. My hands were steady. I want that recorded somewhere: my hands were perfectly steady, and it cost me everything I had.

Dorje found the tin cup at dusk, crushed flat, and held it up and said, softly, "For tea," and Pemba turned away and pretended to check the tent guys, and nobody said anything for a while, and the wind filled the silence with its own commentary.

* * *

After dark we sat in the cook tent and ate dal and rice, and the stove hissed, and I caught myself watching it — watching the flame — and had to make myself stop.

"So," Namgyal said finally. He's the youngest, and the boldest in the way young men are bold about exactly the wrong things. "Tomorrow we keep digging?"

"We keep digging."

"For what? The treasure?" He said it lightly, testing.

"For answers," I said. "The cache is higher. Above the icefall. But what happened to the eleven—" I stopped. Started again. "What happened here matters more than what's up there. Whatever's in the cache, it belongs to this place first."

Tsering nodded slowly, as if I'd passed some examination he hadn't announced. "And if the answers are bad?"

"Then they're bad answers, and we carry them down anyway. That's the job."

"That is not the job I signed for," Dorje said. His splinted arm lay across his knees like a sleeping animal. "But it is a job." He ladled himself more dal. "Better pay than the last one."

Laughter, small and tired, moved around the tent. It's astonishing how much weight a bad joke can hold at altitude. We are all of us, on this team, practicing the art of carrying things we didn't sign up for, and humor is the padding.

* * *

I took first watch, which surprised no one. Sleep and I have been estranged for weeks now; we exchange brief formal visits and part unsatisfied.

At midnight the clouds tore open for twenty minutes and the stars came out with the savage clarity you only get this high, and I stood outside the tent with my breath smoking and looked up the valley toward the icefall. Even in starlight I could see it — a pale scar of broken ice hanging on the mountainside, seracs stacked like crockery in a cupboard someone keeps slamming.

Above that icefall was the cache. Above that icefall was whatever Reyes senior had done, or failed to do, and whatever my father had done, or failed to do, and the truth about whether the man I remembered was a monster or a martyr or just a man who ran out of good options in a whiteout.

And somewhere in the middle distance between here and there, the monsoon was gathering over the plains, weeks early, moving north like a debt collector.

I touched the compass in my jacket pocket. My father's compass, the one I swore I'd sold, the one with the inscription I still hadn't let myself read twice. Its needle swung lazily, finding north, losing it, finding it again — indecisive as me.

"Mara." Tsering's voice, low, from the tent flap. "Sleep. I take the watch."

"It's fine—"

"You dug all day. You will dig all day tomorrow. Sleep." A pause. "The dead are not going anywhere. They have proven this."

I almost laughed. It came out as something adjacent to laughing, which at midnight on a moraine counts.

"Tsering. Thank you. For helping today. You didn't have to ask what it was."

"I did not help you," he said, with terrible gentleness. "I helped them. Whoever they were. That is correct behavior on a mountain." He held the flap open. "You can thank me by being rested enough to lead us up that ice alive."

I went into the tent. I slept, actually slept, three dreamless hours — a personal record for this expedition.

* * *

Morning came gray and windless, which on a glacier is more ominous than a storm. Windless air means weather is deciding something.

We were breaking camp when I saw it.

Namgyal found it, technically — he was walking the perimeter with the radio, hunting signal, and he stopped mid-stride and made a sound I'd never heard him make before, a short sharp syllable in Ladakhi that needs no translation.

Half-buried in the scree at the edge of our grid, where yesterday's wind had stripped away a skin of gravel, lay a rucksack. Rotted canvas, leather straps gone to black lace, frame rusted to lacework. Fifty years of burial, and the wind had peeled the mountain's blanket back overnight, casual as a maid turning down a bed.

I knelt beside it for a long time before touching it. Protocol says photograph in situ, record position, document context. My hands did all of that automatically, the camera clicking, the notebook filling. Inside, I could see the ghost of contents — fabric, metal, something rectangular.

And stitched to the outer pocket, surviving against every probability, a name-tape. Faded almost to nothing. Thread letters on canvas.

I brushed the grit away with the badger-hair brush I have carried for twenty years and never once used, until now.

Three letters. An initial and two more after it.

**M.O.**

My mother's initials.

The windless morning held its breath. Behind me, the team had gone absolutely quiet, five men watching me kneel over a dead woman's bag on a dead camp's edge, and far above us the icefall gleamed like a locked door, and I understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the ground had just rewritten the only childhood I thought I'd had.

My mother died in Delhi. That was the story. That was the story I was raised on, the story I told at dinner parties, the story I had built twenty years of distance on top of.

She was here. She was one of the eleven. She froze on this mountain in a storm, and someone told me she died of fever in a city hospital, and I believed it, because children believe the stories they're given.

"Mara," Tsering said quietly, behind me. "Whose bag is that?"

I opened my mouth to answer, and discovered I had no lie left in me at all.
