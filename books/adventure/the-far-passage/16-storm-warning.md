## 16. Storm Warning

The radio woke me before the light did.

I keep it wrapped in a plastic bag inside my sleeping bag's foot box, because cold kills batteries and batteries are the only honest thing on a mountain — they tell you exactly how much you have left and then they stop lying to you. At 4:40 in the morning it crackled like something chewing through foil, and Dorje groaned from the next tent, and I crawled out into a sky the color of an old bruise to take the call.

It was Pemba, our weather man in Leh, who has never once in eleven years told me what I wanted to hear, which is why I pay him.

"Tsering-la," he said. "The surge has come north early. The models moved overnight. Monsoon landfall in the foothills within seventy-two hours, maybe less. After that, six days of window at altitude, then the passes close for the season."

"Close how?"

"Closed. Snow to the waist, avalanche cycles daily, rivers up to your chest. You know how it closes."

I did know how it closes. I had watched it close once, in 2009, on a mountain whose name I do not say out loud anymore, and I had carried down what was left of a client named Herrmann in a duffel bag because there wasn't enough of him for a stretcher.

"How much window do we have right now?" I asked.

"If you turned around today? Comfortable. If you go up?" He paused, and I could hear him deciding whether to be my employee or my friend. He chose friend. "Tsering-la, whatever she is chasing up there — it isn't worth six days of margin against a monsoon."

I thanked him and sat outside the tent with the radio warm against my chest, watching the first grey light come up the glacier like water filling a bowl, and I thought about what I was going to say, and how I was going to say it, and whether any way of saying it would matter at all.

Because here is the thing about Mara Okafor that people who have only climbed with her don't understand: she hears bad news the way other people hear good news. It lands somewhere in her and gets converted. Fear becomes fuel. Warning becomes schedule. I have seen her take a forecast that would send a Swiss expedition home weeping and turn it into a route plan by lunchtime, cheerful as a woman planning a picnic.

I love her. I want to be clear about that, since everything else I'm going to say in this chapter makes me sound like her enemy. I love her the way you love a river you've ferried things across for years — with respect, with familiarity, and with the permanent knowledge that one day it will not care about you at all.

* * *

At breakfast I laid it out.

We were five around the stove shelter: Mara, me, Dorje, Sonam, and young Norbu, who was on his first big trip and still ate like food was a moral virtue. The map lay between us, folded to its creases so many times it had gone soft as cloth. Mara had it out even at breakfast now. She touched it when she talked, the way some men touch a amulet.

"Pemba says the surge came north," I said. "Landfall below us in three days. We have perhaps six working days before the passes seal. After that we are not climbers, we are residents."

Nobody spoke. Above us the icefall made its small sounds, the settling and cracking of a thing too big to hurry.

"So," I said. "My vote is down. Today. We are fit, we are acclimatized, nothing is broken except Dorje's pride, and we can be in the valley before the rain starts. Whatever is up there will still be up there in spring."

Mara looked at me across the stove. She didn't argue yet. That was the worst part — she took a sip of tea first, giving my words the courtesy of a full swallow, and I knew already that courtesy was the funeral rite.

"You all know why we're here," she said. "Not the fee. The real reason." Her eyes went around the circle and stopped, briefly, on each of us. "There are journals in a cache above the icefall. My father's journals. Twenty years the world has said he abandoned eleven people on this mountain. There may be proof he didn't. And there are families — eleven families — who have spent twenty years not knowing where their people lie."

"And if the proof says he did abandon them?" Dorje asked. He said it without cruelty; Dorje's cruelty lives elsewhere, mostly in his opinions about my cooking. "You carry down the truth either way?"

"I carry down the truth either way."

"Six days," I said. "The cache is two hard days up, if the couloir behaves. Two days back. That leaves two days for weather, for injury, for the mountain changing its mind. In June. On a mountain that eats June."

"The mountain doesn't eat June," Mara said. "The mountain eats carelessness."

"The mountain," said Dorje, "ate Herrmann."

That landed. I hadn't told them the story whole — only the parts that made good teaching, the parts with a lesson attached. Mara looked at me sharply, and I shrugged, because Dorje had been on enough trips to piece it together from the shape of my silences.

"We vote," Mara said quietly. "Everyone votes honestly. No one carries a grudge up the hill. Agreed?"

Agreed, all around, in the way people agree to things they already regret.

I voted down. So did Sonam, who is the most sensible person I employ and the reason I sleep at night on trips where I shouldn't. Dorje voted up — of course Dorje voted up; Dorje would vote up on swimming into a flood if Mara framed it as a route-finding problem — and Norbu, poor boy, looked at his boots and then at Mara and then at his boots again, and voted up, because he is twenty-three and in love with the idea of being the kind of porter history notices.

Three to two. Down.

Mara nodded slowly, and I watched her absorb it, and I thought — for one full, foolish minute — that she would honor it. That the woman who preached honesty on the rope would practice it in the mess tent.

Then she said, "Tsering. Walk with me."

* * *

We walked up-moraine, away from camp, far enough that the others became small figures tending the stove. The cloud had come down to meet us, soft and grey, and the air tasted of snow that hadn't decided yet.

"You're going to ask me to overrule the vote," I said.

"No." She stopped and faced me. "I'm going to ask you to understand why I can't obey it."

"That is the same request wearing different clothes."

She almost smiled. "Almost."

"Mara." I used her name the way you use a hand on someone's sleeve. "Eleven years I have followed you into places that had no business holding human beings, and I have done it because your judgment is the best I have ever worked under. Not because you are always right. Because when you are wrong, you say so, and you turn around. That is the whole contract. That is the entire religion of it. And today you are telling me the religion has exceptions."

"My mother is on this mountain."

The words came out flat and terrible, like stones dropped on ice. I stood very still.

"She came on the expedition," Mara said. "She wasn't supposed to — she was supposed to wait in the valley — but she insisted, and Elias let her, and she was one of the eleven left above the icefall. I found her flask yesterday. Her initials. I grew up believing she died in Manali when I was four. I built my whole life on top of a grave that was empty." Her voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "The vote says go down. The vote is correct. I am asking you to follow me anyway, knowing the vote is correct, and knowing that I cannot."

"Do you hear yourself?"

"Yes. Every word. I've been hearing myself for twenty years, Tsering. It's the one sense I've kept sharp."

I looked past her at the icefall, white and patient, doing what icefalls do, which is wait. Somewhere above it, if the map told true, a dead man had dug a shelter for eleven people and then lain down in it. And here was his daughter proposing to walk up and knock.

"If we go," I said finally, "we go under new terms. Hard terms. Turnaround time is fixed — not judged, fixed. If we are not descending by day five, we descend regardless of where we are, including from the cache door itself. Everyone roped on the icefall, no heroics, no solo scouting. And if anyone — anyone — says the word *down* with fear in their voice, we treat it as data, not weakness."

"Those are good terms."

"They are the price. Say them back to me."

She said them back to me, every one, in the voice she uses for route briefings, precise as a surveyor. And I hated myself a little, standing there in the cold, because I knew exactly what I had just done. I had not refused her. I had negotiated the terms of my own coercion and called it leadership.

A good guide keeps his clients alive. Nobody ever tells you the harder job: keeping yourself alive while following someone you trust more than your own judgment.

* * *

Back at camp, Mara called the team together and did something I have rarely seen a leader do: she told them the truth about the vote.

"Yesterday you voted to descend, and the majority ruled, and I'm overriding it," she said. "I won't dress it up. I'm pulling rank, and I'm doing it because my mother's bones are up there and I will not leave them another winter. Here is what I offer instead: anyone who wants to go down goes down with my blessing, full pay, and no shame. Sonam will lead you out safely — he's the second-best mountaineer I know." A glance at me. "Anyone who stays stays under Tsering's terms, which are better than mine would be, and which are law."

Silence. The stove hissed. Somewhere above, the icefall calved something small and the sound rolled down to us like distant thunder clearing its throat.

Sonam looked at me. I looked at my boots, which had become, in the last hour, very interesting.

"I stay," Sonam said, "because if I leave, you'll die, and if I stay, maybe only some of us die, and I prefer those odds." He said it dryly, but his hand found my shoulder on the way to repack his kit, and it stayed there a moment longer than the joke required.

Dorje cracked his knuckles and said the couloir owed him money anyway. Norbu just started coiling rope, fast, before anyone could look at him too closely and make him examine his choice.

And that was it. That was the whole mutiny and the whole surrender, conducted over twenty minutes and a pot of tea. Four people agreeing to follow a woman uphill against their own votes, because the alternative was letting her go alone, and we had all, by then, spent enough nights around enough stoves to know what she'd actually do alone.

She'd go. Of course she'd go. She'd go lighter and faster and stupider, and she'd probably survive, because she was Mara Okafor, and the mountain seemed to hold some confused old affection for her family it couldn't quite explain to itself.

But we weren't going to find out. That was the decision, disguised as a non-decision. We broke camp at nine, and nobody sang, and nobody joked except Dorje, whose jokes had gone thin as the air.

* * *

One more thing, and then I will let this chapter end, because I have written it three times in my head tonight and each version ends in the same place I don't want to reach.

As we roped up below the icefall, Mara came to me last, checked my harness with unnecessary thoroughness, and said, low, so the others couldn't hear:

"You could have held the line. You could have refused, and they'd have followed you down, and you know it."

"Yes," I said.

"Why didn't you?"

I tightened a buckle that didn't need tightening. "Because in 2009," I said, "there was a man who wanted to turn around, and I was leading, and I knew better, and I overruled him. His name was Herrmann. He is buried under the serac we would have walked around if I had listened." I looked at her. "So I know exactly what I'm doing today. I'm doing it anyway. That's what your father must have felt like, I think, staying up there. Not courage. Just arithmetic with no good answer."

Mara was quiet for a long moment. Then she clipped into the rope behind me and said, "Lead on, Tsering. Day five. Fixed."

Day five, fixed. I put it in my head like a stake driven into ice, and I began to climb toward the thing that had killed my client and taken her parents, and above us the clouds came down the mountain to meet us, patient as debt.

By noon it had begun, very softly, to snow.
