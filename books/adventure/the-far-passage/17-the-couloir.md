## 17. The Couloir

I want it known, before anyone writes anything about this expedition, that I voted to turn back at the moraine. I want it carved somewhere, maybe on the stove plate Mara Okafor carries in her pack like a relic — *Dorje Namgial objected.* History should record that the man with the broken arm saw it coming.

Not that seeing it coming helped. That is the other thing I want recorded: being right on a mountain is worth exactly nothing.

* * *

The morning of the rockfall began with Tsering burning the tea.

This was not entirely his fault. Our fuel situation had gone strange since the storm warning — Mara rationing white gas like a miser counting coins, so we cooked on damp scrub wood we'd carried up from the last green place, and damp scrub wood does not burn so much as it sulks and smokes. The tea came out tasting of ash and regret, which Tsering said was traditional, and Pemba said was punishment, and I said nothing because my mouth was full of it.

We broke camp under a sky that had cleared overnight into that hard, rinsed blue you get after weather, the kind of sky that looks honest and isn't. Above us the couloir went up between two ribs of rock, a gutter of ice and scree running toward the col, and I remember looking at it and thinking what I always think about gutters: things fall down them. That is their whole job. A couloir is a chute the mountain uses to deliver rocks from where they are to where they would rather be, and we were proposing to stand in the delivery lane for four hours.

"Early start means we're through before the sun hits the walls," Mara said. She said it the way she says everything, flat and certain, a woman reading distances off the inside of her own skull.

"The sun is already hitting the walls," I pointed out, because someone had to.

"Higher up it hasn't yet."

"So we run."

"We move efficiently," she said, which is what Mara says instead of *yes.*

Tsering looked at me across the rope coil he was flaking, and something passed between us, the way it does between old colleagues. He had voted to turn back too. He'd been outvoted three to two, with Pemba and young Norbu siding with her because she had never once lost anyone on her routes and because hope, at altitude, is just oxygen debt wearing a nice coat. Tsering's face that morning said: *we follow, we watch, we keep them alive anyway.* My face said: *I know.* Neither of us said anything, because saying things wastes breath, and breath up there is currency.

So we climbed into the gutter.

* * *

For two hours it was only miserable in the ordinary ways. Cold feet. The sullen ice taking our crampons grudgingly. Norbu singing some film song under his breath until Pemba told him that singing above five thousand meters was showing off, and Norbu said his grandmother sang higher than this every day carrying firewood, and Pemba said his grandmother was a yak, and this exchange sustained morale for a surprising length of time.

Mara led. Of course Mara led. She moved through the couloir the way water moves, finding the line without appearing to look for it, and I will grant her this even now, even with my arm the way it is: I have carried loads behind perhaps forty lead climbers in twenty years of this work, and I have seen maybe four who read rock the way she does. It is not cleverness. Cleverness hesitates. She goes, and the mountain, most days, agrees to be gone past.

Most days.

It happened at the constriction, where the two ribs lean toward each other and the route pinches down to a ledge barely wide enough for a loaded man. We were strung out single file, Tsering second, me third, Pemba behind me, and I had just put my hand into a crack for balance when the mountain cleared its throat.

You never hear the rock that gets you. What you hear is the rock that misses — a *tick* somewhere above, small as a knuckle rapping a door, and then your whole body understands before your mind does, and you are pressing yourself against the wall of a gutter that offers no wall, thinking absurd thoughts. I thought about my sister's goats. I thought about whether I had paid the man who was feeding them enough to also feed them in winter. These are the thoughts a falling rock permits you, one or two, no more.

"ROCK!" Tsering's voice, cracked open to its full size, a voice I have heard him use twice in fifteen years.

Then the world became noise.

They came down the chute in a family, half a hillside deciding to relocate, and the sound was not like thunder because thunder ends. This went on. Dust turned the air brown and tasting of iron. Something struck the ledge beside me and burst. Something else — I learned later it was a block the size of a prayer wheel, a big one, the heavy brass kind — came off the rib above and caught me across the left arm on its way to wherever it was going.

I sat down. Not bravely. I sat down the way a sack sits down, all at once, and the dust roared past, and then, after a time that might have been a minute or a season, there was silence, and Norbu's voice saying my name over and over like a man trying to wake me, except I was awake, horribly, completely awake, and my arm did not belong to me anymore.

* * *

Here is what I knew about Mara Okafor before this trip, from the porter lines, from the kitchens of trekking lodges, from the way guides talk when clients aren't listening: she pays full rates and tips on top; she carries her own rope; she has never once raised her voice on a mountain; and no one knows anything else about her. Twenty years of work and she is a locked room. Other guides have divorces, debts, drinking stories, a dead father they mention at the third cup of chang. Mara had a reputation and a silence.

So when she crouched over me in the settling dust, cut my sleeve open with her knife without asking permission — correct instinct, by the way, asking costs time — and looked at what the rock had made of my arm, I watched her face do something I had never seen it do.

It stopped calculating.

Only for a moment. Half a breath. Her eyes went somewhere else, somewhere twenty years away, and came back. Then she was all business again, and I understood that I had seen the locked room's door open one inch, and I filed it away, because a man with a broken arm has little to do but file things away.

The arm was broken badly. Not bone-through-skin badly, thank the gods and my grandmother the yak, but badly — bent where arms don't bend, below the elbow, swelling already like bread rising. Pemba made the sound porters make, a low *hooo* that means *this is beyond us.* Norbu had gone the color of the dust. Tsering was already rigging a shelter out of the packs and a tarp, because Tsering's mind works forward while everyone else's works sideways.

And Mara set the bone.

I want to describe this properly, because people imagine it is quick. It is not quick. She pulled my arm straight — traction, she called it later, as if naming it makes it kinder — and the pain went through me like the rock had gone through the couloir, total, democratic, interested in everything. I screamed. I am not ashamed. I screamed words in three languages, and two of them were about her.

"You," I told her, when I could assemble sentences again, "and your ambition. Your *route.* Your father's ghost and whatever map you carry like a stolen thing. Look what it buys. Look what it costs the men who carry your loads."

She didn't stop working. She splinted the arm with a foam pad and tape, tight and clean, better than the clinic in Leh would have done, and her hands never shook once, and I hated her a little for that, and respected her more than I wanted to.

"Dorje." Quiet. "Drink this."

Water with something bitter in it. I drank. The pain stepped back from a shout to a conversation.

"I'm sorry," she said.

"Sorry doesn't carry loads."

"No," she agreed. "But it's true."

* * *

We got down off the gutter to a shelf wide enough for tents, moving slow, Tsering and Pemba taking my weight on the good side while I cradled the bad arm like a sleeping child I resented. Camp went up. Stove lit. And then evening came down the way it does up there, fast and blue, and the cold arrived, and with the cold came the question everyone was thinking and nobody asked:

Can a man with one arm go down? Or are we now six people carrying seven?

Mara answered it without being asked, which was somehow worse. "We descend together tomorrow. Slow. Dorje walks if he can; we lower him where we can't." She said it looking at the fire, not at me, and I understood that she had already decided, probably before the rockfall ended, and that my vote — my precious, historical, carved-in-stone objection — counted for exactly as much as it had that morning.

Which is why I cursed her. Properly, that time, sitting by the fire with my arm strapped across my chest, while the others pretended to be asleep in their bags and were not. I cursed her ambition and her silences and the map I had seen the edge of in her pack, and the initials chiseled on stone that Tsering had told me about in a low voice at the moraine, and the whole inheritance of it, the whole stubborn bloodline of a dead man's unfinished business landing on the backs of hired men.

She took it. That is the thing I keep returning to. She sat there and took it, hands around a tin cup, steam rising past a face that gave away nothing and everything, and when I finally ran out of curses — and I had a good supply, twenty years' worth, I'd been saving them — she was quiet a long moment.

Then she said, "You're right."

That stopped me harder than any argument would have.

"You're right that it's my ambition. You're right that you're paying for it with your arm. So you should know what it is you're carrying." She looked into the fire. "My father's name is Elias Okafor. You've heard it."

Everyone has heard it. Elias Okafor, the Serrano disaster, the guide who walked down and left eleven men above the icefall. The name mothers used to frighten children out of following foreigners up mountains. The fraud. Even I, who have no head for history, knew that story the way you know a bad smell.

"He didn't leave them," she said. "He stayed. He stayed and dug them a shelter and froze beside them, and the man who actually ran — Reyes, the grandfather of the man following us — came down and told the world otherwise, because the truth would have hanged him. There's proof. In a cache above the icefall. Journals. His hand, writing what happened." Her voice did something small on the word *hand,* a hitch, quickly smoothed. "That's what's in my pack. Not treasure. Testimony. Eleven families have spent twenty years believing their men were abandoned by a coward, and the coward was the one who came home."

The fire popped. Somewhere above us, the mountain ticked and settled, doing its bookkeeping.

"Why tell me?" I asked. It came out rougher than I meant. "Why now?"

"Because you called it ambition, and it is, but it's not mine alone anymore. It's yours the moment you carry a load on this route. You deserve to know what the load is." She glanced at my arm, at the splint, and something crossed her face — guilt, real guilt, the kind that lives in the body and not the mouth. "I can't fix your arm. I know that. I could tell myself the mountain did it, but I chose the couloir, and I chose the season, and I'll choose tomorrow too. So there's no absolution in this story, Dorje. I'm not offering any. I'm offering the truth, because it's the only thing I've got that weighs less than what you're carrying."

I sat with that. The pain sat with me too, patient, an old friend.

"It doesn't fix anything," I said finally.

"No."

"But it's heavier than a lie. I'll give it that." I shifted, found the one position where the arm forgave me. "My uncle says a man who digs up bones should expect to answer some questions himself."

"Your uncle sounds wise."

"He sounds wise. He is mostly a nuisance." I closed my eyes. "Tomorrow we descend. Slowly. And Okafor — if you get us all down, I will carry your testimony to the end of the range and call it fair wages. But if you ever again choose a gutter because the sun *hasn't hit it yet,* I will break my own other arm so I have an excuse to go home."

Something that was almost a laugh moved through her, brief as heat lightning. "Terms accepted."

* * *

I slept, in the end. Not well — the arm woke me twice, throbbing its dull editorial — but I slept, and here is the strange part, the part I have turned over since like a stone in the palm: I slept easier than I had any night before on this expedition.

Confession without absolution, she called it, or near enough. She was right that it fixed nothing. My arm still broken, the monsoon still coming, the cache still waiting above the icefall like a debt unpaid. But a lie shared is a lighter load than a lie carried alone, and I think she had been carrying hers since Leh, maybe since childhood, and I think — though she would deny it with that flat certainty of hers — that putting it down in front of witnesses cost her something and bought her something, both at once.

Before sleep took me I heard her move in the dark, heard the whisper of her checking my splint without waking me, adjusting the tape with those steady, unshaking hands. I kept my breathing slow and let her do it. Some mercies are best received asleep.

In the morning the sky had changed. Clouds sat on the western peaks like uninvited guests who intend to stay the winter, and the wind had turned, and Tsering stood outside his tent looking at it with the expression of a man reading bad news in a language he hoped he'd forgotten.

Mara came out of her tent, looked at the same clouds, and said the thing I knew she would say, the thing I had already begun, in some tired corner of myself, to forgive her for:

"We move now. While the window holds."

Above the icefall, I thought, watching her strike camp with those quick sure hands. Everything waits above the icefall. And below us, far below, the first rain of the monsoon was already falling on country we would have to cross to get home.
