## 22. Two Names on One Mountain

I want to tell you that I handled it well. That I stood in the snow like a reasonable man and weighed the evidence with an open mind. I did not. What I did was hear my grandfather called a murderer and a coward by a woman holding a tin box, and what I felt was not doubt.

It was the feeling you get when the floor of your house turns out to have been painted on.

"Read it again," I said. "Slowly. Out loud."

Mara didn't move. Snow was coming down harder now, fat flakes that had weight to them, the kind that means business rather than weather. She stood over the open cache with her headlamp throwing her shadow across the tin's contents — journals wrapped in oilcloth, photographs in a separate envelope, a supply ledger gone brown at the edges. Everything preserved by cold and altitude and twenty years of nobody looking.

"I've read it," she said. "Twice."

"You've read it once and wanted it twice." My voice came out flat and strange to me. "You came up this mountain wanting him innocent. Of course that's what you found."

"And you came up wanting him guilty?" She said it without heat, which made it worse. "That's the trade we made in chapter one, Devan. You told me Elias Okafor abandoned eleven men. Your grandfather told you that story his whole life. Now there's paper."

"There's paper written by a man who knew he was dying." I heard myself reaching and hated every word. "Alone on a mountain for days. Hypoxic. Grieving. You'd believe anything a frozen man wrote about the person he hated?"

"He didn't hate him." For the first time something cracked in her voice. "He loved him. That's what makes it worse — read it, read how he writes about him, the first hundred pages are practically love letters to Tomas Reyes's competence—"

"Don't say his name like you knew him."

The words came out before I could stop them, and they were unfair, and I knew they were unfair, because she hadn't known either of them and neither had I, not really, and that was the whole problem. But grief doesn't check its arithmetic. It just spends.

Mara went very still. Snow gathered on her shoulders. Behind her, Tsering and Dorje had retreated to the shelter wall, pretending to sort rope, giving us the wide berth people give a fire that might explode.

"My grandfather carried me on his shoulders," I said, quieter now, because shouting into falling snow is like arguing with a river. "He taught me to read contour lines when I was six. He never once raised his voice. He woke before dawn every morning of his life until his heart quit, and he did it — do you know why he did it? Because he said sleep was a debt and he intended to die solvent." My breath fogged and hung between us. "Men who abandon eleven people don't wake up early for fifty years trying to pay something back."

"No," she agreed. "They usually run. Or drink. Or talk too much." She looked down at the journals. "He did none of those things. He married late, kept one photograph face-down in a drawer, and paid for a memorial plaque in Leh that lists eleven names and spells one of them wrong. Did you know about the plaque?"

I did not know about the plaque. A cold that had nothing to do with the weather moved through me.

"The eleventh name," she said. "It's spelled *Okafor* but it isn't my father. It's my mother. Anjali Okafor. He put her on the stone as if she were one of the men he left behind, because that's where she died, Devan. On the ice. With them."

"That's not proof. That's guilt. Guilt makes monuments out of anything."

"It does," she said. "So does love. We're both standing inside somebody else's monument right now, and only one of ours has dates and ration weights in it."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being someone's grandson: you inherit their story before you're old enough to audit it. By the time you can ask questions, the answers have already been poured into you like concrete, and digging them out costs more than most foundations can survive.

My grandfather's story was simple, which should have been my first warning. Simple stories are load-bearing. Elias Okafor — brilliant, arrogant, foreign — took eleven men above the icefall against all advice. The storm came. Okafor saved himself. Eleven men did not come home. Tomas Reyes survived the descent, testified at the inquiry, was cleared of blame, and spent the rest of his life being quietly, politely pitied by everyone who understood what he must have endured.

Pity is a strange inheritance. It looks like respect from the outside. Inside, it's a splinter working toward the bone. I grew up watching my grandfather absorb that pity at family dinners, at climbing-club anniversaries, at the funerals of the last surviving relatives of the eleven, where he always sat in the back row and always left before the food. I built my whole adult self on the project of restoring him. Salvage work, literally — I recovered expedition wreckage, sold it, funded expeditions to find what the mountain still held. If I could bring home enough of the past, I thought, the past would finally acquit him.

And now a woman with her father's compass was telling me the concrete was poured backwards.

"Give me the journals," I said.

"No."

"Mara. Give them to me. I'll read everything. Every page. If your father says what you think he says, I'll—" I stopped, because I didn't know what I'd do, and she saw me stop.

"You'll what? Believe me? You'll believe a dead man over your own grandfather, just like that?" She shook her head slowly. "No. You'll take them down off this mountain and put them in a drawer next to his maps and spend ten years finding reasons. And the families of eleven dead men will wait another decade while two heirs decide whose ghost gets custody."

"They're *my* family's papers as much as yours. His partner wrote half of them — look at the handwriting, some of those entries are Reyes—"

"I know." Her voice dropped. "I know, Devan. That's the part that keeps me awake. Both our names are in that tin. That's why I'm not walking away with it, and why you're not either."

Snow thickened around us until the world contracted to headlamp-radius. Somewhere below, the wind found its voice in the couloir, a long low note like a struck pipe. I became aware, distantly, that we had been standing outside the cache entrance for the better part of an hour and that the storm had stopped being a threat and started being a fact.

Tsering appeared at my shoulder. He did that the way mountains appear — no approach, just presence. He looked at Mara, then at me, then at the sky, with the expression of a man doing arithmetic he'd done many times before.

"The storm is here," he said. "Both of you inside, or both of you become a story someone else argues about."

"We're not finished," I said.

"Yes," he said pleasantly. "You are. Until it breaks." He stepped between us, not physically pushing — Tsering never pushed, he simply occupied the space you were about to occupy — and steered me toward the shelter crevice with a hand on my pack strap like I was a client having a bad day at altitude. Which, in fairness, I was.

Inside, the cache hollow opened into a chamber maybe three meters deep, walled with stacked stone, floored with the flattened supply tins of men who had expected survivors and prepared a home for them. Dorje had the stove going, feeding it slivers of fuel tablet with the economy of the genuinely worried. The light was orange and small. Outside, the snow erased the world at a rate I could almost hear.

Mara set the tin between us. Not near herself. Between us. Centered, deliberate, like a border marker.

Then she took out the journals and began to read.

Not to me. To the room. Her headlamp steady, her voice low and even, she read her father's entries aloud — the route descriptions first, precise and warm, a man cataloguing a landscape he adored; then the dig notes, the pottery shards, the excitement leaking through the professional tone like watercolor through paper. I sat against the far wall with my arms crossed and my jaw set and listened to a dead stranger be charming, and I hated how charming he was, and I hated more that some part of me recognized the voice, because I had spent years reading Elias Okafor's published field reports and the cadence was identical, and identical voices belong to identical liars or identical men, and I couldn't yet bear to consider which.

"Entry for the fourteenth," Mara said, and her voice changed, and I knew before she read it, the way you know a storm is coming by the pressure in your ears.

*"Whiteout since dawn. Tomás insists we descend. I insisted the injured cannot be moved. He took the fixed line with the strong climbers. I will not write what I think happened on that rope. Eleven remain. Anjali remains. We are digging in."*

"Stop," I said.

She stopped. She looked at me across the small orange room, snowmelt dripping somewhere behind the wall, Dorje frozen mid-motion with a fuel tablet in his fingers, Tsering sitting perfectly still by the entrance like a man guarding a door he hoped no one would use.

"My grandfather didn't cut a rope," I said. "There's nothing in there about cutting a rope. He *descended*. With the able-bodied. In a whiteout, on a fixed line, which is what any competent climber does — that's textbook, that's what you're supposed to do—"

"And leave eleven injured men under snow with no line down?" Mara's composure finally tore. "He took the rope, Devan. The *only* rope. He didn't send it back. He didn't anchor it. Read the next entry — my father describes searching for the line in the whiteout for two days. Two days. He dug that shelter with his hands because they had nothing else. And the last entries—" she stopped, pressed her lips together, mastered herself with visible effort. "The last entries are ration weights. He recorded ration weights until he couldn't hold the pencil. There's a final page that's just numbers. Names and numbers. Like a ledger. Like he was keeping the books for the living even after the living stopped arriving."

Silence. The stove hissed. Snow ticked against the stone above us like fingernails.

I looked at the journal lying open on the tin, and I wanted, with a violence that frightened me, to pick it up and throw it into the storm. To let the mountain eat the evidence the way it had eaten everything else. Because that was the other thing my grandfather taught me: when the weather turns, you secure the camp or you lose it. And my entire camp — fifty years of pity converted to purpose, a career built on restoration, a boy on a dead man's shoulders learning contour lines — was secured to a story that was, page by page, coming apart in front of me.

"You want to know the worst part?" Mara said quietly. "I believed your version for twenty years. Before Reyes gave me the map. My father abandoned eleven men — that was just weather to me, background radiation. I built a life on the far side of it. So when I say I understand what this costs, I'm not being generous. I'm being specific."

"You don't understand anything," I said, "because you got the ending you wanted. I'm the one standing in the ruins."

"Then stand in them," she said. "But don't burn the evidence to make the ruins prettier."

Dorje made a small sound, and we both turned. He wasn't looking at either of us. He was looking at the supply tins underfoot — the ones the cache floor was made of — and his face had gone strange.

"These rations," he said. "These were meant for the eleven."

Nobody answered. Nobody had to. The floor we were sleeping on was a rescue that arrived twenty years late, and we were camping on top of it, and outside the snow kept falling on the place where eleven people had waited for exactly this kind of tin and it never came down to them.

I put my head in my hands. And for the first time since the icefall, I didn't pray, and I didn't argue, and I didn't plan. I just sat inside my grandfather's collapsed story and listened to it settle, the way a serac settles, deep and structural and irreversible.

Mara closed the journal. She didn't hide it. She left it on the tin between us, open to the page of names and numbers, facing me.

"Storm's got us for a while," she said. "Read whatever you want. Whenever you're ready."

I wasn't ready. I reached for the journal anyway — because readiness is a luxury of people with time, and the storm was taking that from both of us — and that was when Tsering, still motionless by the entrance, said:

"Someone is coming up the couloir. One man. Alone."

We all turned. Through the gap in the cairn wall, beyond the falling curtain of white, a single headlamp bobbed and flickered in the dark, climbing toward us out of the storm.
