## 23. Whiteout

**MARA**

The storm arrived the way bad news does — first a rumor on the wind, then all at once, everywhere.

We had been in the cache shelter four hours when the light died. Not dusk-died; *erased*. One moment I could see the crevasse field below us, blue and patient as glass; the next, the world was a gray towel pressed against my face. The wind found the crevice mouth and began to scream into it like something with a grievance.

"Cozy," Devan said.

He was sitting where Tsering had put him, which was as far from the journals as the shelter allowed — maybe three meters of packed snow and stone between him and the open tin. He'd read over my shoulder for exactly one page before Tsering had cleared his throat in a way that carried the authority of a border checkpoint. Now Devan sat with his back against the supply tins — my father's supply tins, packed sixty years ago for eleven people who never came — and watched me watch the weather.

"We're pinned," I said. "Three days, minimum. Maybe four."

"You've done this before."

"I've done every version of this before. This is just the first time I've done it with someone I'd rather strangle than share a stove with."

He almost smiled. It was the first thing resembling an expression he'd shown since reading that page — the page where Elias Okafor's careful handwriting described his partner cutting the rope line in a whiteout not so different from this one. "Fuel?" he asked instead.

I checked. The answer was ugly. "Two stoves' worth if we're careful. We melt snow in shifts, we don't boil anything twice, and nobody dries their socks by the flame."

"My socks are beyond drying," Devan said. "They've achieved a new state of matter."

"Tsering will hear you complaining and take them away as punishment."

Tsering, Dorje, and Pemba had the inner alcove, Dorje's splinted arm propped on a rucksack like a visiting dignitary. They were pretending to sleep with the disciplined theater of men who knew the two of us needed to talk and were giving us no choice but to do it.

The truth was, I didn't know how to talk to him. I knew how to interrogate him, negotiate with him, out-climb him. Talking required admitting that the thing in the tin had detonated in both of us at once, and that we were now two people standing in the same crater.

I fed the stove. The flame made our shadows enormous on the ice wall, two giants leaning toward each other.

"Tell me about him," Devan said suddenly. "Elias. Before."

I stared at the flame. "Why?"

"Because I've spent my whole life knowing him as a name on a disgrace. And today I found out the name might be a lie. I want to know what it was before it was either."

* * *

**DEVAN**

She took her time answering, which I was learning was how she did everything — Mara Okafor treated words like rope, coiling them before she trusted them to hold weight.

"He hummed," she said finally. "When he worked. Badly. Some song he picked up in Lagos, I think, before I was born. My mother used to say you could map a dig site by ear — follow the humming." Her voice did something complicated on the word *mother*, and smoothed itself over. "He talked to artifacts. Full sentences. 'Well, hello, who broke you?' That kind of thing. To potsherds."

"That's insane."

"It's completely insane. He'd have gotten along with your grandfather, probably. Two madmen on a mountain."

I looked at the tin. Sixty years of cold had furred its edges with frost crystals, tiny gardens of them. Inside were journals, photographs, a ledger — and a story that unmade my childhood the way a serac unmakes a route.

My grandfather, Tomás Reyes, was the fixed point of my life. Every other man in my family drifted — jobs, wives, countries — but Grandpa Tomás was granite. He taught me knots. He taught me that fear is information. He never once spoke of the Serrano expedition, and I understood even as a boy that this silence was load-bearing, that the whole architecture of him rested on it, and that you did not knock on it.

So I built my own room inside his silence. I became a salvage climber because he'd been a prospector. I learned mountains because mountains were where he'd become himself. I chased this cache for two years because I thought — God help me — I thought I was recovering his honor. Finding proof that Elias Okafor had abandoned eleven men, including, apparently, Okafor's own wife. Proof that would let me hand my grandfather's good name back to him across the grave, polished.

Instead I got a dead man's diary saying the opposite, written in a hand so steady it made me sick.

"What was he like, your grandfather?" Mara asked. She'd turned from the stove, and in the stove-light her face had lost its guide's armor. She looked, for the first time since Leh, like someone's daughter.

"Larger than the house," I said. "You know how some old men shrink? He expanded. Filled rooms. When he laughed, dogs in the next valley started barking." I pulled off a glove and flexed my fingers near the warmth. "He had nightmares, though. My grandmother said he'd wake up calling names. Eleven names. She wrote them down once, from the doorway, so she'd know who they were. She never told him she knew."

Mara was very still. "Did he ever say what happened up here?"

"Once. Drunk, at my wedding, of all places. He grabbed my arm hard enough to bruise and said, 'The mountain doesn't kill you, mijo. It just stops holding you up.'" I shook my head. "I thought it was poetry. Old man poetry."

"And now?"

"Now I think it was a confession with the grammar removed."

* * *

**MARA**

Watch shifts kept the night honest. Tsering set the rotation without asking either of us — two hours each, Devan and me alternating, which meant every changeover we woke each other, and every changeover there was nothing to do but talk, because the storm had eaten all other subjects.

Somewhere around what I estimated was midnight — time goes strange above six thousand meters, stretches like taffy — he asked the question I'd been waiting for since Leh.

"Why did you really come? And don't give me the fee. You don't need the fee. You could buy the outfitter."

I should have lied better. Twenty years of guiding clients up mountains while carrying private cargo — grief, ambition, a father's compass I couldn't stop packing — had made me fluent in deflection. But the altitude strips things. Thin air, thin excuses.

"When I was nine," I said, "he came home from a season and brought me a rock. Just a rock, gray, nothing. He said, 'This came from higher than any bird flies. Hold it when you can't sleep and remember the world is bigger than your bedroom.' I held that rock for years. Then he died, and the world decided he was a fraud, and I—" I fed a pinch of snow to the stove, watched it collapse into water. "I stopped holding rocks. I stopped holding anything. I became very good at distances. Kilometers, vertical meters, days of food. Numbers don't disappoint you."

"And the map?"

"The map was the first thing in twenty years that pointed somewhere numbers couldn't reach." I looked at him. "I told myself I wanted vindication. I think mostly I wanted my father's voice. Even if the voice said something terrible. A terrible true father is better than a heroic ghost."

Devan nodded slowly. "That's the most honest thing anyone's said to me on a mountain."

"Don't get used to it. Hypoxia makes me sentimental."

"Hypoxia," he agreed solemnly. "Definitely the hypoxia."

And then — I don't know which of us moved first, or if the stove-light simply shifted — something loosened. Not forgiveness. Nothing so clean. More like two climbers discovering they'd been roped together all along and neither had checked the knot.

* * *

**DEVAN**

The second night, the storm found a new register, lower, almost conversational, and we talked about mothers.

Hers had come on the expedition. That was the wound under the wound — Mara had grown up believing her mother, Amara, had died years later, elsewhere, ordinary. The flask with her initials had rewritten that in a single afternoon. Elias hadn't just stayed with the stranded eleven. He'd stayed with his wife, dug her a shelter with his hands, and frozen beside her rather than walk down alone.

"She chose to go," Mara said. She'd been turning that fact over for two days; I could see it wearing grooves in her. "The journal says she insisted. Said a dig was a family affair. He argued. There are three pages of argument, transcribed, like he needed to keep the argument itself."

"Maybe he did. Maybe writing it down was how he kept hoping."

"My mother annotated his notes. Did you see? In the margins. Little arrows, corrections. She was better at stratigraphy than he was and she knew it." Something happened to her face that was half laugh, half flood, held at the halfway mark by pure professional discipline. "I have her handwriting. I never had her handwriting. I have one photograph and a secondhand memory, and now I have her arguing about soil layers in the margins of a dead man's notebook, and I don't know whether it's the cruelest or kindest thing the mountain has ever done."

I thought about my grandmother writing down eleven names from a doorway.

"They're both in there," I said. "Your parents. Both voices. Whatever else that tin is, it's the only place in the world where they're still talking to each other."

Mara looked at me for a long moment. "You're better at this than you pretend."

"I'm worse at most things than I pretend. Let me have this one."

* * *

**MARA**

The third day the fuel ran to fumes and we melted snow by candle-stub, which is to say we didn't, and drank our water cold and ate cold food and let the cold do what cold does, which is simplify. By then the shelter smelled like six people and sixty-year-old canvas, and nobody complained, because complaint costs heat.

Dorje's arm throbbed; I could tell by how quietly he lay. Quiet Dorje is loud trouble. I changed his dressing by headlamp and he informed me, with great dignity, that if he lost the arm he was billing me for it, and I informed him that my rates included dismemberment insurance at no extra charge, and Pemba snorted, and the small economy of the team ticked on.

But underneath the small economies, the big arithmetic ran: air thinner, food shorter, storm showing no interest in ending. And Devan and I taking our watches together, hour after hour, two heirs of one catastrophe, keeping a fire alive in a hole in a mountain that had already taken everything from both our families once.

On the third night he said, "When we get down — if we get down — what happens to the journals?"

It was the question I'd been circling for three days. I gave it the respect of silence first.

"Reyes's promise," I said finally. "'Deliver what's in the cache to whoever deserves it.' I've been chewing on that. I thought it meant me. Then I thought it meant you. I think we were both wrong."

"Who, then?"

"The families. All eleven. It's their testimony. Your grandfather's crime, my father's death, their people. We're not owners, Devan. We're couriers. We always were — him with his map, me with my promise. The mountain just made us carry it together."

He was quiet long enough that I checked he wasn't hypoxic. He wasn't. He was crying, silently, the way men cry who learned it from grandfathers with nightmares — efficiently, without drama, letting the dark do the work.

"Yeah," he said at last, rough. "Yeah. Okay."

It was the first thing we'd ever agreed on. It felt less like victory than like setting down a load I'd forgotten I was carrying.

Then Tsering emerged from the alcove, looked at the two of us, looked at the stove, and delivered his verdict with the timing of a man who'd been listening to every word: "Good. Now sleep. Storm sounds different."

I listened. Under the wind's monotone, something had shifted — a lighter note, a gap.

The storm was breaking.

And below us, six thousand meters of descent waited, with a fevered porter, a dying glacier, and the monsoon coming north like a debt collector.
