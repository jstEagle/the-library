## 24. Dorje's Fever

The storm's third day, Dorje began to talk to his arm.

Not to us — to the arm itself, in a low, argumentative mutter, as though the two of them had a long history of grievances and had finally decided to settle accounts. He sat propped against the cache tins with his splinted forearm across his knees and told it, quietly, that it was being ungrateful, that he had carried it up every mountain since he was sixteen, and that if it insisted on this behavior he would leave it behind at base camp like a client who couldn't keep pace.

I translated none of this for Mara or Devan. Some things are private between a man and his body.

But by evening the muttering stopped, which was worse, and when I put my hand against his forehead it came away hot enough to make me think of rocks left out in the sun at 4,000 meters, the kind that scald through gloves. The arm beneath its splint had gone from swollen to shining, the skin tight and dark, and there was a red line climbing toward his elbow like something reading a map of its own.

"Infection," Mara said. She said it the way she said most things — flat, precise, already three calculations deep. "Cellulitis, maybe worse. If it reaches the lymph system—"

"It has reached," I said. "Look at his eyes."

Dorje's eyes were bright and slightly wrong, focused on some middle distance where perhaps his arm was apologizing at last. He smiled at me with tremendous goodwill. "Tsering-la," he said, "tell the memsahib I'm fine. Tell her the mountain and I have an understanding."

"He's delirious," Devan said.

"He's polite," I said. "It's different. Delirious men say true things."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about high-altitude medicine: it is mostly arithmetic performed while terrified. You count pulse. You count breaths. You count the hours of fuel left in the stove, the grams of food, the milliliters of water you can melt per minute from snow you must first shovel into a pot with hands that don't work well above 5,500 meters. And then you do the cruelest sum of all, which is the one where you divide the distance down by the speed a sick man can walk, and the answer comes back in a language you understand perfectly.

Mara did the sums aloud, because she is the sort of person who believes arithmetic shared is arithmetic survived.

"Six days pinned here minimum if we wait for the storm to fully break. Maybe more. He doesn't have six days." She looked at the red line on Dorje's arm the way she'd once looked at a serac overhanging our route — measuring not whether it would fall but when. "He has maybe two. Three if we're lucky and God is bored."

"And below?" I asked, though I knew.

"The couloir. Then the glacier. Then the moraine, then the river country." She ticked them off on her fingers, each one a place where a healthy man could die and a sick one certainly would. "In this weather, no. In any weather, it's brutal. But if the storm lifts even briefly—"

"If," said Devan.

He'd been sitting apart from us for two days now, ever since the journals, ever since the tin came open and his grandfather's name turned inside out like a glove. He hadn't argued again. That frightened me more than arguing. A man who argues still believes the world can be talked into shape. Devan had gone quiet the way rock goes quiet before it moves.

Now he stood, crossed the shelter, and crouched beside Dorje without asking permission. He took the hot hand gently, turned it over, looked at the nails, the pulse ticking in the wrist. He had his grandfather's hands, I realized — broad, careful, made for rope work. It occurred to me that both of them had spent their lives handling lines attached to other people's lives, and that only one of them had ever let go.

"I know the lower route," Devan said. "My grandfather's maps. There's a shelf system on the west flank of the couloir — the expedition used it for the descent in '61. It cuts two days off the moraine approach. Nobody uses it because it isn't on any modern survey, but I've got the annotations. Distances, anchors, everything. His handwriting." He paused. "Apparently the man could draw a route even if he couldn't hold a rope."

Nobody laughed. It was the first joke anyone had made since the journals opened, and it landed in the shelter like a stone dropped down a crevasse — we all heard it falling for a long time.

"You're offering to guide us down," Mara said. Not a question. A blade laid flat on the table between them.

"I'm offering to get your porter off this mountain," Devan said. "You can hate me the whole way down. I'll carry the rope."

Mara looked at him for a long moment. I have watched her refuse clients, refuse storms, refuse her own body's protests for twenty years, and I know all her refusals by heart. This was not one of them. This was a woman doing arithmetic and finding, to her visible disgust, that the numbers had aligned against her pride.

"Your rope work?" she said.

"Better than yours on descent. No offense."

"Some taken." She exhaled. "Accepted. Provisionally. Tsering?"

They both looked at me, which was correct. I am the one who votes last because I am the one whose vote everyone secretly fears.

I looked at Dorje. He had drifted into a shallow sleep, his good hand curled near his chest, and even asleep he seemed to be negotiating with someone. Twenty-two years I had climbed with this man. He had held my rope on the Kangri traverse when my footwork failed. He had carried a client's entire load plus his own for six hours above the icefall rather than let the man turn back humiliated. He had never once, in twenty-two years, asked me for anything except more chili sauce.

"We move when the storm gives us a window," I said. "However small. However stupid it looks."

"It will look very stupid," Mara said.

"Then we will be consistent."

* * *

The window came at four in the morning, the way windows do — suddenly, grudgingly, and with every sign of closing again out of spite.

I woke because the sound of the wind changed. Anyone who has slept through mountain storms knows this: the storm has a voice, and when the voice drops from a scream to a mutter, you have minutes, not hours. I crawled to the shelter mouth and pushed aside the blocked snow and looked out into a world gone strangely gentle. Snow still fell, but softly, in fat unhurried flakes. Above us, through a tear in the cloud no wider than a prayer shawl, stars.

"Up," I said. "Everyone up. We are leaving."

We moved the way you move when every motion costs warmth you cannot replace — no wasted words, no fumbling. Mara melted water and forced antibiotics and tea into Dorje, who woke confused and burning and, when told we were descending, tried to stand and help strike camp, because Dorje would try to help strike camp from inside a coffin. We tied him between Devan and me on a short rope and told him, firmly, that his job was to walk and complain, and he agreed to the first part.

Devan rigged the anchor at the couloir head with a speed that shut something in all of us. He didn't show off. He just worked — flaking rope, placing protection, testing each piece with a sharp downward jerk — and his hands moved like they remembered this route personally, like his grandfather's annotations had been copied not onto paper but into muscle somewhere down the Reyes family line. I found myself watching him and thinking uncomfortable thoughts about inheritance. About how a man transmits himself to his grandson without meaning to, the way a river transmits its course to the valley.

Mara went first. Of course she went first. She carried the journals against her spine in a waterproof sack strapped tight, and I noticed she checked the straps twice, three times, the way other people check on children.

The couloir in fresh snow is a lesson in humility. Every step had to be tested; every hold had to be earned. Dorje descended badly, listing, his boots finding the rhythm of fever rather than the rhythm of rock, and twice Devan caught his full weight on the rope without a word, lowering him past bad ground with a smoothness that made me revise my opinion of the man upward by several degrees. I had spent eleven days thinking of him as an obstacle with a family resemblance. It turns out he was a climber who happened to be carrying a grief.

Halfway down, the clouds began to close again. I felt it before I saw it — that pressure change, that thickening of the air, the mountain drawing its blanket back up.

"Mara," I called. "The window is shutting."

"How long?"

"An hour. Perhaps less."

She looked up the couloir, then down, doing her arithmetic. I watched her do it. I have watched this woman calculate distances instead of feeling them for twenty years, and I have often thought it was armor. But watching her now, with a sick man on the rope and a stranger holding the other end and her father's testimony pressing cold against her spine, I understood it differently. The calculating wasn't the absence of feeling. It was what her feeling looked like when it grew up. Love, in her language, was always expressed as a number: hours of fuel, meters of rope, degrees of fever, hours of window.

"We make the shelf," she said. "Devan — how far to the first bivouac site on your grandfather's route?"

"Two hours. A cut ledge, sheltered, room for five. He marked it 'adequate.' His highest praise."

"Then we're adequate," Mara said. "Move."

* * *

We made the ledge as the storm closed over us like a fist.

It was, as promised, adequate: a shelf of cut-and-natural rock wide enough for four bodies and a stove, roofed by an overhang, walled from the wind by fortune and geometry. Devan got Dorje out of the wind first, then the rope, then the fuel. Mara got the journals under her jacket, against her body, where they would stay warm enough not to crack. I got the stove lit on the third match and felt the small triumph of flame in a white world the way I always feel it — as a personal insult to the weather.

Dorje drank the tea I gave him and looked at me with eyes that were clearer than they had been, the antibiotic finally throwing punches, and said, "Tsering-la. Did I complain adequately?"

"Insufficiently. You'll have to do better tomorrow."

"I'll practice in my sleep."

He slept. Actual sleep, not fever-sleep, his breathing rough but even, his arm cradled on his chest like something he had decided, provisionally, to forgive.

And then there were four of us awake on a ledge in a storm, sharing a fire the size of a cupped hand, and I did something I almost never do, which is speak first.

"You know," I said to Devan, "when you joined us, I told Mara it was a mistake."

"You voted against it?"

"I voted nothing. I wait. Waiting is my whole method. But I thought it." I fed a pinch of fuel to the flame. "I thought: this man wants the journals, and he will take them when we are weak. Now I think something else."

"What's that?"

"I think you want the journals less than you want to know what your grandfather was. And those are different hungers. One steals. The other just walks a long way and stands in the cold looking at a door."

Devan stared into the fire for a long time. Beside me, Mara had gone very still, the way she goes still when she is listening with her whole body.

"When I was nine," Devan said finally, "my grandfather taught me to tie a bowline. He put his hands over mine and he said, 'This knot never slips. Remember that. Whatever else, remember that.'" He turned his palms toward the fire. "Twenty years I've been trying to build a life on a knot that slipped. Eleven men, Tsering. He cut the line and eleven people died, and he came home and taught me knots."

The wind pressed against our overhang and gave up.

"My father," Mara said quietly, "dug where others wouldn't. Someone wrote that on his compass. I used to think it was praise." She pulled the compass from her pocket — she carries it now, always, though its needle is unreliable this high and this far north — and looked at it without opening the lid. "Maybe it was instructions."

We sat with that. Three heirs of other people's mountains, keeping a fire alive for a fourth man who was dreaming of chili sauce.

Somewhere below us, under two days of rock and ice, lay the moraine where the eleven had last been seen, and the crevice where Elias Okafor had chosen to freeze beside his wife rather than descend alone, and the truth that two families had carried in opposite directions for twenty years until the mountain, tired of the arrangement, had handed it back.

"Tomorrow," Mara said, "we reach the glacier. Then the moraine. Then the river country, if the rivers haven't gone ahead of the monsoon." She looked at Devan across the flame. "Your lower route. How much does it actually save us?"

"A day. Maybe more if the shelf holds."

"And after the shelf?"

"After the shelf," Devan said, "we're on ground my grandfather drew from memory, twenty years after he walked it. So I'd say: after the shelf, we find out how good his memory was." He paused, and something moved behind his face that might have been fear and might have been hope wearing fear's coat. "There's one more annotation. I didn't mention it before."

Mara's head came up. "Devan."

"It's a single line, at the bottom of the descent map. In the margin. I read it the night before we met you in Leh, and I've been turning it over ever since, and I think—" He stopped. Started again. "I think it's why he kept the maps. Why he never threw them out, though he burned everything else."

"Read it," Mara said.

Devan looked into the fire, and the fire looked back, and outside the storm gathered itself for the night's work.

"'The mountain keeps what you give it,'" he quoted. "'Give it carefully.'"

Below us, far below, the rivers were already rising.
