## 25. The Descent Begins

The storm lifted the way a liar lifts his eyes — just enough to make you believe him.

Tsering called it at four in the morning, standing at the crevice mouth with the wind gauge cupped in his bare hand like something he was testing for poison. "Two hours," he said. "Maybe three. Then it closes again."

I'd already done the arithmetic by then, done it three times because I didn't like the answer and kept hoping the numbers would get embarrassed and change. Dorje's arm had gone from angry to wrong overnight — the skin above the splint hot and shiny, the fever climbing through him in waves that made him talk to people who weren't there. His mother, mostly. Sometimes a man named Pemba who I gathered owed him money. The infection didn't care about our window. It cared about warmth and time, and we were short on both.

"Two hours down the couloir puts us at the moraine shelf by first light," I said. "After that it's fixed ropes to the glacier."

"And if the storm comes back before we're off the ice?" Devan asked. He was coiling rope with the quick, economical hands of a man who'd spent his whole life preparing for arguments he intended to win. Three days pinned in a stone room together will do strange things to two people who came up the mountain hating each other. We hadn't stopped hating each other exactly. We'd just run out of the energy required to perform it.

"Then we're on the ice in a storm," I said. "Which is worse than being here, but not worse than Dorje dying here."

Nobody argued. That was the thing about altitude and fear — they simplify a group faster than any speech could. Tsering woke Dorje gently, which took skill; Dorje woke up cursing, which took none.

"The arm stays in the sling," Tsering told him. "You do nothing. You are luggage."

"I am not luggage," Dorje said, with the first real strength I'd heard in his voice in a day.

"You are precious luggage," Tsering amended, and Dorje laughed, and the laugh cost him something, and I turned away to pack so he wouldn't see what it cost me to hear it.

* * *

I packed the journals last.

I'd sewn them into a wrap of tent fabric the night before, working by headlamp while Devan slept and Dorje muttered, because I wanted no loose corners, nothing to snag, nothing to shift. Twenty pounds of sealed tins and oilcloth and my father's handwriting, pressed flat against the frame of the pack. When I shrugged into the harness I settled the load low, against my spine, between shoulder blades — the place where a pack talks to you all day, where you feel every gram as opinion.

Twenty years I'd carried my father one way or another. This was the first time the weight was honest.

"You're smiling," Tsering said behind me. "That's suspicious."

"It's the pack. It sits right."

"Mm." He looked at me the way he looks at weather coming over a ridge — politely, thoroughly, without illusion. "The tin box is heavier than twenty pounds."

"I know."

He nodded once and turned to wake the ropes, and that was the whole conversation, because Tsering had made his terms back at base camp and had kept them better than I had: no more secrets carried high. What remained between us now wasn't secret. It was just mine.

Devan checked my harness himself. He did it without asking, the way you check a stranger's knots when your life is about to hang from their judgment, and I let him, and found his hands were careful.

"Lowering pitch first," he said. "Sixty meters. Then traverse left across the apron, then the long scree. If the light holds."

"If the light holds," I agreed.

Above us the couloir went up into cloud like a throat. Below us it went down into worse cloud, and somewhere under that was the glacier, and somewhere past that was everything I'd spent a month telling myself I wasn't really doing this for. I touched the pack strap once, the way you touch a rail in an unfamiliar house, and stepped to the edge.

* * *

We lowered Dorje first.

There was no argument about this either, though Dorje supplied one anyway, from inside the fever, insisting he could walk, insisting he'd carried loads bigger than himself across passes with names too long for maps. We let him say it. Then we put him in the litter — a rig of pack frames and rope that Tsering built with the patience of a man braiding an argument he'd already won — and we lowered him over the lip into the grey.

The couloir was forty degrees of rock and old snow, running with meltwater in the warm seams, and the light came down it in grudging installments. First grey, then a bruised pearl, then nothing much. I went second, on the same rope as the litter, feeding it through the brake while Tsering anchored above and called the rhythm. Devan took the third position, free-climbing the margin, reading the rock ahead of us and calling holds.

"Loose block left. Don't touch it."
"Water ice under the snow bridge — Mara, runner on the flake, two meters right."
"Litter swings wide on the next drop. Let it. There's a slab below."

His voice got familiar fast. That's the strange mercy of rope work: it makes intimacy out of necessity, and by the fourth call I was answering him in half-sentences, and by the sixth we sounded like a team that had been arguing for years instead of days.

Dorje sang.

It started somewhere around the middle of the pitch — a porter's trail song, low and cracked, the kind they sing on long carries to make the miles lie about their length. Feverish, off-key, missing every third line. Tsering joined in on the refrain without breaking rhythm, and I felt the song come up the rope into my hands like a pulse.

"He does this when he's scared," Tsering said.

"I know," I said. "So do I."

"We know," Devan said, and for once nobody laughed, because it was true, and true things on a mountain get to stand there unlaughed-at.

* * *

The slip happened at the traverse.

The apron below the couloir was a fan of rotten rock and hard old snow, and we crossed it pitched, one at a time, while the light went from bad to theoretical. I crossed first and set a stance on a horn of granite that felt, in the way things feel at five thousand meters, absolutely permanent. Tsering brought Dorje across. Then Devan came, moving fast because he was good and knew it, cutting the corner where the snow looked solid —

— and the snow wasn't.

I heard it before I saw it: a sound like a door opening in the mountain, a soft wet crack, and then Devan was going, backwards, arms wheeling, the broken slab riding down with him toward the rocks at the bottom of the apron, and the rope between us snapped tight and burned through my brake hand and tore the stance and for one full second I understood, with total clarity, that I was going to watch a man die and be able to describe it afterward in perfect detail.

I didn't let go.

I don't mean I decided not to let go. Deciding happens in the part of you that narrates, and there was no narration left. My body did what twenty years of rope had taught it to do while the rest of me stood aside and watched: boot edges in, hip into the rock, brake wrapped twice more, weight committed backward into the fall line. The rope came taut and held, and Devan swung on it like a bell struck once, and hit the slope, and stopped.

The silence after was enormous.

"Mara." His voice came up thin from six meters below. "Mara, I'm — I'm here. I'm caught."

"I have you," I said.

My hands said it better. They wouldn't open. I tried to give him slack and they gave him centimeters, metered out like medicine, and Tsering came down the rope to me and put his own hands over mine and said, quietly, "He's on the ledge. Anchor's good. You can breathe now," and only then did my fingers remember they belonged to a person.

When we got Devan onto the horn beside me he was shaking — not from cold, or not only from cold. He looked at the broken slab below, at the rocks waiting at the bottom of it, and then at me, and whatever he was going to say, he swallowed it and said instead:

"You didn't let go."

"No."

"My grandfather—" He stopped. Started again. "People let go. On mountains. It's the first thing they tell you. You cut the rope."

"I know what they tell you," I said.

And he looked at me then with the whole inheritance sitting between us — his grandfather's rope-cut and my father's frozen shelter, eleven names and two surnames, all of it packed into six meters of nylon — and he nodded slowly, like a man signing something.

"Thank you," he said.

"Don't thank me yet," I said. "We're not down."

* * *

The rest of the descent I'll tell plainly, because some hours don't survive ornament.

We went down the long scree in falling light, lowering Dorje in relays, everyone roped, no one talking except in calls. The storm didn't come back when Tsering said it would. It waited, the way storms do, until we were committed on the most exposed ground, and then it arrived all at once — not snow first but wind, a flat shove from the west that leaned on us like a bored giant, and then spindrift streaming off the ridgelines in banners, and then the world contracting to the length of a rope.

I led. Not because I'm the best — Devan might argue, and would be partly right — but because the journals were on my back and I had stopped, somewhere in the last three days, pretending there was any version of me that could set them down and walk away lighter. Elias Okafor had stayed above the icefall with eleven people rather than descend alone. The least his daughter could do was carry his testimony down a mountain in bad weather without complaint.

Though I complained. Silently, continuously, with great fluency. Complaint is a load-bearing wall in my character.

By full dark we were on the glacier, on the fixed lines we'd placed coming up, and the storm had thickened from weather into presence. We moved as a single animal — Tsering on point probing with his axe, Devan mid-rope with Dorje's litter, me at the rear counting bodies by headlamp flashes: one, two, three, four. Count them again. One, two, three, four.

At the second anchor Devan's lamp found mine and held it.

"How far to the moraine camp?"

"Three hours. Four in this."

"Dorje won't last four."

"He'll last three," Tsering said, from the front, without turning around. "Because I say he will, and he's afraid of me."

Dorje, from inside the litter, made a sound that was either laughter or delirium, and possibly both, and we moved.

* * *

We reached the moraine shelf at what my watch claimed was nine o'clock and my body insisted was some other century. The old camp was still there — our tents half-buried, the cook tent flattened, everything wearing a foot of new snow like furniture in an abandoned house. We dug out the big tent, got Dorje inside, got antibiotics into him and sweet tea and the stove roaring, and then stood in the entrance breathing steam and looking at each other across the yellow light like survivors of something, which is what we were, though of what exactly I couldn't have said yet.

Tsering examined Dorje's arm by lamplight, unwrapped the splint, and was quiet for slightly too long.

"Well?" Dorje demanded.

"It needs a hospital," Tsering said. "Real one. Tomorrow, if the river lets us cross. Day after, if it doesn't."

"The river," I said.

Everyone looked at me.

"The crossing on the way in," I said. "Below the village. It was high then. That was a week ago, before any of this rain." I thought of the radio report from two nights ago, the monsoon's northern surge, the wall of water sitting over the foothills like a debt collector. "If the storm's been feeding it for three days—"

"Then the crossing we used," Devan said slowly, "may not exist anymore."

Tsering began rewrapping the splint, methodical, unhurried, which is how I knew he was worried; hurry is for small fears. "Then tomorrow," he said, "we find out what the mountain has decided about us."

Outside, the wind worked at the tents with patient fingers. Inside, Dorje slid at last into real sleep, the fever broken or merely resting, and Devan sat sharpening his knife by lamplight for no reason a knife needed, and I lay back against the pack with the journals pressing their honest weight into my spine and thought about promises.

Reyes had made me swear to deliver what was in the cache to whoever deserved it. I'd thought, in the hospice in Leh, that deserving was a matter of blood and grief — that I would carry the truth home like a trophy and decide alone whose hands it belonged in. But promises change shape at altitude. This one had become simpler and harder: keep four people alive down a mountain in the monsoon, and deliver the truth to the families of eleven dead men and one woman whose name I was still learning how to say.

My mother. On the mountain. All along, on the mountain.

I closed my eyes and felt her in the margins of my father's pages, annotating, correcting, joking — alive in ink if nowhere else — and I made her a promise too, silently, in the dark, the kind you keep in muscle rather than words:

Tomorrow we cross the river. Whatever it's become.

Through the tent wall, far below us in the black, I heard it — faint, continuous, rising — the sound of water where no water should be, and I understood that the mountain had already decided, and was on its way up to tell us.
