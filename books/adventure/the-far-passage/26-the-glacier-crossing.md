## 26. The Glacier Crossing

The glacier announced itself the way liars do — smoothly.

From the moraine it looked like a highway: broad, white, gently graded, running down from the icefall like something the mountain had built on purpose for people in a hurry. I'd been guiding long enough to know that glaciers never build anything on purpose except traps. A glacier is just a river that has learned patience, and this one had been waiting decades to swallow someone with an itinerary.

"Tsering," I said. "Your opinion."

He stood at the edge of the ice with his boots planted and his chin tucked, doing the thing he did where he looked at terrain as if negotiating with it. The melt had been working all week — afternoons warm enough to make water talk under everything — and what we were looking at was not one surface but two: the white skin of winter snow, and beneath it, hollowed out, the blue architecture of nothing.

"Snow bridges," he said. "Soft ones. We go early, while they're still frozen. We rope tight. And we probe."

"Probe with what?"

He held up his bamboo pole without ceremony, the way a man shows you a key he's carried for thirty years because he knew a door like this would come eventually.

* * *

We crossed at first light, when the cold still owned the glacier and the bridges were honest, or at least less dishonest. Dorje went behind me with his splinted arm strapped across his chest like a broken wing; Pemba and Norbu flanked him; Tsering took point, and Devan — who had stopped being a rival somewhere around the moment he offered us his rope and started being a problem of a different category entirely — walked anchor, watching our backs with the fixed attention of a man who had decided that if anyone was going to die on this ice, it would be from bad luck, not bad leadership.

Roped at eight meters. That's close enough to fall together, which sounds grim until you understand the alternative, which is falling alone.

The first hour was almost boring, and I blessed every minute of it. Boring is a gift above five thousand meters. Then Tsering's pole went through.

No drama. No crack of thunder. Just a sound like a spoon pushed through custard, and the pole sinking to its midpoint, and Tsering stepping back with the unhurried precision of a man declining a handshake.

"Here," he said, and drew a line in the snow with his boot heel. "We go left."

We went left. Twenty minutes later the pole went through again, and we went right. By mid-morning we had crossed perhaps four hundred meters of ground and negotiated with the glacier eleven times, and I had begun to understand that this wasn't travel at all. It was translation. The glacier spoke in holes, and Tsering was our interpreter, and the rest of us were trying to learn the grammar fast enough not to say anything fatal.

Dorje, to his credit, had found a rhythm for fear. He hummed. Low, tuneless, constant, a drone that I suspected was a prayer wearing a disguise, and nobody asked him to stop. When you're roped to a man with a fevered arm crossing rotten ice, you let him pray in whatever dialect he likes.

It was somewhere around the ninth bridge — a stretch where the snow lay thin as paper over a gap wide enough to park a truck — that I made the decision about the journal.

I'd been carrying it against my spine since the couloir, wrapped in oilcloth, and I could feel it there the way you feel a letter you haven't opened. All morning the team had been quiet in the wrong way. Not calm-quiet. White-knuckle quiet. Fear that isn't spoken doesn't disappear; it ferments. And I remembered something my father had written in the first pages, before the entries darkened — something about how a rope team is mostly a conversation, and when the conversation stops, the mountain starts talking instead.

So at the rest halt, on a shelf of old ice solid enough to trust, I unwrapped the journal and sat down where everyone could see me.

"What are you doing?" Pemba asked.

"Reading," I said. "Out loud. If we're going to cross this thing, we're going to do it in company."

Tsering raised one eyebrow, which from Tsering was a full paragraph of commentary. But he didn't object, and Dorje's humming faltered into something almost curious, and Devan settled onto his heels across from me with his hands hanging between his knees, and I opened the book to where I'd left off — the third week of the expedition, my father's handwriting marching across the page in those neat, patient lines, a man describing a route he believed he'd have years to describe again.

I read.

* * *

*"June 9th. The icefall is worse than Reyes allows. He wants to push through tomorrow with half the loads; I've told him the seracs need another night of cold. He called me cautious in front of the sahibs, which stung more than it should have. Note to self: being right is no comfort if you have to watch someone else be wrong about it."*

I paused there. There was something else on the page — smaller marks, crowded into the margin, written at an angle, in ink that had gone brown. I'd noticed the marginalia before, here and there, and assumed it was Reyes's hand, some argumentative rebuttal scrawled by the partner who survived.

It wasn't Reyes's hand.

I knew that slantwise, hurried script. I had seen it on grocery lists, on birthday cards, on the flyleaf of every book in the house I grew up in. My mother's handwriting. Anu Okafor, née something she never told me because she thought surnames were a colonial habit, writing in the margins of her husband's field journal — which meant she had read these pages, which meant—

Which meant she had been holding this book.

*"Being right is no comfort,"* the margin said, in her voice. *"Elias, you were right about the serac and you let him make you feel small for it. Don't. The mountain doesn't care who is right. It only cares who is paying attention. Keep paying attention. — A."*

Nobody spoke. The glacier ticked and groaned underneath us, settling its ancient weight, and I sat with my thumb on my mother's handwriting and felt the ground rewrite itself again, the way it had on the slope with the flask, the way it kept doing, patiently, like water working at stone.

"She was here," I said. Stupidly, out loud, stating the obvious, because the obvious needed saying or I was going to lose the thread of the day. "She read this. She was on the expedition when he wrote it."

"You didn't know?" Devan asked quietly.

"I thought she died in Delhi. Years later. That's the story I was given." I looked at the margin again. *"Keep paying attention."* "Apparently the story I was given had a lot of edits."

Tsering said nothing, but he touched the edge of the journal with two fingers, briefly, the way you touch a shrine wall, and then stood and picked up his pole. "Good," he said. "Now her family walks with us too. More eyes on the ice."

That was Tsering's whole theology, I think. More eyes. Everything else was commentary.

* * *

We moved again, and I kept reading as we went — not continuously, because probing rotten bridges demands attention, but in pieces, at each halt, passing the pages around the rope like bread. It changed the crossing. I don't know how else to say it. The same ice, the same holes opening under Tsering's pole, the same slow arithmetic of steps — but now there was a voice moving along the rope with us, dry and precise and alive, complaining about the tea, admiring the light on Nanda-devi-like peaks to the west, worrying about his wife's knees on the descent.

Because she was there. Once I saw it, I couldn't unsee it. She was everywhere in the margins.

*"June 11th. Anu insists on carrying her own load to the cache site. Reyes says the porters will think it strange, a woman under a full pack. I told him the porters will think it strange that a grown man needs to explain a woman to herself."*

Margin: *"He didn't say it that politely. I'm tidying him up for posterity. Also my knees are fine. — A."*

Dorje laughed — actually laughed, a cracked, surprised sound — and then winced and then laughed again anyway, and Norbu grinned, and even Devan's mouth moved at the corner. Somewhere above the icefall, twenty years ago, a woman had been annotating her husband's pomposity in real time, and today her voice came off a glacier and made a man with a splinted arm forget his arm for ten seconds.

*"June 13th. Cache site chosen. We'll seal the tins against the return party. Eleven names on the ration ledger. I keep writing them and checking them, like a rosary. Eleven. Everyone comes home. That is the entire plan; everything else is detail."*

I stopped reading that one out loud. Some sentences you carry silently, the way you carry the journals themselves — against the spine, where the load is honest.

But the margins kept going, and I kept sharing them, because the margins were lighter. That was her gift, apparently. His pages carried the weight; hers carried the joke that made the weight bearable. Reading them both together, I understood for the first time that I had spent my life inheriting only half of each of my parents — his silence, her absence — when the actual pair of them, on paper, in ink, was this: a careful man and a funny woman, correcting each other's drafts, twenty years dead and still keeping a rope team company on bad ice.

* * *

Early afternoon, the glacier tried to take Devan.

It happened the way these things happen, which is to say faster than language. Tsering had probed a bridge twice and pronounced it good; Devan stepped out onto it, and the bridge disagreed. The snow didn't crack so much as sigh, a soft exhalation, and then his leg was through to the hip and the whole panel was tilting, and he did exactly the right thing and exactly the wrong thing simultaneously — threw himself flat, which spread his weight, which saved him, but flat on a collapsing bridge means sliding, and he slid toward the hole with the smooth terrible ease of ice doing what ice does.

The rope came tight on my harness and I was already down, already braced, boot edges bitten into the slope behind me, and the load hit my hips like a car accident. For one second the whole system hung on the friction of my boots and the question of whether the snow I was lying on was any better than the snow he was falling through.

It was. Barely. The kind of barely you don't examine until later.

"Hold!" Tsering barked, though we were all holding, holding was the only verb left in the world. He came down the rope hand over hand, planted his pole, and got an axe into solid ice beside Devan's shoulder. "Devan. Elbows. Small movements. Push up on the axe."

Devan pushed. The bridge crumbled another inch. He swore in three languages, one of which I suspect he invented on the spot, and pushed again, and Tsering hauled his collar, and between the axe and the rope and whatever stubborn animal lives in a salvage man's shoulders, he came up out of the hole like a diver surfacing, gasping, snow packed down his back, one boot missing entirely.

The boot stayed in the crevasse. We listened to it knock its way down into the blue dark for a long time, longer than seemed possible, a small hollow drumming fading into the interior of the mountain.

"My grandfather's boots," Devan said finally, staring at his sock foot. "Those were — I bought those in Leh. They were supposed to be lucky."

"They were," said Tsering, already re-dressing the bridge edge with blocks cut from safer snow. "You are not in it."

We got him into spare footwear — Dorje's second pair, two sizes wrong, laced over two pairs of socks — and we stood in a knot on the solid shelf and shook, all of us, the delayed shaking that the body does when it has been informed of something the mind hasn't finished processing.

And then, because the shaking wanted somewhere to go, I picked up the journal.

"Where was I," I said. My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "June thirteenth. *'Eleven. Everyone comes home. That is the entire plan.'*" I turned the page. Found a margin. Read it.

*"He wrote 'everyone comes home' and then underlined it twice, which with Elias is practically shouting. So: everyone comes home. Including me. Including you, whoever finds this someday. Come home. — A."*

Silence on the ice. The glacier ticked.

"Well," said Norbu, clearing his throat. "She heard us."

"She heard him," said Tsering. "Same thing."

We clipped back in and crossed the next six bridges without losing so much as a mitten.

* * *

By late afternoon we were through the worst of it — the crevasse field thinning, the ice rising into cleaner, older blue, the kind of glacier you can almost trust because it stopped pretending to be snow decades ago. We made camp on a shelf below the final rise to the pass, and the light went long and copper, and for an hour the mountains did their evening trick of looking like places you'd want to live.

Dorje's arm was worse. I checked it at dinner and didn't like what I found — the redness had climbed past the splint line, and the skin was hot, and when I asked him about it he smiled the smile of a man spending energy he doesn't have. Fever. Infection, probably, seeded somewhere in the rockfall bruising, and now blooming in the warmth of his blood at altitude.

"How bad?" Tsering asked me privately, in the low voice he used for questions that weren't really questions.

"Bad enough that we can't sit here admiring the view."

"The storm window—"

"I know the storm window." I looked north, where the sky had that bruised, pressed-down quality that the monsoon wears like a uniform. "We move at first light. Fast. Down the far side before the weather closes."

"And if the weather closes first?"

I didn't answer, which was an answer, and Tsering nodded slowly and went to check the anchors, because that was his way of praying.

Before I turned in, I sat with the journal one last time by headlamp, wrapped in everything I owned, and read the entry for June fourteenth — the last ordinary day, the last page before the entries turned dark. My father described the cache seal, the tins, the ledger. He described the weather, which was perfect. He described his plans, which were modest and enormous at once: descend, publish, dig again next season, bring Anu somewhere with hot springs.

And in the margin, in that slantwise brown ink, one last note before the notes stopped:

*"Hot springs. Now he's romantic. Say yes, Anu. Always say yes to the man who counts eleven names twice. — A."*

I closed the book and held it against my chest and let myself, for exactly one minute, be a daughter instead of a guide.

Then I heard it — a sound from the north, faint under the wind, so low I might have invented it. Rain. Not on us, not yet. Down in the foothills, miles below, the monsoon arriving at the walls of the world like a debt collector who knows exactly where you live.

Tsering heard it too. He looked up from the anchors, and across the fire Devan lifted his head, and none of us said anything, because there was nothing to say that the sky hadn't already said.

First light. We'd get one first light. After that, the mountain would stop taking appointments.
