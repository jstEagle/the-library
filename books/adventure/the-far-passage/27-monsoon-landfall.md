## 27. Monsoon Landfall

The rain announced itself the way bad news always does in the mountains: not all at once, but as a rumor that keeps getting louder until it's the only thing anyone is saying.

We heard it before we saw it. A sound below us, down in the foothills — a vast, soft roaring, like the earth had turned over in its sleep and couldn't get comfortable again. Tsering stopped on the trail and tilted his head, listening the way a doctor listens to a chest.

"That's not wind," he said.

No, I thought. Wind moves through things. This was moving over them, flattening them, a wall of water walking up the valleys toward us with the unhurried confidence of something that has never once been late.

I'd grown up on my grandfather's stories of the Serrano monsoon, and like everything else he told me, they were half adventure, half apology. "The sky closes," he used to say, hands cupped around his tea as if to keep some small warmth from escaping along with everything else he'd lost. "And when it opens again, you are either below it or you are part of it." He said this looking at me in a way that suggested I was expected to be neither, and that his own life was a cautionary tale on both counts.

He was right about the sky. By mid-morning the clouds had come up the valley like floodwater filling a basement — no drama, just level after level rising past us until we were climbing inside the cloud instead of beneath it. The light went the color of old pewter. The temperature dropped ten degrees in an hour, and then the first drops came, fat and cold and spaced out like scouts reporting back: yes, there's more, so much more.

Mara stood at the head of the column and did her arithmetic out loud, because that was how she prayed.

"Six hours to the moraine camps," she said. "The glacier fords will be rising by tonight. After that—" She didn't finish. She didn't need to. We all carried our own version of *after that*.

Dorje walked with his splinted arm cradled against his chest like a thing he was smuggling, and every few hundred meters he muttered what I had learned to recognize as a complete inventory of his grievances against me specifically, though I was fairly sure at least half of them were aimed at Mara and simply routed through the nearest available target. I'd been the nearest available target for most of my adult life. It's a role I fill well. My grandfather would have been proud, or furious, or both; with him those were usually the same expression.

"Move," Mara said, not unkindly, and we moved.

* * *

You want to know what I argued about, in those last hours before the argument became impossible? The journals. Of course the journals. I had spent eleven days — no. Let me be honest, since honesty was apparently the theme of this expedition whether I liked it or not. I had spent my whole life arguing about those journals, or rather about the man who wrote them, without ever once having read a word he wrote. That is the particular lunacy of inheritance: you defend positions you've never examined, held in trust by people too dead to be questioned.

Three days pinned in that cache shelter while the storm screamed itself hoarse outside, I had read over Mara's shoulder. I told myself I was fact-checking. I read my grandfather's partner describing, in careful, loving, unbearable detail, how Tomas Reyes — my hero, my north star, the reason I owned three sets of ice tools and one set of emotional problems — had cut the rope line in a whiteout and left eleven people above the icefall. Including the guide's wife. Including the guide himself, who stayed.

I wanted it to be forged. I wanted the handwriting to wobble where a forger's hand would wobble. It didn't. Elias Okafor wrote the way surveyors draw: lines so straight they made your eyes ache, and dates in margins, cross-referenced, corroborated by supply weights in a ledger kept with the grim precision of a man who knew exactly what eleven people needed per day to stay alive and exactly how many days they hadn't gotten.

So I argued anyway. Because the alternative to arguing was feeling, and feeling, at five thousand meters with the monsoon coming, was a luxury item.

"You could have shown me years ago," I said to Mara somewhere on the descent, water already sheeting off my hood. "If this existed. If you'd known."

"I found out nine days ago same as you."

"You could have—"

"What?" She turned around in the rain. Water poured off the brim of her hood in a continuous curtain, and behind it her face was doing something I recognized from photographs of myself: the attempt to remain composed while something enormous rearranges itself underneath. "What exactly do you think I should have done, Devan? Walked into Leh, found you — a stranger I'd met once across an outfitter's counter — and said, hello, your grandfather murdered my father, here's the paperwork?"

"He didn't murder—"

"No. He didn't. That's the whole point. He left them. There's a difference, and the journals spend forty pages explaining the difference, and none of it matters because either way eleven people died and one man came down and told the world a story that kept him warm for thirty years." She wiped her face with the back of her glove, which was pointless, which she did anyway. "I'm not asking you to forgive him. I'm not asking you to believe me. I'm asking you to walk faster, because in six hours that river is going to be a freight train, and I would very much like for all five of us to be on the correct side of it when it arrives."

It was a good speech. It deserved a better audience than a man hypothermic enough to be sentimental. I opened my mouth to continue the dispute on principle—

And the mountain answered for me.

The sound came from above and to the left, a deep percussive *whump* that I felt in my molars before I heard it properly. Rockfall. Somewhere above us in the murk, several tons of the Serrano Range decided it had been standing long enough. Dorje hit the ground with the efficiency of a man who had learned this lesson recently and expensively. Tsering pulled us both behind a boulder the size of a bus, and we crouched there while the echo rolled away down-valley, chased by the rain.

"Save it," Tsering said mildly. He wasn't even looking at us. He was watching the slope. "Argue in Leh. Argue in court, if it comes to that. Here, the only judge is weather, and weather has already ruled."

* * *

Here is what I will say about nature, now that nature has had its turn with me: it does not care whose grandfather did what. This is its single greatest virtue.

By afternoon the rain had stopped being weather and become a condition, an atmosphere, a country we were traveling through. It fell with the flat democratic persistence of something that intends to go on forever and has the resources to do it. Our waterproofs gave up their arguments around two o'clock. Not catastrophically — nothing dramatic, just the slow capitulation of seams, the creeping chill along the shoulders, the discovery that your socks have opinions about moisture. The cold got inside the conversation. Nobody talked anymore except in the short imperative grammar of survival: *rope*, *rock*, *careful*, *here*.

Mara led. Of course Mara led. I watched her work and understood, finally, why my grandfather had trusted this family with his map and his guilt and his last chance at a kind of redemption. She moved through whiteout and water like someone reading a book she'd written herself. Every decision arrived pre-made, three steps ahead of need. Where I saw gray chaos, she saw terrain she had apparently been memorizing since childhood — the escape hatch from a house she'd fled twenty years ago and never stopped knowing the layout of.

At the top of the moraine she stopped and looked back up-valley, once, briefly. I followed her gaze. There was nothing to see — cloud, rain, the suggestion of slopes — but I knew what she was looking at. A crevice sealed under a cairn. Two names chiseled in stone beside it, and now, in my head, permanently associated: Okafor and Reyes, side by side on the rock like a bad joke with a forty-year setup.

"We're leaving them," I said. It wasn't a question. It also wasn't quite an accusation, though it wore accusation's coat.

"For now," she said. "Monks come up after the passes open. Proper rites. Tsering's arranging it." She glanced at me, and for a second the guide fell away and there was just a tired woman with rain in her eyelashes. "You could come. If you wanted."

I said nothing, which in my personal language means *yes, but I'll die before saying it aloud*, and I think she understood, because she almost smiled, and then turned and led us down.

Getting down became the whole world. That's the truth of it, and I've tried since to find a more literary way to say it and failed, because there isn't one. All the stakes I'd carried up the mountain — vindication, restitution, the rehabilitation of a dead man, the prosecution of another — the rain took them all. It didn't argue. It simply dissolved them, the way it dissolves footprints, until the only question remaining in the entire universe was *where do I put my next foot*, repeated eleven thousand times, with variations.

Dorje slipped twice on the wet moraine and swore magnificently both times, inventing curses I intend to steal. Tsering moved through the murk like it was a mild inconvenience personally arranged for his amusement. And Mara counted us. That was the thing I kept noticing — every hour, sometimes more often, she'd stop, turn, and count: one, two, three, four, five. Not checking gear. Checking *us*. Counting heads the way her father must have counted the eleven, night after frozen night, until the arithmetic stopped working.

She thought I didn't see. I saw.

* * *

We camped at dusk in the lee of a lateral moraine, if you can call it camping — more a mutual agreement to stop moving before falling became involuntary. No stove would light in the wet. We ate cold food in a huddle under a tarp that had surrendered any pretense of being shelter and was now functioning as a shared delusion. The river below us spoke continuously, a low guttural monologue that had risen half a meter since morning and showed no interest in stopping.

Tsering went down to look at it and came back shaking his head, water streaming off him like he'd gone for a swim voluntarily.

"It is brown," he reported. "It was green this morning. Brown means glacier melt plus rain plus anger." He looked at Mara. "Tomorrow morning, early. Before it finishes being angry."

"First light," Mara agreed.

"And after the river?" Dorje asked, around a mouthful of cold noodles. His splinted arm lay across his knees, and he guarded it like a sleeping animal. "After the river, what?"

Nobody answered immediately. The rain filled the silence, patient, professional.

"After the river," Mara said finally, "we walk out. All of us. That's the whole plan. That's the only plan." She looked across the tarp at me, and in the gray failing light her face was unreadable, or maybe it was fully readable and I just didn't like the reading. "Everything else — the journals, the names, whatever we owe each other — that happens below the snowline, dry, fed, alive. I'm not deciding anything else on this mountain. Neither are you."

"I wasn't going to—"

"You were absolutely going to. You've been drafting arguments since the cache. I can hear you composing. You get this line—" she tapped her own forehead, "—right here, right before you speak. Your grandfather had it too, in the one photograph I've seen. Family trait."

For some reason that undid me a little. Not the insult — the familiarity of it. She had studied him. She hated him correctly, thoroughly, with footnotes, and she had still studied him, because that's what we do with the people who break our families: we learn them better than anyone alive, and then we carry the expertise around like shrapnel.

"Tsering," I said, because I couldn't say anything else yet, "your condition. No secrets above base camp. Does the deal hold if base camp is currently underwater?"

Tsering considered this with the gravity he brought to all questions, including, once, whether to eat the last chocolate bar (he had eaten it; the deliberation was ceremonial).

"The deal holds," he said. "But the definition of 'above' may require renegotiation."

* * *

I took second watch with Mara, because nobody asked me to and because sleep had become a rumor I didn't believe.

We sat under the tarp's failing edge and watched the dark valley breathe rain. Below us, the river rose. I could hear it gaining — a new note in its voice every hour, deeper, wider, more certain of itself. Behind us, up in the cloud, a mountain kept two men and eleven ghosts and a tin box full of the truth, and I found that I had stopped caring about the box, at least for tonight. Tomorrow I would care again. Grief is persistent; it keeps office hours. But tonight there was only the rain and the counting.

"One," Mara murmured, at some point in the black hours, and I realized she was at it again, counting us in her sleep-adjacent way, checking I was real.

"Two," I answered.

She laughed — quietly, surprised, a real laugh with rust on it. "Three," she said.

"Four. Five." I pulled my hood tighter. "All present. For whatever that's worth to a woman who measures life in miles."

Something shifted in her silence. When she spoke again her voice had that careful flatness people use when they're telling you something true.

"I don't measure in miles anymore," she said. "That ended somewhere above the icefall. I haven't figured out what the new unit is yet. Ask me in Leh."

Down in the valley, the river answered for both of us — a long, rising roar, the sound of tomorrow arriving early and demanding to be crossed.

Tsering sat up in the dark without being called. He listened for perhaps ten seconds, the way a doctor listens to a chest.

"That is not a river anymore," he said. "That is a schedule. We leave now."
