## 28. The River

I have crossed this river eleven times in my life, and I would like the record to show that it was never once my idea.

On the way in, three weeks ago now, it was a polite stream. It chattered over its stones like a tourist pleased with itself. We crossed it on stepping rocks, Dorje complaining about wet boots, Mara counting us across with her eyes the way she counts everything. I remember thinking: this is not a river. This is a rumor of a river. This is a river's business card.

The monsoon had read the same card and taken offense.

We stood on the bank in the afternoon light that wasn't light — the rain had come down the valley ahead of us like a landlord arriving early, and the sky was the color of wet slate, and the river below us had tripled its width and gone the brown of strong tea left too long. It carried whole trees. Not branches. Trees, with their roots waving like drowned men's hands, rolling over and over in the current, sometimes surfacing, sometimes not, which is the river's way of introducing itself.

"Well," said Devan, who had been with us four days and was still learning how to be useful instead of merely present. "That's changed."

"Everything changes," Mara said. "The skill is noticing before it changes you."

She was already walking the bank, upstream, then down, reading the water the way other people read letters from lawyers — with total attention and a certain cold expectation of bad news. I watched her do it and thought, not for the first time, that I had spent fifteen years following this woman into places no sensible person goes, and that I had never once seen her hesitate, and that I had only recently begun to wonder what it cost her to be like that. Hesitation, I have decided, is a luxury of people who have somewhere to return to. Mara had spent twenty years making sure she had nowhere. The mountain had been her address. Now the mountain was full of her dead, and she was carrying them on her spine in a waterproof bag, and she was reading a river that wanted to kill us all, and her hands were steady.

I found this admirable and also, in a way I could not have explained then, sad.

* * *

The plan, when she finished reading, was simple, which is how you know a plan is serious. Simple plans are for serious situations. Complicated plans are for people who have time to be clever.

"There." She pointed to where the river bent and split around a midstream boulder the size of a house, leaving a stretch — a short stretch, maybe fifteen meters — where the current came in two weaker channels instead of one strong one. "We anchor a line on both banks. Traverses, not a crossing. Nobody touches the water if the water can be avoided. Dorje first."

Dorje looked up from where he sat against his pack, his broken arm strapped across his chest, his face the color of old candle wax. The fever had broken two days ago in the cave, thanks to Devan's antibiotics and Dorje's own magnificent stubbornness, but the infection had taken its rent. He was weak in the way that frightened me more than the river did, because weakness is a thing that arrives quietly and then makes all your other decisions for you.

"Me first?" he said. "I am the weakest. Why does the weakest go first?"

"Because if the anchor fails," Mara said, "I want it to fail when we can still pull you back. Not when you're the last man on a frayed line."

There was a silence while everyone understood this. Dorje understood it last and loudest.

"So my life is a test of the rope."

"Your life is the one we can save," Mara said. "Yes. It's a test of the rope. I'm sorry."

Dorje muttered something in his village dialect that I did not translate for the others, because translation is a courtesy and he was not feeling courteous. But he began, slowly, working one-handed, to get out of his rain shell, and I went to help him, and he let me, which from Dorje was a kind of love letter.

We built the anchors the way you build anything that people will bet their lives on: twice, and then a third time because you are ashamed of the second. On the near bank, a granite horn that Mara tested with the pick of her axe until she was satisfied it was the mountain itself and not a decoration on it. On the far bank — that was the problem. The far bank was a wall of wet grass and loose stone, and the best anchor was a pine, leaning, its roots half undercut by the flood.

"It holds," Devan said, after he'd hung from it with his full weight and bounced.

"It holds today," I said.

"It holds today is the only tense we have," Devan said, and I disliked him slightly less than I had the day before, which from me is a promotion.

We rigged the line — our climbing rope doubled, tensioned with a prusik system, a second line as backup running parallel. Mara checked every knot. She checked my knots, which was an insult, and then she checked her own, which was honesty, and I forgave her.

The rain came down. The river roared. Somewhere above us, in clouds we could not see, the glacier-fed tributaries were still filling, and every hour we stood there the crossing got worse. Everyone knew this. Nobody said it. That is what a team is: a group of people agreeing not to say the true thing so that they can all keep doing the necessary thing.

"Who goes after Dorje?" Devan asked.

"The order is Dorje, then the packs on a haul line, then Devan, then me," Mara said.

"You last," I said. "You are the guide."

"I'm the strongest swimmer."

"You are the one who pulls everyone else out. You go last. This is not a vote."

She looked at me for a long moment, and I looked back, and for once — for the first time on this whole expedition, maybe the first time in fifteen years — she did not win the argument by being the one who cared less. She won it by being wrong. I saw her know it.

"Fine," she said. "Tsering last."

* * *

Dorje went out onto the line at four in the afternoon, in rain so hard it was less weather than opinion.

Here is what a rope traverse is, for those of you fortunate enough never to have done one: you clip into a horizontal line above a river that is trying very sincerely to kill you, and you pull yourself along it hand over hand, and the line sags, and the water rises toward your boots, and the spray hits you in the face with enough force to make breathing a technical skill. It is not dramatic. That is the worst thing about it. There is no moment of heroism, only the long stupid labor of moving your hands and not letting go, while the rain erases the far bank and the cold climbs up your arms like something patient.

Dorje was brave the way working men are brave, which is to say he did not call it anything at all. He went out over the water, one arm useless, hauling with the other, and the line sagged and his boots touched the white water once, twice, and the third time the river got a full grip on his legs and swung him downstream like a door in the wind.

The line held. His grip held. He swore — I could not hear the words over the river, but I knew their rhythm, having shared tents with the man for many years — and he hauled, meter by meter, and the river pulled, and it went on like that for what felt like a season and was probably four minutes.

He made the far bank. Devan and I hauled the slack and dragged him up the grass until he was lying on his back in the rain, laughing, actually laughing, that high cracked laugh of a man whose body has just informed him it is still alive.

"Tell my wife," he called across the water, "that I was graceful."

"Tell her yourself," Mara called back. "Packs next."

The packs went across on the haul line, one at a time, and then Devan went, and he crossed well — he was a strong man and a careful one, and I noticed that he checked his carabiner twice the way Mara checked hers, and I thought: there is the grandfather in him, or perhaps the grandmother, the good parts of that old expedition, the parts that were not lies.

Then it was my turn, and I went, and I will not describe it, because some things a man keeps.

I made the far bank. We were four on one side, and Mara stood alone on the other, coiling the backup line, and the river between us was doing its tree-carrying trick again, and I felt the particular cold in the stomach that comes from watching the person you trust most stand on the wrong side of moving water.

She clipped in. She stepped off the bank.

* * *

The anchor on our side — the pine, Devan's pine, the one that held today — moved at the moment Mara was at the middle of the span.

I did not see it go. I heard it. A sound like a knuckle cracking, if the knuckle belonged to a god, and then the whole line dropped a half meter with a jerk that threw Devan off his feet and sent a shock up the rope into my hands, and the far anchor — the granite horn, the mountain itself, that one was fine — the far anchor was fine, but the near anchor was leaning, and the line was sagging, and Mara was in the middle of the river with the water at her thighs now, and rising.

She did not scream. I want that in the record too. She looked downstream at the water, and she looked at us, and she called one word: "Tension!"

Devan threw himself on the haul line. I threw myself on the main rope. We hauled, and she pulled, and it was almost enough and not quite, because every meter of slack we took up, the pine gave up another, its roots tearing out of the wet bank in slow motion, a tree committing suicide with dignity.

And Mara, in the middle of the flood, made the calculation that guides make and that I have spent my whole life watching her make and that I have never once seen her get wrong: she let go of the line.

Not all of it. She kept one hand on it, a brake, a tether. But she stopped fighting the sag and let the current take her legs, and she swung downstream on the rope like a pendulum, toward our bank, toward the eddy below the pine, and she shouted — I heard this one clearly, over everything, and I will hear it until I die —

"Tsering! The anchor!"

And I understood what she was saying, because there was only one thing to say. The pine was going. When it went, the line would be anchored on one side only, and she would be a fly on a thread over a flood, and the thread would be attached to nothing. Someone had to re-set the near anchor, and the near anchor had to be the line itself, carried across, and the water between the pine and the eddy where she would pendulum was the fast water, the deep water, the water that was carrying trees.

I took off my pack. I took off my jacket. I did not take off my boots, because a man without boots on wet stone is a man who has chosen a slower way to die, and I heard Devan shouting my name in a voice that had gone up an octave, and I heard Dorje, from the grass, shouting mine too, and I did not answer either of them, because answering takes breath and breath was going to be currency shortly.

The water took me at the thighs and then at the waist and then everywhere.

People ask mountaineers what the cold is like and the mountaineers lie, because the truth is that the cold is not the problem. The problem is that the river has opinions about where your body should be, and its opinions are better informed than yours. It put me down and rolled me and lifted me and put me down again, and the world was brown water and white noise and the taste of the whole mountain's winter arriving all at once in my mouth. Somewhere in it — I will be honest, I do not remember deciding this — I was swimming. Not toward the far bank. Along it, in the eddy line, angling for the pine, for the rope, for the work.

Because here is the thing I understood in the water, in the half-second intervals between breaths: this was not heroism. Heroism is a word that belongs to the people watching from the bank. What this was, was labor. It was the same as carrying a load up a moraine in the dark. It was the same as digging a tent platform at five thousand meters with frozen hands. There was a job, and the job was: get the rope to the tree, get the tree to hold, get Mara out of the water. The river was not a monster. The river was a workplace, temporarily mismanaged.

My hands found the pine's trunk. My other hand found the rope. I got a wrap of line around the trunk — one, two, three, the old fisherman's hitch my father taught me on a much smaller river, in another life, when the water was warm and the stakes were fish — and I jammed myself against the roots and shouted for tension, and Devan hauled, and the line came tight around the pine and the pine groaned and held.

Held today. Held this minute. Held, which is the only tense we had.

Mara came penduluming in on the tightened line, and the eddy took her the way the eddy had been asked to, and her boots found stones, and my hand found her collar, and I hauled her out of the water onto the bank the way you haul a sack of barley, without ceremony, because ceremony is for people who were not just in the river.

She lay on the grass, coughing, and then she looked at me, dripping, shaking, still holding the rope, and she said:

"Your knots are ugly."

"My knots," I said, "are the reason you are not a tree downstream."

"They're both true," she said, and started laughing, and then I started laughing, and then Devan arrived at a run and fell on both of us, and Dorje stood at the edge of the grass with his one good arm raised in the air like a man signaling a very slow aircraft, and for one minute on the bank of a killing river in the rain, the five of us were the happiest people in the world.

Then Mara stopped laughing, sat up, and said: "Everyone across. Now. While the pine still agrees."

* * *

The rest of the crossing went the way crossings should go: quickly, and with everyone pretending the last hour had not happened. Devan ferried the remaining loads. Mara crossed on her own line, hand over hand, steady as a woman crossing a courtyard, and if her hands shook on the far bank, the rain was kind enough to hide it, and I was kind enough not to mention it, and that is also what a team is.

We climbed away from the river through wet forest until the sound of it faded to a rumor behind us, and at dusk we found an overhang — a shelf of rock above a dry streambed, deep enough for five bodies and a stove. Devan got a fire going with wood he had carried, dry, inside his jacket, for two days, against exactly this. I have decided to stop being surprised by him. It wastes energy.

Dorje slept at once, his splinted arm outside the blanket like a flag of a country with one province. Devan slept soon after. Mara sat at the mouth of the overhang, watching the rain come down the valley, and I sat beside her, because someone had to, and because I had earned the seat.

"You went into that water," she said, after a while, "without being asked."

"You were going to ask me."

"I was not going to ask you. I was going to go myself."

"I know. That is why I did not wait to be asked."

She was quiet. The rain filled the quiet, the way it does, generously, so that silence between two people never has to be empty.

"Tsering. If that anchor had gone four seconds earlier—"

"It did not."

"But if—"

"Tsering," I said, "is tired. Tsering has carried your secrets, your map, your dead, and now Tsering has carried your rope. Tomorrow Tsering would like to carry only the load, which is the lightest of these."

She almost smiled. "And what does Tsering want in return?"

"Nothing tonight." I looked at her, this woman I had followed for fifteen years, this woman who had spent a month learning that her father had not abandoned eleven men but had died with them, and that her mother had died with him, and that the whole world's memory was wrong and she was the one carrying the correction down the mountain in a bag against her spine. "Tomorrow, maybe, you tell me what we do when we reach the bottom. Because the river is not the last water, Mara. There are eleven families down there in the valleys, and every one of them is a crossing too, and I have watched you read rivers all my life. But I have never yet watched you read people. And the rain is going to put us in front of them very soon."

She looked at me for a long time. Down the valley, through the rain, the lights of the first village were coming on — small, yellow, impossibly ordinary, each one a family that had been waiting twenty years for a truth we were carrying wet and cold against our backs.

"One crossing at a time," Mara said finally.

"Yes," I said. "But you will have to let me tie the knots."

Outside, the river went on talking to itself in the dark, carrying its trees, keeping its own counsel about everything it had seen — and ahead of us, down in the yellow lights, the first of the eleven families slept without knowing that by morning, everything they remembered was going to change.
