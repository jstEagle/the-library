## 29. What Deserves Delivering

The cave was less a cave than the mountain's grudging concession — a hollow under an overhang of gneiss where the flood-swollen river's old course had gnawed at the cliff before deciding, some glacial epoch ago, to move its business elsewhere. It was dry in the way that things are dry when they've been wet for so long they've given up pretending. There were two feet of standing water at the mouth and a wind coming off the torrent that made even sitting still feel like effort.

We'd been in it for six hours. Dorje lay against the back wall with his infected arm cradled like something borrowed and precious, fever-flushed, muttering occasionally to a deity I hoped was taking notes. Tsering had rigged a tarp over the entrance that did nothing against the sound of the river but at least kept the rain from finding us directly. Devan sat near me, close enough to share the stove's heat, far enough to maintain the fiction that we weren't allies.

The journals were against my spine, wrapped in my sleeping bag's waterproof liner, and I could feel them the way you feel a bruise — not constantly, but every time you shift wrong.

I'd spent the last hour doing arithmetic, because arithmetic is what I do instead of feeling, and there was a particular sum I couldn't make come out.

Reyes's words, in the hospice in Leh, his voice like paper being folded: *Deliver it to whoever deserves it.* At the time I'd understood it as a bequest. A dying man handing his secret to the daughter of the man he'd ruined — or thought he'd ruined — as if the map were a debt instrument and I the creditor. Deliver it to whoever deserves it. Me. Obviously me. The daughter, the guide, the one who'd spent twenty years measuring distances so she wouldn't have to measure anything else.

Except.

"Say it out loud," Devan said. He wasn't looking at me. He was looking at the rain, which came down past the tarp in gray ropes. "You've got the face you get when you're working something. My grandfather had the same face. It's very annoying in another person."

"You never met him when he was working something."

"I met him for nineteen years. He died when I was twenty." A pause. "He worked things right up until the pneumonia took him. Crosswords. Ledgers. Whether the nurse liked him." He turned his head. "What is it?"

I almost deflected. Deflection has been my native language longer than English or Nepali or the sign-language of rope teams. But we'd spent three days pinned in a cache shelter together reading his grandfather's crime and my father's testimony by headlamp light, and there are certain economies of honesty that get forced on you at altitude, the way your body forces breathing on you whether you've agreed to it or not.

"The promise," I said. "*Whoever deserves it.* I've been carrying that phrase like it pointed at me."

"And now?"

"Now I'm doing the math on who deserves it, and I keep getting an answer I don't like."

Tsering looked up from the stove. He didn't say anything. He'd stopped saying things an hour ago, in the manner of a man who has learned that Mara Okafor arrives at her conclusions faster if nobody stands in the doorway.

"Eleven families," I said. "Eleven. We know four of their names for certain — the ledger gives us all eleven, ration weights, kit lists, last entries in some cases. Grandfathers. One grandmother." My mother. I still couldn't say it without the sentence snagging on something. "And for twenty years, the only story anyone got was Reyes senior's — Elias Okafor cut and ran. Some of those families built their grief on top of that story. The village woman whose grandfather was one of the eleven — she spat at my name. Her family has spent three generations believing a man abandoned their blood on the ice."

"And the journals say otherwise," Devan said quietly.

"The journals say my father stayed. Dug them a shelter. Froze rather than leave them." I heard my own voice go flat, which is what it does when the alternative is cracking. "So here's the math. If I take these journals home and publish them under my name — my expedition, my discovery, my vindication — then the truth gets delivered, technically. But it gets delivered *to* me first, and through me second, and everyone else gets it thirdhand, packaged, with my name on the spine."

"That's how books work," Devan said. But he said it carefully, testing the floor.

"That's how *my* life works," I said. "That's the whole disease. I left my father's shadow by becoming the best at something nobody could tie to him. And now the thing I want most in the world — someone to look at Elias Okafor's record and see it clean — I can only get by putting my own name on it. Which means the correction becomes about me. Again. Forever. Another generation of Okafors standing between those families and their own dead."

The stove hissed. Outside, the river made its argument, which was louder than mine and better supported.

Devan was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, he'd taken off the salvage-crew polish, the polite-armed-negotiator voice he'd used at the outfitter's in Leh, and what was underneath was just tired.

"When I found out you had the map," he said, "I told myself a story. That my grandfather had sent it to you out of guilt, and that my job was to intercept it — protect his name, bring home whatever was worth bringing home, keep the family legend intact." He rubbed his face. "Then I read the journals. And the legend is that he panicked in a whiteout and cut eleven people loose, including your mother, and then walked down the mountain and lived forty years on top of it. That's the inheritance. I followed you up here to defend a lie."

"Yes."

"So don't lecture me about names on spines." He laughed, short and without much humor in it. "You think I haven't done the same math? I'm the grandson of the villain. Whatever happens with these journals, I'm in the story. The question is whether I'm in it as the man who tried to bury the proof or the man who helped carry it down. Either way it's *my* name next to yours, and neither of us chose that."

"No," I said. "We inherited it. Like altitude sickness."

He almost smiled. "You've been saving that one."

"Since the moraine field."

Dorje stirred and asked, in a voice sanded down by fever, whether anyone was going to eat the last of the tsampa or merely discuss it philosophically. Tsering fed him without comment, which in Tsering's vocabulary was a paragraph of affection.

But the question stayed with me after Dorje subsided, and I turned it over while the light failed and the rain kept up its work: *who deserves it.*

Not who wants it. Wanting was settled — I wanted vindication the way a bone wants setting, badly and constantly, and I'd admitted it to myself above the clouds with the compass in my hand. Not who earned it. Earning was a market word, and this wasn't a market. Deserve. What did the word actually weigh?

It weighed, I decided, the same as the ledger. Names, ration weights, last entries. Eleven people who had gone onto the mountain with plans — harvests, weddings, children, debts, ordinary futures — and had been erased twice. Once by the ice. Once by the story. The first erasure nobody could help. The second was still ongoing, still maintained, still doing damage in a high village where an old woman spat at a dead man's name because the alternative was grieving properly, and proper grief requires knowing what happened.

The truth belonged to them. Not because they'd suffered most — though God knew they had — but because it was *theirs*. My father's journals were testimony about their deaths. Testimony belongs to the bereaved. That's not sentiment; that's law older than any law. You don't get to own the account of how someone's grandmother died because your father wrote it down and your mother died in it too.

And Reyes — Tomas Reyes, the prospector, the survivor's son who'd carried the map fifty years and never opened the cache himself, or couldn't — what had he meant? I'd assumed he meant me. But he'd known the ledger existed. He'd known there were eleven families. Maybe a dying man's promise isn't a bequest at all. Maybe it's an instruction, and I'd read it selfishly because selfishness was the only lens I'd packed.

*Deliver it to whoever deserves it.*

Deliver. Not keep. Not publish-under-your-imprint. Deliver, like a courier, like a guide — carry the load across the range and hand it over at the destination. I'd been thinking of myself as the heir. I should have been thinking of myself as the porter.

There was a joke in there somewhere, about spending twenty years becoming the best guide nobody's heard of and finally getting hired for the one delivery that mattered, by a dead man, for no fee. I let myself have it, privately, because if I didn't laugh at something the cave was going to win.

* * *

Devan and I talked until the stove went cold, and then we talked some more, because neither of us slept well anymore and conversation was cheaper than fuel.

"We publish jointly," he said eventually. "Or rather — we don't publish at all. Not first. First we deliver."

"To the families."

"All eleven. Copies of everything. Journals, ledger, photographs. Originals to the archives if the families agree, but they see them before any scholar does, before any journalist, before either of our names goes anywhere near it." He counted on his fingers, an accountant's gesture that must have been bred into the Reyes line along with the capacity for catastrophic error. "The woman in the village. The man whose family thinks — whoever else the ledger turns up. We find them. We put the documents in their hands. Then, and only if they say yes, the record gets corrected publicly."

"They might say no."

"Some will. Some will want it buried — you don't get to choose how people receive their own dead." He said it steadily, and I understood he'd already grieved this part, somewhere in the cache shelter during the storm, alone with his hero's handwriting. "And some will want the world to know. We owe them both the option. That's what delivering means. It means the choice stops being ours."

"It stopped being ours the moment we read page forty," I said. "We just hadn't noticed yet."

"My grandfather would have hated this."

"Your grandfather," I said, "cut a rope line in a whiteout and then spent forty years making sure the dead couldn't contradict him. His opinion on document handling is noted and disregarded."

That landed harder than I'd intended. Devan flinched — a small thing, quickly mastered, but I saw it — and I remembered that grief doesn't care about justice, that you can know your grandfather was a coward and still miss him at Christmas. I'd had twenty years of practice missing a man the world called a fraud. He'd had none at all missing a man the world called a hero.

"I'm sorry," I said. "That was —"

"No. It was accurate." He stared into the dead stove. "Keep being accurate. Someone in this partnership has to be, and historically it hasn't been my family."

*Partnership.* He said it lightly, testing, the way you test a snow bridge before committing your weight. I let it stand. There would be time later to decide what the word meant — co-authors of a correction, maybe, or just two people who happened to be holding the same rope when the mountain tested it. For now it stood, and neither of us stepped on it hard.

Tsering, who had been silent so long I'd half-forgotten he was awake, spoke into the dark.

"In my village," he said, "when a family finds bones on the glacier, they do not argue about who found them. They send for the monks, and they tell the relatives, and the relatives decide what is done." A pause long enough to hold a whole philosophy. "This is not complicated. You are making it complicated because you are both rich in the wrong ways."

"Rich in the wrong ways," I repeated.

"You have a name people print. He has money and maps. The families have nothing but the waiting. So give them the waiting's answer, and stop dividing the spoils of a grave." He adjusted Dorje's blanket with the tenderness he denied having. "Also, sleep. Tomorrow we cross the glacier, and the glacier does not care whose fault anything was."

Leave it to Tsering to compress three hours of moral reckoning into four sentences and a logistics order. I lay back against the stone with the journals pressing their familiar bruise against my spine, and for the first time since Leh, the weight felt different. Not lighter. Reassigned. A load you carry for yourself wears you down. A load you carry for delivery — that's just work, and work I knew how to do.

* * *

Morning came gray and undecided, the rain thinned to a drizzle that was somehow worse, because drizzle meant the storm was thinking about continuing rather than finishing. We ate. Tsering checked Dorje's arm and frowned in a way that moved our timetable up without anyone voting on it. The river outside had dropped maybe a foot overnight — enough to attempt the crossing we'd scouted, not enough to make it comfortable.

I packed the journals last, wrapping them, settling them against my spine where they rode best.

"Before we move," I said, and both men looked up. "I want to say it plainly, once, so it's on the record and not just in a cave. The promise was *deliver it to whoever deserves it.* I spent the night deciding who that is. It's not me. It's not him." I nodded at Devan, who didn't flinch this time. "It's the eleven. Their families get the truth first, whole, unedited, before either of our names touches it. We're couriers. That's the job. That was always the job — I just spent twenty years training for it without knowing the cargo."

Devan held out his hand, and I shook it, and it was a strange handshake, formal and slightly absurd in a dripping cave, and completely binding. Some contracts are signed in ink and some in rainwater, and the rainwater ones hold better.

Tsering watched us with the expression of a man who had solved this problem yesterday and was pleased the principals had caught up.

"Good," he said. "Now the glacier. And Mara—" he paused, and there was something in his face I hadn't seen before, something almost gentle, "—your father would have approved of the terms. I have read his margins. He was a man who believed things belonged to the living."

I didn't answer. There wasn't an answer that fit inside my throat. I tightened the pack straps, checked the rope, and led us out of the cave into the drizzle, toward the crevasse field, toward the valley, toward eleven addresses I was going to have to find.

Behind us the river kept shouting. Ahead, the cloud lifted just enough to show the pass we'd come through, and below it, faint in the wet distance, the terraced green of the foothills — and the village where an old woman had spat at my name, and where, in a day or two, I was going to kneel in the mud and put her grandfather's handwriting into her hands, and find out whether restitution weighs more or less than shame.

Only one way to carry it and see.
