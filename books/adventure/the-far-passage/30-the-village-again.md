## 30. The Village Again

There is no graceful way to arrive anywhere in the monsoon. You do not walk into a village in the rain; you are washed ashore there, dripping, diminished, smelling of wet wool and desperation. By the time we came down out of the last defile and saw the stone walls of Tashi Gampa through the murk, I had stopped pretending to be a guide and was simply a woman following Tsering's back because his back knew where it was going.

Dorje walked between Pemba and Devan, his splinted arm cradled like something borrowed from a museum. He had stopped cursing me somewhere around the glacier crossing — not because he'd forgiven me, I suspected, but because he'd run out of curses that carried far enough through the rain to reach me. His fever had broken two days before, thanks to antibiotics pressed on us by Devan's mutinous cook, who turned out to have packed a field kit better than any of ours. There is a particular humiliation in being saved by your rival's kitchen staff. I've decided to carry it with dignity.

The village looked exactly as it had three weeks ago, which felt obscene. Smoke rising from the same chimneys. The same yaks complaining at the edge of the same pens. The barley had come up greener, that was all. The world here had gone on measuring itself in seasons while we'd been up there learning what a single hour could cost.

Tsering's cousin met us at the gate — a broad, cheerful man named Norbu who took one look at Dorje's arm and one look at the rest of us and asked no questions, which in my experience is the highest form of hospitality. He herded us into the same house where the old woman lived, the one whose grandfather had been among the eleven, and for a moment I stood in the doorway with water running off my hood and could not make my legs agree to cross the threshold.

It was her house. Of course it was her house. Tsering had known that when he chose it, and I understood, watching him unbuckle his pack with elaborate calm, that he had been steering us here since the river. Not toward shelter. Toward this.

* * *

She was smaller than I remembered, or perhaps I had grown heavier. She sat by the stove with her hands folded in her lap, and she watched us file in and drip on her floor with the patience of someone who has watched weather her whole life and expects nothing from it.

Her name, Tsering murmured to me, was Dolma. I had not known it before. I had thought of her only as the old woman, which is its own kind of theft, isn't it — reducing a person to their function in your story.

I knelt.

Not gracefully. My knees cracked loud enough that Pemba flinched, and the wet rope coiled at my shoulder slid forward and thumped against the packed earth. But I knelt, because I had rehearsed this in the cave two nights ago while the others slept, and every version I'd rehearsed standing up had sounded like a press conference.

"Dolma-la," I said. "My name is Mara Okafor."

She looked at me for a long time. Rain drummed the roof. Somewhere outside a yak made its opinion known about the weather, and I sympathized completely.

"I know," she said at last, in halting Nepali-accented Tibetan that Tsering translated softly beside me, though I caught enough. "You came before. The daughter."

"The daughter," I agreed. "And I came before to ask you questions. That was wrong of me. I came to take your family's grief and use it as fuel." I reached into my pack, slowly, so she could see everything I did. "I've brought something back. It was never mine to keep."

The ledger was wrapped in three layers of plastic and had ridden against my spine from the cache, above the journals, in the place where I carry things I cannot afford to lose. I unwrapped it there on the floor between us. The oilcloth inside had gone brittle; the canvas cover was stained dark with forty years of mountain damp, and the stitching along the spine had rotted so that the whole thing opened like a mouth.

Elias Okafor's expedition ledger. Ration weights. Rope counts. And eleven names.

I put it in her hands.

* * *

What happens next, I want to tell you, is dignified. That she read it in silence, that we sat in respectful quiet, that grief in the high country is a private country with closed borders.

None of that happened.

She opened it the way you open anything old — carefully, then carelessly once you see it won't break further — and she turned past the supply lists, past my father's small ferocious handwriting tallying kilos of tsampa and lengths of rope, until she found the page with the names. Eleven names in a column. And beside each name, in the margins, entries in a different hand. A looser hand. A hand that wrote quickly, the way people write when they're cold or happy or both.

Her grandfather's handwriting.

She made a sound I have never heard a human being make before and hope never to hear again — not a cry, nothing with edges to it, something lower and older than crying, a sound like ground shifting under snow. Then she wept, openly, hugely, without any of the restraint I had imagined for her, and pulled the ledger against her chest the way you hold a child, and I knelt on her floor and understood that everything I had climbed, every meter of it, had been preparation for sitting still in this room and doing absolutely nothing.

Because there was nothing to do. That's the thing nobody tells you about restitution. You haul the truth down off a mountain through a monsoon, you nearly die four separate times doing it, and when you finally set it down in front of the person it belongs to, you discover the truth doesn't fix anything. Her grandfather was still dead. Had been dead for forty years, frozen into the moraine while the world called him abandoned by a coward. The ledger didn't raise him. It just proved he had been *there*, writing, hoping, annotating rations with little jokes in the margins — Tsering translated one for me later: beside the entry for dried meat, her grandfather had written *Elias counts everything except how much he talks.*

He had joked. Up there. In the last days. They had joked.

I think that's what broke her open, more than the names. Not the confirmation of death — she had grieved him decades ago, in the way villages grieve the lost, half-mourning and half-anger — but the evidence of life. The handwriting. The jokes. Proof that the eleven had not merely endured their final days but had *lived* them, right up to the edge, and that someone had written it all down so that no one could ever say they were only a number.

* * *

She read for over an hour. We ate what Norbu fed us — butter tea, tsampa, a stew I will remember until I die — and Dorje slept by the wall with his arm propped on a saddle, and Devan sat across from me and did not look at the ledger once, which took, I suspect, more discipline than the descent had.

When Dolma finally looked up, her eyes were swollen slits and her voice had changed register entirely, gone soft and certain, the way voices go after they've passed through something.

"My grandmother told stories," she said, through Tsering. "That he was a careful man. That he weighed everything twice." She touched the ration columns. "Here he weighs everything twice."

"Yes," I said.

"And the other name. Okafor." She said it carefully, tasting it. "The one history calls a thief of men."

"That's why I came," I said. "The journals prove—"

"I don't need the proof," she said, and Tsering's translation stumbled slightly, as if he too was surprised. "A man who writes down every gram of food for eleven people does not abandon eleven people. Any woman can see this. It needed no mountain." She looked at me then with an expression I couldn't parse until later, when I was lying awake listening to the rain, and understood it: pity. Gentle, unoffended pity, aimed at me. "But you needed the mountain. So. Good that you went."

I have been praised by clients, thanked by expeditions, commended by rescue organizations. Nothing anyone has ever said to me has landed like *good that you went*, delivered by a woman in her eighties who had already forgiven my father twenty years before I finished needing to.

* * *

Devan spoke near dusk. He'd been holding something all day — I could see it in the way he kept his hands flat on his knees, the way he watched the ledger without touching it.

"Ask her," he said to Tsering, and then, when Tsering glanced at him, he pushed forward in his own broken Tibetan and the two languages braided together. "Ask her if she wants mine."

He meant the letters. Reyes senior had survived, come down, married, raised a son, and never spoken of the mountain — but he had written. Letters to himself, mostly, found after his death, which Devan had shown me in the whiteout three days pinned at the cache, when we had stopped being enemies and become two people sharing a fire and a wound. Pages of a man trying to write his way toward a confession he never mailed. *I cut the line. I tell myself the whiteout decided. I tell myself many things.*

Dolma listened to Tsering's translation with her hands still resting on the ledger. Then she said something long and quiet, and Tsering translated:

"She says the dead man's grandson should bring them himself. Not send them. Bring them. And read them aloud, because her eyes are old now, and words should be heard in the voice of the family that carries them." A pause. "She says both families have carried this mountain too long alone."

Devan nodded. He didn't trust his voice; I recognized the condition intimately.

Then Dolma did something I will carry longer than any summit. She rose — slowly, one hand on the wall — crossed the room, and set the ledger in my hands again.

"It goes to the archive," she said. "To the young ones, the students, the ones who will rewrite the books. But first—" she tapped the cover, once, "—you copy the page of names. By your own hand. And you give the copies to the families. All of them. A name copied by hand travels differently than a name printed. Everyone in the mountains knows this."

Everyone in the mountains knows this. Forty years of oral memory, and she'd handed me the technology of it in one sentence: testimony isn't published, it's *carried*, hand to hand, weight to weight.

"I will," I said. "Every family. Every name."

"And yours," she said, pointing at the column, at the ninth name down, the one with the woman's initials I had matched to a water flask on a wind-scoured slope while the ground rewrote my childhood beneath my boots. "You will take hers yourself."

I couldn't speak. I nodded. Outside, the rain eased for the first time in two days, as if even the monsoon understood that some deliveries shouldn't be interrupted.

* * *

We stayed two nights. Norbu wouldn't hear of less, and Dorje's arm needed it, and honestly the rest of us needed it more than we admitted. On the second evening Dolma had me sit beside her while she went through the ledger page by page, telling me who each name belonged to — whose granddaughter now ran a tea house in Kargil, whose son had died young, whose line had scattered to Delhi and Kathmandu and one improbable nephew in Toronto. I wrote it all down. It was the first fieldwork I'd done in years that felt like my father's kind: patient, humble, done kneeling.

On the third morning the sky cleared to a hard washed blue, the passes behind us sealed white, the valley ahead steaming like a kettle. We repacked. Devan stood at the gate with his pack shouldered, headed the long way around — he had letters to deliver, in person, to a list of houses he'd copied from my notebook without asking, which is how I knew he'd understood everything.

"Mara." He hesitated, then stuck out his good hand. "Toronto. I'll find the nephew."

"You'll get lost."

"Probably." He almost smiled. "Send me the route anyway."

And then we were walking down-valley, five of us instead of six, the village shrinking behind us into green and smoke, and I was carrying a copied page of eleven names against my spine where the ledger used to ride, and ahead of us lay the valley town, the roads, the world with its opinions and its publishers' agents and its clean shirts.

Tsering fell into step beside me and said nothing for a long while, which from Tsering is a paragraph.

"You gave it away," he said finally.

"She deserved it."

"You gave away the part that was yours, too. The money, the rights, whatever the agent in town will offer when word gets down." He glanced sideways at me. "Word always gets down."

I watched the trail unwind ahead, mud and stone and meltwater, ordinary as breathing. Twenty years ago I would have said the climb was the point. Ten years ago I'd have said the summit. Last month I'd have said the truth.

"There's a letter I still have to write," I said. "One family. Mine."

Tsering nodded slowly, satisfied, and lengthened his stride, and the valley opened below us like a page waiting to be written on.
