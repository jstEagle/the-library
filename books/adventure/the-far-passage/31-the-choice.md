## 31. The Choice

The valley town smelled of diesel and wet stone, and after three weeks of smelling only of ourselves, it was luxurious. I'd have paid money for the diesel. I may actually have; there was a transaction at the bus station I don't fully remember, only that I gave someone bills and received tea, and both of us seemed satisfied.

We had come down out of the Serrano Range the way water comes down — by the only channel available, at speed, carrying everything with us. Dorje's arm had been lanced and dressed at a clinic with a ceiling fan that turned with the solemnity of a prayer wheel. Tsering had slept for fourteen hours and then woken and eaten enough for two porters, which by his accounting made him even. Devan had vanished for a day — to wire his family, he said, to tell them the truth before they heard it crooked — and returned looking like a man who had set down a load and discovered his shoulders still remembered its shape.

And I had the journals. All of them. Elias Okafor's last season, wrapped in a plastic dry bag, riding in the bottom of my pack like a second spine.

I told myself I was resting. Resting is what I called it when I sat on the guesthouse roof with a cup of tea going cold and did nothing but watch the clouds come up the valley in long grey caravans. The monsoon had arrived properly now, the way an army arrives — first scouts, then the noise, then the whole weight of it. Rain came down the valley in visible sheets, and the town's gutters ran loud enough to talk over, and nobody in the world could ask me to go anywhere for at least a week.

It was on the third day of this that the clean shirt found me.

* * *

His name was Marcus Feld, and he was staying at the better guesthouse, the one with the generator. I learned this because he came to mine, which told you something about his methods: he'd heard the rumors, traced the party that had come down the Okafor route in monsoon season, and rather than summon me to his territory, he'd crossed the wet street to mine. A man who does his research and then does his walking.

"You're Mara Okafor." He said it not as a question but as a man laying a card on a table.

"I'm a guide," I said. "The name's common."

"It isn't, though." He smiled. He had a good smile, the kind that had been practiced in mirrors until it stopped being a lie and became a tool. "That's rather the point, isn't it?"

He was maybe forty-five, wearing a shirt so white it looked like a rebuke to the entire valley, and he had the particular stillness of someone who had learned that the person who moves first in a negotiation loses a small invisible thing. He bought me lunch at the one restaurant with a roof that didn't leak over the tables. I let him. I was curious, and curiosity has always been my most expensive appetite.

"I'll be direct," he said, after the noodles came. "There's a story coming out of this range. Eleven dead men, a disgraced expedition, a cover story that stood for twenty years, and now — if the tea-house talk is anything like accurate — a set of journals that turns the whole history on its head. Written by the man history called the villain."

"And if the tea-house talk is accurate," I said, "what's it to you?"

"I'm a literary agent. Delhi, London, New York. I make books happen." He said it without apology, the way you'd say *I make rain happen* if you were the monsoon. "And I make the people who hold the story rich, if they move quickly, and poor, if they don't. There will be a film option. There will be a memoir deal. There will be a bidding war, Ms. Okafor, if the material is what I think it is. The only question is who controls it."

I ate my noodles and thought about the word *controls*.

"You understand," he went on, gentler, the way a man gentles a horse he intends to bridle, "that if you don't take this, someone else will. Rumors travel faster than monsoons. In six months there will be three books, and none of them will be yours, and none of them will be careful. The story will be told by whoever is loudest. Isn't it better that it be told by someone who was there? Someone with — " he gestured at me, at all of me, the rope-worn hands and the face I keep deliberately unreadable — "standing."

"Standing," I repeated.

"You're the daughter. You found the journals. You're the one person on earth with an unimpeachable right to that story." He leaned in. "Exclusive rights. I can get you an advance that would buy you out of guiding for life. No more five-thousand-meter passes for paying strangers. No more calculating other people's distances. You'd never have to carry anyone's pack again but your own."

And there it was. Not the money — the money was real, and I'm not going to pretend I didn't feel its weight, because I did; I felt it the way you feel a heavy pack at the end of a long day, as a specific and personal gravity. But underneath the money was the thing he'd actually found to offer: *never again.* Never again the cold, the risk, the arithmetic of other people's survival. He had looked at my life and correctly identified it as a sentence, and he was offering parole.

I put down my chopsticks.

"Mr. Feld. Do you know what my father was accused of?"

"Abandoning eleven men on the mountain," he said promptly. "Leaving them to die and descending with the survivors — with one survivor, I believe. A man named Reyes."

"One survivor," I agreed. "And do you know what the journals actually say?"

"I was rather hoping you'd tell me."

I looked at him for a long moment. He had the patience of his profession; he could have waited out a whiteout. And I understood, sitting there in the steam of the noodle pot, that I was looking at the exact mechanism that had built my father's ruin in the first place. Not malice. Not even lies, exactly. A story, arriving before the truth did, told by someone with standing, and then hardening into history the way mud hardens into a road. Reyes senior had come down the mountain first, and alive, and articulate, and the world had taken his version because his version was *available*.

"Here's what I know about controlling a narrative," I said. "Twenty years ago, one man controlled it. He told his story exclusively, to everyone, forever. He got to decide whose memory counted. Eleven families got a villain instead of a husband, a father, a daughter. My family got a fraud." I turned my cup slowly on the table. "And the reason it worked — the reason it held for twenty years — is that the man who owned the story was the only one telling it."

Feld's smile didn't move, but something behind it did, the way a glacier moves: imperceptibly, and with consequences. "You're comparing me to a man who cut a rope line."

"I'm comparing *exclusivity* to a man who cut a rope line." I said it evenly, and I was surprised at how evenly, how much it sounded like route-finding rather than accusation. "The rope isn't the crime. The crime is deciding that your survival is worth more than everyone else's. An exclusive is a rope line with one end."

He sat back. To his credit — and I want to be fair to him, because fairness is the whole point of this chapter, if chapters can have points — he didn't bluster. He reorganized.

"You're telling me you want to give it away."

"I'm telling you I haven't decided anything yet. But if you're asking what I'd want from a book, it wouldn't be control."

"Then what would it be?"

"Accuracy. Every descendant of those eleven men reading the truth before the public does. The archives getting the originals. No film where an actor plays my mother." My voice did something on the last sentence that I hadn't authorized. I took a breath and let it pass. "And if there's money — and there will be, you're right about that — then it doesn't come to me and it doesn't come to Devan Reyes. It goes to the families. All of them. Equally. Including mine, if mine qualifies, and I'm not sure it does."

Feld was quiet for a while. Outside, the rain came down the street in a brown rope, and a man went by under an umbrella held by two children, all three of them laughing.

"You understand that's not how publishing works," he said at last, but without conviction, the way you say *that's not how physics works* to a thing that is happening.

"Then it's a good thing," I said, "that I'm not a publisher."

* * *

I found Devan that evening under the guesthouse awning, watching the rain with the expression of a man doing long division. I told him about Feld — the offer, the numbers, the parole.

He listened all the way through, which I've come to respect about him. Then: "What did you say?"

"Not yet. I wanted to say it to you first."

"Why?"

"Because it's not only mine to refuse." I sat down beside him. The awning drummed. "That's the trap, Devan. Not the money. The *shape* of it. He offers one person the whole story, and the moment one person takes it, we're back to one man on the mountain deciding what everyone else gets to know. Reyes senior with a book deal."

Devan turned his cup of tea in his hands. He'd been doing that a lot since the glacier; I recognized the gesture, because I'd caught myself doing it too, holding warm things longer than they needed holding.

"My grandfather was my hero," he said. "You know that. Twenty years of him being quiet and decent and teaching me to read terrain, and it turns out the quiet was — " He stopped. Started again. "If we sell it, if you sell it, someone will make him the monster of the piece. And the journals don't make him a monster. They make him a frightened man who made the worst choice a person can make and then spent his whole life paying for it without telling anyone. That's not a monster. That's worse. That's *knowable*."

"Yes," I said.

"And if we give it away —" he looked at me sideways — "then everyone's grandfather gets to be knowable. The eleven. All of them. The woman who insisted on coming." He said it carefully; he had learned, on the mountain, how to hold my mother's name.

"The woman who insisted on coming," I agreed, and we let the rain fill the silence, because some silences are load-bearing.

"Then we refuse the exclusive," he said.

"We refuse the exclusive," I said. "But —" and here was the part I'd been turning over since lunch, the knot I couldn't get my fingers into — "we can't just refuse. Refusing doesn't stop the story. Feld's right about that much. In six months there'll be three books and none of them careful. If we say nothing, the narrative goes to whoever's loudest, and we've learned what that costs."

"So we don't say nothing."

"So we don't say nothing. We tell it first, and we tell it to *everyone at once*. Every family. The archives. All of it, simultaneous, free. Nobody gets to own it, including us. The only way to keep from choosing whose memory counts is to refuse to choose — to make it count for all of them equally, on the same day, in the same words."

Devan was quiet a moment. "That's not a book," he said. "That's testimony."

"That's testimony," I said.

He almost smiled. "Feld's going to hate it."

"Feld's going to hate it," I agreed, "and then Feld's going to try to sell it, because a man like that can sell anything, including the thing that defeated him. And maybe that's fine. Maybe the testimony can wear whatever coat it has to wear to reach everyone. But the coat isn't the testimony, and we don't sign anything that makes us the coat."

He raised his tea to that, or to something near it.

* * *

Feld came back the next morning, as I knew he would, with the numbers improved and the shirt somehow whiter. I met him on the guesthouse roof because I wanted the rain around me, wanted the sound of it, wanted to have the weather on my side for once.

I told him no. Not the improved no, not the negotiable no. The no that has bedrock under it. And then — because he had done his walking across a wet street, and because fairness is the whole point — I told him what yes could look like. The simultaneous release. The families first. The archives. The money, if money came, flowing to the descendants of eleven, every name on the ledger weighted the same.

He listened with his professional stillness, and when I finished he was quiet long enough that the rain got loud.

"You do understand," he said finally, "that you're describing the least profitable book of the decade."

"I'm describing the only version of this book I could live inside," I said. "You've read the tea leaves, Mr. Feld. You know there's a story here. The question your whole profession exists to answer is *who tells it*. And my answer is: everyone it happened to, at the same time, before anyone gets to tell it *for* them. If you can build that, build it. If you can't, walk back across your wet street and let the rumor mill do its worst. It's already done its worst once. We survived it. The truth survived it. It was just twenty years late, and eleven people paid the interest."

He looked at me for a long time. And I'll say this for him: he didn't try the last trick, the one where the agent gets wounded and noble and tells you you're throwing away your birthright. Maybe he saw it wouldn't land. Maybe he'd read the journals' reputation the way he'd read everything else, accurately.

"You know," he said, standing, shaking rain off the awning edge out of habit though nothing there could fall, "in twenty years of this work, I've been called a vulture, a parasite, and once — memorably — a monsoon. You're the first person to explain to me that I'm a rope line." He buttoned his coat. "I'll make some calls. If there's a way to do what you're describing — and there might be; scandals have been given away before, it's called a scandal — I'll find it. Not because I'm a good man. Because a story this honest will sell *more*, and I've never once in my life stood in front of more."

"That," I said, "is the most honest thing anyone's offered me all year."

"Give me a week," he said, and went down the stairs into the brown rope of rain, and the white shirt went grey at the shoulders before he reached the corner, and I found I was glad.

* * *

Tsering found me on the roof after, because Tsering finds me everywhere, which is the tax on having told him everything.

"Well?" he said.

"He'll try. He might even manage it."

"And if he manages it, what have you won?"

I thought about that honestly, because he'd have caught a dishonest answer the way he caught my lie about the fee, back at the first river, and let it stand, which was worse.

"Nothing," I said. "That's the point. That's the whole point. I spent my whole life measuring things — meters, days, other people's distances — because measuring felt like control. Feld offered me the biggest measure there is. My name on a story. My version. My father, corrected, with my hand on the scale." I watched the rain come up the valley. "And I finally understand that the scale was the crime. Not the money. The *choosing*. Reyes senior chose whose memory counted, and it killed eleven people a second time. I'm not going to be the one who chooses. Not even well. Not even for good reasons."

Tsering nodded slowly, the way he did at the top of a pitch, confirming the route before committing to it.

"And the money," he said, "that goes to the families."

"Every family. Equally. Including mine, if mine qualifies." I hesitated. "Does mine qualify, do you think? My mother was one of the eleven."

Tsering considered this with the seriousness he gave to river crossings. "Your mother qualifies," he said. "You qualify as her daughter. Whether you'll take it is a different question, and not mine." He paused. "Though I'd take it. Money is not a memory. Money doesn't decide anything. It just buys yaks."

I laughed — the first real laugh since the icefall, it felt like, since the cache door, since my father's voice came up out of a tin and said he was excited about the dig. "Buy yaks," I agreed. "Notarized yaks."

* * *

That night I took the journals out and read the last entry again, the one I'd read a dozen times, the one where Elias Okafor's handwriting goes loose and cold and he writes, in the voice of a man who has decided something final: *If anyone finds this, don't make a monument of me. Make a record. Records can be corrected. Monuments just stand there being wrong.*

I had thought, when I first read it, that it was an instruction about the mountain.

Sitting there in the lamplight with the monsoon hammering the roof and an agent's business card on the table and the whole machinery of *who tells the story* idling outside my door, I understood it was an instruction about exactly this. About the clean shirt. About the offer. About the sweet, reasonable, well-paid temptation to stand there being right — my right, my version, my vindication — instead of lying flat and letting everyone walk over the truth in their own boots.

My father had dug where others wouldn't. It was carved on his compass, and I'd left the compass on the moraine with the monks' rites, needle pointing home. But the digging wasn't finished. The record wasn't corrected yet. There were letters to write — eleven families, eleven doors to knock on with cold hands, the way the past had once knocked on mine.

I put the journals back in the dry bag, and I got out paper, and I addressed the first letter to a woman in a high village who had once spat at my name and would, I hoped, one day read her grandfather's handwriting and weep for reasons that were finally true.

The rain kept falling. Somewhere below us, the rivers were rising.

And somewhere in the valley, a man with a clean shirt was making calls, and I had a week to decide how much of the telling I could give away before the giving-away itself became a kind of keeping — a question I wouldn't know the answer to until the letters went out, and the first reply came back, and I learned that the dead had been keeping a secret even from me.
