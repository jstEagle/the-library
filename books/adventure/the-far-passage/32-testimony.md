## 32. Testimony

My grandfather taught me two things about money before he died: never lend what you can't afford to lose, and never trust a man who talks about legacy. He said it the way men say things they've already failed at — quietly, while looking at his hands.

So when I sat across from Mara Okafor in a Leh tea house six weeks after we came down off the mountain, and she slid a folder across the table and said, "We give it all away," my first instinct was to check my wallet.

"Give it away," I repeated.

"All of it. The journals, the photographs, the ledger. Scanned, printed, bound. Copies to every descendant of the eleven. Copies to the archives in Kathmandu and Delhi and London. No exclusive rights. No advance. No book deal." She stirred her tea like the matter was settled, which was her way of telling me it wasn't, that she'd rehearsed this argument in front of a mirror and needed me to lose quickly so we could both get on with our lives.

"You've been talking to a lawyer," I said.

"I've been talking to three lawyers. They all think I'm insane."

"They're right."

"Probably." She looked up at me then, and there was something in her face I hadn't seen on the mountain — not the guide's competence, not the daughter's grief. Something more tired and more free. "Devan. My father spent twenty years as a fraud because one man told a story and everyone else decided it was easier to believe him. The journals are the only testimony that survived. If we sell them, we decide who gets to hear it. If we price it, we decide who can afford to. That's how the lie happened in the first place. Somebody owned the story."

"And you want nobody to own it."

"I want everybody to own it."

I should tell you now that I had spent those same six weeks doing exactly what you'd expect from a man who'd just learned his childhood hero cut a rope line in a whiteout and left eleven people to die. I'd gone home to Shimla. I'd sat in my grandfather's study, which still smelled of pipe tobacco and old paper, and I'd read the letters he wrote after the descent — the ones where he described Elias Okafor's "abandonment" in careful, wounded prose, letters that had been quoted in every account of the disaster for twenty years. I'd held them and felt nothing I could name. Not anger. Not grief. Something more like vertigo, standing in a room where the floor turned out to be a painting of a floor.

And underneath the vertigo, I'll be honest, there was a smaller, uglier voice doing arithmetic. Exclusive rights to the rehabilitation of Elias Okafor meant exclusive rights to the condemnation of Tomas Reyes. A book deal would have paid for my mother's roof. It would have bought back the family name at retail price.

Mara saw all of that, probably, because she'd carried the same arithmetic up the mountain and set it down at the cache door.

"Neither of us profits," she said. "That's the whole design. The moment either of us takes a rupee, the story becomes ours again, and it isn't ours. It belongs to a woman in a village who spat at your name last autumn."

"She spat at mine too, technically. Same name."

"Don't make me laugh. I'm being righteous and it's exhausting."

* * *

The work took four months, and it was the least glamorous labor of my life, which is saying something for a man whose previous career was negotiating salvage contracts over satellite phones in airport lounges.

We hired a scanning shop in Leh run by a young Ladakhi named Stanzin who treated the journals like relics, which they were, though he was the only one of us who handled them that way consistently. Mara wore cotton gloves for exactly one day before deciding gloves made her clumsy and her hands were cleaner than most archivists'. She read every page twice — once for transcription, once for the marginalia, because her mother's handwriting kept surfacing in the margins like fish rising in a stream. *Check the ice bridge before trusting it.* *Elias sleeps too little.* *If we don't make the pass by the 14th, turn around, I mean it.* Her mother had known. Reading those annotations, Mara went very quiet for days at a stretch, and I learned not to fill the silence, because filling silences was half of how the original lie survived — people rushing to say something rather than sit with what was true.

I did the genealogy. It turns out finding the descendants of eleven dead climbers from a twenty-year-old expedition is mostly paperwork and stubbornness, and I am, whatever else you want to say about me, my grandfather's grandson when it comes to stubbornness. Expedition manifests. Insurance claims. A pension file in Delhi that listed next-of-kin for nine of the eleven. The tenth was traced through a school register in Darjeeling. The eleventh — the old woman's grandfather, Tashi Wangchuk, the one whose village remembered — needed no tracing at all. We walked up there ourselves in September, and Mara knelt in the dust of that woman's courtyard and gave her the ledger with both hands, and I stood behind her feeling like a man returning stolen goods he hadn't personally stolen, which is its own particular shame.

The woman's name was Dolkar. She was ninety-one and her eyes were going, so she made her granddaughter read the ledger aloud, entry by entry, ration weights and rope assignments and the last notes in her grandfather's hand — *wind rising, E.O. says dig in, we'll wait out the morning* — and when the granddaughter's voice broke, Dolkar took the pages herself and held them an inch from her face and read them silently, moving her lips, for a long time.

Then she looked at me. Just me. And she said, in broken English she'd clearly saved for decades for an occasion like this: "Your grandfather's name was on the other list. The list of the living." She handed the ledger back to Mara. "You brought the true list. Sit. Eat."

That was the closest thing to absolution anyone offered me, and I want to be clear that I didn't deserve it and she knew that and gave it anyway, which is a kind of wealth I have no idea how to repay except by doing what came next.

* * *

The printing was Mara's obsession and my education.

She refused a commercial publisher outright — "they'll want a dramatic arc, they'll want a villain, they'll round the edges until it fits on a shelf" — and instead we paid, out of our own pockets, split evenly down to the rupee, for a print run of three hundred copies. Plain covers. Blue card stock. Title page reading:

**THE SERRANO EXPEDITION, 2003: THE JOURNALS OF ELIAS OKAFOR AND MEENA OKAFOR, WITH THE LEDGER OF THE ELEVEN**

*Published jointly by Devan Reyes and Mara Okafor. Not for sale.*

"Not for sale," the print shop owner said, frowning at the copy. "You're paying me to make these and giving them away?"

"Yes," Mara said.

"That's bad business."

"It's the point," I said, and discovered I meant it, and discovered further that meaning it felt like setting down a pack I'd been carrying since I was nine years old and found my grandfather crying at his desk without explanation.

We mailed copies to forty-one households. Descendants of the eleven, scattered from Kathmandu to Toronto. Some of them had waited twenty years believing their people had died because of a coward named Okafor. Some had believed worse — that their mothers and grandfathers had simply wandered off, abandoned their families, chosen the mountain over them. One man in Pokhara had grown up on the story that his grandmother Meena had run away with another man. He was Meena Okafor's son. Mara's half-brother, though neither of us knew it yet; that letter comes later, and it deserves its own chapter, and I will not steal it here.

To the archives we sent full sets: the Royal Geographical Society, the Himalayan Club, the mountaineering museum in Kathmandu, the national archive in Delhi. With each set went a signed statement — Mara drafted it, I checked every word against my grandfather's letters, and we argued for two days over a single sentence. The sentence that finally survived read: *"Tomas Reyes's account of the abandonment was given in shock and grief and hardened into record; the journals establish that Elias Okafor remained with the stranded party and died with them. We publish this not to condemn the dead but to release the living."*

I fought for that sentence. Not the facts — the facts were undeniable, Elias's own hand describing the whiteout, Reyes senior's panic, the rope cut, the digging of the snow shelter, the final entries growing shorter as the cold took the others and then the handwriting itself going loose and slow. What I fought for was the framing. Because the easy version — the version the world would have devoured — was a swap: Okafor the hero, Reyes the villain, history corrected by inversion. And an inversion is just another story somebody owns.

"The truth doesn't need my grandfather destroyed," I told Mara. "It needs him accounted for. There's a difference, and the difference is whether the families of the eleven get their people back or just get a new enemy."

Mara looked at me for a long moment. "Your grandfather raised you better than he deserved," she said.

"He'd say the same about my father," I said, "and my father would agree, and somewhere in that chain of agreement is everything wrong with my family."

* * *

The histories began to change faster than either of us expected, which tells you something about how badly everyone had wanted permission to doubt the old story.

The first correction came from a climbing historian in Boulder who'd always found the official account "conveniently tidy," her phrase, and who published a twelve-thousand-word reassessment within five months of receiving the archive set. Then a documentary team, whom we refused to cooperate with — no interviews, no access, everything they needed was in the public copies, go read it yourself — produced something better than anything we could have given them: ninety minutes of descendants reading the journals aloud in their own kitchens. The shot that broke audiences, apparently, was an old man in Darjeeling holding a photocopy of Meena Okafor's margin note — *turn around, I mean it* — and laughing, actually laughing, at how much she sounded like his own late wife.

Mara watched that film once, alone, and told me afterward only that the actor they'd gotten to voice Elias was wrong, "too dramatic, he sounded like a movie archaeologist, my father sounded like a man making a grocery list for the end of the world," and that otherwise it was fine, and then she went out and guided a client up a 6,000er the following week as if none of it concerned her.

But the thing I keep coming back to — the thing I'd tell you if you asked me what the whole year was about — happened at a lecture in Delhi. Some institute, wood-paneled room, the usual audience of retired colonels and ambitious students. Mara spoke for forty minutes, plainly, no slides, just the route and the dates and the names of the eleven read slowly enough to be heard. In the question period a young historian raised his hand and asked her, with the gentle condescension of the well-schooled, whether she thought the rehabilitation of Elias Okafor might be "overcorrecting" — whether the pendulum shouldn't be allowed to settle before the record was amended.

Mara considered this with the patience of a woman who has waited out storms longer than this man's career.

"The record isn't a pendulum," she said. "It's a grave. You don't rebalance a grave. You dig down to what's actually in it, and then you leave it honest." She paused. "Also, I didn't rehabilitate anybody. I published what a dead man wrote. If that changes the story, the story was wrong. That's not overcorrection. That's the minimum."

The room applauded. She looked mildly embarrassed, the way she always did at applause, and afterward she told me the only part worth keeping was the young historian's face when she said *grave*, because he'd written his thesis on the expedition and she could see him mentally revising it in real time.

"Revision in real time," I said. "That's the whole business, isn't it."

"It's the only honest business," she said. "Everything else is salvage."

* * *

Here is what it cost us, itemized, because I promised myself I'd keep honest books even if the truth inside them was free.

It cost me the book deal — a real one, seven figures, offered by a London house through an agent who flew to Leh and sat in this same tea house and said the word *legacy* four times in twenty minutes. I declined. My mother's roof got repaired anyway, slowly, with savings, the ordinary way. It cost Mara a partnership offer from an expedition company that wanted her name attached to "the Okafor Route Experience," guided ascents with commemorative summit certificates. She declined with language I won't reproduce here but which Stanzin, who witnessed it, later described to me as "educational."

It cost us both something harder to itemize: the comfortable versions. Mine was the hero-grandfather. Hers was the martyr-father, the fraud-against-his-will, the man she'd spent twenty years defending in her head. The journals gave her back a better man than history allowed — but also a real one, a man who made route errors, who underestimated the weather window, whose confidence contributed to the party pushing past the safe turnaround date. Her mother's margins said so. *Turn around, I mean it.* Elias hadn't listened. The best man in the story was still a man who didn't listen, and Mara had to publish that too, and she did, every word, because a partial truth is just a lie with better posture.

What it earned us is stranger. Letters. Hundreds of them. From descendants, from historians, from strangers who'd lost people to mountains and needed someone to have written down the plain truth of it. We answered them together, splitting the stack like rations, meeting monthly in whichever town the work put us in, comparing notes on grief the way other partners compare notes on routes.

And it earned us this: in November, the Himalayan Club struck the word *abandonment* from its official expedition summary and replaced it with a single line — *"the party was stranded above the icefall; E. Okafor remained with them"* — and mailed us both a copy of the amended page, unasked, unsold, free.

Mara pinned it above her desk in Leh. I pinned mine above my grandfather's desk in Shimla, where his letters still sat in their drawer, no longer evidence of anything except that a frightened man once told a story to survive himself.

* * *

There was one copy left over from the print run. Number three hundred. Mara and I met in Leh in the spring to decide what to do with it, and we sat across from each other with the blue-covered book between us like a client neither of us wanted to guide.

"Auction it," I said. "For the village school. Dolkar's granddaughter goes there."

"Sell the last copy of the thing we swore not to sell?"

"For the school. That's not profit, that's plumbing."

Mara turned the book over in her hands, considering. Then she shook her head. "No. If we auction it, someone buys it, and then one family owns the object, and objects become relics, and relics become arguments." She slid it back across the table to me. "Library. A public one. Somewhere kids find it without looking."

"Which library?"

"Yours or mine," she said. "Coin flip."

I called it in the air — heads, Shimla — and it came up tails, so the journal went to the school library in Leh, catalogued under mountaineering, shelved between a guidebook and a novel, available to anyone with a library card, which is to say anyone at all.

I mention this partly because it's true and partly because of what happened eight months later, which I only learned about secondhand, and which Mara will deny mattered: a girl, fourteen, daughter of a porter Mara has worked with for years, checked the book out for a school report on local history. Read it cover to cover. Brought it back and asked the librarian whether the mountain was still there, and whether people still went up, and whether the cairn with the initials was real.

The librarian said yes, yes, and yes.

The girl said she was going to see it someday.

When Mara heard this, she said nothing for a moment, and then she smiled in the specific way she has — small, sideways, like weather changing — and said, "Good. Someone should. Preferably someone who measures the trip in years."

I didn't understand that remark yet. I would, later, when she started talking about gardens.

But that spring, the last thing that happened was this: a letter arrived at my office in Shimla, forwarded twice, postmarked from a town I had to look up. Handwriting I didn't recognize. On the envelope, beneath my name, in careful block letters: *Regarding Meena Okafor.*

I sat with it on my desk for a full day before I opened it. Some mail you know is going to redraw the map before you break the seal.

I opened it in the evening, with tea going cold beside me, and the first line read: *"You don't know me. I think I may be her son."*
