## 33. Burying Elias

The monks took some persuading.

Not because they were reluctant — the abbot at the gompa above the village listened to my request with the patience of a man who has heard every variety of human stubbornness and found most of it forgivable — but because what I was asking for didn't fit neatly into any category he recognized. A funeral rite, yes. But not in a village, not in a valley, not anywhere with a road. On a moraine field at five thousand four hundred meters, where the wind came off the ice like something with an opinion.

"You want us to carry butter lamps," he said, "up there."

"Tea lights, if that's easier. And prayer flags. And yourselves."

"And the dead man is already — " He made a small gesture, encompassing bones, exposure, the general indifference of altitude to human arrangements.

"He's been there twenty years. Longer than anyone's known it."

The abbot considered me for a long moment. Monks are not easily rushed; it's one of their best qualities and, when you're on a schedule against the snow line, one of their most maddening. Then he said, "You are the daughter?"

"I'm the daughter."

"Then we will come." He said it simply, the way you'd agree to lend someone salt. "The mountain has kept him long enough without prayers. That's not its fault. Mountains don't know any better. But we should tell it now, so it can let go properly."

I wanted to laugh and I wanted to cry, which by then was such a familiar combination that I'd stopped noticing it, the way you stop noticing a stone in your boot.

* * *

Tsering organized everything, because Tsering organizes everything, including things that have not yet occurred to other people as being things.

He hired two of his cousins from the village — Pasang and Norbu, brothers who argued the entire approach march about whether yaks or horses were more dignified transport for monks (the monks walked, which settled nothing) — and he provisioned the trip with the same ruthless competence he'd brought to the glacier crossing, the river traverse, and the long wet argument of the descent. Rope, stove, spare fuel, a tent rated for weather that hadn't happened yet but probably would.

What he did not do, this time, was ask me what I wasn't telling him.

That had been our bargain since the trailhead: no secrets carried above base camp. So before we left Leh I'd laid it all out on my kitchen table like equipment — the journals, the ledger copies, Devan's letters, the publishing arrangement, the whole inventory of what my father had been and what history had done to him. Tsering had read for two hours, drinking tea in complete silence, and then said only: "Your mother annotated better than he did. His margins are a mess."

"That's what you took from all of it?"

"It's what matters. The rest is grief. Grief keeps." He'd stood, rinsed his cup, and added, "We leave Tuesday. You'll want three days' food and your father will want a proper cairn, so bring the good rope."

Now, on the approach, watching him walk ahead of me through the terraced barley fields with the prayer flags bundled on his pack like a folded bird, I understood that he'd given me the only thing I actually needed, which was the pretense that this was an ordinary expedition. Route-finding. Load-sharing. Weather calls. A body of work to lose yourself in while the rest of you catches up.

The old woman in the village fed us again before we climbed past her walls. She didn't spit this time. She pressed a bag of roasted barley into Tsering's hands and said something in Ladakhi too fast for me, and Tsering translated with a straight face: "She says to tell your father the debt is paid. She says he can stop carrying it now."

"I'm the one who was carrying it."

"Yes," Tsering said. "She knows. That's how debts work. They pass down until somebody puts them down."

* * *

The moraine looked different in autumn light.

In the season of the search it had been all glare and shadow, a landscape of hard edges, every rock a potential answer. Now it was softer — rusts and greys, the glacier below us gone quiet and blue-veined, the air thin enough to see forever and cold enough to remind you that seeing forever is not the same as belonging here.

The monks arrived a day behind us, walking steadily, unbothered by the altitude in a way that struck me as either profound discipline or excellent genetics. The abbot carried his own pack. The youngest monk, maybe nineteen, spent the last hour of the climb visibly regretting every life choice that had led him to volunteer, and the abbot spent the same hour pretending not to notice, which is a form of compassion I intend to steal.

We built the cairn first. There was work to do before there could be words, and work is the language I've spoken longest.

Tsering and I carried stones from the slope above — flat ones, heavy ones, the kind that lock together and shrug off wind. Pasang and Norbu hauled the larger blocks with a rope and a system of grunts that constituted their entire vocabulary for the afternoon. We raised it over the place where the wind-scoured hollow held what remained: not much. Bones the color of the rocks themselves. The rotted frame of a shelter dug by hand, by a man with a broken shovel and eleven people to protect and no chance at all, who built it anyway because building it was the only act left that meant anything.

I'd read those journal entries a dozen times now. *Dug another meter today. Amrita says the wall will hold if we bank it with snow. Rations for six days if we're honest, ten if we lie to ourselves.* My mother's handwriting in the margin: *Be honest.*

They'd had nine days. The storm gave them nine days, and he used every one of them digging, and when the digging was done he'd sat down between his wife and a young porter named Sonam and written a final entry that I still cannot read aloud without my voice doing something humiliating.

*If anyone finds this: I did not leave them. Tell them I did not leave them.*

History had told everyone exactly the opposite for twenty years, and the terrible arithmetic of it — that a lie travels faster downhill than truth climbs — was the thing I'd spent my whole adult life outrunning without knowing I was running.

The cairn went up stone by stone. When it was shoulder-high, Tsering called a halt and looked at me.

"Now?" he asked.

"Now," I said.

* * *

The rites took most of a day, and I will not pretend to have understood half of what was said.

There were chants that rose and fell like weather systems. There was the lighting of lamps inside a windbreak we built from packs and stones, the flames bending but refusing to die, which felt less like symbolism than outright defiance. There was flour scattered to the four directions, and barley thrown, and a bell whose sound the wind took and returned in pieces. The youngest monk, having recovered, sang with a voice that went up into the thin air and kept going after mine would have quit.

And through all of it, the abbot worked. He untied knotted cords and burned paper offerings and spoke to the mountain in a low, conversational register, the way you'd speak to an old colleague about a transfer. *This one is finished with you. He did his work here. Let him go home now.*

At one point he beckoned me over and handed me a handful of tsampa.

"Family gives the offering," he said. "You are the family."

I threw it into the wind and watched it scatter white against the grey slope, and something in my chest that had been clenched for twenty years — I want to say since the funeral-that-wasn't, since the telegram, since the day a twelve-year-old was told her father died a coward and decided the only defense was to become a person so competent nobody would ever connect her to him — something in my chest loosened by a single degree.

One degree. On a mountain, one degree is everything.

* * *

That evening, when the monks had retreated into their tents and the light had gone the color of old brass, I took out the compass.

My father's compass. The brass one with the dented lid, the one I swore I'd sold in Delhi at twenty-two along with everything else of his, and then found years later at the bottom of a storage locker like a debt I'd tried to discharge coming back with interest. The inscription on the back, worn nearly smooth: *To E.O., who digs where others won't.*

For the whole expedition it had ridden in my chest pocket, and I had checked it exactly never, because I navigate by landform and memory and the particular arrogance of someone who trusts her own head over any instrument. It had been cargo. Ballast. A thing carried because putting it down was impossible and using it was worse.

But tonight I opened it.

The needle swung, wobbled, settled. Pointed north-northeast, down-glacier, toward the passes and the valleys and the village and Leh and the house where I'd recently, absurdly, planted a garden. Toward everything that wasn't here.

Except that wasn't quite what it looked like anymore.

Because I'd spent the day learning something from the abbot, who navigated by a completely different system and had never once seemed lost: that *home* is not a fixed point. It's a bearing you choose. The needle doesn't tell you where to go. It tells you where you've agreed to return from.

"This is yours," I said. Out loud. To the cairn. To the bones under it. To the man who'd owned it and lost it and been slandered by history and dug a shelter anyway.

I set the compass on the flat capstone, lid open, needle steady, facing down-valley. Facing the way home.

"There," I said. "Now it points wherever you are. Which is here. Which is also, apparently, everywhere I've been walking for twenty years without noticing."

The wind moved the needle a fraction. I chose to believe that was a reply.

* * *

I stayed after the others turned in. Tsering, who notices everything and comments on almost none of it, left the stove burning near my tent and said only, "Take your time. Morning isn't going anywhere."

The stars came out the way they only do at altitude — brutal, abundant, close enough to be rude. The glacier ticked and settled below us, doing its slow geological bookkeeping. Somewhere in the dark, the prayer flags we'd strung from the cairn to a boulder snapped and fluttered, five colors working against the black.

I sat on a stone beside the cairn and talked to my father for the first time.

Not the way I'd rehearsed it, in showers and bus stations across two decades — the arguments, the defenses, the cross-examination I'd prepared for a man who couldn't attend. Not that. What came out instead was smaller and stranger and true.

"Hello," I said. "It's Mara. Obviously. You knew that."

A pause, for effect. He'd have appreciated the pause. Every journal entry had one, that little gap where he gathered his thoughts like a man counting coins.

"I became a guide. I don't know if that would have pleased you or worried you. Probably both. You'd have said something insufferable about how I inherited your sense of direction and your stubbornness and none of your patience. You'd have been right. I checked the margins. Patience: absent."

The flags snapped. The glacier ticked.

"I spent twenty years making sure nobody connected us. Do you understand how much work that is? Being the best at something quietly? It turns out being anonymous is a full-time job with no benefits. And then a dying prospector handed me a map and I found out you'd been up here the whole time, telling the truth to a tin box, waiting for someone to climb high enough to hear it."

My voice did the humiliating thing. I let it. Up here, at five thousand four hundred meters, with the wind for a witness, it seemed pointless to keep up the professionalism.

"Mum was here." That one came out barely above a whisper. "All those years I thought she left us — thought she chose the coast, chose her own life, chose anything over us — she was here. She chose you. She chose them. She wrote *be honest* in your margins and you were honest and it killed you both and somehow that's the best news I've ever had, and I know how that sounds."

I wiped my face with a glove that smelled of rope and stove fuel. Grief, it turns out, smells like an expedition. Everything does, eventually.

"We published it. All of it. Your hand and hers, side by side. Eleven families got letters this year, and I answered every one, and one of them wrote back just to say thank you, over and over, forty times, like he couldn't stop. Devan sends maps. He's restoring your routes, dig by dig. You've gone from a cautionary tale to a footnote to — I don't know what. A person again. A complicated one. The only kind worth burying properly."

I reached out and put my palm flat on the capstone. The stone was freezing and enormous and utterly indifferent, and it was also, somehow, warm. Both things. The mountain keeps its contradictions; I've stopped arguing with it.

"The abbot says you can stop carrying it now. The debt. He says mountains don't know any better, which I think is the wisest thing anyone's said to me all year. So — put it down. Whatever you've been holding. The rope, the blame, the last entry. Put it down. I've got it from here."

I sat with him until the cold drove even me — a woman who once guided clients through a whiteout on pride alone — back toward the tents. At the flap I turned around one last time. The compass sat on the cairn, catching starlight, needle steady, pointing home in whatever direction home had decided to be.

"Goodnight, Dad," I said. "Dig where others won't. I finally get it."

* * *

We descended the next morning under a sky scrubbed clean, the kind of post-storm clarity that makes the whole world look newly manufactured.

The monks went ahead, faster downhill, the youngest one practically skipping. Pasang and Norbu resumed their yak-versus-horse debate from the exact syllable where they'd left it. Tsering walked beside me in the easy silence of a partnership that no longer requires narration.

Halfway down, at the point where the moraine gives way to grass, he glanced at my chest pocket — the empty one, where the compass had ridden for a thousand kilometers — and then at my face.

"Gone?" he asked.

"Gone."

"Good." He adjusted his pack strap. "Lighter?"

I thought about it honestly, which is the only way he accepts answers.

"No," I said. "Same weight. Better distributed."

Tsering nodded slowly, the way he nodded at route decisions that cost effort but saved lives, and said nothing, and that was the eulogy, and it was enough.

Below us, the valley opened — green fields, white walls, smoke rising from morning fires, the whole ordinary machinery of the living getting on with it. And cutting down the trail toward us, moving fast, a figure with a familiar stride and a courier's satchel bouncing against his hip.

Devan. Out of season, off his routes, weeks early and climbing toward us with the look of a man carrying news that wouldn't wait for politeness.

Tsering saw him at the same moment I did.

"Well," he said mildly. "That's either very good or very bad."

"With Devan," I said, already picking up my pace, "it's usually both."
