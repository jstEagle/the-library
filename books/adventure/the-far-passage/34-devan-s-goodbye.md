## 34. Devan's Goodbye

The trailhead at Choksti smelled of juniper smoke and wet canvas, which is to say it smelled like every trailhead I've ever stood in while trying to say something difficult to a woman who had recently held my life on the end of a rope.

I'd arrived an hour before her, which was deliberate. There is a particular humiliation in being caught waiting for someone — you stand there with your hands full of nothing, visibly available, like a shopkeeper whose only customer has died. So I told myself I hadn't come early to wait. I came early to organize my pack. Three times. The same pack. It contained, by the third inventory: one stove, two fuel canisters, a sleeping bag rated for temperatures I hoped never to verify, a waterproof case of maps, and a grief I had stopped trying to repack because it wouldn't compress.

Mara came down the last switchback with Tsering behind her and Dorje bringing up the rear, his arm still splinted, his gait carrying that peculiar bounce of a man who has decided his body owes him nothing and is therefore free. She looked terrible. Sunburned, thinner, her lips still cracked from the wind, and she moved like someone who had set down a load so heavy that her spine hadn't yet learned its new altitude.

She saw me and stopped on the trail for a moment — just a beat, long enough for me to watch her decide whether to pretend she hadn't. Then she kept walking, and I understood that this was the greeting. We were past pretending, she and I. The mountain had stripped that off us somewhere above the icefall, along with most of our other insulation.

"Reyes," she said.

"Okafor."

"You look like you slept."

"I didn't. I have a face that lies about it. You look like you slept in a river."

"Close. A cave. The river came later." She dropped her pack against the tea-house wall and looked at me with those gray eyes that did arithmetic instead of sentiment. "You're packed for a long trip."

"I am."

"North?"

"West, first. Then north. Then wherever the maps say."

Tsering, who had the gift of hearing everything while appearing to hear nothing, began unloading gear with great ceremony, giving us the privacy of his industry. Dorje settled onto a bench and demanded butter tea from no one in particular until someone brought it.

* * *

Here is what I had not said to anyone, including myself, until roughly day two of the whiteout, pinned in that crevice shelter with three days of thin air and Mara Okafor's silence for company:

My grandfather was the hero of my childhood. This is not unusual — grandfathers are built for the role, all legend and lap — but mine came with documentation. Framed photographs of him at twenty-six, rope coiled over one shoulder, standing before a peak with a name I learned to spell before I learned my own surname. Newspaper clippings. A medal from a mountaineering club that had since folded, which should have been a warning about how history gets kept. And the story, told every winter by my father, always the same way: Tomas Reyes, the survivor. The man who came down when eleven others didn't. The man strong enough to live with it.

Nobody ever said the second part out loud. That was the trick of it. *Survivor* is a word that does its work in the dark, like a root vegetable. You don't ask what it survived *from*, or what it had to do to manage the surviving. You just serve it at dinner with pride.

And Elias Okafor — Elias Okafor was the villain in that same story, the partner who abandoned his men, the fraud whose "discoveries" were dismissed as fabrication within a decade of his death. My father said the name the way you'd spit out a bad seed. I grew up knowing two things about that mountain: my grandfather was brave, and Elias Okafor was a coward. Both facts arrived pre-installed, like furniture in a rented flat.

Then I sat in a hole in the ice reading a dead man's handwriting by headlamp, and the furniture rearranged itself.

* * *

"You're really going," Mara said. Not a question. She was watching my pack the way she watched weather — assessing what it would do next.

"Your grandfather dug sites across half the range," I said. "Everyone remembers the Serrano cache because it ended badly. Nobody remembers the other six. The salt deposits near Langtse. The burial cairn he wrote about in the early pages — the one with the bronze work. The river terrace site your mother annotated in the margins." I tapped the map case. "His journals reference field notes for each one. Notes that are presumably still sitting under stones in six different valleys, rotting, unread, attributed to nobody."

"And you want to dig them up yourself?"

"No." I shook my head. "That's the point. I'm not going to dig anything. I'm going to find them, document them, and hand them to the people who should have had them all along. Local archives. The university at Leh. Descendants, where there are any. My grandfather spent forty years being believed because he was the one who came down. The least I can do is spend a few years making sure the record stops believing him by default."

Mara was quiet for a moment. Below us, the valley went about its business — a woman beating a rug, two boys arguing over a bicycle with more conviction than brakes.

"You know what my father would have hated most about all this?" she said finally. "Not the disgrace. He could survive that; he had the ego for it. What would have killed him is being *believed wrongly*. Being famous for the wrong thing. He was precise, Devan. Obsessively precise. Every measurement twice, every note dated. And then the one thing he couldn't control — the story — got written by the least careful man on the mountain."

"The most frightened man," I said.

"Yes." She looked at me. "That too. I've stopped confusing the two."

This was new, this capacity of hers to hold both things at once — the accusation and its extenuation — without letting either cancel the other. I'd watched her do it for three days in that shelter, and it had done more to dismantle my grandfather than any argument could have. Anger I knew how to resist. Fairness was unbearable.

* * *

I should say something about the rope, because everyone who hears the story eventually asks about the rope.

On the descent, in the couloir, in that failing light, Devan Reyes slipped. That's the whole of it — a foothold gave, the world tilted, and for perhaps two seconds I was a falling object rather than a person. And Mara Okafor, who had every historical reason to let the universe settle its accounts with my family, held the line. Didn't hesitate. Didn't even swear, which for her I understand constitutes a miracle.

When I asked her about it later, in the cave, she said: "You weren't your grandfather. You were just weight on the rope."

But that's not true, and we both know it. She wasn't holding a weight. She was making a choice, every second of it, with her hands. And I have thought about those two seconds more than I have thought about anything else in thirty-one years of living, because they contain the entire argument against inheritance-as-destiny. Your grandfather cut a rope. Her father froze rather than descend alone. And then she — the daughter of the frozen man, heir to the abandoned — held on.

If that isn't a correction, I don't know what is. But corrections need to be written down to stick. Hence the maps.

* * *

We ate lunch at the tea house because Dorje declared that a man with a splinted arm had earned noodles and would hear no appeals. Tsering drank his tea and watched the two of us talk with the expression of a man observing weather systems that might merge into something dangerous.

"So the maps," Mara said, when the bowls were cleared. "How many are we talking about?"

"Nine. Six with usable coordinates. Grandfather was thorough about locations and silent about everything else — typical. He marked them, numbered them, and never once wrote down why he wanted them found." I hesitated. "There's a tenth mark. On the oldest sheet. No coordinates, just a peak sketch and a word in his handwriting I can't make out. Could be a name. Could be nothing."

Her eyes sharpened. "Show me."

"Not yet." I surprised myself saying it. "Because if I show you, you'll come. And you've got a season of guiding booked, a team to rebuild, and—" I gestured generally at her entire person, "—a life you're apparently attempting to have now. I heard about the garden."

"Tsering talks too much."

"Tsering talks exactly enough. It's one of his skills." I leaned back. "The tenth site will keep. Mountains are patient. It's their whole personality."

She almost smiled. It was a narrow thing, quickly rationed, but I counted it.

* * *

What I did not tell her, and will record here because a correction that omits its own costs is just another kind of lie: I nearly burned the maps. The night after we came down, in the guesthouse in the valley town, I sat with the map case open on my knees for four hours. Because here was the arithmetic as I saw it then: every site my grandfather documented was a site tied to his name, and his name was now attached to the worst thing a man can do on a mountain. Restore the record, restore the man — and somewhere in the restored record, a grandson might find himself defending Tomas Reyes again, explaining again, flinching again. Destroy the maps and the past stays buried. Everyone keeps their ghosts in their original graves.

I didn't burn them. Obviously. But I want it noted that virtue and I are recent acquaintances, and that I chose the harder thing mostly because Mara Okafor had spent three days in a hole in the ice refusing to choose the easier one, and I could not bear to be outdone by her a second time.

Envy, it turns out, composts into purpose given enough heat and time. Who knew.

* * *

Late afternoon, the light going amber on the ridge lines, we walked up past the mani wall together — unarranged, both of us apparently needing to move after a day of sitting. Prayer flags snapped overhead in the evening wind, their colors already sun-bleached to the shades of old bruises.

"My grandfather never spoke of it," I said. "The descent. Forty years, and not once. My father asked, once, as a boy, and got hit hard enough to learn the question was closed." I picked a bit of dried mud off a flagpole. "I used to think it was modesty. Hero-types and their burdens, all that. Now I think he knew exactly what he'd done and carried it the way you carry a stone in your boot — painfully, privately, and without ever once taking the boot off, because taking it off would mean looking at it."

"Elias stayed," Mara said quietly. "He could have come down. The journal makes it clear — Reyes had the rope, the strength, the margin. He could have gone back up. Instead he told the world my father ran." She paused. "The lie wasn't cowardice's second act, Devan. It was cowardice's *only* skill. Everything else about him — the survival, the endurance, the forty years of silence — that was just the interest accruing on one bad decision made in a whiteout."

"Do you hate him? Still?"

She considered it honestly, which I'd learned was her way — she treated questions like route-finding problems, actually studying the terrain before answering.

"Hate requires energy I've stopped spending," she said. "Pity, maybe. And anger on behalf of the eleven, which isn't the same thing and doesn't expire. But him, the man himself?" She shook her head slowly. "A frightened man in a storm. I've been a frightened person in a storm. The distance between us is smaller than I'd like, and pretending otherwise is how the last lie survives."

I looked at her then — this woman I had followed up a mountain to rob and left it owing everything — and felt the strange lightness I'd been carrying since the cave, the one I hadn't had a name for yet.

"I think it's called forgiveness," I said aloud, before I could stop myself.

"What is?"

"Whatever you just did. To him. To my grandfather. Possibly to yourself." I shrugged, embarrassed. "I read about it somewhere. Always seemed inefficient."

"It is," she agreed. "Terrible return on investment. Takes years, pays out in nothing you can spend." She turned back toward the tea house. "Do it anyway."

* * *

We said goodbye at the trailhead proper, where the path forked — hers toward Leh and the season's bookings, mine west toward Langtse and the first of the six sites.

Dorje insisted on shaking my hand with his good arm and informing me gravely that if I fell into a crevasse, I should scream in a high register, because low screams carry poorly at altitude. Tsering simply nodded at me, one professional to another, which from Tsering I understood to be roughly equivalent to a knighthood.

Mara and I stood a moment in the space between farewells, that awkward acreage where people who have shared a rope try to figure out what they are to each other now.

"Co-authors," she said.

I blinked. "Of what? Neither of us has written anything."

"We're writing it right now. Slowly. In six valleys and a bunch of letters and whatever the archive does with the journals." She stuck out her hand — guide's grip, dry and certain. "A correction doesn't get published once, Devan. It gets republished every time somebody tells it true. You'll be doing the fieldwork. I'll be doing the telling. Between us we might actually fix the record."

I took her hand. "Partners, then. God help us both — historically, that word hasn't gone well for anyone involved with these mountains."

"Historically," she said, "the partnerships failed because one partner lied. So we won't." She released my hand and shouldered her pack in one motion, that economy of hers, everything purposeful. "Send word when you find the first site. Send *coordinates*, not poetry. I don't trust your prose."

"It's my best feature."

"That's what worries me."

She turned down the trail toward Leh, Tsering and Dorje falling in behind her, three figures shrinking into the blue evening of the valley. I watched until the path bent and took them, and then I stood there a while longer anyway, because some departures deserve the courtesy of being witnessed.

Then I turned west, toward the passes and the old maps and the ten marks in my grandfather's hand, and started walking.

Behind me, far below in the valley, I heard — or imagined I heard, though I have decided to believe it — Tsering's laugh, carrying up the wind like something set free.

The tenth site waited. Whatever word my grandfather had written beside that unnamed peak, I would read it soon enough. And for the first time in my life, I found I wasn't afraid of what my family's handwriting might say.

I was only afraid of not going to find out.
