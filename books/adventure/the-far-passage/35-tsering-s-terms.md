## 35. Tsering's Terms

My daughter thinks I am a patient man. She says this the way children say such things — as accusation, not praise. "Apa," she told me last spring, watching me re-tension a door hinge for the fourth time, "you would wait out a glacier." She is eleven and already knows more about her father than most people learn in a lifetime, which is that patience and stubbornness are the same animal wearing different coats.

But I will tell you what actually happened after we came down from the mountain, because Mara Okafor will never tell it correctly. She tells stories the way she walks switchbacks — cutting the corners, arriving early, leaving out the part where she was afraid.

* * *

We came down into the monsoon like men being wrung out by God's own hands. Three days of rain, Dorje's arm going from bad to worse to the kind of bad where you stop talking about it and start doing arithmetic about how far it is to a hospital. The answer, every time I did the sum, was too far.

It was Mara who found the jeep track. Not on any map — there are no maps of that country worth the paper — but by standing in the rain with her eyes half shut, listening, the way she does, as if the land were telling her something in a language she'd been studying all her life. A tractor road, barely a scar, running down toward the valley town. We put Dorje in the one vehicle that came along in two hours, a vegetable truck driven by a man who charged us triple and earned it, and that was the end of the expedition as an expedition.

After that it was just people getting smaller behind us. Devan went east with his grandfather's maps rolled in oilcloth. The porters took their pay and their stories and dispersed like water finding its level. And then it was Mara and me in Leh, drinking tea in my sister's kitchen, both of us clean for the first time in two months and neither of us knowing quite what to do with skin that didn't hurt.

She said, "I want to hire you again."

I said, "No."

This surprised her. It should not have surprised anyone who had been above base camp with us.

* * *

Let me explain something about guiding, since everyone believes they understand it. People think the guide's job is to know the route. This is wrong. Any fool with a good memory can know a route. The guide's job is to be the person whose judgment other people bet their lives on without ever seeing the receipt. Every client who ties into my rope is making a purchase they cannot inspect. They buy my certainty, and my certainty has to be honest goods.

For twenty years, Mara Okafor sold honest goods. That is why I signed on for a late-season run into the Serrano Range on the word of a dying stranger — not for the fee, which she lied about, and not for the mountain, which owed me nothing. I went because when Mara looked at a slope, what she saw was true. I have guided beside men whose eyes added flattering details to everything. Hers subtracted. She saw mountains the way accountants see ledgers: no sentiment, no rounding up.

And then, at six thousand meters, I learned she had been carrying a secret the whole way up. A date on a map. Three days after the abandonment. She knew — or suspected — that something was waiting for us above the icefall besides a cache, and she let five people walk into it on her silence.

Now. You may say: the secret turned out to be nothing dangerous. It turned out to be journals, and grief, and the truth about her father. Fine. But the mountain does not grade on outcomes. If the annotation had marked a serac zone, if we had camped beneath it trusting her the way we trusted her, we would have died polite. Trust is not graded on outcomes either. That is the whole point of it, and the whole terror.

So when she sat in my sister's kitchen with her clean hands around a cup and said *I want to hire you again*, I said no, and I watched her take it the way she takes rockfall — flinch, assess, keep moving.

"Because of the map," she said.

"Because of the map."

"I told you why I kept it quiet."

"You told me afterward. Above base camp, you told me nothing." I set down my cup. My sister's kitchen smelled of cardamom and rain coming off the roof, and I remember thinking: this is the smell of the life I almost didn't come back to. "Mara. I have carried clients up mountains who cried, who lied about their experience, who hid heart conditions. I forgive all of them, because they are passengers. You are not a passenger. When you hide a thing, it becomes part of the rope."

She was quiet for a while. Outside, the monsoon worked at the gutters.

"What do you want?" she asked finally. Not *what will it take* — she didn't haggle, which I respected — but *what do you want*, as if my terms might be a real thing and not a price.

"One condition," I said. "No secrets above base camp. None. Not a date on a map folded small. Not a doubt you're nursing. Not a fear you think is beneath mention. If you carry a question onto the ice, it goes in the open, at the evening circle, where everyone roped to you can weigh it. Even if the question shames you. Especially then."

"That would have killed this last trip before it started," she said. "You'd have voted to turn around at the moraine."

"Yes," I said. "Probably."

"And the truth would still be sitting under the snow."

"Yes," I said. "And Dorje's arm would be whole, and the old woman in the village would still be spitting at your name, and you would still believe your father was a fraud." I leaned forward. "You got everything you wanted, Mara. Do not mistake that for proof the method works. You gambled five lives against your need, and the dice came up kind. I will not stand next to you while you roll them again."

She looked at me for a long moment, and I want to describe her face accurately, because novelists get faces wrong. They make them dramatic. Her face was not dramatic. It was the face of a woman doing arithmetic — the same arithmetic I had done over Dorje's arm, the kind where every answer is too far. She was counting what her silence had cost, and for once she was not rounding down.

"All right," she said. "Your terms. Full disclosure above base camp. Anything else?"

"Two things more, since you ask." I refilled her cup, because terms go down easier with tea. "First: partnership. Not head guide and hired help. If my name goes on the roster, my vote counts the same as yours on whether we go up. You don't get to override me with charisma. You've seen how far charisma gets you in a whiteout."

"And second?"

"Second." I hesitated, because this one was harder to say and I am not a man who finds hard words heavy until they are about myself. "You tell me things below base camp too. Not as a rule — as a habit. Your father. Your mother. What you're carrying when you're not carrying rope. I don't need the whole ledger. But a partner should know what weight his rope-mate is already carrying, so he knows how much room is left."

She stared at me. "That's not a professional term. That's friendship."

"It is whatever it is," I said. "Take it or leave it."

And here is the thing I will deny having said if you print it: she smiled. Not the guide's smile, the one that sells competence. A smaller one, tired, real, the smile of a woman setting down a pack she'd forgotten she was wearing.

"Tsering," she said, "do you know you're the first person in twenty years who's asked me what I'm carrying?"

"Then you have been hiring the wrong people."

"I've been hiring exactly the right people," she said, "and telling them nothing. It worked beautifully right up until it nearly killed everyone I respect." She put out her hand across the table. "Partnership. Full disclosure above the snowline. And — I'll work on the rest."

I shook her hand. Her grip was still strong from the rope work. Somewhere in the walls, the rain kept up its argument with the roof, and my sister pretended very loudly not to be listening from the courtyard, which is another thing my family does that I will also deny.

* * *

The season turned the way seasons do in Leh — all at once, overnight, as if someone had flipped a page. The rains moved south, the light went thin and gold, and the town filled up with trekkers buying down jackets they would photograph more than wear.

We hired for the autumn runs. Mara ran the interviews; I sat beside her and said little, which is my role in partnerships and one I perform excellently. She had changed more than she thought. Twice I watched her stop mid-interview, look at some young guide with hungry eyes and a résumé full of summits, and ask — instead of *how fast do you move at altitude* — the question I had taught her without meaning to:

"What's something a team wouldn't know about you that could matter on the mountain?"

The first boy lied, smoothly, and she thanked him and did not hire him. The second, a woman from Kargil with a stammer she only had when nervous, answered honestly: "I freeze at exposed crossings. I've been working on it. You should know before you rope me in." Mara hired her on the spot. "Everyone freezes somewhere," she said. "I'd rather know where."

You see? Terms travel. You set a boundary honestly and it propagates outward through everyone who agrees to it, like heat through iron. I did not say this to her. Some lessons you let people discover they've learned.

Dorje came back in October, arm healed crooked — the bone set well enough to work, not well enough to forget. He stood in the doorway of our office, which was Mara's front room with a map table in it, and he looked at the two of us and said, "So the partnership is official. Does this mean I get paid when you argue?"

"It means," Mara said, "that when we argue, you get hazard pay."

"Then I'm rich already." He grinned, and then he did the thing I am writing this whole account to record, because Mara will never describe it and it deserves describing. He straightened up, brought his splinted arm — he still wore the brace sometimes, out of what I suspect was vanity of a particular Dorje-shaped kind — up in something between a salute and a mock bow, and held it there, solemn as a monk, until she laughed.

"To the best worst boss on the mountain," he said.

"To the porter who swam a flood to reset an anchor," she said back, and meant it, and he knew she meant it, and the salute collapsed into a handshake and then into him stealing biscuits from the tin on the map table, which is how affection works among mountain people: we steal each other's food and call it love.

The team scattered the way good teams do — outward, not apart. Pemba took a winter job teaching rope craft to army recruits and swore he'd be back by spring. The Kargil woman — Sonam — signed on permanent. Devan sent a postcard from the east, no return address needed, just a line in careful handwriting: *Found the first dig site. Elias was thorough. More soon.* Mara read it three times and pinned it above the map table, and I pretended not to notice that she'd pinned it level with her eyeline, so she'd see it every morning.

* * *

One evening in November, past the season, when the office was closed and the light came sideways through the window, Mara spread a map on the table and didn't say anything for a long time. I know her silences now. This one had weight in it.

"The Far Passage," she said finally. "Reyes's name for it. The route over the range." She tapped the high country west of the icefall, the blank space where the passes seal by October. "Nobody's run it in the modern era. The old surveyors tried twice and lost men. If we opened it properly — mapped, documented, with the villages' consent this time —"

"You're asking me to look at a map," I said, "and pretend you haven't already decided."

"I'm asking my partner," she said, with the word placed carefully, like a foot on new ice, "to look at a map."

I looked. The route ran high and long, through country that had eaten better expeditions than ours. Below it, in the corner, she'd written figures — distances, elevations, ration weights — in her tight accountant's hand. And beside the figures, in different ink, newer: a list of names. The descendants. Eleven families, and beside each name a village or a town, and beside some of them, questions marks.

"What are these?" I asked.

"People I owe letters to," she said. "People who deserve to hear about the route before anyone walks it. Their grandparents' mountain." She met my eyes. "Full disclosure. Starting below base camp."

I studied the map a while longer, mostly so she wouldn't see my face do what it was doing. Then I took the pencil from behind my ear — a guide's pencil, worn to a stub, honest goods — and wrote my name on the route, just under hers, where a partner's name goes.

"There's a storm system building over the western passes," I said. "Early this year. If we're going to scout the approach, we leave inside two weeks."

She laughed — the real one, the small one. "You've already checked the weather."

"I am a patient man," I said. "Patience is just stubbornness with a calendar."

Outside, the first snow of the season was starting to fall on Leh, soft and unhurried, settling on the rooftops and the poplars and the map table visible through the window — settling, I noticed, thickest along the line of the high route, as if the mountain had already begun writing its reply. Mara watched it come down and reached for the pencil again, and on the corner of the map, under the eleven names, she began a twelfth list: what to pack, whom to warn, what to tell, and to whom, and when.

Everything, this time, in the open.
