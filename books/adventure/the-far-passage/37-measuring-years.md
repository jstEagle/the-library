## 37. Measuring Years

The apricot tree in the courtyard of my Leh house died sometime during the winter, and I found myself mourning it with a sincerity that would have embarrassed me two years ago.

I stood there in March with my hands in my pockets and my breath making small clouds, looking at the black branches against the blue, and I thought: *you never watered it.* Which was true. I'd bought the house eleven years earlier as a place to store things between expeditions — a locker with windows, a filing cabinet with a roof. The tree had been old when I got it, half-dead already, and I had treated it accordingly, which is to say I had treated it like everything else in my life that couldn't be carried.

So when spring came, I dug it out. And then, because a hole is an argument for filling it, I planted a garden.

I want to be clear about what this meant, because it will sound trivial and it was not. For twenty years I had measured my life in miles. Vertical meters gained, horizontal kilometers crossed, days of food remaining, hours of daylight left. Distance was honest. Distance could be counted, planned, rationed. A season was just weather that happened between trips. I had once told a client — a banker from Singapore who asked what I did in the off-season — that I didn't have one, and he'd laughed like I'd made a joke, and I'd let him.

Now I was on my knees in dirt that smelled like winter leaving, pressing seeds into rows I'd measured with a piece of string, and the unit of account had changed on me entirely. Radishes would be ready in forty days. Spinach in fifty. The apricot sapling I bought from the nursery down the road — the young man there looked at me with pity when I asked whether it would fruit soon — would take four years, maybe five, before it gave anything back.

Five years. I stood up, brushed soil off my knees, and felt something in my chest unclench that I hadn't known was clenched.

I was planting things I intended to be alive for.

* * *

Tsering came by in April, ostensibly to drink tea, actually to inspect.

He walked the courtyard twice with his hands behind his back like a government official assessing irrigation fraud, prodded the soil with one finger, examined the drip line I'd rigged from a salvaged hydration hose, and said, "You're watering too much."

"It's been dry."

"You're watering too much," he repeated, "because you are afraid they will die, and things that are afraid die." He sat down on the low wall and accepted tea. "My wife says the same about children."

"I'm not raising children."

"No," he agreed. "You are raising radishes. It is practice."

We drank our tea while the poplars along the lane did their new-leaf shimmering, that green so bright it looks temporary even though it comes every year. Dorje's arm had healed crooked — the bone set well enough, but he held it slightly differently now, and when he'd come through Leh in February he'd shaken my hand with his splint side and grinned and said, "Next time break the other one, madam, so I am symmetrical." He was guiding again already. Of course he was. You don't nearly lose a man to a fever in a snow cave and expect him to take up accounting.

"Devan wrote," Tsering said, watching me over the rim of his cup.

"I know. I answered."

"He is in Zanskar. He says he has found the second dig site. He says the records there confirm—" Tsering paused, choosing the words the way he chose footholds, testing each one before committing weight. "He says your father dug where others wouldn't, and what he found there, no one else wanted to find."

"That's the inscription."

"That's the inscription," Tsering agreed. "But now it is also true in the way that matters. There are three universities writing to him. Three. Last year they would not return his letters." He set down his cup. "You do not seem surprised."

"I stopped being surprised by that particular thing a long time ago," I said. "The part where institutions ignore you until you're right, and then congratulate themselves for discovering it. That part I know by heart."

"And the other part? The part where it cost you?"

I looked at my garden. Four neat rows of nothing yet, brown earth with pale seed markers, looking exactly like what it was: an act of faith performed by a woman who had spent her whole professional life telling clients that faith was not a survival strategy, planning was.

"The other part," I said, "is that I sleep at night now. Which took some getting used to. For years I slept like a guide — half awake, listening for weather. Now I sleep like a person who has said what she came to say."

Tsering nodded slowly. Then, because he was Tsering and sentiment between us had always traveled under proper documentation: "Your spinach row is crooked."

"It's my first season."

"Mm." He stood, stretched, and paused at the gate. "The season starts soon. Pemba wants to know if you are taking clients this year or if you have become a farmer."

"Both," I said. "That's the whole point."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about vindication: it arrives quietly, without trumpets, and mostly it looks like paperwork.

The journals went out in February — Devan and I had spent three weeks in December sitting across from each other at his kitchen table in Manali, arguing over annotations with a ferocity that his wife described, fondly, as "like watching two cats who have decided to share one sunbeam." We sent copies to the archives in Kathmandu, Delhi, and London. We sent copies to every descendant of the eleven we could trace. We sent one to the mountaineering club that had struck Elias Okafor's name from its rolls in disgrace twenty-three years ago, with no note attached, because the document didn't need a note. Documents like that never do.

The letters started coming in March.

Not from institutions — those came later, and they smelled of lavender and self-interest. From people. A woman in Shimla whose great-uncle was one of the eleven; she wrote nine pages about him, about his laugh, his bad knee, the harmonica he'd carried, and ended with: *For twenty years we were told he was led to his death by a coward. Thank you for telling us he was led by a liar, and that he did not die alone.* A man in Darjeeling sent a photograph of his grandmother holding the letter, her hands shaking too much for the picture to be sharp, and wrote beneath it: *She says now she can be buried in peace. She is ninety-one and has waited longer than you have been alive.*

And one letter — I keep it in the kitchen drawer with the string and the spare seeds — from a schoolteacher in Srinagar whose grandfather had been the expedition's cook. *My grandfather never spoke of the mountain,* she wrote. *He taught me to make bread instead. I understand now that these were the same lesson.*

I read that one standing in my garden with dirt under my fingernails, and I cried into the spinach row, which Tsering later informed me was excellent fertilizer and I should not let it go to waste.

* * *

I still guide. That surprised people more than anything else — the assumption being that after all of it, the storm and the cache and the truth and the burial, I would retire to write a memoir and give talks. But the mountain didn't take anything from me that it hadn't given back with interest, and besides, I was good at it. Twenty years of competence doesn't evaporate because you've finally unpacked your ghosts. It just stops being armor and becomes, simply, work. Good work. Work I loved.

What changed was the shape of the year.

Before, the year had been a corridor of departures: out in May, back in October if the weather allowed, gone again in November for the winter routes, home for six weeks to repack. My house had been a waypoint. Now I built the year around the center instead of the edges. I took bookings from June through September and refused the rest. When clients asked why, I said, "Because I have a garden," and watched them try to reconcile that answer with the woman who'd pulled them off a collapsing serac field with ninety seconds to spare.

In July I guided a party over the western passes — ordinary work, beautiful country, the kind of trip where nothing goes wrong and you remember why you started. On the fourth night, camped below a ridge I'd crossed perhaps thirty times, one of my clients, a retired geologist named Whitfield, asked me how many times I'd stood on that summit.

"Thirty-one," I said. "Give or take. I stopped counting for a few years."

"And does it change?"

I considered lying, or being clever, but the man had earned a real answer; he'd carried his own pack at sixty-eight without complaint through two days of sleet.

"It changes every time," I said. "That's the joke of it. You spend your twenties thinking the mountain is the fixed thing and you're the variable. Then you get older and realize you were both variables, and the only fixed thing is the fact of going." I poked the fire. "I used to measure a good season in vertical meters. Now I measure it in whether I was glad to see the trailhead at both ends."

"Both ends?"

"Leaving it and arriving back. If you're only glad at one end, something's wrong with your life. Took me twenty years and a dead prospector's map to figure that out."

Whitfield laughed, and the laughter went up into the cold air, and somewhere above us a chough rode the last thermal down off the ridge, and I thought: *my father stood on ground like this and was happy, and they called it fraud, and the ground kept the truth anyway.* Ground keeps things. That's its whole nature. It was my job, apparently, to be ground for a while — to hold what fell into me until someone came digging.

* * *

The garden, by late August, was frankly showing off.

Tomatoes — a variety the nursery man swore wouldn't ripen at this altitude, and which ripened out of spite. Peas that Tsering's daughter, Dolma, aged seven, harvested with the ruthless efficiency of a customs inspector whenever her father brought her by. She appointed herself my quality-control officer, sampling each pod with scientific gravity and pronouncing judgment: "This one is ready. This one is a failure. This one is mine."

I had never had a child in my house before. I'd built a life specifically engineered to avoid the entanglements that children represented — the staying, the counting-on, the terrible arithmetic of loving things that can be lost. But Dolma would arrive, eat peas standing in the row like a small horse, interrogate me about the mountain ("Did the snow really trap you for three days?" "Yes." "Were you scared?" "Yes." "Good," she said, satisfied, as though fear were a badge she was issuing), and leave, and the house would feel larger afterward rather than emptier.

One evening in September she asked, apropos of nothing, "Why did you dig up the dead tree?"

"So something new could grow there."

"Was the dead tree sad?"

"I think it was tired," I said. "There's a difference."

Dolma considered this with the gravity of a supreme court. "Papa says you used to be tired too. Before the big snow trip."

"Papa talks too much."

"He says you came back different. Happier-tired instead of sad-tired." She bit another pea pod in half. "I think gardens fix that."

Out of the mouths of seven-year-olds. I paid her in peas and she considered the transaction fair.

* * *

October came early that year, the way it does, sliding down off the glaciers overnight. One morning the poplars went gold all at once, and the light changed from summer's white glare to that slanted amber that makes even Leh's dust look gilded, and I knew the season was closing.

I harvested the last of everything on a bright cold Sunday. The tomatoes came in green and I put them on the windowsill to finish, the way my mother used to — and stopped, trowel in hand, at the realization that I'd learned that from her, from some ordinary autumn I couldn't even remember, some year when she was still just a woman with a windowsill and not yet a name on a list of the lost. The ground gives things back when it's ready. So do the dead.

I stored the tools, mulched the beds, wrapped the young apricot in burlap against the wind. Five years to fruit. I caught myself planning around it — where I'd be in five years, what the springs between now and then would need to look like for the tree to make it — and understood that I was doing the most radical thing of my life, more radical than crossing the Serrano Range in monsoon season, more radical than publishing the truth:

I was making plans that required me to stay.

* * *

The knock came in late October, when the first snows had already whitened the high passes and Leh had settled into its brown-and-blue winter quiet.

I was inside, mending a rope bag by the stove, when I heard footsteps in the courtyard — unhurried, unfamiliar — and then the knock, and when I opened the door there stood a young woman, mid-twenties, wind-burned, with a rucksack that had seen serious use and eyes that had seen less than they pretended. Climber's posture. Guide-watcher's gaze, already cataloguing my gear rack visible through the doorway.

"Are you Mara Okafor?" she asked.

"I am."

She shifted her weight, suddenly younger than her boots. "They call it the Okafor Route now. Above the icefall. In the guidebooks." She said it carefully, like handling something that might be hot. "I've read the journals. Both expeditions. All of it." She took a breath. "I want to climb it. And everyone told me not to ask you — that you don't take that route, that you haven't been back above the moraine since—"

"I've been back once," I said quietly. "To bury him."

Her face did the thing faces do when they realize the history they studied has a pulse. "Then I'm asking anyway," she said. "Will you take me up? I'll pay whatever—"

"Come in," I said, stepping back from the door, the warmth of the stove spilling out past me into the cold. "First you'll have tea. Then we'll talk about what the mountain keeps, and what it lets go, and whether you're ready to carry either one."

She hesitated on the threshold — they always do, that half-second where the story they came for meets the person who lived it — and then she stepped inside, out of the cold, into the house where the tomatoes were still ripening on the sill.

Above us, far up in the white silence of the range, the Okafor Route waited under its first snow, patient as ground, keeping what it keeps.
