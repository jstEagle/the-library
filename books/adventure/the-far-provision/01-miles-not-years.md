## 1. Miles, Not Years

The supercell had been building since noon, stacking itself against the sky like a cathedral someone had abandoned mid-prayer, and Theo Blackwood loved it the way you love a thing that has never once loved you back.

She stood on the flatbed of her truck at the edge of a canola field outside Wrenlee, wind tearing at her jacket, watching the wall cloud rotate with the slow patience of a thing that knew exactly what it was going to do. The air tasted metallic. Somewhere behind her, the mesonet station on a farmer's fence post ticked out wind speeds she didn't need to hear to know. You learned to read a storm in your teeth, in the pressure behind your eyes, in the way the grass lay down all at once like a congregation.

"Base is tightening," said the kid with the drone, a freelance stringer named Petey who'd paid her two hundred dollars to get him within a mile of a tornado for a wire-service photo. "Theo. Theo, the base is tightening."

"I can see the base is tightening, Petey."

"Should we — "

"Get in the truck."

He scrambled. She gave the storm one more second — one more long, honest second of looking at it the way you look at an old friend across a crowded room, someone you owe an apology to — and then climbed into the cab and pulled south on the gravel, keeping the mesocyclone on her right shoulder like a co-pilot she trusted more than any living person.

The hail came first, white and sudden, drumming the roof hard enough to make Petey yelp. Then the rain, horizontal, a gray curtain she drove through with one hand on the wheel and the other resting on the dash-mounted anemometer like it was a rosary. The tornado, when it finally dropped, came down two miles west of them over open range — a gray rope, then a fat gray elephant's trunk, wandering across the grass with the unhurried gait of something that had all the time in the world and intended to use all of it badly.

Petey got his shot. Theo got her cut: twenty percent of whatever the agency paid, which, if the tornado held together and the photo was clean, might come to nine hundred dollars.

She did the math the way she always did the math, automatically, the numbers running in a groove worn so deep in her head they were almost comforting. Nine hundred dollars. The fuel and hail damage would eat maybe three hundred. That left six hundred against a debt that had crossed the forty-thousand line in March and was accruing interest at a rate Cassius Bell had explained to her once with a smile so warm it had taken her a full day to understand the threat inside it.

Forty-one thousand, two hundred and six dollars. She knew it to the dollar the way some people knew their own birthday.

Twenty years ago she had measured her life in miles. Odometer ticks, river miles, storm miles — the distance between herself and whatever she was running from, which was, when she was honest, always the same thing wearing different weather. Her mentor, Elias Vance — no relation to the Syndicate, a coincidence that had cost him more than she'd ever admitted to him — used to say she was the only person he'd ever met who could be homesick for a place she'd never left. She'd laughed. She hadn't understood. You don't understand a thing like that until the miles pile up so high you can't see over them, and then you understand it completely, and understanding doesn't help.

The tornado roped out at dusk. Petey whooped and bought her a burger at the truck stop in Wrenlee, and she ate it in the cab while he uploaded his photos off a laptop balanced on a case of energy drinks, and when he left she sat in the parking lot under the buzzing sodium light and counted the miles since she'd last gone home.

Not home home. There wasn't a home home anymore; there was a house in a river town she'd sold to cover Elias's funeral, and before that there was a bunk on the *Kestrel Queen*, and before that there was a porch with a screen door that banged. She meant the last place that had ever felt like it was holding still for her, which was the dock at Grayling Bend, twenty years and — she did the math, hating herself for doing the math, hating that she couldn't not do it — a hundred and ninety-one thousand miles ago. She'd driven that distance four times around the planet, and every mile of it had been in the same direction: away.

The truck stop's pay phone was still there, bolted to the wall between the ice machine and the bulletin board, a fossil nobody had bothered to excavate. She didn't use it. But she looked at it. That was a thing she did now, at the end of chase days — looked at phones, looked at the little stack of unsent messages in her head, the ones addressed to a dead man and a woman who probably hated her and a crew scattered to the four winds like ashes.

Her phone buzzed against the dash.

She almost didn't look. It was usually Bell's office, or a collections bot with a synthesized voice so pleasant it made her want to put her fist through the windshield, or Petey wanting to book her for the next system lined up over the Rockies. She looked anyway, because forty-one thousand two hundred and six dollars meant you always looked.

It wasn't a call. It was a message, routed through the old storm-net relay — the amateur band, the one the commercial carriers had abandoned, the one only river people and weather people still bothered with. Telegram format, the way the old expeditions had done it, compressed to fit the bandwidth, all the personality wrung out of it except the personality that couldn't be wrung out of a person no matter how hard you squeezed.

**REYES TO BLACKWOOD. STOP.**

Her hand went cold on the phone. Not metaphorically. The cab had gone cold with the sundown and the hail-chilled air, and her fingers were stiff, and she told herself that was why.

**KNEW YOU'D BE ON THE PLAINS TODAY. STOP. YOU ALWAYS WERE EASIEST PERSON IN THE WORLD TO FIND BECAUSE YOU NEVER STOP MOVING. STOP.**

She could hear the voice in it. That was the terrible thing, the thing that made her sit very still in the parking lot with the engine ticking down and the sodium light buzzing: twenty years, and she could still hear Imogen Reyes's voice in the flat text, dry and precise, the faint amusement in it like a blade held flat to the light. *You never stop moving.* Most people would have meant it as a compliment. Imogen meant it as a diagnosis.

**I HAVE A JOB. STOP. RIVER JOB. STOP.**

Theo's thumb hovered over the screen. The truck stop light buzzed. Somewhere out past the dark fields, the storm's dying circulation still turned, invisible now, a ghost spinning down somewhere over the wheat, and Theo felt the old pull in her chest — the pull toward water, toward rivers, toward the one kind of running she'd sworn off, the kind Elias had made her swear off with his last lucid words in a hospital room that smelled like antiseptic and lilies.

*Promise me you'll stay off the Kestrel. Promise me, Theo. Not because it's dangerous. Because it's where you go to disappear.*

**I KNOW WHAT I'M ASKING. STOP.**

She hadn't told Imogen that. She hadn't told anyone that. Which meant Imogen knew anyway, the way she'd always known things — by being smarter than everyone in the room and patient enough to let them prove it.

**I KNOW WHAT IT COSTS. STOP. I'M ASKING ANYWAY. STOP.**

The message ended there. No signature flourish, no threat, no plea. Just the ask, sitting on her screen like a stone dropped into still water.

Theo put the phone face-down on the dash.

She got out of the truck. She walked to the edge of the lot, past the ice machine, past the bulletin board with its index cards — LOST DOG, HAY FOR SALE, a flyer for a church supper that had happened in 2019 — and stood looking east, where the sky had cleared to a hard, cold violet and the first stars were out over the plains. The wind had died. The whole world felt like the held breath after a tornado ropes out, that strange ringing quiet where you take inventory of yourself and the landscape and try to figure out what's changed.

What had changed was nothing. What had changed was everything.

Forty-one thousand, two hundred and six dollars. Cassius Bell's warm smile. The way the Syndicate's collectors had started showing up not at her bank but at her chase sites, standing at the edge of fields with their hands in their pockets, watching her work, saying nothing, saying everything. *We know where you are, Ms. Blackwood. We always know where you are. Isn't it exhausting, being so easy to find?*

Imogen had found her in an hour. Imogen had always been able to find her. There had been a time when that fact had felt like the safest thing in Theo's life, and then there had been a time — one specific afternoon, one specific river bend, one specific set of choices Theo had made while the water was coming up and the radio was screaming — when that fact had become the most dangerous thing in it, and she had spent twenty years and a hundred and ninety-one thousand miles making sure Imogen Reyes would never have to be dangerous to her again.

And now this. *River job. Stop.*

She thought about the pay phone on the wall behind her. She thought about how easy it would be to walk over to it, feed it quarters, and call the number she still knew by heart — Imogen's mother's old number, which meant Imogen's number, because Imogen was the kind of person who kept the same number for thirty years out of pure spite toward a changing world — and say, *No.* Say it kindly, even. Say, *I'm sorry, Im. I made a promise to a dying man and I've kept it for twenty years and I'm going to keep it until I die, and I'm sorry, and I hope you find someone else.*

She thought about how she would say it. She thought about how Imogen would receive it — the silence, the little exhale through the nose that was the only warning you'd ever get that you had genuinely wounded her — and she found that she couldn't finish the thought. She couldn't see the other end of the conversation. Twenty years of not looking at that woman, and she still couldn't see the far side of a phone call with her.

The wind picked up out of the east, off the plains, carrying the green smell of the canola and, underneath it, faintly, impossibly, the mineral smell of moving water. There was no water within a hundred miles. Theo knew that. She smelled it anyway. She'd been smelling it for twenty years.

She walked back to the truck. She picked up the phone.

She didn't call. She typed, instead, into the relay, her thumbs moving with a reluctance that felt almost physical, like dragging a boat over gravel:

**BLACKWOOD TO REYES. STOP.**

She stared at it. Four words. The easiest four words in the world to send, and the hardest, because the moment she sent them the twenty years would start ending, and she had built her whole life out of those twenty years the way a bird builds a nest out of whatever's in reach — grief and guilt and odometer ticks and hail-dented aluminum — and she did not know what she would be made of without them.

The cursor blinked. The sodium light buzzed. Out in the dark, the ghost of the storm finished dying, and the plains lay open in every direction, flat and endless and free, and she had never once in her life felt more trapped.

**WHEN. STOP.**

She hit send before she could take it back.

The reply came in under a minute, which meant Imogen had been sitting on the relay the whole time, which meant Imogen had known — had *known* — that Theo would say yes, and had waited anyway, giving her the dignity of the choice, which was either respect or a trap and had always been both with that woman.

**GRAYLING BEND. THE SALT LANTERN DOCK BAR. STOP. THREE DAYS. STOP. COME ALONE OR DON'T COME. STOP.**

A pause. Then one more line, and Theo knew — she knew with a certainty that settled over her like the pressure drop before a tornado — that Imogen had typed it, deleted it, and typed it again, because it was the only line in the whole exchange that sounded like her:

**BRING YOUR WEATHER SENSE, BLACKWOOD. STOP. WE'RE GOING TO NEED IT. STOP.**

Theo put the phone in her pocket. She got back in the truck and sat with her hands on the wheel, not driving, watching her breath fog the windshield in the cold cab, and did the only math that mattered, the math she'd been avoiding since the first message came in.

Grayling Bend was six hundred and forty miles east. Three days was enough time to make it there and enough time, if she was honest, to turn around a hundred times on the way.

Forty-one thousand, two hundred and six dollars said go. A promise made to a dying man said stay. Twenty years of miles said it didn't matter which she chose, because she'd be running either way — the only question was the direction.

She turned the key. The engine caught on the second try, the way it always did, stubborn and alive.

Theo Blackwood pointed the truck east, toward the river she had sworn off, toward the woman she had spent half a life avoiding, and drove into the dark with the storm's last cold wind pushing at her back like a hand between her shoulder blades — and if it felt, for the first hundred miles, less like running away than it ever had, she decided not to examine that until morning.
