## 2. The Optimist's Ledger

The Vane Syndicate kept its offices the way a spider keeps a web: clean at the center, sticky everywhere else.

Theo Blackwood noticed that every time she came in from the plains. The building on Corliss Street had once been a grain exchange — she could still see its bones under the renovation, the brass fittings gone green with age, the marble floor worn into a shallow river by a century of farmers' boots. Now the traders were gone and something quieter traded in their place. Debts. Leverage. People. The lobby smelled of lemon polish and old money, and the young woman at the desk smiled at Theo with the particular warmth reserved for those who owed more than they owned.

"Mr. Bell will see you now," she said, though Theo hadn't given her name. She never did. They always knew.

She rode the elevator up with her hands in her jacket pockets, one thumb worrying the edge of Imogen Reyes's telegram, folded so many times it had gone soft as cloth. *Kestrel run. Old water. Need you specifically. Terms negotiable. — R.* Fourteen words. Twenty years of silence compressed into fourteen words, and Theo had read them so many times on the drive down from the high plains that she could have recited them backward, forward, in her sleep, in the voice of every ghost she'd ever failed.

The elevator opened onto the top floor, where the windows ran floor to ceiling and the whole gray sprawl of the city lay below like something spilled. Cassius Bell stood with his back to it, silhouetted against the weather she'd chased all morning — the supercell was visible even here, a bruise-colored tower forty miles west, still rotating, still hungry. He'd have watched it all day, she thought. Men like Bell liked to watch storms they didn't have to stand inside.

"Theo Blackwood." His voice arrived before his smile did, warm as bathwater. "You look like you've been driving through the end of the world."

"I drove through the middle of it," Theo said. "The end's usually quieter."

Bell laughed — a real laugh, or an excellent forgery of one — and crossed the office to shake her hand. His grip was dry and precise, the handshake of a man who'd learned early that pressure was a language. He wore a suit the color of river silt and a tie the color of nothing at all, and he was, infuriatingly, exactly what the Syndicate needed him to be: pleasant. Approachable. The kind of man who remembered your daughter's name and your mother's surgery date and the exact figure you'd promised last spring, rounded to the dollar.

"Sit," he said, gesturing to the leather chair across from his desk. "Can I get you anything? Coffee? There's a bottle of the good rye if the morning warrants it."

"Coffee's fine."

He poured it himself, which was part of the performance too. Everything Bell did was part of a performance; Theo had understood that for years, the way you understand a river has a current underneath its surface. You could float on top of it for a long time if you respected what moved beneath.

"So." He settled behind the desk, opened a leather folio, and turned it toward her with two fingers, like a dealer revealing a hand. "Let's look at the ledger, shall we?"

The ledger was beautiful. That was the first thing anyone noticed about Vane paperwork — it was typeset like poetry, bound like scripture, every figure justified to the margin with loving precision. Theo's name sat at the top in small capitals. Below it, the arithmetic of four years of bad seasons, borrowed hulls, a repossessed radar array, interest compounding monthly like mold in a wet wall.

"One hundred and eighty-two thousand," Bell said gently, as if delivering a diagnosis. "Six hundred and change. We've been patient, Theo. Genuinely. Mr. Vane himself said — and I quote — 'the girl's good for it eventually, she's got hands like a surgeon and no sense of self-preservation, that combination always pays.'" He looked up, smiling. "It was meant as a compliment."

"It landed like one."

"But." He held up a finger. "Patience is not infinite. And I'd be failing you as a friend if I let another season pass without being honest about where things stand." He turned the page. Theo made herself watch. "Property secured against the debt: the *Marrow*, your chase truck — lovely old thing, by the way, I saw it downstairs, the hail damage gives it character — and the mooring rights at Pier Nine. If we were forced to call the paper, you'd be walking. Not destitute — I want to be clear, this isn't a threat—"

"It reads like one."

"—it's a map," Bell finished smoothly, unbothered. "I'm showing you the map so you can choose a different road. That's all any of us at Vane want, truly. For people to choose better roads." He folded his hands. "So. Tell me about the telegram."

Theo kept her face still. Outside, the storm ticked westward, and somewhere below them the *Marrow* sat in the loading bay like a hostage with its keys left visible on the hook.

"You open my mail now?"

"We don't open mail. Mail opens itself, when it wants something from someone who owes us." Bell's smile widened by a degree, no more. "Imogen Reyes. There's a name out of the past. You two ran together, didn't you? Before. Before the Kestrel took your mentor and two of your crew and gave everyone else back changed." He said it kindly. That was the worst of it — he said everything kindly, the way a surgeon says *this might sting*. "Twenty years, and now a telegram out of nowhere. Curious timing. Curious route."

"It's a salvage job."

"It's the Kestrel headwaters," Bell said, "in calving season, past the last legal marker, into water your own mentor swore off with a blood oath before he died. Which makes it either the stupidest job ever offered to a competent woman, or the most valuable one." He tapped the ledger. "And you're a competent woman, Theo. So which is it?"

She drank her coffee. It was very good coffee, which annoyed her. Everything about Bell was designed to annoy her in small ways so she wouldn't notice the large ones.

"Why do you care what jobs I take?"

"I care about my friends' futures." He leaned back, and the light from the storm window caught the edge of his jaw, sharpening him for a moment into something less friendly. "Here's what I know, and you can decide how much of it matters. Three weeks ago, a research vessel registered to a shell company out of Meridian filed a route plan with the inland water authority. The route plan was withdrawn the next day, but paper remembers. That route went up the Kestrel. Past the Narrows. Past the portage nobody talks about." He let the silence sit. "Two days ago, Imogen Reyes bought fuel, chain, and dive-grade winch cable in a town whose only reason to exist is that it's the last stop before the Kestrel gorge. Dive cable, Theo. In glacier country. Somebody's going after something that sank."

"You've got spies buying groceries."

"I've got friends who talk to other friends. It's how friendship works." He spread his hands, all innocence. "Now. Here is where I'm supposed to threaten you, I imagine — that's what you came in expecting, some speech about broken kneecaps and boats that catch fire at anchor. But I'm not going to give it to you, because you're smarter than that and I respect you. Instead I'm going to tell you a story."

He stood and walked to the window, hands clasped behind his back, watching the distant storm rotate.

"In 2004 there was an expedition up that same river. Good people. Experienced. A plane went in with cargo worth more than the boat that carried the crew. Two died. The expedition leader carried the blame until he died too, and the one survivor — a young woman with remarkable instincts and terrible luck — spent twenty years running storms across the plains because standing still felt too much like drowning." He glanced back at her over his shoulder. "That's not a threat, Theo. That's just the shape of the water. Some rivers you go down once and they keep flowing through you forever."

Theo set down the cup. Her hands were steady. She'd trained them to be steady in worse rooms than this.

"What do you want, Bell?"

"What I've always wanted. For you to prosper." He returned to the desk and slid a single card across the leather — plain white, a phone number embossed in the corner, no name. "If you take this job — and I think you will, because Imogen Reyes asked, and you've been waiting twenty years for her to ask — then I want one small courtesy. A check-in. When you learn something about what's up there, what's sunk, what's waiting — you share it. That's all. No sabotage, no theft, no drama. Just information. Consider it interest paid in kind."

"And if I say no?"

"Then you say no," Bell said warmly, "and I'll wish you well, and we'll both hope the season treats you gently. Though—" he tapped the ledger once, softly, almost tenderly, "—I'd remind you that debtors who run interesting rivers have a way of arriving at interesting ends. Not because of us. Because of the water. The Kestrel doesn't need our help killing people. It's very self-sufficient that way."

*There it is,* Theo thought. *Dressed in wool and good manners.* The threat wasn't in the words; it was in the architecture around them — the ledger, the secured property, the card, the smile calibrated to the millimeter. Bell never raised his voice because he'd never needed to. The Syndicate had built a machine where men like him only had to describe consequences, and the debtors performed the terror themselves, free of charge.

She thought about the *Marrow* downstairs, twelve years of dents and duct tape and one perfect hail-scarred hood, the only home she'd maintained longer than any lease. She thought about Pier Nine, about the mooring rights her mentor had signed over to her in a will that was mostly apology. She thought about one hundred eighty-two thousand six hundred and change, and about the number zero, and about how far apart those numbers were and how narrow the bridge between them.

And she thought about fourteen words on soft paper. *Need you specifically.*

Nobody had needed her specifically in twenty years. People needed her hands, her boat, her nerve, her willingness to drive toward the thing everyone else drove from. But *specifically* — that word had teeth marks on it, old ones, from a different life.

"If I take the job," Theo said slowly, "and I find nothing worth reporting, I report nothing."

"Naturally."

"And if what I find belongs to somebody's dead father, I'm not handing it to Vane out of neighborliness."

Something flickered behind Bell's eyes — quick, professional, gone. Interest, maybe. Or the satisfaction of a fish testing a hook and finding it seated just right.

"Fair terms," he said. "I'll note them in the file." He closed the folio with a soft clap, and just like that the ledger was gone, tucked away, the arithmetic of her life folded shut between covers. "For what it's worth, Theo — and I know you won't believe me — I hope this job saves you. Whatever it costs." He walked her to the elevator himself, pressed the button, shook her hand again with that dry precise grip. "Give Imogen my regards. Tell her the river's high this year. Higher than anyone expects."

The doors began to close on his smile.

"Bell." Theo put her hand in the gap and the doors shuddered back. "One question. Why does Vane care what's sunk in the Kestrel? Salvage rights up there are worthless. The water's illegal, the season's impossible, and whatever's in that plane has been underwater twenty years."

Bell considered her for a moment, and for the first time since she'd walked in, he stopped performing. What was underneath wasn't cruelty, exactly. It was patience — the deep, glacial patience of an organization that had outlasted every whistleblower, every lawsuit, every grave.

"Because things sink for reasons, Ms. Blackwood," he said pleasantly. "And reasons float back up. Give Imogen my best."

The doors sealed. The elevator dropped, smooth and silent, and Theo watched the floor numbers fall and thought about the way he'd said *reasons* — the way a man says a word when he knows exactly which body it's attached to.

Outside, the afternoon had gone strange and green, the whole sky stained the color of deep water. The supercell had turned; it was coming east now, toward the city, toward the plains, toward everything. Theo stood on the steps of the grain exchange with her collar up and the telegram against her chest, and she took out her phone and dialed the number she'd sworn off dialing twenty years ago, the one she still knew by heart despite having lost the paper it was written on a decade back.

It rang twice.

"Blackwood." Imogen Reyes's voice was older, lower, roughened at the edges like driftwood, but underneath it Theo heard the same current that had always moved beneath that girl on the dock, all confidence and salt. "I'll take that as a yes."

"I haven't said yes."

"You called. That's a yes with extra steps." A pause, and then, quieter: "High water this year, they're saying. Calving might come early. We'd need to move fast."

Theo looked west, where the storm was turning toward her like something that had finally decided to come home.

"How fast are we talking?"

* * *

She drove north through the front edge of it, wipers slapping, the *Marrow* shouldering through gusts that wanted her in the ditch. Behind her the city shrank; ahead the plains opened, and the lightning came down in long white stitches, sewing the sky to the ground. Somewhere up there past the tree line was a river she'd promised a dying man she'd never run again, and a wreck with a dead man's truth inside it, and a debt that would follow her to the bottom of any water she chose.

Her phone buzzed in the cupholder. A message from an unknown number, seven words:

*Enjoying the drive? — C.B.*

Theo didn't answer. She just drove faster, into the rain, toward the coast, toward the bar where a rival waited with terms she already knew she'd accept — because dread, she was learning, dressed itself as opportunity, and opportunity dressed itself as duty, and underneath all the costumes it was always the same old river, pulling.
