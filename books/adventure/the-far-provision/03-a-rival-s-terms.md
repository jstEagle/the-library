## 3. A Rival's Terms

The Anchor & Ash sat at the end of Pier Nine like something the tide had given up on. Its windows were salt-fogged, its sign hung by one chain, and its interior smelled of creosote, fryer grease, and twenty years of spilled beer soaked so deep into the floorboards that no amount of mopping would ever get it out. Imogen Reyes had chosen it for exactly those reasons. Nobody came to the Anchor & Ash to be seen. Nobody came here at all, really, except dockhands, smugglers, and people who wanted to talk without being overheard.

She'd taken the corner booth an hour early. She'd ordered a beer she wasn't drinking and a plate of fried clams she wasn't eating, and she'd arranged the table between them with the same care she gave to expedition manifests: the folder, squared to the edge; the photograph, face down; the second glass, waiting.

Twenty years since she'd last sat across from Theo Blackwood. Twenty years, and Imogen could still feel the shape of that last conversation like a scar under her clothing — the shouting in the rain outside the Corvallis staging yard, Theo's finger jabbing the air, *you don't get to buy your way out of this one, Reyes*, and then nothing. Two decades of silence, punctuated only by the kind of news that travels without anyone sending it: Theo wins the Marlowe Cup; Theo's boat burns at anchor; Theo is seen drinking alone in Whitehorse; Theo owes the wrong people money.

Imogen had kept the file anyway. Of course she had. You didn't spend your twenties racing someone down the Kestrel and through the Narrows and up the throat of the Greywater gorge without learning exactly how they moved when they were desperate. And Theo Blackwood, by every account that mattered, was desperate now.

The door opened at nine minutes past seven, which meant Theo was three minutes late, which meant she was making a point.

Imogen didn't look up right away. She let the bell above the door finish its rusted jangle, let the cold river air roll across the room, let herself hear the particular rhythm of those boots — heavy on the heel, slightly favoring the left knee, an old injury from a capsize in '09 that Theo had never once mentioned and Imogen had never once forgotten. Then she looked up.

Theo Blackwood stood just inside the doorway with her hands in the pockets of a canvas jacket that had been patched more times than it had been washed, and she looked — there was no kinder way to put it — like a woman who had been running out of road for a long time. Thinner than the photographs. Gray coming in at the temples like frost claiming a field. Eyes that did a full tactical sweep of the room before they ever landed on Imogen: exits, patrons, sightlines, the booth, her.

Then the eyes narrowed.

"You're kidding," Theo said.

"Sit down, Blackwood."

"I'm not sitting anywhere near you."

"You drove four hours to say that?" Imogen gestured at the bench opposite. "You read my message. You came. Sit."

For a moment Imogen thought she'd actually leave — thought she'd watch twenty years of planning walk back out into the drizzle on two bad knees and a pride even heavier than the debt dragging it down. But then Theo exhaled through her nose, crossed the room like a storm front, and dropped into the bench across from her, arms folded, radiating the specific hostility of a cat placed involuntarily in a bathtub.

"You look terrible," Imogen said.

"You look rich."

"I am rich." Imogen slid the untouched glass of beer across the table. "It's the only thing about me you've ever approved of."

Theo didn't touch the glass. Her gaze went instead to the folder, then to the face-down photograph, and Imogen watched her catalog both the way she used to catalog whitewater from a ridge — reading the whole run in three seconds, finding the line, finding the hazard, deciding whether the hazard was survivable.

"What do you want, Reyes?"

Straight to it. Good. Imogen had prepared a preamble — some dry remark about the bar, about the old days, something to ease them both past the first hundred yards of rapids — but looking at Theo's face now she understood that any preamble would read as weakness, or worse, as pity. Theo Blackwood would rather take a rock garden at flood stage than accept kindness from a rival.

So Imogen turned the photograph over and set it on the table between them.

* * *

It was a picture Imogen had looked at perhaps ten thousand times and never gotten used to: the *Meridian Star*, tail number 4471, half-swallowed by a scree slope above a lake the color of hammered tin, its wing bent against a boulder field like a broken arm. Snow in the open cargo door. No people in frame. There hadn't been people in frame for twenty years.

Theo went very still. It was the stillness of prey animals and experienced river runners, and Imogen recognized it because she'd spent her whole life studying both.

"That's the upper Kestrel," Theo said quietly. "Above the Greywater. That's—" She stopped. Started again, flatter. "That's the source lakes."

"The Far Provision," Imogen said. "My father's plane."

Something moved behind Theo's eyes — not surprise, exactly. Recognition of a name she'd been avoiding for two decades, arriving now like weather she'd been told was coming.

"Elias Reyes," Theo said. "Your father flew the provision runs for the survey expeditions. He went down in October of '04."

"He went down carrying forty pounds of documents that would have ended the Vane Syndicate before it became the Vane Syndicate." Imogen kept her voice level. This was the part she'd rehearsed, and the rehearsal held, mostly. "Ledgers. Shipping manifests. Correspondence between Vane's founders and three provincial ministers. My father was a pilot, not an investigator — he stumbled onto it hauling freight for men who assumed he was too uneducated to read what he carried. He wasn't. He copied everything. He was flying it out to a journalist when the weather took him."

"And the documents burned with the wreck."

"The wreck was never found. Officially." Imogen tapped the photograph. "Unofficially, the search was called off after eleven days by a ministry that Vane owned outright. My mother spent the rest of her life petitioning to reopen it. She died waiting. I've spent mine buying what petitions can't buy — satellite passes, LiDAR surveys, a glaciologist who owed me a favor, and finally, last spring, a high-altitude flight over the drainage basin during a rare clear window." She turned the folder around and opened it. Inside: contour maps, thermal imagery, a red circle drawn around a shelf of land beneath the glacier wall where the ice had retreated two hundred meters since 2004. "There. Under the moraine. A metal signature consistent with an airframe. Tail number matches."

Theo stared at the image for a long time. When she spoke again, her voice had gone somewhere careful and distant, like a person reading instruments in heavy turbulence.

"That water is closed. You know it's closed. The whole upper Kestrel above the Greywater confluence has been permit-locked since '04 — environmental protection, they said." Her mouth twisted. "We all knew what it actually was. Vane didn't want salvage crews poking around their crime scene."

"I know what it actually was."

"It's also suicide. The calving season opens in six weeks. Once the glacier starts shedding into the source lakes, the drainage channel turns into a cannon. Nobody runs that water. Your father's own lead guide swore off it—"

"My father's own lead guide," Imogen said, "was Ruth Calloway. Who swore off it because two of her crew drowned in the Greywater gorge that same autumn, running a rescue attempt that never should have been launched. And who spent the last year of her life telling anyone who'd listen that the river up there keeps what it takes." She met Theo's eyes. "You were on that crew, Theo. You were seventeen years old, and you watched it happen, and Ruth made you promise — all of you — that none of you would ever go back above the marker buoys. I know. I've known for years. I've read the incident reports, the inquest transcripts, Ruth's own letters — she wrote to my mother, did you know that? Twice a year, every year, until she died. Apologizing. As if it were hers to apologize for."

The silence between them stretched long enough that the bartender's radio, murmuring somewhere behind the taps, became audible — some ballad about trains, worn soft by repetition.

"So you knew," Theo said at last. Her voice was low and very controlled, the control itself a kind of fury. "You knew about the promise. You knew what it cost. And you sent me a telegram anyway — one line, *I have work for you on the Kestrel, come see me* — knowing exactly what hooking that word to that river would do."

"Yes."

"You didn't apologize for asking."

"No." Imogen leaned forward. "And I'm not going to. Because here's what else I know, Theo. I know you owe Cassius Bell and everyone he answers to a sum you cannot pay in this lifetime. I know what happens to Vane debtors who default — I've funded three of their funerals from a distance. I know you've been chasing storms across four provinces for gas money like a woman trying to outrun arithmetic. And I know that in six weeks, the calving season seals the upper river for another year, and the year after that, and eventually the glacier will grind that airframe into shavings and everything my father died carrying will be silt." She spread her hands flat on the folder. "So no. I won't apologize for asking. I'll pay for the asking — half up front, half on delivery, enough to clear your ledger twice over and then some. I'll fund the boat, the gear, the provisions, all of it. What I need from you is the one thing money can't charter: the best river pilot I have ever raced against, and the only living person who's run the Greywater gorge and lived to describe it."

"You haven't run it," Theo said.

"I've run everything below it."

"That's not the same river."

"No," Imogen agreed. "It isn't."

* * *

Theo looked away then, out the fogged window toward the pier, where the fishing boats knocked gently against their tires and the drizzle stitched silver threads down through the harbor lights. Imogen let her look. She understood what Theo was doing, because she'd done it herself a hundred times over the past year: standing at the edge of a decision, testing the ice, listening for the crack.

What Imogen did not fully understand — what she had traveled four hours and swallowed twenty years of pride to find out — was which way the crack would run.

"You want to know why I really came," Theo said, still watching the window. "Since we're being honest with each other for the first time since Clinton's Bend."

"If you want to tell me."

"Ruth died in March." Theo's reflection in the glass didn't move. "Cancer. Fast. I got the letter from her lawyer along with a box of her things, because apparently I was in her will, which — I don't know why. I froze, Imogen. That day in the gorge. Twenty years ago. The *Kestrel Queen* wrapped on the mid-channel rock, and the water was rising, and Pete and Danny were on the rock, and Ruth was screaming for lines, and I froze. Seventeen years old, best young boat handler on the river, and I stood in the stern holding a rope I couldn't throw. Ruth threw it. She got Pete off. Danny—" Theo's jaw worked. "Danny went into the water while I was still standing there. They pulled him out two miles downstream. Ruth took the blame at the inquest. Said her crew wasn't ready, said it was her call to launch. It wasn't. It was mine. I begged her to launch. I was the one who said we could make the eddy chain. And she let the world believe it was her failure so that a seventeen-year-old girl could keep her license and keep her life, and I let her do it, and I have been running miles ever since because miles don't ask you questions."

The words landed in the booth like stones dropped down a well. Imogen heard them hit bottom, one after another, deeper than she'd expected. She had built this meeting around a transaction — evidence for passage, passage for payment — and had braced herself for greed, for desperation, for the cold arithmetic of a drowning woman grabbing a rope. She had not braced herself for confession.

"Why are you telling me this?" she asked, and hated how quiet her own voice came out.

"Because you asked me to break a promise I made on that river, and you deserve to know what you're buying." Theo finally turned from the window and looked at her straight on, and her eyes were dry and terrible and steady. "Ruth's letter — the one in the box — said one thing I can't stop hearing. She wrote: *the river doesn't hold grudges, Theo. People do. Go back someday when it's yours to go back to.*" She shook her head slowly. "I don't know if this is that day. I don't know if anything is ever that day. But I know that if I let your father's plane rot into the moraine because I was too much of a coward to look at that water again, then Ruth's sacrifice bought nothing, and Danny's death bought nothing, and I might as well hand Cassius Bell my deed and lie down in front of the plow."

"So that's a yes?"

"That's a *maybe*," Theo said. "A maybe with conditions. One: I pick the crew. Every soul aboard, my call, no exceptions, no surprises."

"Agreed."

"Two: nobody goes above the marker buoys until I say the water's right. Not you, not me, not God himself if he shows up with a permit. If I call a camp, we camp. If I call a retreat, we retreat, and you eat the cost, and you don't say one word about it."

"Agreed."

"Three." Theo leaned across the table, close enough that Imogen could smell river water and diesel and the faint chemical sweetness of cheap adrenaline gum, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "If we find that plane, and we find what your father hid in it — I don't hand it to you sealed. I read it. All of it. Whatever your family spent twenty years digging for, whatever's big enough to make the Vane Syndicate send Cassius Bell to smile at me in person, I see it before it leaves that river. If I'm going to break a deathbed promise and take my crew into a graveyard, I need to know it's for something true."

For a moment Imogen couldn't speak. There it was — the thing she'd forgotten about Theo Blackwood in twenty years of keeping score from afar: not the talent, not the nerve, but this. The stubborn, unreasonable integrity that had made losing to her almost bearable. Elias would have liked her, Imogen thought, and the thought hurt more than she'd budgeted for.

"You'll read it," Imogen said. "Every page. That was always the point."

Theo sat back. Something in her shoulders loosened by a single degree — not trust, Imogen noted, but the decision to proceed without it, which was perhaps the more valuable thing.

"One more question," Theo said. She nodded at the photograph, at the bent wing and the empty snow. "Why me? You've got money. Money buys pilots. Good ones. Sober ones, even. Why spend a year hunting down the one pilot on this coast with a reason to hate that river?"

Imogen reached for her own glass, found it empty, and set it down again. Outside, the drizzle thickened against the window, and somewhere down the pier a halyard slapped a mast in a rhythm like slow applause.

"Because good pilots run rivers," she said. "And I don't need a river run. I need a ghost laid to rest — mine and yours both — and there's exactly one person alive who knows what that costs." She pushed the folder the final inch across the table. "Besides. You always were better in the water than me, Blackwood. It's the only race you ever won fair."

Theo almost smiled. It was a small, damaged thing, gone in a second, but Imogen caught it, and it was worth the four-hour drive and the twenty years and everything that was coming.

"We launch in twelve days," Theo said, standing, pocketing the folder without asking. "Pier Seven, dawn tide. Bring your own boots — I'm not lending you mine." She paused at the end of the booth, and for a moment the hard profile softened into something almost like the girl Imogen had raced through the Narrows, all those years ago, both of them certain the river belonged to them.

"Oh — and Reyes? When this is over, whichever of us is still talking to the other..." Theo's eyes flicked to the photograph, then away. "Drinks are on you. Forever."

She was gone before Imogen could answer, the bell jangling, the cold air swirling in and settling like a guest who intended to stay.

Imogen sat alone in the booth for a long time, looking at the second glass, still full, still untouched. Then she took out her phone, opened the encrypted thread she shared with no one but her attorney, and typed the message she had promised herself she wouldn't need to send:

*She's in. And Bell was right about one thing — she'll do anything to protect a crew. Which means we'd better make sure she never learns whose money started this.*

She hit send, and out on the dark water, far upstream, the glacier waited.
