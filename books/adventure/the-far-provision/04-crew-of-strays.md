## 4. Crew of Strays

Theo Blackwood had a theory about crews: you didn't hire people, you recognized them. You saw a person standing at the edge of their own life the way she was always standing at the edge of hers — one foot on the dock, one foot over water that had already swallowed better plans than this one — and you knew. Not from résumés. Résumés lied. But from the way someone held a wrench, or read a chart, or watched a river like it owed them an answer.

She needed four someones in nine days. Fewer, if the calving season came early, and it always came early now.

* * *

Jun Park she found where she knew he'd be: under a truck he didn't own, in a gravel lot behind a machine shop in Harlow Bend, with rain coming down hard enough to bounce.

"Park," she said to his boots.

The boots stopped moving. A long moment passed, and then Jun slid out on the creeper, blinking up at her through the rain, a smear of grease across one cheekbone like war paint applied by a careless hand. He was forty-something and looked sixty in bad light, which was what twenty years of fixing other people's mistakes did to a man. His hands were enormous for his frame, knuckles split and scarred, and they were the best hands on any river she'd ever run.

"Theo Blackwood." He said it the way a man says the name of a storm he survived. "I heard you were dead."

"I heard you were retired."

"I am retired." He sat up on the creeper, unhurried, letting the rain hit him like it was a conversation he'd already agreed to have. "Retired means nobody calls me at three in the morning about a fuel pump."

"Nobody's calling you about a fuel pump."

He studied her. Rain dripped off his chin. "You're calling me about the Kestrel."

Not a question. Theo didn't bother denying it; Jun had always been able to read her hull before she'd finished painting it. "Upstream run. Past the marker line."

"Past the—" He laughed once, short and disbelieving, and got to his feet, wiping his hands on a rag that made them dirtier instead of cleaner. "You swore off that water. Everyone who was there swore off that water. Aldous made you swear."

"Aldous is dead, Jun."

"I know he's dead. I sent flowers." His voice went flat and careful, the way it did when he was holding something heavy very still. "That doesn't unmake the promise."

"No," Theo said. "It doesn't. But there's a wreck up there with something in it worth more than the promise, and I need an engineer who can keep a boat alive through water that wants to kill it. And I need him to want the job so badly he doesn't ask too many questions until we're past the point of no return."

"And why would I want it that badly?"

Theo had come prepared with money numbers, debt numbers, the whole ledger. She'd rehearsed it on the drive. But looking at Jun — at the truck he didn't own, the shop that wasn't his, the retirement that fit him like a borrowed coat — she threw the speech away.

"Because you've been fixing other men's machines for six years," she said, "and you used to build things. I remember. You built things out of nothing and they *held*. Come work for me and you'll build again. That's all I've got. It's not much."

Jun looked down at his ruined knuckles, flexed them slowly, and Theo watched him do the arithmetic every stray does — not what the job pays, but what it costs to stay where you are.

"One condition," he said. "When we get to whatever it is you're not telling me about, you tell me straight. No captain's mysticism. No 'need to know.' Straight."

"Straight," Theo said.

He nodded once, went back under the truck, and came out thirty seconds later with a duffel bag already packed, because of course it was. Men like Jun kept the bag by the door. That was how she knew he'd say yes before he said it.

* * *

Odile Vasseur was harder. Odile was always harder.

Theo found her in the back room of a maritime archive in Port Ceres, a place that smelled of mildew and old ink, where Odile spent her days copying charts by hand for collectors who paid well because nobody else could read the old survey marks anymore. She sat beneath a green lamp like a nun at prayer, pen moving in strokes so fine they looked engraved.

"You're blocking my light," Odile said without looking up.

"You're drawing the Kestrel headwaters right now. Third sheet this month. Somebody's buying your maps of water that officially doesn't exist."

Odile's pen paused — just a fraction, just long enough. Then it resumed. "Collectors are sentimental."

"Collectors don't pay three hundred a sheet for sentiment. Someone's assembling a route." Theo leaned against the shelf, arms crossed. "Same someone who's been stripping caches along the old expedition line? Fresh rope cuts at the Narrows haul-out, a cache at Miller's Ledge cleaned out inside the last two weeks?"

Now Odile set the pen down. She turned in her chair, and her eyes — pale, unhurried, the eyes of a woman who navigated by noticing what everyone else missed — settled on Theo with something almost like approval.

"You've been to the Narrows recently."

"I've been everywhere recently. It's a side effect of owing money to the Syndicate."

"Mm." Odile folded her hands. "And you want me to read the river for you. The upper Kestrel. The water Aldous Marchetti took off his charts with his own hand and burned."

"He burned his copies. Yours are in your head."

Something moved through Odile's face then, quick as a fish under ice. Twenty years ago she'd been the expedition's navigator, the youngest and coldest head aboard, the one who'd plotted the line through the gorge that went wrong. She'd never said whether the line was wrong or the water was. She'd never said anything at all. She'd simply vanished into archives and ink and a silence so complete most people assumed she'd died.

"Imogen Reyes," Odile said quietly. "It's Imogen's money behind this, isn't it. I heard she was asking after Kestrel pilots in the delta towns."

Theo didn't confirm it, which confirmed it.

Odile rose, crossed to a locked drawer, and came back with a rolled tube of oilcloth. She set it on the desk between them but kept her hand flat on top of it.

"My price isn't money," she said. "My price is this: when we find whatever's up there — the wreck, the truth, whatever shape it takes — it goes on the record. All of it. What happened in 2004, who did what, whose name gets cleared and whose gets buried. I've spent twenty years navigating around a hole in the map, Theo. I'm done. Either we sail through the hole together, or I stay here with my lamp."

Theo thought of the journal page in her sea chest, the one she hadn't burned yet, the one with Aldous's handwriting and her own guilt pressed between the lines like a flower she couldn't stop watering.

"It goes on the record," Theo said. "Every word."

Odile lifted her hand off the tube. Inside was a hand-drawn chart of the upper Kestrel, complete, annotated in three colors of ink, dated the summer of 2004 — and corrected. Corrected in fresh ink, within the last year. She'd been redrawing it all along. She'd been ready for twenty years.

* * *

Sparrow found *them*, which was how Theo would tell it later, though the truth was messier.

The truth was that Theo caught the kid trying to steal her outboard's spare propeller off the dock at Ceres, at one in the morning, three days before launch. Theo was sitting in the dark doing debt arithmetic in her head — a habit now, a tic, numbers scrolling behind her eyes like ticker tape — when she heard the soft scrape of small sneakers on wet planking.

She didn't turn on her lamp. She waited until the kid had the propeller half off its mount, then said, "Left-hand thread. You'll strip it going that direction."

The kid froze, then bolted — straight into Theo's outstretched boot, tripped, and landed in a tangle of elbows and stolen rope coils. Up close in the lamplight now: sixteen, maybe seventeen, all sharp angles and hollow cheeks, hair chopped short with what looked like fishing line scissors, eyes that did the arithmetic of exits fast and came up empty.

"Please," the kid said, which wasn't an apology or a bargain, just the word itself, worn smooth from use.

"What's your name?"

A shrug. "Sparrow."

"That's not a name, that's a bird."

"It's the only one I've got." The kid sat up, hugging the propeller like a teddy bear, defiant and terrified in equal measure. "You're Theo Blackwood. Everybody on the dock knows your boat. They say you're running the Kestrel headwaters illegal. They say you're crazy."

"They say a lot of things."

"They say you don't come back from it." Sparrow's chin came up. "So I figured, boat that doesn't come back, crew that doesn't come back — nobody's going to look for anybody. Best place on the coast to disappear."

Theo should have thrown the kid off the dock. Should have called harbor security, should have done the sensible thing, the thing with clean edges. Instead she heard herself ask, "Disappear from what?"

And Sparrow's face did something complicated — grief and fear fighting for the wheel — and the kid said, "From people who hold things over you," and Theo understood more than the kid intended her to, and less, and exactly enough.

"Here's the deal," Theo said. "You give back the propeller, and you crew for me. Deckhand. Lowest rank, worst jobs, no pay until we're back. You work, you learn, you keep your mouth shut about what you see. In exchange, when this run's over, you're a person nobody can hold anything over anymore. Deal?"

Sparrow stared at her. "Why would you take a stranger?"

"Because every person on this boat is a stranger," Theo said, surprising herself with the honesty. "That's what makes it a crew."

* * *

Big Marisol required no recruiting at all. Marisol recruited herself.

Theo came back to the boat at dawn on launch-minus-two and found the galley — her galley, the tiny iron shrine she'd maintained alone for a decade — occupied by a woman five foot eleven and built like a lighthouse, stirring something in Theo's only pot that smelled so good it felt like a personal attack.

"Morning," Marisol boomed, cheerful as a foghorn. "Your coffee's burnt. Was burnt. Fixed it. Sit."

"This is my boat."

"It is. Terrible galley discipline, though. Salt next to the flour, knives loose in a drawer, and you've been eating canned chili for — I checked the labels — a shameful length of time." Marisol pointed the spoon at her. "Marisol Ferreira. Cooked twelve years on the coastal freighters, four on a research station up north until the funding died, and the last two years cooking for men who pay me late and call me 'Big Mar' like it's affection. I heard you're running a job nobody comes back from, and I thought: those boys eat like animals and thank me like furniture. I'd rather cook for people who might actually notice if I'm gone."

"So you broke onto my boat."

"I let myself onto your boat. There's a difference, and it's legal-ish." Marisol handed her a bowl. "Eat first. Argue after."

Theo ate. It was the best thing she'd tasted in years, and she hated how much that showed on her face, and Marisol saw it and grinned like she'd won a bet.

"Hired," Theo muttered.

"Obviously," said Marisol.

* * *

They gathered on the deck of the *Provision* the evening before launch, five strangers pretending to be a crew, and Theo stood at the rail and watched the fragile thing try to assemble itself.

Jun was below, elbow-deep in the engine bay, cursing with scholarly precision at a fuel line the previous owner had rigged with optimism instead of skill. Every few minutes he surfaced to bark a question at Sparrow, who'd appointed herself his gofer within an hour of boarding and carried tools with the solemnity of an acolyte. Odile sat in the pilothouse with her charts spread out, saying nothing, watching everything, correcting Jun's terminology twice and accepting his grudging nod both times. Marisol fed them all from the cockpit in shifts, and the food did what food does — loosened shoulders, opened mouths, started sentences that weren't about work.

"So," Marisol said, ladling stew into a fifth bowl and handing it to Sparrow, who took it with both hands like communion. "Since we're all going to die together, apparently, we should know each other's sins. I'll start. I punched a ship's master in Valparaiso. He deserved it. Your turn."

"We're not doing this," Theo said.

"Engineer," Marisol continued serenely. "Go."

Jun climbed up from the engine bay, wiped his hands, and considered. "I built engines for a smuggling outfit for three years because they paid for my mother's care," he said. "Best engines on the coast. Worst reason. Done." He looked at Theo. "Captain's turn."

Theo felt the circle close around her, warm and terrifying. Five bowls of stew. Five sets of eyes. She thought about lying, and found she didn't have the energy for it, and found also — this was the strange part, the part that scared her — that she didn't want to.

"I owe the Vane Syndicate more money than this boat is worth," she said. "If this run fails, they take everything, including probably my hands. And twenty years ago I was on an expedition up the same river we're sailing tomorrow, and two people died, and the man who took the blame for it wasn't the one who should have." Her voice stayed level. Mostly. "That's my sin. That's the cargo."

Silence held for a moment — not the awkward kind, the load-bearing kind, the kind that holds weight.

"Navigator?" Marisol prompted gently.

Odile didn't look up from her charts. "In 2004 I plotted the line through the gorge," she said. "I have spent twenty years deciding whether it was a bad line or bad luck. Tomorrow I find out." She rolled the chart closed with a snap. "Deckhand."

Sparrow hugged the stew bowl tighter. "I can't say mine," the kid said. "Yet. But I'll say it when I can. I swear."

"Good enough," said Marisol, and refilled the bowl, and that was that. No absolution asked, none granted. Just five strangers holding their sins out in the lamplight, checking each other's for cracks, and finding — cautiously, provisionally, the way you test river ice with your weight — that they might hold.

Later, near midnight, Theo stood alone at the rail while the others slept. Downriver, the last legal marker buoy blinked its slow red blink, patient as a border guard. Beyond it the Kestrel ran black and silent toward country that had taken Aldous Marchetti's crew and her mentor's good name and her own ability to sleep through the night.

She thought about what she'd told Sparrow on the dock. *Every person on this boat is a stranger. That's what makes it a crew.* It had sounded wise at the time. Now it sounded like a bet she'd placed with other people's lives, and the odds were terrible, and she'd placed it anyway, because the alternative was placing it with the Syndicate, and she'd seen how that bookmaker paid out.

From the pilothouse, a voice: Odile, awake after all, watching the buoy light with her pale navigator's eyes.

"There's something you should see before dawn," Odile said. "On the 2004 chart. An annotation I found tonight, in Aldous's hand. It changes what I told you at the table."

Theo turned from the rail. "What kind of annotation?"

Odile held up the rolled chart, and her face in the dark was unreadable, and the red buoy blinked, and blinked, and blinked.
