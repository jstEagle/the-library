## 5. The Sworn-Off Water

Jun Park found the page at four in the morning, wedged behind the ballast plate of the *Kestrel's Daughter*, where no one keeps anything by accident.

He hadn't been looking for it. He'd been looking for the torque wrench, which had a way of migrating to whatever dark corner of the hull was least convenient, and he'd gone in shoulder-deep with a headlamp clamped between his teeth like a pirate's knife. The bilge smelled of diesel and old rain and the particular mineral cold that boats carry in their bones the way old men carry shrapnel. His fingers walked along the stringer, found the wrench — and then found paper.

It was folded into eighths, oil-stained soft as cloth, and it had been back there long enough that the creases had begun to fuse. Jun knew what he was holding before he opened it. He'd spent eleven years as an engineer on working vessels, and engineers learn to read a boat the way priests read faces, and this boat had been keeping a secret the way a boat keeps secrets: quietly, structurally, somewhere no one would ever look unless they were already half inside the machinery of her grief.

He should have put it back. He told himself that later, standing in the grey pre-dawn of the Kestrel dock with the page open in his hands, that a decent man would have folded it into eighths again and slid it home behind the ballast and said nothing forever. But his hands were already opening it, because Jun Park had never once in his life been able to leave a mechanism unexamined, and Theo Blackwood was a mechanism, and everyone aboard knew she had a part missing.

The handwriting was younger than Theo. That was the first thing. The letters were rounder, more generous, written by someone who still believed the page might be read by friendly eyes. The ink had feathered where water had touched it once, long ago, and dried.

*Day 9 above the Narrows,* it began. *R. says the barometer is lying but R. always says that. Ice coming down the valley faster than the charts say it should. M. is scared. I told him the river doesn't care if you're scared, only if you're ready, and he laughed and said that's the same thing said mean.*

Jun stood very still. Somewhere down the dock, a mooring line creaked against its cleat, patient as a metronome.

*We lost the portage cache to the slide. R. wants to push through the gorge rather than turn back. Two days saved. I voted with him. God help me, I voted with him.*

The last lines were different. The handwriting had broken its rhythm, crowded toward the right margin as though the writer were trying to outrun the sentence.

*Two dead. M. and Osei. The gorge took them and the ice took the wreck of the raft and I froze. I froze and R. went in after them because I could not make my arms work. He has not looked at me since we came off the water and I do not blame him. Whatever he tells the inquiry, let him tell it. It will be kinder than the truth.*

*The truth is that I am the reason they are dead.*

Below that, nothing. Just the fold lines, and one dark spot near the bottom edge that might have been oil or might have been something else, twenty years old now, pressed flat and kept in the dark like everything else the boat refused to say out loud.

Jun heard the footsteps on the dock planks before he saw her — he'd have known that stride anywhere by now, three days into loading, that long unhurried gait that covered ground without seeming to spend any effort on it. He had perhaps four seconds to decide what kind of crewmate he intended to be.

He turned around and held the page out.

Theo stopped six feet away. She had a coil of line over one shoulder and a thermos in her free hand and the sky behind her was just beginning to consider the idea of light, all iron and pearl at the eastern ridge. Her eyes went from Jun's face to the paper and something happened in them that Jun had no word for — not shock, exactly. More like the sound a house makes when a beam finally settles after decades.

She set down the line. She set down the thermos, carefully, squaring it with the edge of the dock box as if orderliness could hold the rest of her together.

"Where," she said.

"Behind the ballast. Portside." He didn't soften it. Softening would have been worse. "I wasn't looking for it."

"I know." She took the page from him. Her hands were steady, which surprised him; he'd expected them not to be. She looked at it for a long time, and the light came up another degree while she did, enough that Jun could see the river below the dock running fast and brown with snowmelt, hurrying somewhere with no interest in the people above it.

"You could have put it back," Theo said.

"Yes."

"But you're showing me instead."

"Yes."

She studied him then, really studied him, the way she studied an engine that was making a sound it shouldn't — not with suspicion, but with the focused patience of someone who needed to know what was wrong before she could decide whether to fix it or run it until it died. Jun let himself be examined. He was good at that too. Waiting was most of engineering.

"Odile would have read it and started making charts," Theo said at last. "Sparrow would have read it and asked forty questions. Marisol would have read it and fed me until I talked." A breath moved through her, long and controlled, the breath of someone who had practiced breathing through worse. "What are you going to do?"

"Nothing," Jun said. "You already did it. Twenty years ago. I'm just the guy who found the receipt."

Something cracked at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. Closer to the place a smile would go if it ever got permission.

"It's the only page left," she said. "I burned the journal the winter after. All of it. This one must have slipped down behind the plate when I was pulling pages out, and I—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "I couldn't bring myself to tear the boat apart looking. So I just let her keep it."

"Boats are good at that," Jun said.

"Yeah." Theo looked down at the page one more time. Then she walked to the stern, to the little cast-iron stove they used for warming the engine block on cold mornings, and Jun watched her kneel and open its door and feed the page into the coals of last night's fire.

It caught slowly. Twenty years of oil made it burn bright and fast once it started, the ink flaring green-white, the words curling into themselves. *I am the reason they are dead* glowed for a moment, legible, and then it wasn't, and then there was nothing but ash lifting on the draft from the river.

Theo stayed kneeling there until the last fleck went grey. When she stood, she didn't look at Jun, and her voice came out level and stripped, like lumber with the bark planed off.

"The man who wrote that page thought the truth was simple. That there was one person to blame and it was her." She closed the stove door. "It took me fifteen years to understand that R. voted too. That the whole crew voted. That the river doesn't take a vote, it just takes." She picked up her line coil and settled it back onto her shoulder. "Imogen knows about 2004. She knows this water is sworn off. And she asked anyway."

"Are you angry about that?"

"I don't know yet," Theo said honestly. "Ask me when we're past the marker buoy."

She walked aft toward the bow line, and Jun stood alone by the stove with the wrench in his hand and the smell of burnt paper in his nose, and understood that he had just been given something — not trust exactly, not yet, but the raw material of it, smelted hot and handed over to see what he'd build.

* * *

They launched at dawn, because Theo wanted the light behind them and the current with them, and because dawn launches meant nobody on the dock to watch them do what they were about to do.

The *Kestrel's Daughter* sat low in the water with fuel and gear and food for nine weeks, riding heavy and sure, and the crew moved around her in the blue half-light like a body's systems waking in sequence. Odile came first, as she always did, silent as fog, and spread her charts across the cabin table without a word, weighting the corners with brass fittings. Then Sparrow, at a dead run from wherever Sparrow slept, hair wild, hauling a duffel nearly her own size and pretending it weighed nothing. Then Big Marisol, who came down the dock with two crates of provisions balanced on her shoulders and a third carried in the crook of one arm like a loaf of bread, singing something low and tuneless in a language none of the others shared.

Jun ran the final checks. Fuel lines, bilge pumps, the transom seal, the radio — he tested the radio twice, and hated how much he hoped it wouldn't matter. The engine turned over on the second crank and settled into that deep even idle he'd spent three days coaxing out of it, and he laid his palm flat on the housing for a moment, the way you'd steady a horse.

"Talk to me," he murmured to it. "Don't lie to me out there."

"She talks to engines," Sparrow said, dropping into the cockpit beside him. "Have you noticed? Theo talks to the boat too. You two are going to be insufferable."

"Sparrow."

"Yeah?"

"When we go past the buoy today. Do you know what that means?"

Sparrow was seventeen and fearless and had grown up on water Jun himself would have called suicide, so he'd expected bravado, a shrug, some joke about paperwork. Instead she was quiet for a moment, watching the grey water slide under the hull.

"It means we're not legal anymore," she said. "Past the last marker, there's no license that covers us, no insurance, no rescue contract. If we get in trouble up there, nobody's coming." She picked at a fraying thread on her sleeve. "Marisol told me last night. She said everybody gets to choose their own way to hear it, but I should hear it."

"And how do you hear it?"

Sparrow considered this with the seriousness she reserved for questions that mattered, chin tucked, eyes narrowed at the middle distance.

"I hear it like: finally," she said.

Jun laughed despite himself, and the sound carried across the water, and Marisol echoed it from the galley hatch, and just like that the fear in the air got folded into something else, something they could carry.

Theo came down the dock last. She wore her oldest jacket and her newest expression, which was to say her face gave away nothing at all, and Imogen Reyes was waiting at the bow cleat with the mooring line coiled to throw.

They didn't speak. Jun watched them look at each other across the gunwale — twenty years of miles between two people standing eight feet apart — and he watched Theo nod, once, small, and Imogen return it, and that was the whole ceremony. Some lines you cross with trumpets. Some you cross with a nod and a thrown line.

The engine came up. The dock fell away. The town shrank behind them into a scatter of roofs and smoke, and then the river bend took even that, and there was nothing ahead but water and the rising light.

* * *

The last legal marker buoy stood a mile below the mouth of the Narrows, and it was uglier than Jun remembered from the charts — a rusted steel drum on a chain, painted the faded orange of institutional warning, stenciled with a number that corresponded to some regulation nobody aboard had any intention of consulting. Someone had hung a defunct wind sock from its mast that snapped and fluttered in the morning breeze like a small desperate flag.

Theo brought the boat alongside it and killed the engine.

The silence was enormous. River noise filled it — the chuck and mutter of current against the hull, the far-off hiss of the Narrows' first riffles reading like applause from an audience you couldn't see. Everyone came up on deck without being called. Odile stood at the chart table with her hands flat on the paper. Marisol leaned in the galley doorway wiping her hands on her apron. Sparrow perched on the rail like a figurehead. Imogen stood at the bow, arms crossed, saying nothing, offering nothing, letting this belong to whoever it belonged to.

Theo stepped to the drum buoy itself, one boot on the gunwale, and rested her hand on the rusted steel.

"My mentor swore off this water," she said, and her voice carried flat and clear across the stillness. "Twenty years ago, on this stretch of river, we lost two people, and he carried it, and I let him carry it, and when he died he was still carrying it." Her thumb traced the stencil number. "Swearing off the water was supposed to be penance. It wasn't. It was just distance wearing a costume."

Nobody breathed.

"So here's what I need," Theo said. She turned and looked at each of them in turn, and Jun felt the look land on him like a hand on the shoulder. "Not agreement. Agreement you can give me and take back later. I need to know that every person on this boat is choosing this water with their eyes open. Because past this buoy, we're not a licensed expedition anymore. We're just people on a boat, helping each other stay alive. That's the whole contract. If anyone wants off, I'll put you ashore at the ranger station below the bridge and think no less of you, and I'll say so to your face, today and every day after."

Silence. The wind sock snapped.

Then Marisol pushed off the doorframe, crossed the deck, and kissed Theo once on the top of the head, the way you'd bless a child, and went back to her galley without a word, and that was her answer.

Odile rolled up her chart, tapped it twice against her palm, and said, "I've been navigating toward this river for six years. I'm not getting off it now." Which, from Odile, was practically a speech.

Sparrow simply raised her hand like she was answering roll call, grinning, and then spoiled the solemnity entirely by adding, "Also I already signed something in the logbook when you weren't looking, so legally you're stuck with me."

That left Jun. He felt the whole boat's attention come to rest on him, gentle and expectant, and he found that the thing in his chest wasn't fear. It was the feeling he got when an engine he'd rebuilt fired on the first crank — that stunned, grateful disbelief that the parts had all held.

"In 2004," Jun said, "a crew voted to push through a gorge to save two days, and the river took its toll, and one person spent twenty years believing she alone was to blame." He held Theo's eyes. "I found the receipt for that this morning. I watched you burn it. And I want to say, for the record, in front of witnesses: the vote was wrong, but the blame was never single. Rivers don't take votes. They just take." He shrugged. "So I'm voting now. Properly. Out loud. Past the buoy."

For a moment Theo Blackwood — storm chaser, debtor, legend of the Narrows — looked like she might not be able to speak at all. Then she nodded, the same small nod she'd given Imogen at the dock, and turned to the helm.

"Everyone hold on to something," she said, and her voice was steady and new-minted, a voice with no page behind it. "Imogen — cast us off."

The engine woke. The current took the hull. They slid past the rusted drum buoy close enough that Sparrow reached out and slapped it as they went by, a bright ringing clang that rolled away downstream, and Jun watched the orange shape fall astern and grow smaller and smaller until the river bent and took it, the way the river takes everything, and did not give it back.

Ahead, the walls of the Narrows rose up to meet the morning, and the water between them went white.

And far upstream, faint as a memory, came a sound none of them could name yet — a low rolling report, like thunder arriving out of season, from a direction where there were no clouds.
