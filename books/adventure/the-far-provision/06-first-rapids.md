## 6. First Rapids

Sparrow had lied about her age on the crew manifest, and she was beginning to suspect everyone knew.

It wasn't that anyone had said anything. It was the way Odile had looked at her when she'd scrawled *nineteen* in the signature line — a long, level look over the top of her reading glasses, the kind of look that weighed and measured and filed things away for later. It was the way Big Marisol had put an extra helping of rice on her plate that first night without comment, the way you feed something small that might not make it through winter. And it was definitely the way Jun Park had handed her a pair of work gloves before they'd even cast off, saying only, "Rope burns are worse than people tell you," which was either kindness or a warning or both.

Nobody had called her out. That was the strange part. On the streets of Corvane's lower harbor district, where Sparrow had spent the last two years since her brother Tomas disappeared into Vane custody, being caught in a lie meant teeth. Here it just meant gloves and extra rice, like the lie was a thing they could all see and had all agreed to carry together until she grew into it.

She wasn't nineteen. She was sixteen, and three months, and eleven days — she kept the count the way other people kept prayer — and she had never been on moving water before in her life.

Now the water was moving very fast indeed.

* * *

The Narrows announced themselves an hour before they arrived, though Sparrow didn't know enough to read the announcement. She was up on the foredeck of the *Kestrel's Mercy* — a name that felt increasingly ironic — pretending to coil line while actually watching Theo Blackwood at the helm.

She'd been watching Theo for three days straight. Not because she meant to. Because there was nothing else to watch, and because Theo Blackwood moved through the world like a person who had already decided how every next ten seconds would go, and Sparrow had never seen anyone do that. Marisol hummed while she cooked and argued with the stove. Jun talked to the engine in a low, apologetic murmur, like a man coaxing a stubborn animal. Odile read the river with her lips moving, silent arithmetic. But Theo stood at the wheel with her weight settled into her hips and her eyes doing this slow, patient sweep — bank, water, sky, bank again — and Sparrow had the growing, unsettling conviction that Theo saw everything twice: once as it was, and once as it was about to be.

"Greenhorn." Odile's voice came from behind her, dry as driftwood. "If you're going to pretend to work, at least face the rope."

Sparrow's ears went hot. She turned back to the coil, fumbled it, started over.

"I did it wrong too," Odile said, coming to stand beside her at the rail. "My first season. Coiled against the lay. Every length I touched turned into a bird's nest by morning. Captain Blackwood's mentor — Halvard, his name was — he made me redo the entire deck inventory four times before he told me what I was doing wrong." She paused. "He never raised his voice once. That was the worst part. You wanted him to yell so you'd have something to push against."

"What happened to him?" Sparrow asked, and then wished she hadn't, because the whole boat went quiet around that name the way a room goes quiet around a grave.

Odile looked out at the water. The river here ran wide and brown-green, braided around gravel bars, deceptively lazy. "You'll hear it eventually," she said. "Everyone does. Ask me again after the Narrows."

"Why after?"

"Because after the Narrows," Odile said, folding her glasses away, "you'll understand why some questions keep."

* * *

They stopped for lunch on a shingle bar below a bluff striped red and black like burnt bacon, and that was where Theo finally spoke to her directly for more than two words at a time.

"You've got good hands," Theo said.

Sparrow nearly dropped her tin plate. "What?"

"Your knots. You tie them left-handed under pressure. Most people freeze and go right-handed even if it's their weak side." Theo crouched by the cold fire ring, feeding dry grass to a spark, not looking at her. "Left-handed under pressure means your body trusts itself even when your head doesn't. That's rarer than talent. You can't teach it."

"Oh," said Sparrow, who had learned to tie one-handed and upside down in the dark because on the harbor docks you sometimes needed both hands free for other things, and who was absolutely not going to say any of that.

Theo glanced up, and for a second something passed across her face — recognition, maybe, of the shape of a silence, the particular way a kid goes still when a question gets too close to the truth. She didn't ask. She just said, "After lunch, you're on bow watch with Odile. When we hit the Narrows, I need eyes forward calling water. Can you call what you see?"

"Call it how?"

"Honest and loud. Those are the only rules." Theo struck a match and the grass caught, smoking. "The river doesn't care what you don't know. It only cares whether you say so."

* * *

The Narrows began the way disasters begin, which is to say quietly.

The banks drew in first — walls of grey granite rising on either side, close enough that the wind died and the air went cool and mineral-smelling. The water changed color from braid-brown to a deep, glassy green, and it smoothed out, flattened, went still as poured metal. Sparrow stood at the bow beside Odile and felt her stomach tighten around nothing.

"That's the tongue," Odile said quietly, pointing ahead to where the current gathered itself into a single smooth lane between two dark seams of rock. "See how it pulls toward the right wall? Everything wants to go there. We don't."

"Why not?"

"Because everything that ever went there is still there." Odile raised her voice. "Captain. Right seam's eating the tongue. We'll want hard left off the entry."

"Hard left off the entry," Theo repeated from the stern, calm as a woman ordering coffee. "Jun?"

Jun's voice floated up from the engine hatch, strained but steady. "She'll give me everything she's got. Don't ask for miracles in the middle section."

"No miracles," Theo agreed. "Just water."

And then the walls closed in, and the river dropped, and the world came apart.

* * *

Later — much later, sitting wrapped in a blanket with Marisol's terrible sweet tea cooling in her hands — Sparrow would try to explain it to herself, and fail, and try again. What she remembered was not fear exactly. Fear was familiar; fear was Tuesday. What she remembered was sound.

The Narrows made a sound like the inside of a bell. The whole gorge rang with it, water hammering stone, a bass note she felt in her back teeth and her ribs and the soles of her boots. Spray hung in the air in sheets, catching the afternoon light, and for one absurd moment at the top of the first drop Sparrow thought: *this is the most beautiful thing I have ever seen,* and in the very same moment thought: *we are all going to die.*

The bow plunged. Green water came over the rail and hit her like a fist full of ice. She heard herself scream, heard Odile shouting numbers beside her — "Twelve feet! Hole on the left! RIGHT OF THE TONGUE!" — and heard her own voice crack out over the roar, high and ragged: "ROCK! ROCK DEAD AHEAD!"

"Seen!" Theo's voice, from behind, unhurried, impossible. The hull slewed. The rock she'd called slid past close enough that Sparrow could have touched it, its wet black shoulder streaming foam, and then the river picked the boat up and threw them down the throat of the drop.

Everything after that happened in pieces:

Jun's engine screaming a note no engine should scream, and Jun screaming back at it, wordless, a duet.

Marisol in the cockpit doorway with one arm locked around the frame and the other arm locked around a lashed-down crate, her big calm face gone white, her mouth moving in what was unmistakably prayer.

The boat going sideways — sideways was death, Sparrow knew that much instinctively, sideways was how boats became wreckage — and then not sideways, because Theo had done something with the wheel and the throttle together, some impossible counterintuitive thing, using the hole in the water itself to swing the stern true, riding the disaster like a stair step down.

Odile's hand closing on the back of Sparrow's collar, not pulling her down, just holding on. *I have you,* the hand said. Nobody had said that to Sparrow in two years.

And through all of it, Theo's voice, threading the chaos like thread through a needle's eye: "Bow — talk to me—"

"CLEAN LEFT! HOLE RIGHT! WAVE TRAIN — BIG ONE — THREE — NO, FOUR—"

"Four. Good girl. Jun, give me half—"

Half. Half of everything the engine had, in that water, at that angle, and the *Kestrel's Mercy* climbed the fourth wave like a living thing, hung at the top with its propeller singing in open air, and slammed down into the trough beyond, and the gorge opened its hands, and let them go.

* * *

The lower canyon widened. The water gentled by degrees, still fast, still talking to itself, but no longer screaming. Sparrow realized her hands were locked on the bow rail and made them let go, finger by finger. Her whole body was shaking. Her voice was wrecked raw. She was grinning so hard her face hurt.

She turned around.

Theo stood at the wheel, soaked to the skin, grey streaked through her black hair where spray had frosted it, and she was looking at Sparrow with an expression Sparrow couldn't read at first — and then could. It was the way Marisol looked at bread rising. It was the way Jun looked at a rebuilt carburetor turning over on the first pull.

"Four waves," Theo said. "You counted them in order, under load, first time on whitewater." She shook her head slowly. "Twenty years I've run this river and I've thrown grown men off this boat for less."

"She screamed the whole way," Odile observed mildly, settling onto the cargo net.

"So did I, my first run through here." Theo wiped spray off her face with the heel of her hand. "Halvard said the ones who scream are the ones paying attention. The quiet ones are the dangerous ones — they're somewhere else." She looked at Sparrow again, and something in her face shifted, softened by a degree, the way ice softens at the exact temperature where it starts to move. "You were here the whole time. Both feet on the deck. Thank you."

*Thank you.* Sparrow held the words carefully, the way you hold something hot. On the docks, thanks were currency and always counterfeit. This didn't feel counterfeit. This felt like a door standing open in a wall she'd assumed was solid all the way through.

"I lied on the manifest," she blurted.

The boat went quiet. Even the engine seemed to lean in.

"I'm not nineteen. I'm sixteen." Her heart was slamming. "I can work. I'm strong for my size and I learn fast and I won't be trouble, I swear it, I just—" She ran out of air.

Theo studied her for a long moment. Then she looked at Odile, who gave a small shrug that managed to convey *I told you so* and *it doesn't matter* simultaneously. She looked aft, where Marisol had appeared in the doorway wearing the expression of a woman who had known since the first plate of rice. Jun's head popped up out of the engine hatch, grease-streaked, curious.

"We know," Theo said.

Sparrow blinked. "You—"

"Sixteen, maybe seventeen. Odile had it at sixteen. Marisol said fifteen and lost the pool to Jun." Theo's mouth twitched, the ghost of a smile. "You count days, kid. You've got a birthday coming. We figured we'd let you get there before we said anything."

Sparrow stood there with the river roaring gently behind her and something enormous happening in her chest, something with no name she owned, and the only thing she could manage was: "There's a *pool?*"

"There's a pool," Jun confirmed cheerfully. "I'm winning."

"You're not winning," said Marisol. "You guessed seventeen. Nobody's seventeen. Look at her."

And Sparrow laughed — a real laugh, cracked and wet and surprised out of her — and the sound went down the canyon and came back changed, and nobody on the boat laughed at her for it. They laughed with her. There was a difference. She had two years of evidence on the difference, and this was the first time in all of it that she'd landed on the right side.

* * *

They camped that night on a bench of flat ground above a long riffle, under cedars old enough to remember the 2004 expedition, though nobody said so. Marisol built the fire up big and made a stew that smelled like someone's grandmother's kitchen, and Sparrow ate two bowls and pretended not to cry into the third, and Marisol pretended not to notice, and both pretenses were acts of mercy.

Later, when the others had turned in, Sparrow found Theo at the edge of the camp, standing alone looking upstream at the black seam of the gorge they'd come through. The stars over the canyon rim were brutal and close.

"Captain?" Sparrow said.

"Theo's fine."

"Theo." The name felt stolen, borrowed clothing. "Why'd you let me stay aboard? Really. If you knew I lied from the start."

Theo didn't answer right away. When she did, she kept her eyes on the water.

"When I was younger than you, I froze," she said. "In water a lot like that. People got hurt because I froze, and a man took the blame for it so I wouldn't have to carry it, and he carried it until it killed him." Her voice stayed level, but Sparrow heard the effort holding it level, the way you hear the strain in a rope before it parts. "So when I see somebody stand on the deck of a boat in the worst water on this river and call what they see — honest and loud, both feet down — I pay attention. That's not a skill you hire. That's a person you keep."

She turned then, and in the firelight her eyes were tired and old and unexpectedly kind.

"Get some sleep, Sparrow. Tomorrow we pass the last legal marker buoy, and after that, everything's different."

Sparrow lay awake a long time after that, listening to the river talk in its sleep, running the day back through her memory wave by wave. Somewhere in the dark she heard Odile murmuring numbers to herself, and Jun's wrench ticking against the engine housing, and Marisol's snore like distant friendly thunder, and she understood, with a clarity that frightened her more than the Narrows had, that she would lie for these people, steal for them, bleed for them — that she already would have, and hadn't even noticed the moment it happened.

Awe, she decided drowsily, was just loyalty that hadn't found its footing yet.

She was almost asleep when she heard it: far downstream, faint under the water-sound, the low diesel thrum of an engine that wasn't theirs, moving against the current in the night.

She told herself it was a fisherman.

She told herself that twice.
