## 7. What the River Remembers

Odile had a theory about rivers: that they were the world's most honest archives. Paper could burn, ledgers could be cooked, men could lie with their whole faces — but a river kept everything, and it kept it in order. Every flood line, every shifted boulder, every drowned tree was an entry written in water's own hand, dated and cross-referenced by the current itself. You just had to know how to read.

She'd learned to read from her grandmother, who had navigated the coastal freight routes of two archipelagos without a single chart, because charts got you killed when they were wrong and nobody told you. *The paper is a rumor,* her grandmother used to say, tapping Odile's sternum. *This is the map.* It was a good philosophy for a woman like Odile, whose own paper history — passports under three names, a maritime license that technically belonged to someone named Odile Fontaine who had died in a boating accident off Marseilles — was mostly rumor too.

So she stood at the bow of the *Provision* with her feet bare on the wet deck plating and her eyes doing the slow, patient work they did best, while the Kestrel narrowed ahead of them into a corridor of granite and grey-green water.

Three days past the last legal marker buoy, and the river had changed its personality entirely.

Downstream it had been broad and lazy, all braided channels and gravel bars where herons stalked the shallows like patient creditors. Up here it had opinions. The walls closed in. The water went from brown to a cold, mineral green that Odile distrusted on principle, because green meant depth and depth meant the river could hide whatever it wanted. The spray carried a chill straight off some glacier she couldn't see yet but could feel, the way you felt a draft from a door left open in a house that should be empty.

Behind her, Jun had the engine idling at half-throttle, keeping their speed matched to the current so the boat sat nearly still — his trick, one of many, letting her read without fighting the helm for information. Sparrow was coiled on the middeck cargo net pretending not to listen, which was Sparrow's way of listening very hard. Theo stood at the wheel with her forearms resting on the spokes, wearing the particular stillness she wore whenever the geography started looking familiar.

And familiar was exactly what it looked like. That was the problem.

"There," Odile said quietly, and lifted two fingers toward the right bank.

A quarter mile upstream, where the canyon wall stepped back into a shelf of scree, something pale broke the vegetation. At this distance it might have been driftwood snagged high by a flood. But driftwood didn't sit square to the current. Driftwood didn't have corners.

Jun brought them closer along the eddy line, engine barely breathing, and the thing resolved itself out of the willows: a wooden survey post, silvered with twenty years of weather, bolted at the top to a plate of corroded aluminum. Stamped into the metal, half-eaten by lichen but still legible, was a number and a letter.

**K-14-R.**

Nobody spoke for a moment. The river made its cold commentary against the hull.

"Kestrel fourteen," Theo said finally. Her voice came out flat, sanded down. "Right bank." She said it the way you'd recite something you'd memorized so long ago it had stopped being information and become part of your skeleton.

"You know these?" Sparrow asked, trying for casual and landing somewhere nearer to careful.

"The 2004 expedition marked the whole route," Odile answered for her, because it was easier coming from her and she knew it. "Survey posts every kilometer or so, wherever the channel changed. Your captain here ran this water before you were born, little bird."

"I wasn't— I'm sixteen, not five," Sparrow muttered, but she was already leaning over the rail, squinting at the post like it might confess something.

Odile let her look. What Odile herself was looking at required no youth, only attention — and attention was the one instrument she'd never broken, never pawned, never left behind in a hotel room in a country she'd fled.

The post was old. The lichen was old. The rope scar around its base was *not*.

Someone had tied off to this marker recently — within weeks, maybe days. The bark beneath the rope groove was raw, pale wood exposed in a clean crescent, and the fibers hadn't yet greyed or fuzzed with damp. Fresh cuts didn't stay bright long up here; the moisture saw to that inside a month. This cut still had edges.

"Jun," she said. "Hold us here."

She took the boat hook and eased them alongside until she could reach the post. Up close the story got worse. There were actually two rope scars, one above the other — a tie-off and then a second, higher, as if whoever'd moored here had come back and re-rigged. And below the second scar, carved into the wood with a knife in four quick strokes, was a mark: a vertical slash crossed by a short horizontal bar near its top.

Not a random mark. A tally? A claim?

No. She turned it over in her head the way she turned everything, slowly, feeling all sides. It was a direction. A stylized arrow, weathered logic be damned — pointing upstream and slightly toward the far bank. Toward the Narrows' upper entrance.

"Someone's been through," she said. "Recently. More than once."

Theo came forward to the bow, and Odile watched her read the wood the way Odile read water — fast, total, grim. "How recent?"

"Fresh enough that the sapwood hasn't dulled. Weeks. Maybe less." Odile touched the lower scar with one fingertip, testing the fibers. "Two visits. They moored low first, then re-tied higher — water dropped since, or they wanted a faster getaway."

"Getaway from what?"

"From being caught," Odile said. "Or from being followed."

The word landed among them like a stone in still water. Odile felt the ripples go through the crew without anyone moving much. Sparrow stopped leaning. Jun's hand drifted to the throttle, not changing anything, just owning it.

Theo crouched at the bow rail, forearms on the metal, studying the arrow. Twenty years fell away from her face for a moment — not softening her, aging her backward into some harder younger version Odile had heard about in dockside bars and never quite believed. Then she stood.

"Log it," she said. "Position, heading of the mark, condition. Everything."

"Already done," Odile said, and tapped her temple. "But I'll write it down too. Paper has its uses when the head is what gets lost overboard."

Something that was almost a smile crossed Theo's face and didn't survive the crossing.

They pushed on.

* * *

By late afternoon the canyon widened into a long lake-like reach where the current nearly vanished and the mountains stood doubled in the water, upside-down and perfect. It should have been beautiful. It was beautiful. Odile distrusted it anyway, because flat water in a canyon meant one thing: the river was holding its breath.

It also meant the old expedition's markers would be easiest to find here, and she wanted more data points. Two rope scars made a coincidence. Five made a pattern. Patterns were how you survived; coincidences were how you died smiling.

They found K-15-L on the left bank an hour later, tilted, its plate green with age — untouched, unscarred, honest in its decay. Good. Then K-16-R, standing in six inches of water on a submerged gravel spit, and Odile went cold all over despite the sun.

Because K-16-R had been *moved*.

Not fallen — moved. The post stood upright, freshly seated in a new hole of disturbed stones, and the mud ring around its base hadn't cured. Someone had pulled the old marker and re-set it ten meters downstream of where the erosion scars on the bank said the original hole had been. Ten meters downstream, on a gravel bar that would put a boat drawing two feet onto the shallows if you trusted the marker's implied channel line.

Or — she walked the geometry through again in her mind, hating each step because each step made sense — it would steer a boat toward the *deep* channel on the far side. Which was deep. And narrow. And ran hard against a wall with a reversal current at its base that she could see from here, a smooth glassy tongue of water folding back on itself like a lie rehearsed until it sounded true.

"Captain." Her voice stayed level. It always stayed level; it was the one performance she never dropped. "Come look at this, and bring your distrust."

Theo came. So did Jun, and Sparrow, drawn by the tone if not the words. Even Marisol emerged from the galley hatch wiping her hands, because Marisol had a sailor's superstition about Odile's voice going quiet — she called it *the Frenchwoman's frost*, and claimed it preceded every disaster worth remembering.

Odile showed them the moved post. Showed them the fresh mud. Showed them, with a pointed finger, the false channel it advertised, and the reversal lying in wait at the far wall.

"Somebody re-rigged the river," Jun said slowly. He crouched, pressed his palm flat to the gravel, reading the disturbance the way he read engines — by touch, by sympathy with machinery and motion. "This isn't old work. Days. Maybe yesterday, if the rain held off."

"Why?" Sparrow asked. "Who moves a stick?"

"Someone who wants to sink a boat without leaving fingerprints," Odile said. "A rock garden across a channel is obvious. A moved survey marker looks like age and error. If we'd come through here at speed, trusting K-16—" She drew the line in the air with one finger: in fast, over the shallows' false promise, swung wide by the marker's authority, into the reversal, pinned against the wall, rolled. "We would have gone into that eddy fold stern-first, and this river would have kept whatever it took."

Silence, except the water, which continued its perfect mirror impression of innocence.

"They knew we'd use the markers," Theo said. Her voice had gone very quiet, which those who knew her understood to be its loudest setting. "They knew we'd have to. Nobody runs this stretch on memory anymore."

"They knew *we'd* come," Odile corrected gently. "Which is worse. That arrow at K-14 pointed this way, Captain. That wasn't navigation. That was an invitation."

Marisol crossed herself with a flour-dusted hand, which explained the flour, which raised other questions Odile filed away for later.

"So what do we do?" Sparrow looked from face to face, and Odile watched her do the arithmetic adolescents do when they discover the adults are frightened too: adding fear to fear and getting anger, because anger at least pays interest. "Turn back?"

"We don't turn back," Theo said.

"We don't trust the markers either," Odile said. "Both things are true. We run this reach on my eyes and Jun's soundings and nothing else. From here on, every marker is a suspect. Every cache is a crime scene. We assume we're behind someone with a head start, a plan, and no love for us."

"And ahead of someone else," Jun added quietly.

Everyone looked at him.

"Think about it," he said. "The people who rigged this marker wanted a boat to die here. Whose boat? Not ours specifically — they couldn't have known our draft, our timing. This was set for *the salvage run*. Any salvage run. Imogen's outfit talked about this route in three bars before we ever loaded a crate." His jaw tightened. "Somebody heard the same rumors we did. Somebody's racing us to the wreck, and they're willing to drown strangers to win."

The mountains stood in their inverted reflection and said nothing, which was the most eloquent testimony available.

* * *

They ran the reach slow, Odile calling depths from the bow while Jun feathered the throttle and Theo held the wheel like a living thing, and they passed the reversal current close enough to see the drowned branch turning in it, around and around, patient as a clock. A boat's worth of wreckage, waiting for company. Odile noted it in the log with a steady hand and an unsteady pulse, because that was the arrangement she'd had with her own body for thirty years: hands professional, heart freelance.

An hour before dusk, Sparrow spotted the cache.

It was tucked in a scree hollow above a natural landing beach — the kind of spot any expedition would use, sheltered, defensible, with a clear sightline both up and down the reach. A stack of plastic barrels under a tarp, camouflaged with netting, half-swallowed by the willows. Standard expedition practice. Standard *2004* expedition practice, Odile realized, because supply caches every third day's travel was exactly how Halvard Voss — Theo's mentor, the name that lived in this crew like a stone in a boot — had planned his routes. She'd read the man's published field notes twice. Once for the navigation. Once for the grief in them, which was considerable.

Theo went ashore with Jun covering from the boat, and Odile watched her friend — because that was what Theo had become somewhere between the last legal buoy and here, though neither of them had signed anything — walk up the beach with her shoulders set like a woman approaching a grave.

The cache had been stripped.

Not robbed — *stripped*. Methodical, unhurried. The tarps were folded, not torn. The barrels remained, neatly restacked, lids off, empty, arranged with a tidiness that was somehow more frightening than wreckage. Whoever had come through had taken the food, the fuel, the rope, and left the containers aligned like soldiers at a funeral. On the inside of one barrel lid, in fresh grease pencil, someone had written a single word:

**TURNBACK**

Theo stood over it a long time. The evening light came slantwise off the glacier fields above the canyon rim and lit her from the side, half gold, half shadow, and Odile thought: *there she is, both halves at once.*

"Could be a warning," Sparrow offered from the beach, arms wrapped around herself against the cold coming off the water. "Like — a decent murderer's warning?"

"People who drown strangers don't leave polite notes," Jun said.

"No," Odile agreed softly. "People who drown strangers leave moved markers. This—" she nodded at the grease pencil, at the neat barrels, at the whole staged courtesy of it "—this is someone who wants us gone *before* the markers get their chance. Two kinds of people on this river, it seems. Or one kind having an argument with itself."

"Or," Marisol rumbled, from the stern where she'd materialized with coffee that smelled like salvation and tasted like a threat, "someone who knows exactly whose daughter is on this boat, and remembers 2004, and would rather scare us off than bury us. Fear is cheaper than funerals. Believe me, I've priced both."

Theo's head came up sharply. "What do you know about it?"

"What everyone in the delta knows and says nothing to your face, *jefa*. The Voss expedition didn't fail. It was *failed*." Marisol handed her the cup. "Drink. Decide after."

Odile filed that away too — another entry in the archive, undated, pending — and looked back upriver, where the canyon bent west into the last of the light. Somewhere beyond that bend, the Narrows waited, and beyond the Narrows the portage, and beyond the portage the icefields, and somewhere among all of it, the wreck, the evidence, and at least one party of people who had gotten here first, moved the river's bones, and told them plainly, in grease pencil, to go home.

Her grandmother had a saying for nights like the one assembling itself overhead. *When the map starts lying,* she'd say, *stop navigating and start hunting.*

Odile took the log book, wrote the day's entries in her small exact hand — the scars, the arrow, the moved post, the stripped cache, the word TURNBACK, time, position, weather — and then, after a moment, added a private line at the bottom, small enough that only she would ever read it:

*They want us to leave. Therefore we are close to something worth killing for. Therefore we keep going.*

Below the reach, far behind them, so faint it might have been thunder deciding whether to commit, came a sound none of them could name yet — a deep, tectonic grumble rolling down the canyon, felt in the soles of the feet before it reached the ears.

Sparrow's head came up like a bird dog's. "Was that—"

"Engine," Jun said. "Big one. Diesel. Miles off."

"Or ice," Odile said, and marked the bearing in the margin of the log, because whichever it was, it was *behind* them, closing, and the river had already told her everything else.
