## 8. Bell's Shadow

The river taught you to read its moods the way old sailors read the sky, and by late afternoon on the third day it was telling Theo something she didn't want to hear.

The water had gone glassy. Not the honest calm of a deep channel but the flat, held-breath stillness of a reach that had been combed over recently — swept clean of driftwood, of debris, of anything that might betray what had passed through it. The Kestrel didn't do that on its own. Rivers were messy things. Somebody had tidied this stretch.

Theo cut the engine and let the *Vagrant Grace* drift, her hand raised for silence even though nobody aboard had been talking. Ahead, the canyon walls rose sheer and rust-streaked, pinching the river into a corridor barely two boat-lengths wide. The light came down in slats. Somewhere below — downstream, around the blind bend where the channel doglegged east — she could hear it.

An engine. Idling.

Not the ragged cough of an outboard running rough. This was a big marine diesel, the kind of low, patient thrum that said money, that said maintenance schedules and fuel contracts and someone sitting at a helm getting paid whether he caught anything or not. Theo knew that sound the way she knew her own pulse. She'd grown up on the wrong side of it.

"Kill everything," she whispered.

Jun was already moving. He killed the bilge pump first — the loudest thing aboard — then the fridge compressor, then eased the battery bank down to essentials with the tenderness of a man defusing something. Odile stood at the bow, one hand on the painter line, reading the current with her whole body the way she read everything: silently, completely, as though the world were a chart she'd memorized before anyone thought to ask her opinion of it. Sparrow crouched low behind the gunwale, all elbows and held breath. From the galley came the small, deliberate sounds of Marisol putting a lid on a pot.

The drift carried them toward the bend. Theo counted seconds against the idle of that diesel, doing the math she'd been doing since the dock in Corvane, the math she'd been refusing to finish.

*Patrol boats don't patrol the Kestrel.* That was the entire point of the Kestrel. It ran through forty miles of unclaimed territory — no permits, no stations, no law worth the name. The Vane Syndicate kept its hands off it precisely because there was nothing up here to tax and everything up here to lose if they drew attention to it. Her mentor had called it "the last honest water," which was a joke, because nothing about the Kestrel was honest. It was just unwitnessed. There was a difference, and everyone who ran it knew exactly how wide that difference was.

So if there was a Syndicate boat downstream — a real one, with a paid idler at the wheel — then somebody had told them where to wait.

And only one person outside this crew knew their route.

Theo's jaw ached. She realized she'd been clenching it since the dock, maybe since the telegram, twenty years of clenching finally finding its home. *Imogen Reyes.* Of course. Of course it circled back to Imogen. It always had. Every disaster in Theo's life had Imogen Reyes standing somewhere near the edge of it, beautiful and certain and three steps ahead, apologizing afterward in that voice like poured honey over broken glass.

Except —

She made herself stop. Made herself look at the actual shape of it instead of the old scar tissue. Imogen had laid out the route in that dockside bar with a paper chart, hand-drawn, annotated in her own cramped script. She'd given Theo the timing, the caches, the portage. If Imogen had wanted the Syndicate waiting for them, why hire a crew at all? Why spend the money? Why take the risk that Theo would smell the trap — because Theo *would*, had been smelling traps since she was nineteen years old and watching men she loved get pulled under black water?

Unless the trap wasn't for them.

Unless the route itself was the message. Unless someone had gotten the route out of Imogen without her knowing — copied the chart, lifted the notes, listened through a wall. Unless there was a leak somewhere upstream of all of them, and the Syndicate hadn't needed to buy Imogen at all.

That possibility was somehow worse. Theo could fight an enemy. A leak meant she couldn't trust the boat she was standing on.

The diesel sound shifted. Deeper. Moving.

"Down," Theo breathed, and the *Grace* slid into the shadow of the south wall just as the bend ahead bloomed with wake-light.

It came around the corner like a rumor made steel: a gray-hulled patrol cutter, thirty feet of government-surplus muscle retrofitted with Syndicate money, radar mast folded flat for the canyon, twin screws churning the glassy water into confusion. Two figures on deck in dark weather gear. A spotlight rack, unlit because the light was still good. Painted on the bow, small and tasteful, the way rich people did cruelty: a stylized *V*.

Nobody on deck looked up. They didn't need to. Boats like that ran sonar and thermal and patience, and patience above all. They weren't searching. They were *waiting* — parked at the mouth of the Narrows like a spider that understood geometry, letting the river bring its prey to them.

The cutter's wake rolled down the channel and slapped the *Grace*'s hull, once, twice, insolent as a knock on a door. Then the diesel faded around the far bend and the canyon swallowed the sound, and the river went back to pretending it had never seen anything at all.

Nobody moved for a full minute. Then Sparrow, very quietly, from behind the gunwale:

"That was a Vane boat."

"Yeah." Theo brought the bow around with a touch of the tiller, feathering them toward the north bank, where — if Odile's chart and twenty years of other people's bad memories were both right — a side channel split off behind a screen of deadfall about four hundred yards up. "And they're sitting on the Narrows, which is the only way through to the upper river. Which means they know where we're going."

Jun surfaced from his engine hatch, grease to the elbow, face carefully blank. "Somebody talked."

"Or somebody got robbed." Theo watched Odile. The navigator was staring downstream at the empty water with an expression Theo had never seen on her — not fear, exactly. Recalculation. A woman discovering the map she trusted had a fold in it.

"The route was handwritten," Odile said. "Imogen's own annotations. I checked them against my own survey every night. Nobody photographed that chart without me knowing."

"So either Imogen sold us—"

"No." It came out of Odile fast and flat, and it surprised them all, Odile included; she blinked, as if the word had escaped custody. She composed herself. "I'm simply stating that the evidence doesn't support that conclusion yet. Someone accessed her planning materials. That's a different crime than betrayal. We should distinguish before we burn anyone."

"We're not burning anyone," Theo said. "We're hiding. Move."

* * *

The side channel took them half a mile back from the main stem, threading between gravel bars and snags until the canyon walls fell away and they slipped into a drowned alcove beneath a stand of black spruce, deep enough that from the river you'd see nothing but shadow and deadfall. Jun backed the *Grace* in under the branches until the spruce needles actually brushed the cabin roof, and shut everything down except the anchor light, doused, and one small lamp in the galley.

They ate cold beans and hard bread because Marisol declared an open flame was suicide, and she was right, and being right made her insufferable about it. Sparrow picked at her food. Jun cleaned his tools that didn't need cleaning. Odile sat in the bow with her charts spread across her knees, working by the last gray light, annotating, cross-referencing, building alternate routes the way other people prayed.

Theo stood at the stern and watched the water and let herself think the thing she'd been refusing to think since Corvane.

*Cassius Bell.*

The enforcer's smile lived in her memory like a splinter. She'd sat across from him eleven days ago in an office that smelled of lemon polish and old carpet, and he had smiled too warmly while sliding the debt ledger across the desk — the itemized accounting of her father's loans, her mother's medical liens, the refinance on the *Grace* herself, every dollar wrapped around every year of Theo's life like wire. "Miss Blackwood," he'd said, warm as bathwater, "the Syndicate is patient. Patience is our best product." And then, softer, leaning in like a confessor: "But you should know that when people run, we find the running costs more than the staying. We always tell them that. It saves such unpleasantness later."

He'd known she was going to run. He'd *wanted* her to run. Because a debtor who runs is a debtor who leads you somewhere.

And what was upriver? A wrecked plane, a dead whistleblower's inheritance, evidence that could gut the Syndicate from the inside. Evidence Bell's employers would burn rivers to destroy. So you don't chase the evidence. You let your debtor lead you to it. You let her rival hire her. You let the whole expedition assemble itself, crewed by strays with debts and secrets, and then you sit at the mouth of the Narrows with a diesel idling and wait for the river to deliver.

The realization settled over Theo with the terrible comfort of a diagnosis. It wasn't paranoia if the architecture was real. She could almost admire it. Almost.

Almost wasn't the same as forgiving, and the person she couldn't forgive was herself — because she'd felt this coming. In the bar, when Imogen pushed the chart across the table, some animal part of Theo had registered the neatness of it, the way the job fit her desperation exactly, like a key cut to her lock. She'd ignored the animal. Twenty years of ignoring it. Her mentor used to say her instincts were the best he'd ever seen and her willingness to override them was worse than any storm. He'd died proving the second half.

"Captain."

Marisol's voice, from the galley doorway. Low. "Fire's lit. Small one. Come eat something hot or don't come at all."

"There's a patrol boat on the river, Mari."

"And it can't see smoke through a mile of spruce and wet rock, and it definitely can't see through me being stubborn." Marisol crossed her arms, and under the single lamp her face was all planes and patience, a woman built from decades of feeding people who didn't want to be fed. "You can keep watch on the water all night, Theo, and it will change nothing. Or you can sit by a fire with your crew and let them see your face. Your call. But I've cooked for scared crews before, and the ones who ate together were the ones who came back."

Theo looked at the black water a moment longer, at the reflection of the spruce trembling in it, and then she went to the fire.

* * *

Marisol built it small, in a stone ring she'd assembled with geological fussiness, screened on the river side by the hull and a driftwood windbreak. The flames stayed waist-high and apologetic. She'd made stew anyway — of course she had, the woman could conjure a feast from a lifeboat ration, and tonight it was caribou sausage and root vegetables and some spice blend she guarded like state secrets, and the smell alone reorganized the crew's priorities.

They gathered the way stray animals gather: at angles, leaving escape routes, close enough for warmth and not an inch closer. Sparrow sat with her knees drawn up. Jun perched on a log with his ruined-looking hands wrapped around a tin mug. Odile brought her charts, then, after a look from Marisol that could have bent steel, left them in the boat.

For a while, nobody spoke. Fire does that; it holds a silence the way a river holds a boat, without effort.

Then Sparrow said, "How long have you all known each other?"

The question hung there, artless and enormous. Six days. They'd known each other six days, and they'd already run the Narrows together, hidden from a patrol cutter together, and were now committing crimes in proximity to each other by firelight.

"Four years," Jun said, nodding at Theo. "She pulled me out of a scrapyard job in Tern Harbor. Well. She bought the debt. Same thing."

"You bought my debt?" Sparrow turned to Theo, eyes huge in the firelight.

"I bought *options*," Theo said. "Cheap ones. You've been paying them off in labor ever since."

"She means she bought a kid off a dredge crew that worked twelve-hour shifts and locked the bunks," Marisol said mildly, ladling stew.

"They didn't lock the bunks," Theo said.

"They padlocked the hatches. I've seen the scars on the frame in your memory of it, so don't correct me in front of the child."

Sparrow looked between them, recalculating something fundamental. "You never said that."

"You never asked," Theo said, and immediately regretted how much of her own history that sentence exposed, and Marisol's slow smile said she'd done it on purpose.

"What about you, Odile?" Sparrow pressed, emboldened. "Where'd the captain find you?"

Odile considered the fire for a long moment. When she spoke, it was in the tone of a woman reading coordinates aloud — factual, unhurried, and somehow more revealing for it. "I surveyed the Kestrel for a mapping consortium. Three seasons. They published my charts under someone else's name because I wouldn't sign an exclusivity clause." A pause. "Theo found the error I'd planted in the published version. Deliberate error. A false channel marker, ten meters off, that would put any plagiarist's clients on a gravel bar. She wrote to me about it. Not angry. Impressed."

"You sabotaged your own map," Jun said, delighted.

"I signed the map. The map was mine. What they printed was a forgery with my signature on it." Odile's mouth did the thing that was almost a smile. "I believe in accuracy. Aggressively."

Theo stared at her. "You never told me that was deliberate."

"You never asked," Odile said, with devastating serenity, and the whole circle broke — Sparrow laughing into her sleeve, Jun wheezing, even Theo surprised by the sound that came out of her, rusty as an unused winch.

When it quieted, Marisol added wood — one piece, measured, a woman who rationed everything including drama — and said, "You want to know about me? Fine. I cooked on expedition ships for nineteen years. Antarctic runs, Arctic runs, salvage crews, survey crews. You learn everything from a galley, because hungry people tell you the truth. And nineteen years taught me one thing worth passing on." She pointed the ladle at each of them in turn. "Every crew is a family that hasn't admitted it yet. Doesn't matter how they were assembled. Debt, contract, accident, crime — doesn't matter. You put enough hunger and enough danger in one small space and the pretending stops. The only question is whether what's underneath the pretending is worth keeping." She shrugged, vast and calm. "I stay with crews until I find out. Usually takes about six weeks."

"How long have we got?" Sparrow asked.

"Day five," Marisol said. "Ahead of schedule. The captain hid us well and the boy shares his tools and the navigator laughs now, so." She served second helpings without being asked, which in Marisol's grammar was a benediction.

The fire cracked. Up through the spruce branches, the first stars showed, cold and indifferent, and beyond the screen of trees the main river ran dark and secret with its gray wolf of a patrol boat somewhere on it, patient as arithmetic. Theo felt the fear of it sitting in her chest — real fear, the kind she respected — and felt, underneath it, something she distrusted even more.

Belonging. Wary, provisional, firelit belonging. The exact thing she'd spent twenty years avoiding, because the last time she'd belonged to a crew, the river had taken two of them and given her back a guilt she'd been outrunning ever since, racking up miles like a debtor racks up interest.

*Measure in miles, never in years.* That had been her rule. Miles you could outrun. Years accumulated.

"Captain," Odile said quietly. "A question, if the mood allows."

"Mood allows."

"If Bell's people are holding the Narrows, they expect us to come to them. Eventually, hunger or courage makes every crew come to the ambush." Odile turned her tin mug slowly in her hands. "So we don't. I can take us up the Sedge Fork — the abandoned channel, the one the 2004 expedition cut markers for and then abandoned after—" she stopped, precise even in her mercy, "—after. It adds four days. It may not exist anymore. Half of it will be walking the boat through shallows. But no patrol boat fits in it, and no patrol boat will follow us into water that isn't on any living chart but mine."

Silence around the fire. Four days. Calving season wouldn't wait four extra days; everyone aboard had heard Imogen's warning about the glacier window.

"That's the sworn-off water," Jun said. Not objecting. Just naming it, the way you name a reef.

"It's the only water," Odile said.

Everyone looked at Theo. She looked at the fire, and thought of Cassius Bell's warm smile, and of a hand-drawn chart in Imogen Reyes's cramped handwriting, and of the possibility — growing heavier by the hour — that the route Imogen gave them had been stolen, not sold. Stolen meant Imogen might not know. Stolen meant Imogen was sailing blind into the same ambush, wherever she was, whatever boat she was on.

Stolen meant there might still be time to warn her, if they moved fast and moved quiet and moved where wolves couldn't follow.

"First light," Theo said. "We take the Sedge Fork. Cold rations, no fires, no lights after dusk. Jun, I want the shallow-water plates rigged tonight. Sparrow — soundings from the bow, every meter, call everything." She looked around the circle, at the firelit faces of the strays she'd assembled and the family that was assembling itself around her whether she permitted it or not. "Bell bought himself a river. Let's go be the thing he didn't budget for."

Marisol collected the mugs. As she rose, she paused beside Theo and said, low, just for her: "Six weeks, I said. I'm revising my estimate."

"To what?"

"To sooner." And she carried the dishes into the dark.

Theo stayed by the dying fire another minute, listening to the main river breathe beyond the trees, and it was there — in the last ember-light, with the stars sharp above the spruce — that Sparrow came back from the boat, pale in a way that had nothing to do with the cold, and said the words that rearranged the night:

"Captain. The radio. Someone's been transmitting on our band. Burst signals, short ones, encrypted." Her voice shook only a little. "And the bearing they're coming from —"

She steadied herself.

"— it's not from downstream. It's from ahead of us. From the upper river. From Imogen's direction."

* * *

Theo stood, the fire's heat falling away from her back like a hand withdrawn, already moving toward the radio shack, toward the dark, toward the next terrible arithmetic — because if the transmissions were coming from upstream, then either the leak was closer than any of them had dreamed, or Imogen Reyes was out there in the path of the trap, sailing straight into Bell's waiting arms without knowing the chart she'd drawn had already been read by wolves.

"Dawn can't come fast enough," Theo said to no one, and the river, indifferent, agreed.
