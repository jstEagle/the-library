## 9. Imogen's Confession

The storm came down the valley like a debt collector.

Imogen Reyes stood at the wheel of the *Kestrel Queen* and watched it come — a wall of slate and churning white that swallowed the glacier peaks one by one, moving with the patient inevitability of something that had already decided how this day would end. Behind her, the river ran high and brown with snowmelt, and ahead, the canyon walls narrowed into a throat of granite that no sane pilot would enter in weather like this.

"Side channel," Theo said beside her. Not an order. An observation, offered flat, the way she offered everything — as if her opinions cost her something to spend.

"I see it."

"I know you see it. I'm saying it out loud so we both admit it before we do it."

Imogen almost smiled. Twenty years ago that dryness had infuriated her; now it felt like the last honest thing on the river. She swung the wheel hard to starboard and the boat leaned into the turn, cargo shifting below decks with a groan of strapped crates, and then they were sliding into the channel — a slot of green water barely wider than the hull, walled by alder and black spruce, the storm's wind suddenly cut to nothing above them.

The silence that followed was enormous. Rain began to fall through the canopy in fat, deliberate drops, ticking against the deck like someone counting.

* * *

They tied off to two dead cedars and a living one, because Jun insisted on redundancy and nobody argued with Jun about knots. The crew folded itself under the tarp Marisol rigged from the cargo boom — Sparrow curled against a crate with her hood up and her eyes closed but not sleeping, Odile cross-legged with her charts spread over her knees even though there was nothing left to chart until the weather broke, Jun oiling his hands with the grim devotion of a man who knew exactly what tomorrow's portage would ask of them.

Theo sat at the far end, alone by choice, cleaning the carburetor of the backup outboard with a rag gone gray with grease. She always found work when conversation threatened. Imogen had remembered that about her. She'd remembered too much about her, these past weeks — the way Theo's jaw set before bad water, the way she touched the rail twice before trusting it, the way she never said her mentor's name aloud, not once, not even when they passed the tributary where the old expedition had put in twenty years ago.

Marisol pressed a tin mug into Imogen's hands without being asked. Coffee, cut with something cinnamon and sweet. "You look like a woman rehearsing a speech," Marisol murmured.

"I don't have a speech."

"You've been rehearsing one since the Narrows. I cook for people. I can tell when they're chewing on words instead of food." She squeezed Imogen's shoulder and moved on, leaving the mug warm between Imogen's palms.

The rain thickened. Somewhere upstream, thunder rolled along the canyon like a barrel going over stairs.

Imogen drank the coffee and looked at Theo's bent head across the deck, and understood that Marisol was right, and hated that she was right, and knew — with the same cold certainty she'd felt watching the storm come down the valley — that the weather had made this decision for her. They could be pinned here for six hours or three days. And somewhere behind them, if Cassius Bell was half as thorough as she feared, a Syndicate patrol boat was running their route with their own coordinates in its hand.

There would be no better time to say it. There might be no other time at all.

She crossed the deck. Sat down on the crate opposite Theo, close enough that the rain-spatter reached them both, far enough that Theo wouldn't feel cornered. Theo glanced up, registered her, went back to the carburetor.

"The float needle's shot," Theo said. "Spare's in the green toolbox."

"I didn't come about the outboard."

"Mm." But she set the rag down. That was Theo Blackwood's version of turning to face you: putting down the tool.

Imogen wrapped both hands around the mug. "Do you remember my father?"

The question landed harder than she'd intended. She saw it land — saw the small stillness move through Theo like a stone dropped through still water.

"Eduardo Reyes," Theo said slowly. "He ran logistics for the Meridian expeditions. Quiet guy. Kept to the office end of things." A pause. "He signed off on our supply manifests. He used to put extra chocolate rations in the boxes and mark them as weight variance."

"He did," Imogen said, and her voice cracked on the second word, which was not part of any rehearsal. She cleared her throat. "Everyone remembers him as the paperwork man. The careful one. The boring one who made sure the fuel numbers added up."

"And he wasn't."

"No. He wasn't." She stared into the coffee. The rain ticked on the tarp overhead. "Theo, I need to tell you what's actually in that wreck. Not the version I gave you at the dock. The real one."

Theo's eyes came up, gray-green and unblinking, and Imogen recognized the look — the same look she'd worn across a hundred starting lines of a hundred races, the look that said *I will wait as long as it takes, and I will not flinch first.*

"My father wasn't just logistics for Meridian," Imogen said. "By 2003 he was internal audit for the whole Vane shipping arm. Every route, every manifest, every payment that moved through the Kestrel corridor passed over his desk. And what he found—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "What he found was that the Syndicate wasn't just smuggling freight. They were moving people. Contract laborers brought upriver under cargo manifests, worked the extraction camps, and when the contracts 'expired'—"

She couldn't finish it. Theo finished it for her, voice flat as slate: "They never came back down."

"The ledgers showed it. Duplicate manifests. Fuel burns that matched no cargo weights. Payments to camp operators that Vane recorded as 'site services.' My father spent fourteen months copying everything. Fourteen months of eating dinner across from men he knew were murderers, smiling at them, initialing their documents." Her hands had gone white around the mug. "And then he hid the copies. All of them. In the one place in the world the Syndicate would never send anyone."

Theo had gone very still. "The wreck."

"The wreck." Imogen let out a breath that shook on the way out. "In September of 2004, Meridian chartered a supply flight upriver — the *Larkspur*, a twin otter, old airframe, tired pilot. My father booked himself onto it as riding crew. Officially, he was inspecting upstream depots. Actually, he was carrying eleven kilos of documents sealed in waterproof canisters, and he'd arranged with the pilot to stage a controlled emergency landing on the flats below the source lake — gear failure, forced down, salvageable wreck, nothing worth investigating. The plane would sit there looking like every other piece of expedition litter on that river, and the evidence would sit inside it, safe, until he could get it out through channels Vane didn't own."

"But the crash wasn't controlled."

"The crash wasn't controlled." Imogen heard her own voice go distant, the way it always did when she approached this part, as if the words belonged to someone standing on a far shore. "We'll never know exactly what happened. Weather, maybe. Or maybe Vane got suspicious and someone did something to that airframe — my father's notes hinted he thought he was being followed in his last weeks. What we know is the *Larkspur* went down hard in the narrows above the lake, and my father died with the evidence he'd spent fourteen months stealing. And the Syndicate spent the next twenty years making sure nobody ever went looking for that wreck — because they didn't know what was in it, only that Eduardo Reyes had been carrying *something*, and that whatever it was could hang them."

The rain drummed. Down the channel, the river slid past, dark and indifferent, carrying their boat's reflection away in pieces.

"So the inheritance," Theo said. "The whistleblower's estate. The job you hired me for."

"That part was true. It is an estate. His." Imogen set the mug down on the deck because her hands had started shaking and she refused to let Theo see it in them. "I've spent nine years and most of my money trying to clear his name through legal channels. Petitions, archives, retired investigators. Every door I knocked on turned out to have a Vane lock in it. Two of the lawyers I hired stopped returning calls mid-case. One left the country." She laughed, short and bitter. "So I stopped knocking and started planning a burglary. The biggest one anyone's ever attempted. Up a river my father helped map, to a wreck everyone else has sworn off, to steal back the truth and burn the Syndicate down with it."

* * *

For a long moment Theo said nothing. She picked the rag back up and wiped her hands with slow, unnecessary care, and Imogen recognized that too — the ritual of a woman buying time to feel something before she had to show it.

"You knew," Theo finally said. "When you came to the dock. You knew about the 2004 expedition. About Marcus. About why I swore off this water. And you asked anyway."

"Yes."

"You didn't apologize then either."

"No."

"Why now? Why tell me this?" Theo's voice stayed level, but something underneath it had begun to tremble, faint as a hairline crack in ice. "You could've carried this all the way to the lake. Let me think I'm hauling salvage for a payout. Why hand me the truth in the rain?"

Because, Imogen thought, because of the journal page Jun found and you burned, because of the way you look upstream like a woman approaching a grave, because of Sparrow's thin wrists and Odile's fresh rope-cuts and the patrol boat we both know is coming — because if Bell takes us at that lake, you deserve to know what you're bleeding for.

She said none of that. What she said was: "Because you're going to find out anyway. At the lake, in the canisters, my father labeled everything. Dates, names, his own handwriting. And when you read it—" Her breath caught. "When you read it, you're going to learn that the disaster that killed your crew and the murder of Eduardo Reyes are the same event. Same river. Same year. Same company. And I would rather you hear it from me than from a dead man's files."

Theo stood. Imogen's chest tightened — she'd pushed too far, too fast, twenty years of rivalry and four weeks of grudging alliance collapsing under the weight of it — but Theo didn't walk away. She walked to the rail instead, to the gap in the tarp where the rain came through silver in the low light, and stood looking upstream toward the source they couldn't yet see.

"Marcus knew him," Theo said quietly.

Imogen rose. "What?"

"Marcus Vale. My mentor." Theo's hands closed on the rail. "After the crash, after we lost Renny and Cole, Marcus went downriver to coordinate the recovery. When he came back he was different. He'd stopped talking about certain stretches of the river entirely — drew blank spaces on his own charts, told us some water wasn't ours anymore. And once, drunk, in Fairbanks, he told me the crash hadn't been an accident. That there were men asking questions about the *Larkspur* who weren't rescue men. I thought it was grief talking. I thought—" She exhaled. "I thought he was broken, and I pitied him, and I took the blame he offered me for freezing at the helm because it was easier than believing the river itself was rotten."

"It wasn't grief," Imogen said. "It was knowledge. Your mentor figured out what my father died carrying. Maybe not all of it. Enough."

"Enough to swear off the water and die with it." Theo turned, and in the gray light her face had changed — stripped of its usual armor, younger, rawer, the face of the woman who'd frozen at that helm twenty years ago and had been paying for it ever since. "Bell fed our route to Vane. We both know it. Which means when we reach that lake, we won't be salvaging. We'll be walking into the exact trap that killed Renny and Cole — the same architecture, the same bait, twenty years later. And you knew that might happen when you hired me."

"I hoped it wouldn't. I planned for it anyway." Imogen stepped closer, close enough now that the rain reached neither of them, only the sound of it. "I'm not asking for forgiveness, Theo. I'm telling you the truth because you're the only person on this river strong enough to carry it, and because—" Her voice failed, and she let it fail, and said the rest plainly. "Because he was my father. Everyone thinks he was a bureaucrat. A footnote. A man who died in someone else's accident. And he was the bravest person I ever knew, and he's been lying in that wreck for twenty years with the truth in his arms, waiting for somebody to come get it. I couldn't ask a stranger. God help me, I couldn't even ask a friend. So I asked my rival, because rivals keep score, and I needed someone who'd finish the job no matter what it cost."

The silence stretched. Thunder moved downstream now, receding, taking its argument elsewhere.

Then Theo reached out and gripped Imogen's shoulder — hard, brief, the grip of a woman who didn't know how to hug and wasn't going to learn today — and said, "Then we finish it. Both of it. Your father's name and mine. Whatever's waiting at that lake."

Imogen nodded, unable to speak, and for one moment the twenty years between them simply weren't there — two daughters of the same drowned summer, standing in the rain.

From under the tarp, Sparrow's voice, thin and alarmed: "Hey — hey, does anyone else hear that?"

They all heard it. Under the rain, under the river, from downstream around the bend of the channel — the low, diesel-throated idling of an engine that wasn't theirs, holding steady, holding position, waiting.

Theo's hand went to the rail. "That," she said, "is Bell."
