## 10. The Portage Nobody Talks About

The portage didn't appear on any chart. That was the first thing Jun Park understood about it, crouched at the waterline where the Kestrel River simply *stopped* — swallowed whole by a wall of granite and deadfall that rose out of the mist like the spine of some drowned animal. Odile had called it "the carry" in her flat navigator's way, as though a two-mile climb over broken mountain were a matter of carrying groceries. Imogen's old expedition notes called it nothing at all. There was just a gap in her father's handwriting, three days of blank paper, as if even ink had refused to go there.

Jun had been an engineer for nineteen years, and engineers believed in problems with solutions. This was not one of those. The *Marrow*, their patched-together river boat, weighed eleven hundred pounds dry and closer to two thousand with fuel, gear, and Marisol's cast-iron stove lashed to the deck like a stubborn passenger. There were four able bodies, a teenager, and a woman who navigated by memory of rivers she'd never run. And somewhere above them, past the tree line, the glacier was already groaning.

"Two miles," Theo said, standing with her hands on her hips, looking up at the wall of green and gray. She said it the way other people said *two miles of open road*. "Odile scouted the first half yesterday. Switchbacks most of the way. We use the rollers and the winch cable."

"The winch cable that snapped last spring," Jun said.

"It didn't snap. It frayed."

"Fraying is snapping with a delay."

Theo glanced at him, and something passed between them that wasn't quite humor and wasn't quite apology — the currency they'd been trading in since the launch. "Then we don't let it fray."

* * *

They started before dawn because Theo said heat was the enemy, and because none of them wanted to see how bad the portage looked in full light. Jun rigged the hauling harness from the spare anchor line, doubled over twice where he could feel the fibers going soft, and put himself under the stern rail with his shoulder against the keel plate. Sparrow took the bow line. Odile and Imogen worked the rollers — salvaged pipe sections they'd feed under the hull ahead of the skids, then scramble forward to lay the next ones down, a rhythm older than engines. Marisol carried the load that couldn't be hauled: food, water, the med kit, and the small solar rig that kept their radio alive.

The first hour was almost kind. Moss gave under their boots. The grade was merciful. Jun found a cadence and lost himself in it the way he always did — count the push, breathe on the recovery, watch the wood grain of the rail for splinters working loose. His hands were his instruments. He'd rebuilt the *Marrow*'s engine twice with them, stitched her hull with them, held them steady inside a running bilge pump while the boat bucked beneath him in the Narrows. They were scarred and cracked and beautiful, and he trusted them more than he trusted most people.

By the second hour, the mountain stopped being kind.

The switchbacks turned on themselves like a fist closing. Deadfall blocked the old trail — trees downed decades ago, silvered by weather, stacked in tangles that no saw would fix. Someone had portaged here once, long ago, but the forest had spent twenty years erasing the evidence. They went over, around, through. Jun learned to hate the sound of the keel grinding across bare granite, a noise like a tooth being pulled slowly.

It was on the third switchback that his left hand betrayed him.

He didn't notice at first. He noticed things like that late, always had — pain arrived at his door quietly and waited to be acknowledged, and Jun had spent a lifetime pretending not to be home. It was Sparrow who saw it. He heard her stop ahead of him on the trail, heard the bow line go slack, and when he looked up she was staring at his hand where it gripped the rail.

"You're bleeding," she said.

"It's nothing."

"It's really not nothing." Her voice did something complicated — young and fierce and scared all at once, the voice of a kid who had learned early that blood meant trouble. "Jun. Look at it."

He looked. The palm had opened along the old scar line from a repair job years back, a seam splitting the way seams split when you asked too much of them. Beneath it, the skin was pulped and raw, blisters raised across the heel of his thumb where the rope had been chewing for hours. Both hands, actually. When he tried to flex his fingers, they answered slowly, grudgingly, like machinery left out in winter.

Marisol appeared with the med kit before he could argue, which was fortunate, because he'd been planning to argue. She sat him on a log, took his hands in hers — hands like cast iron themselves, warm and enormous — and clicked her tongue at what she found.

"You've been hiding this since the second hour," she said.

"I've been busy since the second hour."

"Mm." She cleaned the split with antiseptic that stung like a personal insult, wrapped the palms in gauze and tape, built him a pair of mittens out of bandaging that left only his fingertips free. "You pull rope again today, these come off and we start over. You understand me? You are the only one who can fix the engine if the engine needs fixing, and I will not have you ruining your instruments before we reach water again."

"They're my hands, Mari."

"No," she said pleasantly, packing up the kit, "they're the crew's hands. You just get to borrow them."

That was the thing about Big Marisol: she won arguments without ever raising her voice, and then fed you afterward so you couldn't even hold the loss against her.

* * *

But hands or no hands, the boat still had to move, and the mountain still didn't care.

Jun stood at the stern after Marisol walked away, flexing his wrapped fingers, doing the arithmetic every engineer does — force over distance, weight over grade, bodies available. The numbers came back ugly. Take him off rope work and the haul got slower by a third. Slower meant later. Later meant calving season, and calving season meant the window slamming shut with them on the wrong side of it.

Sparrow read the same numbers. He watched her read them — saw her eyes flick from his bandages to the stern line to the grade above, quick and calculating in a way that reminded him, uncomfortably, of Theo. Then she picked up the bow line without a word, moved it to the stern, and clipped herself into the anchor-line harness beside his empty spot.

"Sparrow—"

"I'm light," she said, testing the line. "I'm fast. And I don't have anyone telling me to stay off ropes."

"That's not how the load works. You can't just take my share."

"I know." She looked up at him, and there was something in her face he hadn't seen before — not the cocky deckhand grin, not the kid performing toughness. Something quieter and much older. "I'm not taking your share. I'm adding mine."

And she leaned into the harness and pulled.

Jun stood there for a moment, useless, watching a sixteen-year-old throw her whole slight body against eleven hundred pounds of boat, boots skidding on wet root, breath coming in white bursts. She couldn't do what he could do. She wasn't strong enough, wasn't heavy enough, wasn't anything enough, technically speaking. But she pulled anyway, and the boat moved anyway, an inch at a time, and Jun understood suddenly that the mathematics had been wrong from the beginning. The load was never divided by strength. It was divided by willingness, and Sparrow had more of that per pound than anyone aboard.

He fell in beside her — not pulling, because Marisol would materialize out of the treeline if he did, but steadying the hull over the worst rocks, guiding the skids, being the engineer in the ways that didn't require grip. And when Sparrow stumbled on the fourth switchback, skinning her knee through her trousers, she got up faster than she went down and never once mentioned it.

Neither did he mention that he saw.

Some things you keep. That was the whole religion of the crew of strays, Jun thought. Not confession. Keeping.

* * *

The rain came at midday, fine and needling, turning the granite slick and the moss into grease. By then they had climbed above the river's roar into a silence that felt manufactured, and the trees began to change — firs thinning, trunks twisting, the forest giving way to scrub that clung to the slope like a held breath. Odile called a halt at a shelf of flat rock where the old expedition had evidently rested; there were rusted tent pegs still in the ground, and half a crate stamped with a logo Jun recognized from Imogen's photographs.

Imogen sat apart from the others, as she usually did at stops, going through her father's notes with rain beading on the plastic sleeves. Jun lowered himself onto the rock near her, careful of his hands, and nodded at the pages.

"The gap," he said. "Three blank days here. Your father wrote everything else down. What happened to this part?"

Imogen was quiet for a long moment. Rain ticked on the plastic.

"I used to think he tore them out," she said finally. "Because it was bad. Because whatever happened on the carry was something he didn't want recorded." She folded the notes closed. "Now I think maybe he just couldn't. Some things you don't write down. You carry them instead. Same as the boat."

Ahead of them, Theo stood at the lip of the next rise, looking at something Jun couldn't see yet. Her shoulders had gone very straight, the way they did when the past got close. Sparrow appeared at her elbow unasked, the way she'd appeared at his, and said something too quiet to hear, and Theo laughed — a short, surprised sound, like a lock giving way — and put a hand briefly on the kid's head.

Jun watched that and felt something in his chest he didn't have engineering language for. He was forty-six years old. He had fixed machines his whole life because machines told you the truth: broken was broken, fixed was fixed, and nothing pretended otherwise. People were harder. People broke in places you couldn't reach with tools. But watching Sparrow take his share without being asked, watching her hand Theo a laugh like handing someone a match in the wind, he thought maybe people got repaired too. Just slower. Just by each other.

"Break's over!" Theo called, and the silence ended, and they leaned into the mountain again.

* * *

The last hour was the worst, because the last hour always was. The trail steepened toward the pass, and the rain turned to sleet, and the *Marrow* had to come up the final pitch on the winch cable after all — the frayed one, because there was no other. Jun directed the operation with his ruined hands tucked in his armpits like a penguin, shouting angles and tensions, hating every yard of it. Odile anchored the tail line. Imogen and Marisol took the crank. Sparrow rode the bow, calling the hull's clearance over the rocks in a voice that never wavered, and Theo hauled at the cable itself with her gloves smoking, refusing help, refusing rest, refusing the mountain's terms.

The cable sang. Then it buzzed. Then, six feet below the crest of the pass, one of the frayed strands let go with a crack like a rifle shot, and the whole line shuddered, and everyone froze except Sparrow, who threw her weight on the bow line and held the nose true while the others brought it up the last six feet by hand, by will, by sheer refusal, until the keel scraped over the crest and settled onto the downslope rollers with a groan of relief that sounded, to Jun, exactly the way he felt.

They stood in the sleet at the top of the world, five people and a boat, breathing steam. Below them, through a tear in the cloud, the far valley opened — and there it was. Water. A river, high and cold and glacial-green, falling away north toward country none of them had ever seen.

"Downhill from here," Odile said, which was the closest thing to poetry she ever allowed.

Jun looked at his hands. Under the bloody gauze, his fingertips had gone wrinkled and pale, and he flexed them experimentally and found they still answered. Instruments, Marisol called them. The crew's hands, on loan.

Sparrow dropped onto the skid beside him, soaked to the bone, knee filthy, grinning like she'd personally defeated the mountain. Maybe she had. "Told you I could add my share."

"You did more than that."

"Yeah?" She bumped her shoulder against his arm, gentle, mindful of the wraps. "What'd I do?"

Jun considered the question. Considered the truth in it — that she'd carried weight that wasn't hers, silently, the way love actually works when it's real: not announced, just shouldered.

"You made the math wrong," he said. "In a good way."

She frowned at that, decided she liked it, and grinned wider.

Then Odile, standing at the edge of the overlook with her binoculars trained on the river below, went very still in the particular way that meant the world had changed.

"Jun," she said quietly. "You need to look at this."

He came to the edge. Followed her lens down to the green water in the valley, to the gravel bar at its bend where the current slowed —

— where a fire had burned recently, and a boat sat moored that wasn't theirs, and figures were moving on the bank, and one of them stood facing upstream, directly toward the pass, directly toward them, as if he already knew exactly where they'd come over the mountain.

Even at this distance, Jun knew the silhouette. Broad shoulders. Patient stance. A man who smiled too warmly.

"Cassius Bell," Theo said behind him, and her voice had gone flat and cold as the glacier above. "He's not following us."

She raised the binoculars.

"He's waiting for us."
