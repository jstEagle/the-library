## 11. Icefield Light

The trees gave up two hours after dawn.

Theo felt it happen the way you feel a door close behind you — a soft change in the air, the sudden nakedness of a landscape with nothing left to hide behind. One moment they were threading the boat through channels walled in black spruce and alder scrub, the river braided and secretive as gossip; the next, the forest simply stopped, retreating downslope in a ragged line like soldiers who'd decided the war wasn't worth it. Beyond that line lay rock and lichen and water the color of old bottle glass, and beyond that — rising out of the morning haze like something that had been waiting for them all along — the ice.

"Kestrel Head," Odile said from the chart table, not looking up. She didn't need to look up. She'd been navigating by half-buried brass markers for three days now, markers set by an expedition twenty years dead, and her voice had acquired a certain flatness Theo recognized because she'd heard it in her own mouth once, long ago, coming up this same stretch of water. It was the voice of a person reading coordinates off a gravestone.

"I see it," Theo said.

Everybody saw it. The glacier filled the head of the valley the way grief fills a house — utterly, without apology, taking up all the space there is. It came down between two granite shoulders in a slow blue cataract, ten miles of compressed time, its face a sheer wall of fractured light at the far end of the lake they were entering now. Even from four miles out, even through the haze, Theo could feel it looking back.

Sparrow stood on the foredeck with one hand on the rail and her mouth slightly open, which was as close to speechlessness as the kid ever got. "It's *big*," she said finally, in the tone of someone who has just discovered that all their previous ideas of big were children's drawings.

"It's bigger than it looks," Theo said. "That's the trick of it. Your eye keeps telling you it's close. It's not close. Nothing up here is close." She eased the throttle back and let the hull settle into the lake's long glassy swell. "Ice lies about distance. That's the first thing Hal ever taught me about country like this."

She said his name before she could stop herself. Hal Voss. It still sat in her chest like a swallowed stone, twenty years on, and she watched Marisol — ladling coffee in the cockpit doorway — catch the name and glance away with the practiced delicacy of a woman who'd been pretending not to overhear things for a week now.

Jun was in the engine bay, of course. He'd been in the engine bay since four in the morning, coaxing the port motor through some complaint only the two of them understood, and when Theo climbed down the ladder into the hot mechanical dark he looked up with grease to his elbows and that open, patient face that made him so easy to underestimate and so hard to lie to.

"We're through the Narrows?" he asked. "I heard the water change."

"You heard right. Flat water from here to the iceface." She handed him a rag. He wiped his hands the way he did everything — carefully, thoroughly, like the task deserved his full attention. "We'll make the source by tomorrow if the wind holds. I want to check the cooling intakes before we push on. Glacier flour will chew them up."

"Mm." He took the rag but didn't turn back to the engine. In the cramped space, lit by the single work lamp, he studied her with that quiet directness that had been accumulating all trip, the way sediment accumulates. "Theo."

"Don't."

"You've got that look."

"What look?"

"The one where you're steering away from something." He folded the rag over the rail of the workbench, squared it, squared it again. "You've had it since the portage. Since before the portage, maybe. And we're about to be alone together for two hours of flat water, and I'm not going anywhere, and neither are you."

Above them, muffled by the deck plates, Sparrow's voice rang out — *"There's a BLUE part! Marisol, come LOOK at the blue part!"* — and Marisol's laugh rolled across the water like warm bread. The engine ticked as it cooled. Somewhere far off, so faint it might have been imagination, came a sound Theo's body knew before her mind did: a deep, subterranean groan, like a giant turning over in its sleep. The glacier, talking to itself. Glaciers did that. They always had more to say than anyone wanted to hear.

"All right," Theo said.

* * *

They ran the last miles with the motors at idle, because the lake was littered with growlers — small calved-off bergs riding low and silent, white above and that impossible blue below, drifting like the ghosts of bigger things. Up here the sun had real teeth despite the cold; the light came off the ice in sheets and bounced until the whole valley glowed from underneath, and Theo steered around each growler with the automatic tenderness of someone handling another person's bones.

Jun sat beside her in the bow, wrapped in two coats, watching her hands on the wheel.

"When I was nineteen," Theo said, because apparently she was going to do this after all, "I came up this lake for the first time. With Hal. With the whole crew — you know the names from the memorial, if you ever read it. Ruthie Okonkwo. Daniel Ferreira. Six of us besides me and Hal. We were running supply for a survey team doing ice-core work on the Kestrel Head, same as half the idiots who ever came up here. Hal had run this water eleven times. Eleven. He knew every rock in the river the way you know your own teeth."

Jun said nothing. He was good at that. Most people rushed to fill silence; Jun let silence do its work, the way he let solvents do their work.

"There was a storm coming in that nobody read right. Not the forecast — those were always wrong up here anyway. I mean the *river*. There's a thing that happens when a storm system sits on the divide and dumps rain onto snowpack. The gauge stations don't catch it. The river just... changes its mind. Rises four feet in an hour. Hal called it 'the river getting honest.' Said the Kestrel was honest exactly once a season, and your job was to make sure you weren't standing in front of it when honesty arrived."

The light shifted. A cloud crossed the sun and the whole glacier dimmed from white-gold to bruised silver, then brightened again, and Theo realized she had stopped steering around the growlers and was steering a straight line, staring at nothing.

"We got caught in the lower gorge. You remember the gorge."

"I remember the portage trail around it," Jun said quietly. "And what the water in it looks like from above."

"Yeah. Well. Twenty years ago it looked different, because the river was four feet higher and coming up." Her voice had gone somewhere flat and distant; she let it. This was the part she'd rehearsed for two decades and never performed. "Hal put us ashore on the left bank and started lining the boat up through on ropes — the standard move, walk her down the bank hand over hand, keep the bow pointed upstream, everybody safe, boat maybe lost. Textbook. Except the bank was scree and the scree started moving under us, and Ruthie went in. Just — went. No drama, no scream. One second she was on the rope next to me and the next the river had her."

The words came easier now, which was somehow worse. Confession, Theo thought, was like rapids: terrifying at the top, mechanical in the middle, and you didn't get to stop halfway down.

"And I froze."

She said it plainly. Jun's breath caught — she heard it — but he kept still.

"Not for a second. Not even for a dramatic, movie-shaped moment. For forty seconds. Maybe fifty. Long enough for Danny to go in after her. Long enough for both of them to be past the point where anything I did mattered. I stood on that bank holding a rope attached to nothing, and my body would not obey me, and the river took them both, and then Hal went in — Hal, who was sixty-one years old, who'd told us all a hundred times *never* go in, never, the river gets two, you do not make it three — Hal went in because his crew was dying while his best student stood there like furniture."

The glacier groaned again, closer this time, a sound like a cathedral cracking. Neither of them flinched. It seemed appropriate, somehow, that the ice should talk during this.

"He got Ruthie out. Dead, but out — she washed into an eddy a quarter mile down and he pulled her onto a gravel bar and did compressions until his arms gave out. Danny they never found. And when the inquiry came, Hal stood up in front of the board and said the failure was command error. His error. He said he'd misjudged the storm window, pushed into the gorge against his own rule, and lost two people. Which was true, mostly. What he didn't say — what he never said, not once, not to the board, not to Ruthie's mother when she asked him straight out what happened on that bank — was that his best student had frozen solid while two people drowned within arm's reach of her."

"Why?" Jun asked. Not accusingly. Genuinely asking, the way he asked about engines.

"Because he told me if I carried it publicly, I'd never pilot again. Boards talk. Licenses die. And he said —" Here Theo's voice finally cracked, and she hated it and let it happen, because Jun had earned the crack. "He said, *'You'll spend the debt better alive than ruined, Theo. Take the years I'm giving you and pay it back in kind.'*" She laughed, or made a sound adjacent to laughing. "Pay it back in kind. Twenty years of chasing storms for money I didn't need and couldn't keep, running rivers I swore off, hiring strays nobody else would touch — I thought that was paying it back. Turns out it was just running with extra steps."

Jun was quiet for a long moment. Below them, the hull knocked softly against a growler Theo had failed to dodge, a small polite collision, and she corrected without thinking.

"Hal Voss," Jun said finally, "took the blame so you could keep working. And you've spent twenty years working like the blame was true."

"That's about the size of it."

"No." Jun turned to face her fully, grease-stained and serious, and the light off the ice fell across his face and made him look briefly like a man in an old painting. "That's not the size of it. I've watched you for eight days, Theo. I watched you hold position in the Narrows for six minutes while the eddy line ate our stern and everyone else was screaming. I watched you go into floodwater after Sparrow's dropped line at the portage without a second's hesitation. Whatever happened to you on that bank in 2004 — your body doesn't freeze anymore."

"My body doesn't freeze," Theo agreed. "It's the rest of me."

"Then say the rest of it out loud, to somebody, and see if it doesn't thaw too."

The cloud passed off the sun. The whole glacier lit up like a held breath released, and for a moment the lake was so bright that Theo had to squint, and in that squinting brightness she felt something shift in her chest — not healed, nothing was healed, but moved. Rearranged. The way a room feels different after a window's been opened.

"Hal died four years ago," she said. "Heart, in his sleep, up at his cabin on the lower river. I didn't go to the funeral. I sent flowers and kept driving. His grave is up here, Jun. Above the tree line, on the point where the survey camp used to be. He asked for it in the letter his daughter sent me — asked that his crew scatter him where the expedition ended. I've been carrying that letter in my kit for four years like it was live ordnance."

"Are you going to go?"

"After we salvage the wreck. After Imogen's father's evidence is out of that plane and the Syndicate is bleeding in the press." Theo found she was gripping the wheel and made herself loosen, finger by finger. "After. Because if I stand at that grave with the job unfinished, I won't survive the conversation I have to have with him."

Jun nodded slowly. Then, in the tone he saved for diagnoses: "Your cooling intake's fouled, by the way. Has been since the gorge. If we push hard tomorrow, starboard runs hot."

Theo blinked. Then she laughed — really laughed, helpless, the first true laugh since the telegram had reached her in that motel outside Meridian — because of course. Of course Jun Park would receive a confession twenty years buried and respond with a maintenance report. Of course that was the language of love aboard this boat.

"Fix it tonight?"

"Already planned to."

* * *

They anchored at dusk in the lee of a granite island no bigger than a chapel, and the glacier turned colors as the sun went down — gold to rose to a blue so deep it looked deliberate, chosen, the blue of a thing that had been deciding for ten thousand years. Marisol produced stew and the good bread, hoarded for an occasion none of them had named, and Sparrow narrated the glacier's colors like a sports announcer until Odile threatened to navigate her overboard.

Theo stood at the stern with her coffee going cold and watched the iceface, and that was when she saw it — or almost saw it, in the last violet light: high on the lateral moraine, a mile short of the source, a smudge of orange. Fabric. Tent fabric, or tarp. Man-made color in a country that owned no such color.

"Odile," she said quietly.

Odile appeared at her shoulder, binoculars already coming up. A long silence.

"Somebody beat us here," Theo said.

"Or somebody's waiting for us." Odile lowered the glasses. Her face, in the failing light, was very still. "Theo — that orange. I've seen that orange before. Cassius Bell's patrol boats fly it off their antennas."

Behind them, unnoticed, Sparrow slipped below decks with the handheld radio, and the glacier groaned its long slow warning across the darkening water.
