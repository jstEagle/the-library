## 12. The Turncoat

Odile had learned long ago that rivers told the truth and people lied, and that the trick to surviving both was to watch the water where it eddied — where things slowed enough to be seen.

She was doing it now, in a manner of speaking. Not watching water. Watching people.

The crew had made camp at dusk in a gravel bowl below a shelf of black slate, the boat hauled half out of the current and lashed to two spruce stumps with the double bowline Odile herself had tied, because she tied everything twice now, because twenty years on the Kestrel taught her that the river forgave nothing twice. Marisol's fire burned low and orange, throwing long shadows against the slate. Jun was asleep sitting up, his ruined hands cradled against his chest like something broken he intended to fix later. Theo stood at the waterline, staring upstream into the dark the way she always did, as if she could read the next day's rapids by squinting hard enough.

And Sparrow was gone from the fire.

Not far. That was what caught Odile's attention first, the way a wrong note catches a musician's ear. Sparrow never strayed more than ten paces from the group after dark — a habit she'd worn like armor since the Narrows, when she'd frozen on the line and Theo had talked her through it with that low, gravel-calm voice of hers. But now the girl had drifted upriver along the bank, past the woodpile, into the shadow where the slate shelf met the water, and she'd taken something with her.

Something small and rectangular. Something that fit in a coat pocket.

Odile rose without hurry, the way you rise when you don't want the river to notice you moving. She refilled her tin cup from the kettle as an excuse, took a sip of tea she didn't want, and followed the curve of the bank at an angle, keeping the fire between herself and the others so her absence read as nothing more than a woman stretching her legs.

She heard the voice before she saw the girl.

It was pitched low, flattened, careful — a voice shaped for hiding. Odile stopped behind a tangle of deadfall, close enough to see through the branches: Sparrow crouched with her back against the stone, the radio unit cradled in both hands, its little green indicator light pulsing like a slow heartbeat.

"—again, I'm telling you again," Sparrow whispered. "Grid mark forty-one, side channel off the main stem. They're holed up for the night. Boat's the *Provision*, hull number—" She stopped. Swallowed. Odile heard the swallow from six meters away, or imagined she did. "You said if I gave you the route you'd move him. You *said*."

A pause. Static hissed like sand through an hourglass.

Then a voice came through the speaker, smooth and warm as bathwater, and Odile felt the temperature of the whole night drop around her. Cassius Bell. She'd only heard that voice once before, over a harbor frequency in another life, but a voice like that didn't wear out. It was built to last, the way rot is built to last.

"Sparrow." The name drawn out, fond, terrible. "You've been a great help. Truly. And your brother is fine. He eats three meals a day. He asked about you just this morning."

"You said—"

"And here's the thing about tomorrow." The warmth didn't change. That was the horror of it; the warmth never changed. "Tomorrow they'll run the gorge above the icefield. Deep water, fast water. A lot of ways for a small boat to have an accident. So I need one more thing from you. The takeout point. Where Reyes plans to portage. Give me that, and Denny walks free inside the week. I'll send a car for him myself. You have my word."

*You have my word.* Odile closed her eyes. She had spent forty-one years navigating by instruments and instinct, and both told her the same thing about a man who gave his word over an open frequency to a sixteen-year-old girl holding a debt over her brother's head.

Sparrow's hands were shaking. The green light pulsed. And Odile watched the girl look down at the radio the way you look at a rope bridge you know won't hold — knowing, already knowing, and stepping anyway, because the other side held someone you loved.

"Takeout's—" Sparrow began.

Odile stepped out of the deadfall.

"*Don't.*"

The word cracked across the gravel louder than she meant it to, and Sparrow flinched so hard she nearly dropped the unit, scrambling upright, backing against the slate, one arm coming up across her face as though Odile were going to strike her — and that flinch, that practiced, automatic shield, told Odile more than any confession would have. Children who flinch like that have been struck before, by someone who claimed to love them.

They stood there breathing at each other. Behind them, faintly, the river went on saying the only honest thing in the world.

"It's not—" Sparrow's voice splintered. "It's not what it looks like."

"No," Odile agreed quietly. "I don't think it is. I think it's worse. I think it's exactly what it looks like, plus a brother."

Sparrow's face did something complicated — guilt and fury and terror all trying to use the same muscles. "You don't know anything about me."

"I know you're sixteen. I know you eat like a starving thing and sleep with your boots on. I know you check the locks twice and sit facing the door." Odile set her cup down on a flat stone, deliberately, slowly, the way you move when a wild animal is deciding whether to bolt. "I grew up in a house like yours. I know what it does to a person. It teaches you that love is leverage and everyone has a price, including you. Especially you."

"Stop it."

"They have Denny."

The girl's whole body jerked at the name, and then she broke — not gradually, not gracefully, but all at once, the way ice lets go of a shoreline in spring. She slid down the slate until she was sitting in the gravel, the radio clutched to her sternum, and she cried the way children cry, horribly, silently, with her fist jammed against her mouth so no one at the fire would hear. Because even drowning, she was still managing their comfort. Still protecting them from the sound of her.

Odile lowered herself down beside her. Her knees complained. She ignored them.

"How long?" she asked.

Sparrow's answer came out mangled. "Since before the Narrows. Since before I ever signed on. Bell found me at the hiring docks — he knew my face, knew Denny's file. He said the Vane Syndicate doesn't chase runners, it just... holds them. Like a bank holds collateral." She wiped her nose on her sleeve. "Denny got caught skimming cargo manifests two years ago. He was working off a different debt, somebody else's debt, ours, my mother's — and they kept him. They said he could work it off in five years or buy it out, and we don't have that kind of money, nobody we know has that kind of money, and then Bell showed up and said all I had to do was one little job. One radio call a night. Coordinates. That's all. Just coordinates." Her laugh was wet and awful. "Just coordinates. Like it's not the same as putting the knife in someone's hand."

"It is the same," Odile said. "And it isn't. Both are true. That's how they build traps — they make sure every choice is wrong, so that whatever you pick, you pick the guilt yourself. Then they own you twice. Once with the debt, once with the shame."

Sparrow looked at her, eyes swollen, searching. "Why aren't you yelling?"

"Would it help?"

"...No."

"Then it stays in my pocket." Odile picked up a flat pebble and turned it over in her fingers. Below them, the dark water slid past, patient as arithmetic. "But hear me now, Sparrow, and hear me straight. Bell will not release your brother. Men like Bell do not spend collateral; they reinvest it. The moment you stop being useful, Denny becomes a liability, and liabilities get moved somewhere you'll never find them. Every coordinate you give him buys you another day and costs us one. There is no version of this where you trade well enough to win. I've navigated waters like that. There is no channel through."

"I know." The words scraped out of her. "I know, okay? I lie awake doing the math every single night and there's no version where it works, and I do it anyway, because the alternative is—" Her breath hitched. "The alternative is admitting I can't save him. And I'm the only one who's even trying."

That was the sentence that undid something in Odile's chest. Not the betrayal — betrayal she understood, betrayal was just fear wearing work clothes. But *I'm the only one who's even trying* — that was the loneliest arithmetic a person could carry, and Odile recognized it, because she had carried a version of it up this same river twenty years ago, in a different life, with a different crew, before the disaster taught her that carrying alone was how crews died.

"Theo lost someone to these waters," Odile said. "Her mentor. Two others. Twenty years ago, on the run above the source. Did you know that?"

Sparrow nodded miserably. "Jun told me. He told me not to ask her about it."

"He should have also told you why she never talks about it. Because the man who betrayed that expedition — the one who fed their route to the men who ambushed them — he wasn't paid either. He was leveraged. Family. Debt. The same architecture." Odile turned the pebble over once more, then skipped it out into the dark; they both listened for the splash and never heard it. "Theo Blackwood has spent twenty years hating a turncoat, and I promise you she has never once, not for a second, considered that he might have been a boy like you, crying into his fist by a river so no one would hear."

Sparrow went very still.

"So," Odile said, standing, joints cracking like kindling. "We're going to walk back to that fire, and you're going to tell them yourself. Before Bell calls back. Before it grows in the dark. Shame is like water that way — it only gets stronger when it pools."

"They'll put me off at the takeout. They should."

"Probably," Odile agreed. "But it isn't our decision, and it isn't yours. It's the crew's. That's the whole point of a crew, child. Nobody carries the math alone." She held out her hand. "Come on."

* * *

The fire had burned to embers, and the faces around it were ember-lit too, all of them turned toward Sparrow as she stood with her back to the river and told them everything — the docks, Bell's warm voice, the nightly coordinates, Denny's file, the offer of the takeout point. She told it badly and completely, which was the best way anyone tells such things. When she got to *you said you'd move him*, her voice failed entirely, and Marisol crossed the circle and sat down beside her without asking permission, the way Marisol did everything, and stayed there.

Silence held the camp for a long moment. Jun looked at his bandaged hands. Odile watched Theo.

Theo hadn't moved since the confession began. She sat with her forearms on her knees, staring into the coals, and Odile — who had navigated beside this woman for weeks now, who had charted her silences the way she charted channels — recognized the expression on her face. It wasn't anger. Anger would have been simpler. It was recognition. Somewhere behind those eyes, a younger Theo was looking at a boy on a dock twenty years ago, handing a folded paper to a stranger, and seeing, for the first time, the shape of the hand that had destroyed her life.

"The takeout point," Theo said finally. Her voice was rough, level, dangerous in its calm. "Did you give it to him?"

"No." Sparrow lifted her chin, and there was something fierce under the ruin of her, something that had survived worse than this. "I couldn't. Odile stopped me. But I was going to. I want you to understand that. I was going to give it to him, and I'd have given it tomorrow too, and the day after, as long as he kept saying Denny was fine. I'm not — I'm not sorry in the way you want me to be sorry. I'd do it again. He's my *brother*."

Something flickered across Theo's face — pain, yes, but underneath it, unmistakably, agreement.

"Good," Theo said.

Every head in the circle turned.

"If you'd apologized your way out of it, I'd have put you ashore myself." Theo leaned forward, and the firelight carved her face into planes and shadows. "Mercy that comes from pretending the sin was small isn't mercy. It's bookkeeping. What you did was real, and it nearly killed all of us, and Bell will try to use you again because that's what he's for. Do you understand what I'm telling you? I'm not forgiving you tonight. Forgiveness is downstream. Tonight I'm telling you that you're still aboard."

"Why?" Sparrow's voice cracked on it. "After what I—"

"Because twenty years ago a man sold my crew's route for the same reason you gave yours, and I let myself believe he was a monster, because monsters are easier to hate than frightened boys, and that belief cost me two decades and two graves." Theo's jaw tightened. "Bell engineered this. All of it. Maybe from the start. Which means the only thing he doesn't control on this river is what we do to each other. I'm not handing him that too."

Marisol snorted softly. "What the captain means is, nobody's perfect, and also she's a hypocrite, because she's been running from her own ghost since before this child was born."

"Thank you, Marisol."

"De nada."

Jun spoke up then, quiet, flexing his wrapped fingers. "For what it's worth — I say she stays. Not just mercy. Math. She knows Bell's operation from the inside. Frequencies, protocols, maybe codes. That's worth more than the risk." He glanced sideways at Sparrow, and something dry touched his mouth. "Also she took a third of my portage load without being asked, and I don't forget debts like that."

"She stays," Odile said simply. "My vote was cast the moment I followed her instead of reporting her."

All eyes went to Imogen, who had said nothing through any of it — Imogen, whose father's name sat unspoken at the center of this whole voyage like ballast below the waterline. She studied Sparrow for a long moment, and when she spoke, her voice was clipped and precise.

"My father spent the last year of his life writing down what the Syndicate does to people who owe them. Families held as collateral. Debts that grow interest faster than wages. He called it 'the ledger that eats children.'" She paused. "If I put you off this boat, I make his evidence true and useless at the same time. So. You stay. But hear me, Sparrow — if Bell calls that radio again, you'll answer with us standing behind you, and we'll decide together what he hears. No more solo runs. Agreed?"

Sparrow nodded, then looked around the circle — at all of them, really, as if counting them for the first time, as if checking whether they were real. "Agreed," she whispered. And then, because she was sixteen and honesty was still a raw new tool in her hands: "I thought you'd throw me off. I had a plan for it. Where I'd go, I mean. I always have a plan."

"We know," said Marisol. "You sleep with your boots on, child. We all noticed."

A ragged, surprised almost-laugh went around the fire, thin as new ice but real, and Odile felt the circle close — felt it happen physically, the way a formation closes around a breach. Not healed. Nowhere near healed. But bound. There was a difference, and the difference was everything.

Theo stood, kicked dirt over the embers, and looked upstream into the black.

"One more thing," she said. "Bell asked for the takeout point because he thinks we don't know he knows the route. Which means he's expecting us to run the gorge blind tomorrow, on the coordinates Sparrow gave him." A grim smile touched her mouth, there and gone. "So here's what we're going to do. We're going to give him exactly what he expects — right up until the moment it matters. Sparrow, tomorrow night, when he calls, you're going to feed him a false takeout, one bend east of the truth, into water shallow enough to strand his patrol. And then, come morning—"

She stopped.

Because that was when they all heard it — distant, arrhythmic, rolling up the valley like the world clearing its throat. Not thunder. Thunder repeated. Thunder with weight behind it, deep and glacial, a sound Odile had heard only once before and had prayed never to hear again this far south, this early.

Everyone turned toward the icefield above the tree line, invisible in the dark but suddenly, terribly present.

"That's early," Odile breathed. "That's weeks early."

Theo was already moving for the boat, waking the lanterns, her silhouette sharp against the last of the fire-glow.

"Calving season's opened," she said. "Nobody sleeps. We run tonight."
