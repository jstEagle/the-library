## 13. Calving Season Opens Early

The sound came at dusk, rolling down the valley like the sky had cracked open its knuckles.

Theo was on deck with a mug of Marisol's coffee going cold in her hands when it reached them — a deep, percussive boom that she felt in her sternum before she heard it, the kind of sound that didn't belong to weather. It came from upriver, from beyond the shoulder of granite where the Kestrel bent north into the high country, and it kept going, a long grinding rumble like a mountain clearing its throat.

Every head on the boat turned toward the sound. Nobody said anything for a full ten seconds.

"That's not thunder," Sparrow said finally, from her perch on the rail.

"No," Theo said. "It isn't."

She set the mug down and walked to the bow, one hand on the gunwale, and listened. The rumble faded, but underneath it — fainter, continuous, like something enormous breathing through its teeth — there was a new sound in the valley. Water. More water than there had been an hour ago. The river had a voice, and it had just changed pitch.

Odile emerged from the wheelhouse with her charts already unrolled against the bulkhead, pinning them flat with her forearm. She'd heard it too. Of course she had. Odile heard everything the river said; it was why Theo had hired her, back when hiring meant anything more than collecting debts of a different kind.

"Tell me I'm wrong," Theo said.

"You're not wrong." Odile's finger traced the channel upstream, past the Narrows they'd run two days ago, past the portage scars, to the wide blue smudge at the head of the map that the cartographers had labeled, with bureaucratic delicacy, *Provisional Glacial Lake — Survey Incomplete.* "That was ice letting go. Early. Weeks early."

"How early?"

"Calving season shouldn't open for another nineteen days." Odile looked up, and in the last grey light Theo could see what she almost never saw: fear, worn openly, like a watch face. "The meltwater's been running high since the icefield. I told myself it was a warm spring. It wasn't a warm spring. The glacier's been rotting from underneath, and we've been floating on its schedule this whole time without knowing it."

Jun came up the companionway wiping grease off his hands with a rag that had been clean three weeks ago. He'd been below decks all afternoon wrestling the port engine's fuel line, and he had the look of a man who had finally won an argument with machinery and did not trust the peace. "What's the noise?"

"Glacier's calving," Theo said. "Ahead of schedule."

Jun went still. Then, quietly, in the tone of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't want the answer to: "How much does the lake drop when a shelf goes?"

"Depends on the shelf," Odile said.

"And if it drops fast?"

"Then the river downstream gets a wave. And then it gets a season's worth of extra volume all at once, and every rapid between here and the source rewrites itself overnight."

Marisol appeared in the galley doorway, flour to her elbows, a wooden spoon still in her fist like a scepter. She'd been baking bread because bread was how she held the world together, and now she stood there holding the spoon and looking at the four of them gathered around the chart like mourners around a bed.

"Somebody say something good," she said. "Quickly. Before I start thinking."

Nobody said anything good.

* * *

They held council in the wheelhouse with the lamp turned low, because low lamps felt less like funerals. Imogen sat at the chart table with her arms crossed, and Theo watched her do the same math everyone else was doing, watched it land behind her eyes like ballast.

"The window's collapsing," Imogen said. No drama in it. That was the thing about Imogen Reyes that had infuriated Theo twenty years ago and that she now, grudgingly, respected: she delivered catastrophe the way other people delivered grocery lists. "We planned on nine days to the source lake. We have maybe three. Maybe two, if the shelf goes the way I think it's going to go."

"Two days means night running," Jun said.

"Night running means the Narrows again," Odile said. "In the dark. At flood stage."

"Night running means blind," Sparrow said, and then, catching the look on their faces, added quickly, "I'm not complaining. I'm inventorying."

"Inventory away," Theo said. "It's accurate."

She stood at the forward window with her back to the lamp, watching the last light die on the water. The river ran black now, shot through with the first pale threads of evening mist, and somewhere above the tree line, out of sight, a wall of ice older than every nation on the map was shedding itself into a lake that fed this water. She thought about her mentor — about Elias, about the journal page she'd burned at the put-in, about the promise neither of them had ever quite spoken aloud: *we don't go back up the Kestrel.* He'd sworn off this river because it had taken two of their crew, and here she was, twenty years later, considering running it blind at night because a dead man's evidence was worth more than the living crew's odds.

Except it wasn't the evidence anymore, was it. It was Imogen's father. It was Imogen's name, and her grief, and the twenty years of silence between them that had calcified into something that looked, from a distance, exactly like respect.

And it was Bell. Cassius Bell was somewhere behind them or ahead of them — probably ahead, by now, if he'd cut cross-country from the patrol station — and if the window closed before they reached the lake, Bell reached it alone, and everything they'd bled for on this river would amount to a very scenic way of losing.

"Options," Theo said, turning around. "Somebody give me options that don't end in a eulogy."

"We wait," Marisol said. "Daylight. We lose a day, we run the Narrows clean tomorrow morning, we make up time on the flats."

"If the shelf holds till tomorrow morning," Odile said.

"It might not," Imogen said. "When a glacier starts, it doesn't negotiate."

"Then we go now," Jun said. Everyone looked at him. He shrugged, uncomfortable being the voice of speed, being any voice at all. "I'm not saying it's smart. I'm saying if the river's going to change anyway, better we're on it while we still know its shape than after it forgets us. Every hour we sit here, the map gets less true."

"The map is already a liar," Odile murmured. "But he's right. It lies less right now than it will at dawn."

Theo looked at Sparrow. "Deckhand. You've got the youngest eyes and the least sense of your own mortality. How do you vote?"

Sparrow straightened up like she'd been called to the front of a classroom. Theo watched her weigh it — watched the kid understand, maybe for the first time, that the vote was real, that her word carried weight on this boat, that belonging meant your mistakes counted too.

"I vote we go," Sparrow said. "Because if we crash in the dark, at least we crash moving toward the thing. And because—" she hesitated, then committed, chin up— "because you're the one driving, and I've seen you drive."

A small laugh went around the wheelhouse, thin but real. Theo let herself have half a smile.

"That's either faith or poor judgment," she said.

"Both," said Marisol. "It's both. That's what family is." She untied her apron with the finality of a woman closing a shop. "I'll pack the galley. Nothing loose. If we're doing this, we're doing it fed."

* * *

They ran the first hour on the last of the light, which was to say they ran it on memory and nerve.

Theo took the helm herself. There was no question of anyone else. The Kestrel above the portage narrowed and quickened, braiding around gravel bars that the rising water had drowned into mere suggestions, and the boat answered her hands the way it always did — reluctantly at first, then wholly, as if it too had decided that if this was happening, it would happen properly.

Imogen stood at her shoulder with the spotlight, sweeping the banks in long arcs, calling hazards in a voice gone flat and professional. "Deadfall left. Big one, root wad the size of a truck. Mid-channel's clear past it." A pause. "Fence post. Somebody's fence. Twenty years underwater and it picks tonight to surface."

"Everything surfaces eventually," Theo said, and eased the bow left.

Behind them, the sound came again — that deep boom, closer now, or maybe just clearer in the dark, followed by a long white-noise rush that Odile, listening at the open window, identified grimly as ice meeting water. Each report sent a shiver through the river. Theo could feel it in the hull: a subtle swelling, a tightening of the current's grip, the water gathering itself like a runner in the blocks.

"Headlamps on," she said, and the deck bloomed with five points of light that swung and jostled and steadied.

Running rapids at night was a different sport entirely. By day you read the water — the V's of green going-down flow, the lines of white that marked rocks hiding just beneath, the smooth glassy tongues that lied about what waited below them. By night you read nothing. You read the sound of the water against the hull, the pull of the current on the rudder, the ghost-logic of a channel you'd memorized hours ago and prayed still existed. The headlamps showed them foam and darkness and foam again, and between the two, Theo drove on faith and fury.

The Narrows came at midnight.

She knew it before Odile called it — knew it by the walls, by the sudden compression of the echo, by the way the current leaned on the boat like a hand on her chest. The gorge swallowed their lights whole. Black rock rose on both sides, close enough to touch, and the river inside it had changed since they'd come up. Higher. Faster. Angrier. The standing waves that had been chest-high on the ascent were head-high now, and the hole at the bottom of the main drop — the one Odile had named *the Laundromat* on the way up, because it spun things until they were clean of everything including hope — had grown teeth.

"Talk to me," Theo said.

Odile braced in the doorway, one arm hooked through the frame, her face lit strobe-like by the bouncing headlamps. "Right side's gone. The eddy fence we used coming up is submerged — no eddy, no bail-out. It's the tongue or it's the wall."

"Left wall's undercut?"

"Was. At this level?" Odile shook her head slowly. "At this level, the left wall is a rumor."

So: the middle. The tongue. The one line, the honest line, the line that forgave nothing. Theo throttled up — Jun had rebuilt that engine's soul over three weeks of swearing, and she sent a silent thank-you down into the bilge — and aimed the boat at the narrow ribbon of smoother water threading between the boils.

"Hold on to something that loves you back," she called aft, and heard Sparrow's bright scared laugh, and heard Marisol answer it with something in Spanish that was definitely a blessing and possibly a threat.

The drop hit them like a decision made by someone else.

The bow rose, hung — that terrible weightless moment, the engine roaring at empty air — and then the world tilted and they were going down it, thirty feet of river folded into one screaming instant, spray sheeting over the wheelhouse windows in cold fists. Theo's hands moved without consulting her. Rudder left, throttle down, rudder right, reading the chaos through her palms, through the hull, through twenty years of rivers that had tried to kill her and mostly failed. The Laundromat yawned off the starboard quarter close enough to feel its suction tug at the transom, hungry, disappointed.

Then they were through. Below the drop the river spread out into a long dark chute, and the boat settled, and the crew made sounds — whoops, curses, Marisol declaring loudly that she had never doubted anyone, which fooled no one.

But Theo didn't celebrate. Because in the second after the drop, in the ringing quiet under the adrenaline, she heard it again: the boom. Closer. Unmistakably closer. And beneath it, new, a sound that raised every hair on her body — a long, sustained crack, like the icefield was unzipping.

"Odile," she said quietly. "How far to the lake?"

"At this pace? Fourteen hours. Fifteen."

"And how long has that shelf got?"

Odile was silent for a moment. When she answered, her voice had gone very steady, the way voices do when steadiness is a choice rather than a condition. "I don't know. But I think the glacier is answering that question faster than we are."

* * *

They ran until the fuel needed resting and the people needed it more, and even then they only stopped because Jun mutinied — gently, in his way, appearing at Theo's elbow with coffee and the observation that a dead-at-the-wheel captain saved nobody. They tied off in a wide eddy below a gravel fan, engines ticking as they cooled, and the crew ate Marisol's bread and beans in the red glow of a shielded lantern, passing food in silence, the good tired silence of people who have trusted each other with their lives and been repaid.

Theo sat on the bow hatch with her back against the mast step, mug cradled in both hands, and watched the northern sky pulse faintly above the black silhouette of the peaks. Somewhere up there, past the gorge, past the flats, past the last miles of river that climbed into stone, the source lake lay under its wall of ice, and the wreck sat in the lake, and the truth sat in the wreck, and the glacier was coming down to meet all of it.

Imogen climbed up and sat beside her. Neither spoke for a while. The river talked enough for both of them — louder now, insistent, a full octave above yesterday's voice.

"My father used to say the mountains keep time differently," Imogen said at last. "That they don't care about our schedules, so the only sin is arriving unprepared." She turned the tin cup in her fingers. "He was wrong, you know. About that. The mountains keep perfect time. It's us who show up late and call it fate."

"He sounds like someone I'd have argued with."

"You'd have liked him." A pause. "Everyone did. That's partly why it worked — why they killed him and called it an accident. Nobody looks hard at a death everyone agrees was sad but unsurprising. Reckless man, reckless river." Her jaw tightened. "Twenty years of people telling me my father died the way he lived. And the proof that they murdered him has been sitting in sixty feet of meltwater the whole time, waiting for someone desperate enough to go get it."

"Desperate's the family business," Theo said.

Imogen huffed — almost a laugh. Then, quieter: "If we miss it, Theo. If the shelf goes before we get there and takes the lake floor with it—" She didn't finish. She didn't have to. Everyone on this boat knew what a draining glacial lake did to a valley: it emptied itself in hours, a moving ocean with a grudge, and anything in its path — wreckage, evidence, men in Syndicate camps — went downstream as confetti.

"Then we don't miss it," Theo said.

"That's not a plan."

"It's the only part of the plan I'm sure of. The rest is navigation." She drained the coffee, cold now, bitter, perfect. "Get some sleep, Reyes. I need your eyes at dawn, and I need them angry."

Imogen slid down off the hatch and paused, one foot on the ladder. "For what it's worth," she said, not looking at her, "the way you ran the Narrows tonight. Elias would have—" She stopped. Started over. "It was good piloting, Blackwood."

Then she was gone below, and Theo sat alone in the dark with the compliment sitting in her chest like a coal, warmer than it had any right to be.

* * *

They cast off at 3 a.m., and the river had become a stranger.

The rise was visible now even by headlamp — willow tops drowned along the banks, gravel bars erased, driftwood riding past them at a walking pace, then a jogging pace, then faster. The current had thickened. That was the only word Theo had for it: the water moved like something with mass and intention, and the boat surfed it, and every eddy they grabbed to check their line was smaller than the last.

Hour four. Hour five. The sky went from black to iron to a bruised violet, and still the booms came, nearer each time, and the intervals between them shortened like a heartbeat climbing toward something.

It was Sparrow, up on the foredeck with binoculars she barely needed, who saw it first.

"Captain." Her voice came back strange — stripped of everything young in it. "Captain, you need to look at the horizon."

Theo killed the throttle and let the boat swing broadside in the flood, and stood, and looked north.

Above the notch in the peaks where the valley ended, where the glacier lived, the sky was glowing. Not dawn — dawn was still an hour west of them and the wrong color anyway. This was a pale, flickering luminescence hanging over the ice, pulsing softly, and it took Theo's mind a long, refusing moment to assemble what her eyes were telling her: that the glacier was throwing up a plume of ice-dust fine enough to catch the first light, a halo of itself, a crown of pulverized mountain.

As she watched, the horizon flashed white — a silent bloom, because sound lagged distance — and then, seconds later, the boom arrived, the biggest yet, and with it a tremor she felt through the soles of her boots.

And then the river spoke.

Not rose. *Spoke.* A single convulsive surge lifted the boat as if a hand had slapped the hull from below, dropped it, lifted it again, and downstream — she could hear it, a low continuous roar building like a freight train still over the horizon — the flood pulse was coming. The shelf hadn't just calved. It had begun to fail, and the lake was letting go in installments, and every installment was headed straight down the throat of the Kestrel.

"Odile!" Theo was already at the wheel, already throttling up, because there was exactly one direction to go and it was up. "Distance to the lake mouth?"

"Eleven miles! Nine, if the current keeps giving!"

"Then we take the gift." She looked back once — at Jun braced in the engine hatch, at Marisol lashing the galley like a woman battening for a hurricane, at Sparrow gripping the rail with white knuckles and a grin that had no fear left in it, only ferocity, at Odile with her charts and her calm and her terrible arithmetic, at Imogen standing at the bow pointing north toward her father's name frozen in the ice.

"Everybody listen," Theo called over the roar. "The window isn't closing. The window is *gone*. What's left is a door, and we go through it now, or the river closes it for us. Eleven miles. One wreck. One chance. Full throttle — and nobody, *nobody*, touches anything without calling it first."

The engine answered like it had been waiting all night for permission.

The boat surged north into the flooding dark, chasing the thunder to its source, and far ahead, above the glowing ice, the morning began — whether for them or over them, the river had not yet said.
