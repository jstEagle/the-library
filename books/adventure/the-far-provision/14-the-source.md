## 14. The Source

The river stopped being a river an hour before they arrived.

Imogen felt it in the hull first — the current thinning beneath them like a pulse going quiet, the water turning glassy and green-black with rock flour, so cold it seemed to drink heat straight through the plating. Above the tree line now, above everything, the channel widened into a corridor of stone and gravel bar, and at the end of it rose the wall.

She had seen photographs of the Kestrel Glacier. Her father had taken most of them, back when he still believed the world would listen to a man with a camera and a ledger. The photographs had not prepared her. Nothing prepared you for a wall of ice four hundred feet high, blue at its heart like something drowned, streaked with gray moraine and old ash, fissured everywhere with veins of meltwater that caught the low sun and burned. It did not look like scenery. It looked like a verdict.

"Source lake," Odile said from the bow, not looking up from her charts. "Half a mile across. Depth unknown. Officially, it doesn't exist."

"Officially," Theo said, "neither do we."

They came around the last bend slow, engine idling, and Imogen stood at the rail with her father's field notebook open on her palm — the one she'd carried for twenty years without ever once opening it on the water — and let herself look at the place where he had died.

Not here. Not exactly. The wreck had gone down in the narrows below, in the flood year, and the glacier had spent two decades doing what glaciers do: taking things apart and hiding the pieces. But the survey coordinates in his notebook ended here, at this lake, because this was where the 2004 expedition had staged its final camp. This was where the canister was supposed to go if anything went wrong. *If we don't come back down,* her father had written, in handwriting she knew better than her own, *the provision is at the source. Tell no one. Especially not Vane.*

*Especially not Vane.* She had been nineteen years old and furious at him then — furious that he cared more about ledgers than about her, more about strangers' stolen wages than his own daughter's graduation. She had told Cassius Bell's predecessor, in a moment of teenage spite, that her father was planning something. She had never known if that small betrayal had traveled upstream to become the storm that caught him. She suspected. She had suspected for twenty years, and the suspicion had grown teeth.

And now she was bringing his provision out of the ice with a crew of strays and a rival who hated her, while the man who might have killed him waited somewhere behind them on the water.

"There." Sparrow's voice cracked high from the crow's perch. "Starboard shore. That's — that's a camp."

Imogen raised the binoculars and her stomach dropped through the hull.

Three dome tents, khaki and new. A generator humming a thin note across the water. A Zodiac pulled up on the gravel, and beside it — her breath went shallow — a helicopter skid, folded and tarped, the kind that could be reassembled in forty minutes by two men who knew what they were doing. And figures. Four, maybe five, moving unhurried among the tents. Unhurried the way people are unhurried when they have been waiting a long time and know the thing they're waiting for is coming.

One of them raised an arm. Not a weapon. A greeting.

"Cassius Bell," Theo said quietly, beside her. Imogen hadn't heard her cross the deck. "That's his wave. He used to do that at regattas. Like he's welcoming you to a party you didn't know you were attending."

"You know him."

"I owe him my life in installments." Theo's jaw worked. "He's the Syndicate's smile. I should have known. I *did* know. I just didn't want to know that knowing him and being known by him were the same trap."

Odile had gone very still over her charts. "There's no way they beat us here. Not up the Narrows, not through the portage. Unless—" She looked up. "Unless they never came up the river at all. Helicopter staging. They flew the heavy gear in over the icefield and ran the boats light from below."

"They've been here for days," Jun said from the engine well, wiping his hands on a rag that had given up being clean. "Generator's been running long enough to warm the exhaust. Days."

Which meant Bell had known the route. Which meant Bell had known the route before Imogen had ever written her telegram to Theo Blackwood, and the cold that settled over Imogen then had nothing to do with the glacier.

She had planned every mile of this run. She had memorized the survey marks, bribed the fuel dock, cut the manifests. And somewhere in all of it, someone had been reading over her shoulder — or worse, she had been following a route drawn for her, baited for her, the way her father had been.

"Theo," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "When Bell offered to clear your debt—"

"He didn't offer. Not yet." Theo was watching the camp through her own binoculars, and her face had gone into the flat, unreadable mask Imogen remembered from twenty years ago, from the one season they'd raced against each other and Theo had lost everything but never once let her see it land. "But he will. On this beach, in front of everyone, he'll make me an offer designed so that refusing it costs me everything I love. That's how he works. He doesn't break people. He hands them the knife and calls it a choice."

"And what do we do?"

Theo lowered the binoculars. For a moment — just a moment — the mask slipped, and underneath it Imogen saw something she had spent two decades telling herself wasn't there: fear, yes, but under the fear, a kind of terrible clarity. The look of a woman who has already done the arithmetic and doesn't like the answer but refuses to lie about it.

"We land," Theo said. "Because if we run, he takes the lake, and your father stays buried twice. And because I want to hear what he thinks I'm worth."

* * *

They grounded the boat on the eastern shingle, fifty yards from the camp, close enough to be polite and far enough to matter. Imogen watched her crew do it — watched Jun kill the engine and check the bilge twice, watched Odile mark their position on three separate maps as though paper could hold them here, watched Sparrow coil lines with hands that weren't quite steady, watched Marisol stand at the rail with her arms crossed like a wall built of a woman.

Five against five. Except Bell's five had a helicopter, rifles slung with casual theatricality, and the patience of men on salary. Her five had a teenager, a cook, a ruined engineer, a navigator who talked to maps, and Theo Blackwood, who owed the devil money.

Bell came down to the waterline alone.

That was the performance, Imogen understood immediately. The others stayed back among the tents, visible, armed, relaxed. Bell walked the fifty yards of gravel with his hands loose at his sides and his coat open and a smile already built, and he moved like a man who had never once in his life needed to hurry, because the world always hurried toward him eventually.

"Cassius Bell," he said, stopping a respectful six feet away. His voice was warm gravel, a voice made for negotiating over good whiskey. "Ms. Reyes. And Theo Blackwood. You have no idea how long I've hoped to get the two of you in the same room. Or," he glanced around at the blue wall of the glacier, the dead-green water, the silence pressing in from every side, "the same cathedral."

"This isn't a negotiation," Imogen said.

"Everything is a negotiation. Most people just don't realize they're holding currency until someone polite points it out." He turned to Theo, and his smile deepened by a degree, became something almost tender, which was the most frightening thing about him. "Theo. Twenty years I've watched you run numbers in your head at night — I could see your lips move through the wall of that flophouse on Delancey. Miles to dollars. Dollars to days. You measure your whole life in distance covered against debt accrued. So let me save you the arithmetic."

He reached inside his coat, and half her crew tensed, and what he drew out was not a gun but a slim leather folio, which he held up between two fingers like a card trick.

"One document. Signed, sealed, filed with Vane legal before sundown. Your debt — all of it, principal, interest, penalties, the whole ugly sum — erased. Not deferred. Erased. You walk off this beach owing nothing to anyone, for the first time since you were twenty-two years old." He let that sit. He was very good at letting things sit. "In exchange, Ms. Reyes surrenders the canister. Contents unexamined. We fly out together, civilized as churchgoers, and nobody has to learn what the calving schedule looks like from the wrong side of it."

The wind came off the glacier and moved the water in long silver wrinkles, and Imogen watched Theo's face while Bell watched it too, and she understood that this was the real theater — not the folio, not the rifles, but this: watching a drowning woman being offered air.

She saw it land. Of course she saw it. She'd have had to be blind not to. Something moved behind Theo's eyes like a fish under ice — the whole shape of a life passing through: the flophouse, the collection notices, the years of sleeping in the truck, the way she flinched at the sound of telephones. Twenty years of miles. One signature to stop the odometer.

"That's it?" Theo said at last. Her voice was even. Too even. "I hand over the evidence, and Vane just — lets me go? Lets all of us go?"

"I'm authorized to extend the same courtesy to your crew." Bell spread his hands, magnanimous, a man bestowing weather. "Jun Park's medical debts. Odile's outstanding warrants — all three jurisdictions. Even young Sparrow's brother, who would find himself on a bus home by Thursday." His gaze slid, gentle as a scalpel, to where Sparrow stood frozen by the rail. "Everyone goes home whole. That's the entire price. A piece of metal full of dead man's paperwork. Paper doesn't grieve, Ms. Blackwood. People do."

And there it was. Imogen felt her own hands curl at her sides. He wasn't just buying the canister. He was pricing each of them individually, aloud, in front of each other — making the crew into collateral, making mercy itself the merchandise. Making Theo responsible, if she refused, for every consequence that followed. It was elegant. It was obscene. It was exactly how the Syndicate had operated for thirty years, and how her father had died trying to prove it.

"Bell," Imogen said, stepping forward, "you're wasting your breath. She won't take it."

"Ms. Reyes." He turned to her with genuine regret, and that was the worst part, that the regret looked real. "With respect — you've known this woman twenty years and hated her for nineteen of them. I've known her four and owned her for three. Allow me a professional's assessment." He looked back at Theo. "You're not thinking about the canister, are you? You're thinking about the number. What zero feels like. Waking up tomorrow and owing nothing to anyone. Go ahead. Say it out loud. Nobody here will judge you. God knows I wouldn't."

The silence stretched. The glacier ticked and groaned somewhere in its depths, ice settling against ice, patient as geology.

Imogen wanted to speak. She wanted to grab Theo's arm and say *don't*, and she made herself not do it, because she understood suddenly — with the awful clarity of a woman watching her own history repeat in someone else's face — that a rescue offered too fast becomes just another debt. Whatever Theo chose, it had to be chosen with no one's hand on her. That was the only gift Imogen had left to give her.

So she stood in the wind and waited, and hated waiting, and waited anyway.

Theo looked at the folio for a long time. Then she looked past it — past Bell, past the tents, past the tarped helicopter — at the glacier wall itself, at the blue heart of the ice where the light pooled like something kept.

"My mentor had a saying," Theo said finally. "Miles aren't a life. They're just how you count the running." She exhaled, slow, and Imogen watched twenty years leave her body on that breath, or begin to. "You're right, Cassius. I thought about the number. I thought about nothing else for three years. And then I spent two weeks on a river with people who feed each other, and I'll tell you what I learned."

She stepped closer to him, close enough that the crew behind her collectively forgot to breathe.

"Zero isn't freedom if you buy it with somebody else's name. My debt dies today or it doesn't. But it dies *mine* — not traded, not laundered, not signed over in a tent on a beach where a dead man's daughter is standing right here listening to you auction off her father's corpse." Her voice didn't rise. That was what made it terrifying. "No deal."

Something flickered across Bell's face — disappointment, yes, but underneath it, briefly, something colder and more satisfied, like a man whose trap has finally been stepped in. And Imogen saw it, and her blood went to ice, because satisfaction was the wrong expression. Satisfaction meant the offer had never really been an offer. It meant the refusal was also part of the design.

"Well," Bell said softly, folding the folio away. "I did hope you'd surprise me."

Behind him, unhurried, the four men among the tents began to walk down toward the waterline. And on the far side of the lake, so faint it might have been thunder, so near it might have been a heartbeat, the glacier spoke — a long, deep crack rolling across the water like the sound of a door beginning, very slowly, to open.

Bell glanced up at the ice wall, then back at them, and smiled the way a man smiles at a schedule arriving early.

"I'd suggest we all talk quickly," he said. "The mountain, as it turns out, keeps its own ledger. And it's calling in debts too."
