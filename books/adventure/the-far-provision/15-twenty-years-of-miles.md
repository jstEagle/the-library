## 15. Twenty Years of Miles

The lake at the foot of the glacier was the color of a bruise — that deep, milky turquoise of rock flour suspended in meltwater, so opaque it looked like the earth had been bleeding for ten thousand years and never told anyone. The *Kestrel's Daughter* sat low against the shingle beach where they'd run her aground, her hull scarred from the last set of rapids, and above it all rose the ice wall: three hundred feet of blue-green glass veined with gray, groaning softly to itself like an old man talking in his sleep.

And on the shore, forty yards from the wreck they had crossed a wilderness to find, stood Cassius Bell with six armed men and a smile like a handshake you couldn't withdraw from.

"No," Theo said.

Bell tilted his head, as if he hadn't quite heard, as if the word were a foreign coin he wanted to examine in better light. The wind came off the glacier and flattened the grass along the shingle, carried the smell of cold stone and old ice. Somewhere behind Theo, she heard Jun shift his weight on the gravel, heard Sparrow's breath go shallow and quick.

"I'm sorry," Bell said pleasantly. "I thought I'd offered you something extraordinary."

"You offered me a leash with a longer rope." Theo kept her voice level. Twenty years of reading rivers had taught her that panic was just bad information delivered loudly, and she refused to give this man good information. "You want me to hand over the canister, walk away clean, debt erased, no hard feelings. And then what? You burn it. You burn him twice." She nodded toward the wreck half-submerged in the shallows, the tail fin of the 2004 expedition plane breaking the water like a shark's dorsal, furred with twenty years of silt and moss. "Elias Vane put my mentor in the ground with his name in the mud. You think I rowed two hundred miles of river that killed my family so you could keep the ledger balanced?"

Bell sighed — genuinely, theatrically, the sigh of a man burdened by other people's stubbornness. He was dressed absurdly well for the source of the Kestrel: waxed canvas jacket, leather gloves, boots that had never known a portage. Behind him his men stood in a loose crescent, rifles slung but hands near them, and Theo counted without wanting to — six, spread wide enough to cover the boat, the wreck, and the treeline where Odile had vanished five minutes ago with the flare gun and Theo's silent nod.

"Here is what fascinates me about you, Blackwood," Bell said, taking a step forward onto the shingle, boots crunching. "You people who chase storms. You who run illegal water. You always believe you're the ones taking the risk. That danger is something you seek out, something you choose." Another step. "But risk isn't a choice. It's a current. And you were in it long before you ever picked up a paddle."

"Theo," Imogen said quietly, at her shoulder. "The canister—"

"Is not yours to negotiate either," Theo said, without turning. "You hired me to salvage it. Salvage means it comes out of the water. It doesn't mean it goes into his pocket."

"Ms. Reyes knows why she's really here," Bell said mildly. "Don't you, Imogen?"

Theo felt Imogen go still beside her. Not guilty-still. Braced-still, the stillness of someone who has already decided how much truth to spend and when.

"You fed her the route," Theo said. Her eyes stayed on Bell. "The coordinates. The window before calving season. That telegram didn't come out of nowhere — you let it come out of nowhere. You wanted us on this river."

"I wanted *you* on this river," Bell corrected. "Reyes was the bait because bait has to be believable. A stranger's offer, you'd have burned it unread. But your old rival, asking for exactly the thing you swore off—" he spread his gloved hands, "—well. Pride is the best lure there is. Second only to grief."

The words landed in Theo's chest like ballast dropped into still water. She thought of the dockside bar, the lamplight, Imogen sliding the chart across the table and saying *I know about the promise, and I'm asking anyway.* She had thought, even then, even through the anger, *this isn't like Imogen.* Imogen was ruthless but she wasn't careless, and this job had been careless in every particular — the timing too tight, the crew too green, the route threaded past a Syndicate patrol station as if nobody had checked.

Because nobody needed to check. Because the patrol boat in Chapter Eight — Bell's Shadow, Marisol had named it, and they'd all laughed, and it hadn't been funny — had turned away too easily. Had *let* them pass.

"You've been herding us since the Narrows," Theo said.

"I've been patient with you since the Narrows," Bell said. "There's a difference. Herding implies I knew where you'd break. I only knew where you'd end up." He gestured at the lake, the wall of ice, the drowned plane. "Everything drains here, Ms. Blackwood. Rivers, debts, secrets. Even you."

* * *

It happened fast after that, the way disasters do — not building but arriving, fully formed, the way a supercell goes from towering cumulus to rotating wall cloud while you're still arguing about whether to turn back.

"Take them," Bell said.

Two of his men moved for the boat. Jun stepped in front of the gangway with his ruined hands half-raised, useless and brave, and got a rifle stock across the temple for his trouble — not hard enough to break anything, hard enough to make a point. Sparrow screamed and Marisol's arm came around her like a boom vang, hauling her back, and then it was done, the way these things are done: four of his men aboard the *Daughter*, weapons loose, faces bored, and the crew of strays standing on the shingle with their hands visible and their futures suddenly very small.

Odile was still in the trees. Theo held that fact the way you hold a knife underwater — carefully, pointed away from yourself.

Bell walked the beach while his men lashed Imogen's wrists with zip ties, unhurried, a man inspecting property he already owned. He stopped at the waterline and looked out at the wreck with something almost like reverence.

"Do you know what I admire about Elias Vane?" he said. "He understood that evidence is just weight. It sits somewhere until someone moves it. His mistake — the firm's mistake — was leaving this much weight sitting in one place for twenty years." He turned back to her. "A whistleblower dies, the story dies with him, except the proof doesn't. Proof is patient. Proof waits in a lake at the top of the world for someone foolish or desperate enough to come get it. All we had to do was wait for the current to bring it to us."

"And if we'd sunk in the Narrows?" Theo asked. "If the calving season had closed early? If we'd died on the portage like—"

"Like the 2004 expedition?" Bell smiled again, and this time it reached his eyes, which was infinitely worse. "Ms. Blackwood. Who do you think built that expedition?"

* * *

The world went very quiet. Not silent — the glacier was still groaning, the water still working the shingle, Sparrow still crying quietly by the boat — but inside Theo's skull everything dropped away except the sentence, hanging in the cold air like ice fog.

"What did you say?"

Bell studied her face with the frank interest of a man watching a gauge climb. "You really don't know. Twenty years, and you never once asked who chartered the flight. Who insured the cargo. Who hired a pilot with a suspended license and a drinking problem and put him over the worst approach in the range." He shook his head slowly. "Your mentor took the blame — the inquiry found pilot error, found the charter company negligent, found everyone negligent except the people who mattered. And the man who organized it, the man who chose the route and the weather window and the crew—" he tapped his own chest, once, "—was promoted. Twice."

"You're lying."

"I'm confessing," Bell said, and laughed, short and genuine. "That's the joke, Ms. Blackwood. There's no crime in it anymore. Statutes expire. Witnesses scatter. The only thing left of that disaster worth anything is sitting in that canister, and the only person alive who cares enough to die for it is standing right in front of me, refusing a perfectly reasonable offer." He spread his hands. "History doesn't repeat. But it rhymes. And I am a great admirer of rhyme."

And Theo saw it — saw it whole, the way you see a rapid from the scout ledge, the whole architecture of it laid out below you at once. The 2004 expedition had been built the same way. A crew assembled from people who owed something or ran from something. A route chosen to be barely survivable. A prize placed at the far end, past the point of no return, so that everyone committed would keep going forward rather than back. Her mentor had called it the far provision — the extra fuel, the spare part, the margin you carry for the day everything fails. Vane's expeditions had none. They weren't expeditions. They were filters. Machines for finding out who would survive, and what they'd be willing to carry afterward.

And she had walked into the new one with her eyes open, because the lure had her name on it.

Her legs nearly went. She locked them. Thought of Jun's hands, wrapped and ruined from the portage. Of Sparrow radioing coordinates to save her brother, and the crew voting to keep her anyway. Of Odile in the treeline with a flare gun and no orders except the look Theo had given her, which meant *wait*, which meant *there will be a moment, be ready for it.*

"You need me," Theo said. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "The canister's sealed. Pressure-locked, marine-rated. Your men crack it wrong and the drives fry — saltwater gel packs rupture, everything inside corrodes in an hour. You've got divers down there right now pulling up twenty years of mud and a dead man's luggage, and none of it matters without the canister, and the canister doesn't open without me."

Bell's smile thinned by a degree. "Jun Park could open it."

"Jun Park could open it," Theo agreed. "In a workshop. With tools. In three days, maybe four, if his hands worked, which they don't, because your employer's river ate them. And in three days the calving season opens and this whole valley becomes a flood channel and every drive in that canister ends up in the Pacific." She took a breath. "So here's my counteroffer, Cassius. You take the cuffs off my crew. You put down the guns. And I open the canister — for everyone. Not for you. For the record. For the news services, the regulators, the families of everyone your firm buried, starting with a pilot named Reyes and a deckhand named Okafor and a mentor whose name you never bothered to learn."

Something moved behind Bell's eyes. Not fear. Recalculation.

"You'd trade your debt," he said slowly, "your clean slate, your life — for a headline."

"I'd trade it for the truth," Theo said. "The payout was always somebody else's currency. I'm done spending in it."

For a long moment the only sound was the glacier, groaning, shifting its ancient weight. Then Bell exhaled through his nose, almost regretful, and nodded to his men.

"Search the boat," he said. "All of it. If the canister's aboard, we don't need her cooperation at all — we need her *absence*. And Ms. Blackwood—" he turned back to her, and the warmth was gone now, replaced by something flat and administrative, the face of the ledger itself, "—the offer expired when you refused it. What happens next is just weather."

And that was when Theo heard it.

Not the glacier's usual groan — its *crack*. A sound like the sky splitting along a seam, rolling down the valley and echoing off the walls, followed by a second sound underneath it, deeper, wetter: the lake itself moving. The water at the wreck's tail fin shivered. Ripples crossed the shallows against the wind.

Sparrow looked up first. She was fourteen and she'd grown up on this water's cousins, and her face went white in a way that had nothing to do with the guns.

"That's not thunder," she whispered.

High on the ice wall, three hundred feet up, a seam of blue light opened like an eye.
