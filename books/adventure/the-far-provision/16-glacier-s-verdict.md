## 16. Glacier's Verdict

Sparrow had learned to read men like weather, and Cassius Bell's crew read like a barometer falling fast.

She'd spent two days among them now — long enough to learn their rhythms, their laziness, the particular way they checked their weapons without checking the treeline. Bell's people were accountants with guns. They counted everything: fuel drums, rope coils, minutes between radio check-ins with the patrol boat downstream. They counted Sparrow too, but they counted her as nothing, a scrawny kid in an oversized flotation coat who cried when they zip-tied her wrists behind her back and shoved her onto a supply crate beside the others.

The crying was real. The rest was arithmetic.

Theo was lashed to the landing skid of the wrecked expedition plane, twenty feet away, close enough that Sparrow could see the blood drying at her hairline where one of Bell's men had introduced himself with a rifle butt. Jun sat slumped against a fuel drum, his ruined hands cradled in his lap like something already buried. Odile was bound to Marisol back-to-back, the cook's bulk the only thing keeping the navigator upright — Odile hadn't stopped shivering since they'd dragged her out of the water. Imogen Reyes knelt apart from all of them, alone, because Bell liked it that way. He'd been talking to her for an hour in a low, reasonable voice about fathers and legacies, and Imogen hadn't said a single word back, which frightened Sparrow more than the guns did.

And above all of it — above the camp, the plane, the hostages, the whole frozen cathedral of the source lake — hung the glacier wall.

It was wrong. That was the thing nobody else seemed to hear. Sparrow had grown up on water that talked, on tidal flats and river ice and the groaning hulls of boats she wasn't supposed to be aboard, and she knew what a glacier sounded like when it was thinking. This one wasn't thinking. It was *deciding*. A deep, tectonic mutter ran through it every few minutes, longer each time, like a held breath stretching past what lungs could bear. The calving season had come early. Everyone on this shore was standing inside the season's open mouth.

Bell stood at the waterline with the evidence canister at his feet, speaking into a satellite phone. "No. I have it. All of it." A pause. His smile did the thing it did when he wanted to be believed. "Because I don't leave loose ends on rivers, Mr. Vane."

Sparrow looked down at her wrists.

The zip ties were industrial grade, the kind Bell's crews used on cargo pallets — she'd watched them load a thousand of them at the Syndicate depot in Corvane Bay, back when she was eleven and small enough to crawl through the gaps between shipping containers, back before her brother told her to stop coming around, back before she understood that the man who fed them both was the same man who owned them. She knew these ties. She knew their weight, their teeth, and most importantly she knew that Bell's warehouse stock was old — recalled twice for brittleness, never pulled from service because pulling stock cost money and Vane didn't spend money on things that might matter later.

Everything about the Vane Syndicate was brittle, if you knew where to look. Sparrow had made a life out of knowing where to look.

The problem was the guard. There was only one on this side of the camp — a heavyset man called Ferro who chewed tobacco and kept his rifle slung rather than shouldered, because he was bored, and boredom was the most dangerous gift a hostage could be given. Ferro paced a lazy circuit along the crates. Every fourth pass, he stopped near Sparrow and looked at the glacier instead of at her, because the glacier was louder than anything a skinny deckhand could possibly do.

Every fourth pass. Sparrow had counted nine circuits. She could set a clock by him.

Above the lake, the light was doing something terrible and beautiful. The sun had come through a tear in the storm clouds and struck the ice wall full-on, and the whole face of it lit up blue — not the blue of sky or water but the blue of deep places, of pressure and age, a blue that seemed to come from inside the world. It would have been the most beautiful thing Sparrow had ever seen if it weren't also the last thing some of them might ever see.

The glacier muttered again. Longer this time. Somewhere high on the face, a crack propagated with a sound like a rifle shot, then another, stitching down through three hundred feet of ancient ice.

Ferro stopped pacing. Even he felt it.

*Now,* Sparrow thought. Not because the timing was perfect — because there was no such thing as perfect timing anymore, only timing that came before the mountain finished making up its mind.

She twisted her wrists against each other and snapped the left tie on the third try, the plastic parting with a whisper she covered by coughing. The right one took five tries and skin off both thumbs. Then she was free, crouched on the crate, and the whole plan — the plan she'd been building in her head since the moment Bell's men put her down here, the plan built entirely out of other people's carelessness — lay open in front of her like a chart.

Step one: Odile's knife. It lived in a sheath sewn into the lining of her left boot, because Odile trusted no boat, no crew, and no universe. Sparrow crossed the six feet between crates while Ferro's back was turned, dropped to her knees behind Odile, and worked the sheath open with fingers that wouldn't quite stop shaking.

Odile went rigid, then very deliberately relaxed, which was its own kind of message: *I know it's you. Do it.*

The knife was small and viciously sharp. Sparrow sawed through Marisol's bindings first — the cook's arms were the strongest thing on this beach and everyone knew it — then Odile's. Neither of them looked at her. Looking would kill them all.

"Slow," Odile breathed, barely a sound. "Cut Jun. Get him to the skiff."

But Sparrow was already shaking her head, and she was already moving toward the thing that made her stomach turn over — toward the camp's edge, where Bell's gear lay stacked under a tarp: the radios, the flares, and the crate of detonators.

Because here was the truth Sparrow had carried like a stone in her chest since Odile caught her at the radio, since the crew voted with raised hands to keep her aboard when mercy would have been easier and smarter: she knew Bell's operation from the inside. She knew the depot codes and the patrol schedules and the way Bell always, *always*, rigged his camps with demolition charges — not to use, but to threaten. Scorch-the-evidence insurance. Two sticks of shaped charge wired to the wrecked plane's fuel cell, armed by a key switch in Bell's breast pocket, so that if anyone ever came to take what he'd taken, he could turn twenty years of proof into smoke and a crater.

She'd seen the wiring spool in his kit on the first night. She'd said nothing, because saying nothing was the habit of a lifetime.

The charges were still wired. She could see the det cord snaking from beneath the tarp across the ice to the plane's flank, pale yellow against the white, thin as a lie.

Which meant the charges could be *un*wired. Or better — repurposed.

The glacier cracked again, a long tearing groan, and a shard the size of a church fell from the upper face and hit the icefield below with a boom that rolled across the lake like God clearing his throat. The report echoed off the walls of the valley. Under its cover, Sparrow reached the tarp, lifted it, and found the detonator box exactly where Bell's crews always left it: clipped shut, keyed to the master switch, one red light blinking patient and stupid.

Her hands knew this box. Her hands had dusted around this box in three depots, had been smacked away from it by men who laughed, had memorized it out of pure spite. The arming harness was Bell-standard: master switch, channel selector, fire button. And the disconnect — the thing the manuals called the abort loop and the field crews called the widow's clip — was a simple spring collar on the firing line, designed so a nervous technician could pull the charge off-line without cutting wire.

Nobody ever used it. Nobody ever checked it. But it was there, because regulations were the one god the Syndicate half-believed in.

Sparrow popped the clip on the line running to the plane. The red light died. Twenty years of whistleblower's evidence, sitting in a canister ten feet from Bell's polished boots, was suddenly just metal and paper again — mortal, but not murdered.

Then she did the thing she'd been afraid to even think, the thing that made her feel like her brother's sister after all, like someone worth the vote they'd taken on her behalf.

She moved the det cord.

There was a second charge — Bell always packed a spare — coiled in the crate, unfired, unplaced. Sparrow dragged it out by its tail of yellow cord, low, behind the tarp, and paid the line out along the ground toward the ice shelf at the lake's outlet, where the meltwater was already running fast and brown with rock flour, where the draining had begun. She didn't know demolitions. But she knew that Bell's spare charge was waterproofed, she knew the firing box had a test tone, and she knew — from a hundred nights listening to men talk over cards — that a shaped charge set at the toe of an already-failing ice shelf would do what avalanches do when you shout at them.

She didn't want to bring the glacier down.

She wanted to give it an excuse.

* * *

"Hey."

Ferro had come around on his circuit and found her crouched at the tarp, charge in her hands, ten feet from anything explainable. His rifle came off his shoulder with a scrape of webbing, and his eyes went wide, and his mouth opened—

—and Marisol hit him like a landslide.

The cook had crossed forty feet of ice without a sound, which should not have been possible for a woman her size, and she took Ferro around the torso and drove him backward into the supply crates with a crash that scattered tinned peaches across the ice. The rifle spun away. Ferro was strong and panicked and Marisol was neither fast nor gentle, and the two of them went down in a tangle of fists and curses while the whole camp turned to look.

Bell turned last. Bell turned the way a chess player turns — unhurried, already calculating — and his eyes found Sparrow first, then the dead red light on the detonator box, then the severed firing line, and for the first time since she'd known him, since Corvane Bay, since the depot and the containers and the meals he'd bought her brother with one hand while holding the leash with the other, Cassius Bell looked genuinely surprised.

"You," he said.

"Yeah," Sparrow said. "Me."

And the glacier answered for her.

It did not groan this time. It *shrieked* — a rising, shrieking, splintering roar as a fissure opened across the entire face of the wall, three hundred feet of ice leaning forward all at once, and the first serac went, and then the whole lower shelf went with it, calving not in pieces but in continents. The sound was beyond sound. It was pressure. It came through the ice underfoot and through Sparrow's ribs and through the fillings in her teeth, and the wall of the glacier folded outward into the source lake like a door being kicked open by something enormous.

"RUN!" Theo's voice, raw and carrying, from the skid where she was still tied — and then Odile was there with the knife, sawing through the lashings, and Jun was hauling himself up with his bandaged hands, and Imogen was moving at last, finally, like a woman released from a spell.

The wave, when the ice mass hit the water, would be thirty feet high. Maybe fifty. The lake had nowhere to send it except sideways — across the camp, across the plane, across everything — and then down, down the outlet channel, down the drainage gorge they'd climbed, a flood with the whole sky's worth of water behind it.

Sparrow didn't run for cover. She ran for the boat.

The skiff was moored at the camp's edge, and the flood would take it in the first ten seconds unless someone cut it loose and got it pointed downstream, into the surge instead of against it. It was the kind of thing Theo would do. It was the kind of thing Sparrow had watched Theo do in the Narrows, calm as arithmetic while the river tried to kill them, and Sparrow had decided somewhere in the last two days — maybe somewhere in the last two years — that she wanted to be the kind of person who did that too.

She slashed the mooring lines with Odile's knife and leapt aboard as the first ice boulders came down at the far end of the lake, each impact a depth charge, each one shoving the water higher.

"Sparrow!" Theo's voice again, closer now — the crew sprinting for the shoreline, Bell screaming orders at his scattering men, the detonator box abandoned in the snow, the evidence canister still sitting untouched at the waterline where Bell had set it down like a trophy.

Sparrow leaned out over the gunwale and stretched her arm as far as it would go, and Theo Blackwood — bleeding, half-free, twenty years of guilt on her shoulders — grabbed her hand, and Sparrow hauled, and Marisol pushed from behind, and they piled into the skiff in a tangle of limbs and rope as the wave came around the lake's shoulder, a gray moving hill wearing a crown of white.

"Everyone DOWN—"

The world became water.

* * *

Sparrow came up coughing, soaked to the bone, one hand still knotted in the tiller line. The skiff had surfed the surge somehow — Theo's trim, Odile's shouted corrections, luck with teeth — and now they were moving, actually moving, swept out through the shattered outlet shelf and into the drainage channel at a speed no engine could have given them. Behind them, the source lake was visibly dropping, the new gap in the ice shelf roaring like a opened sluice, the wrecked plane vanishing inch by inch into the maw. Ahead of them, the gorge waited: ten miles of whitewater they'd portaged *up*, now transformed into something no one had ever run, at a flow no one had ever measured.

Bell was on the shore, small and shouting, his extraction point drowning, his leverage flooding away. One of his men was dragging him uphill. It wasn't enough. None of it was going to be enough for him, and Sparrow knew with cold certainty that a man like Cassius Bell, cornered and wet, was more dangerous now than he had ever been with a gun.

Imogen was bailing with a fuel drum cut in half. Jun had the evidence canister — someone had grabbed it, she'd find out later who, she suspected Theo — lashed to the hull with a bowline Jun tied one-handed, because of course he did. Marisol was praying in Spanish and steering at the same time. Odile was reading the water ahead like scripture, calling the line:

"RIGHT side! Right of the tongue — LEFT oar — *hard* left!"

Theo dropped into the stern beside Sparrow, took the tiller, and looked at her — really looked, the way nobody had looked at her since before her brother was taken — and said, over the roar of the water, with the ghost of something that might have been a grin:

"Nice work with the widow's clip."

Sparrow's throat closed. She managed one nod.

The gorge swallowed them, and the flood carried them down into the dark.
