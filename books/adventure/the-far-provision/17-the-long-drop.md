## 17. The Long Drop

The sound reached Jun before the water did.

It came up the drainage channel like a freight train running on rails of stone — a low, grinding roar that he felt in his molars before he heard it with his ears, a vibration in the hull beneath his boots, in the wrench handle in his pocket, in the fillings he'd been meaning to have replaced for six years. He had spent enough time around engines to know what a machine sounded like when it was about to let go, and the glacier behind them was making exactly that sound: something enormous failing at its seams.

"Jun!" Theo's voice cut across the chaos at the shoreline. "Tell me she'll float!"

He looked down at the *Kestrel's Daughter* — Imogen's boat, patched and scarred, her portside seam weeping where the ice had grazed her coming up. He'd done what he could with epoxy and a sheet of aluminum scavenged from Bell's abandoned camp, riveted over the wound like a tin badge on a soldier's chest. It would hold. Probably. He'd learned long ago that "probably" was the only honest word an engineer got to use.

"She'll float," he called back. "She just won't float *well*."

"Neither do I!" Sparrow shouted, hauling the last coil of line aboard. "Nobody does! Let's go!"

The lake was draining.

That was the fact of it, plain and terrible, visible even now as they shoved off from the shore — the waterline had dropped four feet since dawn, exposing a rim of gray mud around the shore like the ring left in a bathtub, and through the notch in the moraine where the river exited, the outflow had gone from a stream to a torrent to something that no longer deserved the word *river* at all. The source lake, dammed for centuries by the ice shelf, was emptying itself into the drainage channel they'd spent nine days fighting their way up. And everything the channel had taken from them on the ascent — every bruise, every portage, every white-knuckled hour — it intended to give back all at once, with interest.

Theo stood at the helm, and Jun watched her take one long breath, the kind he'd seen her take before every run that mattered. She didn't look back at the lake. None of them did, except Odile, who checked their wake once, twice, then turned forward and never looked again. Whatever Bell's people were doing back there — whether they'd made it to high ground or whether the flood was settling accounts of its own — that was behind them now. Ahead was eight miles of channel turning into a chute.

"Lash it down," Theo said. "Everything. If it isn't tied, it's gone."

Jun lashed down the evidence canister last.

He'd carried it up the mountain himself, on the worst day of the portage, when his hands had split open along the knuckles and Marisol had wrapped them in tape and river-rage. It was a steel case the size of a breadbox, military-grade waterproofing, heavy as sin and twice as consequential. Imogen's father's life's work. Twenty years of proof that the Vane Syndicate had poisoned three watersheds and buried the man who said so. Everything they'd bled for sat inside that box, and Jun lashed it to the deck plates amidships with four ratchet straps and a bowline he tied twice because once didn't feel like enough.

He patted the lid the way you'd pat a sleeping dog. "Hold on," he told it quietly. "We're going home."

Sparrow, coiling line beside him, gave him a look. "You talk to boxes now?"

"This box is worth more than both of us."

"This box is worth more than the whole Syndicate," she said, and there was something fierce and young in her face that made him want to tell her to be careful, to be small, to survive — and made him know, at the same time, that she wouldn't hear it. Nobody on this boat ever heard it. That was, he suspected, why they'd all found each other.

The channel took them.

There was no other way to describe it. Where they had clawed upstream against current for days, the flood now carried them downstream at a speed that turned navigation into prayer. The banks blurred. Standing waves rose where none had been, brown-green and house-high, and the boat surfed between them like a leaf in a gutter. Odile crouched at the bow calling lines — "*Right channel! Right! Hard right!*" — and Theo threw the wheel over each time, and each time the *Daughter* answered with a groan from her patched seam that Jun felt in his chest like a pulled stitch.

He stayed at the engine.

It was the one thing he could still do that felt like control. The intake kept sucking debris — twigs, foam, once the bloated carcass of something he chose not to identify — and every ninety seconds he had to kill the cooling flow, reach into the housing, and clear it by hand while the channel roared past and the boat yawed and someone screamed about rocks. His taped hands went numb after the third clearing. He stopped feeling the cold water. He stopped feeling much of anything except the rhythm: clear the intake, hold the revs, trust the woman at the wheel.

Trust. There was a word he'd run from for most of his adult life. He'd trusted an engine once — a generator, actually, back in his old life, a big diesel he'd sworn was fine because he needed it to be fine — and it had thrown a rod and taken two fingers' worth of function from his left hand and his entire livelihood with it. After that he'd trusted machines and nothing else. Machines didn't lie. Machines didn't leave. You fed them and they ran, and if they broke, it was physics, not betrayal.

But somewhere between the last legal buoy and here, this boat had stopped being a machine. And these people had stopped being passengers.

"*ROCK GARDEN!*" Odile's voice cracked across the deck. "*Left side's open — LEFT —*"

The world became noise and water. Jun held the throttle wide and watched Theo drive them through a field of granite teeth that hadn't existed yesterday, boulders uncovered by the dropping lake, and the *Daughter* kissed one — a glancing blow that spun her stern toward the current — and then Theo corrected, impossibly, gracefully, the way she did everything that mattered, and they were through.

And then the seam let go.

It wasn't dramatic. That was the awful part. No explosion, no tearing shriek — just a soft metallic pop under his feet, aft of the patch, and then the bilge alarm screaming and water coming in the way water always comes in: faster than you think, faster than that, faster than you can believe.

"Jun!" Theo's eyes found him in the spray. "How bad?"

He lifted the floor panel and looked into the bilge, and the bilge looked back, black and rising. "Bad," he said. "Bailing won't keep pace. I need to get at the seam from outside."

"You can't go in the water—"

"I can go in the water." He was already pulling the spare line off the cleat, tying a bowline around his own waist, checking it the way he'd taught Sparrow to check hers. "Somebody just has to pull me back."

Silence for half a second — the kind of silence that contains an argument nobody has time to have.

"I've got him," Odile said.

Jun looked at her. She was braced at the stern rail, rope wrapped twice around her forearms, her face calm in the way that only truly frightened people can be calm. She nodded once. He nodded back. Somewhere in the last nine days, without either of them signing anything, these two had become the kind of people who held each other's lives on a length of rope, and neither of them had said it out loud, and neither of them needed to.

He went over the side.

The cold hit him like a door. Not cold — *cold*, the glacier's own temperature, the water that had been ice ten minutes ago and resented being anything else. It closed over his head and squeezed the air out of his chest and for one full second his body simply refused to function, every nerve screaming *wrong wrong wrong*. Then training took over, the animal discipline of a man who had spent years keeping things running in impossible conditions, and he found the seam by feel — a finger-width gap weeping river into the hull, right where the ice had struck, right where his patch had held and the metal beside it had given up.

He worked.

Hammer in his teeth, plug wedged into the gap, mallet blows that felt like hitting stone through frozen mittens. The current dragged at his legs. The boat surged ahead of him and the rope snapped taut and hauled him bumping through the wake, and he lost the hammer, caught it again on its tether, lost his grip on the rail, found it. Above him the sky churned gray. Below him the channel dropped away into green-black nothing. He got the plug seated. He got the second wedge driven. He felt the inflow slow from a fist to a trickle and thought — with the giddy, stupid triumph of the exhausted — *that's it, that's the worst of it—*

The channel dropped him.

Not the boat. Him. A standing wave lifted the stern, the rope went slack for one merciful instant, and the recirculating hydraulic behind the wave — the hole, the keeper, the thing every river runner fears and names like a god — took him under and *kept* him.

The world became tumbling. Up was a rumor. The rope tore at his waist and then, horribly, went loose — torn free, or slipped the cleat, he'd never know — and the surface became a ceiling of hammered silver that moved further away every time he reached for it. His lungs began to burn. His hands, his ruined, taped, heroic hands, scrabbled at water, which is the one substance in the universe that cannot be grabbed.

*So this is it,* he thought, with strange clarity. *Drowned by the same river we conquered. That's almost funny.*

He thought of the generator. Of the two fingers. Of twenty years of keeping his hands in his pockets, keeping his heart behind bulkheads, trusting nothing that could break him. He thought of Sparrow taking half his load on the portage without a word. Of Marisol's stew after the Narrows, and how everyone had gone quiet eating it, the quiet of people remembering they were alive. Of Odile's voice at the bow, steady as a compass needle, calling the safe line through the dark.

*I don't want to be a machine anymore,* he thought. *Machines don't get to come home.*

Something hit him from the side.

A boathook — no, a hand, gloved, iron-fingered, closing on his collar with a grip that had nothing gentle in it whatsoever. The silver ceiling rushed down to meet him. It broke. Air. Light. The gray roaring world.

Odile hauled his head above the waterline of the chaos and screamed, directly into his face, with a fury that was indistinguishable from love:

"*BREATHE, YOU IMPOSSIBLE MAN!*"

He breathed.

Somehow — he would reconstruct it later from bruises and hearsay — she had gotten him to the stern, gotten the trailing end of the rope, gotten leverage off the rail with her whole body laid out flat against the deck like an anchor with opinions. Somehow Sparrow was there too, both of them pulling, and somehow his arms remembered how to work, and he came over the gunwale in a rush of river and landed on the deck plates beside the evidence canister, coughing so hard he saw colors.

Marisol was on her knees beside him with a blanket before he finished his first full breath. Theo never left the wheel — couldn't, the channel demanded her — but her voice came back over her shoulder, roughened to gravel:

"Jun. Report."

He tried to speak. Coughed. Tried again.

"Seam's plugged," he rasped. "Pump's running. We're — we're going to make it."

"We're going to make it," Theo repeated, to the boat, to the river, to whatever gods preside over drainage channels. "You hear that? We're going to make it."

The gorge opened ahead of them — the final throat of it, walls narrowing, water stacking — and the *Kestrel's Daughter*, half-swallowed and wholly stubborn, drove into the light at the far end like a needle pulling thread.

* * *

They came out below the gorge into a world gone strangely, shockingly calm.

The channel fanned wide here, spreading its fury into braided shallows, and the boat wallowed down to a walk, wallowing and listing but moving, the bilge pump chattering its small brave song. The roar of the flood faded behind them into a long geological grumble. Ahead, the valley opened toward the lowlands, toward the Kestrel proper, toward everything.

Jun lay on the deck with the blanket over him and his hands held up against the pale sky, watching them shake. Marisol had replaced the river-soaked tape with clean bandages. Sparrow sat cross-legged nearby, close but not touching, the way she did when she wanted to be near someone but couldn't quite say so. Odile stood at the rail, looking downstream, her forearms striped red where the rope had burned them.

Jun got up. His body filed seventeen separate complaints; he overruled all of them. He crossed the deck, took Odile's arm, and turned her wrists over gently to look at the rope burns.

"You should wrap those," he said.

"You should stop drowning," she said.

"Noted. I'll add it to the maintenance schedule."

She almost smiled. Then she didn't, and instead she did something rarer: she leaned, very slightly, until her shoulder rested against his, and stayed there. Neither of them said anything. On this boat, they had all learned which things were better left as ballast than spoken aloud.

From the helm, Theo's voice drifted back, quiet and level in a way that made everyone listen:

"There's the extraction pad."

Jun followed her gaze. Downstream, on a gravel bar where the channel met the Kestrel's broad shoulder, sat a flat square of cleared ground, helipad markers freshly painted, a fuel cache under netting. Bell's exit route. Bell's ride out.

Empty.

"The files," Imogen said slowly, from where she'd been sitting silent since the lake, the steel canister's shadow falling across her knees. "If we broadcast from here — before his helicopter comes—"

"Then the whole world hears it first," Theo finished.

Jun looked at the bandages on his hands, at the burns on Odile's arms, at the water still dripping from the hem of everything he owned. He thought about the ceiling of hammered silver, and the hand that had broken through it.

"Well," he said, pushing himself upright. "Somebody help me carry the box."
