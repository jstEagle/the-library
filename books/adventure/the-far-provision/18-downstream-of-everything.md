## 18. Downstream of Everything

The river spat them out below the gorge like something it had no further use for.

Theo felt the change before she saw it — the water widening, the roar behind them softening into a long exhausted hiss, the walls of granite falling away on either side until there was nothing but sky and gravel bars and the pale green shallows where the Kestrel remembered how to be gentle again. The *Provision* — what was left of her — wallowed in the current with a list to port that Jun kept correcting by feel, one hand on the tiller, the other pressed flat against a hull seam that had started weeping somewhere above the last drop.

They were all still alive. That fact sat in Theo's chest like a coal she was afraid to look at directly.

Sparrow lay against the transom, soaked to the bone, shivering so hard her teeth chattered in a rhythm Theo could hear over the engine's ragged stutter. Odile had wrapped her in the tarp that had covered the fuel cans an hour and a lifetime ago. Imogen stood at the bow with the evidence canister lashed across her back, its straps cutting grooves into her shoulders she hadn't bothered to adjust, staring downstream at something only she could see.

Theo knew what it was. She'd seen it on the satellite photo Imogen had shown her in that dockside bar, back when this whole thing had still been a map instead of a wound: a flat shelf of graded gravel on the east bank, three miles below the gorge mouth, with a wind sock and a fuel bladder cache and a radio mast folded down for transport.

Bell's extraction pad. The door out of the wilderness he'd never intended to leave open for anyone but himself.

"Three miles," Theo said, and her voice came out scraped raw, like the river had run through her too. "Maybe less if the current holds."

Jun didn't ask three miles to what. He'd been listening to the same river she had. He opened the throttle as far as the wounded engine would allow, which wasn't far, and the *Provision* limped downstream through water gone suddenly, treacherously calm.

* * *

They grounded her on the west bank two hundred meters short, in the shadow of a gravel island, because Theo wanted eyes on the pad before Bell had eyes on them. It cost them minutes they might not have, but Bell's people were waiting for a boat full of hostages, not a boat full of ghosts, and minutes bought with stealth were worth more than minutes bought with speed.

Theo crawled up the last rise on her belly with Odile beside her, both of them wet and filthy and breathing through their mouths, and looked down at the extraction site.

It was worse than she'd imagined and better than she'd feared.

Worse, because Bell hadn't come alone. Four men moved around the pad with the bored efficiency of hired muscle, checking tie-downs, unrolling the wind sock, dragging the fuel hose toward a helicopter that sat on the gravel with its rotors tied down and its paint the flat gray of money that doesn't need to introduce itself. Cassius Bell stood apart from them near the radio mast, hands clasped behind his back, watching the gorge mouth the way a man watches a door he expects someone to come through.

Better, because the helicopter was empty. And better, because bolted to the mast beside him, cased in weatherproof housing with its antenna half-raised, was a broadcast transmitter — the kind of rig salvage outfits used to bounce signals off relay satellites when they were operating outside licensed corridors. The kind of rig that didn't care who it talked to, or how many someones heard.

"Odile," Theo breathed. "Tell me you're seeing what I'm seeing."

"The mast." Odile's voice was flat and quick, the voice she used when the numbers were good. "He brought a transmitter to report the recovery. If we get to it first—"

"We don't get to it first. We don't have to." Theo's mind was moving fast now, faster than it had moved in years, all those miles of avoidance finally paying interest. "That rig is set to bounce off relays. You know what else bounces off relays? Everything. News desks, emergency bands, open channels — every outlet within range has been sniffing this corridor since the calving reports went public. They know something's happening upriver. They just don't know what."

Odile turned her head slowly. In the gray light her eyes were very bright. "You want to hijack his own radio."

"I want to give the story away." Theo felt something crack open in her chest — not grief, not yet, something adjacent to laughter. "Bell spent twenty years and God knows how much money making sure nobody ever heard Elias Reyes's name spoken in the right sentence. We're going to say it on every frequency in the basin."

* * *

Getting down to the pad meant crossing open ground, and open ground meant Bell's men, and Bell's men meant guns, and Theo had exactly one gun and no appetite left for using it. So they did what the river had taught them to do all week: they stopped fighting the current and read it.

The wind off the glacier was still coming down-valley in gusts, erratic since the calving had begun, and it was pushing a low scud of mist off the meltwater pools along the east bank. Not cover. Never real cover. But enough of a shimmer to make four bored men doubt their eyes for ninety seconds, and ninety seconds was a currency Theo had learned to spend carefully.

She laid it out in the gravel shadow of the island, drawing lines with a wet finger while the others huddled close enough to touch. Marisol would take the boat around the far side of the channel and run the engine hard — noise, movement, a target worth looking at. Sparrow would stay with her, which Sparrow argued about with a ferocity that would have been funny any other day, until Marisol put one enormous hand on the girl's shoulder and said, "You've paid enough this week, little bird. Let the old ones be stupid," and Sparrow went quiet in a way that meant she'd understood something about the shape of the sacrifice.

Imogen, Jun, and Odile would move on the mast from the north, through the mist. Theo would go alone, straight down the middle, because Bell would be watching for everyone except the one person whose debt he thought he owned.

"And when he sees me?" Theo asked, and answered herself. "He'll talk. Men like Bell always talk. Every second he spends talking is a second Odile isn't spending on that transmitter."

Imogen caught her arm as they broke the huddle. Her fingers were shaking — Theo noticed it for the first time, this woman made of iron and grudges, shaking like a wire in wind. "If it comes down to the canister or the broadcast," she said, "the canister burns and you broadcast. Promise me. My father's dead either way. The truth isn't."

"It won't come to that," Theo said, which was a lie, and they both knew it, and Imogen let go anyway, because twenty years of rivalry had taught them both that some lies are a form of respect.

* * *

Marisol's engine roared to life on the far channel at 6:14 by Odile's watch, and every head on the pad snapped toward the sound.

Theo came down the bank through the mist at a dead run.

She heard shouting — one of the hired men, then another, voices cracking with the particular panic of people whose payday has just grown teeth. A shot cracked past her left ear and hit the gravel with a sound like a knuckle rapping glass. She didn't stop. Twenty years ago, on a different river, she had frozen, and two people had died, and she had carried that ice in her chest ever since; but the ice had melted somewhere above the tree line, spilled out of her in a confession to Jun under a sky the color of knife blades, and what was left underneath was just a woman running downhill toward the worst man she knew.

Bell turned at the sound of her boots and did exactly what she'd known he would do.

He smiled.

"Theo Blackwood." His voice carried across the gravel, warm as ever, warm as a heating vent in a cold office. "You continue to disappoint my expectations, and I continue to admire you for it. Now. The canister, if you please, and we can discuss—"

The rest of it was lost, because Odile reached the transmitter.

Later, Theo would try to reconstruct those four minutes and fail. Memory refused to file them in order. What stayed was fragments: Jun taking a rifle stock across the shoulder and staying upright through sheer stubbornness. Imogen walking out of the mist toward the hired men with her father's name in her mouth like a drawn blade, saying *Elias Reyes* over and over, loud, louder, making them hear it whether they wanted to or not. Bell's smile dying by degrees as the wind sock snapped in the gusts and the mist thinned and he understood, with the slow horror of a man watching water rise past his ankles, that the numbers had stopped being in his favor.

And Odile's hands on the transmitter board, moving like they'd been born there.

"Relay handshake confirmed," Odile called out, her voice pitched to carry and to cut. "Open frequencies, all bands. Everyone in range, listen up, because I'm only saying this once per band and I'm saying it on all of them."

She keyed the mic. And she read.

She read the manifest of the *Kestrel Star*, the expedition plane that had gone into the source lake in 2004 carrying a dead man's inheritance. She read the account numbers Bell's own patrol logs had helpfully corroborated. She read the names of the officials Vane had bought, the routes the Syndicate had run, the two crew members of the 2004 disaster whose deaths had been ruled accident and were nothing of the kind. She read Elias Reyes's final message, the one he'd hidden in the wreck before the Syndicate's plane found him, the words of a whistleblower who had known he wouldn't survive the knowing.

His daughter stood beside her and did not cry. Theo watched her hold herself absolutely still, absolutely silent, while her father's voice — his actual recorded voice, recovered from the canister, played through the transmitter at Odile's signal — said *if you are hearing this, then the river kept its promise even though the people didn't* — and Theo understood that some broadcasts are funerals, and some are resurrections, and this one was somehow both.

The replies started coming back inside two minutes.

First the emergency bands, confused, then sharp. Then a news desk in Fort Meridian, then another in Kestrel Falls, then a national wire service that had clearly been sitting on the calving story and smelled blood in the water. Voices stacking on voices, questions tumbling over questions, and Odile answering each one with coordinates, dates, document references, the cold clean architecture of proof.

On the gravel, Bell's men began to drift. Not run — these were professionals — but drift, the way ice drifts, each of them calculating privately and simultaneously that the side paying in silence had just lost its funding. One of them simply walked to the tree line and kept walking. Another sat down on a fuel drum and put his head in his hands.

Bell stood alone in the middle of his ruined extraction, and for the first time since that warm office with its warm smile, Theo watched him have nothing to say.

"You can't—" he started, and stopped, because the sentence had nowhere to go. There was no debt to invoke. No ledger. The ledger was public now. The whole rotten arithmetic of the Vane Syndicate was being read aloud on eight frequencies by a navigator with salt-cracked lips and a dead man's documents, and every creditor, every official, every bought judge in the basin was hearing their own name in the mix.

"Bell." Theo walked toward him across the gravel, and her legs were steady, and she marveled at that from a great distance, the way you marvel at a river running calm over a deep place. "Twenty years ago your people sent an expedition up this river to bury a truth, and my mentor took the blame for what your sabotage did, and I let him, because I was young and frozen and grateful to be alive. Today the river gave it all back." She stopped an arm's length away. Up close, the warmth was entirely gone from his face, and what remained looked almost relieved, which was somehow the most frightening thing about him. "Your helicopter's fueled. Go explain yourself to someone with a warrant. Or stay, and explain yourself to the river. It's got opinions."

Bell looked at her for a long moment. Then, incredibly, he laughed — a small, genuine, terrible sound.

"Miles, not years," he said softly. "That was always your problem, Blackwood. You measured everything in distance. Distance is cheap." He turned and walked toward the helicopter, and the rotors began to turn, and Theo let him go, because the story was already loose in the world, running faster than any machine, and there was nothing left to chase.

* * *

The helicopter lifted off at 6:51 and shrank northward into the scud, and nobody watched it go except Theo, and she only watched long enough to confirm it wasn't turning back.

Then she sat down on the gravel beside the transmitter, and the coal in her chest finally showed itself, and it was grief.

Jun was sitting against the mast with an ice pack Marisol had improvised holding his shoulder, and Sparrow had appeared from the boat at some point without anyone noticing, and was standing very close to Odile as if the transmitter were a campfire. Imogen was on her knees in the gravel with her forehead nearly touching it, and no one went to her, and no one needed to. Some prayers are private even in company.

"They'll want statements," Odile said quietly, nodding at the transmitter, where the voices were still stacking up, insistent, hungry. "Fort Meridian wants a live interview within the hour. Kestrel Falls is asking for the physical documents. There's a federal prosecutor's office on the band, Theo. An actual one. Asking how they can help."

Theo looked down at her hands. They were bleeding in three places she hadn't noticed, and shaking in a fourth.

"Tell them everything," she said. "Every page. Hold nothing back. And tell them the salvage crew that recovered it will meet them wherever they say — publicly, on the record, names attached." She glanced at her crew, her strays, her family assembled out of debts and damage on a gravel bar at the bottom of the world. "We're done being ghosts."

Marisol laughed, wet-eyed, and pulled a battered thermos from somewhere inside her coat, because of course she had, because Marisol understood that revolutions run on coffee the way boats run on fuel.

But it was Imogen who came to stand beside Theo at the water's edge, when the interviews were done and the transmitter had gone quiet and the river ran on ahead of them, wide and silver and indifferent, carrying everything downstream of everything.

"He'd have liked them," Imogen said. Her voice was wrecked and level. "Your crew. He always said the best expeditions were held together with duct tape and stubbornness."

Theo watched the current slide past their boots, going somewhere neither of them would follow it today.

"Imogen," she said. "There's something upstream we still have to do. Before the season closes. Before anything else."

Imogen followed her gaze upriver, toward the gorge, toward the source, toward a grave on a bluff above the water that had waited twenty years for someone to come back and say a name out loud.

"I know," Imogen said. "I'll bring the shovel."
