## 19. What We Salvaged

Marisol had cooked for funerals before. She had cooked for weddings, christenings, prison releases, and one memorable quinceañera where the birthday girl arrived on a stretcher with a broken collarbone and refused to cancel. She knew the arithmetic of grief in a kitchen: how much food sorrow demanded, how it always asked for more than anyone could eat, and how the leftovers mattered more than the meal — because leftovers meant tomorrow, and grief was a thing that needed convincing there would be a tomorrow.

So when she waded ashore below the gorge, boots squelching, hair full of glacial silt, and looked back at what remained of the *Providencia* — half-beached, hull stove in along the starboard side like a rib cage kicked by something enormous, her galley flooded to the countertops — she did not cry. She did what she had done at every funeral of her long and wandering life.

She started planning a menu.

* * *

The riverbank they'd washed up on was a wide gravel shelf two miles downstream of the drainage channel, sheltered by a stand of black spruce that had somehow survived everything the Kestrel had ever thrown at it. The boat was dead — Jun had pronounced it with the gentle finality of a doctor who has known the patient too long — but her bones were salvageable, and Marisol salvaged what cooks salvage: the cast iron, the propane burner, three crates of provisions lashed high enough to survive the flood, and the great dented stockpot that had crossed four borders and two marriages with her.

By late afternoon she had a fire going behind a windbreak of driftwood, and the smell began its work before any words could. That was the secret nobody wrote down about cooking: you didn't feed people to fill their stomachs. You fed them so their bodies would remember, against all evidence, that the world still made good things.

She built the menu out of what the flood had spared and what the forest offered. The smoked pork belly from the cold locker, sliced thin and crisped over the coals. Wild onions Odile dug from the bank, their green tops charred just to sweetness. A pot of beans that had been soaking since before the source lake, finished with juniper berries Sparrow gathered without being asked — the girl had been hovering at the edge of camp all afternoon like a bird deciding whether a hand held seed or a snare, and gathering juniper was as good a way as any to land.

And bread. Of course bread. Marisol had flour, she had yeast, and she had decided somewhere in the churning dark of the flood channel that if she lived through this, she would never again serve anyone anything less than fresh bread. The dough rose under a tarp while the fire settled into coals, and she worked it with hands that ached in the way hands ache after you've gripped a rail through white water for six hours — an honest ache, a paid-in-full ache.

Theo came to the fire first. She came the way she did everything now: quieter than before, moving like someone relearning the weight of her own body. Her jacket was torn at the shoulder where the ice had taken its tax. She stood at the edge of the firelight watching Marisol work, and Marisol, without looking up, said:

"Sit. You look like a woman who hasn't eaten since Tuesday."

"It's Thursday."

"Then you're overdue." Marisol nodded at a stump she'd dragged up as a seat. "Wash your hands first. We're not animals."

Theo almost smiled. It was a small thing, that almost-smile, but Marisol had spent twenty years reading faces across galley counters and mess tables, and she knew what it cost. Theo Blackwood had come down that river carrying a debt that ate her from the inside like rust eats a hull, and she had set it down somewhere above the gorge, drowned it in the flood with Bell's patrol boat and the Syndicate's whole rotten ledger. What was left of her was lighter and stranger and not yet sure of its own shape.

"You saved the stockpot," Theo said.

"I saved the *crew*. The pot came along because it was attached to me."

Theo sat. She washed her hands in the basin Marisol pushed toward her, and she dried them on her trousers like a person who had finally stopped caring about appearances, and then she just sat there, letting the fire have her face.

"Marisol," she said, after a while. "The boat."

"The boat did its job."

"It's my boat."

"It was your boat," Marisol corrected gently, shaping a loaf. "Now it's kindling with a history. You want to mourn it, mourn it after dinner. Grief on an empty stomach is just hunger wearing a costume."

That got a real laugh out of her — short, surprised, rusty, but real. Marisol counted it the day's second-best catch, after the pork belly.

* * *

They came to the fire as the light went long and gold across the water: Odile with her charts wrapped in oilcloth, carrying them the way other people carry infants; Jun with his bandaged hands held carefully in front of him like something borrowed; Imogen Reyes, who had spent the afternoon rigging the radio antenna on the highest snag of driftwood and now sat down heavily, looking ten years older and twenty years lighter; and last, Sparrow, who came sideways, the way she always did, and took the seat farthest from everyone, which happened to be next to Marisol's cutting board.

Marisol handed her a knife and a bowl of wild onions. "You're on garnish."

Sparrow blinked. "I don't—"

"Nobody's born knowing how to cut an onion. Hold it like this. Curl your fingers. Let the blade do the work." Marisol adjusted the girl's grip with two fingers, brief and businesslike, and moved on. Behind her she heard the small rhythmic *tok, tok, tok* of a knife learning confidence, and she let herself smile at the fire where no one could see.

The girl had been the turncoat. Everyone knew it now — had known it since the night Odile caught her at the radio, since the confrontation, since the vote that kept her aboard. And then she had used everything she'd learned inside Bell's operation to free them all while a glacier came apart over their heads, and no one had said much about either fact since, because some things are too large for conversation and need to be handled in smaller units, like meals.

But Marisol noticed things. She noticed that Jun sat where he could see Sparrow without turning his head. She noticed that Odile had stopped flinching when the girl spoke. She noticed that Theo, passing behind Sparrow's stump, had put a hand briefly on her shoulder — two seconds, no words — and that the girl had gone very still under it, the way a stray goes still the first time a hand comes down gentle instead of fast.

The brother was the thing. Marisol had heard it secondhand, fragments from Odile: Vane held him, some contract town, some debt of Sparrow's own making, and every coordinate she'd radioed had been priced in his safety. And now the files were loose — Imogen's broadcast had gone out on every frequency within range that morning, the whistleblower's evidence scattered to newsrooms and regulators like seed thrown to the wind — and somewhere south, in some fluorescent office, a man in a suit was learning that holding hostages had just become very bad business.

"He'll be on a bus by Sunday," Odile said suddenly, to no one and everyone. She was studying her charts, but her voice carried the particular certainty of a navigator who has calculated a fix. "Vane doesn't keep collateral once the ledger's public. Liability, not leverage. They'll release him inside seventy-two hours."

Sparrow's knife stopped.

"You don't know that," the girl said. Her voice was steady the way ice is steady.

"I know ledgers," Odile said. "I've run from enough of them. When the book burns, the debts written in it burn too. Your brother is a line item, mija. He's about to be erased."

Sparrow looked down at the onions she'd ruined — uneven, ragged, cut by shaking hands — and Marisol reached over and took the bowl away and replaced it with bread dough.

"Punch that," she ordered. "Hard. It's therapeutic and I mean it."

The girl punched the dough. Then again. Then she was crying, quietly, furiously, both fists buried to the wrist in the loaf meant for dinner, and Marisol put an arm around her shoulders and kept talking about gluten structure as though nothing whatsoever were happening, because that was the service she provided, the one that never appeared on any manifest: she made it possible for people to fall apart without anyone having to watch.

Jun looked away, giving her privacy the only way he knew how, which was to study the fire with the intensity of a man reading blueprints in the flames. Odile turned her charts another degree. And Theo, who had seen everything, said loudly, "Is that bread going to be edible or are we eating dough?"

"It'll be edible," Marisol said, "if she stops assaulting it."

Sparrow laughed wetly, wiped her face on her sleeve, and went back to work. The dough, abused beyond all tradition, rose anyway. Marisol had always liked that about dough.

* * *

The feast, when it finally landed, was not elegant. It was pork belly and charred onions and beans dark with juniper, and bread torn hot from the Dutch oven and passed hand to hand with butter that had softened in Marisol's pocket. There was coffee brewed strong enough to strip paint, and for dessert — because Marisol did not recognize the concept of *no dessert* — fried dough dusted with the last of the cinnamon, which Sparrow ate four of before anyone could comment.

They ate off tin plates around a fire on a gravel bar in the middle of nowhere, and it was, Marisol thought, watching them, the best table she had ever set.

Not because of the food, though the food was good. Because of what the table held. A navigator who had lost her maps twice and her bearings once and found her way anyway. An engineer with ruined hands who had nearly drowned for a box of papers and would do it again and everyone knew it. A rival-turned-ally who had spent twenty years and her father's ghost to get here. A captain who had chosen justice over money and was still figuring out whether that choice had a floor under it. And a kid who had betrayed them and been forgiven and had repaid the forgiveness in the only currency that counted anymore, which was action, at speed, in a calving field.

Grief and gratitude shared that table the way old married couples share one: familiarly, taking turns, finishing each other's sentences.

They told stories. That was Marisol's doing — she'd learned long ago that people don't process catastrophe until someone makes them narrate it, and the fastest route to narration is through a second helping. So she refilled plates and asked questions like a smuggler running contraband across a border: *Jun, tell them about the portage log you kept. Odile, what was the water actually doing in the Narrows? Sparrow, how did you know the ice shelf would go when it did?*

And bit by bit the day came apart into pieces small enough to hold. Jun described the flood in numbers — flow rates, drop gradients, the exact angle at which the *Providencia* had hit the rock that killed her — and his voice shook only once, when he got to the moment the evidence canister tore free and he went in after it. Odile pulled her sleeve up without comment and showed the rope burn she'd gotten hauling him out, and the two of them looked at each other across the fire with an entire unspoken paragraph passing between them, and Marisol pretended to check the coals.

Imogen talked about her father. Not much — a few minutes, a story about him teaching her Morse code with a flashlight through a wall when she was seven — but she talked, and the crew listened, and when she finished, Theo raised her tin cup of coffee and said, "To Emilio Reyes," and they drank, even Sparrow, even though the coffee was terrible.

"And to the *Providencia*," Marisol added. "Twenty-two years of service. Cooked eleven thousand meals, sank once, died with her boots on."

"She sank twice," Jun said. "There was also the incident in Bahía—" 

"We do not speak of Bahía."

"We speak of Bahía," Odile said, grinning, "because I wasn't there and I demand details."

And it went on like that, the fire burning down to orange jewels, the river murmuring its endless commentary behind them, until Theo stood, brushed off her trousers, and walked alone down to the waterline. Marisol watched her go and did not follow, because some reckonings are private, but she saw the shape of it: Theo standing at the edge of the current, looking upstream toward the source they'd fled, toward the grave she hadn't visited yet and the promise she'd broken and kept and broken again, measuring something out there in the dark.

When she came back, her eyes were wet, but her stride was even.

"Tomorrow," she said, sitting back down, "we walk out. There's a ranger station forty miles downriver. From there, phones, buses, whatever passes for civilization." She looked around the fire, at each of them in turn. "I can't pay anyone. The boat's gone, the salvage's gone, the payout never existed. All I've got is—"

"Us," Sparrow said.

Theo stopped.

"You've got us," the girl repeated, steadier now, chin up, the way she'd stood in the calving field. "That's not nothing."

"No," Theo agreed quietly. "It isn't."

Marisol ladled out the last of the beans and thought: *there it is.* Twenty years she'd cooked for crews, and she could name the exact moment a collection of strangers became a family — it was the moment someone offered the only thing they had left and someone else accepted it without haggling.

"Well then," she said briskly, standing and brushing off her apron. "If we're walking forty miles tomorrow, everyone sleeps tonight. Dishes rotate. Sparrow, you're with me — I'm teaching you to make stock before we lose the pot to nostalgia. And somebody bury those bones deep or we'll have every fox in the valley at our bedsides."

The crew broke apart into the small chores of evening, and the fire settled, and the stars came out over the Kestrel in their ancient indifferent abundance.

It was later — Marisol banking the coals, the others already rolled in tarps and sleeping bags — that Imogen appeared at her elbow, quiet as a heron.

"A word?" the rival captain said.

"For you? Two words and a cup of coffee."

Imogen glanced back toward the sleeping forms, then at Marisol, and her voice dropped low. "When we get out. When this is all settled — the hearings, the fallout, all of it. I'm putting together expeditions. Legitimate ones. Licensed routes, insured hulls, none of the sworn-off water." She paused. "I'll need a cook. A good one. Someone who can hold a crew together with a wooden spoon."

Marisol looked at her for a long moment. Then at the sleeping crew — at Sparrow curled against Jun's side like the stray she'd been, at Odile's arm flung over her charts even in sleep, at Theo's face slack and young-looking in the ember-light, unburdened for perhaps the first time in twenty years.

"You'll need more than a cook," Marisol said. "You'll need all of us. And we don't come separately."

Imogen followed her gaze, and something moved across her face — not quite a smile, but its foundation stones. "I figured," she said. "I was hoping you'd say that."

"Then ask me again in the morning," Marisol said, handing her the coffee. "Everything looks negotiable after breakfast."

Imogen took the cup, and for a moment the two women stood together at the dying fire, captains of nothing, stewards of everything, while the river ran on toward the sea with all it carried — the drowned boat, the burned ledger, the names read aloud at last.

Downstream, forty miles and a lifetime away, a phone was waiting to ring with news of a freed boy on a northbound bus. But that was tomorrow's provision.

Tonight there was bread, and there was family, and there was sleep.

Marisol banked the fire one last time and went to teach a stray girl how to make stock from almost nothing — which was, when you thought about it, the whole job description.
