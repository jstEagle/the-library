## 20. Debts and Dividends

The news reached Imogen Reyes the way most important news did on the Kestrel: late, garbled, and carried by someone who wanted something.

In her case it was a fisherman named Okonkwo who ran a smokehouse at the last settlement below the gorge, a man with forearms like dock rope and a radio he'd jury-rigged from three broken ones. He came down to the riverbank where the *Provision* lay beached and dying — hull holed twice, gunwale sprung, her name scorched off in some earlier life that none of them talked about — and he held out his handset like an offering.

"Reyes. You'll want to hear this."

She took it. Static first, then a voice reading bulletins in the flat cadence of a wire service: federal warrants issued for four officers of the Vane Syndicate; assets frozen across two provinces; a whistleblower archive broadcast from an unlicensed transmitter near the head of the Kestrel Gorge, now verified authentic by three independent forensic accountants. The anchor's voice stumbled once over the phrase *posthumous exoneration*, and Imogen felt the word go through her like a fishhook through soft tissue.

Her father's name. Spoken on the air. Not as a liar. Not as the man whose forged ledgers had been used to hang twenty years of crimes around a dead man's neck. As a whistleblower. As a father.

"Ma'am?" Okonkwo said. "You've gone white as the glacier."

"I'm fine." She wasn't. She handed back the handset with hands that were not quite steady and walked to the waterline, because there was nowhere else to put her body while her chest tried to contain whatever this was — grief and vindication tangled so tightly she couldn't tell which one was bleeding.

Behind her, the camp was already stirring. Marisol had a fire going, because Marisol always had a fire going; the woman treated combustion as a moral position. Sparrow sat cross-legged on a crate turning the radio dial with the concentration of a safecracker, hunting other stations, other confirmations. Jun was asleep sitting up against a wheel, his bandaged hands curled in his lap like sleeping birds, and Odile had draped a tarp over him without waking either of them.

And Theo Blackwood stood at the bow of the wrecked boat, looking upstream toward the gorge they'd come down out of, wearing the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like.

Imogen knew that expression. She'd been watching it across rivers, regattas, salvage docks, and two decades of grudging respect since they were both nineteen years old and stupid enough to race each other through the Narrows for a bottle of terrible whiskey. Theo Blackwood calculating costs. Theo Blackwood deciding what a thing would take out of her before she let herself want it.

Well. Imogen Reyes had spent twenty years losing races to that woman by half a length and winning everything that mattered afterward, and she was done waiting for Theo to finish the sum.

* * *

They walked upriver together that evening, past the smokehouse, past the last mooring stake, to a gravel bar where the Kestrel ran shallow and loud and the light came down the canyon walls in long amber slabs. It was the kind of place their old mentor — Theo's mentor, though Imogen had loved him too, in the way you love a lighthouse — used to call a thinking bank. You couldn't hear yourself worry over water that loud.

"You heard," Theo said. It wasn't a question. News traveled faster than grief on this river.

"I heard." Imogen skipped a stone. It went four hops and died. She'd never beaten Theo at stone-skipping either. "Four officers. Frozen accounts. The whole rotten ledger read into the record." She paused. "Your debt dies with it."

Theo looked at her then, and Imogen watched her do it — watch her refuse to believe it, watch her check the statement for hooks, for interest clauses, for the catch that had lived in every good thing offered to her since Cassius Bell first smiled at her across a desk. Twenty years of miles had taught Theo Blackwood that nothing arrived free, and the cruelest thing the Syndicate had ever built wasn't the debt. It was the reflex.

"There's no catch," Imogen said quietly. "That's what I'm telling you. The ledger is ash, Theo. Bell is in custody — they pulled him off the glacier pad an hour after your broadcast. Vane's own creditors are eating each other alive trying to prove they were victims, not accomplices. Nobody is coming to collect from you. There is no one left to collect."

Theo crouched, picked up a flat stone, turned it over in her fingers. "I keep waiting to feel different," she said. "Lighter. Like a weight came off. And instead I just keep checking my pockets."

"That's not the debt," Imogen said. "That's just you."

It earned her the ghost of a laugh, which had been the entire objective. They stood a while in the roar of the shallows. Somewhere above them, faint as memory, the glacier still spoke — a deep irregular percussion, the ice settling into its new shape after the calving, the mountain rearranging its furniture. Imogen found she could listen to it now without her stomach dropping. Give it a week.

"My father kept a ledger too," she said, when she could trust her voice. "Not debts. Provisions. Every expedition he outfitted, he wrote down what they carried and why. Rope, 'because someone always underestimates the portage.' Extra boots. A tin of peaches, 'for the day we need to celebrate something small.'" Her throat tightened. "He called it the far provision. Supplies you carry not for the trip you planned but for the one you can't imagine yet. He said the difference between a survivor and a casualty was almost always what they'd thought to pack for a day that hadn't happened."

"The peach tin," Theo said. "From the wreck."

"From the wreck." Imogen nodded. "Twenty years in the ice and the peaches were still good. Jun ate one. Don't tell me you didn't see him cry."

"He said it was the altitude."

"He cried into a second peach."

That got the real laugh, short and cracked, and Theo threw her stone — six skips, because of course six — and sat down heavily on a drift log, and Imogen sat beside her, shoulder to shoulder the way they'd never once managed to sit in twenty years of rivalry, and let the river fill the silence until it was bearable to speak again.

* * *

"I have a proposition," Imogen said eventually.

Theo groaned. "Every time you say that, I end up hauling a boat over a mountain."

"This one involves no mountains. No sworn-off waters. No illegal anything." Imogen pulled the folded paper from inside her jacket — she'd written it out that morning by the fire, in pen, because some things deserved ink — and smoothed it on her knee. "My father's outfitting company still exists. Shell of it, but the licenses are clean, the insurance is paid, and as of yesterday nobody named Vane has any claim on it. I'm reopening it. Legitimate expeditions. Scientific charting, search and rescue contract work, guided runs on every legal mile of this river and three others. Real wages. Real insurance. A boat that isn't held together with prayer and Marisol's opinions."

She handed him the paper. Theo read it the way she read rapids — fast, then again slower, hunting the hole hidden under the surface.

"A partnership," Theo said. "Fifty-fifty."

"Fifty-five, forty-five, my company."

"Fifty-fifty, and I get final say on crew."

"You already have the crew. That's the point." Imogen watched her. "Jun as chief engineer with a salary that lets him fix his mother's roof. Odile running navigation and training Sparrow properly — real certification, not river-orphan apprenticeship. Marisol feeding all of us until we're too heavy to drown. And Sparrow—" she hesitated, gentled, "—Sparrow gets a bunk that's hers, and a brother who calls every Sunday now that Vane isn't holding him over anyone's head. I checked. He called this morning. She cried into her breakfast, and if you tell her I told you, I'll deny it."

Theo was quiet for a long moment. The light was going copper on the water. Downstream, faintly, they could hear Marisol calling something about dinner and Sparrow yelling back something insolent, and the sound of it did more arguing than Imogen's whole document.

"Why me?" Theo asked finally. "You've spent twenty years beating me to every contract, every salvage right, every dry dock berth. You don't need me. You never needed me."

"No," Imogen agreed. "I didn't need you. That was the problem." She looked out at the river rather than at Theo, because some things were easier said to water. "I spent two decades proving I could do everything alone, and it turns out alone is just a slower way of losing. My father ran his best seasons with a partner. Did you know that? Before the Syndicate squeezed him, before the forged books — he ran the Kestrel with a man named Aldous Vane, back when Vane was just another outfitter with big ideas. Partners. Then Vane decided partnership was a bad investment, and everything after that was just the interest compounding." She shook her head. "I'm not going to spend my life being the second Vane. I'd rather be the second chance."

Theo looked at her for a long time. Imogen had seen that look before — on the dockside bar, when she'd laid out the job knowing about the sworn-off promise; on the source lake, when the evidence canister came up dripping and Theo had chosen justice over the payout without a visible hesitation, as if the choice had been made years ago in some room Imogen had never been shown. It was the look of a woman deciding whether to let herself be known.

"In 2004," Theo said slowly, "when the *Meridian* went down — you know I froze. You know Hal took the blame for it. Took it to his grave."

"I know," Imogen said. "And I know what it cost you to say it out loud, because Odile told Marisol, who told everyone, because Marisol has no concept of confidentiality." She bumped Theo's shoulder with her own. "Here's what I know about freezing, Blackwood. Everybody freezes. The good ones are just the ones who come back to the same water afterward. On purpose. With crew."

Theo looked down at the paper in her hands. When she spoke again her voice was rough. "Hal used to say the river doesn't forgive. It just forgets, and it's our job to remember better next time."

"Hal was right about exactly one thing per decade, and that sounds like this decade's."

Another laugh. Then Theo took the pen from Imogen's jacket pocket — took it without asking, the way she took everything, channels and chances and the last word — and signed her name at the bottom of the page in handwriting like a storm front.

Then she held out her hand, and Imogen took it, and they shook on it there on the thinking bank with the glacier muttering overhead and the Kestrel running clean below, twenty years of rivalry folding itself away into something with a different name, a name neither of them said aloud because saying it would have required admitting how long they'd both been lonely.

"Partners," Theo said.

"Partners," Imogen said. "God help the river."

* * *

They walked back to camp in the blue hour, and the fire had grown into a proper feast — Marisol had traded Okonkwo half her spice stores for two enormous trout and was cooking them over alder coals with the ceremony of a coronation. Sparrow had the radio going low, cycling through stations, catching fragments of the story spreading outward like dye in water: the hearings announced, the frozen accounts, the name *Emilio Reyes* read out in a list of the wrongfully condemned, posthumous, posthumous, posthumous.

Imogen stopped at the edge of the firelight when she heard it the third time. Theo stopped beside her and said nothing, which was its own kind of fluency.

"He'd have hated the attention," Imogen said. "He'd have hated it, and he'd have secretly cut the article out and kept it in the chart drawer."

"He'd have packed a tin of peaches to celebrate," Theo said.

"Yeah." Imogen cleared her throat. "Yeah, he would."

At the fire, Jun had woken and was holding his bandaged hands up to the warmth, and Odile was showing Sparrow a chart, tracing tomorrow's route downstream with a pencil stub — legal water, marked water, the ordinary beautiful miles below the last rapid. Marisol looked up and saw the two of them standing there and read the whole thing off their faces in one glance, because Marisol missed nothing except calories.

"Well?" she demanded. "Are we employed or are we unemployed? I need to know how much salt to be angry about."

"We're employed," Theo said.

A cheer went up — Sparrow's whoop cracking across the water, Jun raising both ruined hands like a referee signaling touchdown, Odile allowing herself a single nod that on Odile constituted jubilation. Marisol banged a spoon on a pot and declared that the trout were now a celebration and there would be consequences for anyone who didn't eat thirds.

Imogen sat down between Odile and the fire and let it all wash over her — the noise, the smell of alder smoke and trout fat, Sparrow immediately demanding to know if partners got matching jackets, Theo pretending to be annoyed and fooling no one. Above the canyon rim the first stars were out, and the glacier had gone quiet at last, settled, done with its breaking for now.

Later, when the plates were scraped and Sparrow had fallen asleep mid-sentence with her head on the chart case, Theo drifted over to where Imogen sat and lowered herself onto the gravel beside her.

"One condition I didn't put on paper," Theo said, eyes on the fire.

"Name it."

"Next season. We go upstream. All the way up." She said it steadily, but Imogen heard the cost of it, the way you hear the strain in a rope. "Past the marker. To Hal's grave. I want to plant a new buoy at the old line — the legal one — and then I want to sail past it. Not running from anything. Not racing anyone." She glanced sideways. "Just going home the long way."

Imogen looked at her oldest rival, her newest partner, this woman who had measured her whole life in miles because years had hurt too much to count, and saw that somewhere between the source lake and this fire, the arithmetic had changed.

"First thing on the chart," Imogen said. "I'll draw it myself."

Theo smiled — the rare full one, the one from before 2004, before the debt, before any of it — and reached over to turn the radio dial, hunting the night stations, the far signals, whatever provision the dark might be carrying down toward them even now.

Out of the static, faint and crackling, came a bulletin neither of them expected: a request, from a university research station two hundred miles north, for an experienced crew to run a supply and survey expedition up the Whitewater Fork — uncharted in its upper reaches, unrun in thirty years, paying well and starting in three weeks.

Sparrow, asleep, smiled.

Theo and Imogen looked at each other over the fire, and neither of them had to say it out loud.

But Imogen reached for the pen anyway.
