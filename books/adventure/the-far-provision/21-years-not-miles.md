## 21. Years, Not Miles

The river ran quiet in the way it only did after it had tried to kill you and failed.

Theo Blackwood stood at the rail of the *Provision* — what was left of her, patched hull, new planking over the old scars, a boat that had drowned once and decided against it — and watched the morning fog lift off the water in slow ribbons. Upstream, somewhere past the bend where the Kestrel narrowed to a throat of grey stone, there was a grave. There had been a grave for twenty years, and for twenty years she had charted every route on the continent that kept her from it.

She'd told herself it was the miles. That was the old arithmetic, the one she'd learned young and trusted like scripture: distance as currency, distance as anesthesia. Every storm chased, every debt accrued, every river run at speed was another mile between her and the things she couldn't look at straight on. She had measured her whole life that way, in odometer ticks and river miles, because miles were honest. Miles never asked her to sit still.

But she was sitting still now.

Behind her, the crew moved through their morning with the easy friction of people who had earned each other's noise. Jun was below deck re-torquing the engine mounts, muttering to himself in two languages, his bandaged hands working the wrenches with a patience that would have been unthinkable three weeks ago. Odile stood at the chart table correcting their survey notes in her small, ruthless handwriting, redrawing the Kestrel from memory now that the memory had been paid for. Sparrow sat on the cabin roof with her legs dangling, a battered radio manual open on her knees, teaching herself frequencies the legitimate way this time. And Marisol — Marisol had commandeered the galley and was filling the boat with the smell of coffee and frying bread, which was its own kind of navigation, holding them all steady on a bearing none of them could see yet.

Imogen stood at the bow, hands in her coat pockets, watching the same stretch of water Theo was watching. Twenty years of rivalry had burned down to something simpler and stranger: two women who knew exactly how much the other one had lost, and no longer needed to pretend otherwise.

"You're going to do it today," Imogen said. It wasn't a question.

Theo didn't answer right away. The fog was thinning. Through it, upstream, she could just make out the shape of the headland her mentor had called the Old Man — a granite shoulder leaning over the river, weathered into something almost like a profile, almost like a face looking down-current, always down-current, toward the sea he never reached.

"He's four miles up," Theo said finally. "Past the Narrows. There's a gravel bar on the east bank where we camped the night before." Her voice came out level, which surprised her. "I buried him there myself. Dug the cairn with my hands until they bled. I was twenty-four years old and I thought if I stacked enough stones the weight of them would hold me down too."

"And you've never gone back."

"I've flown over this drainage eleven times." Theo laughed, but there was nothing in it. "Eleven. Chasing storms, chasing contracts, chasing anything that moved fast enough to keep ahead of—" She stopped. Started again. "You know what I did instead of visiting? I sent money. Every year, to the regional survey office, earmarked for marker maintenance on the upper Kestrel. Paid strangers to tend the buoys along the route he died running. Like that counted. like paying rent on a guilt."

Imogen turned from the bow. In the grey light her face looked older than it had in the dockside bar a lifetime ago, softer around the eyes, harder around the mouth — a woman who had spent two decades carrying her father's name through the mud and had finally set it down. "My father's grave is a wall in a cemetery in a city I can't afford to live in," she said. "I visited it twice in twenty years. Both times I talked to a slab of granite about stock prices." She shook her head slowly. "At least yours is somewhere beautiful."

"It's somewhere forbidden."

"Was." Imogen smiled, and it was the first smile Theo had ever seen from her that didn't have an edge on it. "The Syndicate's writ died in a newsroom three days ago, Theo. There's no legal line on this river anymore. There's no illegal line either. There's just the river."

* * *

They ran the four miles slow.

That was the thing Theo kept noticing, the new habit she couldn't shake: slowness. For twenty years she had driven boats and trucks and herself at a pace calculated to outrun reflection, and now here she was, idling up the Kestrel at barely six knots, reading the water the way her mentor had taught her to read it — not as an obstacle course, not as a clock, but as a text. The V of current below the first riffle meant a shallow bar, shifting left since last season. The smooth green tongue against the far bank meant depth, an invitation. The standing wave train beyond it meant the river was talking in its sleep, dreaming of the flood it used to be.

Sparrow appeared beside her at the helm, silent in the way the kid had learned to be when silence was wanted. Fifteen years old, still too thin, still flinching at loud voices, but standing at Theo's shoulder now without being asked, the way an apprentice stands. In her hands she carried the old brass compass — the mentor's compass, salvaged from the wreck of the expedition plane along with everything else they'd pulled out of the source lake, cleaned and gimballed and running true.

"You should do it," Sparrow said. "Read the name out loud. Marisol says that's how it works. You say the name so the river knows who you're talking to."

"The river doesn't need my permission to know him."

"No," Sparrow agreed seriously. "But maybe you do."

Theo looked at her — this stray they'd pulled off a dock in a port town, this kid who had sold them out to save her brother and been forgiven for it, who had repaid that mercy by cracking Bell's operation open like an egg when it mattered most. Forgiveness, Theo was learning, wasn't a feeling. It was a practice. You did it in motion, the way Jun's ruined hands were healing: badly at first, then better, then as though they'd never been any other kind of hands.

"Steer us past the next bend," Theo said, and gave her the wheel.

* * *

The gravel bar was exactly where memory had put it, which felt like a small betrayal. Places weren't supposed to wait for you. Places were supposed to move on, flood over, forget.

But there it was: the east bank shingle, grey and gold in the strengthening sun, backed by alder scrub going scarlet at the edges with the turn of the season. And there, above the high-water line where the floods couldn't reach, the cairn. Smaller than she remembered. Twenty years of freeze-thaw had settled the stones together, tumbled some, grown moss in the joints like the mountain itself had begun the work of taking him back — or the work of holding him, depending on how you wanted to read it. Theo chose, deliberately, for the first time, to read it as holding.

She went ashore alone.

The others stayed with the boat — she heard Marisol's voice carrying across the water, telling some story or other, and Jun's laugh, and the clink of the coffee pot, the ordinary music of a crew at rest. They knew. Of course they knew; Odile had redrawn the charts and Imogen had laid out the route and nobody had said a word about why this stop was on it. That was the found-family contract, Theo thought, wading ashore through the shallows: you don't ask about the graves. You just make sure the boat stops near them.

The cairn was knee-high. She knelt in front of it, and her knees cracked, and she thought: forty-four years old. He was fifty-one. She had already outlived the gap between them by more than half, and every year now was a year he hadn't gotten, and for twenty years she had treated that fact like a debt too — one more ledger entry she could never pay down, so she stopped opening the book at all.

There was no plaque. She'd never been able to afford bronze, and later she'd understood that she hadn't wanted one. Names cut in metal were for strangers. What she'd left instead was carved into the capstone with a chisel, her own hand, the night of the burial, letters gone soft-edged with weather:

**E. VASQUEZ — HE READ THE WATER BETTER THAN ANY OF US**

Elias Vasquez. Fifty-one years old. The best riverman on the continent and the only one who ever swore off the Kestrel, because the Kestrel had taken two of his crew and very nearly taken his protégée, and because he believed — he used to say it with that half-smile, cleaning his pipe on his boot heel — that a man should be allowed to draw a line and keep it. *Some water isn't yours, Theo-girl. Some doors you knock on, you don't get to un-knock.*

And then she had frozen. In the whiteout, in the capsizing cold, with the raft tearing apart in the gorge below, she had frozen — twenty-four years old, competent, cocky, and utterly empty, while the older crew fought for the lines. And Elias had gone into that water in her place, or near enough to her place that the difference didn't matter, and the report had blamed his judgment, his route, his sworn-off promise broken under pressure, and she had let the report stand because the truth was a weight she couldn't lift and the lie at least floated.

She had burned the journal page in Chapter Five of this story — no. She corrected herself, kneeling in the shingle, blinking at the sun on the water. She had burned the journal page before they launched, weeks ago, and Jun had watched her do it and asked nothing, and the burning had not helped, because burning evidence is not the same as burying a body, and the body was this: *she froze, and he died, and she let him take the blame.*

"I'm sorry," she said.

Her voice sounded strange in the open air, unarmored, aimed at a pile of rocks. A raven lifted off the alders and complained about it and flew upstream.

"Elias Vasquez." She said the name properly this time, full volume, the way Sparrow had instructed, the way Marisol had promised would work. "Elias Vasquez. I froze. Do you hear me? I froze, and you went in, and I let them write it wrong for twenty years because the truth was heavier than I was." The words came faster now, twenty years of pressure behind them, the way the lake beneath the glacier had come down the drainage channel all at once when the ice finally let go. "I let them blame your judgment because I couldn't survive blaming mine. And I built a whole life on top of that lie like building on a sinkhole, and I called the building miles. I called it a career. I called it staying ahead."

The river ran. The moss held the stones together.

"But here's what I know now," she said, quieter. "Here's what the crew taught me, these strays, these debtors and deserters and kids in over their heads. Mercy is a choice, not a mistake. Sacrifice is a language. And a line you draw in fear isn't a line — it's a wall, and walls don't protect anybody, they just decide who gets locked out." She put her palm flat on the capstone, on the worn letters of his name. "You drew your line after 2004 and I've spent twenty years thinking the lesson was *never come back.* But that wasn't your lesson, old man. Your lesson was *know why you're on the water.* Know what you're running from and what you're running toward, and never confuse the two."

She took a breath, and it went all the way down, all the way to the bottom of her, where the frozen girl was still standing in the whiteout — and found, touching her, that the girl wasn't frozen anymore. Hadn't been for weeks, maybe. Maybe since the moment she'd said no to Cassius Bell on the shore of the source lake with a gun trained on her crew and chosen justice over the payout, chosen the truth over the ledger, chosen to break the pattern instead of repeating it.

"I forgive you," she said to the cairn, and then caught herself, and laughed — a real laugh this time, wet and cracked. "No. Wrong direction. Twenty years of getting the bearing backwards." She wiped her face with the back of her hand. "I forgive *me.* And I think — I think you forgave me the day you went into that water, Elias, because that's who you were, and I'm the only one who's been holding out."

She stayed a long time after that. Long enough for the sun to clear the headland and lay a stripe of gold across the shingle. She told him about the crew — about Jun's hands and Odile's charts and Marisol's kitchen and Sparrow's frequencies, about Imogen and the father whose name was clean now, about the files broadcast crackling out across every receiver in the basin while Bell's helicopter pad stood empty. She told him about the debt dying with the ledger. She told him she was measuring the year in birthdays now, not odometer ticks, and that it was harder than it sounded and worth it.

Then she did what she'd come to do. From her pack she took the new buoy — bright orange, factory-fresh, stenciled with the survey office's mark, paid for not with guilty anonymous donations this time but with money earned honestly and split five ways. She carried it down to the waterline, to the exact spot — she checked the old coordinates twice, because some precision is love — where the last legal marker had stood for sixty years before the Syndicate bought the river and moved the line.

She planted it in the shallows, anchored it with chain and stone, and stepped back.

The buoy rode the current, turning gently, orange as a struck match against the grey water. The old line. The line Elias had sworn off crossing. The line she had crossed in grief and rage and secrecy, and nearly died for it, and lost him.

Theo stood on the bank and looked at it for a long moment.

Then she waded back to the boat, and fired the engine, and took the wheel from Sparrow — who watched her with those sharp, waiting eyes — and swung the *Provision*'s bow upstream, past the buoy, across the line, into the water that had never belonged to anyone.

Nobody said anything. Nobody had to. Marisol raised her coffee mug from the galley doorway. Odile made a small notation on the chart and, Theo noticed, drew the line on the map and then, deliberately, crossed it out. Imogen leaned against the rail beside her and didn't smile, because it wasn't a smiling thing. It was a breathing thing.

"Not because the line's gone," Theo said, to the crew, to the river, to the man under the cairn falling astern. "Because I'm not."

The *Provision* ran upstream into the light, and for the first time in twenty years, Theo Blackwood was not running from anything at all.

Ahead, far upstream where the valley bent north, a smudge of canvas showed at the next bend — a sail, where no sail should be, on a river that had been empty of strangers for a month. Someone else had heard the river was free.
