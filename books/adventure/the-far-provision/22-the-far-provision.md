## 22. The Far Provision

The river was high with spring melt, the color of old bottle glass, and Sparrow had been awake since four.

She told herself it was because of the launch — first mile of a new season, and she was navigator-in-training now, which meant she'd been up half the night checking charts by lamplight until Odile had come down the dock, looked over her shoulder, and said only, "You've got the current backwards on the third bend," before walking away. That single sentence had kept her warm all night like a coal in her pocket. Odile didn't waste words. Odile's corrections were worth more than other people's praise.

But that wasn't really why she was awake. She was awake because a year ago this morning she had been standing on a dock in Kestrel Falls with a stolen radio handset pressed to her ear, feeding coordinates of the people who would become her family to the man who wanted them dead. A year ago this morning her brother was still a name on a Vane Syndicate ledger, a debt with a heartbeat. And a year ago today — or close enough; the exact date lived somewhere in the middle of the calving season, and nobody aboard liked to mark it precisely — the glacier had spoken, and the source lake had drained, and everything had changed.

Sparrow had learned that anniversaries don't wait for you to be ready. They just arrive, like weather.

She sat on the bow of the *Far Provision* with her boots dangling over black water and wrote the first entry of the new season's log in her careful, blocky hand:

**Season Three. Day One. Mile 0.0. Crew: seven souls, one dog, provisions for forty days. Weather: clear, cold, wind out of the north-northwest. River: high, fast, honest about it.**

Honest about it. That was Odile's phrase, and Sparrow had stolen it shamelessly, the way she stole most good things now. A year ago she would have written something clever instead, something sharp-edged to prove she belonged. Now she just wrote what was true. It turned out truth took less effort than performance, once you got used to it.

Behind her, the dock creaked under a familiar weight.

"You're supposed to log the mile *after* we make it," Theo said, settling beside her with two tin mugs. She handed one over without looking. Coffee, cut with condensed milk, sweet enough to strip paint. Marisol's recipe. Everything on this boat came from somebody's hands.

"We haven't made mile zero yet?" Sparrow said. "We're tied to the dock."

"Exactly. Zero is aspirational." Theo blew across her mug. In the gray pre-dawn light her face looked softer than it did at noon, the lines around her eyes less like scars and more like the record of a lot of laughing that had happened late in life. "Odile says you caught the current error."

"She said I had it backwards."

"That's how she says 'well done.' You'll learn to read her eventually. Took me three seasons." Theo sipped. Downriver, the first true light was coming off the water in long copper smears. "Nervous?"

"No," Sparrow said, which was a lie, and then, because the boat had cured her of lying too: "Yes. But not about the river."

Theo nodded slowly, watching the water as if she could read it from here, which — after twenty years and one catastrophic season and one impossible one — she probably could. "About the anniversary."

"It's not even the right day."

"Anniversaries drift. Like icebergs. You chart where they were and they show up where they want to." Theo's voice was easy, but Sparrow had learned to hear the ballast under it. "For what it's worth — Bell's people didn't come for you because you were weak. They came because you loved your brother more than you were afraid of them. That's not a flaw, kid. That's the whole engine."

Sparrow stared hard at the water so she wouldn't have to let Theo see her face do whatever it was doing. Her brother had been free for eleven months now. He sent postcards from a fishing town on the coast, terrible handwriting, worse jokes. Last month he'd asked if there was room aboard for a deckhand who could gut a fish in nine seconds. Jun had said yes before Theo finished reading the letter aloud.

"He'll be here Thursday," Sparrow said. "He wants to run the Narrows with us."

"Then he'd better be ready," Theo said, and grinned, and the grin was the same one Sparrow had watched from the stern during the first rapids run of her life, the one that had made her think: *I want to be that when I grow up.* She was sixteen now. She was working on it.

* * *

By full light the dock was chaos, which meant it was normal.

Jun was already elbow-deep in the engine well, arguing with the starboard motor in low, affectionate Korean, his scarred hands moving with a gentleness that still surprised people who hadn't seen him portage a boat over a mountain. The scars had faded to pale seams. He never talked about the Long Drop anymore except once, quietly, to Sparrow, on the one-year mark of the flood: *"The water took my breath and gave it back. That's the deal you make with rivers. You just have to mean it."*

Marisol came down the gangplank carrying a crate of provisions balanced on one shoulder and a bag of limes in the other hand, singing something in three-quarter time that was either a work song or a love song or both. She had put on weight over the winter, cooking in a shore kitchen in Kestrel Falls, feeding half the town, and she claimed it was ballast. Nobody argued. Marisol's food had gotten them through more than hunger; it had gotten them through the nights after, the ones where nobody could sleep and nobody could talk, and the pot on the stove did the talking instead.

Odile arrived last, as always, carrying nothing but her chart case and a look that suggested she had been awake since before the stars went down, memorizing the sky. She was older than the rest of them by a margin nobody could pin down, and she moved like the river owed her money. She stepped aboard, glanced at Sparrow's open logbook, read the entry upside down, and said:

"Honest about it. Good."

Sparrow felt the coal in her chest flare again.

And then Imogen Reyes came down the dock in expedition khakis that had clearly never seen an actual expedition, carrying a clipboard, and the whole mood of the morning shifted into something brighter and more ridiculous.

"Manifest," Imogen announced. "Signatures. Insurance — yes, real insurance, Theo, don't make that face, we are a *legitimate enterprise* now—"

"We are a salvage outfit with a newsletter," Theo said.

"We are a *licensed* salvage outfit with a newsletter and a waiting list." Imogen handed the clipboard around. Her hair was shorter than it had been a year ago; she'd cut it the week the Syndicate fell, she'd told them, because she wanted to feel the weather on her neck again. Her father's name was cleared now — formally, publicly, in print, with apologies from institutions that had spent twenty years calling him a liar. There was a small stone marker for him in Kestrel Falls cemetery, near another one, older, that the whole crew visited together every spring. "Two paying clients this season. Scientists. Glacier survey. They want the upper reaches."

"The upper reaches," Theo repeated.

"The legal upper reaches," Imogen said quickly. "Below the line. I checked. Twice."

"I know you checked twice. I'm teasing." Theo signed the manifest with a flourish that Sparrow suspected was deliberately theatrical. "Though I'll admit the forbidden water has lost some of its charm now that it's allowed."

"It was never the forbidden part you loved," Odile said dryly, stowing her charts. "It was the being trusted with it."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Above the tree line, invisible from the dock but present in everyone's mind the way a scar is present under a sleeve, the glacier sat in its valley of light. The calving season had opened early again this year — earlier every year, the scientists said, and they were the clients, and they were paying to understand why. The crew would take them up as far as the law allowed and no farther, and they would watch the ice give way from a respectful distance, and they would bring back data instead of secrets.

That was the trade now. Knowledge instead of treasure. It paid worse. It slept better.

* * *

They cast off at 7:14 in the morning, and Sparrow logged it: **Mile 0.0, departed.**

The new strays came aboard the way strays always did — sideways, watchful, pretending they weren't hoping to be kept.

There was Dev, nineteen, a mechanic who'd washed out of three jobs for "attitude problems," which Jun diagnosed within an hour as "asking why too many times," and pronounced a virtue. There was Ruth, mid-forties, a former schoolteacher who had sold everything after a divorce and shown up at the dock with a duffel bag and a knife roll and said she could cook, and Marisol had tasted her soup and hired her on the spot, which everyone understood was the highest honor Marisol conferred. And there was the dog — a rangy, river-colored mutt who had simply walked aboard during loading, lain down on the spare coil of rope, and declined all invitations to leave. Theo had named him Buoy. Buoy had accepted the appointment with the gravity of a man accepting a judgeship.

Sparrow watched the new ones the way she remembered being watched, that first dawn a year ago — no, longer ago than that, the day she'd signed on as a deckhand with stolen radio equipment hidden in her kit and a brother's life chained to her silence. She remembered how Theo had looked at her then. Not warm. Not cold. *Measuring.* Taking her weight like a navigator takes a fix: where is this one, really, and can I steer by her?

Now Sparrow stood at the chart table beside Odile and did the same thing, and found she could read the new strays almost as easily as water. Dev was hungry to be useful. Ruth was hungry to be needed. Both of them kept glancing at the crew the way you glance at a fire you've been invited to stand near — wanting the warmth, not quite believing it yet.

"They'll ask to stay by the end of the season," Odile said, not looking up from her charts. "They always do."

"Did you?" Sparrow asked. "Ask?"

A pause. Odile's pencil hovered over the third bend, the one whose current Sparrow had finally gotten right. "No," she said. "I was already aboard when I boarded. Some of us just take longer to notice."

Sparrow wrote that down later, in the back pages of the log where she kept things that weren't navigation. She had filled eleven pages that way over the winter. Things Odile said. Things Theo said at the grave upstream, though Sparrow had only heard that secondhand, from Jun, who had been there. Things Marisol sang when she thought no one was listening.

* * *

The first rapids came at noon, nothing serious — Class III, a long churning chute the locals called the Washboard — and Sparrow took the bow call position for the first time in her life.

Her mouth went dry. Her hands knew the lines; she'd drilled them all winter against the chart table until Odile said "enough" in the voice that meant *good*. But knowing and saying were different animals, and the river was loud, and behind her were six people and a dog and a season, all of them steered by her voice.

Theo stood at the helm and looked at her. Just looked. Waiting.

Sparrow planted her feet, gripped the bow rail, and called the water.

"*Right channel! Rock garden center — thread left of the white tongue — hold — punch — HARD LEFT, eddy line coming—*"

The *Far Provision* went through the Washboard like a needle through cloth. Water broke green over the bow and hit Sparrow full in the chest, shockingly cold, shockingly alive, and she whooped — actually whooped, loud and undignified, and heard Dev whoop back from amidships, and Ruth laughing, and Buoy barking at the river itself, and Marisol's voice rising over all of it in that three-quarter-time song, and underneath everything the deep steady diesel hum of Jun's engines running clean.

In the sudden quiet below the rapids, Theo called forward: "Navigator. Your line or mine?"

"Mine," Sparrow said.

"Damn right it was."

* * *

They camped that night on a gravel bar below the Washboard, and Marisol and Ruth cooked together — the first collaboration, the beginning of some new tradition nobody named — and the smell of onions and river fish and woodsmoke settled over the camp like a blessing.

Sparrow found Theo sitting apart, a little upstream, knees up, watching the water go by in the last light. She carried two mugs, because that was how it was done aboard now, and sat down without asking, because that was also how it was done.

For a while neither of them spoke. The river talked enough for both of them.

"Do you ever count it?" Sparrow asked finally. "The miles? Since... everything?"

Theo considered the question with the seriousness she gave all of Sparrow's questions, which was one of the thousand reasons Sparrow would follow her anywhere. "I used to count miles the way other people count years," she said. "Every one of them a place I wasn't staying. Twenty years of odometer ticks, and if you'd asked me what any of them added up to, I couldn't have told you. Distance isn't a life, kid. It's just the shape avoidance leaves behind." She turned the mug in her hands. "This year I counted differently. I counted birthdays. Jun's, in the fall — he let us put a candle in a canned peach, which he pretended to hate. Yours, in the winter. Sixteen. We stopped the boat for it, middle of nowhere, and Marisol made a cake in a Dutch oven on a wood stove, and it burned on the bottom and it was the best cake I've ever eaten. Odile's birthday, we still don't know the date, so we picked one, and she let us, which from Odile is basically weeping with joy." She smiled at the water. "Imogen's is next month. She thinks we forgot. We're going to ambush her at the confluence."

"That's not much of a measurement," Sparrow said. "Birthdays. One a year."

"It's the only one that holds still," Theo said. "Miles move. People stay. You measure your life by what stays." She knocked her mug gently against Sparrow's. "Happy new season, Navigator."

"Happy new season, Captain."

Upstream, the others were laughing at something Dev had said, and Ruth was telling Marisol about a soup her grandmother used to make, and Buoy was asleep in the coals' warmth with his paws twitching through some dream of rivers yet unrun. The firelight made a small gold country on the gravel bar, population seven and a dog, and beyond it the dark water went on north toward mountains none of them could see, carrying them toward a season none of them could chart.

Sparrow opened the logbook on her knees and wrote by firelight, slow and careful:

**Day One. Miles logged: 34. Birthdays ahead: five known, one to be discovered. Strays aboard: three human, one dog, all provisionally home. Weather: clear. River: honest about it. Crew: —**

She paused, pencil hovering, and looked up at them all — Theo with her face finally at rest, Jun's ruined gentle hands turning a spit, Odile charting stars, Marisol singing, the new ones leaning toward the fire like plants toward light.

**Crew: family. All hands accounted for.**

Far downstream, past the last bend where the firelight couldn't reach, something glinted on the water — a buoy, maybe, or a bit of wreck-flotsam from some older season, riding the current toward them out of the dark. Sparrow watched it come, and reached for the spotlight, and the new season turned its first page toward whatever the river was bringing next.
