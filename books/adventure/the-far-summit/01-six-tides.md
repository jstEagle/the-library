## 1. Six Tides

The storm came in off the Far Summit coast like something owed.

Ansel Thornbury had been watching it build for three days on the offshore feeds—a low, sullen knot of pressure crawling up from the warm water, too disorganized to name, too stubborn to die. By dawn it had found its spine. By noon it was throwing spray over the cliff road in sheets that turned the world gray and moving. She stood at the pull-off above Cutter's Reach with her camera braced against the gale and filmed the sea hitting the rocks like a debt collector's fist on a door.

*Six tides,* she thought, and let the number sit in her chest where it had lived for eleven days now, heavy as ballast. Six tides until the Vane brothers' deadline. Six tides until whatever patience Dree Vane possessed—and Ansel had met enough of the family to know it wasn't patience but appetite—ran out entirely.

The wind tried to take the camera out of her hands. She leaned into it, boots planted in the shingle, and kept rolling. The footage would be good. It was always good; that was the one thing she could still do without lying about it. Storm cells along this coast were unpredictable enough that the syndicates paid for fresh imagery—salvage fleets wanted to know what the weather intended before they committed hulls to it, and the insurance brokers in Port Calloway paid by the minute for clean, timestamped proof of conditions. Nobody asked a storm chaser for references. That was the beauty of the trade. You were hired precisely because you were the kind of person who stood alone on cliffs in weather that killed people, and nobody who did that had a past worth checking.

She checked the light meter with numb fingers. Forty minutes of usable light left, maybe less if the squall line came through early.

Behind her, parked crooked on the gravel shoulder, sat everything she owned: a rust-spotted chase rig with a cracked windshield and a fuel gauge that lied optimistically, a roof rack of instruments she'd built herself from salvage, and inside the cab, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, three objects that constituted the entire ledger of her life.

A compass. A camera. And a forged surveyor's license bearing the name *Maren Voss.*

She'd take inventory again tonight, the way she did every night, because counting what you had was the only accounting left when you couldn't count on anything else. But she knew them already, knew them the way you know your own teeth. The compass had belonged to her father—brass-cased, glass clouded at the edges, the needle still true after forty years, which was more than could be said for most things. The camera was newer, mortgaged in its own quiet way, every lens a month of payments. And the license—

The license was the newest thing she owned. Three weeks old, bought from a document man in Port Calloway who worked out of a chandlery and charged double for quality. *Maren Voss, Coastal Surveyor, Licensed Under Authority of the Harbor Commission.* The seal was good. The paper stock was right. The photograph was her own face, which was the risk and also the point: a surveyor needed a face that looked like it had been rained on for years, and hers did.

Ansel Thornbury, meanwhile, existed nowhere. She'd made sure of that two years ago, in another province, in another life, with a fire and a ferry ticket and a name left behind like a coat too recognizable to keep. Invisibility wasn't a condition for her. It was a discipline. You didn't stand out. You didn't sign anything twice with the same hand. You paid cash, tipped exactly average, learned the names of everyone and gave yours to no one. You became weather—present everywhere, fixed nowhere, impossible to subpoena.

It had worked for two years. Then the Vanes had found her anyway.

Not her—the rig. That was the bitter joke of it. She'd financed engine repairs through a broker in Salt Hollow's neighboring port, signed something without reading all of it because the mechanic was holding her transmission hostage and the storm season was starting, and six months later the paper had migrated, the way bad paper does, into the portfolio of Vane Salvage & Recovery. The interest had grown like kelp. The terms had shifted like sandbars. And then, four months ago, a letter had arrived—not a demand, not yet, but an invitation. *Dree Vane would be pleased to discuss restructuring.* Dree Vane was never pleased to discuss anything. Dree Vane discussed things the way a tide discusses a sandcastle.

Now the deadline: six tides. And the sum owing had swollen past anything the storm footage would cover, past anything honest work would cover in a year, and Ansel had begun to understand—with the slow, cold certainty of a hull taking water—that the debt was not designed to be repaid. Debts like these were designed to do something to the debtor. She just didn't know what yet.

The first squall line hit while she was packing the camera. Rain came sideways, hard as flung gravel, and she got the gear stowed and herself into the cab and sat there a moment with the engine running and the wipers losing their argument with the sky, watching the white water detonate against the black rocks below.

Her comm unit crackled. She almost didn't answer. Then she saw the routing code—Vane Salvage & Recovery, direct line—and answered, because ignoring it would be worse.

"Thornbury." Dree Vane's voice came through smooth as oiled rope. "My brother tells me you're filming today."

"I'm always filming when there's weather."

"Mm. And how is the weather?"

"Building. Landfall inside a week, if it holds together." Ansel kept her voice flat, professional, a tradeswoman reporting conditions. "You'll want your fleet berthed or bled dry, depending on what you're planning."

A pause. On the line, faintly, the sound of a harbor—gulls, halyards, the great patient groan of ships at rest. "We're planning," Dree said, "to be busy. Which is why I'll remind you, gently, that your account comes due at the sixth tide from today. My brother has counted them on the chart already. He's very thorough."

"I know the terms."

"Do you?" Something almost kind entered the voice, which was the most frightening thing about it. "I hope so, Thornbury. I'd hate for you to be surprised. Surprises are for people who haven't been paying attention, and I've always had the sense that you pay attention to everything." Another pause, longer, and when Dree spoke again the kindness was gone, folded away like a chart no longer needed. "Six tides. Bring the account current, or bring me something better than money. We'll talk either way, I'm sure."

The line went dead.

Ansel sat with the comm in her lap and the rain hammering the roof and did the arithmetic she'd been doing for eleven days, the arithmetic that never changed. Footage sale: maybe a tenth of the debt. Rig, sold whole: another tenth, and then she'd have no trade, no shelter, no wheels. Everything she owned, liquidated at panic prices, wouldn't make a dent. The Vanes knew that. They had to know that. So why set a deadline at all?

Unless the deadline wasn't about the money.

She put the thought away the way she put away all thoughts that had no actionable edge to them—carefully, in the dark, where they couldn't see her flinch—and drove north along the cliff road into the thickening weather, because the storm was building and the storm was the only thing today that would pay her, and a woman six tides from drowning takes what work the sea offers.

* * *

She rode the storm's leading edge for two hours, leapfrogging the squalls, filming from three pull-offs as the cell organized itself into something with intent. The light went strange and greenish, the way it did before real weather, and the sea turned the color of gunmetal and began to move with a long, rolling purpose that made even her stomach tighten. Good footage. Sellable footage. She logged timestamps and coordinates with mechanical precision and told herself the numbers meant safety, the way she always did, knowing they didn't.

By late afternoon the rain had settled into a steady, businesslike downpour and the wind backed around, and Ansel pulled the rig onto a shelf of ground above a stretch of coast she didn't know well—north of Cutter's Reach, past the last of the marked roads, where the cliffs ran higher and the charts went thin. No harbors here. No fleet traffic. Just the Far Summit headlands rising black out of the murk and the long empty strand below, being eaten by the tide.

She ate cold rice from a tin and drank coffee from a thermos gone lukewarm and took inventory, because it was night now, and night was for counting.

Compass. Her father's. Brass case worn bright at the pivot where his thumb had rested for thirty years. It pointed north the way old men point at the horizon—without hurry, without doubt. It was the only thing she owned that had never once lied to her, and she kept it in her breast pocket, over the heart, like a talisman she was almost too practical to believe in.

Camera. Two bodies, four lenses, a waterproof housing she'd rebuilt twice. Worth perhaps a month of the debt's interest. She loved it anyway, in the way you love a tool that has kept you alive.

License. *Maren Voss.* She held it up to the dash light and studied the photograph—her own face, two years older than the woman who'd burned her name, harder at the eyes. Forged documents were a strange kind of property. They appreciated with use. Every job she took under Maren's name, every signature, every small verified lie, added weight to the fiction until it might someday outweigh the woman underneath it. Some nights that felt like freedom. Tonight it felt like erosion.

And beneath those three, the invisible inventory, the liabilities column: one debt, compounding. One deadline, six tides. One identity, borrowed and thinning. And somewhere behind all of it, unspoken even in the privacy of her own skull, the question she refused to ask: *what do the Vanes actually want from me?*

Because they wanted something. Money was the shape the want wore, but money was never the substance. She'd watched the syndicate operate from the outside for two years—watched them buy harbormasters, foreclose on boats, absorb whole fishing families into "recovery contracts" that outlived the men who signed them. The Vanes collected obligations the way other families collected heirlooms. And Ansel Thornbury, who owed them more than she could ever pay, had finally become an heirloom waiting to be claimed.

She washed the rice tin in the rain and racked the camera and told herself, as she told herself every night, that tomorrow she'd find the angle. The footage sale plus a side contract plus—something. There was always something. Weary defiance was a fuel like any other; it burned dirty, but it burned.

Sleep came in shallow pulls between gusts. Once, deep in the small hours, the wind dropped so completely that she woke thinking the storm had died—and heard instead the sea doing something she'd never heard it do in two years on this coast: pulling back. A vast, wet, sucking exhalation, going on far too long, the sound of an ocean drawing breath.

She sat up in the dark cab, heart tapping fast, and told herself it was the storm surge oscillating, some tidal resonance, nothing. The charts said the springs weren't due for days.

But the water kept withdrawing, and the silence where the surf should have been stretched on and on, and Ansel Thornbury—who paid attention to everything, who survived by noticing what the sea intended before it intended it—sat very still in the darkness and felt the hair rise on her arms.

Whatever the tide was doing out there, it was doing something no chart predicted.

And when the tide gave something back—as tides, eventually, always did—she had the sudden, unshakable feeling that it was going to give it back to her.
