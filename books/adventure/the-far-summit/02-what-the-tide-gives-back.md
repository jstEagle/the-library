## 2. What the Tide Gives Back

The tide went out like a held breath finally released.

Ansel had been watching the water all morning from the cliff path, thermos of bitter tea cooling in her hand, and she'd never seen anything like it. The ocean didn't just recede—it withdrew, peeling back from the coast in long silver sheets, leaving the seabed naked and steaming under a bruised, low sky. Gulls stalked the exposed flats in bewildered silence. Somewhere down the coast, the fishing boats at Salt Hollow would be dragging their hulls up on the sand, muttering about signs and omens.

She'd read about tides like this in her grandfather's almanac—spring tides stacked on a storm surge trough, the moon pulling one way and the monsoon's low pressure pushing the other until the sea forgot itself entirely. A once-in-a-generation drawdown. The kind of thing that happened to other people.

Her camera was already in her hands before she'd made the decision to climb down.

The cliff path was a goat track at best, switchbacking through gorse and crumbling shale, and she took it too fast, boots skidding, heart hammering with something that wasn't fear—or wasn't only fear. Below her, where the charts said there should be ten fathoms of cold green water, lay a landscape no living soul was supposed to see: kelp forests flattened like drowned meadows, boulders furred with barnacle, channels of trapped seawater glinting like spilled mercury.

And beneath the headland, half-sheltered by a fallen shelf of rock, a black opening in the cliff face.

A cave. Dry, or nearly dry, its mouth yawning above a floor of wet stone still dripping with the retreating sea.

Ansel stood at the bottom of the path for a long moment, wind tearing at her hair, and told herself the truth she'd been dodging since dawn: she hadn't come to film storm footage. She'd seen the tide tables last night, run the numbers, set her alarm. She'd known.

*You're not a surveyor,* said the small honest voice she kept locked in the back of her skull next to her real name. *You're a scavenger with a camera.*

"Shut up," she said aloud, and started across the seabed.

* * *

The cave swallowed sound whole.

Inside, her footsteps echoed off walls ribbed with mineral stain, amber and rust and deep sea-green, as though the rock had been bleeding color for centuries. Her headlamp carved a cone out of the dark. The air tasted of salt and old wood and something else—something organic and sweetish, like the inside of an antique shop, like time itself going soft.

The floor rose gently away from the entrance, shelved stone worn smooth, and forty paces in she stopped dead, because the light found what shouldn't be there.

Timber.

Not driftwood. Not flotsam. *Ship's timber*—great curved ribs of black oak rising out of the cave floor like the skeleton of some beached leviathan, running back into the deeper dark of the cavern. Planking clung to them in places, caulked seams still tight after—she did the math twice because the first answer felt impossible—at least two centuries. The wreck had been carried in by some ancient surge, or hauled here deliberately, and then the sea had kept it, sealed it, hidden it behind a wall of water no salvager could cross.

Ansel sank into a crouch, breath fogging in her headlamp beam. Her hands were shaking. Not from cold.

She touched the nearest rib. The oak was dense as iron, preserved in the cold and the dark and the anaerobic mud that still crusted its base. And there, where the growth of stone and shell had been chipped away—

A mark.

A six-pointed star inside a circle, stamped deep into the timber, the punch-blow crisp enough that she could count the points. She knew it. Every salvage man and wreck diver between here and the capital knew it, the way they knew the shape of their own greed: the mark of the *Meridian Star*, the colonial treasure ship that had broken apart off the Far Summit coast in 1743 carrying—according to every ledger, lawsuit, and drunk sailor's story since—a cargo of gold so vast it had bankrupted two insurance houses and launched a hundred doomed expeditions.

They'd all looked in the wrong place. For three hundred years, they'd all looked in open water.

"It was never lost," Ansel whispered. "It was *put here*."

Because now she saw the rest of it. The timbers weren't scattered the way a storm scatters a wreck—they were arranged. Ribs braced against the cave wall, planking stacked, cargo hold sections nested inside each other like cups. Someone had brought this ship into this cavern piece by piece, or buried it here whole and built the cave around its bones, and either way it meant hands. Human hands. Generations of them.

She swung her lamp along the wall and found the proof.

Carvings. Cut into the living stone in rows, at heights that suggested children had helped make some of them: spirals, wave-patterns, a repeated motif of a star being lowered into an open hand. And beneath them, older still, almost worn to nothing, lines of script in no alphabet she recognized—but with the same cadence, the same patient repetition. A record. A ledger written in stone. Names, maybe. Dates, maybe. An oath, renewed so many times the rock had gone glassy under the chisel marks.

The message wasn't subtle, even if the language was: *we were given this, we keep this, we do not give it up.*

Ansel sat back on her heels in the dark, in the belly of the thing everyone wanted, and felt two feelings arrive in the same instant, jammed together like opposing swells.

Wonder came first—pure, unmoneyed awe, the kind she hadn't felt since she was nine years old chasing thunderstorms across her grandfather's wheat fields, when the world was still enormous. This was the find of a lifetime. Of fifty lifetimes. She was standing inside a secret older than the debt that owned her, older than the Vanes' syndicate, older than the lie of her own name.

Then greed arrived, right on schedule, wearing wonder's face.

Because she knew exactly what this was worth. She could see the auction catalogue in her mind, the salvage-rights hearing, the Vane brothers' faces when she walked into Dree's office with coordinates instead of excuses. Three hundred years of gold, plus the wreck itself, plus whatever those carvings proved about who'd hidden it—and the carvings might be worth more than the cargo, if they pointed at someone. They always pointed at someone. Fortunes had foundations, and foundations could be undermined, and people paid fortunes to make sure theirs stayed standing.

*One photograph of that star stamp,* she thought, *and I'm free.*

Her camera was already up. That was the terrible part—her hands knew the work even while her conscience was still arguing. Shutter click: the ribs of black oak receding into dark. Click: the star-and-circle stamp, macro lens, every point of it sharp. Click: the carved wall, raking light across the chisel marks so the relief stood proud. Click, click, click—the spiral, the waves, the star lowered into the open hand. She worked the cavern methodically, corner to corner, the way she filmed storms: assume you won't get a second pass.

The memory cards went into the waterproof pouch inside her jacket, against her sternum, where she kept the things she couldn't afford to lose: her compass, her forged license, and now this.

She told herself she was documenting. She told herself evidence needed protecting, that the sea would take it all back within hours, that knowledge was neutral. She told herself she'd decide later what to do with it, and she let herself believe that, the way you can believe anything with your pulse up and gold-adjacent oak under your hands.

But her eye caught on the carving one more time as she packed up—the star in the open palm—and what snagged her wasn't the symbol. It was the care. Whoever cut that line had taken their time. Generations of someones had taken their time. This wasn't treasure hoarded; it was treasure *entrusted*. And somewhere out past the cave mouth, on the cliffs above, there were people who had spent three hundred years keeping an oath she'd just photographed for leverage.

"They'll never know," she said to the dark, and hated how much it sounded like a prayer.

* * *

The tide turned while she was still climbing out.

She heard it before she saw it—the seabed below the cave mouth beginning to whisper and gurgle, channels refilling, the first thin sheets of water sliding back over flats that had been dry stone an hour ago. The sea was coming home. In ninety minutes the cave mouth would be thirty feet underwater, and the Meridian Star would go back to being a rumor.

Ansel stood on the ledge outside the cavern and watched the ocean return, wave by patient wave, erasing her footprints from the seabed like a creditor reclaiming collateral.

Her phone had one bar of signal, clinging to the cliff face like everything else out here. Two messages. The first was automated: a weather advisory, monsoon tracking northwest, landfall estimate revised—*six days*. Six days until the fleet couldn't sail and the coast shut down and every option but one closed forever.

The second message was from a number she knew better than her own name.

**Progress?**

One word. Dree Vane didn't waste punctuation on her debtors.

Ansel looked at the phone. Looked back at the water swallowing the cave. Somewhere in her jacket, pressed against her heartbeat, were photographs worth more than her life, and somewhere behind that single question mark was a woman who collected lives the way other people collected debts—which is to say, professionally.

Ansel typed with numb thumbs:

*Promising lead. Need four more days.*

She hit send before she could soften it, then stood there in the wind doing the arithmetic she was best at: six days of tide left before the monsoon. Four days promised. One set of photographs that could buy her freedom or bury her, depending on which way she played it, and no version of the math where everybody walked away whole.

Far down the coast, half-hidden in the rain haze, smoke rose from a cluster of chimneys she'd never paid attention to before. A village. Salt-stained rooftops, a breakwater, boats pulled up high above the tideline—pulled up *very* high, she noticed, higher than any ordinary spring tide required, as if the people there had known exactly how far the sea would withdraw today, and planned around it.

People who kept calendars older than the charts.

Ansel pocketed the phone and started walking toward the smoke.

If the cave had keepers, she wanted to meet them before the Vanes' fleet did—and if her photographs were going to sell somebody out, she decided, walking faster, telling herself it was reconnaissance and nothing more, she'd at least learn the price of what she was selling.
