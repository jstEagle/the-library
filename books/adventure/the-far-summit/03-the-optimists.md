## 3. The Optimists

The harbor office at Far Summit kept a single working telegraph line, and it cost more per word than Ansel's breakfast. She stood at the counter with her message half-composed in her head, watching the clerk's fingers hover over the key, and counted words like a miser counts coins.

She had learned long ago that optimism was a currency. The Vanes' syndicate ran on it—the way a shark runs on water. Every debtor was an optimist by necessity; you had to believe the next job, the next sale, the next season would clear what the last three hadn't. Dree Vane collected optimists the way other collectors gathered stamps or shells, and she kept them alive precisely as long as their optimism remained profitable.

Ansel had been optimistic for eleven months now. It had bought her a boat with a soft spot in the hull, a camera with salt corrosion in the shutter, and a debt that grew like kelp—fast, silent, and anchored somewhere below where she could see.

*FOUND PROMISING LEAD STOP SURVEY WORK COAST NORTH OF FAR SUMMIT STOP REQUIRE FOURTEEN DAYS EXTENSION STOP WILL REPORT WITH DOCUMENTATION STOP A. THORNBURY*

Fourteen days. Six tides, if she was honest, though she'd padded the number the way surveyors pad estimates—enough slack to absorb disaster without looking like she was begging. She paid for the transmission with coins she needed for fuel and watched the clerk's hand send her lie down the wire to the Vane brothers' office in Corvane City, two hundred miles south.

Then she walked out into the gray morning and told herself the second lie of the day: that she had time.

* * *

Her camp was a lean-to above the tideline, three miles from the cave's mouth, pitched where the cliff shadow swallowed it after noon. She'd spent the night going through the photographs, and by lamplight they looked less like evidence and more like scripture.

The timbers stamped with the Meridian Star's mark—she'd counted nine frames of them. The carvings on the cave wall: a ship rendered in profile, lines cut deep and deliberate, and beneath it a row of names worn nearly smooth by salt air but still legible if you crouched and angled your light just so. Not a memorial, she'd decided. A ledger. Someone had wanted the names kept, and someone else had wanted them hidden, and both someones had been dead for a century and a half.

And the gold—or the rumor of it. She hadn't found coin. What she'd found was better: a manifest fragment, wax-sealed, its edges soft as old bread, listing cargo in a clerk's cramped hand. *Specie, assorted. Bonds, colonial issue. Papers, sealed, hold B.* Papers. Gold could be melted down and made anonymous. Papers had names on them.

She sat with the manifest fragment wrapped in oilcloth inside her coat, against her ribs, and felt the familiar arithmetic begin. A salvage claim on a wreck that age would be contested, litigated, bled dry by lawyers before she saw a copper. But sold privately—to a buyer who didn't ask questions about provenance—it could clear the debt twice over. Three times. She could disappear. New coast, new name, new camera. The fantasy arrived on schedule, warm as brandy, and she let it sit in her chest for exactly as long as it took to remember that the Vane brothers did not accept payment. They accepted ownership. Everyone who ever cleared a debt with them stayed cleared because they'd been absorbed, folded into the syndicate's ledger as assets rather than freed as people.

Still. Fourteen days. She could work with fourteen days.

The reply came faster than any reply from Corvane City had ever come.

* * *

It arrived at the harbor office two mornings later, and the clerk handed it over with a look she couldn't read—an expression she'd later understand as pity, though at the time she took it for indigestion. She carried the envelope up the cliff path before opening it, out of habit, out of the animal instinct that said never open Vane correspondence where Vane eyes might find you.

Dree Vane wrote in a fine, upright hand, the ink always a deep expensive blue. Ansel had four of her letters saved from the past year, and she knew their grammar: the opening warmth, the middle courtesy, the closing hook. This one ran longer than usual.

---

*Dear Miss Thornbury,*

*Your message was received with great pleasure. A promising lead! How the brothers and I have enjoyed your persistence this past year. You chase storms, we are told, and now it seems you have chased one all the way to something worth the chasing.*

*We grant your extension gladly. Take your fourteen days—take twenty, if the documentation requires it. We ask only that you be thorough. Thoroughness is a virtue we prize above nearly all others.*

*You will forgive an old woman's enthusiasm, but your letter stirred memories. Our grandfather, Osric Vane, kept a journal in his last years, and in it he writes often of a ship called the Meridian Star. He writes of it the way men write of things they have lost—not gold, mind you, though there was gold—but of papers. Hold B, I believe your profession would call it. He spent thirty years searching the Far Summit coast for that vessel, and died believing it lay under a particular stretch of cliff, though he never found the mouth of it. The sea keeps its own schedule, I suppose. Perhaps the tide has finally turned in our favor.*

*Do be careful with the papers, Miss Thornbury. Old documents are fragile things, and old fortunes fragile-er. Some families build on sand and are much distressed when the sand is examined too closely.*

*A small practical matter: our records show your account standing at four thousand, two hundred and sixty marks, with interest accruing daily. We note this not to press you—we are patient people—but because we have taken the liberty of reclassifying your file. You were listed as a debtor. You are now listed as an asset. It is a promotion, of sorts. Assets are worth protecting.*

*With confidence in your thoroughness,*
*Dree Vane*

---

Ansel read it three times. Then she sat down hard on the cliff grass, because her legs had decided the matter without consulting her.

*Hold B.* She had never transmitted those words. She had never written them anywhere the wire could carry them. The manifest fragment was wrapped in oilcloth against her ribs at this very moment, and no living soul knew the phrase except her—and whoever had written the original, a hundred and fifty years dead.

Which meant either Dree Vane was guessing with the precision of a knife thrower, or—

Or the Vanes had known about the Meridian Star before Ansel ever found it. Before her debt. Before her first late payment, her first threatening letter, her first desperate acceptance of a loan whose terms she hadn't read closely enough. Osric Vane had hunted this wreck for thirty years and died short of it, and his grandchildren had inherited the hunt along with the fortune, and somewhere in that inheritance a decision had been made: stop searching the coast. Start searching the people. Buy up every diver, every fisherman, every broke storm-chaser with a boat and a hunger, and wait for one of them to stumble onto what a generation of salvage crews could not find.

Ansel looked down at her hands. They were steady, which surprised her. Inside, something was dropping—a long vertical fall, the floor of her plan giving way floor by floor, each landing worse than the last.

The debt had never been meant to be repaid. That was the thing she finally understood, sitting in the wind with the letter trembling in her grip. Debts were bait. Four thousand marks was nothing to the Vanes—it was the price of a fishing hook, and she was the fish, and the hook had been set the day they approved her first loan against equipment she barely owned. They hadn't lent her money. They'd rented her eyes and her boat and her willingness to go places respectable people wouldn't, and now that she'd found the cave, the extension wasn't mercy. It was a leash being played out slowly, so she'd swim a little further from the boat before the line came tight.

*Asset.* The word sat in her stomach like swallowed ice.

And underneath the fear, colder still: *someone told them.* The phrase *hold B* existed in exactly two places—the flooded cave, and the manifest fragment against her ribs. Unless it existed in a third place she hadn't found yet. Unless the carvings, the names, the whole hidden architecture of that cave belonged to a community that had kept the secret for generations, and one of them had talked. Or unless the Vanes' knowledge predated everything, passed down from Osric's journal like a disease through blood.

Either way, the arithmetic had changed. If the Vanes already knew the wreck's location, her photographs weren't leverage—they were redundancy. And redundant assets, Ansel knew from a year of reading between the lines of Vane correspondence, had a way of becoming liabilities.

She fed the letter to her camp stove and watched Dree Vane's fine blue handwriting curl and blacken and lift away on the updraft. Then she took out the compass—the one decent thing she owned, brass-cased, its needle settling north with a small faithful shiver—and turned it over in her hands.

Fourteen days. Except the Vanes had granted twenty, which meant they expected her to need them, which meant they were already moving. Somewhere south, a fleet was being provisioned, or had been provisioned weeks ago, waiting on a wire that read *PROMISING LEAD* to weigh anchor.

She had gone out to buy herself time, and instead she'd learned the exact shape of the trap: not a cage but a current, carrying her gently toward a destination chosen long before she entered the water.

Ansel packed her camp in forty minutes and walked north along the cliff path, toward the village whose smoke she'd seen rising beyond the headland—Salt Hollow, according to the coastal chart, a place too small to warrant a harbor symbol. If the Vanes knew about hold B, then somebody near that wreck knew more than they'd told the sea. And if she was going to be used as an asset, she intended to learn, first, who else had been spending her.

The tide was turning. Behind her, far out past the shoals, the water had begun to move the way water does before weather—flat, oily, attentive.
