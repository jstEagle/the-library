## 4. The Keepers of Salt Hollow

The carvings didn't make sense until Ansel stopped looking at them as marks and started looking at them as a map.

She'd spent two days in the cave with her headlamp and a grease pencil, copying the spirals and notched lines from the walls above the waterline onto the back of a tide chart. The timbers of the Meridian Star had told her the wreck was old. The carvings told her someone had wanted it forgotten — no, not forgotten. *Kept.* The spirals repeated in a rhythm she finally recognized from her grandmother's kitchen, from a tin of old charts sold off in the bankruptcy: the wave-marks fishermen cut into their hulls to read current and shoal. Someone had written the cave's own dangers into its walls, in a shorthand older than any survey she held a license for.

And at the center of the largest spiral, cut deep and worn smooth by centuries of salt air, a single mark she'd seen before — burned into the sternpost of the wreck itself. A circle with a line through it, like an eye that had been closed on purpose.

Someone had come back. Over and over, across generations. Someone had tended this grave.

Ansel sat back on her heels in the dark, the tide sighing somewhere below her, and felt the arithmetic shift under her. Photographs of a wreck were worth money. Photographs of a *tended* wreck were worth something else entirely — something that came with questions she didn't want asked, by people she didn't want asking them.

She climbed out through the cave mouth as the water rose, and by the time she stood on the shingle with her boots wet to the laces, she'd made the decision the way she made all of them: fast, and telling herself it was the only option.

She needed to know who the keepers were. And then she needed to decide whether they were an obstacle or an asset.

* * *

Salt Hollow announced itself by smell before sight — woodsmoke and fish guts and tar, riding out on a wind that had crossed forty miles of open water without meeting anything worth slowing down for. The village sat in a fold of the coast where the cliffs stepped down into terraces, houses shingled gray as driftwood, boats hauled up on rollers above the tideline. From the headland it looked like something the sea had coughed up and the land had grudgingly agreed to keep.

Ansel came in on the coast road with her surveyor's satchel over her shoulder and the forged license in the inner pocket, next to her heartbeat. *Maren Voss, Coastal Cartographic Survey, Provisional Writ.* The ink was good. The lamination was good. The name was a stranger's, and she wore it the way she wore all her clothes — loosely, ready to shed.

The harbor had one working jetty and a ferry ramp, and at the ramp a man was arguing with an outboard motor in the patient, profane way of someone who has been arguing with it for years and expects to be arguing with it for years more. He was maybe forty, built like the jetty itself — salt-cured, functional, not going anywhere — with a face that had been weathered into permanent mild skepticism. When he saw her he stopped arguing with the motor and started, silently, assessing her instead.

"Surveyor," she said, before he could ask. She held up the satchel. "Coastal charting. Provincial writ covers the headland to the south. I'm told there's a ferry."

"There's a ferry." He wiped his hands on a rag that was more tar than cloth. "Ferry's for villagers. Where you walking to?"

"The headland."

"That's six miles of nothing."

"Nothing's what I chart."

Something moved behind his eyes — not suspicion exactly, more like the flicker of a man recognizing a species he'd seen before. He looked at her boots, her camera, the way she stood with her weight already pointed at the road. "Ilo Kestrel," he said. "I run the ferry, when the motor consents. If you're back before the evening tide I'll take you up the coast and save you the walk."

"Ansel—" She caught it a half-second too late, the name already out, and covered it with the rest. "—Ansel Voss. Maren's what's on the license. Everyone calls me Ansel."

It was a good lie. It was even a true lie, which was the most dangerous kind, because she could hold it under pressure without sweating. Ilo nodded, unsurprised by the tangle, and went back to his motor, and that was that.

She should have walked. She knew that even then. Six miles of nothing was safer than an hour in a boat with a man who watched hands the way she watched exits.

She took the ferry.

* * *

The headland gave her what she needed and nothing she wanted. From the cliff top she could see the shoal field south of the cave — a drowned ridge of rock that explained the Meridian's end and the carvings' warnings both — and she shot a full photographic sequence, the long lens eating distance, the cave mouth a black seam in the cliff face that only a low tide and a knowing eye could find. From up here, with the surveyor's eye, she could see what the keepers had done: the village sat precisely where it sat because from the harbor jetty, on a clear day, you could see the whole approach to the cave. Salt Hollow wasn't a fishing village that happened to be near a secret.

Salt Hollow was a sentry post that had learned to fish.

She was still turning that over when the evening light went copper and she headed back down, and Ilo was waiting at the ramp as promised, and the motor, grudgingly, consented.

They were ten minutes out from the jetty, the cliffs sliding past dark on their right, when he said it.

"That's a Kestrel compass."

She didn't understand at first. He was nodding at her satchel, where the compass hung from its strap — brass, gimbal-mounted, the case dented on one side where it had once taken a fall she'd never learned the history of. She'd bought it eleven years ago from a junk dealer in a port town she didn't remember the name of, for less than the cost of a hot meal, and it had been the one possession she'd never sold, through every bad year, because it *found north* when nothing else in her life did.

"It's mine," she said.

"I didn't say it wasn't." Ilo kept his eyes on the channel. "I said it's a Kestrel. Gimbal mount, brass case, the dent on the port side — my grandfather made twelve of them, the year before the Meridian went down. He was the wreck's last keeper. He went out with her, or with what was left of her, depending on which elder you believe." He shrugged, a small motion, the boat steady under him. "Eleven were accounted for. The twelfth went missing the same night he did. My mother spent her whole life saying it'd wash up someday."

The engine ticked. The water ran dark and glassy. Ansel held the strap and felt the compass swinging against her hip like a small dense heart, and understood that she had walked into the one village on this coast where her most honest possession was a piece of evidence.

"Where'd you get it?" Ilo asked, and his voice was still mild, still ferryman's-pleasant, but underneath it was the same question the elders would ask, and the same question the Vanes would ask, and there was only one answer that kept her name clean in any of those mouths.

"Junk dealer," she said. "Port town. Eleven years back."

"Long way from home, that compass."

"Things travel."

He was quiet for a long moment. Then he brought the boat alongside the ramp with a competence that made the maneuver look like nothing, and said, without looking at her: "Elders meet Thursday, after the nets come in. They ask anyone new to the Hollow to sit with them. It's not law. It's just what's done." He handed her up onto the planks. "You've got an honest face, Ansel Voss. Or a well-made one. Thursday'll sort out which."

* * *

She told herself she'd leave Wednesday night.

She told herself that Tuesday, too, standing in the room she'd rented over the smokehouse, her photographs spread across the bed like a hand of losing cards, calculating the payout one more time. The Vanes' number was still good. The coordinates were still hers to sell. All she had to do was not go to the Thursday meeting, and Salt Hollow would be a place she'd passed through, and the Kestrel compass would be a coincidence, and the twelfth keeper's legacy would be six figures in a salvage syndicate's account, and her debt would drown quietly, and she'd never have to think about the man at the ferry ramp again.

Wednesday evening, Ilo brought her a plate of smoked fish at the smokehouse, unasked, and sat on the step beside her while she ate, and didn't say anything at all for twenty minutes, which was longer than anyone had ever sat with her without wanting something.

"Thursday," he said finally, standing. "They'll feed you first. It's harder to lie on a full stomach — my grandmother's rule." At the corner he paused. "The compass. If it's the twelfth, it should come home eventually. No rush. But eventually."

Ansel sat in the dark after he left, the fish gone cold on the plate, and did the thing she never did, which was nothing. No calculating. No exit routes. Just the sound of the village around her — a dog, a mended net, someone laughing two houses down — settling over her like weather she hadn't dressed for.

Belonging, she thought, was a trap with better upholstery than the Vanes ever built.

She went to the meeting anyway.
