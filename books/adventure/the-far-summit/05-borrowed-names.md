## 5. Borrowed Names

Ilo Kestrel had spent thirty years reading water, and he read people the same way: by what they carried beneath the surface.

Ansel Marrow — if that was even her name — sat across from him in the stern of the *Petrel*, her hands folded around a mug of tea she hadn't touched, watching the Hollow's harbor lights come up through the drizzle like a string of drowned stars. She had the stillness of someone who'd learned that motion attracts attention. He'd seen it before, in smugglers and revenue men and one customs inspector who'd nearly broken the village a generation back. Stillness wasn't innocence. Stillness was practice.

And then there was the compass.

It lay on the thwart between them, brass worn to gold at the pivot, the lid engraved with a gull over three waves. His grandfather's compass. The one old Tam Kestrel had carried aboard the *Meridian Star* the night she sailed from Salt Hollow and never came home — or so the village story went. Ilo had held it once, as a boy, when his grandmother let him touch it through cloth because it was too holy to handle bare. It had been buried with her. Or it should have been.

"You said you found this in the cave," he said.

"Wedged in the timbers near the stern post." Her voice was level. Too level. "The hinge was fused with salt. I soaked it in vinegar for two days to get it open."

"The needle still swings?"

"True as anything." A pause. "You recognize it."

It wasn't a question, but he answered anyway. "It belonged to my grandfather." He watched her face for the flicker — the small recalculation of a liar adjusting their story — and there it was, quick as a fish turning, gone before it finished. "He was the Meridian's last keeper. The village has songs about him. We have his chair. We do not have his compass, because the compass went down with the ship."

"Then how—"

"That," Ilo said, "is what I've been asking myself since you walked into the Salt House wearing a surveyor's license that smells of fresh ink and no salt at all."

The rain thickened. Out past the breakwater, the sea made its long gray argument against the cliffs, against the cave hidden in their roots, against everything Salt Hollow had spent two hundred years pretending not to know.

"I'm not going to ask you who you are," he said. "Not yet. I'm going to show you something instead, and then you're going to decide whether to tell me the truth or row back to the mainland and take your chances with whoever sent you."

Her eyes came up sharply. "Whoever sent me? Ilo, I—"

"The tide gave you that cave," he said. "Tides don't give. They lend. And nothing lends itself to a stranger unless something under the water wants it found — or unless someone above the water paid to have it looked for." He took up the oars. "Hold on. The crossing is short, but the last part is steep."

* * *

The archive was not a building. That was the first thing outsiders never understood about Salt Hollow, and the reason the Hollow had kept its secret through wars, censuses, and two centuries of mapmakers. The archive was the church of the Drowned Saint, and the archive was also the cold cellar behind Maren Tull's smokehouse, and the archive was most of all the Keeper's Loft above the Salt House itself — a low room smelling of cedar and lamp oil, its rafters hung with bundles of oilcloth wrapped documents, each bundle tagged with knotted cord in a code only three living people could read.

Ilo was one of them. He'd sworn the keeping at nineteen, kneeling on wet stone while old Mother Brine pressed her thumb, salted, to his forehead, and told him: *What we hold is heavier than gold and worth less to anyone honest. Carry it lightly and carry it forever.*

He lit the hanging lamp now and watched Ansel Marrow take in the room. He knew what she saw: a fisherman's loft, cramped and poor, nothing worth stealing. He also knew what she might see, if she was the kind he feared she was: paper. Leverage. A fortune in proof.

"This is your surveyor's report?" she said dryly.

"This is why there's never been a surveyor's report." He climbed the ladder to the third rafter, fingers finding the knot by feel — the gull hitch, three waves below it — and brought down the oldest bundle, the one whose oilcloth had been replaced four times and whose contents had been touched by fewer hands than the village had boats. "Sit down, Ansel Marrow. What I'm going to tell you, I've told no one born outside the seawall. Not because it's secret from the world. Because it's a debt, and debts are dangerous things to name out loud."

She sat. She didn't touch the tea. Of course she didn't; she thought it might be drugged, or she thought accepting hospitality created obligation. Both instincts were correct, and both told him more about her than any license.

"In 1741," Ilo began, "the Meridian Star wasn't a wreck. She was a packet ship running charter out of this harbor, owned jointly by nine families of Salt Hollow — fishing families, kelp-burners, salters. Poor families who'd spent eleven years buying shares of her hull a plank at a time. My grandfather was her keeper, which meant he kept her books as well as her lamps. Every share, every payment, every signature. He kept duplicates of everything." Ilo set the bundle on the table between them. "Because the man who financed the ninth share — the majority share, the controlling interest — was a salvage broker named Edouard Vane."

He heard her breath change. Small. Controlled. But changed.

"Vane put up the coin for the ship's final refit," Ilo went on, "and the contract gave him cargo rights — which was normal, and the families agreed, because a broker's seal got their herring into ports their own names couldn't. What the contract did *not* say — what Vane wrote privately into the ship's true manifest, the one my grandfather copied — was that the Meridian Star's return cargo would be colonial gold, consigned to Vane alone, bought with money Vane had borrowed against collateral he did not own." Ilo untied the gull hitch. "The collateral was the harbor itself. The tidelands. The salting flats. Nine families' holdings, pledged without their knowledge or consent, under forged marks that my grandfather recognized because he'd taught half those men to make their signatures."

Ansel's hands had gone still on the table. Completely still. "What happened to the ship?"

"She came home heavy," Ilo said. "That's in the log — my grandfather's log, the copy, the original burned with everything else. She came home heavy in October of 1741, riding low enough that the pilot refused her the channel. And the night before she was due to unload, Edouard Vane did not come to the harbor. He sent his brothers. And the brothers did not come to unload. They came with fire."

The lamp flame leaned in the draft from the loft hatch. Ansel hadn't moved.

"There was no storm that night," Ilo said. "I want you to understand that. No storm, no wreck, no act of any god. The Meridian Star was burned at her moorings with six of our own aboard standing watch — my grandfather among them, though the songs say he escaped, and the songs are wrong, they're wrong on purpose, because a dead keeper can't testify. The gold went down with her, and the fire destroyed the ledgers, and with the ledgers gone, Vane's claim on the tidelands stood unchallenged. He sold them within the year. That sale built the first Vane warehouse. The warehouse built the fleet. The fleet built the syndicate that today owns half this coast and every debt on it." He pushed the bundle toward her. "Everything else — the true manifest, the forged pledges, my grandfather's copies in his own hand — has been in this loft for two hundred and eighty-four years. The wreck in your cave isn't treasure, Ansel Marrow. It's the witness. The hull, the burn marks, the manifest chest sealed in pitch in the hold. Proof that the Vanes' fortune is stolen goods, and always has been."

Silence, except the rain on the shingles and the sea working the rocks below.

Ansel reached out and drew the top document from the bundle — careful, reverent, the way his grandmother had handled the compass — and read. He watched her lips move slightly over his grandfather's copperplate. He watched something move behind her eyes that was not greed. He'd expected greed. He'd braced for greed. What moved in her face instead looked almost like grief, and that undid him worse.

"If this is real," she said quietly, "and the wreck confirms it — the Vanes wouldn't just lose a salvage claim. They'd lose everything. Legitimacy. The courts would strip the charters. Every debt they hold—" She stopped.

"Every debt they hold becomes an accounting error," Ilo finished. "Yes."

"And they know the ship exists." Her voice had gone flat again, the practiced flatness. "They must. Why else would a surveyor's license get me onto this coast so easily? Why else—" She cut herself off, jaw tightening, as if she'd said one word more than she'd budgeted.

*Why else did they send you,* Ilo thought. *Or let you come.*

He should have stopped there. He should have rewrapped the bundle, tied the gull hitch, thanked her politely for the tea she hadn't drunk, and rowed her back to the mainland where she could do whatever harm she'd been paid to do, because that was what a prudent keeper did with a stranger who lied about her name and flinched at the word *debt*.

Instead he heard himself say: "The elders meet tomorrow after the tide. I can get you into that room. But you'll stand in front of Mother Brine's granddaughter and swear on the salt, and the salt doesn't forgive a false oath, Ansel Marrow. It remembers. So before tomorrow — tonight, before you sleep — you're going to tell me your real name, and what the Vanes have on you, and why a woman who photographs wrecks for money looks like she's about to cry over a dead fisherman's ledger."

She looked at him then, fully, for the first time since the harbor — and he saw the exact moment the practiced stillness cracked, the weariness underneath it old and deep as the cave itself.

"Ilo," she said, "if I tell you the truth, you'll row me to the mainland yourself."

"Probably," he admitted. "But you'll sleep better, and so will I."

A sound escaped her that was half laugh, half surrender. Outside, the rain was easing, and far out beyond the breakwater, where the channel markers blinked their patient red, Ilo thought he saw — though he told himself he imagined it — a light that did not belong to any buoy. A white light, steady, high-masted, holding position in the dark like something waiting for morning.

The fleet was coming. He felt it in his chest the way he felt weather, and he had just handed its captain's agent the keys to the loft.

*God forgive me,* he thought, and began banking the lamp.
