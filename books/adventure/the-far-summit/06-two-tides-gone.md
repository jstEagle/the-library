## 6. Two Tides Gone

The boy came running up from the shingle at first light, barefoot, shouting a word that didn't belong to Salt Hollow: *fleet*.

Ansel was on the sea wall with a mug of Ilo's grandmother's tea going cold in her hands when she heard him. She set the mug down. Her body understood before her mind did—the way it always had, in twelve years of chasing weather. The stillness first. Then the arithmetic.

She walked to the point where the villagers were gathering, and she looked north along the coast, and there it was.

Three ships. No—four, the last one low and slow, riding heavy. Salvage cranes folded flat against their decks like sleeping insects. They were hull-down still, just a smudge of smoke and geometry against the pale morning water, but Ansel had spent half her life reading vessels the way other people read faces, and she knew what she was looking at. Not fishermen. Not navy. The Vanes' fleet moved with a particular economy, every line of it purposeful, every ship carrying too much machinery to be anything but hungry.

The flagship would be Dree Vane's *Ledger*. Ansel knew its silhouette from harbor gossip and debt notices both.

"How far?" asked Maren Oduya, the eldest of the Hollow's elders, her voice level in a way that made the younger villagers go quiet.

"Two days," Ansel said. "Maybe less if they've got a favorable current." She heard her own voice come out steady, professional, the voice of a surveyor who had never lied to anyone in her life, and something in her chest turned over like a stone in surf.

Maren nodded slowly. "Then we have two days."

*You have two days,* Ansel thought, *because someone told them where to look.* And she stood there among these people who had fed her and named her and taught her which rocks held lobster pots and which held nothing, and she knew—with a certainty that arrived whole, no need for evidence—that the someone was her.

* * *

She went back to her room above the net shed and took out the camera.

It sat in her pack where it always sat, wrapped in oilcloth, and she unwrapped it with hands that wanted very much not to know what they were doing. The photographs. Forty-one exposures of the cave, the timbers, the Meridian Star's mark burned into oak that had waited under the sea for two hundred years. She'd taken them because taking them was what she did—document, catalog, convert wonder into collateral. It hadn't occurred to her, not once, not even at three in the morning counting tides, that the camera itself might be the leak.

But now she turned one of the plates toward the window light and saw it: the surveyor's stamp she'd etched into each frame, her forged license number, neat as a signature. Every plate she'd developed had passed through the postal office at Harrow Point, through the courier run that served the whole coast, through hands she'd never counted. The Vanes didn't need to follow her. They only needed to read her mail. Her photographs hadn't been evidence for a negotiation. They'd been a delivery.

Dree Vane had never needed Ansel to find the wreck. She'd needed Ansel to find it *for* her—cheaply, deniably, with a forged license that could be disowned the moment it became inconvenient. The debt, the deadline, the six tides. None of it had ever been about money. It had been a leash, and Ansel had jogged along it all the way to the cave and then photographed the door.

She wrapped the camera back up and did not throw it in the sea, though she stood at the window holding it over the sill for a long moment, the tide sighing below, before some instinct older than panic made her keep it. Evidence cut both ways. She'd learned that in another life, under another name, running from another thing she'd done.

Below, the village was already moving. She could hear it through the floorboards—hammers, voices, the particular organized urgency of people who had done this before.

* * *

They had done this before. That was the thing that undid her, sitting in Maren's kitchen that evening while the elders laid out their plans on a table scarred by two centuries of similar tables.

"The last time the syndicate came sniffing," said old Tam Broadbelt, tapping a chart gone soft as cloth with age, "was my grandfather's day. They sent a single ship then, a survey brig flying merchant colors. We doused our fires for a week and let them believe the coast was empty. They sounded the bay, found nothing worth the trouble, and left."

"And this time?"

"This time they bring cranes." Tam's mouth was bitter. "Cranes mean they know. Cranes mean someone drew them a map."

Ansel kept her eyes on the chart. The fire crackled. Somewhere outside, Ilo was hauling his ferry higher above the tideline, out of reach of whatever surge the monsoon would push ahead of it—two storms now, the human one arriving just ahead of the natural one, or maybe behind; nobody could say anymore.

"There are ways," Maren said quietly, watching Ansel across the firelight. "Ways to make a fleet think a coast is worthless. False soundings. Moved beacons. A village that has nothing, shows nothing, says nothing." Her eyes stayed on Ansel a beat too long. "We will need everyone. Even guests."

"I'm not a guest," Ansel heard herself say. "I'll work."

And she did. Through the next day and into the night she worked beside people whose names she was learning to love, and every hammer stroke landed on her own guilt like a coin on a counter. She helped false up the beacon channel markers, shifting them three fathoms off true so any unfamiliar keel would read deep water where there was shoal. She helped carry the archive chests—those paper-wrapped generations—from the meeting house down to the root cellars, and when her arms ached under the weight of documents proving that the Vane fortune had been carved out of these very families, she found a grim satisfaction in it. Let the thieves' descendants come. Let them dig for gold and find, instead, their own indictment waiting in the dark.

Ilo worked beside her most of the day. He didn't talk much. When he did, it was small things—where to set a foot on wet rock, how the Hollow marked its channels, why his grandfather had trusted the sea more than any chart. Once, passing her a coil of rope, he said, "You're good at this. Better than a surveyor should be."

"A surveyor hauls rope same as anyone," she said.

"Mm," he said, and let it go, and the letting-go was worse than an accusation.

That was the shape of it, she realized, hauling the last chest into the cellar dark: confession was a door standing open in front of her, and through it lay the only honesty she'd known in twelve years—and beyond the door, Ilo's face changing. Maren's kitchen closing to her. The word *spy* traveling faster than any fleet. She could confess and lose them, or stay silent and betray them by inches, and either way the ships were coming, and either way her photographs had already done the worst of the work.

Guilt, she discovered, doesn't stay fluid. It calcifies. By the second dawn it had hardened into something she could stand on, and she stood on it, and made it resolve instead of shame: *they will not take this place while I'm on its rocks.*

* * *

On the second morning, the fleet rounded Far Summit Point and entered the outer roads, and the whole village turned out on the headland to watch them come.

Four ships, anchors down in the gray water, patient as creditors. From the flagship's mast flew no pirate's flag and no merchant's colors but something worse: a plain white ensign with a black chevron, the mark of the Vane salvage syndicate, legal as a mortgage. A longboat dropped from the *Ledger*'s side and began pulling for shore under oars, a white cloth lashed to its bowstaff like a parody of truce.

"They'll want to parley," Tam said. "Buy first. Take later."

"Then we send someone who can't be bought," Maren said.

Every eye on the headland, Ansel felt, slid toward her without quite arriving. She looked at the longboat growing larger across the water, and at the figure standing in its stern—even at this distance she knew the posture, unhurried, proprietary, a woman who treated weather as a personal instrument—and she understood that this meeting was not chance. Dree Vane had sent a boat to this shore because Dree Vane knew exactly who was standing on it.

Ansel pulled her coat tight around the name stitched hidden inside it and started down toward the landing rocks.

"I'll talk to them," she said.
