## 7. The Price of the Hold

Dree Vane had learned long ago that the sea did not negotiate. It simply waited, and whatever you owed it, it collected. She had built her life on the same principle.

She stood at the stern rail of the *Cormorant's Tithe*, flagship of the Vane salvage fleet, watching Salt Hollow's cliffs darken under a sky the color of wet slate. Six ships rode at anchor in the bay behind her, their hulls groaning against their chains like animals that smelled blood. The monsoon was still two days out—her barometers said three, but she trusted her own joints more than her instruments, and her left shoulder had been aching since dawn—and until it made landfall, there was nothing to do but wait for the woman she had spent two years and a considerable sum of money arranging to meet.

"Tea, ma'am?" asked Pell, her cabin steward, a narrow man with hands too soft for salvage work and a memory too good for comfort.

"Two cups," Dree said. "She'll want something warm. People always do, when they're frightened."

"And if she isn't frightened?"

Dree smiled without turning around. "Then she's a better liar than I paid for."

* * *

Ansel Thornbury came aboard at slack water, rowed out from the shingle beach by a boy who wouldn't meet her eyes. Dree watched from the quarterdeck as the boat bumped against the chains and the surveyor climbed up the pilot ladder with the easy, economical movements of someone who had spent years being small enough to slip through cracks. The forged license was in her breast pocket; Dree could have told her the exact weight of it. Ansel Thornbury, surveyor, third class, licensed out of Harrowgate—a town whose records office had burned four winters ago, which was why the forgery had cost so little and worked so well.

The real name was Marisol Vance. The crime was eleven thousand crowns, moved through a shipping concern in Corvane City that had belonged, briefly and catastrophically, to a man named Aldous Renk. Renk had survived. His business had not. And Marisol Vance had run until running brought her to the Far Summit coast, where Dree Vane's moneylenders had been waiting with open hands and a loan structured so precisely that default was not a possibility but a schedule.

"Miss Thornbury." Dree extended her hand on the quarterdeck, and felt the other woman's grip: dry, steady, controlled. Good. "Thank you for coming. I know how a fleet at anchor must look from shore."

"It looks like an invasion," Ansel said. Her voice was level. Her eyes went past Dree's shoulder—counting masts, counting men, doing arithmetic. "I'd hoped we might talk on land."

"The land belongs to people who don't want us here. The ship belongs to me." Dree gestured toward the stern cabin. "Come inside before the weather does."

* * *

The cabin was paneled in dark teak salvaged—Dree noted the irony and enjoyed it—from a wreck off the Corvane shelf, and it held one table, two chairs, and a chart of the Far Summit coast weighted at the corners with brass fittings pried from hulls that no longer needed them. Pell set down the tea and withdrew. Dree poured for both of them and sat, and let the silence do its work.

Ansel sat across from her and did not drink. She looked at the chart instead—at the bay, at the cliffs, at the small pencil mark Dree had placed there herself, weeks ago, roughly a mile north of where the cave actually was. A decoy. Bait for anyone who might glance at the chart uninvited. Ansel's eyes lingered on it half a second too long, and Dree filed that away with everything else.

"You've been busy," Dree said. "A promising lead, your letter said. Promising enough to ask for another extension."

"I'm close."

"You are." Dree sipped her tea. "You're closer than you know. Shall I tell you what I admire about you, Miss Thornbury? You photograph everything. Every timber, every carving, every tide line. You keep meticulous notes. You date them." She opened a drawer and laid a photograph on the table between them: the cave mouth, low tide, the timbers just visible within. Ansel's own work, printed on her own paper. "You sent copies to a developer in Harrowgate to raise capital. He sold them to my agent within the week. You really should vet the people you beg from."

Ansel's face did not move. That was the thing about her, Dree thought—the face never moved, but the eyes were always moving, always calculating exits. "Those photographs prove nothing," she said. "A cave. Some old wood."

"They prove a location. And this—" Dree produced a second item, a ledger page, and turned it so Ansel could read it upside down, the way debtors always could, "—proves a name. Marisol Vance. Eleven thousand crowns, misappropriated, Corvane City, six years ago. I bought your debt from the Renk estate's receivers. Do you know why?"

Ansel's hand had gone very still around her cup. "Because you collect debts."

"Because I collect *wrecks*," Dree said. "Debts are only how I find them. My grandfather's fortune began with a cargo he never earned—gold out of the colonies, written into his ledgers as salvage when it was theft, taken from a crew that drowned with their ship while his boats stood off and watched. The Meridian Star. You've seen her timbers, Miss Thornbury. You've photographed her mark. My family has hunted that hold for sixty years, and every map, every record, every witness has been buried by the same village that buries its dead above the tide line so the sea can't have them back." She leaned forward. "Salt Hollow knows exactly where the Meridian lies. They have always known. They call it cursed because a curse is cheaper than a confession. And you—you walked into their trust wearing a borrowed name, and they gave you their archive."

The silence stretched. Outside, the wind had begun to speak in the rigging, a low note that would rise over the next two days into a voice.

"So here is the price of the hold," Dree continued, gently now, the way one speaks to the drowning. "Give me the coordinates—exact, charted, verified—and every crown you owe me is ash. Not deferred. Forgiven. You walk away clean, with a new name if you want one, funded if you need it. Or refuse, and I take the cave anyway, and you remain what you are: a thief who owes me thirty-one thousand crowns and has nowhere left to run that I haven't already bought."

* * *

Ansel set down her cup. Dree watched her do it—watched the deliberate care of it, the way a gambler sets down cards she has already decided not to play.

"You knew my name before I ever signed your paper," Ansel said. It wasn't a question.

"I've known it since the day you fled Corvane. You think the loan was charity? You think a woman with no collateral, no history, no references gets thirty-one thousand crowns at fair interest?" Dree smiled. "You were bait, Miss Thornbury. We seeded the coast with stories about colonial gold and waited to see which fish rose. You didn't find the Meridian Star despite your debt. You found it *because* of it. Everything you've done these six months, you've done on my money, in my direction, at my pace."

Something flickered then—not fear. Dree had expected fear, had budgeted for it. This was something colder and more interesting. Grief, maybe, or its first cousin.

"You want me to say yes," Ansel said slowly. "But you don't trust me to say yes."

"I trust you to survive," Dree said. "Survival is the most reliable instinct there is. But you've made friends in Salt Hollow. The ferryman. The elders. I'm told the Kestrel boy shows you the archive." She let that sit. "So I'll be plain, because I respect plainness: if those coordinates arrive even one fathom wrong—if my divers find sand where you promised gold—I will not come for you. I'll go to Salt Hollow, and I will make the village pay your debt instead. Every boat. Every net. Every roof. Do you understand the shape of the offer now? It isn't forgiveness or ruin. It's *whose* ruin."

And there it was. Dree watched it land, watched the calculation behind Ansel's eyes collapse into something that wasn't calculation at all. She had seen that look before, on men signing away ships, on widows signing away houses. The look of a person realizing the game was never a game.

"I'll need three days," Ansel said quietly. "To verify the soundings. To chart it properly. You want exact coordinates. Exact takes time."

Dree considered her. The monsoon was two days out, perhaps less. Three days meant the storm would be on top of them, which meant the fleet would anchor in the bay and wait, which meant—

Which meant the woman across the table wanted the storm between them.

"Three days," Dree agreed. "Pell will see you to your boat." Then, as Ansel rose, as she moved toward the door with that careful, controlled economy: "Marisol."

Ansel stopped. Did not turn.

"Whatever you're planning," Dree said pleasantly, "plan it knowing that I have never once been surprised by the sea, and I do not intend to begin being surprised by you."

Ansel said nothing. The cabin door closed behind her, and Dree sat alone with two cups of tea going cold, and listened to the wind climb the rigging, note by note, like a debt accruing.

She reached out and moved the pencil mark on the chart a mile south, closer to the truth, and waited to see which way the fish would run.
