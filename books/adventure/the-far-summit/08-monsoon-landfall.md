## 8. Monsoon Landfall

The storm came a day and a half early, the way storms do when you've built your whole life around their schedules.

Ansel felt it before she saw it—a pressure change that made her back teeth ache, a smell like wet iron rolling down off the Far Summit ridge. The gulls went silent all at once, which was worse than any siren. Then the horizon to the southeast turned the color of a bruise going bad, a wall of cloud stacked so high it seemed less like weather than like geography, some new mountain range deciding where it wanted to live.

She was on Ilo's dock when it happened, helping him haul his skiff above the tide line. The rope burned her palms. Neither of them spoke. There wasn't time for speaking anymore; there was only the work of small boats and shuttered windows and oil lamps filled while they still had light to fill them by.

Then she looked up at the bay, and her hands stopped moving.

The fleet was already there.

Six hulls riding low in the gray water, anchored in a rough crescent off Salt Hollow's point—salvage ships, crane-armed and barnacled, flying no colors because they didn't need any. Everyone on this coast knew what the Vane brothers' fleet looked like the way everyone knew what a knife looked like. They'd come in on the storm's leading edge, using its wind the way Ansel herself used it, and now they sat at anchor with their lights doused, patient as ticks.

"They're waiting out the monsoon," Ilo said quietly. He'd seen them too. He'd probably seen them first—he always did. "They'll ride it here. Two days, maybe three."

"Maybe four," Ansel said. Her voice came out flat and strange. "If the system stalls over the ridge like the last one did."

Ilo turned and looked at her. In the failing light his face was hard to read, but not impossible. He'd been getting easier to read every day she'd known him, which was the problem, which had been the problem from the beginning.

"You knew the schedule," he said. Not accusing. Just knowing.

"I knew the forecast." She set down the rope. Her fingers didn't want to let go of it. "Everyone knew the forecast."

* * *

The village sealed itself the way a body seals a wound. Shutters up. Boats dragged into the churchyard on rollers, twenty families working without being asked. Old Maren Sallow stood in the archive doorway directing traffic with a lantern in one hand and her cane in the other, and when Ansel passed carrying sandbags, Maren caught her sleeve.

"The cave," the old woman said. "You're the only one who's been inside since my grandmother's time. If the surge comes up the channel, the entrance will take water. Is there anything in there that can't be replaced?"

Everything, Ansel thought. Everything in there can't be replaced. That's rather the entire point of me being here.

"Stack the bags two deep at the channel mouth," she said instead. "And if the surge tops them, don't go in after anything. The ceiling's older than the cliff face. It'll come down soft, but it will come down."

Maren studied her with eyes gone pale as sea glass. "You sound like you care what happens to it."

"I—" Ansel began, and found nothing behind the word.

Maren patted her arm once, almost gently, and moved on to the next pair of hands.

That was the worst part. Not the fleet in the bay, not the debt, not Dree Vane's voice from six tides ago still coiled in her ear like wire: *We never expected repayment in money, Miss Thornbury.* The worst part was that these people kept handing her things to carry.

* * *

The storm hit at dusk, and it hit like a door slamming.

Rain came sideways in sheets thick enough to drown in. Wind screamed through the hollow with a sound Ansel knew intimately—the same note her chase rig's torn awning used to sing in Kansas cells, the same note that had once meant data and dollars and now meant only cold. She stood in Ilo's doorway watching lightning walk the ridgeline, counting seconds between flash and thunder, doing the arithmetic she couldn't stop doing even now.

Five seconds. The cell's core was close. Closer than the forecast said it would be tonight.

Which meant the surge would peak higher too. Which meant—

"Ansel."

Ilo stood behind her holding an oil lamp, and in its light his expression had finally finished becoming something she could read, and what it read was: *enough.*

"You've been counting something since the fleet dropped anchor," he said. "You count everything—tide tables, rain rates, how many sandbags, how many boats. But since those ships came in, you've been counting something else. So either you tell me what it is, or I go ask Maren to bar the doors against you too."

The lamp flame bent in the draft. Outside, something loose tore free of a roof and cartwheeled away into the dark.

"Ilo," Ansel said. "Sit down."

He didn't sit. She hadn't expected him to.

So she told him standing up, in the doorway, shouting over the wind because there was no other volume left. She told him about the debts—the real number, the one she'd never said aloud to anyone, large enough that she'd stopped seeing it as money years ago and started seeing it as weather. She told him about the Vanes, about the forged surveyor's license folded in oilcloth in her kit, about the photographs, forty-one frames of the Meridian Star's timbers already developed and hidden in the false bottom of her camera case, each one a coordinate waiting to be read by anyone who knew how to look.

"I sold you out," she said. "Not on purpose. But my pictures were traced. That's why the fleet is in your bay. That's me. I'm the reason."

The wind took her last words and shredded them, but she could see by his face that none of them needed repeating. Ilo Kestrel stood very still, the way men stand when the ground has shifted under them and they haven't decided yet whether to fall or plant their feet.

"My name isn't Marlow Vance," she added, uselessly, because apparently she intended to burn the whole ledger. "It's Ansel Thornbury. There was a thing I did, three years ago—I'm not going to tell you what, not tonight, maybe not ever—but I ran to this coast to disappear, and disappearing is the one thing I've never been good at."

Lightning whitened the room. For one full second she could see every line of his face, and then it was night again, and the thunder arrived like a verdict.

"The compass," Ilo said at last.

"What?"

"The compass you carry. The one you told me belonged to your father." His voice was very quiet now, quieter than the storm, which somehow made it louder. "My grandfather's compass went down with the Meridian's last keeper. You knew that story before I ever showed you the archive. You let me hand you our history, piece by piece, and you carried it straight to the people who built their fortune stealing it."

"Yes."

"And now you're telling me this *now*. With their ships in our bay and the sky falling. Why?" He stepped closer, and she made herself hold still. "What changed?"

"Nothing changed." She laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "That's the whole point, Ilo. Nothing changed except that I ran out of ways to be both people. I thought I could keep the lie in one pocket and you in the other. I was wrong. I've been wrong since the day I rowed into that cave with a camera."

"So what happens now? You give them the coordinates, they forgive your debt, and you sail away rich."

"No." She said it fast, before courage could fail her. "No. Because I have one more thing to confess, and it's the only thing I own that's worth anything."

* * *

She laid it out on his table under the lamp, drawing on the wood with a charred stick because there was no paper that would survive this damp and no time to find any anyway.

"Here's the point. Here's the hollow. Here's the shoals—your grandfather called them the Teeth, right? Six feet down at low water, thirty feet at high. And here's the thing nobody on those ships knows better than I do." She tapped the stick against the table, twice, hard. "Surge timing."

Ilo frowned at the map. "Explain."

"The fleet's anchored in the lee of your point because Dree Vane is careful. She won't move during the storm—too much risk to her hulls, and she doesn't need to risk anything, because she thinks the wreck isn't going anywhere. She'll wait for the surge to crest, then fall, then send her divers in on the neap window." Ansel looked up at him. "But the surge doesn't just raise the water. It *moves* it. Peak surge through that channel runs eight knots, maybe ten, and it rips straight across the Teeth. Any ship that tries to weigh anchor in that window gets picked up and thrown onto the shoals like a toy. I've watched it happen to a chase truck. I know exactly how long the window lasts, and I know exactly when it opens."

"You want them to try to leave."

"I want them to *have* to try." She met his eyes. "Dree Vane doesn't wait for tides. She waits for leverage. So we give her leverage. We light a beacon on the north cliff—my old rig has a xenon array that reads, from open water, exactly like the survey markers the coastal authority uses. From her bridge, in this rain, it'll look like the cave mouth has been exposed and marked for salvage. First come, first claimed. She won't be able to help herself. She'll cut her anchors and run for the light, and she'll do it at the exact hour the current across the Teeth is strongest."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere outside, the wind found a new register, deeper, like the whole coast clearing its throat.

"And the villagers?" Ilo said. "While your light is pulling those ships onto the rocks?"

"Your boats stay hauled. Your people stay indoors. Nobody goes near the water." She hesitated, then made herself say the rest. "Except anyone who chooses to help me cut lines. But that's a choice. I'm not asking anyone to make it. I'm asking you to decide whether to let me try."

Ilo was silent for a long moment. Rain hammered the roof like a crowd demanding entry. When he finally spoke, he didn't look at her; he looked at the charcoal map, at the little X she'd drawn over the hollow, over the grave his family had guarded for six generations.

"You lied to me for weeks," he said. "You photographed our dead and priced them. And now you're offering to drown yourself along with them, on a plan that might fail, to protect people who—" He stopped. Started again. "Why should I believe you?"

"Because I could still sell you out." She spread her empty hands. "Right now. Tonight. Signal the flagship from the cliff, trade the coordinates, walk away clean. Dree would forgive every coin I owe. I'd never have to look at a tide chart again as long as I lived. And I'm standing here instead, telling you how to kill that option forever." She swallowed. "I don't know what that makes me. Maybe nothing better than I was. But I'm telling you the truth at the worst possible time I could tell it, because after tonight there's no version of this where I get to keep lying."

Ilo closed his eyes. She watched him breathe, three slow breaths, the way he breathed before hauling a net against a bad current—and she understood, suddenly and completely, that he was going to say yes, and that he was going to hate it, and that both of those things were true forever no matter what happened next.

"If the beacon fails," he said, opening his eyes, "if the current shifts, if one of those ships comes apart on the Teeth with people aboard—we own that. All of us. This village will live with whatever happens tonight longer than you will."

"I know."

"And you'll tell Maren yourself. Tonight. Before we do anything. She deserves to hear it from your mouth, not mine."

"Yes."

Ilo nodded once. Then he reached beneath the floorboards and pulled out a coil of rope, a hatchet, and a storm lantern, and began laying them on the table beside her charcoal map with the unhurried precision of a man who had decided.

Outside, the pressure dropped again, sudden as a held breath. Ansel felt her ears pop, felt the old animal certainty rise in her spine that she had spent half her life learning to trust: the worst of it was coming, and it would arrive sooner than anyone predicted.

The surge would crest in four hours.

They had a light to build.
