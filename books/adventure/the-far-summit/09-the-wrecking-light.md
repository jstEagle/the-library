## 9. The Wrecking Light

**Ansel**

The storm did not arrive. It *arrived at her* — a wall of gray-green water and wind that had been building out past the Far Summit shelf for two days, and now it came down on the coast like a judgment nobody had bothered to read aloud.

Ansel crouched on the cliff above Salt Hollow with her rig strapped to the rocks at four points and her hands shaking in a way that had nothing to do with cold. The storm-chasing rig had cost her everything once — the mast, the lens array, the beacon housing she'd rebuilt from salvage three winters ago. She had spent years pointing it at storms to sell their faces. Tonight she pointed it at the sea to lie to one.

Below, the Vane fleet rode at anchor in the bay: six hulls, riding lights burning yellow through the rain, the flagship *Charity's Due* squat and black at the center like a spider that had decided the web was the world. They had anchored there to wait out the monsoon, believing the cave — and the Meridian Star, and the gold, and the debt that had been a hook all along — would still be waiting when the weather cleared.

Dree Vane did not believe in weather. Ansel had seen that in the woman's eyes across the table of her own flagship. Dree believed in ledgers. And ledgers, Ansel was counting on, could be drowned.

She checked the surge gauge Ilo had rigged from a fisherman's depth line and a church bell. The water in the bay had already climbed two fathoms past the safe mark. The shoals off the eastern point — the Teeth, the villagers called them, old coral spines that had gutted a hundred ships and been blamed for the Meridian's own wreck — lay just beneath the surface, invisible in the churn.

The beacon was set to sweep on a slow rotation, timed to the surge. To a ship's officer looking out from the flagship's quarterdeck, it would read as the harbor light of Salt Hollow — a light that, in any sane sea, marked deep water and safe passage to the anchorage.

In any sane sea.

Ansel pressed her palm flat against the beacon housing, feeling the hum of the generator through her glove.

"I'm sorry," she said, though there was no one in the rig to hear it. Or maybe there was. Maybe the compass in her coat, the one Ilo's grandfather had carried and lost and that had come back to her like a debt of its own, was listening. "Not for this. For everything before it."

The first sweep of light went out across the water, thin and gold and steady, and Ansel turned her face into the rain and began to count.

* * *

**Ilo**

The sea was trying to take the boats back.

Ilo Kestrel stood in the shallows of the village slip with a knife in his teeth and salt water to his thighs, and the storm tore at him like it had opinions. The fleet's anchor lines did not reach the village — but the fleet's *longboats* did. Three of them had been sent ashore before the worst of it, crewed by salvage men with crowbars and lanterns, hunting the cave mouth at low water. The villagers had let them land. Had let them walk up the beach into the teeth of the surge. Had waited, as the elders had taught them to wait for three generations, until the sea itself was ready.

Now the surge was ready.

"Cut!" Ilo roared, and the word came back to him off the water in six voices. The longboats had been tied to a mooring buoy the salvage men had dragged up the sand — arrogant, certain, treating Salt Hollow's beach like a harbor they'd bought. Kessa Vane's crew, the youngest of them, was already climbing into the nearest boat when Ilo waded in and sawed through the painter in one stroke.

The sea did the rest. It took the longboat sideways, spun it, and drove it onto the rocks below the slip with a sound like a door slamming on a house.

The salvage men scattered up the beach, shouting, and the villagers let them go. There was no honor in drowning men. There was only the work.

Ilo turned toward the bay, and his stomach dropped.

The *Charity's Due* had weighed anchor. He could see it even through the rain-hammer — the riding light swinging, the hull swinging with it, coming about slow and heavy toward the eastern point. Toward the light on the cliff. Toward the gold.

"She's moving," he shouted to Old Maren, who stood hip-deep beside him with a hatchet and sixty years of storms in her face. "She's going for the cave."

"Then the light's working," Maren said, and grinned with all the warmth of a shark. "Cut the next one."

* * *

**Ansel**

Through the rig's lens, Ansel watched the flagship come about, and every prayer she had ever pretended not to know crowded into her throat.

The *Charity's Due* moved like something that had never once been refused. Her bow came around through the surge in a long white scar of foam, and her running lights burned steady, and Ansel could imagine Dree Vane on her quarterdeck with rain streaming off her and her eyes fixed on the cliff light — reading it, the way she read everything, as a promise that the world would finally pay her what she was owed.

*Come on,* Ansel thought. *Come on. Trust the light. You've trusted nothing else in your life. Trust the light.*

The flagship crossed the bay's center. The surge lifted her — a mountain of water, the peak of the monsoon's first landfall, exactly where Ilo's tide-math had said it would be — and set her down again forty yards closer to the Teeth.

Ansel's hands were steady now. That was the terrible thing. All the shaking had gone somewhere else, into the storm, and what was left was the cold clean focus she'd only ever felt in the eye of a chase, when the world narrowed to wind-speed and bearing and the next ten seconds.

The flagship's bow rose on the second great surge, and Ansel saw it happen — saw the moment the hull came down and did not come *up*. A shudder went through the ship, visible even at this distance, a full-body convulsion like a struck animal. Then a grinding, and then the sound reached her across the water: a long tearing groan of timber on coral, the voice of a ship discovering the bottom.

The *Charity's Due* grounded on the Teeth with her bow pointed at the gold and her heart opened to the sea.

* * *

**Ilo**

From the cliff path, halfway up, Ilo watched the flagship die and felt something he had no name for — not triumph, nothing so clean. The *Charity's Due* heeled hard to starboard, her decks shedding crates and boats and men into the surge, and the villagers on the rocks below were already launching their own boats into it, because that was what Salt Hollow did. The sea wrecked them; they pulled the drowned out anyway. Even these ones. Even the ones who had come to rob them.

Even Dree Vane, if she'd let them.

She would not. Ilo knew it the way he knew weather. He could see the flagship's stern through the rain, and he could see the cluster of figures there, lanterns swinging, and none of them moving for the boats. They were moving *inward*. Down. Toward the hold.

"She's not abandoning," he said to Ansel, who had come down from the rig with the beacon still sweeping gold behind her, painting the rain in slow arcs. Her face was gray. "She's going for the cargo."

"I know." Ansel's voice was flat and strange. "The hold's forward. She's going to drown on the wrong end of her own ship."

"Ansel." He caught her arm. "The boats are going out. We can still take them off."

Ansel looked at him, and in the beacon-light her eyes were wet and furious and afraid, and she said, "Ilo, if we pull her off that ship, she will burn this village down by spring. She told me so to my face. She knows my name. She knows *why* I ran to this coast. She offered to erase it all for a set of coordinates, and the only reason she's on that ship instead of in my head is that the sea got there first."

"Then we let her drown."

"I don't know." It came out of her cracked down the middle. "I don't *know*, Ilo. That's the truth. I've spent my whole life knowing exactly what everything cost, and I don't know what this costs."

Below them, a village boat went out into the surge, Maren at the tiller, and turned toward the wreck.

* * *

**Ansel**

The cave flooded at midnight.

Ansel felt it before she saw it — a change in the wind's note off the cliff, a pressure drop, the sea exhaling. She went to the edge with the lantern and looked down into the churning dark where the cave mouth had been, and it was gone. Not hidden. Gone. The surge had collapsed the lip of the entrance, and tons of cliff had come down behind it, and the Meridian Star and her gold and her documents and her three generations of careful silence were sealed under the Far Summit coast like a letter in a stone envelope.

The curse, the elders called it. Ansel was starting to think it was just the sea, keeping its own accounts.

She was still standing there when Ilo's shout reached her, ragged with wind: the flagship's stern was going. The *Charity's Due* had taken water for an hour, her stern settling by degrees, and now the sea was simply collecting her. The village boats had pulled eleven men off her rocks and rigging. The hold was already under. And Dree Vane —

Dree Vane stood at the sinking stern with a lantern held high, and even at this distance, even drowned in rain, Ansel knew the posture. It was the posture of a woman reading a ledger that refused to balance. The last light Ansel ever saw of her was the lantern, swinging once, twice — and then the sea closed its hand, and the *Charity's Due* went down off the Teeth with her captain and her ambition and her founding fortune's final claim, and the bay was suddenly, shockingly dark.

The beacon swept on behind Ansel, gold and steady, lying to no one now.

She sank to her knees on the cliff grass, and Ilo knelt beside her, and the two of them shook in the rain like the last two posts of something that had somehow, impossibly, held.

* * *

**Ilo**

Dawn came the color of pewter, and the storm had spent itself to a long gray moan, and the bay was littered with the honest debris of the night: spars, crates of nothing valuable, eleven salvage men wrapped in blankets on the slip who would be fed before they were questioned, because that was Salt Hollow.

The flagship's masts showed above the water off the Teeth, tilted like a drowned church.

Ilo stood at the cliff edge with Ansel, and neither of them had slept, and neither of them had spoken in an hour, and it was the easiest silence he had ever kept with another person. That frightened him more than the storm had.

"There'll be others," Ansel said finally. "The syndicate. Brothers, cousins, creditors. They'll come asking where the fleet went."

"The fleet went to the sea," Ilo said. "That's the only answer this village has ever given, and it's the true one." He hesitated, then reached into his coat and brought out the compass — his grandfather's compass, the one lost with the Meridian's last keeper, the one that had come back to Salt Hollow in the pocket of a liar. He held it out to her. "The elders want you to carry it. Not as proof of anything. As an invitation."

Ansel looked at the compass, then at him, and her eyes were red-rimmed and steady.

"I can't keep the name I came here with," she said. "It's burned. Everything it touched is burned."

"Then don't," Ilo said. "The sea gives back what it wants to. It's been giving this village new names for three hundred years."

Below them, the tide was turning, and the first calm morning in six days was coming up the coast — and Ansel Thornbury, whoever that had ever been, reached out and took the compass in both hands.
