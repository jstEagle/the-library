## 10. A New Name Among Strangers

The morning after the storm came in the color of hammered tin, and Ansel Thornbury stood at the cliff's edge and watched the sea give back what it had taken.

The flagship was gone. In the gray light she could see only its bones now—the top of the mainmast breaking the swell off the shoals like a drowning man's hand, and the scattered debris the tide was sorting along the beach: a hatch cover, a coil of rope, one lacquered door from the great cabin with the Vane crest still bright on it, gold paint stubborn as a lie. Villagers walked the tideline in silence, collecting what the sea offered and burning what they didn't want in a small fire that smoked sideways in the wind.

Three days since the Wrecking Light. Three days since Dree Vane had stood on her tilting quarterdeck and refused the boat sent out for her, because the hold was still sealed and the gold was still down there and Dree had never in her life been able to tell the difference between a fortune and a grave. Ansel had watched the flagship go from the cliff, the false beacon's lamp still warm in her hands, and she had felt—she was honest enough with herself to admit it—not triumph. Something quieter. Something like the moment after a fever breaks, when you're too empty to feel anything but the fact of being alive.

The syndicate had not survived its flagship. She'd heard it in pieces over the following days: the two remaining salvage ships turning for the open sea before the storm had even finished, their captains reading the shoals and the arithmetic and choosing survival over loyalty. The Vane brothers' credit had been built on the flagship's guns and Dree's patience, and both were now under twelve fathoms of cold water. Somewhere in the port towns, men who owed the Vanes were waking up to discover they owed no one at all. Somewhere else, men the Vanes owed were doing the same math from the other side, and smiling.

And Ansel's debt—fourteen months of it, the number that had lived behind her eyes like a second sun—was drowned. There was no one left to collect it. No ledger, no syndicate, no Dree. The paper had probably washed up on some beach by now, ink running, the sum dissolving back into the sea that had never cared about it.

She should have felt free. She kept waiting for it, the way you wait for a tide that the almanac promised. It didn't come. What came instead was a stranger's face in a stranger's mirror, because Ansel Thornbury had been built entirely out of debt and flight, and now the debt was gone and there was nothing left to run from, and she didn't know what remained.

* * *

They found her on the fourth morning, or perhaps she found them—afterward she could never say which it was.

She'd gone down to Salt Hollow to help mend the net racks the surge had splintered, and when the work was done the elders were waiting for her outside the archive house, standing in a row the way they stood for funerals and landings. Ilo was among them, his arm still in a sling from the anchor lines, his face unreadable in the way of a man who had trusted someone once and was deciding whether to make a habit of it.

Maren Ostrey, the eldest, held out her hands. In them lay the compass.

Ansel's breath stopped. She knew it instantly, though she'd only seen it once, in the archive, under glass—brass gone the color of old honey, the lid engraved with a wave and a star, the needle housing cracked and mended with a strip of silver so old the repair had become part of the thing. The compass of the Meridian's last keeper. Ilo's grandfather's compass. The one that had been lost with the ship, or so the village had believed for a hundred years.

"It came ashore," Maren said. "Two days after the wrecking. In the wrack line, below the cave mouth, as if the sea had been holding it until the sea was finished with us." She turned it over in her palms. "The needle points true. We checked."

"I can't take that," Ansel said. Her voice came out rough. "That belongs to—"

"It belongs to the Hollow," Maren said, "and the Hollow is giving it to you. Not because we think you have keeper's blood. We had a hundred years to look for keeper's blood and we know exactly what it looks like, and it isn't you." A dry sound went through the row of elders that might have been laughter. "We give it because a compass doesn't care where you were born. It only asks where you stand. And three nights ago, in the worst weather this coast has seen in a generation, you stood on that cliff with a lamp in your hands and you pointed the fleet at the shoals instead of at us."

Ilo stepped forward. His sling was salt-stiff, and he held the compass out to her with his good hand, and his eyes were wet and furious about being wet.

"You could have sold us," he said. "You had the photographs. You had the coordinates. You had a debt that was eating you alive, and one word to Dree Vane would have bought it all back." His jaw worked. "You stood in my grandfather's archive and lied to my face, and then you stood on that cliff and told the truth with fire. I have decided those are both true things about you, and I have decided to keep the second one."

Ansel took the compass. It was heavier than it looked. It was heavier than anything she'd ever held.

"I don't deserve a gift for finally doing one right thing," she said.

"No," Maren agreed. "You don't. That's what makes it a gift and not a wage." She looked at Ansel a long moment, and her eyes were the gray of the sea before weather. "The Hollow doesn't forget the Meridian, and it doesn't forget you. There's a house on the north lane with a broken roof and a stove that draws well. It's been empty since old Sef went into the ground. It could be empty another hundred years, or it could have someone in it who knows how to read weather and isn't afraid of a cliff. That's not a debt we're offering. It's an invitation. You can walk up that coast road tomorrow and no one here will follow you, and no one here will forget you either. Or you can stay."

The wind came off the water and pushed at all of them, gentle as a hand on the back.

Ansel looked down at the compass in her hands. The needle swung, steadied, and pointed—not north, not home, because she had no north and no home, but somewhere, fixed and certain, as if the world had a direction after all.

* * *

She burned the surveyor's license that night in the stove of the house on the north lane.

It took three tries; the laminate didn't want to catch. She fed it in piece by piece and watched the forged seal curl and blacken, the false name of the false office, the photograph of a woman she had invented to survive. The firelight moved on the walls of a room that was hers now, or close enough to hers that no one would ask her to leave. There was a bed with a quilt on it that smelled of cedar, and a shelf where someone had left a stack of almanacs, tide tables going back forty years, as if the village had already guessed what she would need.

When the license was ash, she took out her coat—the old coat, salt-ruined, camera-scuffed, the one thing she'd owned through every port and every flight—and she sat by the stove with a sailmaker's needle and waxed thread, and she did what she had decided to do on the cliff with the lamp burning in her hands.

She unpicked the lining at the left breast. She wrote her old name on a scrap of oilcloth in her own hand—*Ansel Thornbury*, the name her mother gave her, the name the debt had owned, the name Dree Vane had said aloud like a knife going in—and she folded the scrap small and sewed it into the lining and closed the seam with tight, even stitches, the way Ilo had shown her for mending nets.

She wasn't erasing her. She was keeping her. Folded small, next to the heart, where she could be visited but not obeyed.

Then she took the compass and turned it over and studied the engraving on the back, the wave and the star, and beneath them, half worn away, a single word in the keeper's old script that the archive glass had never let her read. She'd brought a rubbing of it home in her pocket, charcoal on paper, and now she held it to the lamplight and read it clean for the first time.

*Hollow-kept. Sea-found. Given.*

Not a maker's mark. Not a name. An instruction, or a history, or a promise—the old keepers had believed a compass was a covenant, that you didn't own it, you *answered* it. She sat with that a long while, the needle trembling and settling, trembling and settling, like something breathing.

The village had asked her, through Maren, what name to call her now. Not what her name was—what name she *chose*. She'd never been asked that before. Every name she'd ever had had been assigned: by her mother, by her creditors, by the forgery, by the flight. She'd told them she'd answer to anything they liked until she decided, and Maren had said, dry as driftwood, *Take your time. The sea took a hundred years to give up one compass. We can wait a season for a name.*

But she had decided, in the small hours, watching the stove settle. She would take the name the elders had already begun using for her, half as a joke and half as a christening: *Wrecklight*. The woman who lit the false beacon. The woman who turned the fleet. It wasn't a name you were born to. It was a name you *did*—and that, she thought, was the only honest kind.

* * *

The first calm morning came a week later, and it was so still the sea looked like poured pewter, and the Far Summit stood up out of the haze to the north with its crown of cloud burned off, visible for the first time since she'd come to this coast. She walked out to the cliff at dawn with the compass in her pocket and the camera—the old camera, the one she'd meant to sell—hanging at her hip, and she found Ilo already there, mending the cliff-path fence, his sling off at last.

"You're staying, then," he said. Not a question.

"I'm staying," she said. "Through the morning. Through the season. We'll see about after."

He nodded as if she'd said something enormous, and maybe she had. Below them, the tide was going out—farther out than it had any right to go, the locals said, farther than it had gone since the wrecking, as if the sea were clearing its throat. The cave mouth beneath the cliffs was emerging into the light for the first time since it had flooded, dark and patient, and Ansel felt the old pull in her chest, the greed and the wonder in the same breath, and she let the greed go out with the tide and kept the wonder.

"Sea's low," Ilo said, squinting. "Lower than Maren's ever recorded. She wants a party down in the cave before it turns—says the water's moved things. Says the sea's *shown* us something." He looked at her sideways. "You're the one who reads weather. What do you think?"

Ansel took out the compass. The needle pointed—not north. It pointed down the cliff, toward the cave, toward the dark, and it held there, steady as a struck bell, in a way needles were not supposed to hold.

"I think," she said slowly, "that the sea isn't finished giving things back."

The tide was still falling. They had perhaps an hour before it turned, and whatever was down there in the dark had waited a hundred years, and then one storm, and now it was waiting for them.
