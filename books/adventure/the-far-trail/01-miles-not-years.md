## 1. Miles, Not Years

The storm was a beautiful liar, and I loved it for the same reason I loved everything else in my life that was about to hurt me.

It had gone up east of Amarillo around two in the afternoon—a tower of gray-green muscle punching through the cap like a fist through wet paper—and by three it had organized itself into something with rotation, structure, intent. By four it had a name among the people who named such things. I didn't name storms. Naming them made them pets, and pets die on you, and I'd had enough of that for one lifetime.

What I did instead was chase them across the flat sea of the high plains with a truck full of instruments, a tank full of diesel, and a phone full of numbers I refused to look at.

"Target's holding together," Teo said from the passenger seat, drone controller cradled in his lap like a sleeping cat. He was sixteen and had the reflexes of a ferret and the emotional range of a fence post, which are exactly the qualities you want in a teenager riding shotgun through hail country. "Mesocyclone's tightening. Base's lowering."

"I can see the base," I said.

"You always say you can see the base."

"That's because I can always see the base."

"One time you said you could see the base and it was a scud cloud and we drove forty minutes out of our way."

"We were forty minutes closer to dinner," I said. "That's called strategy."

Teo made a sound that wasn't quite a laugh and wasn't quite contempt, which is the native language of sixteen-year-olds everywhere, and went back to his screens. Behind us, in the crew cab, Bo was asleep sitting up, arms crossed over his chest like a man guarding something even in his dreams. Bo could sleep anywhere—standing at a gas pump, in a moving vehicle, once, memorably, during a lightning strike within four hundred yards. Ex-paramedics learn to sleep when sleep is offered. They've seen what happens to people who put it off.

The plains unspooled ahead of us, wheat stubble and red dirt roads and telephone poles marching toward the horizon like they had somewhere better to be. The sky did what the sky does out here, which is everything. It went green-black in the southwest and bruised yellow along the edges, and the light turned that particular shade that makes photographers pull over and makes old ranchers glance at their cattle and make no comment at all.

This was the part of my life that worked. I want to be clear about that. There was a version of Wendell Montrose that functioned perfectly, and he lived entirely inside this truck, inside these hours, inside the twenty-mile gap between him and a rotating wall cloud. Everything else—the bank statements, the voicemails, the spreadsheet I kept hidden in a folder labeled RECIPES on my laptop—was weather happening in some other county, some other life, to some other man.

The phone buzzed in the cupholder. Unknown number. Then, immediately after, a text from a number I knew by heart, which is its own kind of sad:

**MERIDIAN FUND: Mr. Montrose, we haven't connected regarding your account. Corliss Ames would appreciate fifteen minutes. Today if possible.**

I watched the screen light up and go dark, and I kept my hands at ten and two, because that's where hands go when they're not doing what they want to do.

"You gonna answer that?" Teo asked, without looking up.

"It's spam."

"Nobody's spam texts you about 'your account' six times a day."

"Persistent spammers."

"Wendell." He said my name the way kids do when they're about to say something true and want credit for the courage first. "Junie says you owe those people sixty-two thousand dollars."

I took the next turn a little harder than the turn deserved. "Junie says a lot of things."

"She reads your mail. You leave it on the dashboard."

"I leave *junk* on the dashboard."

"You leave everything on the dashboard. You live out of that truck like a hermit crab." He finally looked up, and behind the fence-post face there was something younger and more careful than he liked anyone to see. "Is it sixty-two?"

The storm saved me, or seemed to. The wall cloud dropped a ragged funnel fragment—nothing that touched down, just a show of force, a flex—and both of us turned our attention to it with the gratitude of men handed an exit. I took it. I always took it. That was, if I'm honest, the whole architecture of my character: a man built around exits.

Here's the thing about measuring your life in miles instead of years: miles don't compound. Years accrue interest. Years pile obligations on top of obligations until you're standing under a debt-shaped sky and every phone call is thunder. But a mile is just a mile. It asks nothing of tomorrow. When someone asks how far you've come, you tell them a number, and the number is clean, and nobody audits it.

I owed the Meridian Fund sixty-two thousand dollars. I owed them it at an interest rate I still couldn't say out loud without hearing my father's voice telling me about the family talent for arithmetic optimism. I'd borrowed it eighteen months ago to rebuild the truck after a hailstorm in Kansas shredded the roof and the radar dome and most of my faith in actuarial science, and the Fund had been so friendly about it. That's the thing people miss about the Meridian Fund. They're not sharks. Sharks are honest about being sharks. The Fund is a group of smiling people in open-collar shirts who talk about your "journey" and use words like "partnership," and they have never once raised their voice, and they will take the fillings out of your teeth with the same pleasant expression they'd use to ask about your mother's health.

Corliss Ames was the friendliest of them all. Corliss had a laugh like a door opening onto a party you weren't invited to.

The phone buzzed again. Then again. Then it started ringing—actual ringing, which meant they'd escalated to the number that lets itself through Do Not Disturb, which meant Corliss himself, which meant the party was coming to me whether I RSVP'd or not.

Bo woke up mid-ring, the way soldiers wake, all at once and already oriented. "That the Fund?"

"It's spam," Teo and I said simultaneously, and then looked at each other, and Bo snorted and settled back down, because Bo's policy on other people's financial catastrophes was a model of enlightened neutrality: not my circus, but I'll help bury the clowns.

I let it ring out. Eleven rings. I counted. Somewhere in there, between ring seven and ring eight, the funnel fragment reorganized, tightened, and touched down west of a wheat field owned by somebody whose name I'd never know, and for ninety glorious seconds none of us thought about money at all.

That's the honest truth about storm chasing, whatever the documentaries tell you. It's not science and it's not adrenaline and it's certainly not heroism, though there's a market for all three. It's a legitimate way to not think about things. The storm demands every scrap of attention you have and gives you nothing back except the memory of having been fully present somewhere, anywhere, other than inside your own ledger. Sixty-two thousand dollars cannot follow you into a tornado warning. Nothing compounds inside a wall cloud. For as long as the rotation holds, I am a free man.

Then the rope thins, and lifts, and dissipates into the ordinary air, and the phone starts buzzing again, and you point the truck at the next one, because the next one is another mile and miles are free.

We chased it north through the golden hour, me driving, Teo launching the drone twice for footage he'd sell to a weather channel for enough to cover fuel and half a motel, Bo waking up long enough to eat half a gas-station burrito and deliver his verdict ("structural failure of the tortilla; the filling survived"), and then the storm died the way they do—not dramatically, just a slow deflation, the atmosphere spending itself against the dark, and the plains went quiet and enormous, and the stars came out the way they only do out here, like someone had spilled something expensive.

We stopped at a crossroads with a closed café and a water tower leaning against the sky, and I sat on the hood while Teo offloaded footage and Bo stretched his back with the sound of a man cracking a walnut. Junie called in from wherever Junie was—she'd stayed home this run, working a hydrology consulting gig that paid actual money, which she claimed was temporary and which I suspected was permanent, because Junie was the only one of us with any sense.

"Where are you?" she asked.

"Crossroads outside of—" I checked the road sign. "Pampa. Sort of."

"You're two hundred miles from home and it's eleven at night."

"The storm went north."

"The storm ended three hours ago, Wendell. I track you. You know I track you."

She did. She had a feed with the truck's GPS on it and a habit of calling whenever we idled too long, because Junie believed—with the calm certainty of a woman who reads rivers for a living—that a stationary object in moving water is either dead or lying.

"Talk to me about the calls," she said.

"There are no calls."

"Wendell."

"Junie."

"Corliss Ames left a message on *my* line today. My line, Wendell. He was very polite. He used the word 'circle.' He said he wanted to keep the circle of communication open. What circle? Who talks like that?"

"People who went to business school."

"He said the Fund views your account as, quote, 'a relationship entering a new phase.'" A pause, and when she spoke again her voice had dropped its exasperation and picked up something else, something quieter and worse. "Wendell, I ran the amortization. I do math for a living. The way that contract is structured, you're not paying it down. You can't. Every payment covers the interest and a rounding error. It's not a loan, it's a leash. In five years you'll owe more than you owe now."

I watched the water tower hold up the sky and said nothing, which is my second-best skill after driving.

"Say something."

"What do you want me to say? I know the numbers."

"Do you?" And here Junie did the thing that made her Junie, the thing I both resented and relied on: she skipped past the surface and went straight to the channel underneath. "Because I don't think you do. I think you know the number and refuse to know what the number means. Those are different things. A river looks calm until you understand what the gauge readings mean, and by then you're already in the current."

"A metaphor. From you. Shocking."

"I'm serious." A breath. "He's going to offer you something. Men like that don't escalate to personal calls and messages to your friends unless they're setting up an offer. Whatever it is, it isn't for your benefit. Water doesn't flow uphill, Wendell. Nobody lends money at ruinous rates and then gets nicer unless there's downstream value somewhere."

Somewhere behind me, Teo laughed at something on his phone, quick and bright, and Bo told him to turn the volume down, gentle as a man talking to a dog he liked, and I sat on the hood of a truck worth less every month than the interest I owed on it, and felt the full weight of the arithmetic settle over me like the storm had never lifted—just moved inland, just changed shape, just started raining somewhere inside my chest.

"I hear you," I said finally.

"You don't, but you will." Junie sighed, and I heard her lean back in whatever chair she was in, three hundred miles away, keeping watch over a man who measured his life in miles specifically so it wouldn't add up. "Come home tomorrow. All three of you. I'll cook. And Wendell—if he calls again, answer. Avoiding him costs more than talking to him. That's not philosophy, that's hydrology."

She hung up before I could argue, which is also very Junie.

I sat out there a while longer. The stars wheeled. The truck ticked as it cooled. Inside, Teo had fallen asleep across the back seat with his drone bag as a pillow, and Bo had draped a jacket over him with the practiced motion of a man who'd done it a hundred times and would do it a hundred more and had never once been thanked, because that's what family is: the people who put jackets on you while you're unconscious.

Family. That word again. I'd assembled mine out of strays and wreckage—a navigator who read water like scripture, a medic with a suspended license and a heart too big for his pay grade, a kid the courts were fighting over like property—and I'd done it partly out of love, which I'd admit, and partly out of avoidance, which I mostly didn't. Because a family needs feeding, and fuel, and motel rooms, and every one of those things cost money I didn't have, and the Fund knew it, and I knew the Fund knew it, and the whole ugly machine ran on the fact that I would rather sign anything than let these people see the truck repossessed and the family scattered.

Miles, not years. It had worked for a long time. It had worked right up until tonight, sitting on the hood under a leaning water tower, when I understood—for the first time, really understood, the way you understand a gauge reading after you're already in the current—that miles don't actually go anywhere. They just accumulate. Like interest. Like rain upstream. Somewhere out there in the dark, water was gathering that hadn't fallen yet, moving toward channels I couldn't see, toward a basin I'd never heard of, toward a debt that was going to come due all at once, the way water does.

My phone lit up one more time. Not a call. A message, short and cheerful and signed with a single initial, and I read it twice and felt the ground tilt under the plains.

*Wendell — Heard your crew had a good chase today. I have a proposition that could clear the board. One meeting. No pressure. — C.A.*

No pressure. From the Meridian Fund, that phrase carried the same reassurance as a tornado warning saying *conditions favorable.*

I put the phone face-down on the hood, looked north toward home, and thought: one more mile. One more night of not knowing. Tomorrow I'd drive back and cook nothing and let Junie feed everyone and pretend for one more evening that the ledger was a problem for a future Wendell.

But the future Wendell was already out there, somewhere up the road, waiting for me with a contract in his hand and a smile like an open door.

I got in the truck, turned the key, and drove toward him.

* * *

The next morning came in gray and low, the plains hung with a ceiling of cloud that couldn't decide whether to rain, and I drove the two hundred miles home with my crew sleeping and my stomach doing slow, grinding arithmetic. Every mile marker was a countdown now. I'd spent years using miles to outrun the numbers, and now the numbers had learned the trick—they were counting down alongside me, patient as rainfall.

Home wasn't much. A rented shop bay on the edge of town with an apartment above it that Junie had somehow made livable, the truck parked under a leaky awning, a whiteboard on the wall where we tracked chases and fuel and, in Junie's neat hand, a column labeled OBLIGATIONS that I had never once looked at directly, the way you don't look directly at an eclipse.

She was waiting when we rolled in, coffee in hand, hair tied back, wearing the expression of a woman who has already won an argument that hasn't started yet.

"You answered him," she said. It wasn't a question. She'd seen the message—of course she'd seen the message, I'd left the phone on the dash like a hermit crab leaving its shell out in the open.

"I haven't answered him."

"You're going to."

I looked at the whiteboard. At the column I never read. At the three people who'd drifted, over years and disasters and shared gas-station burritos, into being the only structure my life had that held weight.

"Yeah," I said. "I'm going to."

Junie nodded slowly, and set down her coffee, and looked at me with those gauge-reading eyes that saw straight down to the channel bed.

"Then before you sign anything," she said, "you should know what the Redwater Basin is."

I stared at her. "The what?"

And that's when I should have known—should have felt it in my teeth the way you feel pressure drop before a storm—that the mile I was driving toward wasn't going to be like the others.
