## 2. The Optimists

The Meridian Fund kept its offices above a bakery on Delancey Street, which I always figured was deliberate. You walked in smelling cinnamon and thinking about warmth and sweetness, and by the time you climbed the stairs to the second floor, where the carpet was gray and the air conditioning hummed like a held breath, you'd already been softened up. Nobody lends you money out of a place that smells like burnt toast. They want you to associate them with croissants.

I knew the routine by then. Eleven months of knowing it.

The receptionist—Dana, or Dara, something with a D and a smile that never reached her eyes—waved me through without asking my name. That's a specific kind of greeting, being waved through like weather. Like something that happens *to* the building rather than in it.

Corliss Ames had the corner office. Of course he did. Corner offices are how predators mark territory without lifting a claw. His had a view of the parking structure across the street, which meant either the building's architect hated him or Ames had chosen the room where nobody could sneak up on him from behind. I'd have bet on the second. Ames struck me as a man who thought about sightlines the way other men thought about golf handicaps.

"Wendell Montrose." He came around the desk with his hand already extended, warm and dry, a handshake calibrated to feel like friendship without committing to anything. "You look tired."

"Thanks for noticing."

"I notice everything. It's my whole job." He gestured me into the chair across from his desk—the good chair, leather, positioned so you had to look slightly up at him—and poured coffee from a carafe into two ceramic cups. Real cups. Not paper. Another calculated kindness. "How's the season treating you?"

"Season's fine."

"Chased anything good lately?"

"There was a supercell over the panhandle three weeks ago. Beautiful thing. Rotated for six hours and never dropped."

"And you drove nine hundred miles to watch it not happen." He smiled like this was charming. Everything Ames said came wrapped in the assumption that your life was charming, the way you'd call a stray dog scrappy. "That's the part I've never understood about you storm people. All that driving, all that fuel, all those motel receipts, and the payoff is *watching*. You don't catch the storm. You don't sell it. You just watch it and drive home."

"Data," I said. "We sell data. Universities buy it. Insurance companies buy it."

"Right." He said it the way you'd humor a child explaining that their crayon drawing was a horse. "And how's that market treating you?"

I drank the coffee instead of answering. It was good coffee, which annoyed me. Even the coffee at Meridian was better than what I could afford.

Here's the arithmetic, since Ames was about to do it for me anyway and I prefer to get there first. I owed the Meridian Fund sixty-two thousand dollars, original principal forty-one, the rest interest compounding at a rate that should have been illegal in any state with a functioning conscience. I owed another nine thousand on the truck, which was also technically collateral for the Meridian loan, a detail buried in paragraph fourteen of a contract I'd signed at two in the morning in a diner outside Amarillo while my hands were still shaking from a hailstorm that had cost me a windshield and most of my roof rack. I owed Junie four months' pay. Bo had covered diesel twice out of his own pocket and never mentioned it, which was worse than mentioning it. Teo worked for gas money and the right to sleep somewhere his mother couldn't find him, though none of us said that last part out loud.

I measured my life in miles because miles were honest. A mile is a mile whether you're running toward something or away from it. Dollars lie. Dollars compound.

"So," Ames said, settling back against the edge of his desk rather than sitting behind it—a man who understood that looming from furniture reads as threat but looming from proximity reads as intimacy—"I'm going to tell you why I asked you here, and you're going to think it's bad news, and then I'm going to watch your face change when you realize it isn't."

"That's quite a pitch."

"It's not a pitch. It's an opportunity." He opened a folder on the desk—not sliding it toward me yet, just opening it, letting me see there were photographs inside. "You know the Redwater Basin?"

"Closed basin. Federal survey territory. You need permits to sneeze out there."

"You do indeed." He turned one photograph around so I could see it: an aerial shot of a river canyon, brown water threading between walls of red rock, nothing else for fifty miles in any direction. "Eight months ago, a government hydrological survey team went in there. Six people, top-of-the-line equipment, satellite uplinks, the works. Their contract was to map the headwaters. Water rights out west are about to be worth more than oil, Wendell. Whoever knows where the water actually comes from owns the next century. So the government sent six very smart people into a canyon to find out."

"And they didn't come back."

"They did not come back." Ames let that sit. He had a gift for silence, Ames. Most people fill quiet space because it makes them nervous. Ames used it like a crowbar. "Search teams went in. Found the camp intact. Tents, instruments, food. No bodies. No tracks leaving. Nothing. Officially, the team is listed as missing, presumed lost to flash flooding—which is interesting, because there hadn't been rain in that drainage for three weeks before they vanished. But official stories don't require weather to make sense. They only require closure."

The photograph looked ordinary. Canyons always do, from the air. From the air, everything looks survivable.

"What does this have to do with me?"

"The equipment in that camp is worth north of two million dollars. Government surplus rules say abandoned assets can be recovered under salvage contract by licensed private operators. Meridian holds interests in several companies that hold such licenses." He finally slid the folder across the desk. "Your company—Montrose Field Services, LLC—is one of them. Congratulations. You incorporated yourself into an opportunity."

"I incorporated because your people told me to incorporate. Said it would 'simplify the relationship.'"

"And hasn't it?" He beamed. Ames's smile had a lot of gears in it. "Here's the offer, plain as I can make it. You go into the Redwater Basin. You recover the survey team's equipment—data drives especially, but the hardware too, whatever's portable. You bring it out. We liquidate it, we credit the proceeds against your balance, and we restructure the remainder at a rate a human being could actually live with. Do the job well, and in eighteen months you're clean. Zero. A man who owns his own truck again."

I looked at the photographs. Canyon country, all of it. The kind of terrain where a river is both the road and the thing trying to kill you. I'd run water like that in my twenties, before the debt, when risk was a flavor and not a bill.

"And if I say no?"

Ames spread his hands, all apology. "Then nothing happens today. We're not monsters, Wendell. But the restructuring offer expires—it has to, incentives matter—and the standard terms continue. And I'll be honest with you, because I respect you: at current accrual, your balance crosses seventy-five thousand by spring. Your truck's assessed value is nineteen. The math stops working sometime around February, and when the math stops working, we stop being a fund and start being a collector. Different department. Less pleasant carpet."

He wasn't threatening me. That was the terrible elegance of it. He was just describing the future, patiently, the way a doctor describes a prognosis. The threat was implicit in the universe he operated, and he merely lived closer to its controls than I did.

"Why me?" I asked. "You've got recovery companies. Actual ones. With insurance and lawyers."

"We have companies with overhead," Ames said. "What we need is somebody who can read country. Somebody who's spent his life looking at weather systems and terrain and knowing where trouble pools. And frankly"—he leaned forward, and for one unguarded half-second the smile dropped and something older and tireder looked out of his face—"somebody expendable. If there's something strange in that basin, and the search teams' reports suggest there might be, I'd rather lose a man who owes me money than a man I owe a salary. Is that honest enough for you? I find honesty lubricates these arrangements."

It was the most honest thing anyone at Meridian had ever said to me, and it made my skin crawl worse than any threat would have.

I turned the photographs over one by one. Canyon walls. A bend of brown river. One shot of the camp itself, taken from a ridge by some long lens: six dome tents, a solar array, a mast with instruments on it, all of it sitting neat and orderly as a model village. Abandoned camps are supposed to look ransacked, scattered, desperate. This one looked like everyone had just stepped out to stretch their legs.

"When?"

"You'd leave within the week. Dry season's coming; the upper basin gets impassable once the river drops. You'd have maybe five weeks of workable water, less if the monsoon fails." He tapped the folder. "There's a route document inside. Put together by our people. River profile, put-in points, the location of the survey camp. Everything you need."

Everything except a reason to believe any of it.

I want to be clear about what happened next, because I've replayed it enough times to have opinions. I didn't sign because I believed the story. I didn't even fully believe the opportunity—I've been around money long enough to know that when a lender offers you a door, you should check it for hinges on the wrong side. I signed because I sat there doing the math Ames wanted me to do, the only math he'd left me, and every branch of it ended the same way. Take the job: possible ruin, possible freedom. Refuse: certain ruin, on a schedule, with a collections department that had a reputation I'd heard whispered about by other borrowers in other waiting rooms. There was no branch where I kept what I had. There was no branch where the truck stayed mine and the crew stayed paid and February never came.

When a man offers you two doors and one of them is on fire, he doesn't need to push you through the other one. He just waits.

"One condition," I said.

Ames raised his eyebrows, delighted, like I was a dog that had learned a new trick. "Name it."

"My crew comes on as employees of the contract. Paid, insured, named. If anything goes wrong out there, it's on the company, not on them personally. I'm not signing anything that makes them liable for my debts."

Something flickered across his face—surprise, maybe, or recalculation. For a moment I thought he'd refuse, and I genuinely didn't know what I'd do then, whether I had any cards at all or just the memory of them. Then the smile reassembled itself, gear by gear.

"Family man," he said, and there was something almost wistful in it, which frightened me more than the threat of February had. "Fine. Named personnel, standard indemnification. See? This is why I like you, Montrose. You negotiate like a man with nothing to lose, which is the most dangerous kind." He spun a pen across the desk toward me. "Page twelve. Initial each page. The last signature's the fun one."

I read page twelve. I read all of it, or I told myself I did—the truth is that contracts written at this altitude of legalese aren't meant to be read, they're meant to be survived, and I skimmed for the words that bite: *liability*, *forfeit*, *sole discretion*, *in perpetuity*. I initialed. I signed. My handwriting came out steadier than I expected, which felt like a betrayal of something.

Ames stood, shook my hand again, warmer now, a handshake between partners, conspirators, co-owners of a small ugly secret. "Wendell. Genuinely—this is going to work out. I have a feeling about you."

"Everybody at Meridian has feelings about me. None of them are good."

"Oh, I wouldn't say that." He walked me to the door himself, past Dana-or-Dara, past the gray carpet, and paused with his hand on the frame. "For what it's worth, the survey team was good people. Smart, careful, experienced. Whatever happened to them, it wasn't carelessness. Keep that in mind out there."

It took me until the stairs to realize he'd meant it as a warning dressed as sympathy, and until the sidewalk—bakery smell rising up around me like a tide—to realize that everything Corliss Ames said worked exactly that way. Dread dressed as opportunity. Threat dressed as honesty. Warning dressed as kindness.

Outside, the afternoon had gone the color of old tin, and there was a wall cloud building off to the northwest, fat and slow and gorgeous, the kind of sky that made my hands itch for the wheel. Any other year I'd have pointed the truck at it and chased it until dark, chasing being the only prayer I knew how to say.

Instead I sat in the cab with the engine off and the contract on the passenger seat, and I called Junie.

She answered on the second ring, the way she always did, like she'd been holding the phone. "Tell me you're calling with good news."

"I'm calling with news."

"That's what you said last time."

"Yeah." I watched the wall cloud lean against the horizon, beautiful and indifferent, going nowhere near me. "Pack for rivers. We're going to the Redwater Basin."

There was a long pause, long enough that I heard wind moving past wherever she was standing. When she spoke again, her voice had gone careful, the way it did when she was reading water nobody else could see.

"Wendell. Nobody goes to the Redwater Basin."

"I know," I said, and started the truck.
