## 3. Crew of Strays

The contract was signed at 9:14 in the morning, and by 10 I'd already spent the advance twice in my head—once on the Meridian Fund, once on the Meridian Fund again. That's the thing about debt: it gets first dibs on every fantasy you have, including the fantasies about escaping it.

So there wasn't much left over for hiring a crew, which was fine, because I wasn't really hiring anyone. I was calling in favors from people who had nowhere better to be, which is how all my best crews have been assembled. You don't recruit a good salvage team. You recognize one, the way a stray dog recognizes another stray dog across a parking lot. There's a look. A tired, hungry, *still-here* look.

I started with Junie because Junie always answers on the second ring, like she's been sitting next to the phone waiting for it to be me.

* * *

Junie Okafor lived in a houseboat marina outside Bakersfield that smelled like diesel and creosote and, faintly, like someone's dream of the ocean that had never quite survived the drive inland. She came out on deck before I'd even finished tying off, which told me she'd seen my truck coming from a half mile out. Junie saw everything coming. It was her whole gift.

"You look terrible," she said.

"Good morning to you too."

"That wasn't a greeting. It was a diagnosis." She leaned on the rail. Junie was fifty-one and built like a piece of driftwood—all weather and no give—and she had the particular calm of a person who had spent forty years reading rivers and had concluded, correctly, that most problems downstream were caused upstream by people in a hurry. "You signed something."

It wasn't a question. Junie could read contracts on people the way she read water, from the set of their shoulders.

"How can you possibly know that?"

"Because you've got your 'opportunity' face on. It's the same face you wore when you bought the second truck. And the third truck. And when you married Dana." She let that sit for a second, not cruelly, just accurately. "Sit down, Wendell. Tell me who owns you now."

So I sat down on her deck, under a wind chime made of bent spoons, and I told her about Corliss Ames and his warm handshake and his warmer smile, about the Redwater Basin and the vanished survey team and the equipment that somebody with money wanted back. I told it straight, because lying to Junie was like lying to a river. It just carried you somewhere worse than where you started.

She listened without interrupting, which was its own kind of terrifying, and then she looked out at the flat brown water of the canal for a long time.

"They didn't vanish," she said finally.

"The contract says they vanished."

"The contract also says the basin is closed for 'environmental remediation,' which means somebody's hiding something, and it says the recovery window is six weeks, which means whatever's hiding it has a clock on it." She turned and looked at me. "You're going anyway."

"I'm going anyway."

"And you want a navigator who knows the Redwater drainage."

"I want *you*, Junie. The rest is negotiable."

She laughed—a short dry sound, like a rock skipping once and sinking. "Flattery. From a man with negative net worth." She went inside and came back with a battered canvas roll, which she dropped into my hands. Inside were her charts. Hand-drawn ones, ink on waterproof paper, of canyon country I knew from maps existed only as blank space marked *closed area*. She'd drawn them herself. Over years. Which meant she'd been up there, or near enough, long before any of this.

"You've been in the Redwater," I said.

"I've been along the edges of the Redwater." She took the roll back and rewrapped it with the care other people reserved for sleeping infants. "Water comes out of that system doing things water shouldn't do. Temperature wrong for the season. Clarity wrong for the geology. Flow rates that don't add up against the snowpack." She tied the cord off. "Rivers tell you what's underneath them, Wendell. That one's been telling a story nobody wants written down. I've wanted to go read the source of it for fifteen years."

"So that's a yes?"

"That's a yes, and don't mistake it for loyalty. I'm coming for the river." Then, quieter, almost too low to hear over the wind chime: "And maybe a little bit for you, since you'll drown inside a week without me and I've grown accustomed to your face."

That was Junie's version of a hug. I took it.

* * *

Bo Calloway was harder to find and easier to convince, mostly because when I found him he was working an unlicensed medic tent at a motocross event outside Barstow, stitching up a teenager's forearm under a canopy while a race announcer screamed something about a triple jump into a microphone that cost more than my truck.

He saw me coming through the crowd and didn't stop stitching. "Whatever it is, no."

"You don't even know what it is."

"It's you walking toward me with a problem shaped like a job offer. I know the shape." He tied off, snipped the thread, taped gauze down with the efficiency of ten thousand repetitions, and sent the kid off with instructions he absolutely would not follow. Only then did he turn around. Bo was built like a refrigerator that had decided to become kind, big hands, gray creeping into his beard, eyes that had seen everything twice and flinched at none of it except, I suspected, himself. "Hey, Wendell."

"Hey, Bo."

We stood there in the noise and dust. There was history between us, most of it good, some of it the night in Kingman I didn't bring up and neither did he—the night his license stopped existing and my truck acquired a dent shaped like a guardrail. We'd saved each other's lives in ways we never talked about, which is the strongest glue there is.

"Salvage contract," I said. "Redwater Basin. Six weeks, maybe less. Government survey team went missing, gear needs recovering, client is—" I paused, because saying *the Fund* out loud still tasted like swallowing a coin. "Client is financing."

"Financing." He wiped his hands on a rag. "Wendell. Look at me. Am I about to regret asking this?"

"Probably."

"What's the actual job? Because 'salvage' from a place that's sealed off by the feds isn't salvage. That's trespassing with extra paperwork."

"Maybe. Or maybe it's exactly what it says and everybody's jumpy for nothing." I shrugged, and Bo watched the shrug the way Junie watched water, reading everything underneath it. "I need a medic. Real one. Canyon country, whitewater, heat. If something goes wrong out there, there's no helicopter coming, and I'm not taking people I love into that without you."

Something moved behind his eyes when I said *people I love*. He tried to hide it and failed, the way big men always fail at hiding softness—they just aren't built for concealment.

"Suspended license," he said. "You remember that."

"I remember a hearing that was more about politics than medicine. I remember you doing CPR on a guy for twenty-two minutes in a diner parking lot because the ambulance was stuck in traffic, and the state deciding the crime scene was more important than the heartbeat. Yeah. I remember."

"Twenty-four minutes." He corrected me quietly, looking away. "Twenty-four minutes, and he walked out of the hospital nine days later, and I still lost the license." He folded the rag over and over until it was a small tight square. "You know what I do now? Rodeo injuries and dirt bike crashes. Kids with broken collarbones whose parents pay cash and ask no questions. I'm good at it. I hate it. Every splint is a reminder that I used to matter in rooms where it counted."

"You matter everywhere you show up, Bo. That's not the license. That's you."

He looked at me for a long moment. Around us the announcer screamed, engines snarled, somebody's cooler lid clapped open and shut. Bo lived in places like this now, loud places, temporary places, places where nobody asked questions because nobody planned to stay. I understood that architecture intimately. I'd built my whole life out of it.

"One condition," he said.

"Name it."

"We do this clean. Whatever's actually going on in that basin—I've been around long enough to smell it, same as you—you don't lie to the crew. Not to protect them. Not to protect yourself. Especially not to protect yourself." He pointed the folded rag at me like a small blunt weapon. "I've watched you carry things alone, Wendell, and it never once made anything lighter. It just made you slower and everyone else blinder. So. Full honesty, or find another refrigerator."

"I'll be honest."

"Wendell."

"I said I'll be honest." And I meant it, right then, standing in the dust with the engines screaming. Whether I'd mean it in two weeks, on a satellite phone call with Ames's voice in my ear—that was a debt I hadn't come to yet.

He stuck out one enormous hand. "Then I'm in. God help us all."

* * *

Teo was the hard one, and not because Teo was hard to find. Teo lived in my guest room, or technically in the space that had once been a guest room and had since evolved into something between a mission control center and a teenage disaster zone—three monitors, a soldering iron, a foam-padded case containing a drone worth more than my truck, and a constellation of energy drink cans arranged with the casual geometry of a kid who had stopped pretending anyone was going to make him tidy up.

Teo had shown up eight months ago, sixteen years old, after I'd hired him for two days of aerial survey work on a flood assessment job. Two days became a week became "can I crash on your couch" became a couch that now had a name and a blanket and a charging station. His mother was in Phoenix fighting a custody war with his stepfather that had gone from lawyers to allegations to something darker, and Teo had done what smart kids do when the adults start using them as ammunition: he'd gone mobile. He'd picked the one adult he knew who was never home.

Which meant, of course, that the one adult he knew was about to leave for six weeks into closed canyon country with federal interest and predatory lenders circling. You see the problem.

I found him on the couch editing flight footage, goggles pushed up in his curls, feet on the coffee table. He glanced up, saw my expression, and muted the video.

"You're leaving," he said.

"I haven't said anything."

"You have the leaving face. You get a specific face. It's like a dog before thunder." He sat up. "How long?"

"Six weeks. Maybe less."

"Where?"

I hesitated, and Teo caught the hesitation the way he caught thermal updrafts—instantly and completely. Sixteen years old and already reading people like text, same as Junie read water. I was surrounded. I'd built it that way and hadn't noticed until now.

"Redwater Basin," I admitted.

Teo whistled. He was a geography nerd under all the screens; he kept topographic maps as phone wallpaper. "That place is sealed. Like, *federal sign* sealed. What's the job?"

"Recovery. Equipment. For a client." I sat down on the arm of the couch, which put me below his eye level, which felt honest. "Here's the thing, Teo. I'm not asking you to come."

His whole body went still. Teenagers are bad at many kinds of acting and excellent at this one—the total freeze that means *I care so much I will not move a muscle in case caring scares it away.*

"This isn't a flood survey," I went on. "There are government people involved, and some private people I trust less than the government, and I don't know yet exactly what's true out there. What I know is it might get complicated, and complicated is not where I get to take a minor."

"I'm sixteen, not furniture."

"I know what you are."

"Do you?" And there it was—not anger, exactly, but the wound showing through, quick and bright. "Because everyone keeps deciding what I am. My mom decides I'm evidence. Ray decides I'm leverage. The court decides I'm a schedule item. You decide I'm a kid you have to leave home so he's safe." He gestured at the apartment—at the drone case, the monitors, the life he'd assembled here out of spare parts. "Nobody ever decides I'm *crew*."

The word landed in the room and stayed there. Crew. I thought about Junie on her deck with her hand-drawn charts, fifteen years of wanting to read a forbidden river. I thought about Bo folding his rag into a small tight square, twenty-four minutes of CPR that saved a life and cost him his own. I thought about myself, measuring my life in miles because miles were the only unit that never asked what I was running from.

Strays, all of us. Recognizing each other across the parking lot.

"Okay," I said. "New rule. Nobody decides for anybody. Crew votes. Including you. But you vote knowing what I know, which means I tell you everything, which means—" I stopped. Bo's voice was already in my ear. *Full honesty.* "Which means I tell you everything. Starting with the fact that my client is the outfit that holds my debt, and they may not be telling me the whole truth either."

Teo blinked. Of everything I might have said, I think that was the last thing he expected—an adult volunteering his own mess. He turned it over.

"That's a terrible pitch," he said finally. "You're describing a nightmare."

"Yeah."

"I'm in." He was already up, pulling the drone case toward him, checking latches, and I realized the decision had been made somewhere in him long ago—maybe eight months ago, the night he showed up on my couch and I didn't ask a single question, just handed him a blanket. Some debts run deeper than money. I owed the Meridian Fund sixty-some thousand dollars. I owed this kid the truth, and I intended to pay that one first.

"Rules," I said. "You fly, you don't cross the ridgelines alone, and if Bo says sleep, you sleep."

"If *Bo's* going?" Teo lit up like a monitor booting. "Wait, is Junie going? Oh man, Junie's going. Okay. Okay okay okay." He was grinning now, sixteen again instead of sixty, and the grin did something to my chest I wasn't prepared for. "When do we leave?"

"Two days. I need to provision."

"I'll build a comms kit. Backup antennas. We should have relay capability in the deep canyons, the walls will kill line-of-sight—" He was already turning to his bench, hands moving, mind three steps ahead, and I let myself out onto the balcony and looked at the evening coming down over the city, orange and dusty and ordinary.

Three strays and a drowning man. No permits, no insurance, a client with a smile full of fine print, heading into a basin the government had sealed for reasons nobody would explain. By every rational measure it was the worst decision of my life, and I'd made some contenders.

But standing there, listening to Teo's soldering iron hiss through the screen door, watching the light go gold and then blue, I felt something I hadn't felt since before the debt, maybe since before Dana, maybe since before the miles started substituting for years.

I felt like I was going *toward* something instead of away.

My phone buzzed in my pocket. Unknown number. Text, four words:

**Safe travels upstream, Mr. Montrose.**

Corliss Ames, wishing me well. Somehow that made the hair on my arms stand up worse than any threat could have.
