## 4. Gear and No Tracks

I've read water my whole life. Rivers tell you everything if you know the language—the way a riffle stutters over a shallow bar, how a cutbank whispers about what it's eating, the particular silence a deep pool keeps like a held breath. Water is honest. It can't help itself.

People lie. Rivers don't. Which is why I trusted the Redwater long before I trusted anything else about this job, and why, when we rounded the last bend below Miller Fork and saw the survey camp sitting neat as a postcard on its gravel terrace, my stomach dropped before my brain caught up and told me why.

The river was low and glassy there, running green over pale stone, and the camp sat above it on the bench like something set down by a careful hand. Four dome tents, taut. A mess fly strung between two junipers. Solar panels angled at the morning sun. A satellite dish pointed at a sky that had been lying to it for months.

"Looks cozy," Bo said from the stern of the second raft. "Somebody's mom packed for them."

"Somebody's mom didn't pack for them," I said. "Look at the guy lines."

Bo looked. Bo is a big man with small, precise eyes, the kind of eyes that used to triage car wrecks for a living, and when he went quiet the whole river seemed to go quiet with him.

"The guy lines are tensioned," he said finally.

"Tensioned," I agreed. "Four months in wind and rain, and every tent on that bench is pitched like it was done ten minutes ago."

Wendell brought our raft alongside the gravel bar and we nosed in, and I'll be honest—part of me wanted to find wreckage. Torn nylon, scattered gear, the honest chaos of people leaving in a hurry or not leaving at all. Chaos tells a story you can read. What we found instead was order, and order with nobody in it is the most frightening sentence the desert knows.

* * *

We spent an hour just walking the perimeter before anybody touched anything. That was Wendell's call, and it was a good one, because Wendell has a talent for caution that he usually disguises as laziness. He walked the terrace with his hands in his pockets, reading ground the way I read water, and Teo flew the drone in slow circles overhead while Bo stood at the rafts and watched the canyon mouth behind us like it owed him money.

The ground told us things. The ground always does.

There were boot prints everywhere—dozens of overlapping sets, the churned palimpsest of a working camp. Surveyors walk a lot; they'd stamped trails from tents to instrument stations to the water's edge and back, hundreds of passes, the gravel polished along their routes. But here's what the ground said, in its flat, factual voice: all of those prints pointed *in*. Or rather, they pointed everywhere, in every direction, the way a living camp's prints do—and then they stopped.

Not faded. Not blown over. Stopped.

At the edge of the terrace, where the bench dropped to the river, the traffic simply ended, mid-stride almost, as if the last person to walk out had been lifted off the earth like a punctuation mark removed from a sentence. And downstream, where any sane party would have hiked out—the way we'd come, the open canyon, the route with water and gradient in your favor—there was nothing. No trail worn by departing boots. No sign anyone had ever left on foot at all.

"They came in by boat," Wendell said, crouched at the landing. He turned over a flat stone near the waterline. Underneath, pressed into the mud that had dried hard as pottery, were the keel scars of heavy rafts, four months old at least, maybe five. "Two boats minimum, probably three. Fully loaded."

"And then?" Teo asked. She had the drone controller hanging off her neck and her arms crossed, which is her posture for *I am fourteen and unimpressed*, except her voice cracked on the *then* and gave her away.

"That's the question, isn't it." Wendell stood and looked up-canyon, toward the source, though none of us said the word yet. "Nobody walked out. And I'm not seeing a helicopter pad, are you?"

"I'm seeing three months of dust," Bo said, "and dust doesn't lie either. Nothing's landed on this bench since they did."

* * *

Inside the camp, it got worse. Better, if you're the kind of person who likes mysteries. Worse, if you're the kind of person who likes sleeping at night. I am both, which is why I drink.

The mess fly was the first thing I really looked at, because kitchens tell the truth faster than bedrooms do. There were four plates on the folding table. Not washed and stacked—*set*. Half-eaten meals under a fine skin of dust. One plate had rice and something bean-shaped fossilized to it. Another still held a mug, upright, a ring of ancient coffee dried at the bottom like a brown geologic layer. Somebody had been halfway through breakfast—or halfway through dinner, the light in the canyon made it hard to say—and then.

Then what?

Chairs pushed back, but not knocked over. A card deck mid-game on a crate, solitaire, six cards laid out in a row that no wind had disturbed. A paperback facedown on somebody's cot with a pencil marking the page. A jacket hung on a tent pole. Reading glasses folded on top of a data logbook.

"It's like they evaporated," Teo said, and her drone buzzed somewhere overhead like a nervous insect. "Like the Rapture but for government nerds."

"People don't evaporate," Bo said. He was standing very still in the middle of the camp, turning in a slow circle, and I realized he wasn't looking at the gear. He was looking at the gaps. "People get taken. Or people leave. This looks like neither."

"What does it look like, then?" I asked.

He thought about it with his whole body, the way he does. "It looks like a stage set," he said finally. "After the play. Like everybody knew the show was over and just... stepped off."

I didn't like that. I want to be clear about that. Of all the explanations available—flash flood (no; the camp sat thirty feet above any flood line, and flash floods leave mud), bear (no; no sign, and bears don't fold jackets), fire, illness, mutiny, alien abduction, which Teo offered twice with increasing sincerity—none of them fit. The only thing that fit was the thing that didn't make sense: that these people had intended to come back. Every object in that camp said *we're coming back*. And they hadn't. For four months. In country where four months without your camp means you're dead, unless—

Unless they weren't dead. Unless they'd gone somewhere they could live. My eyes went up-canyon, where the walls pinched together and the river narrowed into water I couldn't read from here, and I made myself stop thinking about it, because the alternative thoughts were worse.

* * *

The instruments were the part that got to Wendell. I watched him stand in front of the monitoring station—a folding table under its own fly, bolted equipment cases, a laptop tethered to solar batteries—and I watched his face do something complicated.

"It's still recording," he said.

"Yeah," I said.

"No, Junie. It's still *recording*." He tapped the screen. Green waveform, scrolling. Numbers ticking over. "Somebody left this running. On purpose. Four months of battery management—" he nodded at the panels, the charge controller, the tidy cable runs— "somebody has been keeping this station alive. Or set it up so it would keep itself alive. Either way, that's not a camp that got abandoned. That's a camp that got *left as a message*."

"A message for who?"

"For whoever came looking." He looked at me, and behind all that easygoing slouch there was a mind working fast, and for once he wasn't hiding it. "Corliss sent us to salvage equipment. You don't send salvagers to a site somebody deliberately preserved. Unless the person who sent you doesn't know it's preserved. Unless the person who sent you thinks there's nothing here but dead men's gear."

"Or," I said, "unless Corliss knows exactly what's here, and wants somebody else to touch it first."

Wendell was quiet a moment. Downstream, the Redwater ran over its stones, saying the only true thing in the canyon, over and over.

"Well," he said. "Let's touch it carefully, then."

* * *

We worked the rest of the afternoon, and I'll give the crew this: we work well together, four strays who shouldn't fit and somehow do. Bo inventoried the medical kit and the food stores with a field nurse's economy, calling out items in a low voice—first aid mostly untouched, food caches sealed, a water filter whose cartridge was recent. Teo catalogued the electronics, photographing serial numbers with her drone camera because she doesn't trust her own hands not to shake, though she'd die before admitting it. Wendell went through the paperwork: survey permits, shipping manifests, a duty roster on a clipboard.

And I read the camp the way I read the river, which meant I ended up knowing things none of us wanted to know.

Four people lived here. Four plates, four cots, four sets of everything. But the camp had been maintained by fewer than four, lately. The dish had been re-aimed—there were fresh scuff marks at its base, weeks old, maybe two months. The solar panels had been cleaned; dust doesn't wipe itself, and the panel glass was clear while everything around it wore its gray coat. Someone had been here. Recently. Keeping the lights on. Keeping the story going.

Or someone had never left at all.

I stood at the edge of the terrace at dusk with that thought in my teeth, watching the water go bronze and then gray, and I did what I always do when the land confuses me: I looked at the river. The Redwater came down from the narrows upstream, quiet and cold, and I swear to you—I swear—it smelled wrong. Not polluted-wrong. *Alive*-wrong. Cold and mineral and faintly sweet, like water that had recently been somewhere dark and deep and pressurized, somewhere it had picked up the taste of stone under pressure. Headwater smell. Source smell. I'd tasted it once, years ago, in a spring that came up through limestone in a place no map admitted to.

This river was carrying that smell down from up-canyon. Fresh.

"Junie." Wendell's voice behind me, low. He'd come up quiet, holding the clipboard. "Duty roster ends on a Tuesday. Four names, checkmarks through eleven weeks. Then nothing. Last entry, somebody wrote one word across the bottom of the sheet."

"What word?"

He handed me the clipboard. The pencil letters were small and hard-pressed, the writing of somebody making sure the message survived:

*UPSTREAM.*

Below it, nothing. No signature. No date. Just that one word, and the paper's faint tooth showing where an eraser had scrubbed away whatever had been written after it.

* * *

That night we camped downstream of the survey site, because none of us said it out loud but all of us wanted the abandoned camp out of eyeshot. We ate freeze-dried whatever around a small fire, and Teo fell asleep against Bo's arm halfway through her second helping, the drone controller still clipped to her belt like a teddy bear with propellers. Bo carried her to her bag without waking her, which is a two-hundred-pound man being gentle with sixty pounds of teenager, and if you want to understand who Bo is, that's the whole biography right there.

Wendell sat across the coals from me, turning his satellite phone over and over in his hands. Not checking it. Just holding it, the way you'd hold a snake you hadn't decided whether to keep.

"You felt it too," I said. "Up-canyon."

"The water?" He nodded. "Cold for the season. Sweet. It's coming from somewhere it shouldn't be coming from."

"There's somebody up there, Wendell. Somebody's been maintaining that station. Weeks, not months. And whoever they are, they left us a word instead of a note, which means they didn't want to be found by just anybody." I poked the fire. "They wanted to be found by somebody who reads carefully."

"Or they wanted to warn us off."

"Same handwriting," I said. "Warnings and invitations look identical when you write them in pencil."

He laughed, short and quiet, so as not to wake Teo. Then his face settled into something I'd seen on him only a few times—the look he gets when the numbers catch up with him, when the debt and the danger and the miles all land on the same side of the ledger.

"Tomorrow we go through their data drives," he said. "Teo's already drooling over the laptop. Whatever those surveyors found, it's in there, and Corliss Ames wants it bad enough to forgive a debt I couldn't pay off in ten lifetimes." He fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. "I keep trying to figure out what kind of salvage job comes with a secret this size, and every answer I get makes me want to row the other direction."

"So why aren't we rowing the other direction?"

He looked at me across the fire, and for a second he looked young, and tired, and honest—the three things he works hardest to hide.

"Because nobody walks out of a camp like that," he said. "And somebody's still up there keeping the lights on. And I need to know, Junie. Before Ames does. Before Reyes does. Before anybody else decides what happens to the people who found whatever's at the head of this river." He glanced at the sleeping shapes of Bo and Teo. "I need to know before it happens to us."

The fire popped. Up-canyon, the narrows breathed out its cold sweet air, and the Redwater ran past us in the dark, carrying its secret south one honest gallon at a time.

In the morning, Teo cracked the survey team's laptop in under twenty minutes, and the first thing on the screen made her call out for all of us in a voice I'd never heard her use before—not the tough-kid voice, not the drone-pilot voice. A scared kid's voice.

"Guys," she said. "You need to see what these instruments saw."
