## 5. What the Instruments Saw

The thing about dead people's laptops is that they're heavier than regular laptops.

Not physically. Physically they weigh the same as any other ruggedized field unit—three pounds of scuffed polycarbonate and hope. But when you pick one up off a folding table where somebody left it mid-sentence, with a coffee mug fossilized to the desk ring beside it and a paperback splayed face-down like the reader meant to come back, it acquires mass. Gravitational mass. The kind you feel in your wrists.

I'd been in the survey camp for maybe twenty minutes before I touched anything, because Junie had rules about touching things, and Bo had rules about Junie's rules, and Wendell had a general policy of agreeing with whoever was loudest, which was usually Junie. So we stood around in the morning light filtering through the canvas of the main tent like tourists at a museum exhibit titled *People Who Were Here and Then Weren't*, and I did what I always do when adults go quiet: I got on my knees and started looking under furniture.

"Teo," Junie said.

"I'm not taking anything. I'm *looking*."

"You're fourteen."

"I'm fourteen and a half." This is technically true for eleven more months and I intend to use every one of them. "And nobody else was going to look under the table. That's where the good stuff always is. Ask any kid."

She didn't argue, which from Junie is basically a standing ovation. Under the table there was a hard-sided equipment case, latched but not locked, and inside it, nested in foam cutouts shaped exactly for them, were four data drives. Government-issue, matte gray, each one stenciled with a string of letters and numbers that probably meant something to somebody with a clearance. Each one also had a strip of colored tape across its lid—green, green, yellow, red—and written on the red tape in ballpoint, in handwriting so small and cramped it looked like the pen had been fighting the paper:

*DO NOT TRANSMIT. HAND-CARRY ONLY.*

"Well," I said. "That's not ominous or anything."

Bo came over and read it upside down, which is his preferred reading angle since he's roughly the size of a vending machine. "Could mean anything. Could be boring. Survey data's classified all the time. Water tables, mineral rights, boring government stuff."

"Yeah," I said. "Boring government stuff that made eleven people evaporate out of their own boots."

We didn't know yet that it was eleven. At that point we just knew it was *somebody*, plural, because there were six tents and every single one of them told the same story: life interrupted, not ended. Not ransacked, not burned, not looted. Interrupted. A card game frozen mid-hand on a crate outside the mess tent—the ace of spades still sitting on top of the pile like it had won something. A pair of hiking boots drying on a rock by the creek, laces still tied. Somebody's phone charger plugged into a solar panel that had been quietly charging nothing for months, blinking its little green light at an empty camp like a night-light left on for a kid who moved away.

Junie walked the perimeter twice and came back with her jaw set in the way that means she's done math she doesn't like. "No tracks leaving," she said. "There are tracks coming in—survey boot tread, lots of it, months of it. There are tracks going down to the water. There are no tracks going anywhere else. It's like they walked into the river."

"People don't walk into rivers," Bo said. "Eleven somebodies don't walk into a river."

"Maybe they boated out," Wendell offered, in the voice he uses when he's trying to keep a room from tipping over. Wendell has a lot of voices. That one's my least favorite, because it means even he doesn't believe it.

"The rafts are here," Junie said. "All three of them. Inflated. Staked."

So yeah. Nobody argued after that. We just sort of stood there in the middle of a camp full of unfinished sentences, and I decided, privately, that the best thing I could do with the crawling feeling in my stomach was give it a job. That's my whole strategy for fear, honestly. Fear is just energy with bad PR. You point it at something and it becomes focus, and focus becomes competence, and competence is the only currency anybody's ever paid me in that didn't bounce.

The job I gave it was the drives.

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about being the tech guy on a crew of four: you're also the janitor, the IT department, the complaints department, and the person who gets blamed when the satellite modem does the thing where it pretends to have signal and then laughs at you. I'm sixteen months into teaching myself everything from YouTube videos filmed by guys named Dave, and I will say this for Dave: he has never once let me down, unlike every adult who was supposed to be in charge of me before Wendell found me sleeping in the cab of his truck behind a storm-chaser convention in Amarillo.

But that's a different chapter. This chapter is about the drives.

I set up on the mess table because it was the biggest flat surface and because working in the mess tent felt less like grave-robbing than working in the personal tents did. I told myself that. I believed it for about an hour.

The drives weren't encrypted—which was the first weird thing. Government field units, hand-carry-only, do-not-transmit, and the file structure is just... open? Folders with plain names? Either whoever built this system trusted their people completely, or they never imagined a teenage drone pilot with a pirated copy of a forensic toolkit would be the one reading it. Both options said something interesting about the government's imagination.

The logs went back fourteen months. I started at the beginning because that's how stories work, and for the first three hundred pages of data, Bo was right. It was boring. Beautifully, blessedly boring. Water chemistry readings. Flow rates. Sediment cores catalogued with the loving precision of people who genuinely loved sediment, which, respect. Seismic baseline surveys—thumps and echoes, the canyon's skeleton X-rayed over and over. Temperature gradients. pH levels. Eleven names in the personnel roster, and little metadata fingerprints of each one all over the files: someone initials every calibration entry *E.M.*, someone else leaves snarky comments in the code (*// if this breaks again I'm quitting to farm almonds*), someone photographs every core sample against the same blue bandana for scale.

They felt real. That's the thing I kept coming back to. You can fake data, but you can't fake the almond joke appearing forty times across fourteen months, evolving, getting funnier, getting tired near the end. These were people. I knew them a little by then. I liked them.

Then I got to the seismic files from the last six weeks, and I stopped liking anything, because the numbers stopped making sense.

Okay. Seismic refraction, super simplified because I'm explaining it to myself as much as to you: you make a noise, you listen to how the ground echoes back, and the echo tells you what layers are underneath. Rock reflects one way. Loose sediment another. Water another. It's basically sonar for dirt. And the baseline surveys—the early ones—showed exactly what you'd expect under the Redwater headwaters. Limestone, shale, gravel, the river aquifer sitting in its channel like it was supposed to.

Six weeks before the end, the reflections started coming back wrong.

Not noisy. Not degraded. *Wrong.* Echoes returning from depths where the survey maps said there was solid bedrock—echoes with the signature of open space. Vast open space. And the space was moving.

I want to be careful with that word because I know how it sounds. Moving. Cavities don't move. Aquifers shift, sure, slowly, seasonally, but this wasn't seasonal drift. Between survey passes—some only days apart—the boundary of whatever was down there changed by hundreds of meters. One pass it sat east of the main channel. Three days later it was under the channel. Two days after that it had spread west, and the western edge showed a reflection pattern the software flagged with an error message I had to look up, and the error message essentially said: *this return doesn't correspond to any known material class.*

One of the surveyors—E.M., the initialer, the bandana photographer—had annotated that file. Just one line in the notes field, dated thirty-nine days before the end:

*Re-ran the array four times. Same result. Whatever is down there, it is not geology.*

I sat back from the screen. The mess tent was very quiet. Outside, the Redwater did its patient silver work over the rocks, sounding exactly like a river is supposed to sound, which suddenly felt like a performance it was putting on for my benefit.

Bo stuck his head in around noon, saw my face, and came all the way in instead of making a joke, which tells you how bad my face was. "What've you got?"

"Probably nothing," I said, which is what you say when you've definitely got something. "Seismic stuff. Weird readings. Could be instrument error."

"Is it instrument error?"

I thought about the four re-runs. About E.M.'s note. About the fact that professional scientists with government funding had looked at this same screen and then, apparently, walked into a river. "The instruments worked fine," I said. "That's the problem."

He nodded slowly, the way Bo does, absorbing it through some process that takes place mostly in his shoulders. "Wendell's gonna want to push upstream anyway. He's got that look."

"What look?"

"The look he gets when running away starts feeling too much like giving up. You'll see it. It's the same look he had when he decided to hire a minor."

"I'm a *professional* minor."

"You're a minor with a soldering iron and a warrant in two counties."

"One county, and it was expunged, and the drone was *technically* not over the courthouse." I turned back to the screen because I could feel the last folder sitting there at the bottom of the directory like a stone at the bottom of a clear pool, and I wanted to get it over with while Bo was in the tent, which—I'll admit it—I wanted a witness.

The folder was called SHUTDOWN, and it contained exactly three files.

* * *

File one was a memo. Internal, formal, addressed to the whole team, dated nineteen days before the end. It ordered the suspension of all deep-array seismic work pending "programmatic review," instructed that all primary data be consolidated onto physical media for hand-carry transport, and prohibited any further transmission of findings by radio, satellite uplink, or cellular network. No reason given. Signed with a clearance number instead of a name.

"They were told to stop looking," I said out loud, to hear it in a human voice. "Somebody above them read these same numbers and shut the whole thing down and told them to put it on drives and carry it out by hand."

"Carry it where?" Bo asked.

File two answered that. It was a route plan, drafted by E.M., timestamped nine days later. An extraction plan. Downriver, by raft, at night, avoiding the two established monitoring stations, to a rendezvous with—there was a redaction bar right across the contact name, applied so recently the digital ink was still wet, metaphorically speaking. Whoever sanitized this file had done it in a hurry, and had missed one thing, because below the redaction bar, in the document's revision history, the original text survived for exactly one keystroke:

*contact: R. Reyes*

I wrote the name on my wrist in pen because I didn't trust the camp, the drives, or the universe to hold onto it for me.

And file three was the last log entry. Audio plus transcript, recorded by the camp's ambient monitoring station, timestamped 03:12 on the final night. The transcript was auto-generated, clunky, the software doing its best with wind and distance. I played the audio anyway, headphones on, volume low, even though part of me—the smart part, the part that has kept me alive this long—said don't.

What you hear first is the river. Then boots on gravel, several sets, moving toward the water. Then voices, low, male and female mixed, too far gone for words except fragments the mic caught. And threaded underneath all of it, rising through the ground itself into the recording the way sound travels through a rail when you press your ear to it—a sound I've replayed maybe sixty times since and still can't describe without lying about it.

It wasn't a roar. Roars end. This sustained, low, vast, like the river had a second voice an octave below hearing, like something enormous drawing breath through stone. The instruments on the table registered it too—I checked afterward; every seismometer in camp lit up for ninety seconds and then flatlined to zero, all channels, simultaneously, at 03:14.

A deliberate shutdown. Not failure. Somebody issued a command. The final line in the system log, entered manually at 03:14:07, reads: *cease acquisition. secure media. proceed as planned.*

And then the audio records the boots reaching the waterline, and the river sound swallowing everything, and then—this is the part I keep coming back to—forty seconds of nothing. No footsteps leaving. No engines. Nothing. Just the low vast tone fading under the water noise until the mic's own power died at 03:15.

Eleven people walked into the Redwater at three in the morning carrying four data drives, and the river took the sound of them and gave nothing back.

I pulled the headphones off. My hands were shaking, which annoyed me, so I put them flat on the table until they stopped. Bo was watching me with the expression he saves for triage—the one that means he's already decided how bad it is and is now deciding whether to tell you.

"Teo," he said. "What did we just find?"

"We didn't find anything," I said. "We found what somebody else found. And then they found us a name." I held up my wrist. "R. Reyes."

Bo read it, and something crossed his face that I didn't understand yet, some old reflex, some memory of paperwork and badges. Later I'd learn why. Right then all I knew was that the sun had come around the tent wall and was lying warm and ordinary across the mess table, across the four gray drives, across the ace of spades still winning its frozen game outside, and that none of it felt warm or ordinary anymore. It felt like the quiet part of a storm chase—that greenish hush when the sky has loaded the gun and is deciding where to point it.

Outside, Junie's voice cut across the camp, sharp, stripped of everything casual: "Wendell. Boat. Now."

I grabbed the drives and ran.
