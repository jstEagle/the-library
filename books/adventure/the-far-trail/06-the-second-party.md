## 6. The Second Party

I've patched enough chest wounds to know the difference between a body that's sleeping and a body that's pretending. Sleeping people breathe with their whole ribcage. Pretenders breathe shallow, up top, like they're keeping the bottom of their lungs in reserve for something.

The camp had been breathing that way for three days when the second party arrived.

I noticed them before anyone else, because I'd spent the morning doing what I always do at a site like this—noticing things and hating what I notice. The survey tents still stood, guy-lines taut, which meant nobody had left in a hurry. The mess table still held plates with fork-scraped sauce gone to amber lacquer. A pair of boots sat outside one tent like their owner had just stepped into the river to rinse off. And the instruments—the big magnetometer rig on its tripod, the water samplers chained to a steel post—were still running, little green lights blinking away on battery banks that somebody, somewhere, was either maintaining or had forgotten about.

Nobody forgets about a battery bank for eleven weeks.

So when I heard the engine—a diesel, low gear, coming down the switchback above the camp slow and deliberate, the way you drive when you want to be heard but not seen—I wasn't surprised. I was relieved, almost. Uncanny places feel better with witnesses.

"Wendell," I said, low. He was crouched by the sampler post with Junie, both of them wearing the particular frown of people who've been arguing about data without saying they're arguing. "Company."

Junie's head came up like a heron's. She cocked an ear at the sound, did whatever math she does—she reads water the way I read triage, fast and unromantic—and said, "That's not a local truck. Local trucks don't coast the corners."

Teo appeared from behind the supply tent with his drone controller half-raised, already thumbing it awake, and Wendell put a hand out flat: *no*. Not yet. That's Wendell all over—he wants to see a thing with his own eyes before he lets the machines see it first. It'll get him killed someday, or save us, depending on the day.

We gathered by our own vehicles, four stray dogs arranged in a loose semicircle facing uphill, and watched the convoy come down.

Three vehicles. Two white Suburbans with government plates scrubbed to blank gray, and between them a flatbed carrying a generator, crates, and something long and tarp-wrapped that I chose, professionally, not to speculate about. Eight people climbed out. They moved well—too well, no wasted motion, no chatter—and they wore the uniform of people who don't wear uniforms: khaki, gray, sunglasses, holsters worn under light jackets in ninety-degree heat because comfort is for civilians.

The man who walked out front was the one my gut filed under *problem* before he said a word.

He was maybe fifty, silver at the temples, built like a man who'd been athletic once and had since converted all that muscle into pure administrative function. He carried a tablet against his forearm like a waiter carries a tray, and he didn't look at our faces when he reached us. He looked at our vehicles, then our gear, then the survey tents, cataloguing. When he finally raised his eyes, they were brown, dry, and patient as arithmetic.

"Morning," Wendell said, easy as Sunday. Wendell can do easy-as-Sunday at gunpoint; I've seen him nearly do it at actual gunpoint. "You folks lost?"

"No." The man's voice was quiet and level, a voice that had never needed volume. "Are you Bo Calloway?"

My spine went cold in a way I didn't show. "Do I know you?"

"You're listed as crew on the Meridian salvage contract. All of you are." He turned the tablet around. There we were—our names, our license numbers, a photo of Teo taken through a truck window that made my jaw tighten in ways I kept off my face. "Agent Reyes. Federal Emergency Survey Directorate." He pronounced the acronym like he was reading it off a form, which, in fairness, he was. "You're standing inside a closed federal exclusion zone, operating under a private contract that has no legal standing here. So. Before this becomes paperwork."

"Paperwork sounds nice," Wendell said. "I like paperwork. Very soothing."

Reyes looked at him for a full three seconds, and I watched something pass across his face that wasn't annoyance. It was closer to recognition—the look of a man identifying a species he's handled before. "Mr. Montrose. Yes. Your file is thicker than your crew's combined."

"Flattering."

"It isn't." Reyes turned the tablet back and swiped, unhurried. "Here's what happens now. You surrender any materials recovered from this site—data drives, documents, personal effects of the missing team—and you depart the basin by the southern route within six hours. In exchange, no citations, no impoundment, no further inquiry into how a man with your debt profile funded an expedition into a restricted watershed."

"And if we don't?" Junie asked. She hadn't moved from where she stood, arms folded, weight even on both feet. Junie doesn't posture. She just stands there like terrain.

"Then impoundment becomes seizure, citation becomes obstruction, and 'further inquiry' becomes considerably less pleasant for everyone involved." Reyes said it the way you'd read a medication dosage. No threat in it. Threat would have required him to care whether we believed him.

Wendell glanced at me. Just a flick. We've known each other long enough that a flick carries paragraphs, and the paragraph he sent me was: *you seeing this too?* And I sent back what I'd been chewing since Reyes said *Meridian*: that this man knew our contract terms. Knew our names off a list he shouldn't have had. Which meant either the Fund reported us—which, fine, creditors do that—or the Fund and the federal government were drinking from the same well.

And there was a third option underneath that one, cold as groundwater: that the Fund hadn't hired us to *salvage* anything at all. That we were a metal detector somebody else aimed at a buried secret, and now that we'd swung over the spot, the real owners had come to dig.

"I want to be clear about jurisdiction," Wendell said, and his voice had shifted into the register he uses when he's decided to enjoy himself, which is how I know he's scared. "Because I've been burned by jurisdiction before. Who exactly signed your exclusion order? Because I called three agencies last month trying to find out if this basin was closed, and every one of them told me the Redwater Survey was a state contract. State, Agent Reyes. Not yours."

Something ticked behind Reyes's eyes. Small. A ledger entry. "The scope of the survey changed."

"When?"

"That information isn't public."

"Convenient."

"It's accurate." He swiped the tablet again. "Six hours, Mr. Montrose. I'd take the offer. Men who don't take offers in this basin have a way of becoming part of its history." And then—the part I'll be turning over for a long time—he glanced past us, at the survey tents, and said, quietly, almost to himself: "Eleven weeks. And they still haven't found the third cache."

He didn't say it *to* us. But he said it loud enough for us, and I've been a paramedic long enough to know a man talking near a patient instead of to one. It was a test. Or bait. Or both.

They worked while we packed. That was the worst of it, honestly. Eight of them moving through the dead men's camp with latex gloves and evidence bags, photographing everything, lifting the boots, opening the tents, and doing it all with such gentle, methodical care that it felt less like an investigation than an autopsy performed by the murderer. One of them knelt at the sampler post and began disconnecting the battery bank, and I watched the green lights go out one by one, and something in my chest went out with each one. Eleven weeks those machines had kept watch over an empty camp. Now the watch was over, and the relief shift had arrived to erase the log.

Teo hovered at my elbow, drone case hugged to his chest, whispering through barely-moving lips. "Bo. Bo, the drives. The ones I pulled from the logger—they're still in my bag. Under the—"

"Don't say it."

"—under the—"

"*Don't.*"

He shut up, but his eyes were enormous, and I gave him the smallest nod I could produce. Under the trauma dressings in my medical kit, wrapped and taped where nobody searches, because nobody on earth searches a fat man's gauze. Some advantages of the profession.

Reyes reappeared as we loaded the last of it. He stood watching us with his tablet tucked away now, hands clasped, a man whose work was essentially done. "One more thing," he said. "A courtesy. Whatever the Fund promised you for this job—it won't be paid. You should understand that going forward. Contracts tied to this site have been voided at the source."

"At the source," Wendell repeated. Something moved through his face, quick, like weather. "Voided by whom?"

"By events." Reyes extended a hand, and after a beat Wendell shook it. Dry grip. Two pumps. "Six hours, gentlemen. Lady." His gaze touched Junie, then Teo, and lingered on Teo a half-second longer than professional—cataloguing, filing, weighing the value of a minor—and I stepped sideways, casually, into the sightline. Reyes noted that too. He notes everything. That's the whole horror of him: he's not cruel, he's *complete*.

Then they were packing up their own convoy, and we were pulling out southbound, and for eleven miles nobody in my truck said anything at all.

It was Junie who broke it, at the junction where the southern road climbs toward the basin rim. She had the map open on her knees, but she wasn't looking at it.

"The third cache," she said.

"Yeah," Wendell said.

"He said it like he wanted us to look for it."

"He said it like he thought we wouldn't dare," Bo—me, that is—said, and everyone politely ignored that I'd answered myself.

Wendell drummed the wheel. Behind us, dust hung over the basin where the Suburbans had gone, and ahead the road ran flat and empty toward a world where a debt waited and a contract had just been voided by *events*, and I could see him doing the math he always does, the math that keeps him up at night, except this time the numbers weren't money.

"Six hours," he said finally. "They gave us six hours to leave."

"They're expecting us to use them," Junie said.

"Yeah." Wendell looked in the mirror, then at each of us in turn, and I saw the exact moment the dread in him dressed itself back up as opportunity, same outfit, new occasion. "So let's disappoint them."

Teo, from the back seat, very quietly: "Which way's upstream?"

And that, friends, is how you know a family decision has been made—when the teenager asks the question everybody else was too polite to ask first.

* * *

We camped that night at a spring Junie knew, twelve miles north of the checkpoint road, hidden in a fold of rock she swore didn't appear on any map printed after 1974. While the others rigged tarps, I checked Teo's hands—they'd been shaking since the junction, though he'd have denied it under torture—and repacked my kit with the drives deep in the middle, wrapped in a foil blanket, because foil defeats casual RF sweeps and I'd rather be paranoid than robbed.

Wendell sat apart, radio earpiece in, listening to nothing, or to something. I brought him coffee and sat down without being invited, because that's the arrangement between us: I sit where I want, and he pretends to mind.

"You caught it too," he said.

"Caught what."

"What he said about the Fund. 'Contracts tied to this site have been voided at the source.'" Wendell turned his cup slowly. "Meridian lent against that contract. Corliss Ames doesn't void contracts; Ames *renegotiates* them. Unless someone above Ames told him to. Someone with initials instead of a name."

"So the government and your loan sharks are on the same dance card."

"Or the government owns the dance hall." He drank. Out past the firelight, the basin breathed its night noises—insects, wind, the far-off complaint of some bird with opinions. Somewhere north of us, up that black canyon, the Redwater was running toward its source, carrying whatever the instruments had seen, and two different packs of wolves had now marked the trail to it.

"Bo," he said, after a while, quieter. "If this goes bad—if it goes *really* bad—you and the others should peel off. There's no reason Teo should—"

"Stop." I said it gently, but I said it. "You're doing the thing. The lone-martyr accounting thing. I've seen your books, Wendell. Every entry's in red except the ones with us in them. Don't you dare start editing."

He looked at me a long moment. Then he laughed—one short, rough syllable, surprised out of him—and clinked his cup against mine.

Up on the ridge, Junie stood watch, a silhouette against the stars, and below her Teo slept with the drone controller in his arms like a kid with a stuffed animal. Eleven weeks, an empty camp, a buried secret, and now a federal agent who knew about a third cache and wanted us to run straight at it.

Sleep well, Agent Reyes, I thought. We're coming.

And God help me, part of me—the part that used to run toward wrecks for a living—was glad.
