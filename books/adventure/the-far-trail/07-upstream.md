## 7. Upstream

There are two kinds of people who read water for a living: the ones who do it from an office with a coffee mug that says WORLD'S OKAYEST HYDROLOGIST, and the ones who do it standing hip-deep in a river at midnight while a government helicopter thumps around a bend somewhere behind them, looking for their trucks.

I'm the second kind. I always have been. My mother said I'd end up in trouble or in science, and she was right twice.

We left the trucks at the survey camp, parked neat as churchgoers, because Reyes's people would be back for them and I wanted them to find exactly what they expected to find: three abandoned vehicles, a mess of gear we'd been too sloppy to pack out, and no sign of where we'd gone. Teo had spent the afternoon making our departure look like panic—scattered supplies, a dropped glove, one of Bo's terrible energy drink cans rolled into the brush like we'd fled in the night without planning. Which was almost true. We had planned it in about four hours, which by my standards is practically geological time.

The rafts were another matter. Wendell had bought them cheap off a guy in Delta who swore they'd never been punctured, which turned out to be technically accurate—they'd been *thoroughly* punctured and then patched, repeatedly, by someone whose idea of patching was duct tape and prayer. But they floated. Mostly. And at eleven forty on a moonless night, floating mostly was the best offer anyone had made me all week.

"Last chance," Wendell said, standing at the waterline with his hands jammed in his pockets. He does this thing where he offers you an exit he doesn't want you to take, so he can feel like a decent man when you refuse. "Reyes gave us until dawn to clear the basin. If we're caught upstream of the closure line, that's not trespassing anymore. That's federal."

"So is tax evasion," Bo said, hauling the cooler into the raft, "and yet here we all are."

"That's not—I've never evaded taxes."

"You've never *paid* them either," Teo said, not looking up from strapping the drone case down. "That's not the same as not doing it."

Wendell opened his mouth, closed it, and looked at me. Junie, his eyes said. Back me up.

"Water's dropping eight inches a day," I said instead. "Dry season started early this year. In ten days, maybe twelve, the upper canyon goes unnavigable. After that there's no running the river, no portaging the narrows with full gear, nothing. Whatever window exists, it's closing." I zipped my dry bag shut. "So the question isn't whether we should go. It's whether we can afford to still be standing here having the conversation."

He grinned—that crooked, tired grin of his, the one that means he got the answer he wanted and gets to pretend it wasn't his idea. "Cast off," he said. "Before I come to my senses."

Too late for that. None of us had been in possession of our senses since we found those instruments still recording in an empty camp. You don't walk away from a question like that. It follows you. It sits in the passenger seat and reads over your shoulder and asks, at three in the morning, *but what did they see?*

We pushed off into the dark.

* * *

Running a river at night is either madness or the purest thing you'll ever do, depending on how much sleep you've had. I'd had none, so I got purity.

Junie reads water the way some people read faces. Every river is talking constantly—it tells you what it's done and what it's about to do, if you know its grammar. A V of smooth water pointing downstream means the current's found the channel. A line of standing waves means rocks underneath, shallow and mean. Water that goes glassy and slow for no reason is deep, or hiding something, and you treat both possibilities with respect. The Redwater spoke a dialect I half knew—canyon rivers are all cousins—but it had an accent I couldn't place. Something older under the words. Something that made the hair on my arms stand up even before anything happened.

For the first hour it was easy. Wide water, gentle gradient, the canyon walls black shoulders on either side and a sky so crowded with stars it looked like a spill. Bo sat in the bow of my raft with a headlamp off and his paddle resting across his knees, feeling the current through the shaft the way a blind man reads braille. Teo rode with Wendell, which surprised me until I understood it: after the camp, after Reyes's voice saying *you have twenty-four hours* in that tone like a man reading a grocery list, the kid didn't want to be more than one boat-length from Wendell. Not because Wendell was brave. Because Wendell was scared too, and honest about it, and there's more comfort in shared fear than in borrowed courage.

"Left channel," I called back at the first real fork. "See the seam? Stay on the smooth side of it."

"I see a lot of dark," Wendell called back.

"The smooth dark. Trust me."

"I trust you," he said, and then, quieter, to Teo, but the river carried it: "That's the whole job, kid. Find the person who sees the smooth dark and follow them."

I pretended I hadn't heard. I'm not made of stone.

The second hour stopped being easy. The canyon narrowed the way canyons do—all at once, like a door closing—and the gradient picked up, and the river's grammar shifted from conversation to argument. I heard the drop before I saw it: that low, continuous rumble that every boater's spine recognizes before their brain does. I put my paddle blade in the water and held us against the current, and Wendell's raft swung in behind mine, and for a moment we just sat there in the eddy, listening.

"Portage or run?" Bo asked.

I swept my light across the drop. It was maybe three feet of vertical, ugly, into a churning hole with a recirculating backwash—the kind of hydraulic that takes a swimmer and keeps them, tumbling, until the river gets bored. At daylight, at full flow, I might have threaded the tongue on the far right. At night, with patched rafts and a crew that included a teenager and a paramedic whose medical license was suspended for reasons he refused to explain and I refused to ask about—

"We line it down," I said. "Ropes, boats light, walk them along the bank."

"That's going to take an hour," Wendell said.

"And running it wrong takes forever," I said. "Line it down."

Here is something nobody tells you about commitment: it doesn't arrive as a lightning bolt. It arrives as an hour of hauling wet rope along a rocky bank in the dark, your shoulders burning, mosquitoes finding the one spot on your ankle your waders don't cover, while a federal helicopter passes overhead twice and you crouch under a juniper holding your breath like a child playing hide-and-seek. Commitment is boring and cold and repetitive, and then at some point you look up and realize you passed the point of return so long ago that going back would now be harder than going forward, and that's when it stops being a decision and becomes a fact. Like gravity. Like debt.

Somewhere in that hour of lining the boats down, I stopped calculating whether we could still turn around. The math came out the same every time anyway: behind us, Reyes; ahead of us, whatever emptied a survey camp and left the instruments running. Between us and either option, ten days of shrinking water. There was only one direction with any miles left in it.

Upstream. Always upstream. Rivers only make sense in one direction, and so, apparently, did we.

* * *

We made camp at three in the morning on a gravel bar shaped like a crescent moon, tucked under an overhang that hid our firelight from the sky. Bo built the fire small and hot, the way he does everything—efficient, contained, slightly alarming. Teo was asleep before his sleeping bag finished unrolling, curled around the drone case like it was a stuffed animal, which in every way that mattered it was.

Wendell sat beside me at the fire with a tin cup of coffee that Bo had made strong enough to strip paint. For a while neither of us said anything. The river talked enough for both of us.

"You felt it too," he finally said. "The water. Tonight. Something's off."

I turned my cup in my hands. I'd been turning the observation over all night, looking for a rational explanation, and hadn't found one I liked better than the irrational ones.

"The flow data from the survey camp," I said. "I re-read it while you were loading. Gauge readings from six months of records. And the discharge numbers don't add up, Wendell. The Redwater gains volume between the camp and the confluence—way more than the tributaries account for. Hundreds of cubic feet per second appearing out of bare rock." I looked at him. "Rivers don't do that. Unless somebody's feeding them from somewhere that isn't on any map."

"The aquifer," he said. "The thing in the logs."

"Maybe. Or maybe the logs are wrong and the gauge is wrong and I'm wrong, and tomorrow we float into a dead-end box canyon and Reyes arrests us and it turns out we ruined our lives over a rounding error." I drank the paint-stripper coffee. "But I stood in that river tonight and I felt it moving under my feet. Not past me. *Under.* Like the whole riverbed was breathing."

Wendell nodded slowly, staring into the fire. "Corliss Ames wants the source data," he said. "Reyes wants us gone from the basin. Two different people, two different reasons, same patch of river." He rubbed his jaw. "When everybody powerful in a story wants the same rock, you don't walk away from the rock. You go look under it."

"When did you get wise?"

"About an hour after it stopped being optional." He smiled, tired. "Get some sleep, Junie. Long day tomorrow. Longer after that."

I didn't sleep much. I lay in my bag listening to the river, and near dawn I heard what I'd been half-hearing all night and refusing to name: beneath the surface sound, low and rhythmic, a pulse. Like a heartbeat in the current. Like something vast, somewhere under the canyon floor, keeping time.

At first light we broke camp fast and silent, and pushed back onto the water, and rounded the first bend into a stretch of canyon no map in my collection admitted existed—a slot so narrow the walls leaned together overhead, the river running green and glassy and impossibly deep, and carved thirty feet up the right-hand wall, fresh enough that the dust still hung in the grooves:

a survey marker. And beneath it, in spray paint, in a hand that had been shaking:

**IT ISN'T GEOLOGY.**

Bo read it upside down from the bow, and said, very quietly, "Well. Hell."

Teo raised the drone.

And downstream, faint but unmistakable, we heard the helicopter coming back.
