## 8. The Narrows

The river told us about the Narrows a full mile before we got there, the way rivers do if you're listening. Junie heard it first—she always hears it first—a low grinding under the water's voice, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

"Pull out," she said. "Scout it."

We dragged the rafts onto a gravel bar the color of old bone and climbed the bank through tamarisk until we could see downstream, and there it was: the canyon pinching down to maybe forty feet across, walls rising sheer and rust-red on both sides, and the whole river funneling into that gap like it had somewhere to be and no patience about it. The drop was maybe eight feet over two hundred yards of chaos. In whitewater terms that's not a rapid, that's an argument.

"That's a class four," Bo said. "Maybe five with this much water."

"It's a class four," Junie agreed. "Maybe five."

I looked at Teo, who was filming the whole thing on his phone with the particular calm of a seventeen-year-old who has not yet internalized his own mortality. "Opinions?"

"Cool rocks," he said.

Great. The kid's assessment was in. Cool rocks.

Here's the thing nobody tells you about running rivers for a living: the danger isn't really the water. Water is honest. Water tells you exactly what it's going to do to you and then does it, no subtext, no fine print. I'd spent three years dodging calls from the Meridian Fund because *people* lie, because contracts have footnotes, because Corliss Ames smiles while he ruins you. The river had never once smiled at me before taking my money.

But honesty doesn't keep you alive. Skill does. And the Narrows was going to demand all of ours.

"We portage the left side," Junie said, crouching over her map, tracing the line with one finger. "There's a shelf here—" she tapped a spot where the canyon wall stepped down in terraces— "if we can get the boats up and over. Carrying everything. It'll take hours."

"And if we run it?"

She looked up at me. Junie has these pale gray eyes that make you feel like weather being evaluated. "If we run it clean, we save six hours. If we flip, we lose the boats, half our gear, and possibly Teo." She didn't say *possibly anyone else* because we're all adults and adults are allowed to drown themselves; but the kid was another matter, and everyone on that gravel bar knew it without anyone saying it.

"I can run it," Teo said.

"You can run a class three in Oklahoma," Bo said. "This is different."

"This is why I'm here." He said it quietly, which was unlike him. Usually Teo filled silence the way water fills a hole. "You guys keep treating me like cargo. I'm crew."

Nobody answered right away. The river ground away below us, patient as a creditor.

I thought about my father, who measured his life in years and died with none of them wasted, and about myself, who measured mine in miles because miles don't ask what you've done with them. Somewhere back at that first camp, in the tents with the meals still half-eaten, we'd stopped being a salvage contract and started being something else. Something that would have to decide, sooner or later, whether it could survive actual danger or just the kind you drive toward.

"We scout it properly first," I said. "Then we vote. Everyone votes. Including cargo."

Teo almost smiled. Almost. On his face that was a parade.

* * *

The scouting took two hours and confirmed what Junie already knew: the left-side portage existed but was ugly, a scramble over slick rock with two hundred pounds of raft per trip, in heat that came off the canyon walls like an open oven door. The run itself had a line—there's always a line, the river leaves you one thread of mercy threading through the mess—but the entry was blind. You committed around a bend and then found out what you'd committed to.

"The hole at the bottom is the killer," Junie said, lying flat on the rimrock, looking down at the churning recirculation where the water dropped off the last ledge and folded back on itself. "Hit that sideways and the boat flips and you swim in that." She pointed at the eddy fence below, where foam tracked in slow circles. "That current will hold you. Recirculate you. People drown in holes like that after swimming for twenty minutes and never getting more than thirty feet from where they went in."

"So we don't hit it sideways," Teo said.

"No," Junie said. "So we don't hit it at all if we can help it. But plans are for people who get to choose."

We voted. Four hands for running it, zero for the portage. I want to say I weighed it carefully, that I did the math on risk versus time versus the dry season dropping the river out from under us, and maybe I did. But underneath the math there was something simpler: we'd come upstream to find out what happened to eleven people who walked into this basin and didn't walk out, and every hour we spent being careful was an hour for whoever else was looking for us to close the distance. Fear makes a bad navigator. So does hurry. You pick your poison and you paddle.

Bo checked Teo's PFD twice, then a third time, adjusting straps with those big careful paramedic hands, saying nothing, which from Bo was a speech. Then he clipped a throw rope to his own vest and said, "I'm behind the kid. Whatever happens, I'm behind him."

"You don't have to—"

"Shut up and row, Wendell."

We ran it in two boats. Junie took the gear raft solo because she's the best boat handler I've ever seen and she knew it and didn't waste breath pretending modesty. I took the second raft with Teo on oars and Bo riding the stern frame, rope coiled in his lap like a promise.

The entry was exactly as blind as promised. One moment we were in moving flatwater, green and deceptively gentle, and the next the canyon walls leaned in and the river stood up and started shouting.

I have run a lot of water. I want to be able to tell you I was calm, that my hands moved on the oars with professional detachment, that I read the drops and chose my lines like a man selecting items from a menu. What actually happened is that the next ninety seconds deleted every part of me except the animal. There was only water and the oars and Teo's voice calling the line ahead of us—he'd memorized it from the scout, this kid who couldn't remember to refill the water jugs but had the rapid burned into him like scripture—and the white noise and the red walls strobing past and the feeling, very deep down, of being a leaf in something that had been falling for ten thousand years and did not intend to stop for us.

We hit the third drop a little left of where we wanted. Not fatally wrong. Just wrong enough.

The raft swung. The current grabbed the bow and turned us, and for one long, glittering instant I saw the last ledge and the hole beyond it coming at us broadside, the way you see headlights, the way you see everything clearly in the final moment before clarity stops mattering.

"Hard right!" Teo screamed, and dug his oar in, and I dug mine in, and the raft fought us like a live thing, and we were losing—

—and then the world turned over.

Not us. The *gear raft*. Ahead of us, Junie's boat caught the ledge wrong—I'll replay that frame forever, the yellow hull standing up on edge, Junie thrown clear, her body cartwheeling into the foam below—and the raft went over and pinned against the rock at the bottom, wrapped, and Junie surfaced in the eddy fence, swimming hard, and wasn't winning.

And Teo, God help him, Teo stopped rowing to look.

Our bow swung again. We were going into the hole. I felt it take the stern, felt the whole boat start to rotate into that folding water, and I understood with total simplicity that in about four seconds I was going to be swimming in the drowning machine, and so was the kid.

Bo moved.

I don't have a better word for it. One second he was on the stern frame and the next he was past me, past Teo, hand fisted in the kid's PFD strap, and when our raft hit the hole and stood up on its side, Bo threw them both clear—threw them *upstream*, into the eddy behind the rock, the one safe pocket in the whole churning mess—while I went off the other side into water that closed over my head like a fist.

Here's what a hydraulic feels like from inside: it feels like being shaken by something that wants to know if you're serious. I tumbled. I came up. I went down. My vest did its job and my body did what bodies do badly, and somewhere in the middle of it I had the lucid, absurd thought that I owed the Meridian Fund forty thousand dollars and they would absolutely bill my estate for the equipment.

Then a rope snapped tight around my chest and the river let go of me all at once, grudgingly, the way it gives anything back, and I was dragging on gravel with Bo's arms under mine and Junie coughing beside me and Teo on his knees vomiting river water while Bo held him by the shoulders and talked to him in the flat, steady voice I'd heard him use once on a car wreck outside Amarillo—the voice that says *you're alive, stay that way, breathe*.

"Look at me. Look at me. You're out. You're done. Breathe."

Teo breathed.

* * *

We lost the gear raft. Wrapped and pinned below the ledge, hull cracked, and after two hours of rigging lines and hauling we admitted the river was keeping it. Half our food went with it, and the satellite modem, and most of the spare fuel. Junie sat on the rocks watching the water chew the wreckage and said nothing for a long time, which from Junie was louder than shouting.

"I should have taken the left channel wider," she finally said.

"You made the same call any of us would've."

"I know." She picked up a stone and skipped it, badly, out of character. "Knowing doesn't help."

Bo sat apart with Teo, not talking, just sitting, the way you sit with someone after their body has learned something it can't unlearn. Every few minutes Teo would look at the hole in the river and look away. Every few minutes Bo would put a hand on his shoulder, brief as punctuation.

When the light went amber on the canyon walls we made camp on a bench above the waterline, and it was Bo who found the cache.

He'd gone upslope to stretch his legs and came back down holding a metal ammunition box, army green, stenciled with a survey number that matched the ones from the first camp. Our salvage contract, delivered by accident. The universe has a sense of humor; it's just not a kind one.

Inside: a waterproof notebook, a data drive in a padded case, some medical supplies we needed worse than air, and a foil blanket. And loose in the box, folded small, a single page torn from a journal, dense handwriting gone blurry in places where water—or something else—had gotten to it.

Junie read it by headlamp. I watched her face change.

"What?"

She handed it to me without a word.

Most of it was coordinates, flow measurements, notes on tributaries—the dry litany of people doing their jobs. But near the bottom, the handwriting changed. Got bigger. Pressed harder into the paper, like the pen was being driven by something stronger than diligence.

*Day 41. Ran the sounding again because I assumed instrument error. It isn't error. The volume doesn't match the surface catchment—not close, not by an order of magnitude. There is more water coming out of this river than goes into it.*

*I ran the numbers four times. Went back up to the source myself. There's a cavern system under the headwaters, unmarked on every survey. The aquifer feeding it isn't on any state map either. It isn't geology. Or it is, but not any geology anyone filed. Something down there is moving, and it's been moving a long time, and the team wants to log it and go home and I think some of them want to log something else entirely.*

*If anything happens to us, this box is where we told each other the truth.*

I read it twice. Around me the fire crackled and Bo bandaged the scrape on Teo's arm and Junie re-rigged the remaining raft by headlamp, and none of them knew yet what I was holding.

*It isn't geology.* Four words, and the whole salvage job tilted under me like a boat hitting a ledge wrong. Eleven government surveyors hadn't wandered off and gotten lost. They'd found something worth erasing people over, and they'd known it might happen, and they'd cached the truth in boxes along the river like a man hiding letters from a fire.

"Wendell." Junie was looking at me. She'd stopped working. "What does it say?"

I looked at the page. At my crew—the strays, the family I hadn't planned on, the kid who'd almost drowned today because I voted to run a rapid to save time. Tomorrow Ames might call. Reyes might already be behind us. And whatever this paper was pointing at, we were now floating straight toward it with half our food and one boat.

I handed it to her. "Read it out loud," I said. "Everybody should hear this."

Because here's the thing I decided somewhere between the hole and the gravel bar, coughing up the Redwater with Bo's rope around my chest: I was done carrying things alone. Whatever was waiting upstream, we'd meet it the way we'd met the Narrows—together, on one raft, with everybody rowing.

Junie cleared her throat and began to read, and the canyon took her voice and gave it back to us doubled, like the walls themselves wanted to listen.
