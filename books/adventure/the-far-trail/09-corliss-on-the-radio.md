## 9. Corliss on the Radio

The satellite phone sat in the footwell of the truck like a coiled snake, and I'd been stepping around it for two days.

It had come with the contract, of course. Meridian Fund issue, matte black, pre-paid for a year, with a single number programmed into it under the contact name HOME. Corliss Ames had a sense of humor the way a pit viper has a sense of humor—you only appreciated it from a safe distance, and preferably never.

We'd made camp that evening in a bend of the river where the canyon walls leaned back just enough to let the sky in. Junie had declared it defensible, which was her way of saying beautiful: high bench above the water, cottonwoods for cover, one approach she could watch all night. Bo built a fire small enough to hide in a coffee can and cooked rice with dehydrated beans that tasted like regret and salt. Teo flew the drone until the light died, bringing back footage of the river running silver through red rock, and for ten minutes nobody owed anybody anything, and I let myself believe we were just people on a river.

Then the phone rang.

I knew before I touched it. There's a particular weight to a phone when the person calling owns a piece of your future, and I'd been carrying that weight so long I could feel it through the canvas of my pack. I picked it up out of the footwell and walked upriver along the bench, far enough that the firelight couldn't reach me, and answered on the fifth ring like a man who hadn't been waiting for it.

"Montrose." Ames's voice came through clear as a hymn, warm and unhurried, the voice of a man who had never once needed to raise it because everyone around him did the raising for him. "You're hard to reach."

"I've been driving."

"Through some of the prettiest country in America, I hear. The Redwater Basin. Closed to the public since spring, but then, you were always more comfortable where you weren't supposed to be." A pause, comfortable on his end, less so on mine. "How's the salvage going?"

"Slow. The camp's a mess. Gear's scattered. Whoever left didn't leave in any hurry I can find."

"Mm," he said, which was not agreement and not disagreement, just a sound he kept in his pocket for moments exactly like this one. "Wendell. Can I be honest with you?"

"You're going to be anyway."

That got a laugh out of him, brief and genuine, which was somehow worse than if he'd gotten angry. "I am. Because I like you. That's not a thing I say often, and it's not sentiment—it's an assessment. You're a man who finishes what he starts. In my line of work, that's rarer than talent. Talent's everywhere. Finish is what costs."

The river talked to itself below me, patient, indifferent. Somewhere behind me Bo laughed at something Teo said, and the sound carried up the bench and broke against the dark like a wave.

"The survey team," Ames said, "carried data drives. Ruggedized, government spec, serial numbers logged. Their instruments recorded everything they saw down there—every reading, every sample, every measurement. That's the salvage. Not tents, not trucks, not their fancy coffee maker. The drives. Everything else is decoration."

"We found the camp," I said carefully. "No drives."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "Because they took them upstream. Which means your job isn't over, Wendell. It's barely started."

I looked downriver, back the way we'd come, toward the abandoned camp with its half-eaten meals and its instruments still humming to themselves in the empty dark. Toward Reyes and his men, who'd told us to leave with the flat courtesy of people who clean up after other people's accidents. And I looked upriver, into the black throat of the canyon where the water came from, and felt the two directions pull against each other inside my chest like a current trying to tear a boat in half.

"They went up past the Narrows," I said. "Into closed country. If they took the drives, they went deep."

"They did. And here's the honest part I promised you." His voice didn't change at all—that was the terrible thing about Corliss Ames, the way bad news arrived in his mouth dressed exactly like good news, same warmth, same patience, same smile you could hear. "The Fund has extended itself considerably on your behalf. Equipment, insurance riders, the advance, the interest accruing on all of it. Your balance, as of this morning, stands at a figure I won't insult us both by naming twice. Recover the drives and deliver them to a location I'll provide, and we square it. All of it. Every dollar, every day of interest, gone. You walk away owing nothing to anyone. You know how long it's been since you could say that sentence about yourself."

"And if I don't?"

The pause this time was longer, and warmer, and I understood that the warmth was the message.

"Then the debt doesn't go away, Wendell. It grows. And—" and here his voice softened, the way a man softens when he's about to tell you your dog died, "—the Fund structures its obligations individually. You signed personally. But the crew—the navigator, the medic, the boy—they've been working under your contract. Under your authority. In the event of default, the Fund pursues recovery from all parties who benefited from the advance. It's standard. It's boring. It's paperwork. But it means that if you turn around tomorrow and paddle home empty-handed, you won't be the only one carrying the balance. They will too. Every one of them. For years."

I said nothing. There was nothing to say that wasn't a confession.

"Wendell." Gentle now. Almost kind. "I'm telling you this because I want you to make a free choice. That's all I've ever wanted for you. The drives are sitting somewhere up that canyon, and three states' worth of trouble is sitting on top of them, and none of that is your problem. Your problem is arithmetic. Bring me the drives, and the arithmetic ends. Keep your crew together, keep them moving, keep them focused on the job—and everybody gets to go home whole. Doesn't that sound better than the alternative?"

It did sound better. That was the devilry of it. It sounded like a door opening in a wall I'd spent years pressing my face against, and the fact that the door opened onto something I was increasingly afraid to look at—that was a detail Corliss Ames trusted me to notice and expected me to ignore.

"When do you need an answer?" I asked.

"Oh, no answer needed. You already gave it when you signed." A rustle of paper on his end, unhurried, a man straightening his desk. "I'll call again in four days. Same time. Have good news ready, Wendell. I do hate delivering the other kind."

He hung up without goodbye. He always did. Goodbyes implied the conversation might end, and Corliss liked to leave lines open, doors ajar, debts breathing.

* * *

I stood by the river for a long while after the screen went dark, holding the phone like it might still be listening. It probably was. I turned it off anyway, then thought better of it and turned it back on—if it went silent, someone would come looking for why.

Four days. I had four days to decide whether I was a salvage contractor or something else, and the problem was that both of those men wanted the same thing for entirely different reasons, and only one of them knew it yet.

The fire had burned low when I came back down the bench. Teo was asleep in his bag with the drone controller still zipped inside the outer pocket, one hand resting on it like a kid with a teddy bear. Bo sat up nursing the last of the coffee, and Junie stood at the edge of the overlook with her arms crossed, reading the water in the dark the way other people read letters from home.

She didn't turn around when I stopped beside her. She never had to.

"That was him," she said. Not a question. "Ames."

"How'd you know?"

"Because you walked away from the fire to take it, and you've come back wearing the face you wear when you're doing math instead of living." She finally looked at me. Starlight caught the river in her eyes, or maybe the other way around. "What did he want?"

"The usual. Reassurance. Encouragement. A reminder of deadlines."

"What kind of deadlines?"

"Contractual ones."

Junie studied me for a long moment, and I watched her decide—not whether I was lying, because she already knew that, but whether to make me say it out loud tonight or let me carry it another day. That was the thing about Junie. She read water like text and people like weather, and she knew exactly when a storm was coming and exactly how much warning a man could stand.

"Okay," she said finally. "Contractual ones." And she turned back to the river, and let me have it.

That was worse than an argument. An argument would have given me somewhere to put it.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat up by the dead fire and ran the ledger in my head, because that's what I'd always done—when the numbers got ugly, I got quiet, and I got quiet alone, because sharing a debt felt like handing someone else a shovel at my own funeral.

Here's what the ledger said. Option one: bring Ames his drives. Debt erased, crew freed, and whatever the survey team found—whatever made a government fixer named Reyes look at me like I was already a stain to be mopped up—gets handed to the people who sent Reyes to bury it. Option two: don't. Keep the drives, or hunt them, or lose them, and watch the Fund come after Bo's suspended license and Teo's custody case and Junie's nothing-to-her-name with the cheerful, patient ruthlessness of people who genuinely enjoy their work.

There was supposed to be an option three. There's always supposed to be an option three. But options three cost things, and I'd spent my whole life paying retail for courage and buying wholesale excuses, and I knew myself well enough to know which aisle I shopped in.

Except.

Except Bo had pulled a teenage boy out of whitewater without hesitating, with a suspended license and everything to lose. Except Junie had led us into closed country at night because the map said the truth was upstream and she trusted the map more than the law. Except Teo had voted with his whole body to be here, fleeing one broken home toward a raft full of strangers who'd become the opposite of strangers.

They weren't employees. I'd been keeping the books like they were, line items and liability, and the whole time they'd been the only family I had, and families don't sign personally. Families sink together or swim together, and the worst thing I could do to mine was decide for them which one it would be while they slept.

But telling them meant watching the fear land on their faces. Telling them meant admitting that the door I'd led them through had a price tag on the far side. And old habits are older than good intentions—I know that now, though I didn't know it yet that night. So I did what I've always done. I folded the phone call up small, put it in the pocket where I kept everything heavy, and told myself I was protecting them.

The lie fit so well I almost couldn't feel the seams.

Near dawn, Junie woke without waking—she did that, surfacing just enough to check the world—and her eyes found me across the cold fire ring. She looked at me for a long moment, at the phone in my hand, at the math still running behind my face.

"Wendell," she said quietly, so as not to wake the others. "Whatever he said. It's going to touch all of us. It always does."

I started to answer. Out on the water, upstream where the current bent around the first riffle of the morning, something caught the early light—a glint, orange and wrong, snagged on a rock at the river's edge. Something man-made, washed down from above.

Something that hadn't been there yesterday.
