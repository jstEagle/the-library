## 10. Dry Season

The river died on a Tuesday.

That's not poetic license. I know because Bo had been counting days out loud since the Narrows—"Day nine, still alive"—in this cheerful, medic-y way that was supposed to be comforting and mostly wasn't. Day nine was the Tuesday we woke up to find the Redwater had shrunk overnight from something you could float a raft down to something you could spit across.

I'm sixteen, so I'm contractually obligated to notice patterns adults miss, and the pattern here was bad. Rivers don't just get smaller. Rivers are like bank accounts that way. When they start shrinking fast, somebody upstream is either taking money out or the whole system's about to eat itself.

"Headwaters," Junie said, standing ankle-deep in what used to be the channel, reading the water like she always did—except now there wasn't enough of it left to read. "Or what's left of them. We're losing the snowmelt pulse. Dry season's early."

"How early?" Wendell asked.

Junie looked at him for a long second. "Early enough."

Nobody said anything after that, which is how you know things are really bad on a crew like ours. We're not quiet people. Bo hums when he's nervous. Junie mutters coordinates under her breath like prayers. I talk to my drones—I named them, which Bo says is a red flag, but Bo also names his trauma shears, so he doesn't get an opinion. Even Wendell talks constantly when things are fine, this running commentary about truck engines and cloud formations and the exact wrongness of gas station hot dogs.

But that morning: silence. Four people staring at a dying river like it owed them money.

Which, honestly, it kind of did. The water was our road. Without it, we were just four idiots with three hundred pounds of gear and a growing list of reasons why walking out of the Redwater Basin might be the last thing any of us ever did.

"We portage," Wendell said finally, and clapped his hands once, like the sound could make it true. "Boots on the ground. We follow the channel up on foot. It's maybe forty miles to where the canyon forks."

"Forty miles through closed basin," Junie said, "carrying everything, in heat that's going to hit a hundred and ten by Thursday, with two parties of people behind us who want us dead or gone."

"When you say it like that it sounds bad," Bo said.

"It *is* bad."

"Yeah." Bo shrugged and started breaking down his raft. "But have you met us? Bad is sort of our brand."

I wanted to laugh. I didn't, because my throat had closed up somewhere around *a hundred and ten*, but I wanted to, and I think that's what saved us over the next six days—not laughing would've killed us faster than the heat.

* * *

Here's what portaging actually is, versus what it sounds like: it's not carrying a boat. It's carrying a boat, plus your food, plus your water, plus everyone else's share when they can't, plus the growing suspicion that the thing you're carrying toward is worse than the thing you're carrying away from.

We rigged harnesses out of throw ropes and cam straps. Bo carried the medical kit and half a raft on his back and never once complained, which I learned was his way of complaining—loud cheerfulness about everything except the actual weight. Junie navigated off the channel itself, following the dry wound of it up through the canyon country, reading gravel bars and flood debris like a book with most of its pages torn out. Wendell took the heaviest single load, the gear bags with the survey team's data drives sealed inside, and he carried them like they were made of glass and guilt, which, looking back, they were.

And I flew.

The drones were my job, and for the first few days it felt almost good—scouting ahead, finding the slots where the canyon walls pinched in and we'd have to climb, mapping the shade patches like oases. Teo the teenage drone pilot, useful at last. My mom would've said I was finally applying myself. My mom also filed eleven police reports about me in one year, so her opinions on application were not high on my list of things to care about.

By day three the heat stopped being weather and started being a roommate. A bad one. The kind that takes up all the space and eats your food.

Because that was the other thing. Supplies.

We'd planned for river miles, easy miles, where the current does the work and you eat real food at camp. We had not planned for hauling everything through a furnace. The food math went wrong fast. By day four we were splitting freeze-dried meals into thirds and calling it "portioning strategy." By day five Bo caught me licking peanut butter off the foil lid when I thought nobody was watching, and instead of making it a joke—which is what he usually did, catch someone at their worst moment and turn it into something survivable—he just looked away, and that scared me more than Reyes's helicopter ever had.

Tempers went next. Not all at once. In pieces.

Junie got short, snapping at me when I flew too high and burned battery. Wendell got quiet, which for Wendell was like the sun getting dim—the whole crew runs on his noise, and without it we were all just stumbling around in the dark bumping into each other. Bo stayed cheerful, but it went brittle, like ice over moving water, and once, when I tripped on a scree slope and nearly took the med kit down with me, he grabbed my arm hard enough to bruise and said, "*Careful,*" in a voice I'd never heard him use, and then apologized for an hour straight.

We were coming apart. Slowly, politely, in thirds of meals and unshared secrets, we were coming apart.

* * *

It happened on day five, in the worst hour of the afternoon, when even the shadows were hiding from the sun.

We'd stopped in the lee of a rockfall—a shelf of shade barely big enough for the four of us, and I was lying flat on my back watching the heat shimmer make the canyon walls swim, half asleep, drone controller loose in my hand. That's how I heard it. Not the words. The tone.

Wendell had walked off around the bend of the rockfall with the satphone, and I wasn't trying to eavesdrop, I swear—but sound carries weird in canyon country, bouncing off stone, and his voice came back to us flattened and strange, like it was coming from underwater.

"—doesn't matter what I told them, Corliss, it matters what I do—no. No, listen to me—"

A pause. Then, lower, and this part I'll hear in my sleep:

"If it doubles, it doubles. Leave them out of it. They're not party to anything. *Leave them out.*"

Another pause. Then, quieter than I'd ever heard him: "Yeah. Yeah, I know what you're holding. I know exactly what you're holding."

He came back around the rockfall with his face arranged into something normal, and I shut my eyes and pretended to be asleep, and I lay there with my heart going like a rotor while the heat pressed down and I thought: *he didn't tell us.*

He knew something. Something with a name—Corliss—and something with a price, and he'd known it since before the Narrows, maybe since the beginning, and every mile we'd hauled since then, every third-of-a-meal, every blister and snapped temper, had been walked by four people who thought they knew the plan when only three of them did.

I should have told Junie right away. That's what I tell myself now. But I was sixteen and scared and I'd spent my whole life learning that telling adults the truth gets you sent somewhere worse, so instead I did the coward's math: I waited, and I watched, and it turned out I wasn't the only one who'd heard.

Junie had been on watch.

* * *

She waited until dusk, which was tactically smart and emotionally terrifying. Camp was set in the mouth of a side slot, a sliver of shadow between walls, the dry riverbed glittering faintly below with mica. Bo was boiling the last of the real coffee over a whisper of a stove flame—rationing even the smell of it—and Wendell was checking the data drives for the fourth time that day, which was already twice too many times.

Junie stood up. She didn't raise her voice. She never had to.

"Wendell. Who's Corliss?"

The spoon Bo was holding stopped halfway to the pot. I kept my eyes on my drone batteries like they were the most interesting thing in the world, which, given that they were at nineteen percent, was saying something.

Wendell didn't even try to play dumb. I'll give him that. He set the drives down very carefully, the way you put down something that might go off.

"Where'd you hear that name?"

"The phone call. Yesterday. Sound carries in the canyon, Wendell. It carries *up*." She stepped closer, and in the stove light her face had gone flat and cold in a way I'd only seen once before, back at the survey camp, when she'd read the empty footprints. "So let's try again. Who is Corliss, why were you telling him to leave *us* out of it, and what exactly is he holding over you?"

"Teo." Wendell looked at me, and there was something in his face—not anger, worse, something like pleading. "You too?"

"Heard the whole thing," I said. My voice came out smaller than I wanted. "Sorry."

"Don't apologize." He rubbed both hands over his face. Behind him, Bo set the spoon down and crossed his arms, and the cheerful mask was gone entirely, and underneath it Bo looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with the portage. "Okay," Wendell said. "Okay. You want it? Here it is."

He told us. All of it, in the flat voice of a man reading his own autopsy: the Meridian Fund, the debt, the numbers that had been chasing him longer than we'd been chasing the river. Ames. Corliss Ames, the smiling enforcer, the man who'd handed him the salvage contract dressed up as opportunity. And then the new part, the part from the call: recover the source data or the debt doubles, and if the debt doubles—

"Crew liability," Junie said quietly. "He said *crew liability*. That means us. Our names are on something?"

"They're not. I wouldn't sign—"

"You signed *something*," Junie said. "With these people. For all of us. And you didn't tell us."

"I was going to."

"*When?*" Her voice cracked on it, and that crack was worse than shouting, because Junie's voice cracking meant the thing underneath had been there a long time. "When the canyon fork? When the source? When one of us is bleeding and you finally do the math out loud? Wendell, we have been eating thirds. We have been carrying your secret along with your gear bags, and we didn't even know we were carrying it, and you *let* us—"

"I know."

"—because that's what you do! You carry it alone until it crushes you and then you act surprised when it does!" She was shaking now, and so, I realized, was I, sitting there with my dead batteries, watching the family come apart at the seams in real time. "We are not your debt, Wendell. We're not line items. We're the people who followed you into a *closed basin* because we trusted you, and you let us walk blind."

The silence afterward was total. Even the river, which wasn't a river anymore, seemed to hold its breath. Down in the mica glitter, a pebble ticked loose somewhere and rolled a long way.

Bo moved first. He always did. He crouched by the stove and poured the coffee—one cup, split four ways, into a tin mug that got passed around without anyone speaking—and when it came to me it was mostly grounds and steam, and I drank it anyway, and it tasted like burnt honesty.

"I'm not mad that there's trouble," Junie said finally, softer, to the fire more than to him. "There's always trouble. I'm mad that you decided alone that we couldn't handle it. That's the part that hurts. That's the part that makes us strangers."

Wendell sat with that. A long time. His jaw worked like he was chewing on something too big to swallow, and I watched him do the thing I'd watched him do a hundred times—the old pattern, the solitary burden, the man building a wall out of his own silence—and then, for the first time since I'd known him, I watched him stop doing it.

"Say it plain, then," he said. "What happens tomorrow."

"Tomorrow," Junie said, "you tell us everything, as it comes in. Every call, every threat, every number. No more editing. We vote on what we do with it—all of us, equal votes, including the kid." She glanced at me. "Especially the kid. Sixteen counts. He's carried more of this than anybody knows."

My throat did something embarrassing. I nodded so it would look like I was being tough about it.

"And after that?" Wendell asked.

"After that," Junie said, "we keep walking. Because whatever Ames is holding over you, Wendell—it's nothing compared to what Reyes is holding over all of us. And because the truth about what's up this river is worth more than any ledger he's got. But hear me on this." She leaned forward, stove light catching her eyes. "If there's a door out of this that saves you and spends us—you don't take it. You hear me? Whatever deal shows up, however good it looks, however much it promises—you don't take it alone."

Wendell looked at each of us, one by one. Bo, arms crossed, nodding slow. Me, clutching my dead-drone tin cup. Junie, waiting.

"No deals alone," he said. "All of us or none."

"All of us or none," Bo echoed, and held out his fist, and after a second Wendell bumped it, and then me, and then Junie, who did it like she was sealing a treaty, which I guess she was.

We slept badly, packed earlier, and started walking in the dark to beat the heat. And it was maybe two hours into that gray first light, with the canyon walls going from black to rust above us, that I launched the one drone with any charge left for a morning scout and saw, on the little cracked screen, far up the channel where the canyon forked—

Tires. Vehicle tracks, fresh, cutting across the dry riverbed.

Somebody had reached the fork before us.
