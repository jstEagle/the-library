## 11. The Surveyor's Grave

I've patched a lot of holes in people over the years. Gunshot, chainsaw, farm equipment that should never have been operated by anyone with fewer than six arms. You learn things about bodies doing that work—things you can't unlearn. You learn that a body tells the truth even when the mouth doesn't. Wounds don't lie about what made them.

So when we found the cairn on the third morning above the Narrows, I already knew before Junie knelt and lifted the first stone that the man underneath hadn't drowned, hadn't fallen, hadn't died of heat or thirst or any of the polite ways a canyon kills you.

The cairn itself told me first. Nobody builds a cairn like that in grief alone. It was careful work—flat stones fitted in courses, sized by hand, stacked over a grave dug into ground so hard it must have taken two people half a day with pick and shovel they couldn't spare the weight for. Somebody had loved this man enough to spend calories they were rationing to stay alive themselves. That's not panic. That's mourning with a budget.

"Bo," Teo said behind me, quiet in the way he gets when his brain is running faster than his mouth. "You seeing this?"

"Seeing it."

Junie had the top stones off in under a minute. She's efficient that way—reads everything like text, including graves—and I watched her hands slow down as she got deeper, the way mine would've, because some things deserve slowness.

He was wrapped in a survey tarp, tied at head and feet with paracord knots I recognized from rescue work. Underneath, he'd been dressed for the field, boots still on, which is how you know the people who buried him respected him as a professional and not just as a corpse. He'd been dead a long time. The dry air had done what dry air does—leathered him instead of rotting him—and what was left of him was light as kindling when I finally reached in to do the part of the job I'd been dreading.

Because somebody had to look at the wound.

It was in his back. High, left of center, angled downward and forward. One entry, no exit that I could find without turning him, and I wasn't going to turn him. Some dignity you leave where it lies. But one entry wound, high in the back of a man buried face-up with his arms arranged at his sides—that's not an accident. Accidents come from the front. Accidents happen between people looking at each other. This came from behind, from someone he trusted enough not to watch.

"He was shot," I said. My voice came out flatter than I meant it to. Flat is what I do instead of feeling things all the way through; twenty years in EMS teaches you to flatten, and then you retire and discover the flattening is permanent. "Close range. From behind."

Nobody said anything for a while. The river talked instead, low and constant over its rocks, the way water does when it has nothing better to offer.

Wendell stood at the edge of the grave with his hat in his hands. He does that—removes the hat like he's entering a church—even though I'm fairly sure Wendell hasn't been inside a church since a wedding in 2011 that ended in a fistfight. But the instinct's real. Somewhere under all that debt and swagger is a man who knows when to be bareheaded.

"His own team," Teo said. Not a question. He'd done the same math I had. "One of them shot him?"

"Or whoever found them did." Junie sat back on her heels, dust on her knees. "But if outsiders killed him, why bury him? Outsiders don't bury people they shoot. Outsiders walk away."

She was right, and being right made it worse. Because the only explanation that fit the evidence—the care of the burial, the angle of the wound, the months these people had been missing—was that his own teammates had put him in the ground. And that meant either they'd turned on each other out here in the dark, or—

Or he'd been executed, and they'd buried him anyway, because whatever else happened, he was theirs.

"We should keep going," Wendell said. His voice had that forced-lightness thing it does when he's managing a room. "Whatever happened here, it happened months ago. We're burning daylight."

"Nobody's moving until we finish," Junie said.

"Finish what?"

"Finishing." She nodded at the cairn stones, stacked neat and waiting. "We took him apart. We put him back together. That's the deal."

And here's the thing about our crew: Wendell signs the contracts, Junie reads the water, Teo flies the drone, and I carry the medical bag. But when Junie says something in that level voice of hers, we all do it, including Wendell, especially Wendell, because Wendell knows—as well as any of us—that she's the closest thing we have to a conscience that isn't hungover.

So we rebuilt the cairn. All four of us. Teo carried stones from thirty yards out so he wouldn't have to look at the grave directly, which I let him do because there's no honor in forcing a seventeen-year-old to stare at a murder victim, and also because carrying stones gave his hands something to do besides shake. Wendell set the cap piece himself and pressed it down like he was sealing something.

It was Teo who found the notebook.

* * *

It was tucked in a waterproof pouch wedged under the capstone, placed where only someone dismantling the grave would ever find it—which meant it was meant for exactly us, for whoever came after with enough respect to open the grave and enough need to go through it. The surveyors had known someone would come. They'd left a message the way you leave messages in war: addressed to whoever's listening, trusting nothing else.

Teo brought it to the fire that night, and we read it by headlamp, taking turns holding pages because nobody wanted to hold them too long.

The man's name was Daniel Okafor. Field hydrologist, according to the flyleaf, along with a phone number for a wife in Tucson that Teo copied down with shaking hands and put somewhere safe without being asked. The entries started ordinary—readings, coordinates, complaints about freeze-dried eggs—but the last forty pages were a different animal entirely.

Okafor had found water.

Not river water. Something older. An aquifer, unregistered, unmapped, sitting under the basin like a held breath—a body of fresh water the size, by his estimates, of Lake Erie, feeding springs across three states, feeding the Redwater itself. Water rights in the West are worth more than gold and twice as bloody, and Okafor had understood immediately what he was standing on top of. His early notes are almost giddy. I remember thinking, reading them, that this was a man watching his career make itself.

Then the entries turned.

There was a meeting described in careful, furious detail. A man from a development consortium—he never names it fully, but he references "the Meridian people" more than once, and when Teo read that line aloud, Wendell went very still beside me, stiller than I've ever seen him, stiller than the time the tornado dropped a grain silo forty feet from his truck—had offered the team a choice. Falsify the findings. Reclassify the aquifer as contaminated, non-viable, geologically isolated. Sign the suppression report. And in exchange, positions, money, silence.

Okafor wrote: *They aren't asking us to hide water. They're asking us to help steal it from three states and sell it back to them.*

Somebody on the team said yes. That was in the notebook too, written in a hand that shook so hard the ink feathered. Okafor didn't name them outright. He just called them "the signature," and after that entry the camp split into two groups, and the group that stayed honest kept moving upstream toward the source to gather proof that couldn't be falsified, and the group that signed stayed behind.

Daniel Okafor was shot in the back three days later.

The final entry was dated the day before he died. It's short. I've read it maybe fifty times since, and it hasn't gotten easier:

*If you're reading this, they sent you. I'm sorry. Whoever comes up this river next will be coming for the same reason we did—for money, for orders, for a way out of a hole. All I can tell you is that the hole doesn't fill. I sold ten years of my life to creditors to get here and I'd give them all again to be the man who walks away from this clean. Don't let them buy your signature. It's the only thing you own.*

Wendell closed the notebook very carefully, like it might bruise.

Nobody looked at him. Everybody looked at him.

Because here's what we all knew, and what none of us said: Wendell Montrose owed the Meridian Fund more money than God. Wendell Montrose was on this river because the Fund put him here. And every mile we'd gained upstream was a mile bought and paid for by the same people who'd bought Daniel Okafor's teammate's signature, and possibly his trigger finger too.

"I'll take first watch," Wendell said, and walked out of the firelight.

* * *

I found him an hour later sitting on a rock above the water, hat off again, turning it in his hands like a man shuffling a deck he already knew was marked.

In my old life I would have given him space. Space is what you give people in triage when they're stable—you move on to the ones bleeding. But Bo Reyes retired from triage, and besides, this crew isn't patients. It's the first group of people I've chosen, and I've learned that chosen people get honesty whether they want it or not.

"You read the entry," I said. Sitting down next to him. Rock was cold. Everything out here is cold once the sun quits.

"Which part."

"The part where the dead man describes you."

That got a huff out of him. Almost a laugh. "Yeah. That part."

"You want to talk about it?"

"Nope."

"Good. Then listen instead." I pulled my knees up. My knees complain these days; they earned it. "That man spent his last week alive knowing he was probably going to die, and what he chose to do with the time was write instructions for strangers. Not goodbye letters. Instructions. He wasn't grieving himself, Wendell. He was trying to arm us."

"He armed us into a gunfight."

"He armed us into a choice. Different animal." I nodded back toward the fire, where Teo had fallen asleep sitting up with the notebook's coordinates sheet still in his lap, and Junie sat awake beside him, keeping her own watch hours early, because Junie guards people the way other folks guard property. "You saw what they did to him for refusing. You saw what they did to his friends for staying honest. And tomorrow we keep walking upstream toward the exact same thing, following the exact same trail, and everybody on this crew knows it, and nobody's packing their bag to leave. You want to sit with why that is?"

"They haven't seen the ledger," Wendell said quietly. "They don't know what I owe. What happens to them if I default. Ames made it clear the liability spreads."

"So tell them."

"It's not their debt."

"It stopped being just yours the day you signed them onto a salvage contract with your name on it and the Fund's fingerprints on it. That's not me judging you. That's arithmetic." I looked out at the river, black and murmuring, running cold out of whatever cathedral Okafor died trying to document. "You've been carrying this thing solo since Corliss Ames smiled at you in that office, and I've watched you do it, and I've said nothing, because it's your load. But Okafor's dead because a good man tried to carry his piece of this alone too. Solitude isn't nobility, Wendell. Out here it's just a slower way of getting shot in the back."

He was quiet a long time. Long enough that I thought he'd do what he always does, which is absorb, deflect, joke, survive. That's his whole operating system. Miles, not years. Keep moving, don't count, don't feel, don't owe anything you can't outrun.

But then he said, "Bo. If it comes down to it—if Ames gives me a door that's just big enough for me—"

"Don't ask me what to do." I stood up. My knees filed their usual complaint. "Ask yourself what Okafor would say. You've got his handwriting in your pocket. Man left you his answer in advance."

I walked back down to the fire. Behind me Wendell stayed on his rock a while longer, and I didn't watch him, because some conversations a man needs to have alone, and the important ones are almost always with the dead.

* * *

We broke camp at gray light, and I noticed three things inside the first hour.

First: Junie had taken the notebook overnight and copied every coordinate onto her charts in her small, precise hand, so that the information now lived in two places and could not be lost with any one person. She handed it back to Wendell without comment. She'd heard everything. Junie hears everything. I've stopped being surprised.

Second: Teo had rigged the drone for a dawn flight and came back with thermal imagery showing nothing moving within five miles except bighorn sheep and one very confused coyote. He reported this with the gravity of a general presenting theater intelligence, and we thanked him accordingly, because you thank Teo properly or he sulks, and a sulking drone pilot is a tactical liability.

Third: Wendell had changed. Not dramatically—I doubt anyone but me and Junie would catch it—but the performative lightness was gone from him. He gave orders shorter and quieter. When we passed the cairn on the trail out, he didn't take his hat off again; he touched the brim instead, quick, like a salute between professionals, one field man to another.

And around midday, when we crossed a bench of slickrock where the canyon opened wide enough to see twenty miles upriver—all of it empty, red, silent, waiting—Wendell stopped and looked at it for a long moment and said, mostly to himself but loud enough for the rest of us, which was deliberate, everything Wendell does is deliberate:

"Whoever's up there has been hiding for months. They'll be scared. Armed, maybe. Half-starved." He settled his hat. "Let's try to look like the friendly kind of strangers."

"The friendly kind," Junie repeated, deadpan. "With the guns."

"With the guns," Wendell agreed. "Friendly's relative out here."

We laughed, a little, because you laugh when you can, and then we shouldered packs and kept climbing up the far trail toward the source of the Redwater, four strays and a dead man's notebook, carrying water news worth killing for into country built for burying things.

By late afternoon the wind had turned, and Junie—who reads air the way she reads water—stopped on the trail, lifted her chin, and said, "Smoke. Faint. Upstream."

Somebody was alive up there. Somebody had a fire.

And we had no way of knowing yet whether we'd just found the survivors—or walked into the second half of an execution.
