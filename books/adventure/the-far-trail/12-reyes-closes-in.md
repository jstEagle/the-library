## 12. Reyes Closes In

I heard the helicopter before I saw it, which is the only reason any of us are alive to argue about it now.

That's not false modesty. I grew up on the Colorado reading water the way other kids read comic books, and water tells you things air doesn't. The river went quiet. Not low—quiet. A hundred yards of moving water doesn't just decide to hush itself unless something above it is pushing down, some pressure change, some big slow hand flattening the surface. I'd felt it once before, years ago, when a medevac chopper came in low over a rapid to pull a drowned rafter's body off a rock. The whole canyon had held its breath.

So when the Redwater did that same thing at four in the afternoon, I put two fingers in my mouth and whistled the way you whistle a dog off a highway.

"Down," I said. "Now. Everybody."

Wendell didn't ask questions, which told me how far we'd come since the Narrows. Three weeks ago he'd have wanted to know why, wanted data, wanted to weigh it against his own read of the sky. Now he just grabbed Teo by the pack strap and hauled him toward the bank like the boy was luggage, and Bo was already moving, because Bo has the reflexes of a man who spent ten years running toward the thing everyone else runs from.

The slot canyon was maybe forty feet upstream on the left—a crack in the sandstone wall, no wider than a doorway, the kind of place you'd never notice unless you were looking for somewhere to disappear. We'd scouted it that morning without knowing why. Junie wants a bolt-hole, I'd said, and nobody argued. That's another thing about this crew: they've learned to trust my paranoia. It has a better track record than their optimism.

We got inside maybe ninety seconds before the helicopter came around the bend.

* * *

A slot canyon does strange things to sound. The rotors should have been deafening, but the walls ate most of it and gave back an echo that seemed to come from everywhere and nowhere, a vibration more than a noise, thrumming up through the stone and into your teeth. I pressed my back against the rock and watched a slice of sky through the crack of the entrance turn the color of dirty copper as the machine swung over us.

It was black. Not government-white with a friendly seal, not news-black with a camera hanging off the side. Just black, matte, unmarked, the color of a thing that doesn't want to be identified. It hung over our campsite for a long moment, and I could see the shadow of its skids sliding across the sand where our rafts were pulled up, three long dark bars crawling over the bright fabric like fingers feeling for a pulse.

Then it set down on the gravel bar, and men started getting out.

Six of them. I counted twice because counting matters; it's the difference between *we can take them* and *we cannot take them*, and I wanted very badly to be wrong the first time. Six men in gray and tan, no insignia, moving with the particular economy of people who've done this before and been paid well for it. They weren't soldiers exactly. Soldiers have a looseness, a barracks humor even on task. These men moved like auditors. Like the job was inventory.

One of them stayed with the helicopter. Five fanned out toward our rafts.

Beside me, Wendell had gone very still. Stillness looks different on different people. On Wendell it looks like a man doing arithmetic he hates. His jaw was working, and I knew—I knew the way you know weather coming—that he was calculating what six armed men cost per hour, who was paying that invoice, and how much of it traced back to a smiling man named Corliss Ames. He'd been carrying something alone since the satellite call. I'd heard half of it through the tent wall one night, Ames's voice thin and cheerful as a paper cut: *the debt doubles, Mr. Montrose, and the crew becomes liable too.* Wendell thought nobody knew. Wendell is a good man with a bad habit, which is believing that protecting people means lying to them.

I wasn't going to let him keep that habit. But that was a conversation for after we stopped being prey.

Teo was shaking. Not scared-shaking—angry-shaking, fourteen years old and vibrating with the injustice of being hunted by adults with a helicopter. Bo had a hand on the back of his neck, thumb moving in slow circles, the universal paramedic gesture for *stay with me, stay calm, you're not dying today*. Bo's eyes never left the men on the gravel bar.

"What are they doing?" Teo whispered.

"Stealing our stuff," Bo whispered back. "Rude, honestly. We paid deposits on those dry bags."

The wry thing is armor. I've worn it so long I've forgotten what's underneath. But even I couldn't find a joke in the next part, so I'll tell it straight.

* * *

They went through our rafts the way Reyes would have gone through them—methodically, from bow to stern, everything lifted and turned over and photographed. Because Reyes was there. I recognized him from the survey camp, from the day he stood in the middle of our salvage operation with his laminated jurisdiction and his voice like a filing cabinet closing, and told us the basin was closed and we were leaving. He hadn't raised his voice once then. He didn't raise it now. He walked the line of rafts with his hands clasped behind his back like a man inspecting a garden, and every few steps he'd stop and hold out his palm, and someone would put something in it.

Our charts. Our satellite phone. Teo's spare drone batteries. The waterproof case where we kept the first cache of survey documents—the ones we'd already copied, thank God, thank whatever watches over fools on rivers, because Wendell had made us all memorize the backup plan and split the copies three ways. They took the originals and never knew they were taking nothing but weight.

Reyes held the empty case for a long time. Turned it over. Opened it, looked into the space where papers should have been, and closed it again. Then he looked up—not at the rafts, not at the river, but at the canyon wall. At the general idea of the canyon wall. And though there was no possible way he could see forty feet of sandstone into the crack where we were pressed together sweating, I felt his gaze land on me personally, the way you feel a drop of cold water find the gap in your collar.

"He knows," Teo breathed.

"He suspects," Wendell breathed back. "There's a difference, and it's the only one keeping us breathing."

Because here's what I understood in that moment, standing in the dark with my family's heartbeats hammering around me: Reyes wasn't searching our rafts for equipment. Equipment doesn't matter. You don't send six men and a matte-black helicopter to recover dry bags. He was searching for confirmation. He wanted to know if we'd gone upstream, and how far, and whether we'd found what the survey team found. And the empty document case told him yes—or told him enough. We weren't trespassers anymore. We weren't even witnesses in the legal sense, because witnesses get subpoenas and witness protection and their names in a court record where hurting them becomes inconvenient.

We were loose ends. And Reyes struck me as a man whose entire professional identity rested on never leaving one behind.

One of the gray men crouched at the edge of the water and studied the sand. Even from forty feet away I could see what he saw, because I'd seen it myself that morning and cursed it: our tracks, going upstream along the bank, plain as handwriting. Four sets of boots and a teenager's sneakers, heading upriver into the closed basin, dated by last night's wind like a signature.

The man looked up at Reyes and said something too quiet to carry. Reyes nodded once. Then he took out his own radio—the old-fashioned kind, antenna stubby as a cigarette—and spoke into it for perhaps fifteen seconds. I caught none of the words. I caught all of the tone. It was the tone of a man ordering lunch, if lunch were people.

"They're calling ahead," I whispered. "Upstream. There's a team above us, or there will be by morning."

"You can't know that," Wendell said.

"I can absolutely know that. I've been reading sign since you were in short pants, Wendell. That man just placed an order." I kept my eyes on the gravel bar. "Question is whether we're the appetizer or the entrée."

Bo made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Teo had gone pale under his freckles. And Wendell—Wendell did the thing that made me finally, permanently forgive him for the secrets, right there in the dark with death shopping on our doorstep. He reached over without looking and gripped the back of my harness, the way you grip a rope you're trusting your life to, and he said, "Okay. So we don't sleep tonight, and we don't take the main channel past the red cliffs, and we get ahead of them instead of behind them. Junie. Tell me how."

Not *are you sure*. Not *maybe they're just recovering equipment*. He asked me to navigate. Which is, if you think about it, the most intimate question one human being can ask another: *tell me how we live.*

* * *

We stayed in the slot until full dark, which took forever, because time moves like molasses when you're listening to strangers dismantle your camp. They took the rafts' inflation valves—popped them, actually, so no one could follow quickly. They burned what wouldn't carry: our spare food, the extra paddle, a tarp. The smoke smell drifted into the crack and sat with us, acrid and intimate, the smell of our own trail being erased behind us. Clever. Cruel-clever. Burn the bridge and the bridge's twin, so the animals in the wall have no choice but to go forward, deeper, into the narrowing country where the waiting team will be.

Or so the thinking goes, if you assume the animals are stupid.

When the helicopter finally lifted off—it went downstream, which interested me almost as much as anything else, because it meant Reyes believed the net upstream was sufficient—we waited another hour. Bo counted it. Bo counts everything; suspended licenses give you a lot of practice sitting still. Then we came out of the crack into a camp that looked like a crime scene photographed by someone tidy. Nothing scattered. Everything arranged. Our absence documented, our direction inferred, our future filed under pending.

We traveled that night by starlight, which I don't recommend, hauling what remained of our gear in the two rafts we'd hidden separately two days earlier—an instinct I'd had at the fork in the canyon that Wendell had humored the way you humor a woman who talks to rivers. The spare rafts were four miles upstream, cached in a side wash under a slab of sandstone, and getting to them meant a portage over slickrock in the dark with Bo's shoulder screaming and Teo navigating by drone-light, a rectangle of soft glow held low over the stone like a lantern carried by a ghost.

Nobody complained. That's the thing I want to remember about that night, when I'm old and the rivers have all been renamed after donors. Nobody complained. Teo carried double and didn't say a word. Bo, who'd taken shrapnel from a flipped raft in the Narrows and hadn't fully admitted it, rigged his own sling out of a cam strap and kept hauling. And Wendell walked beside me the whole portage and, somewhere in mile two, in the dark, without ceremony, he started talking.

"The call," he said. "You heard it."

"I heard enough."

"Ames says the debt doubles. Says the crew's liable now. Says if we bring him the source data, everything goes back to normal." A long breath. "I was going to figure out how to tell you all when I'd figured out how to fix it. Same mistake I always make. Carrying the ledger alone."

"Wendell." I stepped over a ledge and reached back to haul him up it. "The ledger isn't yours anymore. It hasn't been since the Narrows. You signed us onto this river, fine—but we signed ourselves onto each other, and nobody consulted you about that. So here's the new arrangement: you carry your half, I carry mine, and when the numbers get bad we say them out loud. Deal?"

He was quiet for a few steps. In the dark I heard him do the thing he does instead of crying, which is exhale like a man setting down something heavy.

"Deal," he said.

* * *

We found the second cache at dawn—the survey team's upstream stash, tucked in a hollow beneath a fallen cottonwood, exactly where the journal page said it would be. *It isn't geology,* the page had said, in handwriting that started confident and ended shaky. Inside the cache: more instruments, more notes, and a map with the source marked and a route marked and, in the margin, in that same deteriorating hand, a single sentence that Bo read aloud while the sky went pink over the rim:

*"If anyone finds this: they know we know. Don't be the second group that trusts them."*

We stood around it in the early light, four strays with salt crust on our lips and the river muttering past, and I watched the understanding move through us like current through water. First group that trusted them: the survey team, disavowed, hunted, one of them dead in a grave dug by friends. Second group: us, our rafts deflated, our trail burned, a radio order for lunch placed somewhere upstream.

"We're not witnesses," Teo said quietly. He'd been chewing on it all night; you could see it land in him now, the way hard truths land on the young, whole and unblunted. "Witnesses testify. Witnesses get to exist afterward. We're—" He stopped. Couldn't find the word, or wouldn't.

"Evidence," Bo said. "Loose evidence. And somebody sent professionals to collect us."

Above the canyon rim, faint and far, a sound rolled down to us across the miles of stone. Rotor-wash. One helicopter or two, impossible to tell, and getting neither closer nor farther—which meant it was holding position. Waiting. Somewhere between us and the source, in one of the hundred narrows and slots and amphitheaters this country offers a man with a rifle and patience, Reyes's colleagues were settling in like herons at a fish run.

Wendell looked at me. "Can we beat them?"

I put my hand flat on the sun-warmed rock, then dipped it in the river, feeling the cold pulse of snowmelt still fighting the season, feeling the water's opinion of the days ahead. The dry season was closing its fist. Every day the channels narrowed, the portages lengthened, the hiding places shrank. But the water was still high enough to run fast, and Reyes's team was on foot, and foot travel in canyon country is a tax paid hourly.

"Not if we're careful," I said. "So we won't be. We'll be fast instead."

And I pointed upstream, toward the source, toward the truth somebody had killed to bury, and my crew—my ridiculous, wounded, chosen family—shouldered their packs and followed me into the narrowing light.

Behind us, far downstream, the helicopter turned and began to climb.
