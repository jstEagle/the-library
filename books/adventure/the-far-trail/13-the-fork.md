## 13. The Fork

The canyon fork announced itself the way most important decisions do: without any warning at all.

One minute we were poling through a stretch of water so shallow it barely qualified as wet, and the next the walls split open ahead of us like a book falling apart at the spine. The left channel ran wide and slow, sliding south toward the basin's edge and everything that word implied—roads, radios, people who weren't trying to kill us. The right channel narrowed into a throat of red rock that climbed northwest, up toward the source, up toward whatever had made a government survey team decide to stop existing.

Junie stood in the bow with her hand shading her eyes, reading the water the way other people read street signs.

"Left goes somewhere," she said. "Right goes somewhere worse."

"That's not exactly a ringing endorsement of either."

"It wasn't meant to be." She turned around, and I saw what I'd been seeing more and more lately: the tiredness under her competence, the way her jaw was set like she was holding something between her teeth and refusing to let go. "Wendell. We need to talk about where we're going. All of us. Not you deciding on the move and telling us after."

"I don't decide on the move," I said. "I just—"

"You decided to take this contract alone. You decided to hide Ames's call for three days. You decided we were going upstream before anyone voted on it." She wasn't angry, which somehow made it worse. Junie angry was weather; Junie calm was a verdict. "I love you like a brother, but you run your life like a man being chased, and lately you've been dragging us behind you."

Bo, who had been pretending to check the medical kit for the last ten minutes, cleared his throat with the delicacy of a man dropping an anvil. "She's got a point," he said. "And I say that as someone whose entire professional philosophy is 'don't make points.'"

Teo said nothing. He sat on the gear pile with his drone controller in his lap, fourteen years old and watching me with those eyes that had seen too many courtrooms already. Kids like Teo learned early how adults made decisions about them without asking. I'd been doing it to him for weeks.

"Okay," I said. "Okay. We camp here. We vote."

* * *

We pulled the rafts onto a gravel bar beneath a wall of sandstone the color of old blood, and while Bo got a fire going—small, screened, the kind of fire you build when you know helicopters exist—I climbed the talus slope at the fork's mouth to get a look at both channels.

The left one was honest country. Open sky, a line of cottonwoods marking the river's path south, and beyond them, if you squinted, the faint dust haze of the basin road. Two days' travel, maybe three with the wounded pace we'd been keeping since the shallows. After that: cell signal, county sheriffs, a border of the basin that existed mostly as a line on a map but which Reyes's people treated like the gates of a prison. Run for it, and maybe we lived. Maybe. Reyes had a helicopter and no discernible conscience, and the collectors had boats we hadn't yet managed to sink permanently.

The right channel was a question mark wearing a canyon's clothes. It climbed, it narrowed, and somewhere at the end of it was a cavern where three surveyors had been hiding from their own government for months, and beneath all of it was an aquifer feeding three states that somebody very powerful had decided to sell in secret. Somewhere back downriver was a grave dug with care by men who knew they were next.

I stood there long enough for the wind to find every gap in my jacket, and then I climbed down and told the truth to my family, or tried to.

* * *

"We could make the border," I said, once we were sitting around the fire with dinner rehydrating in four dented pots. "If we push hard tomorrow, we're out of the basin in two days. We ditch the data drives in the river, scatter, and get lost. Ames loses his leverage because there's nothing to seize. Reyes can't chase witnesses who never saw anything worth killing over."

"You'd give up the drives," Junie said. Not a question.

"The drives are why Bo's got stitches. The drives are why we're eating half-rations. The drives are a piece of plastic full of numbers, and I'm getting real tired of watching people bleed for pieces of plastic." I turned my pot in my hands. "I took this contract to pay a debt. Somewhere along the way the debt stopped mattering and I didn't notice, because that's what I do. I don't notice things until they've already happened to me."

"Modest, too," Bo said.

"Bo."

"Sorry. Continue. You were saying something honest, which is rare enough I should shut up and let it happen."

I looked at each of them. "Here's the honest version. If we run, we probably live. And Marsh and her two survivors stay in that cave until Reyes finds them, because the only reason they've lasted this long is that nobody's been able to follow our track upstream. And the aquifer gets sold, and three states drink somebody's private reservoir for the next fifty years, and the guy in the grave stays a footnote. That's the deal. That's what running buys."

"And staying?" Teo asked quietly.

"Staying might buy nothing. Staying might buy us a slot in the ground next to the surveyor. I want to be straight with you about that. I've been doing the math all day and the math says we should run. Every version of the math says we should run." I set the pot down. "But math isn't the only thing I know how to count anymore."

The fire popped. Downstream, far off, a coyote started up and thought better of it.

"So vote," Junie said. "All of us. Real votes, counted out loud. And Wendell—" she pointed her spoon at me like a fencing master, "—you vote too, but you don't get to break ties by pulling rank you don't have. If it ties, we sleep on it."

"Fair."

"Bo first. Then me. Then Teo. Then you."

"Why am I last?"

"Because you talk longest when you're nervous, and I want to hear you after everyone else has already said their piece. Makes it harder for you to manage us." She smiled, thin and real. "Old navigator trick."

* * *

Bo fed another stick to the fire and looked into it for a while. When Bo went quiet, it wasn't shyness; it was a man assembling words the way he'd once assembled splint kits, carefully, because somebody's outcome depended on the construction.

"My license is suspended," he said finally. "You all know that. What you maybe don't know is why. I had a patient, a kid, eighteen, OD'd in a parking lot outside Bakersfield. Protocol said wait for transport. Transport was eleven minutes out. I made a judgment call and moved him, and he lived, and the board suspended me anyway because the call wasn't mine to make." He shrugged his big shoulders. "Eleven minutes. He lived because I broke a rule. The rule survived; my career didn't."

He looked up. "I've been thinking about that kid a lot since the shallows. About rules. There's a rule that says when you find evidence of a felony, you report it to the authorities. Well. We found evidence of a felony committed *by* the authorities. So the rule's got a hole in it big enough to drive a helicopter through." He tapped the ground beside him, where the surveyor's notebook sat wrapped in a dry bag. "That man in the grave did everything right. Followed procedure, documented everything, buried his friend with respect. Doing everything right got him executed. So no, I'm not voting for the system. I'm voting for the eleven minutes. Push on."

"Push on," Junie repeated softly, like she was testing the weight of it. Then she sat forward, elbows on knees, and I realized I'd never actually heard her tell her own story all the way through. She'd given us fragments—the river guide years, the flood, the town that stopped existing—but never the whole shape of it.

"When I was twenty-three I guided the Verde during the flood year," she said. "You remember the flood year. Everybody remembers the flood year. The agency had forecasts saying the river would come up six feet by Thursday. They sat on the forecast because releasing it would have cost the rafting companies their season, and the companies paid the agency's fees, and that's how the world works when nobody's watching." Her voice stayed level, which is how I knew how much it cost her. "The river came up nine feet on Tuesday night. Four people died, including a man named Harlan Oakes who'd been coming down that river every spring for thirty years. I found his boat wrapped on a strainer two miles down. I was the one who told his wife."

She looked at me. "I have spent my whole life since then reading water, Wendell, because water doesn't lie—it's the only thing that doesn't. People lie. Agencies lie. Ledgers lie. But the river tells you exactly what it's going to do if you know how to listen, and nobody ever went to jail for Harlan Oakes, and I have carried that in my chest for eleven years like a stone I swallowed." She picked up a pebble and turned it over. "This is the first time anybody's handed me a chance to put the stone down. I'm not giving it back so we can run and be comfortable. Push on."

Then it was Teo.

He'd gone still in the way he did when the custody hearings came up in his head—that careful, folded-in stillness of a kid who'd learned that attention was dangerous. His drone controller sat in his lap and his thumbs moved over the sticks without flying anything, just keeping his hands busy.

"You guys are gonna say I'm a kid and my vote shouldn't count the same," he said.

"Nope," Bo said.

"Not how this works," Junie said.

"But I'm gonna say it anyway, because I need you to hear it." Teo took a breath. "For two years, everybody decided things for me. My mom, my dad, two lawyers, a judge, a caseworker named Denise who was actually really nice but still—every single adult in my life sat in rooms and decided my future while I waited in hallways. I got really good at hallways. I could tell you the vending machine prices in three different courthouses." He almost smiled. "Nobody ever asked me what I wanted until you guys did. First day. You handed me the drone and said 'show us what you can do,' and that was the first time in two years anybody treated me like I had something to offer instead of something to protect."

His thumbs went still.

"So here's what I want. I want to not be in the hallway anymore. I want to be in the room. And I want—" his voice cracked and he pushed through it, fourteen years old and braver than any adult I'd ever met, "—I want the people who dug that grave to lose. Not escape, not negotiate. Lose. Because if we run, they win, and if they win, then everywhere is a hallway forever, for everybody." He looked at me, dead level. "Push on. That's my vote."

Three votes. I felt something loosen in my chest and something else tighten around it, because now it was my turn, and Junie had engineered it perfectly: whatever I said, I'd be saying it to people who'd already shown me who they were.

* * *

"I keep a ledger in my head," I said. "Have since I was nineteen. Everything owed, everything owing. It's how I kept score when I had nothing else—miles and dollars and debts. My dad died owing money to a man who sent flowers to the funeral, and I swore I'd never be that guy, and then I spent fifteen years becoming exactly that guy, just with better trucks." I laughed, and it came out rougher than I meant. "Ames offered me a personal out yesterday. On the radio. Debt forgiven, walk away clean, leave the crew liable. And I said no, and I want to be clear that I said no for the right reasons, but part of me was still doing arithmetic. Part of me was calculating whether the crew would forgive me later."

"Part of you is always doing arithmetic," Junie said. "It's your worst quality."

"It's in the top five." I held up my hands. "So here's my vote, and it's not arithmetic. My dad used to say a man's life is measured in miles, and I believed him for forty years. Miles are easy. Miles don't ask anything of you except that you keep moving. But I've been counting wrong. It's not miles. It's the people in the truck." I looked around the fire—at the ex-paramedic who'd broken his career to save one kid, the navigator who'd swallowed a stone for eleven years, the teenager who'd chosen us over every hallway in the world. "I can outrun a debt. I can't outrun looking at you three in ten years and knowing I drove away from the only justice we'll ever get because it was safer. Push on. Unanimous."

"Unanimous," Bo said, and stuck out his hand palm-down over the fire.

Junie put hers on top. Teo put his small hand on hers. I put mine on top of the stack, and Bo pressed down, and that was that—notarized by campfire, witnessed by nobody but the canyon and the stars starting to come out over the rim.

"One condition," Junie said, as we broke apart. "From here on, decisions get made in the open. All of them. You feel a secret coming on, Wendell Montrose, you bring it to the fire. Deal?"

"Deal."

"And Teo flies high at dawn. I want eyes on the right channel before we commit to it."

"Already planned it," Teo said, and there was something new in his voice—something that sounded, for the first time since I'd met him, like a boy who expected to be listened to.

* * *

I took first watch, because I always did, but tonight it wasn't avoidance. I sat on the gravel bar with the notebook in my lap and read the dead surveyor's careful handwriting again by firelight, the pages naming the aquifer, the deal, the names involved, and I understood that we were carrying something heavier than data drives. We were carrying four people's answer to the question of what a life is for. The surveyor had answered it with a grave dug for his friend and a notebook hidden against the day someone honest came upstream. Marsh and her survivors had answered it with months of cave water and patience. And my crew—my strange, wounded, magnificent crew—had answered it with a unanimous vote cast by firelight in the middle of nowhere, choosing a hard right turn over an easy left one.

Around midnight, Teo padded out of his bag and sat next to me without a word. We watched the river slide past the fork, splitting around the gravel bar like a decision being made and remade forever.

"Wendell?"

"Yeah, kid."

"If we find them—the surveyors—and they tell us everything, and we get it out..." He trailed off, working at it. "Do you think it changes anything? Like, actually? Or do powerful people just... absorb it, and it's a news cycle, and then it's over?"

I thought about the flowers at my father's funeral. I thought about Harlan Oakes's widow. I thought about a judge in a courtroom deciding a boy's life while the boy counted vending machine prices in the hall.

"I think," I said slowly, "that powerful people absorb everything except witnesses. A story can be spun. A document can be buried. But four people standing in front of cameras saying *we were there, we saw it, here are the names*—that's harder to digest. It gives them indigestion for years." I bumped his shoulder with mine. "And I think it matters even if it fails. Harlan Oakes still mattered. The kid in Bakersfield still mattered. Justice isn't a place you arrive, Teo. It's a direction you hold."

He nodded like I'd handed him something fragile and important, and went back to his bag.

At 4 a.m., with the stars wheeling over the rim and the fire down to coals, the satellite phone buzzed against my hip. Caller ID: MERIDIAN FUND — C. AMES.

I looked at the phone. I looked at the sleeping shapes of the people who had, twelve hours ago, made me promise to bring secrets to the fire.

I woke Junie up.

"Ames," I said, handing her the phone before I answered, letting her see the screen. "You wanted it brought to the fire. Here it is."

She took it, read the name, and handed it back with a nod that was almost a smile. "Answer it," she said. "And put it on speaker. Let's hear what the optimist has to say."

Downstream, in the dark, the left channel whispered its promises. I pressed the button, and the right channel waited.
