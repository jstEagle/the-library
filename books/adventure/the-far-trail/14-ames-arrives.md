## 14. Ames Arrives

The collectors found us at the worst possible hour of the day, which was, knowing Corliss Ames, exactly how he'd planned it.

We'd made eleven miles since dawn through country that hated us personally—slickrock shelves that dropped away without warning, drainages that funneled heat like kilns, a stretch of canyon bottom where the only shade was the shadow we cast ourselves. Bo's shoulder had gone from bad to worse overnight. The wound from the shallows was angry now, red spreading past the edges of Junie's field dressing, and he'd stopped pretending it didn't hurt somewhere around mile six. Teo carried his pack without being asked and lied about its weight without being asked to lie. That's the kind of kid he was becoming out here. That's the kind of man any of us were becoming, maybe.

I was in front when I heard it—not a sound so much as an absence. The canyon went quiet the way it does when something large has recently passed through and everything small is still holding its breath. I put up a fist, the old storm-chase signal, and the crew folded into the rocks behind me like water finding cracks.

They came around the bend on foot, six of them, moving with the unhurried confidence of people who have never once been lost. Meridian Fund contractors, dressed in the Fund's idea of backcountry gear—expensive, clean, wrong. And walking at their center, hands clasped behind his back like a man touring a garden, was Corliss Ames.

He was wearing a linen shirt. A *linen shirt*. In the Redwater Basin, where the sun came down like a verdict and the dust got into your teeth before breakfast. He wasn't even sweating. I've chased storms for fifteen years and I have never once seen a human being defy weather the way that man defied it.

"Wendell," he called up, pleasant as a neighbor borrowing a ladder. "You look terrible."

"You look like a catalog," I said.

"I'll take that as a compliment." He stopped at the base of the shelf where I stood and tilted his head back to consider me, the way you'd appraise a used truck you already knew the blue book value of. "Mind if we talk? Just you and me. Your crew can keep the high ground. I know how attached they are to it."

Junie's hand found my sleeve. "Wendell."

"It's fine," I said, and I almost believed it.

* * *

We walked down to the gravel bar together, Ames and I, while five armed men watched us with the relaxed posture of people who'd been told violence was unlikely but not off the table. The river ran shallow here, ankle-deep over copper-colored stone, talking to itself in that flat dry-season voice. Upstream, maybe two days' travel if Bo held together, was the source. Was Elena Marsh and whatever remained of her team. Was the truth, sitting in my pack in the form of three data drives and a dead man's notebook, wrapped in a shirt that used to be white.

Ames crouched by the water, dipped two fingers in, and studied them like a sommelier.

"Shame about this river," he said. "Another month it'll be a creek. Another month after that, a rumor." He stood, brushed nothing off his immaculate trousers. "You know what I admire about you, Wendell? You're consistent. Most debtors get creative when the numbers stop working. They hide assets. They invent relatives. One gentleman claimed to be deceased—I checked; he was not. But you. You just keep driving toward the thing you owe. It's almost religious."

"Is there a point coming?"

"There's always a point. Points are my whole business." He turned to face me fully, and here's the thing about Corliss Ames: he never raised his voice, never threatened directly, never did anything you could take to a lawyer or a judge or even a bar fight. He just described the world to you, accurately, until you saw which way the door opened. "Here's mine. The Fund is prepared to settle your account today. Full balance. Interest, penalties, the collection costs—all of it. Gone. Zeroed. You walk out of this basin owing nothing to anyone."

I waited. There's always a second half with Ames, the way there's always a second half to a supercell—the part where the pretty tower collapses and the tornado drops out.

"The drives," he said. "And the notebook. You hand them to me, you tell your crew you're taking a walk, and you walk south with my men to the vehicles. They're compensated for their time and released at the basin boundary. Everyone goes home. Nobody's reputation requires further discussion."

"And if I don't?"

"Then the debt doubles tomorrow morning, and your crew becomes jointly liable. Junie Okafor. Bo Reyes—" he caught himself, smiled thinly at the coincidence, "—forgive me, Bo Calloway. Teo Vasquez, who is, I should mention, a minor whose custody case would benefit enormously from a documented history of association with armed fugitives. The Fund has resources, Wendell. We've been very patient with you because you're good collateral. But collateral is all you've ever been."

There it was. The old arithmetic, laid out clean as a ledger line: my freedom against theirs. My zero against their ruin. And God help me, some tired animal part of me—the part that had spent years measuring life in miles because miles were the only unit that didn't judge him—felt the pull of it. Zero. I hadn't been zero since I was twenty-six years old. I could taste it, that weightless feeling, like the stillness at a storm's core where the pressure drops and your ears pop and everything goes quiet right before the worst of it.

"Teo's custody case," I said. "You looked into that."

"We look into everything. It's what makes us thorough rather than cruel. There's a distinction." He said it gently, like a doctor delivering a prognosis. "His grandmother has legal counsel now, incidentally. Well-funded counsel. Funny how money finds these things."

"You'd do that to a sixteen-year-old."

"I'd do it to a liability. Age is incidental." He glanced upstream, at the canyon walls going gold with late light. "But I want to be clear, Wendell, because I respect you more than the numbers suggest: I'm not threatening the boy. I'm describing consequences. There's a difference, and honest men learn to see it. Now." He extended his hand, palm open, empty—a collector's gesture, offering nothing and asking for everything. "The drives."

I looked at his hand. I thought about the number. Zero. The number I'd been running from across a thousand miles of plains, the number that had signed me onto this salvage contract in the first place, the number that had sat on my chest every night like a sleeping dog I couldn't wake. All I had to do was hand over three pieces of metal and a dead surveyor's notebook, and the number died forever.

And then I thought about Bo, carrying Teo's pack so the kid wouldn't have to feel it. About Junie reading the river like scripture, finding us water when there was no water to find. About Teo's face at the fork in the canyon, casting the deciding vote for truth with his chin up and his voice shaking and nobody laughing. About Elena Marsh, whoever she was now, waiting two months in the dark for someone honest to come down the river.

About the surveyor in the grave, executed for writing down what he found.

"Corliss," I said, "do you know why I chase storms?"

Something flickered behind his eyes—the first genuine expression I'd ever seen on him. "Enlighten me."

"Because a storm doesn't pretend to be anything else. It's the most honest thing on earth. It tells you exactly what it is, and then it kills you or it doesn't." I looked at his open hand. "You spend your whole life dressing up theft as arithmetic. Consequences instead of threats. Thoroughness instead of cruelty. But you're just weather too, Corliss. You're just a front moving through. And fronts pass."

"Poetry," he said softly. "From a man with a seven-figure balance."

"From a man who just figured out the difference between a price and a cost." I stepped back from his hand. "No."

He closed his fingers slowly, one at a time, like a machine powering down. For a long moment he just looked at me, and I understood that I was watching recalculation—the ledger updating in real time, my value dropping, my classification changing from asset to obstacle. It should have frightened me. Some of it did. But underneath the fear was something I hadn't felt in years, something with actual weight to it, and it took me a second to name it.

It was relief. Terrible, liberating relief. The relief of a man who finally stops swimming and lets the current decide.

"No," I said again, louder, so the crew on the rocks above could hear it, so *I* could hear it. "Keep the debt. Keep the interest. Tell your well-funded lawyers whatever you need to tell them. But those drives go downstream to every news desk and water authority in three states, and the notebook goes with them, and when the hearings start—and they will start—you can explain to Congress why your Fund financed the cover-up of an aquifer that belongs to everybody."

Ames exhaled through his nose. Almost a laugh.

"You understand what happens now," he said. "This stops being business."

"It was never business. Business implies both parties might profit."

He straightened his cuffs. Adjusted nothing else, because nothing else needed adjusting. When he spoke again, the warmth was gone from his voice entirely, and what remained was somehow worse than anger—it was procedure. "Mr. Montrose. It has been a genuine pleasure doing no business with you whatsoever." He turned to his men. "Gentlemen. We're done here."

They started back down the gravel bar, and I stood there watching them go, heart slamming, hands steady, feeling lighter than I had any right to feel with a doubled debt on my back and five armed men between me and home.

Then Ames stopped. Didn't turn all the way—just his head, profile against the copper water.

"One professional courtesy, Wendell. Reyes's team crossed the divide this morning. Helicopter support. They'll reach the source before you do." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "I'd hurry."

And then he was gone around the bend, linen shirt and all, and I was running for the rocks, shouting for Junie, shouting for everyone, because the light was dying and we had two days of canyon left and now we had to make it in one.

Behind us, far to the north, I swear I could already hear it—the thin blade-whine of helicopter rotors, coming up the river like weather.
