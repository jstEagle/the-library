## 15. Blood in the Shallows

I've been shot at twice in my life. The first time was a hunting accident, my cousin Dale and a twelve-gauge and a bottle of rye, and I'll say this for Dale: he at least had the decency to cry about it. The second time was Tuesday morning, in a canyon so narrow the sun only visited it on weekends, and nobody cried. There wasn't time.

Let me back up.

We'd been running the shallows since dawn, dragging the rafts over gravel bars where the river had given up pretending to be a river. Dry season had chewed the Redwater down to its bones—knee-deep water braided across a quarter mile of stone, the kind of terrain where you can't float and you can't hide and you can't move fast. Junie called it "the washboard." She said it the way you'd name a disease.

Wendell wanted to push through it before noon. Teo wanted to fly the drone up-canyon to scout. I wanted coffee, which is how you know I'm getting old, because ten years ago I'd have wanted a fight instead.

I got the fight anyway. It just came with company.

* * *

The collectors came up from behind us, out of the sun-glare off the western wall, and if Ames hadn't been with them I'd have said they moved like soldiers. But Ames was there—Corliss Ames, in his pressed shirt and his hiking boots that cost more than my truck, walking the gravel like he was strolling a farmers' market—and soldiers don't smile like that. Soldiers don't wave.

"Dr. Okafor!" he called, because apparently he'd read my file, all of it, including the license suspension and the malpractice complaint that got me here. "You're a long way from your practice."

"I don't have a practice," I said. "That's sort of the theme."

"Right, right." He stopped maybe forty yards out, close enough to talk without shouting, far enough that his men could do whatever they'd been paid to do. Four of them. Big ones. Two carried the flat black cases that I knew, from a previous life, held more than camping gear. "Mr. Montrose. We should talk."

"We're talking."

"Privately."

"He talks fine in public," Wendell said. He had his hand on Teo's shoulder, casual as anything, but I'd seen Wendell's hands enough to know when they were holding something down. "Whatever you've got, say it to all of us."

Ames sighed like a disappointed teacher. "Fine. Here's the shape of it. You give me the drives—the survey data, everything you pulled from the camp—and Mr. Montrose's account resets to zero. Clean slate. And the rest of you walk away with no liability, no exposure, nothing but a good story you'll never be able to sell." He spread his hands, magnanimous. "It's a generous offer. I'd take it."

"You'd take it," I said. "You take everything. That's your whole business model."

"Bo." Wendell's voice was quiet. Not warning-quiet. Something else.

But here's the thing about me: I spent fifteen years running toward emergencies other people were running from, and then one bad night and one suspended license turned me into a man who runs toward nothing. I'd been running toward nothing for three years, and I was tired of it, and Ames's smile was the exact same smile the review board wore when they took my card.

So when one of the big men started moving wide along the eastern bank—flanking, angling toward Teo and the drone case—I didn't think. That's the honest truth. I didn't think at all. Thinking is what got my license suspended; maybe not-thinking was going to get it back.

* * *

Here's what happened next, reconstructed from the parts I remember and the parts other people told me later, because memory goes funny when adrenaline floods the tank:

The flanker reached the shallow channel between us and Teo. Teo, bless him, did exactly the wrong thing and turned to look at him, drone case in both hands like a kid carrying a school project. The flanker smiled. I remember the smile. Guys like that always smile right before, because somebody taught them that smiling makes it legal.

I crossed thirty feet of slick gravel in about four seconds, which is a personal best I never want to attempt again, and I hit the channel just as the man's hand closed on the case strap.

"Hey," I said. "Kid's with me."

He swung. I'll give him this—he was fast. But I've caught falling people in floodwater, I've caught a two-hundred-pound man mid-seizure on a gurney bouncing down a stairwell, and catching things is mostly about reading weight before it arrives. I read it. I turned with it. And then his partner came out of nowhere—which is to say, out of the rocks where flanking partners come from—and something hard caught me across the side of the head, and the world went white and sideways and full of river.

I went down in eight inches of water. Eight inches doesn't seem like much until someone's knee is on your chest and the water is filling your ears and every breath costs you half of it. I heard shouting. I heard Teo screaming my name in a voice cracked straight down the middle. I heard a gunshot—not aimed, a warning, the crack of it doubled and tripled by the canyon walls—and I thought, distantly, professionally, clinically: *concussion, probable temporal contusion, keep the airway clear, stay awake, Bo, stay awake.*

Stay awake. That's the rule. You stay awake because if you go under you might not come back, and also because staying awake is the last act of stubbornness available to a man face-down in a creek.

So I stayed awake. I grabbed a fistful of gravel and I stayed awake.

* * *

What broke the pile was Teo.

Not the way you'd expect. He didn't swing the drone case like a club, though God knows he wanted to. What he did was smarter and dumber and more Teo than anything I can imagine: he threw the case into the deepest pool in the channel, and while every one of those hired men tracked the splash—because men like that are paid to protect property, and property splashing into water is a reflex trigger—Teo dropped his shoulder and drove into the pile like a fourteen-year-old linebacker, all ninety pounds of fury and custody-battle abandonment issues, and knocked the knee off my chest.

Which meant I could breathe. Which meant I could move. Which meant the fight was suddenly fair, or at least differently unfair.

Wendell came through the water like weather. I've watched that man chase storms for years and I never fully understood what I was looking at until I saw him hit a standing human being the way a supercell hits a wheat field. Junie was there too—Junie, who reads water like text, who knew before any of us that the gravel bar under the flankers' feet was undercut and rotten, and she was shouting "left bank, left bank!" and the bank obliged her by collapsing under one collector's boots and dumping him into the current, sputtering, his rifle held high and useless overhead because even mercenaries don't want to drown their equipment.

And Ames stood on the dry gravel the whole time, watching, and did not help, and did not run, and did not stop smiling until the moment he understood that we were not going to lose. Then the smile went away, and what was underneath it was worse, and I made a note of it through the blood in my eyes: *that's the real face. Remember the real face.*

* * *

"Bo. Bo. Look at me."

Teo's hands on my face, turning my head, and I looked at him and thought: *he has his mother's jaw and nobody's fear.* Three months ago this kid wouldn't make eye contact. Now he was pressing a wad of gauze—he'd raided our med kit without being told, knew where it was, knew what to pull—to the gash above my ear like he'd done it a hundred times.

"How many fingers?"

"One. And it's rude."

"Concussion humor. Great. Stay with me."

"Wasn't planning on leaving." I sat up, which the canyon objected to strenuously, and let him steady me. My hands were shaking. His weren't. Funny how that works—the wounded one shakes, the scared one doesn't, because fear burns off in the doing and all that's left is work.

Wendell crouched beside us, breathing hard, knuckles split. He looked at Teo, then at me, and whatever he was going to say got stuck somewhere behind his teeth, the way it always does with him. Feelings are the one debt Wendell Montrose can't figure out how to service.

"They offered you the out," I said instead, because my head was ringing and filterless. "Back there. Before the flanking move. They offered you clean slate, everybody walks. You didn't take it."

"No," he said.

"You hesitated."

"A second and a half."

"I counted," Teo said.

"It was a long second and a half," Wendell admitted. He looked out at the water, at Junie hauling one of the collectors' rafts onto the rocks, at the whole mess of us bleeding and gasping in eight inches of river. "Then I looked at you two idiots and did the math."

"And?"

"And the math came out wrong. Came out to people." He said it like an apology. For Wendell, it was practically a love letter.

* * *

We took their boats.

All of them—two inflatable rigs and the gear sleds, everything the collectors had hauled up the canyon on their backs, because you don't bring helicopters into the Narrows and everyone knows it. Junie slashed the pontoons of the one raft too damaged to use and dragged the rest above the high-water line, and then she stood there with her knife still open, looking upstream, doing the thing she does where her eyes trace the water and read it like a page.

"They'll be stranded till their backup comes," she said. "Two days, maybe three, through this terrain. By then the upper canyon closes for the season." She sheathed the knife. "We just bought ourselves a head start with their own boats."

"That's theft," I said.

"That's salvage," Wendell said. "Look it up. It's a gray area. Everything worth doing is."

I laughed, and laughing hurt, and hurting made me laugh again, which Teo informed me was "a concussion symptom and also deeply annoying."

We loaded fast. My job, self-assigned despite all reasonable objection, was triage: I taped two ribs I was fairly sure were cracked, mine; I cleaned the gash; I checked Teo over twice because he wouldn't admit the flanker had gotten a fist into his shoulder before I arrived, and I found the bruise anyway, blooming purple under his sleeve like a secret. He watched me tape it and didn't say anything, and I didn't say anything, and that silence was the most honest conversation either of us has ever had.

Before we pushed off, I walked over to where the collectors sat on the rocks—one bleeding nose, one soaked, one rubbing his collapsed-bank hip, all of them watching us with the flat resentment of professionals outplayed. Ames stood apart, phone in hand, no signal, smiling again now, the mask back on.

"Hey," I said to him. "Doc to patient. Free advice. That cut on your man's nose needs closure inside six hours or it scars ugly."

Ames tilted his head. "You're offering medical care to the people who put you in the creek."

"I'm offering it to him. You I wouldn't spit on." I tossed the gauze roll; one of the collectors caught it on reflex, muscle memory, and stared at it like it might bite. "Somebody in your line ought to know how to stop bleeding. Seems like you cause a lot of it."

Then I got in the boat, because my family was leaving, and I have missed enough departures in my life.

* * *

The current took us upstream—well, "current" is generous; Junie poled, Wendell paddled, Teo ran the small outboard we'd salvaged at low throttle, and I sat in the bow holding pressure on my own head and watching the canyon walls slide past like pages turning.

Behind us, small and receding, the collectors' camp shrank to nothing. Ahead of us, the canyon narrowed toward the fork, toward the source, toward whatever it was that a government team had found and died for and that Corliss Ames would burn the whole basin to bury.

My head throbbed in time with the water. I thought about the moment in the channel—no thinking, just moving, my body deciding before my brain got a vote—and I realized that somewhere between the suspension and the shallows, I'd gotten myself back. Not the license. Not the career. The part underneath, the part that runs toward.

Teo slid down next to me, careful of the ribs, and didn't say anything, just leaned his shoulder against mine the way kids do when they want contact but not conversation. After a while he said, "You didn't have to do that."

"Yeah, I did."

"Why?"

I watched the water braid and re-braid itself around the stones, finding its way downstream no matter what the dry season did to it.

"Because you're mine," I said. "That's the whole answer. You're mine, kid. All of you are."

He went quiet. Then, very quietly, in a voice I pretended not to hear so he'd never have to know I heard it: "Okay."

Upstream, around the bend, something glinted—a flash of light off metal, there and gone, high on the canyon rim. Junie saw it too. I watched her eyes track it, watched her face do the arithmetic.

"Helicopter?" Wendell asked.

"No," she said slowly. "Too slow. Too high." She adjusted her pole against the current. "But somebody's up on the rim. Somebody's been up there a while."

The boat moved into the shadow of the fork, and the light on the rim followed us.
