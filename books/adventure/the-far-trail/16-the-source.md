## 16. The Source

The river stopped making sense about a mile before it stopped entirely.

I'd been reading water since I was six years old—my grandmother taught me on the bayous, back when I still believed every current had a reason—and I'm telling you that what we paddled through that last stretch defied everything she ever put in my head. The Redwater came out of the canyon wall in three braided channels, all of them running *uphill* for thirty yards at a time before surrendering to gravity again. Junie's rules of hydrology, rule one: water is honest. It goes where the land tells it. Rule two, learned that morning: sometimes the land lies.

"Tell me you're seeing this," Teo said from the bow of my raft. He had his drone up, and his voice had gone strange and small.

"I'm seeing it."

"The flow meter says negative. Like, actually negative. The river is running backward."

"It's not running backward," Bo said behind us, calm as a man discussing breakfast. "It's being pulled. There's a difference."

Bo had been saying things like that all morning—short, certain statements that sounded like they'd been lifted from somebody else's mouth. Ever since the grave. Ever since Marsh's notebook. He hadn't slept much; none of us had, but Bo wore it differently, wore it like a coat he'd decided not to take off until the job was done.

Wendell brought up the rear, and when I looked back at him he gave me the nod. The one that meant *keep going*. We'd been trading that nod for eleven days now, through the Narrows and the portages and the slot canyon where we'd pressed ourselves flat against stone while Reyes's helicopter chewed the air above us. It had become a whole conversation, that nod. *Are we still doing this? Yes. Are we sure? No. Keep going.*

The canyon narrowed the way canyons do when they're hiding something—not gradually but all at once, walls pinching in until our paddles scraped both sides, until the sky was a blue ribbon overhead and the light went green and submarine. And then the ribbon opened, and the walls fell away, and there it was.

I want to describe it properly, because I've spent my whole life around water and I have never seen anything like it, and I suspect I never will again.

The river didn't come from upstream. It came from *below*, and from everywhere. The canyon opened into a natural amphitheater maybe two hundred yards across, ringed by walls streaked with mineral stain—red and white and a deep impossible blue-green—and in the center of it all, the water simply *rose*. Not a spring. Not a seep. A boil, wide as a city block, pushing up from the dark with enough force to stand the surface into a permanent trembling dome, silent except for a low hum you felt in your sternum more than heard. The water coming up was impossibly clear, so clear it looked solid, and where it spilled over the lip of the basin and became the Redwater again, it flashed white as struck flint.

And carved into the far wall, half-swallowed by centuries of mineral bloom, were stairs.

Human stairs. Old ones, older than anything had a right to be out here, climbing from the waterline up toward a shadowed ledge.

"Junie," Wendell said quietly. "Your read?"

I made myself look away from the boil long enough to scan the walls, the ledges, the shadows under the overhangs. My grandmother also taught me this: when a place feels too beautiful, look for what's watching you.

"We're not alone," I said.

"I know," Wendell said. "Been feeling it for ten minutes." He raised his voice without raising its volume—a trick of his, filling a space without disturbing it. "We're looking for Elena Marsh! Survey team! We found your camp, we found Daniel Okafor's grave, and we found the notebook!"

The hum filled the silence after his voice died. The dome of water trembled. Somewhere above us, a pebble clicked loose and skittered down the red rock.

Then a voice, from the ledge by the stairs—a woman's voice, cracked from disuse but steady underneath, the way a rope is frayed on the outside and sound at the core:

"How much did the notebook say?"

"Enough," Wendell called back. "It named the aquifer. It named the buyers. It said the rest of you refused to falsify the findings, and that Okafor was murdered for it."

Another silence. Then, closer now, figures moving at the top of the stairs—three of them, gaunt as fence posts, blinking against the daylight like creatures coming out of the earth. Because that's exactly what they were.

"They buried him well?" the woman asked.

"He buried him well," Bo said, stepping forward. "Somebody did. With care. We figured it was his teammates."

A sound came down from the ledge that took me a second to identify, because it was halfway between a laugh and a sob. "Six months," the woman said. "Six months I've wondered if anyone would ever find that grave, or if Danny would just be a hole in the desert forever." She started down the stairs, one hand on the rock, moving carefully on legs that had forgotten how to be legs. "Elena Marsh. That's Ruiz and Calloway behind me. You're either the most foolish salvage crew in the history of the Basin, or you're the answer to a prayer I stopped believing in around month four."

"Both, probably," Wendell said. "Wendell Montrose. This is Junie, Bo, and Teo."

Teo lifted his drone controller in something between a wave and a salute. Marsh looked at it, then at him—at how young he was—and something crossed her face that I recognized. It's the look people get when they realize the world kept turning while they were underground.

"You're children," she said.

"We're crew," Teo said.

* * *

They fed us first. That's the thing nobody tells you about people who've survived six months in a cave: hospitality comes before everything, because hospitality is the last proof you're still human. They had a fire going in a hollowed alcove near the stairs, vented through a crack in the canyon roof, and they cooked us a thin stew of cattail root and the trout that lived in the clear water—fish that had never seen the sun and came up pale as ghosts, blind-eyed, tasting of clean stone.

While we ate, they told us.

Ruiz talked first, because Ruiz was the kind of man who couldn't hold a story inside him any longer without it eating through. He was the team's geophysicist, big-boned and hollow-cheeked now, hands that wouldn't stop moving. "The contract said aquifer survey. Standard. Map the recharge zones, depth-sound the channel beds, file the report. Except the numbers started coming back wrong. Wrong *good*. The source wasn't a source—it was a window. What you're sitting next to is the crown of an unregistered aquifer that runs under four states. Four. Trillions of gallons, artesian pressure, self-recharging off snowmelt that shouldn't reach this drainage at all. There's a fault system acting like a pipe. It's the biggest freshwater discovery west of the Mississippi in a century, and it isn't on anybody's map because it was never supposed to be on anybody's map."

"So who's map is it on?" Wendell asked.

Calloway answered. She'd been quiet, a small sharp-eyed woman who kept glancing at the canyon mouth like six months hadn't cured her of being hunted. "Ours. Then theirs. The Meridian Fund commissioned a parallel review through a shell consultancy—we traced it. And when our data crossed their desk, the offers started. Sign-off bonuses. Consulting positions. For me, a research chair. All we had to do was reclassify the aquifer as geothermal contamination—unusable, unstable, not worth developing. Bury the finding inside bureaucratic language and let the Fund's partners buy the water rights from the state for pennies before anyone knew what was under them."

"And you said no."

"Elena said no," Ruiz said, with a tired smile at Marsh. "Loudly. In writing. The rest of us took about nine hours longer, which I'm not proud of, but we got there. We filed the true report through the internal channel. And then—" he spread his hands, "—our security clearance got revoked mid-expedition. Our satcom went dead. Our extraction date came and went. And Agent Reyes arrived with a jurisdictional order and a very reasonable suggestion that we hand over the drives and hike out to a debriefing that no one would ever hear from us again."

"Okafor didn't go," Bo said.

"Daniel saw the shape of it first," Marsh said. Her voice had gone very level, the way voices go when the alternative is shaking. "He said: if we go down that trail, we die separately. If we stay, we die together, or we don't die. So we staged the camp to look abandoned in a hurry—left the instruments running, left meals half-eaten, because we figured whoever came looking would read panic, not planning. And we came up here, into the one place the helicopters don't fly, because the canyon walls are too tight and the thermal updraft off the boil plays hell with their sensors." She almost smiled. "The thing that makes this place a miracle makes it a fortress. The river guards its own head."

Teo had gone still, which for Teo meant his mind was moving fast. "The instruments at the lower camp. They were still recording when we got there."

"Recording what we told them to record," Calloway said. "Ambient noise. Nothing incriminating. But the real drives—the real logs, the sonar mapping, the chemical assays, and the correspondence trail proving who tried to buy our silence—" she tapped her own temple, "—have been up here with us. Six months. Wrapped in waterproof bags, checked weekly, guarded like the ark."

"Why not send them out?" I asked. "Raft, runner, carrier pigeon—anything."

"Because anyone we sent would be caught, and the drives would be erased, and then we'd be loose ends with no evidence," Marsh said simply. "You don't mail a bomb. You wait for someone to carry it in person. And then—" she looked around our fire, at Wendell's sunburn, at Bo's bandaged forearm from the Narrows, at Teo, who was fifteen years old and had already been shot at twice this month, "—and then you hope whoever finds you has something we lost. Which is standing. And witnesses. And each other."

Nobody said anything for a moment. The hum of the rising water filled it, that great slow heartbeat under everything.

Then Wendell set down his bowl, and I watched him do the thing he does—the thing that drove me crazy for eleven days and that I finally understood right then. He went quiet, and inward, and alone, even in the middle of a crowd. Carrying the ledger in his head.

"There's a problem," he said. "And I'm the problem. The Meridian Fund holds my debt. Ames—their man—he's offered to void it if I bring him these drives. And as of three days ago, the debt extends to my crew. To all of you." He looked at each of us, and I'll give him this: he didn't flinch. "You should know that before you decide anything. You should know what I am to these people."

Marsh studied him for a long moment. "And what are you to these people, Mr. Montrose?"

"That's what I'm trying to figure out," he said.

Ruiz laughed—a short, disbelieving bark. "You came upriver past a federal containment line, past a murder cover-up, on a salvage contract from the same fund that tried to buy us, and you're telling us you might be working for them?"

"I'm telling you everybody starts somewhere," Wendell said. "I'm asking where I finish."

Bo passed the stew pot down the line, refilling bowls without being asked, the way he does, feeding people like it's medicine. "For what it's worth," he said, "he already chose. Back at the fork. Back in the shallows, when they had guns and we had paddles. Man doesn't get to keep re-deciding it every time it gets scary. That's not how choosing works."

Marsh looked at Bo, then at Wendell, and something in her face eased—the first real ease I'd seen on it, like a knot loosening after months. "Good," she said. "Because there's something you need to see. All of you. Downstairs."

"Downstairs?" Teo said.

"The aquifer doesn't just feed the river," Marsh said, standing, joints cracking like green wood. "There's a way in. The old stairs aren't decorative—people have known about this place a lot longer than the government has. Come on. And bring your lights." She paused at the top of the steps leading down behind the falls, and looked back at us, and the firelight made her face look carved. "What's below is the reason they killed Daniel. And it's the reason that in about forty-eight hours, when the dry season closes this canyon for good, whatever happens here is going to be final."

Behind us, far downstream, faint as a memory, we heard it: the thrum of helicopter blades, riding the wind up the canyon.

Reyes had found the river.

And the river, faithful and honest and rising out of the dark heart of the earth, had just led him straight to us.
