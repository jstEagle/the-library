## 17. Witnesses

**Elena**

Three hundred and eleven days in the dark, and the first thing that comes to rescue us is a debt collector.

I want you to sit with the absurdity of that for a moment, because I've had a year to sit with worse. We were eight of the best hydrologists, geologists, and field technicians the federal survey service ever wasted money on. We found the largest unregistered aquifer on the continent. And when we refused to bury it, they buried us instead—professionally speaking—and left us to die in a hole in the ground where the river is born.

So yes. When boots finally crunched down the gravel bar of the source chamber, I expected guns. What I got was a sunburned man with storm-chaser antennas on his truck forty miles downstream and a look on his face like he'd been expecting the end of the world and was mildly disappointed by the turnout.

"You're real," he said.

"Last time I checked," I said. "Though the definition gets flexible after ten months of cave water."

He laughed. It was a bad laugh—the kind people make when their body needs to do something before their brain catches up—but it was honest, and honest was the currency I'd stopped accepting years ago.

My name is Elena Marsh. I was the team lead on Survey Contract 44-C, Redwater Basin hydrological assessment. Officially, my team vanished on June third of last year. Unofficially, we ran. There's a difference between those two sentences, and the difference is about four hundred pages of data and one dead friend, and if you'll give me a few minutes, I'll explain why the difference matters more than anything else in this canyon.

* * *

**Wendell**

The survivors' camp was tucked into an alcove behind a curtain of flowstone, and it looked less like a camp than like the skeleton of a life somebody had tried very hard to keep alive. Solar panels scavenged from their own survey gear, angled at the one shaft of daylight that reached the chamber. Rain catchment rigged off a seep. Three bedrolls worn thin as paper. A wall covered in maps drawn by hand, over and over, the way you'd draw a face you were afraid of forgetting.

Three people stood up out of that alcove like ghosts being asked to prove it. A tall woman with gray coming in early at her temples—Marsh, obviously, she had command written into her posture the way Bo had medicine and Junie had water. A younger guy with a beard that had gone feral, holding a makeshift spear made from a tent pole and a survey stake, which told me everything about what they'd been afraid of. And a third, older man, sitting, favoring his leg, watching us with eyes that had done all his talking for months.

Teo made a sound beside me. Not a word. Just a sound, small and cracked, and Junie put a hand on his shoulder without looking away from the strangers.

"You're the salvage crew," Marsh said. Not a question.

"We're the crew," I said. "Salvage was the cover story."

"Whose?"

"Mine." I held up my hands. "Long version's ugly. Short version: a lender sent me down here to collect your equipment. The government sent some other people to collect something else. Neither of them mentioned you were alive."

The bearded one—his name turned out to be Okafor, and the spear never left his hand the whole first hour—made a noise through his teeth. "Of course they didn't."

* * *

**Elena**

Let me tell you how a survey team disappears on purpose.

In March of last year, our instruments started lying to us. That's what we thought at first—that the piezometers were miscalibrated, that the seismic array had a fault, because the numbers they returned were impossible. The Redwater Basin sits on sandstone and shale, mapped and remapped for sixty years. There is no aquifer under the Redwater headwaters. Except there was, and it wasn't sitting still. It moved. Pressure waves rolling through it like weather systems, vast and slow, feeding not just the Redwater but, by our modeling, three other major drainages across state lines. An ocean hiding under a desert, and every map on file said it couldn't exist.

We spent six weeks verifying. Six weeks of Okafor recalibrating until his hands shook, of Priya running tracer tests that came back the same impossible answer every time. It was real. It was bigger than any aquifer in the national inventory. And it was stable enough, drinkable enough, accessible enough to end water wars before they started—or to start them, depending on who held the paper.

That's when the directive came down. Not from our agency—from above it. Findings inconclusive. Instrumentation error suspected. Recommend continued assessment under private-sector partnership. Which is bureaucratic for *someone bought this before you finished finding it.*

Our contract liaison—a pleasant man named Voss, no relation to anyone worth knowing—delivered the revised reporting templates personally. Blank fields where our conclusions went. Pre-written language: *no significant unregistered resources identified.* He smiled the whole time. They always smile. I've met men since who don't smile, and I can tell you the smiling ones are worse.

We voted. Eight of us. Seven said no. The eighth—Denny Ruiz, our logistics chief—said yes, and then, two nights later, tried to take the data drives downriver alone to sell them back to the people who wanted them buried. Marcus caught him at the boats. There was a struggle. Denny had a knife.

I won't describe it. I've described it too many times, in my head, and each time it costs me something I don't get back. What I will tell you is that we buried him ourselves, at the top of the terrace, with cairn stones and his notebook, because whatever he became in the end, he was ours first. And then we burned our own extraction plan, sabotaged our own boats, walked our own footprints into the river, and let the world believe we were dead.

Because dead teams get searched for. Disavowed teams get erased. But dead teams leave evidence, and evidence was the only weapon we had left.

* * *

**Wendell**

She told it flat, Marsh did. No drama in it, no self-pity, just facts laid out like specimens on a tray, and somehow that made it hit harder than any sob story would have. Bo sat down halfway through, slow, like his legs had decided independently. Teo didn't sit. Teo stood there with his jaw tight and his fists in his pockets, and I knew that look—I'd seen it in mirrors across a decade of creditors' offices. The look of somebody learning that the rules were rigged before they ever agreed to play.

"The drives," Junie said. She'd been quiet the whole time, but I could see her working it. "The originals. Where are they?"

Marsh nodded at the alcove. "Waterproof cases, sealed, duplicated twice. One set stays here. One goes out with whoever's honest enough to carry it." Her eyes came around to me, and they weren't hostile, exactly. They were weighing. "Which raises the obvious question. Who are you people, and why should we hand the only copy of the most dangerous dataset in the country to a salvage contractor?"

Fair question. I gave it the fair answer.

"Because the people who want it buried hired me to fetch your gear, and when I stopped doing what they paid for, they doubled my debt and put my crew on the hook. Because a federal agent named Reyes burned our rafts yesterday and would've burned us if we'd been in them. And because—" I looked at my people. Bo with his arm still wrapped from the shallows. Junie with river mud dried white up both shins. Teo, seventeen years old, standing in a cathedral cavern at the end of the earth because he'd voted for truth over survival. "Because nobody sane would be here. So either we're the worst liars the Fund ever hired, or we're what's left."

Okafor snorted. "That's not a résumé."

"No," I said. "It's a confession. Best I've got."

* * *

**Elena**

Here is what waiting does to you. It doesn't make you patient. Patience implies calm. Waiting makes you precise. You learn exactly how much water a person needs. Exactly how many calories you can cut before judgment slips. Exactly how long you can watch the light move across a stone ceiling before you start talking to it.

For the first month, we waited to be rescued. For the second, we waited to be killed. By the third, we understood nobody was coming for either purpose, and we started waiting for something else—for a variable. Any variable. A flood season that didn't come on schedule. A helicopter that flew wrong. Footprints.

Ten months. Then, three days ago, instruments we'd kept running on trickle power picked up boat traffic below the narrows. Then gunfire, faint, upstream of the second cache. Then nothing. And then, today, five strangers walking up the gravel bar with a wounded man, a teenager, and the particular exhausted alertness of people who have been hunted long enough to stop flinching at it and start conserving.

I've spent my career reading landscapes. Aquifers, fault lines, drainage patterns—the truth of a place is always legible if you know the script. People are the same. Wendell Montrose read like a man whose ledger had been eating him alive for years and who had recently, abruptly, stopped balancing it. You could see the arithmetic going out of him and something else moving in. His navigator watched water the way I watch pressure gradients—which is to say, correctly. The medic checked our elder, Havel, first, before introductions even finished, professional habit overriding caution. And the boy—

The boy looked at our data wall the way I once looked at my first aquifer map. Like the world had just gotten bigger and he intended to hold it accountable for that.

"We need to go down," Montrose said. "Dry season closes the upper canyon in days. Maybe hours. Your Havel can't make that portage fast."

"He can make it," I said, "because he'll make it. That's how it works now."

Montrose studied me. "You've been down here almost a year. Why not try the run yourselves? You had boats once."

"Because whoever came for us would follow the same route, find the same camp, and connect the same dots," Okafor said. "We needed someone the Fund trusted enough to open the door. Someone whose disappearance wouldn't trigger the protocol."

"And instead," I said, "we got someone the Fund wants dead. Which is better. A door opened by the enemy stays open longer."

* * *

**Wendell**

We spent the rest of the light packing. It turns out three people surviving a year underground develop an efficiency that makes my crew look like tourists—every gram weighed, every case sealed, Havel's supplies distributed across five backs so no single loss could kill him. Marsh ran it like a drill, and Junie fell in beside her inside twenty minutes, the two of them talking in the shorthand of people who read terrain the same way, and I watched that happen with a feeling I didn't have a name for yet.

Bo re-dressed Havel's leg and pronounced it "a mess I can keep alive," which is Bo's version of optimism. Teo got the data drives squared away in his pack, triple-wrapped, and then stood at the edge of the pool where the river rose out of the dark—out of the aquifer itself, cold and impossibly clear, welling up through a fracture in the world like the planet bleeding honesty—and he just looked at it for a long time.

"It's real," he said finally. "All of it. It was real the whole time and they were going to say it wasn't."

"Yeah," I said.

"That's the part that—" He stopped. Shook his head. Seventeen years old, and already he'd learned the thing I'd taken thirty years to learn: that the lie isn't the worst part. The worst part is how ordinary the liars are.

Marsh came over as we loaded the last case. "One condition," she said. "When this goes out—it goes out everywhere. Every outlet, every authority, every archive. No deals, no exclusives, no leverage. The moment this becomes currency instead of testimony, we've lost."

"Agreed," I said, and meant it, and noticed—with a small internal shock—that meaning it cost me nothing. A year ago I'd have been calculating what an exclusive was worth. The ledger was quiet. I was starting to like the silence.

* * *

**Elena**

We broke camp at dusk, which felt right. Ten months of arriving in the dark; it seemed fitting to leave in it.

Havel walked on Bo's shoulder and mine, refusing the litter, stubborn as geology. Okafor carried the spear. Old habits. Ahead of us, the Montrose crew moved through the black water like they'd been born in it—the navigator calling depths from feel, the boy running his drone low and silent as a bat, the big medic steady as ballast. And Montrose himself at the point, watching the canyon walls, watching the sky, wearing the expression of a man who knows exactly what's chasing him and has decided, for better or worse, to stop running sideways.

I asked him once, on the gravel bar while the others rigged the boats, why he'd come upstream when every rational path led out.

"Debt," he said. Then he thought about it. "No. That's what got me here. Staying's different."

"What's staying?"

He looked back at his crew—at Junie arguing current lines with Okafor, at Teo laughing at something Bo muttered, tired, real laughter, the first I'd heard in this canyon since we'd arrived. He looked at them the way I used to look at my team, back when we were eight and whole.

"Turns out it's these guys," he said.

I understood him completely. I had lost my family to a knife in the dark and a decision made in an office I'd never see. He had found his in a truck and a storm and a string of bad contracts. Different roads, same destination: people who choose you are the only ones who count.

We pushed off into the current, eleven of us, carrying four hundred pages of truth and one dead man's notebook, and the river took us toward the darkening narrows like it had been waiting all along for someone honest enough to ride it down.

Behind us, the source closed over its own secret, patient as water, certain as morning.

Ahead—by the smell of dust on the wind and the drop in the river's voice—we had maybe a day before the dry season shut the upper canyon like a fist.

And somewhere in that fist, I'd have bet my life on it, Reyes was already waiting.
