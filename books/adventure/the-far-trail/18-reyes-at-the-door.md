## 18. Reyes at the Door

The dark in that cavern had a texture to it. Not the absence of light you get above ground, where your eyes adjust and the world comes back in grays. This was older than that. Thicker. The kind of dark that had been down there since before anything had a name for it, and it didn't appreciate visitors.

We'd been in the source chamber maybe six hours when Bo's hand closed on my shoulder in the black.

"Engines," he whispered.

I'd been half-dozing against a limestone shelf, Elena Marsh's voice still running through my head like a stuck record—*we refused, they disavowed us, we've been drinking cave water for four months waiting for someone honest*—and I'll admit my first thought was that the river itself had gotten loud. But no. Junie was already at the mouth of the passage where the water came in, head cocked, listening the way she listened to everything, like the world was a document she was translating.

"Two boats," she said. "Maybe three. Coming up the shallows."

Reyes. It could only be Reyes. Ames's collectors were on foot somewhere behind us, bruised and boatless after what happened in the blood shallows, and even Corliss Ames didn't have the budget for helicopters and assault boats both. No—the government fixers had circled back. They'd found our abandoned rafts, torn them apart looking for something, and now they'd followed the one route nobody was supposed to survive.

I stood up in the dark and felt around until my hand found Teo's shoulder. The kid flinched, then leaned into it. Four months of hiding in a hole in the earth will do that to a person; so will being seventeen and hunted.

"You got signal?" I asked him.

"Barely." His voice was steady, which surprised me. Two weeks ago he would've stammered. Something about nearly dying in the Narrows, or maybe about watching Bo take a bullet graze shielding him, had burned the stutter out of him. "One bar if I stand on the shelf by the seep. It comes and goes."

"How long to push everything?"

He didn't answer right away, and in the dark I could hear him doing math. Teo did his best thinking out loud, but this time he kept it inside, and when he spoke again his voice had gone quiet and certain, the way Junie's went when she read water.

"If I stage it all now—every drive, every scan, Marsh's notebook photos, the execution report—if I queue it all up, it's one button. Thirty seconds once I've got a solid connection. Maybe less."

"And if the connection drops mid-push?"

"Then it sends what it sends, and I keep sending until it's done."

Bo made a sound low in his chest that might have been a laugh. "Kid's got a plan with redundancy. Who taught you that?"

"You did," Teo said. "You always pack two tourniquets."

* * *

Here's the thing about standing in a cathedral of stone while men with guns come up the river toward you: there's nowhere to run, and once you accept that, it gets strangely peaceful. All the noise drains out. You're left with just the facts of yourself.

My facts were these: I owed more money than I could earn in three lifetimes to people who smiled while they ruined you. I had burned my own escape route—a canyon road, a fuel cache, a whole season's worth of exits—to keep the evidence moving upstream instead of me moving out. And I was standing in the dark next to four people who weren't family by blood or contract, plus three government surveyors who'd been left to die for telling the truth, and every single one of them was here because I said yes to a salvage job I should have walked away from.

If Reyes put a bullet in me in that cavern, the ledger would finally balance. That's a strange thing to feel calm about. I don't recommend it as a life philosophy, but I won't pretend it wasn't there, sitting in my chest like a smooth cold stone.

Junie found me in the blackness—I don't know how she does that, finds people—and pressed something into my hand. My headlamp. Dead battery, useless weight. A talisman, then.

"They'll bring lights," she murmured. "When they do, don't look at the beam. Look at the edges. That's where the mistakes are."

"That's storm advice."

"All advice is storm advice, Wendell."

Elena Marsh materialized beside us, moving with the economy of someone who'd spent four months learning every inch of this cave in the dark. Her voice was barely breath. "There's a high gallery above the chamber. Narrow climb. We put the kids up there—Teo with his equipment, and anyone else who can't fight."

"There's not going to be any fighting," I said.

Marsh was silent a moment. "You believe that?"

"I believe Reyes is methodical. Methodical people don't shoot witnesses in front of other witnesses unless they're sure no witness is walking out. So our job is to make sure she's never sure." I turned toward where I judged Teo to be. "Get up on that shelf. Get your bar of signal. And when I say go—or when things go bad enough that waiting gets us killed—you fire everything."

"What counts as bad enough?"

I thought about it. "Trust yourself. You've got better judgment than most adults I know. Including me."

"That's a low bar," Teo said, and I heard the grin in it, and God help me, I grinned too, there in the dark with the engines getting louder.

* * *

They came in with white light.

It poured into the chamber from the low tunnel where the river entered, six beams swinging in disciplined arcs, and behind the light came the shapes of men in tactical gear who moved like they'd rehearsed this in some concrete room a thousand miles from any real water. Last came a woman whose beam stayed pointed at the floor, because Agent Reyes didn't need light to find people. She'd already found us. She always knew where we were. That was the whole horror of her.

"Wendell Montrose." Her voice carried off the stone without echoing, flat and precise. "You've had a long walk. I'm offering you the short version of how this ends."

"We've met," I said. "At the camp. You ordered us out with impressive paperwork."

"The paperwork has improved." I heard her boots stop on wet stone. "You're standing inside a restricted hydrological site in possession of stolen federal property. Three of the people behind you are fugitives under active disavowal orders. One of them—" a pause, surgical, "—is a minor whose mother filed a custody petition in April. I can make that petition very simple for everyone involved, Mr. Montrose. Or very complicated."

And there it was. The old arithmetic, the one Ames used, the one the Fund ran on: find what a person loves, price it, threaten it. Everyone has a number. Everyone has a soft spot you can press until they hand over the drives.

What she didn't know—what none of them ever seemed to learn—was that I'd already spent my number. Back at the fork in the canyon, when a seventeen-year-old voted for truth and I watched my crew raise their hands one by one, I'd paid out everything I had. There was nothing left in the account for her to freeze.

"Here's my counteroffer," I said. "You walk out, you write your report honestly, and you tell whoever sent you that the story's already bigger than their ability to sit on it."

"Mr. Montrose—"

"No, let me finish, because I've been rehearsing this since the camp." My voice bounced around the chamber and I let it. "Your survey team found an unregistered aquifer feeding three states. Somebody in your chain of command decided to sell it quietly—water rights, private buyers, the biggest quiet land grab since the railroads. When your own people wouldn't falsify the findings, one of them ended up dead with a bullet in him and buried by his friends in a grave they dug with their hands. I've read the notebook. I've seen the photographs. And in about"—I checked a watch I couldn't see, purely for theater—"ninety seconds, every water authority, every news desk, and every congressional staffer with a pulse in this region gets a copy of all of it."

Silence. Then, softly, in the dark beyond her lights, Reyes said: "Where is the boy?"

That was the moment I understood we'd already lost her. Or won. With Reyes it was hard to tell the difference, and I suppose it still is.

* * *

She didn't wait for an answer. Of course she didn't. Her team started up the walls.

I'll give them this—they were good. Ropes went up the high gallery on the far side within two minutes, and beams began crawling across the ceiling like pale insects. Bo put himself between the nearest climber and the shelf where Teo crouched, arms spread, taking up space the way only a man built like a load-bearing wall can. Junie killed her lamp and vanished, which meant somewhere in that blackness she was doing something clever that I'd only understand later. Marsh and the other survivors pulled back into the deep passages they knew better than their own names.

And I stood in the middle of the floor with my dead headlamp in my hand and talked, because talking was the only weapon I had left, and I have never in my life been accused of underusing my voice.

"Thirty seconds," I called out. "That's all it takes, Agent. One bar of signal and thirty seconds. You can't climb fast enough to stop a upload that's already queued. Ask yourself—is whatever they're paying you worth being the agent who was standing right here when it went out?"

A beam swung and caught me full in the face, and through the glare I saw her, finally—close, unhurried, pistol held low and easy, eyes going past me to the shelf, calculating angles. Methodical to the last.

"Mr. Montrose," she said, almost gently, "do you know what happens to data that matters? It doesn't get deleted. It gets reclassified. Six months from now the aquifer exists, the deal exists, and the public record says a storm chaser with nine hundred thousand dollars in consumer debt fabricated evidence to extort his creditors. Nobody will remember your name except as a cautionary tale. I've read your file, Wendell. You're exactly the kind of man this happens to."

"Yeah," I said. "Probably. But here's the difference between you and me, and I want you to really hear this, because I've been working up to it for about four hundred miles."

I took a step toward her, into her light, which is either the bravest or stupidest thing I've done, and the competition for that title is fierce.

"You measure people by what they can lose. I've been measuring myself by what I owe. Both of those are just ways of counting, and counting was the whole problem. These people with me—" I swept my arm back at the darkness where my crew was, "—they're not assets. They're not liabilities. They're the first thing I ever chose, and chosen things don't show up on ledgers. So take your shot if that's the job. But know that the button's already been pressed in a seventeen-year-old's head, and the only question left is whether you want to be standing in this cave when history finds out what you were guarding."

For one long moment, nobody breathed.

Then Teo's voice came from the shelf above, young and clear and absolutely steady:

"It's done."

* * *

I want to tell you the chamber erupted, that there was cheering, that the agents dropped their weapons and wept. What actually happened was stranger and quieter.

Every beam in the place froze. Six men and one woman stood in a cathedral of stone, lit like a stage set, listening to a silence that had just changed shape. Somewhere above us, Teo's phone showed a progress bar completing—first the water authorities, then the news desks, then the archives, three redundant copies each, timestamped and hashed, because my kid plans with redundancy—and the information went out over that one bar of signal like water finding its level, which is a phrase Junie uses, and it fits.

Reyes lowered her pistol. Not surrendered—filed away. She looked at the ceiling of that cavern for a long moment, and I swear on my truck's rusted frame I saw something pass over her face that in a lesser woman would have been relief.

"Confirm," she said into her radio.

A pause. Static. Then a voice, tight and fast, reading back headlines that were already forming: wire services picking it up, a senator's office requesting comment, two state water boards convening emergency sessions, the words *Redwater Aquifer* trending in places that had never heard of the Redwater Basin before tonight.

Reyes clicked off her radio. She looked at me.

"Six months ago," she said, "I would have arrested every one of you and driven you out in zip ties, and the story would have died in a filing cabinet." She holstered the pistol. "Tonight I'd be the headline. My people don't become headlines, Mr. Montrose. That's the entire job description."

"So you're walking away."

"I'm withdrawing." She turned, and her beam swept across Bo, still planted like a wall; across Junie, reappearing out of the dark with mud on her hands and mischief in her eyes; across Teo on his shelf, phone raised like a lantern. "For the record, Montrose—because somebody in this mess should keep one—I believed the surveyors. From the beginning. I was told to bury their findings, and I told myself jurisdiction was a neutral word." Her jaw tightened. "It isn't. Nothing is."

She snapped two fingers and her team started down the ropes, folding up their climbing gear with that same terrible discipline, packing the operation away like it had never existed.

At the tunnel mouth she paused, her light pointing downriver, toward the long dark miles between us and the outside world.

"Dry season closes the upper canyon in four days," she said. "After that, nothing gets out—not boats, not bodies, not stories. If I were you, I'd already be moving." A beat. "The injured ones especially. They'll slow you down. Move anyway."

And then the lights went down the tunnel and out, and the river took the sound of them, and we stood alone in the source chamber with three rescued surveyors, one uploaded truth, and four days.

Junie found my hand in the dark. Squeezed it once.

"Four days," she said. "Downriver with wounded passengers, low water, and collectors somewhere behind us who don't work for the government and don't care about headlines."

"One crisis at a time," I said.

"Wendell." Her voice was gentle, which from Junie meant serious. "That's never once been true."

She was right, of course. She usually is. Above us, Teo's phone buzzed with the first incoming message—the outside world answering back—and I looked up at the kid silhouetted on his shelf against the faint glow of the screen, and I thought: four days, a hundred miles of dying river, and Bo bleeding slow.

Time to see what this family could carry.
