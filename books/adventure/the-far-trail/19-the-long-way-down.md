## 19. The Long Way Down

The morning the story broke, I was standing waist-deep in the shallows below the source cavern, rinsing Bo's bandages in water so cold it felt like it had opinions about me personally.

Teo's voice came echoing down from the ledge above—cracking, then steadying, then cracking again. He'd been up there since before dawn with the satellite phone and the survey team's backup drives, reading coordinates off a scrap of paper Elena Marsh had written out by hand, because some things you don't trust to a screen. Every news outlet in three states. Every water authority. The Bureau's own public tip line, which was either poetry or spite, depending on how you looked at it.

"Junie!" he shouted down. "It's live! It's actually—it's everywhere!"

Bo, propped against a boulder with her arm strapped to her chest and her color somewhere between bad and worse, managed a grin. "Kid's got a future in broadcasting."

"He's got a future in something," I said. "I'm not sure shouting at canyons is it."

But my hands were shaking on the bandage roll, and not from the cold.

Here's what nobody tells you about the moment everything changes: it doesn't feel like triumph. It feels like holding your breath for eleven days and finally letting it out all at once, except your body doesn't know how anymore, so you just stand there in a river gulping air like a landed fish while a teenager yells the good news off a cliff. The aquifer data was public. The execution evidence was public. The names of the men who'd signed off on selling three states' worth of hidden water were public. There was no version of this where Reyes's people could make us disappear quietly now. We were too loud. We were *everywhere*.

Wendell came down the talus slope two minutes later, moving fast, and I watched him do the math on our faces before he let himself believe it.

"It's real?" he said.

"It's real," I said. "Elena's name is already in the wire stories. 'Discredited hydrologist vindicated.' They're calling the aquifer the Redwater Reserve. Somebody at a hearing subcommittee is already demanding testimony."

"And Reyes?"

"Reyes," said a dry voice behind us, "is having a very bad morning."

Elena Marsh had come down to the water with the other two survivors—a quiet man named Okafor who hadn't spoken more than ten words in the day we'd known him, and a geologist named Pruitt whose hands still shook when he thought nobody was watching. Eleven months they'd lived in that cavern, drinking cave water and eating what the river gave them, watching the world decide they were dead or traitors or both. Elena looked like she'd been carved out of the canyon itself—all edges and patience—but her eyes had gone bright in a way that made me think of Junie-the-navigator reading flood lines on stone. Some things you just know how to read.

"Reyes is a professional," she said. "Professionals don't become headlines. He'll withdraw."

"You're sure?"

"I've known him for six years." She said it flatly, without explaining, and none of us asked. Everyone in this story had a history with somebody else in it; that was practically the admission fee. "He'll pull his team back to the basin boundary and start writing reports about how he was always investigating the Fund. Watch."

* * *

She was right, which annoyed me more than it should have.

By noon the helicopter came through the canyon—not hunting us this time, just passing, high and steady, headed downstream. Teo tracked it with the drone from a ridge until it shrank to a speck over the southern rim. Then the radio, which we'd stripped off one of Ames's abandoned boats, crackled with traffic: units pulling back, a perimeter dissolving, someone with an official-sounding callsign telling someone else to "stand down and preserve all materials for review." Preserve for review. The bureaucratic equivalent of whistling past a graveyard.

"They're leaving," Wendell said, like he didn't trust it.

"They're leaving," I confirmed.

"Nobody leaves. Not in this story. Every time we've turned around, somebody's been—"

"Wendell." I put my hand on his arm. He was thinner than when we'd started, all of us were, and there was a new gray in his beard that hadn't been there in chapter one of this disaster. "Look at the kid."

Teo was sitting on the ledge with the drone controller in his lap, watching the helicopter go, and he was crying. Not sobbing—just crying, quietly, the way you cry when something you'd stopped hoping for shows up anyway. Bo got up despite her shoulder and went and sat next to him without saying anything, which was more medicine than any paramedic could've prescribed.

Wendell watched them for a long moment. "Okay," he said finally. "Okay. So we get them out."

"We get them out," I agreed. "All of them. Downriver, before the dry season closes the upper canyon. Because here's the thing—" I pointed upstream, toward the narrows we'd nearly died in coming the other way. "That water's dropping. Fast. Three days, maybe four, and the upper canyon turns into a hike instead of a run. After that, anybody still up here walks out through forty miles of open rim country where every government satellite and every Fund drone can see them."

"So we run the river."

"We run the river. Inflatable salvage rafts, patched, overloaded, carrying three people who haven't walked more than a mile in a year." I shrugged. "Easy."

Wendell laughed—the first real laugh I'd heard out of him since the Meridian Fund entered our lives, and it sounded rusty, like a gate that hadn't swung in years. "You know what I keep thinking? Two weeks ago I'd have sold every one of you down the river for a clean ledger. Now I'm running rapids for free."

"For free," Bo called over. "Hear that, Teo? We're volunteers now."

"Speak for yourself," Teo said, wiping his face. "I'm charging Elena by the mile."

* * *

We spent the afternoon turning salvage into escape.

Ames's collectors had left three boats behind when Bo scattered them in the shallows—one holed, two sound—and we spent the hot hours patching, lashing, and loading. Elena directed the packing of the survivors' few possessions with the economy of someone who'd learned to carry a life in both hands. Okafor turned out to be good with repairs, working silently alongside Bo, and Pruitt knew the river's lower reaches from the survey, drawing us a map in charcoal on a strip of tent fabric: rapids marked, portages flagged, a spring where the water ran clean even in drought.

"The dry season's your friend now," Elena told me as we worked. "Lower water means fewer hydraulics in the middle canyon. You'll scrape more than you'll flip."

"You say that like scraping isn't its own religion."

"I say it like I've read your file, navigator." She almost smiled. "Junie Voss. Reads water like text. That's why we signaled your crew, back at the camp. Of all the salvage outfits the Fund might have sent, we needed one that would notice the instruments were still recording."

I stopped with a strap half-ratcheted. "You *signaled* us."

"Torn tent seam. Boot prints that circled but never left. A meal left half-eaten in a pattern no animal makes." She looked at me steadily. "We couldn't leave. Reyes had the rim. But we could leave a message for anyone careful enough to read it. I've been waiting eleven months for someone to read it."

I thought about that first morning at the abandoned camp, the uncanny hush of it, the wrongness I'd felt in my gut and couldn't name. She'd written that whole scene. For us. For whoever came.

"You could've just left a note," I said.

"A note gets found by the wrong people first." Her mouth twitched. "Water reads true. People lie. I figured whoever found the camp would have to be someone who trusted the water."

I decided right then that if Elena Marsh ever wanted to join a ragtag crew of river rats and storm chasers, we'd carry her gear for free.

* * *

We launched at first light.

Three rafts, seven people, and everything we owned in the world stacked and strapped down. Wendell took the lead boat with Elena, who knew the water even if her body had forgotten how hard it worked. Bo rode center raft with Teo on the oars and Okafor keeping watch on her color. I had the sweep boat with Pruitt, whose hands had steadied the moment they touched the frame—some people are just calmer holding a rope than holding nothing.

The upper canyon let us go grudgingly.

The first rapid was a long boulder garden where the dropped water had turned what would've been a flush-and-pray chute into a technical slalom, and we threaded it slow, walking each raft down by rope through the worst of it. Elena called lines from memory and Wendell called corrections from instinct, and twice they disagreed and twice the water proved Elena right, and Wendell took it with a grace that would've been unthinkable two weeks ago.

At the second big drop, the one the survey map had named *Sawtooth*, we scouted from the rocks and found the channel choked with fresh debris—deadfall the receding water had deposited overnight. We portaged the loads around it and lined the empty rafts through, everyone hauling on ropes in the heat, sweat and dust and Okafor laughing for the first time, a short surprised bark of a laugh when a raft clipped his hip and soaked him to the waist.

"Sorry," I called.

"Don't be," he said. "First bath in months."

By midday we were through the worst of the upper section, and the canyon changed character around us—walls dropping from sheer red monoliths to stepped terraces, the river widening, slowing, going from combat to conversation. The dry season was closing in behind us exactly like I'd predicted: side canyons that had dripped two weeks ago now stood silent, seeps gone to mineral stain, the river itself a handspan lower every time we stopped.

But it was letting us through. That was the thing about rivers—they don't care about your deadlines, but they'll give you a window if you read them right. And for once, everybody in my life was reading the same page.

* * *

We camped that night on a gravel bar under a wall of banded sandstone, and Elena built a fire small enough to hide and large enough to matter.

Pruitt slept early, exhausted clean through. Okafor sat with Bo, re-taping her shoulder with a gentleness that surprised everyone including Bo. Teo had the drone up on a low orbit, mostly because he didn't know how to be idle anymore, and the little machine's lights blinked patient circles above us like a tame star.

Wendell and Elena sat apart, talking low. I caught fragments. Testimony. Subpoenas. Names. The machinery of justice grinding into motion downstream of us, slow and loud, doing in committee rooms what we'd done in canyons. Elena was telling him things about the Meridian Fund's structure—shell companies, the water deal's financing—that made Wendell go very still, and I saw him filing it all away behind his eyes with the look of a man rewriting a ledger.

I went and sat with Teo instead.

"Hey, navigator." He didn't look away from the controller. "You think they'll really leave us alone now?"

"Reyes will. Reyes has self-preservation." I nodded upstream, into the dark where the canyon walls swallowed the last light. "Corliss Ames is another question. Ames doesn't scare. Ames recalculates."

"You think he'll come after us?"

"I think Ames is somewhere right now realizing that the debt he holds is worthless, the collateral is worthless, and the only asset left on his books is information about the people who burned him." I bumped his shoulder with mine. "Which means we stay sharp. But it also means, for the first time since I met that man, we're ahead of him."

Teo was quiet a moment. "When we get out. Do you think... they'll let me stay with you guys? For the custody stuff. My mom's lawyer called the sat phone today, before the battery died. She said—" His voice did the cracking thing. "She said the judge wants to hear from the people I've been living with. Like, officially."

I looked at this kid—sixteen, flying drones through government blockades, broadcasting state secrets, saving evidence that three states would fight over—and felt something in my chest go tight and fierce.

"Then they're going to hear," I said, "about the bravest person on this river. And about the woman who kept him fed, and the idiot who taught him to read clouds, and the navigator who yelled at him about battery discipline approximately four hundred times."

"That was mostly fair."

"It was entirely fair. Batteries are life, Teo."

He laughed, wet and real, and leaned his head briefly against my shoulder the way he used to when he first came aboard and thought nobody noticed. I let him. Some cargo you don't log.

Upstream, far behind us now, the upper canyon was closing. I could feel it the way you feel a door shutting in another room—a change in pressure, a finality. The dry season was sealing away the source, the cavern, the graves, the whole impossible secret, folding it back into stone until the hearings and the subpoenas pried it open again in the daylight where it belonged.

We'd carried the truth out. Now the river was carrying us.

* * *

The third day, the canyon opened its hand.

The walls fell away into broken country of mesas and washes, the river braiding wide and shallow over gravel, and suddenly we could see the horizon again—real horizon, weather-horizon, the kind Wendell had been starving for since we left the plains. He stood up in the lead raft so fast it rocked, staring east, where a thunderhead was building over the high ground with that green-black architecture he loved more than most people.

"Look at that," he breathed. "Look at *that*. Supercell. Right-mover. Look at the anvil on it—"

"Wendell." Bo, from the center raft. "Focus."

"I'm focused. I'm focused on the fact that the sky is doing something *interesting* for the first time in a month." But he sat down, grinning, and I realized I was grinning too, and so was Teo, and even Okafor looked faintly amused, and that was the moment I understood we were going to make it. Not because the river was easy—it wasn't, we scraped and swore and portaged two more times—but because a crew that can laugh at a man falling in love with a thunderhead mid-escape is a crew that has stopped being afraid.

Near dusk we passed the ruins of an old homestead on the north bank, foundation stones and a rusted windmill, and Elena asked Wendell to pull over.

She stood among the stones for a while, saying nothing. Then she took something from her pocket—a small brass instrument, a compass, worn smooth—and set it on the largest stone, facing upstream. Toward the source. Toward the fourth member of her team who hadn't made it, the one buried with care in the high country, the notebook in whose pages we'd found the words that started all of this.

"Hayes carried this," she said, when she came back to the raft. "Twenty-two years in the field. He'd have wanted it to end looking at water."

Nobody spoke for a while after that. The river talked enough for all of us.

* * *

On the fifth morning, we reached the take-out.

A dirt ramp at the edge of the closed basin boundary, a gravel lot, a single vehicle waiting—and I want it noted that every one of us tensed at the sight of that vehicle, hands drifting toward whatever passed for weapons in our inventory, before we saw the press stickers on its doors and the tired-looking reporter leaning against the fender with a thermos.

"You're them," she said. "You're the salvage crew."

"We're them," Wendell said warily.

"I've been driving out here every day for three days." She straightened up. "Ma'am—" this to Elena, and her voice actually shook— "the subcommittee counsel wants you in Phoenix by Thursday. There's a plane. There's lawyers. There's—" she gestured vaguely at all of us, at the rafts, at the whole improbable convoy— "there's people who want to buy you breakfast."

Bo looked at me. I looked at Wendell. Wendell looked at the sky, where the supercell from yesterday had matured into a full-blown storm system marching across the eastern plains, lightning flickering inside it like a thought trying to form.

"Eat first," he said. "Decide after."

And that was when I saw the second vehicle, coming up the access road in a long dust plume—dark, official, unhurried. It stopped a hundred yards out. A door opened.

The man who stepped out wore a cheap suit and an expensive smile, and even at a hundred yards, even dusty and diminished, I knew the walk.

Corliss Ames had come to see the wreckage of his investment in person.

He raised one hand—not a wave, exactly. An acknowledgment. A man paying his respects to a funeral he'd attended from the wrong side.

"Well," Bo said quietly, cracking the knuckles of her one good arm. "Looks like breakfast is going to wait."
