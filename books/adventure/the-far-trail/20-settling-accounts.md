## 20. Settling Accounts

Corliss Ames was waiting for me at the mouth of the canyon, sitting on a folding camp stool like a man at a golf tournament, and I have to hand it to him: the man understood theater.

He'd come alone—or alone in the way that mattered, which is to say no visible guns, just two of his collectors leaning against an ATV fifty yards back with the studied boredom of men who wanted you to know they were bored. The Meridian Fund never sent muscle first. They sent smiles. Muscle was the encore.

"Montrose." He stood when I got close, brushed dust off his trousers—khakis, out here, in the Redwater Basin, because Corliss Ames would dress for a boardroom on the floor of hell—and spread his hands like he was welcoming me home. "You look terrible."

"I've been told it's my best angle."

"You look like a man who's been living out of a dry bag for three weeks." His eyes did a quick inventory over my shoulder—the crew hanging back near our battered rafts, Bo's arm in a sling, Teo holding the satellite phone like it might bite, Junie watching Ames the way she watched water: reading him, finding the current underneath. "And where are the drives, Wendell? That's really the only question I drove all this way to ask."

The canyon walls went up sheer on either side of us, red stone going amber as the afternoon leaned west. Somewhere above us, a raven said something rude. I thought about how many times I'd sat across from this man—in a diner booth in Amarillo, in the Fund's glass office with its inspirational posters about *opportunity*, once in the cab of my truck while he ate sunflower seeds and explained, cheerfully, what happens to people who miss payments. Every time, I'd been the one sweating. Every time, I'd been doing math in my head, trying to find a door.

I wasn't doing math anymore. That was the strange part. Three weeks of river and blood and a dead surveyor buried under cairn stones had burned the arithmetic right out of me.

"Before we get into that," I said, "you should probably check your phone."

Ames's smile flickered—not much, just at the corners, the way ice shifts before it cracks. "Wendell."

"Humor me."

He held my gaze for a long moment, then took out his phone with the unhurried grace of a man who refused to be rushed even by his own curiosity. He looked down. I watched his face while he read, and it was one of the better shows I've seen this year.

The story had broken six hours earlier. Teo hadn't just sent the aquifer data to news outlets—he'd sent everything. The falsified reports the survey team had refused to sign. The internal memos from the water consortium. And threaded through all of it, neat as a vein of gold through quartz, were the Meridian Fund's own documents: the loans structured to buy influence, the salvage contract written to bury evidence instead of recover it, the payments routed through shell companies that traced back, hop by hop, to the same men who'd planned to sell three states' drinking water in secret. Teo had cross-referenced it all against public filings at two in the morning by flashlight, running on adrenaline and Bo's painkillers, and the kid had built something beautiful. A map. Everything connected, everything named.

The hearings were already being scheduled. Two senators had used the word *criminal* on camera. One of them had used it twice.

Ames read for maybe ninety seconds. Then he put the phone away, and when he looked up, the smile was still there—but it had gone thin, stretched over something else, like paint over rot.

"Well," he said. "That's unfortunate timing."

"Yeah," I said. "Real shame."

"You understand," he went on, and here he was, the professional, rallying, "that none of that changes your contractual obligations. The debt stands. The Fund's exposure is a legal matter between the Fund and—other parties. Your signature, however, is between you and me." He gestured pleasantly at the canyon around us. "And I've driven a very long way, Wendell. So let's not make this tedious. The drives. Where are they?"

"They're everywhere," I said. "That's the thing about data, Corliss. It's not a truck you can repossess. It's not gear sitting in a warehouse you can lien. Copies exist in places neither of us will ever find them all. You're standing in the middle of a canyon asking me to hand you smoke."

His jaw tightened, a small mechanical motion, like a man adjusting a cufflink. "Then I'll take the originals. As collateral. As a gesture of good faith while things—settle."

"The originals are with three federal agencies and a newspaper," I said. "Want their addresses?"

For the first time since I'd known him, Corliss Ames didn't have anything to say. He looked past me at the rafts, at the crew, at the canyon, and I could see him running numbers of his own now—the kind he'd spent his whole career making other people run. What's it cost to press? What's it worth? Is there a door?

There wasn't one. I knew exactly how that felt. I'd lived there for years.

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about debt: it isn't the money. Money is just the shape of it, the number on the statement, the figure Ames used to quote me over diner coffee with that warm salesman's cadence—*forty-one thousand, Wendell, and honestly you're getting favorable terms*. The debt itself was something else. It was the way I checked my phone with my stomach clenched. It was the contracts I took because I couldn't afford to be picky, chasing hail cores and tornado damage surveys for insurers who paid late and argued about every hour. It was the way I measured my life in miles because miles were the only unit that didn't owe anybody anything. A mile doesn't compound. A mile doesn't call you at dinner with a cheerful voice and new terms.

I'd been running from a number for four years. And the whole time, the number had been sitting in a nice office wearing a good suit, telling himself he was a businessman, telling himself everybody signs, everybody knows the terms, nobody made Wendell Montrose borrow against his truck.

Standing there in the canyon with nothing left to seize—that was the first morning in four years my stomach didn't hurt.

Because here's the arithmetic Ames never understood: he'd built the whole machine on the idea that I needed something from him. Forgiveness. Patience. One more extension. A man who needs something can be squeezed, and a man who can be squeezed will always, eventually, pay. But I'd walked into that basin carrying the last thing I owned of any value—my time, my crew, my name—and I'd spent all of it. On purpose. On the truth, and on the people standing behind me who'd bled for it.

You can't squeeze a man who's already given everything away. There's nothing left to hold. I was, for the first time in my adult life, completely uncollectable.

It felt like the ten seconds after a tornado passes. That ringing quiet. The air scrubbed clean.

* * *

"You should go," Junie said.

She'd come up beside me without a sound, the way she did everything, and now she stood with her arms crossed, looking at Ames with an expression I'd last seen her aim at a flash flood. "Water's dropping fast," she went on, conversational, almost friendly. "Dry season closes the lower canyon in about nine days. After that, the only road out of this basin is the north track, and it's forty miles of the worst washboard you've ever seen. If I were leaving, I'd leave today."

Ames turned the smile on her, recalibrating. "Juniper Vane. The navigator. I've read your file too."

"I'd be disappointed if you hadn't."

"Your file says you have a talent for finding routes other people miss." He tilted his head. "It also says you've got a sister in Flagstaff you haven't called in two years. Funny thing, family. People think it's permanent. It's more like a subscription. Lapse long enough—" He shrugged, spreading his hands. "Anyway. I'm sure the Fund would be happy to help you reconnect. We're very good at finding people."

The temperature of the moment dropped about ten degrees. Behind me, I heard Bo shift his weight, the leather of his sling creaking. Teo stepped forward until he was level with my shoulder, sixteen years old and glaring at a man twice his age with the pure unfiltered fury only teenagers and saints can sustain.

But I found I wasn't angry. That surprised me most of all. Ames was reaching for levers because levers were all he had, and every lever he touched came up empty. Junie's sister had already gotten a call—from Elena Marsh, actually, three days ago, from the source cavern, using the satellite phone, because survivors tell their families the truth before anyone can weaponize it against them. Bo's suspended license was about to become a reinstatement hearing with half the state's news media asking why a paramedic who pulled a boy out of whitewater was ever suspended at all. Teo's custody case had lawyers calling *him* now. Ames was flipping through a catalog of threats that had all been recalled by the manufacturer.

"Corliss," I said. "Look around. Really look. This basin is about to be the biggest water story in thirty years. Every reporter in the Southwest is going to be driving these roads inside a month. Congressional staff. Investigators. Cameras." I nodded at the canyon walls, the river running low and clear over its red stones. "Anything that happens to us out here, anything at all, lands on the front page with your name attached, because you're the one man from the Fund stupid enough to show up in person. You're not a collector anymore, Corliss. You're a liability. And I promise you, the people above you have already figured that out. Check your phone again in an hour. See whose job you still have."

Something moved behind his eyes then—not fear exactly, but its first cousin. The look of a man realizing the current has changed and he's been reading the old chart.

"You're bluffing," he said, but his voice had lost its warmth, gone flat and technical, and a flat voice is a confession. Bluffing was his game. He knew the sound of it from the inside.

"I'm a storm chaser," I said. "I don't bluff about weather. I watch it, I measure it, and I get out of the way before it takes me. You should try it sometime."

* * *

He didn't leave right away. Men like Ames never do; surrender isn't in their repertoire, so they perform departure instead, drawing it out, hunting for one last card. He walked the length of our camp with his hands clasped behind his back, inspecting the rafts, the salvaged instruments stacked in dry bags, the survey team's data crates we'd hauled out of the source cavern. He picked up one of the old recording units, turned it over, set it down with exaggerated care, like a museum visitor pretending he hadn't already decided the exhibit was worthless.

"Forty-one thousand dollars," he said finally, to no one in particular. "Plus accrued interest. Plus the recovery costs of this expedition, which the Fund fronted, which you'll note were substantial." He turned to face me. "You understand that legally—"

"Legally," I said, "your entire portfolio is about to be subpoenaed. Every loan, every shell company, every salvage contract. Including mine. You want to stand in front of a committee and explain why my debt to you should survive the scandal? Be my guest. Put it in writing. Make it official. Please."

I meant it, and he could hear that I meant it, and that was the whole end of it. The Fund's claim on me had only ever existed in the dark. Debt like theirs lives in shadow the way certain fish do—drag it into daylight and it dies. Forty-one thousand dollars, and the cheapest thing I could do with it was nail it to the front page.

Ames looked at me for a long moment. And then—this is the part I keep turning over, the part I'll probably still be turning over next year—he almost laughed. A short exhale through the nose, no humor in it, something closer to respect, or its fossil record.

"You know what your problem always was, Montrose?" he said, folding his camp stool with brisk little snaps. "You treated money like weather. Something that happened *to* you. Something you chased and dodged and measured but never actually handled." He tucked the stool under his arm. "Men who handle money own men who chase it. That's the whole equation. It's not complicated."

"It's not," I agreed. "But here's the part you never ran the numbers on. Weather wins. Every single time. Ask anybody who builds in a floodplain." I glanced back at my crew—Bo grinning despite the sling, Teo filming openly now because of course he was, Junie already studying the sky like it owed her a forecast. "You spent your career collecting from people in the floodplain, Corliss. Somebody finally opened the dam. Nothing personal. It's just hydrology."

He walked to the ATV. His two collectors climbed on behind him, and one of them—the big one, the one who'd once put a dent in my truck door as a friendly reminder—gave me a look that promised nothing in particular, which from a man in his position was practically an apology.

At the edge of the light, Ames paused. Didn't turn all the way around. Just enough that I caught his profile against the red rock.

"For what it's worth," he said, "the offer I made you at the fork—the personal out, the clean slate—you were the first one in eleven years to turn it down. Do you know how many take it?"

"No," I said. "How many?"

"All of them." He settled onto the ATV. "Every one. That's how I always knew it would work." The engine caught. "Take care of yourself, Wendell. Genuinely. You're bad collateral and worse business, but you're the only client I ever had who beat the house."

Then he was gone, dust rising off the north track in a thin brown plume, and the canyon closed behind him like water over a stone.

* * *

We didn't celebrate. I want to be honest about that, because some part of me had imagined it would feel like a movie—the cheering, the back-slapping, the triumphant music. Instead we stood around in the amber light feeling mostly tired, the way you do after a storm passes and you walk outside and start counting what's left.

Bo sat on a crate and flexed the fingers of his bandaged arm, testing the range. "So that's it?" he said. "Gone? Just like that?"

"Just like that," I said.

"Forty-one grand." He whistled low. "Man, I've seen people ruin their whole lives over a tenth of that. I've *treated* people who ruined their whole lives over a tenth of that." He shook his head slowly. "You just watched it evaporate and your pulse didn't even spike."

"It spiked," I said. "Just in a different place than usual."

Teo lowered his camera. He'd filmed the whole exchange, and I knew why—documentation had become reflex for all of us—but now he looked at me with an expression that was older than his years. "What he said about subscriptions," Teo said. "Family lapsing. That was supposed to scare us, right?"

"That was supposed to scare us," I confirmed.

"It didn't work."

"No," I said. "It didn't."

Junie had climbed onto the highest rock at the camp's edge, and she was looking south, down the canyon, toward the long drop toward home. When she spoke, she didn't turn around. "Nine days before the lower canyon closes," she said. "Three injured survivors, one wounded paramedic, forty-some miles of falling water, and a national news cycle that's going to send helicopters into airspace they shouldn't be in." She finally looked back at us, and she was smiling, that sharp bright smile she saved for rapids she intended to run anyway. "So. Who wants to go home the hard way?"

Everybody raised a hand. Even Bo, with his good arm.

I looked at them a moment longer than I needed to. Four years I'd measured my life in miles, because miles were honest—they didn't ask what you were worth, only how far you'd come. But standing there, broke for the first time since I was nineteen, with nothing to my name but a wrecked truck somewhere downstream, a boat full of borrowed gear, and three people who had chosen me over safety, over money, over sense—I realized the miles had only ever been the space between the people. The distance was never the point. They were.

"Load it up," I said. "We're burning daylight."

Downriver, the water ran low and silver toward the plains, and somewhere far off, past the rim of the world, thunder was building over country I hadn't seen yet.
