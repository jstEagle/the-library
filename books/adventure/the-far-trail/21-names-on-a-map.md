## 21. Names on a Map

The subpoena arrived in a manila envelope, which felt anticlimactic. I'd figured that when the federal government finally came for me, it would be helicopters and agents in windbreakers, not a seventeen-year-old kid in a mail truck who said "have a good one" like he meant it.

Junie made me sign for it anyway. "Chain of custody," she said, which is Junie for *I read the whole document before you were allowed to touch it.*

"It's not custody," I said. "It's testimony. There's a difference."

"Not to them there isn't."

She had a point. In the six weeks since we came down out of the Redwater Basin—six weeks since Teo's little broadcast went out to every news desk and water authority between the canyon and the coast—the government had been very interested in establishing exactly what belonged to whom. The aquifer, it turned out, belonged to everybody. The data proving it existed belonged to us. And the story of how we got it belonged, apparently, to a congressional subcommittee with a name so long they printed it on two lines.

*Subcommittee on Water Resources and Interstate Commerce, Hearing on Unauthorized Diversion and Suppression of Hydrological Findings, Redwater Basin.*

Bo read the summons twice and then read it a third time out loud, slowly, like she was checking it for traps. Old habit. You don't survive a decade running calls without learning that paperwork can hurt you worse than traffic ever will.

"They want us Thursday," she said. "All four of us. Plus—" she squinted at the last page, "—'any surviving members of the Redwater Survey Team as available.'"

"Elena's available," Wendell said from the kitchen doorway. He was holding a coffee mug he hadn't drunk from, which was how I knew his brain was somewhere else. It had been somewhere else a lot lately. Somewhere upstream, maybe. Or somewhere downstream, in an office where Corliss Ames was learning what it felt like to smile at someone who wasn't smiling back.

"She's testifying first," Junie said. "I checked the witness list. Marsh, Elena, lead hydrologist. Then Okafor, then Lindqvist. Then us." She tapped the paper. "We're listed as 'civilian witnesses.' Under 'other.'"

"Other," Bo repeated, tasting the word. "Six weeks ago these people wanted us erased off the map. Now we're an exhibit."

"Exhibits get protected," Junie said. "That's the whole point now."

I didn't say anything. I was thinking about my mom, who was going to be watching this on C-SPAN from her sister's couch in Tucson, and about the caseworker whose voicemail I'd been leaving messages on for a month, and about how all of this—the hearings, the honors, the nice men in suits saying *civilians played a crucial role*—was going to matter exactly as much as what one judge in one courtroom decided about where I got to sleep at night.

Wendell caught me doing the thing where I go quiet and stare at middle distance, and he crossed the room and put his hand on my shoulder, heavy as a spare tire.

"Hey," he said. "One thing at a time."

"That's what you always say."

"And has it been wrong yet?"

It had been wrong plenty of times. But I said no, because some lies are load-bearing.

* * *

The hearing room looked like a church designed by accountants. Wood paneling, brass rails, a dais raised up like an altar, and thirteen members of Congress arranged behind microphones in a shallow arc, most of them looking at their phones. Cameras everywhere. I counted nine before Bo told me to stop counting and breathe.

We sat behind Elena Marsh while she testified, and I want to tell you what that was like, because the TV version doesn't capture it.

On television, Elena Marsh looked composed. Steel spine, steady voice, the kind of witness they write movies about. What the cameras didn't show was her hands. She kept them flat on the table the whole time, fingers spread, pressing down like the table might float away if she let go. Six months in a cave will do that. Six months eating cave water and ration bars while the people who signed your paychecks decide whether you're a whistleblower or a casualty.

She told it straight. The survey found the aquifer—an unregistered reservoir under the basin big enough to water three states, cold and clean and impossibly deep. The contract came down from above: falsify the flow numbers, understate the yield, make the basin look worthless so it could be bought cheap through cutouts. Meridian Fund money. Names attached. When her team refused, they weren't fired—they were disavowed. Their extraction was cancelled. Their deaths were pre-written into the paperwork as a flash flood.

"One of us didn't get to refuse quietly," she said, and her voice did something I've never heard a voice do on camera—it stayed perfectly level while everything underneath it broke. "Dr. Harlan Reyes—no relation to the agent—was executed on the bank of the river on August ninth. We buried him ourselves. His notebook is in evidence. I would ask the committee to read page forty aloud, because I have read it every day for six months and I am tired of being the only one who knows it by heart."

They read page forty aloud. A senator's aide stood at a microphone and read the words of a dead man describing the deal to sell a river, and the hearing room went so quiet I could hear the cameras humming.

Beside me, Bo had gone very still. I knew why. She'd seen the grave. She'd knelt next to it in the dust and read the dates carved into a flat stone with a knife, and she'd been carrying that stone around inside her ever since. Now it was being entered into the Congressional Record, and even that couldn't put it down.

* * *

Our turn came in the afternoon, and I'll be honest: I'd rehearsed. I had note cards. Junie had drilled me over pancakes that morning—*they'll ask you leading questions, don't take the bait, answer the question asked, only the question asked.* Teo, the drone pilot, the kid who'd flown the broadcast that ended everything, giving measured testimony like a small adult.

Then a congressman from the state that shall remain generous but geographically confused leaned into his microphone and asked me, "Son, isn't it true that you committed multiple federal violations entering a closed basin, and that your evidence should therefore be considered fruit of a poisoned tree?"

And every note card in my head blew away like napkins in a storm.

"Yes, sir," I said. "Probably. I mean—I didn't check the closure order, if that's what you're asking. I was sixteen and I was following a guy who measures his life in miles instead of years, and a woman who reads rivers better than the Army Corps does, and a paramedic who wasn't allowed to be a paramedic anymore, and none of us checked. That's kind of our whole thing. We're not careful people. We're just—we were the ones who were there."

Somebody in the gallery laughed. Not mean. The congressman did not laugh.

"So you're telling this committee the integrity of your evidence rests on luck."

"No, sir. I'm telling you it rests on three surveyors who hid in a cave for six months rather than sign their names to a lie, and on a dead man's notebook, and on instruments that recorded what they recorded whether or not the people reading them were trespassing. The river doesn't care who's holding the gauge. That's the whole point of data. It's true even when the truth is inconvenient. Especially then."

There was a pause where I could hear my own heartbeat, and then the chairwoman—a gray-haired woman who'd spent the whole morning looking bored—said, "For the record, the witness is seventeen and has already shown more respect for this process than several of its members. Continue."

Bo testified after me and was magnificent, which surprised nobody who knew her. Asked about the standoff at the shallows, about the blood, about the collectors' boats, she gave it to them plain: "I've been a paramedic eleven years. I know what it looks like when people are willing to hurt somebody. Those men were paid to be willing. We weren't paid at all. Draw whatever conclusion you're supposed to draw from that."

Junie, asked how she navigated a closed canyon system at night during dry season, said, "The same way the survey team did. By reading the water." Then she declined to elaborate, and something in her face made the congressman decide not to insist.

And Wendell—Wendell, who owed more money than I'll ever understand to people who smiled while they collected—got asked the question I'd been dreading for him: whether he'd have taken the salvage job if he'd known what it was.

He thought about it for real. You could see him doing it, turning it over, refusing the easy answer.

"No," he said finally. "If I'd known, I'd have been too scared to do it right. I took it because I was broke and cornered and it looked like a door. Turns out it was a door. Just not the one anybody built it to be." He glanced back at us, quick, and then forward again. "You want to know what I recovered? Equipment. You want to know what I actually recovered? Three people everyone powerful had decided were already dead. Everything else is paperwork."

* * *

The custody hearing was two days later, in a county courthouse that smelled like floor wax and old coffee, before Judge Okafor—no relation to the surveyor, though when she learned the name overlap she raised one eyebrow like the universe was showing off.

My mother was there. That's the part I still can't untangle. She sat on the other side of the aisle in a blouse I'd never seen, hands folded, and when our eyes met she did the small wave she used to do when I was little and she'd drop me at school. Like nothing had happened. Like the last two years of disappearing acts and revolving doors and the boyfriend with the temper hadn't happened either.

The state's position was reunification. My mother had completed her program, sixty days clean, stable housing, a sponsor. All true. All verifiable. I sat there listening to the caseworker list these facts like items on a receipt and felt the ground doing that thing it does—not moving, exactly, just reminding you it could.

Then Judge Okafor said, "I understand the respondent has character witnesses."

And my crew stood up.

Not smoothly. Bo bumped the bench, Junie stepped on Wendell's foot, and I'm pretty sure Wendell was wearing the same shirt from the hearing because it was the only one without a stain. They filed up to the stand one at a time looking like exactly what they were—storm chasers, ex-paramedics, runaways, strays—and swore to tell the truth.

Bo talked about pulling me out of the Narrows, about how I'd held the radio dry and kept transmitting coordinates with shaking hands until help reached the others. "I've triaged a lot of people," she said. "I know what panic looks like and I know what steadiness looks like. That kid is steady. Somebody taught him, and it wasn't circumstance."

Junie talked about the portage, ninety miles of heat and dust, and how I flew the drone ahead every morning to map the route before anyone carried anything. "He never once complained. He mapped escape routes for all of us before he mapped one for himself. A child who does that has already decided who he wants to be. The question in front of you is who gets to be near him while he becomes it."

Wendell went last, and he didn't perform. He just sat there in his stained shirt with his big weathered hands around the edge of the witness box and said:

"I'm not going to pretend I'm a model guardian. I owe money to people I shouldn't, I drive too fast, and until about two months ago I measured my whole life in miles because miles don't ask anything of you. This kid showed up with a drone and a chip on his shoulder and nowhere to go, and somewhere between a flash flood and a gunfight in the shallows he became mine. Not legally. Just true." He looked at me then, in front of the judge and my mother and God and the county recorder, and said, "I'm not asking you to give him to me. I'm asking you to look at who shows up for him. Because that's the only definition of family I've ever seen hold water."

My mother cried. I want to report that fairly. Whatever else is true about her, she cried, and I believed some of it.

Judge Okafor took off her glasses and looked at me directly, which judges aren't supposed to do but did anyway.

"Teo," she said. "You're seventeen. In ten months you can petition on your own behalf. So I'm going to ask you something I'm not supposed to ask, and your answer will carry weight. Where do you want to be?"

Every answer I'd rehearsed evaporated. What came out was just true:

"With the people who came back for me. Every time. Even upstream."

The judge looked at my mother for a long moment—not unkindly, but completely—and then at the caseworker, and then she wrote something down for a long time.

"Reunification services are terminated," she said finally. "Guardianship is transferred to Mr. Montrose, pending standard home study, which I suspect will find a house full of maps and smell like burnt coffee and pass without incident." She looked up, and for half a second the judge fell away and there was just a tired woman who'd seen a thousand broken arrangements trying to hand-build a less broken one. "This court has spent two days reviewing what happened in the Redwater Basin. The record says a group of unrelated adults, none of them legally obligated to a single child among them, repeatedly chose each other at cost to themselves. I cannot manufacture that in a courtroom. I can only recognize it when it walks in. Guardianship granted."

Bo made a sound like she'd been shot, except happy. Junie grabbed my arm hard enough to bruise. And Wendell—Wendell put his hand over his face and stayed like that for a second, and when he took it away his eyes were wet and he pretended they weren't, and I let him pretend, because that's family too.

My mother hugged me outside on the courthouse steps. It was stiff and brief and smelled like her old perfume, and she said, "You turned out okay anyway," which was the closest thing to an apology she had in stock, and I took it, because you can carry a wound and accept an apology at the same time. Nobody tells you that. Both things fit in the same arms.

* * *

That night we ate dinner at the diner off Route 9 where the waitress now refused to let Wendell pay, on account of the *hero* thing, which he endured like a man being slowly smothered with a warm towel. Elena Marsh and her two survivors drove up from the city for it—Okafor the surveyor brought a cake that said CONGRATULATIONS TEOTL in icing, having misheard my name over the phone, and we decided collectively that Teotl was canon now.

Halfway through dessert, Elena set down her fork and said, "The committee announced the second round of hearings. Closed session. They're subpoenaing the Meridian Fund's internal records." She paused, and there was something in her face that wasn't satisfaction exactly. Something hungrier. "And Agent Reyes resigned this morning. Effective immediately. No statement."

The table went quiet.

"Resigned," Wendell repeated. "Or disappeared?"

"With that man, is there a difference?" Elena said. But she wasn't smiling. Neither was Junie, who had stopped chewing, staring at her phone where a news alert had just come through, and when she turned the screen around, the headline was simple and cold:

**FORMER REDWATER BASIN FIXER REYES UNACCOUNTED FOR; FUND RECORDS SUGGER THIRD PARTY STILL AT LARGE**

"Third party," Bo said slowly. "Meaning whoever hired the Fund in the first place."

"Meaning," Junie said, setting the phone down face-up between the plates like a card she didn't want to hold alone, "the people who wrote page forty are still out there. And they just lost their last layer of cover."

Wendell picked up his coffee, looked into it like it was a scrying pool, and said the thing he always says when the horizon goes green and the air goes still and every animal in the field starts walking the same direction:

"Well," he said. "One thing at a time."
