## 22. The Far Trail

The truck cost eleven thousand dollars and I'd have paid twenty. That's not sentiment talking—that's arithmetic, the kind I've spent my whole adult life getting wrong. Eleven thousand for a 2008 F-350 with a rebuilt transmission, a bed big enough to swallow a radar dome whole, and a previous owner who'd clearly loved it the way you love a dog: imperfectly, but with your whole chest. Bo found it on a lot outside Amarillo run by a man named Denny who talked like a man who'd never once been lied to, which in the used truck business is either sainthood or a head injury. I chose not to investigate.

"You know what we could've gotten for that money in Tulsa," Junie said, running her palm along the fender like she was taking its pulse.

"We're not in Tulsa."

"We could be."

"We're not." I popped the hood and looked at the engine the way I look at supercells—like something that could kill me but probably won't today. "This one's ours. No lien, no note, no fund with a smile and a calculator. Denny handed me the title and I put it in the glove box and nobody on earth has a claim on it."

Junie was quiet a second, then: "That's the most romantic thing you've ever said, and it was about a title."

"It's a very good title."

* * *

We loaded it over three days in Bo's sister's driveway in Lubbock, which is a sentence that would have meant nothing to me a year ago and now means almost everything. Bo's sister is named Carla and she pretends not to be delighted by company, which is a lie she tells with a casserole. Teo slept in the guest room. Junie slept on the couch and declared it superior to every bed she'd ever owned, which is a Junie way of saying thank you without having to say it. Bo slept in his old room like the intervening decade was a commercial break.

The gear went in first, because gear is the skeleton of a season. The radar dome—old, patched, held together partly by bolts and partly by Teo's stubbornness—went in the bed, strapped down with ratchets Bo tested twice because Bo tests everything twice, especially after a man bled out in a river shallows shielding a kid. The weather station. The anemometer. Junie's maps, which are paper, because Junie says paper can't lose signal, only dignity. The med kit, restocked, expanded, Bo adding things with a quiet intensity that made me glad and sad in the same breath.

Teo's drones went in a padded case that used to hold ammunition, which Teo found hilarious in a way that only a sixteen-year-old who's been shot at can find hilarious.

"You're grinning," I said.

"Reyes's guys checked every case we had," Teo said, snapping the latches. "Every single one. And the whole time the real evidence was a memory card in my sock." He patted the case. "Never trust a case. Trust a sock."

"That's going on a T-shirt."

"I'm having one made."

He was, too. I'd seen the mockup. It had a sock on it.

* * *

On the second day, a package came. No return address, but I knew the handwriting before I cut the tape—precise, unhurried, the handwriting of a woman who'd spent eight months writing in the dark by headlamp. Elena Marsh.

Inside was a framed photograph: the four surviving surveyors, taken on the courthouse steps the week the hearings opened, squinting into the sun like people relearning what daylight was for. On the back she'd written: *For the ones who came upstream. We measured everything in that basin except what mattered. You measured that instead. — E.*

I put it on the dash of the truck, wedged against the windshield, where it would ride with us. Junie saw it and didn't say anything, which from Junie is a eulogy and a christening both.

The hearings were still going. They'd be going for months—the aquifer, the deal, the falsified reports, the name of the surveyor they'd executed, Daniel Okafor, spoken aloud in a marble room by people who had never once gotten mud on their boots. Reyes had resigned before the first subpoena landed, which is what cold men do when the weather turns. Ames—

Ames was a different story, and I'll get to him.

* * *

The third day, the money showed up. Not a lot. A settlement, technically—Teo's footage, licensed by three networks and a documentary outfit, split four ways with a fifth carved out for Okafor's daughter, because Bo had insisted and Bo was right. My share came to a little over nine thousand dollars.

I sat in the cab of the truck with the deposit notification glowing on my phone and did the math I've done my whole life, the reflexive, hungry, frightened arithmetic of a man who measures everything in miles because miles can be outrun. Nine thousand. The Meridian Fund had claimed I owed them sixty-one. Then the Fund's ties to the water deal came out—the quiet investment vehicle, the shell company, the way Corliss Ames's cheerful loans had a habit of landing on people who'd seen things—and the debt didn't just become unenforceable. It became evidence.

I'd spent two years thinking of that number as a mountain. Turns out it was a paper mountain. You burn those.

* * *

Ames came to see me the day before we left. That's not a figure of speech. He walked up Carla's driveway at nine in the morning with two coffees and that smile, the one that had once made my stomach drop like a barometer in a hurricane, and he handed me a cup like we were old friends, which in a way I suppose we were—the way a man and his own worst habit are old friends.

"Wendell." He looked at the truck, the gear, the radar dome. "Going to make an honest living?"

"That's the plan."

"Storm chasing." He said it the way he said everything, warmly, as though the word were a gift he was wrapping for me. "No contracts. No retainers. Just you and the sky and hope."

"Hope and a rebuilt transmission."

"Mm." He sipped his coffee. "You know, I've been doing this a long time. Twenty years. And I'll tell you what I've learned: everybody's got a number. Everybody. The trick of my business is finding it. Yours was the easiest I ever found—debt and pride, the two great levers. I pulled them and you went exactly where I aimed you." He smiled wider. "Except upstream. That part I didn't aim."

"No," I said. "That part was Junie."

"Junie." He nodded like he was filing the name somewhere. "You know what I can't figure out, Wendell? You won. The debt's gone, the story's everywhere, you're a minor folk hero on two cable networks. And you're standing in a driveway in Lubbock loading a truck that smells like a dog. You didn't get rich. You didn't even get comfortable. So what exactly did you get?"

I thought about it honestly, because Ames deserved that much—he'd been the most honest dishonest man I ever met.

"You wouldn't believe me," I said.

"Try."

"I got the crew." I nodded at the house, where Teo was arguing with Bo about antenna placement and losing with tremendous volume, and where Junie stood in the doorway drinking coffee she'd made herself, watching us with the expression of a woman who trusted me to handle it and was enjoying watching me handle it. "I had a number, you were right. Sixty-one thousand. And you know what I figured out in that canyon? A number's just a way of not counting what counts. I spent my whole life running from a figure on a page, and the whole time the actual ledger—the real one—was riding in the truck with me. I just never added the column."

Ames looked at me for a long moment. And here's the thing I'll remember: the smile didn't drop, exactly. It just went thin. Went to its true weight. Under all that warmth was a man who genuinely did not understand what I was saying, and who would spend the rest of his career not understanding it, pulling levers on people who had nothing but numbers, and losing, every so often, to people who had something else.

"Well," he said finally. "It's been a pleasure doing business with you, Wendell."

"It hasn't," I said. "But it's been instructive."

He set the coffee down on the driveway, unopened, and walked to a gray sedan that had been idling at the curb the whole time, and he drove away, and I picked up the coffee and poured it out on the grass because I'm not a monster but I'm also not drinking his coffee.

Junie came down the steps. "That went well."

"Did it?"

"He poured his own coffee out." She looked at the wet spot. "Symbolically. That's growth, Wendell. That's a whole therapy arc in one driveway."

* * *

We left at dawn, because if you leave at dawn the whole day is road and the whole road is yours. Teo called shotgun and nobody fought him for it, which was its own kind of ceremony—there was a time when that seat was a negotiation, and the fact that it wasn't anymore told me more about us than any of the speeches I could give.

The radar dome rattled in the bed. Junie spread her maps across the back seat and traced the dry line with one finger, that invisible boundary where the desert air meets the gulf moisture, where the sky builds its cathedrals. "Cold front comes through Tuesday," she said. "Dry line sharpens Wednesday. Panhandle, maybe western Oklahoma." She glanced up. "Could be a big one."

"Could be," I said, and felt the old pull in my chest, the one that used to be the only thing in there besides dread. It's still there. It'll always be there. But it's got company now.

We drove north out of Lubbock with the windows down because the air conditioner made a sound Bo didn't trust, and the plains opened up around us the way they always do, flat and enormous and patient, a stage waiting for weather. Teo had his feet on the dash and his phone out, editing footage, occasionally laughing at something on it. Bo was asleep in about four minutes, because Bo can sleep anywhere now, the way soldiers do, and I understood that I'd never fully know what the river cost him and that he'd never want me to. Junie navigated without being asked, calling turns we both already knew, because that's what she does—reads water, reads roads, reads me.

Somewhere past Plainview the sky started doing something in the west. Nothing serious. Just a towering cumulus, building fat and white over the horizon like a thought a storm was having about itself. Teo spotted it first, because Teo always spots it first.

"Hey. Hey, look at that."

"Convection," Junie said, without looking up from her map, but she was smiling.

"Cap's gonna break," Teo said, with the authority of a kid who'd watched a supercell eat a canyon sky and lived. "I'm telling you. Wednesday. It's gonna be a Wednesday storm. Those are the best ones."

"Wednesday storms," Bo said, without opening his eyes, "are the ones that kill you."

"Wednesday storms," Junie said, "are the ones worth driving to."

I looked in the rearview at all three of them—Bo with his arms crossed and his mouth twitching, Junie with her finger on the dry line, Teo already angling his phone out the window to catch the cumulus building—and I did the thing I'd been practicing since the canyon, the new arithmetic, the one that doesn't come natural but comes truer.

I stopped counting miles.

I counted them instead: the kid who chose us in a canyon fork with his whole voice shaking and didn't shake when it mattered. The medic who bled in cold water so a sixteen-year-old wouldn't have to. The navigator who read the river like a text and read me like a map and never once, not in two years of my lying about phone calls and hiding ledgers, gave up on what I might still be. Three names. That's the whole fortune. That's the entire account, and it's the first one in my life that's ever been in the black.

The storm built in the west, patient and enormous, and the road ran straight at it, because that's what roads on the plains do—they run at the weather, and you decide, every time, whether you're running toward it or away.

"Montrose," Junie said, from the back seat, using the name like a hand on my shoulder. "Where to?"

I checked the sky. I checked the mirrors. I checked the glove box, where a clean title sat with nobody's claim on it but mine.

"North," I said. "Wednesday's coming."

Teo whooped. Bo opened one eye. Junie folded her map along the dry line and settled in, and the truck rolled north into the wide gold light with the storm building on the horizon like a promise, and for the first time in my life I wasn't running from anything, and I wasn't chasing anything either.

I was just driving home, and home was riding with me, and the road went on ahead, open all the way to the edge of the sky.
