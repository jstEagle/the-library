## 1. The Map of Closed Lines

The freight out of Kessel Junction ran eleven cars and no passenger service, which suited Ulyana Thornbury fine. Passenger trains asked questions. Freight asked only that you stay clear of the couplings and keep your boots off the brake lines, and even then it mostly said so in rust.

They rode in the gap between a reefer car and a load of creosoted ties, back against cold iron, knees drawn up, the hand-inked map spread across their thighs like something holy or stolen. Both, maybe. The wind coming off the last cut before the viaduct smelled of coal smoke and river mud, and every few miles the whole train shuddered as if it were trying to remember how to be afraid.

Ulyana held a pencil stub between two fingers and traced a line that had not carried a train in nineteen years: the old Vessmarki branch over Sable Ridge, abandoned when the annexation boards pulled up everything with a dead nation's name on the station signs. The line on the map was drawn in their own ink, but underneath it, faint as a watermark, was another line — older, finer, drawn by a steadier hand. Their father's hand. They had never once explained this to anyone, and they never would. That was the first rule. The second was simpler: never sleep twice in the same town. The two rules had kept them alive for eleven years, and alive was the only country they still held citizenship in.

The pencil moved. *Sable Ridge branch — trestle three gone, culvert washed, rails intact to milepost 14.* They wrote small, in a shorthand of their own devising, because a map like this one was worth more than money and cost more than blood. Every closed line in four provinces lived here: the flooded spurs, the stripped rights-of-way, the tunnels sealed with dynamite and paperwork. Some had been closed for good reasons. Others had been closed the way mouths get closed — quickly, and by people who preferred you didn't ask what went in.

Ulyana knew all of them. Not from books. From walking them, riding them, sleeping in their culverts when the weather turned mean. A railroad engineer who specialized in railroads that no longer existed was either a fool or a historian, and Ulyana had been paid too consistently for too long to be called a fool to their face.

The train slowed for the viaduct. Through the gap in the cars Ulyana could see the valley floor drop away, black and silver under a half moon, the Sorrel River somewhere down there running high and brown with spring melt, eating its banks the way it did every April. Eating bridges, too. Which was why, three days ago, a telegram had found them at a freight office in Kessel — telegrams always found them eventually; being hard to find was a profession, not a miracle — and why they were riding south instead of east tonight.

*THORNBURY. FLOOD DAMAGE RIVER SORREL LINE MILES 40 THRU 63. SURVEY AND COST ESTIMATE REQUIRED BEFORE JUNE. ENGINEER OF YOUR REPUTATION REQUESTED BY NAME. TERMS GENEROUS. REPLY HALLORAN & VANCE, CIVIL WORKS, MARROWFORD.*

*By name.* Somebody had gone looking. In Ulyana's experience, people who went looking usually wanted either your skills or your silence, and the price of both was higher than the fee.

The pencil hovered over the map. South of Kessel, following the Sorrel's course, lay a cluster of lines Ulyana had inked but never walked — closed early, closed quietly, closed within months of the annexation. Their father's faint underlines converged there like threads pulled toward a knot. He had been heading that direction the last time anyone heard from him, forty-one years ago now, though Ulyana had been six and hadn't known to listen. By the time they were old enough to ask questions, everyone who might have answered had learned to say *gone*, and nothing after it.

Gone along these same lines. That was the thing Ulyana never said aloud, not to clients, not to drinking companions in freight-yard bars, not to the occasional warm body who lasted a night and was gone by morning, which was how Ulyana preferred it. You didn't tell people that your entire career was a search grid drawn so slowly and so widely that it looked like work.

The train gathered itself and crossed the viaduct, iron singing under the wheels in a long minor chord. Ulyana folded the map along its worn creases — there were more creases than flat places anymore — and slid it into the oilcloth sleeve, and the sleeve into the canvas satchel that never left arm's reach. Then they climbed up onto the reefer's ladder and sat where they could watch the track behind them unspool into darkness, because a person who watched the track behind them saw who followed, and nobody had followed Ulyana Thornbury in eleven years.

That record had exactly one more night left in it, though Ulyana didn't know it yet.

* * *

Marrowford announced itself an hour before dawn, the way river towns do: first the smell of wet earth and mill waste, then the lights strung along the levee like a low constellation, then the yard — small, muddy, half the sidings drowned. Ulyana dropped off the slow order just past the water tower, landing in cinders with the ease of long practice, and stood a moment letting the train's draft die against their back.

The survey camp was already pitched on the levee when they walked in at gray light: four wall tents, a cook wagon, a stack of creosote stakes, and a crew of eight breaking fast on coffee strong enough to trot a horse across. Ulyana counted them the way they counted everything — automatically, professionally, looking for the weak point in any structure, human or otherwise.

"You'd be Thornbury." The speaker was a broad man with a clerk's hands and a foreman's voice, standing up from a crate. "Halloran. Halloran & Vance. You got my wire."

"I got your wire." Ulyana set the satchel down but kept the strap looped around one wrist, an unconscious habit nobody had ever remarked upon twice. "Show me the damage."

Halloran blinked. Most engineers wanted breakfast, terms, a bed. "It's four miles up. Water's dropped nine feet since Tuesday. I'll warn you—" he hesitated, and Ulyana read the hesitation like a gauge reading, "—the river gave back more than it took."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning we've got debris fields where there wasn't even a bank before. Whole sections of shoreline rearranged. And some things," Halloran said carefully, "that don't look like they came from anywhere around here."

Ulyana shouldered the satchel. "Then let's go see what the river's been keeping."

The walk upstream took an hour through ground that had been underwater a week ago and looked offended by air. Everything was slicked with gray silt. Trees stood knee-deep in deposited mud as if wading. The old Sorrel line itself appeared and vanished beside them — rails emerging from the muck in long tarnished ribbons, ties rotted soft, a milepost leaning drunk. Ulyana catalogued it without stopping: *line open to traffic in maybe six weeks, ballast shot, two culverts to rebuild, bridge at mile 47 needs full inspection.* This was the part of the work that came easy, the part that felt like breathing. Give Ulyana a broken thing made of iron and grade, and they went quiet inside, the way other people went quiet in churches.

The trouble started at mile 52, where the flood had peeled the riverbank back like the skin off a fruit.

The crew found it just after noon. One of the rodmen, a young fellow named Petter, was setting a stake when his iron shoe rang against something that didn't sound like stone. Twenty minutes of digging later, eight men stood in a widening circle around a shape rising out of the silt, and nobody was talking.

It was a chest. Iron, banded, the size of a sea trunk, and heavy enough that it had taken the river forty years to decide whether to bury or deliver it. The corrosion had eaten the hasps into lace, but the face of the lid was still legible, stamped deep in the metal where the water couldn't reach: two four-pointed stars, interlocked.

Ulyana's breath went out of them slowly.

They knew that seal. Every engineer in the region knew it from textbooks — the double star of Vessmark, kingdom of the Karst, a nation that had drawn its last border and signed its last treaty four decades ago and thereafter existed only in archives and arguments. But knowing it from textbooks was different from standing over it in the mud while eight men breathed around you.

"That's worth something," Petter said, and his voice had already gone greedy. "Iron like that, antique, a nobleman's piece—"

"It's a government dispatch chest," Ulyana said. Their voice came out level, which surprised them, because nothing inside them was level. "See the lockplate? Dual-cylinder, staggered tumblers. That's not furniture. That's for documents somebody didn't want opened."

"So we open it," said another man, the dredge operator, wiping his hands on his thighs. "Finders' law. Half to the crew, that's fair."

Ulyana crouched. Up close, under the crust of river, the lockwork showed through the corroded housing — and there it was, the thing that stopped Ulyana's heart and restarted it out of rhythm. The cylinder housings weren't standard Vessmarki issue. They were offset, asymmetric, built to a custom pattern with a signature quirk: a third false keyway cut at seven o'clock, decorative, useless, meant to teach any thief a lesson.

Ulyana had grown up watching hands cut that false keyway into practice blanks on a kitchen table, by lamplight, while a child was told *remember this shape, little compass, because someday the shape will remember you.*

Their father had designed this lock. Their father had locked this chest. Or someone who learned from him had, which amounted to the same inheritance.

"Nobody opens anything," Ulyana said, and stood, and put the full weight of their reputation into the words. "This is a survey contract, not a salvage claim. Anything of archaeological value goes to the provincial registrar, that's the law, and if you crack a state seal on a dead nation's dispatch box, you're not finders, you're felons. I'll take custody pending registration. My bond covers it."

Half of that was true. The crew grumbled, but Halloran backed the play — engineers outrank dredge crews when lawyers loom — and by late afternoon the chest sat in Ulyana's tent, wrapped in tarpaulin, with Ulyana sitting outside it in the lamplight doing absolutely nothing, which was the hardest work they'd ever done.

Because here was the arithmetic they couldn't stop running. The Sorrel line. Miles 40 through 63. The exact stretch their father's faint underlines converged on, the exact stretch he'd been traveling toward when he became the word *gone*. Forty years the river had held this thing, and the year Ulyana finally came walking up the bank, the water gave it back.

Coincidence was a word for patterns you hadn't finished measuring yet.

Inside the tent, the chest sat silent under its tarp. Somewhere under that corroded dual-cylinder lock — a lock whose secret lived in Ulyana's own hands, taught to them across a kitchen table by a man who had then walked into a closed country and never come out — whatever Vessmark had thought important enough to drown rather than surrender was waiting.

Ulyana didn't sleep. Around midnight they unwrapped the tools from the bottom of the satchel — beneath the map, beneath the oilcloth, beneath eleven years of careful moving-on — and took out a slim pick forged to a pattern they had never shown another living soul.

The first tumbler gave at 2 a.m. The second fought longer, and when it finally rolled over, the sound it made was soft as a door opening in an empty house.

Ulyana lifted the lid.

* * *

Dawn found them still there, motionless, the lamp burned to a stub, the contents of the chest laid out on the camp table with the reverence due relics and the precision due evidence.

Survey documents. Dozens of them, vellum and linen paper, sealed in oilskin sleeves that had done their job across four decades of drowning. Final engineering surveys of the Vessmarki Royal Corps — and not of any ordinary works. Ulyana read the title sheet twice, then a third time, because the numbers refused to behave.

A pass. Through the Karst massif itself, through the range every treaty in the region treated as a wall given by God. Graded, benched, tunnel-estimated, complete with water tables and rock analyses and a construction schedule in a dead nation's careful hand. A pass that, if it existed — if it could be proven — would run straight through the middle of four border treaties like a locomotive through a paper fence.

And beneath the surveys, in an envelope aged amber, a letter. The address on it was written in the fine, steady hand Ulyana would have known anywhere, the hand that had inked the ghost-lines under their map.

*To the Thornbury line,* it began. *To whoever holds the compass now.*

Outside, the river ran brown and busy, carrying off the last of the flood, minding its own ancient business. Ulyana sat down slowly on the camp stool, the letter unopened in their hands, and understood that the map had never been theirs alone — that they had spent eleven years tracing a route someone else had drawn, toward a destination someone else had chosen, and the journey was only now beginning.

They broke the seal.
