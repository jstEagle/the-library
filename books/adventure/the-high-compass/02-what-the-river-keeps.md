## 2. What the River Keeps

The Sorrel had dropped nine feet overnight, and what it left behind looked like the river had been robbed.

Ulyana Thornbury stood on the scoured bank at first light, boots sinking into mud the color of wet slate, and read the damage the way other people read a newspaper. The flood had taken the low trestle's western footing, chewed a channel through the ballast bed, and peeled back the earth along three hundred yards of embankment like a hand lifting a blanket to see what was underneath. Which was, in this case, exactly Ulyana's job: see what was underneath, decide how much it would cost to put back, and write down a number that the railroad company would hate and pay anyway.

"Ground's still weeping," Ilse said beside her. She crouched and pushed two fingers into the mud, came up with water beading between them. "Give it two days before we stake anything."

"We don't have two days." Ulyana checked their watch against the light coming up gray over the far bluffs. "Company wants figures by Friday. We work today."

Ilse grunted. She was a big woman built like something meant to hold weight up, which she had been doing for bridges for twenty years until a collapsed span outside Marrowgate ended her career and started her drinking, in whichever order nobody could prove. She'd sobered up enough to take survey work from Ulyana twice a year, and she was the only person alive who'd seen more than one page of the map of closed lines and been allowed to keep her memory of it.

"You always say that," Ilse said. "'We work today.' Like it's a religion."

"It is." Ulyana turned upstream, where the morning fog was burning off the water in long rags. "Today it says dig."

* * *

By mid-morning the crew was strung along the embankment in a ragged line—six local hires the company had provided, plus Ilse, plus Tomas, who was not officially on the payroll but had appeared at the camp two nights ago with a stolen bicycle and an appetite, and whom Ulyana had not yet found a reason to send away. He carried stakes now. Badly. Enthusiastically.

The flood had done most of the heavy work already. It had torn away the topsoil and dumped the old riprap downslope, and where the water had curled hardest against the bank it had opened a raw wound in the earth, exposing things that had been buried since before anyone on the crew was born: a section of culvert nobody had surveyed, a scatter of bricks stamped with a kiln mark from a company that hadn't existed since the war, and, forty yards downstream, the corner of something iron.

It was the youngest hire, a boy named Petter with forearms like rope, who found it. His shout carried the whole length of the cut.

"Boss! Boss, there's metal here—big metal!"

Ulyana walked down through the mud, unhurried, because hurrying told crews things you didn't want them to know you knew. The object was half-exposed at the base of the embankment where the flood had undercut it, a box shape maybe two feet long, furred with rust and river clay. Petter was attacking it with a shovel.

"Don't," Ulyana said, and Petter stopped.

"Why not? If it's iron it'll fetch—"

"If it's iron it'll bend. Dig around it. Hands when you get close." Ulyana knelt and began clearing silt away with their palms, and the shape emerged slowly, the way shapes emerge in dreams: rectangular, banded, corners reinforced with riveted plates. An iron chest. Not a strongbox, not a munitions case—a chest, made to be carried by two men or dragged by one determined person, made to last.

The clay came off in sheets. Underneath, the iron was pitted but sound, black-green with age, and across the lid, stamped deep enough to survive fifty years underwater, was a seal: two four-pointed stars overlapping, points outward, inside a double ring.

Nobody spoke. Even the crew who couldn't have named Vessmark if their wages depended on it—and most of them could not; the country had lasted barely a century and had been dead for forty years, its name scraped off signs and out of schoolbooks along this whole stretch of border—felt the weight of the thing. Old nations leave a pressure behind them, like weather.

"That's Vessmarki," Tomas said. He'd come up quiet as a cat, mud to his knees, and he was staring at the lid with his head cocked. "The double-star. My gran had a spoon with that on it. She wouldn't let anyone eat with it."

"Your gran should've kept it buried," said Petter, who had recovered his appetite. "That chest is worth money. There's collectors pays good for Vessmarki iron. My uncle sold a helmet once—"

"It's not yours," Ulyana said, without looking up. They were running their fingers along the edge of the lid, tracing the seam, and they had gone very still, the way they went still over lockwork.

Because it was lockwork they were touching. Set into the front face of the chest, mostly hidden under a crust of clay, was the mechanism: three wheels visible through a brass escutcheon, each wheel drilled with a pattern of holes, and above them a small plate stamped with a maker's mark worn nearly smooth.

Ulyana knew that mark. Had known it since they were seven years old, sitting on a workbench in a shop that smelled of oil and brass filings, watching a man with ink-stained fingers turn a wheel of exactly that design and say, *the trick isn't keeping people out, little bird, the trick is deciding who gets in.*

Their father's mark. Their father's wheels. The hole-patterns were his—the irregular spacing he used so that false keys made in a hurry would shear off instead of opening, the offset drilling he'd shown them how to read with a fingertip before they could read letters.

"Boss?" Petter again. "You going to open it?"

"No," Ulyana said.

"But—"

"This is a survey site. Unregistered finds go to the company registrar, and the registrar will sit on it for six months while lawyers argue about whether it's archaeological or criminal. You'll all be paid for the day it took us to find it. That's the arrangement." They heard their own voice come out flat and official and were grateful for it. "Ilse. Help me lift it to the wagon."

* * *

They did not take it to the registrar.

They took it to Ulyana's tent, which was at the end of the camp row, set apart the way Ulyana always set their tent apart—not from vanity, but because a person who never slept in the same town twice learned early to keep a wall of canvas and habit between themselves and everyone else. Ilse helped carry it without being asked and without comment, which was one of the reasons Ulyana kept hiring her, and then she stood in the tent flap with her arms crossed and watched Ulyana brush the last of the clay from the escutcheon plate.

"You lied to them," she said.

"I delayed them. Lying comes later."

"And the registrar?"

"There is no find to report until I say there is." Ulyana sat back on their heels. The lamp was lit now, and in its yellow circle the chest looked less like treasure than like a body—something dredged up, something that had been drowned a long time and had opinions about being raised. "Help me or wait outside, Ilse. Either way, close the flap."

Ilse closed the flap. She stayed. Of course she stayed.

Ulyana worked the lock the way their father had taught them to work locks, which was not the way thieves worked them. Thieves forced. Engineers listened. Ulyana pressed a fingertip to each wheel in turn, feeling for the drill offsets, reading the hole-patterns like braille, and their hands remembered everything even as their mind insisted they were remembering nothing, that this was coincidence, that thousands of locksmiths had used offset drilling, that the world was large—

But the world was not large. The world was a river valley, a dead nation's seal, and a lock made by a man who had vanished off the face of the earth eleven years ago along lines that existed only on a map folded in Ulyana's breast pocket.

The third wheel seated. The bolt drew back with a sound like a held breath released.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, were papers. Bundles of them. Ulyana lifted the top bundle into the lamplight: heavy stock, water-stained at the edges but legible, covered in fine ruled linework and elevation figures and a script Ulyana didn't recognize—Vessmarki, presumably, the language of a country that no longer issued documents. Beneath the papers lay a letter, sealed with wax, addressed in the same script. And beneath the letter, a smaller envelope, thinner, softer paper, bearing a single line of handwriting that Ulyana did recognize, that they would have known blindfolded, that hit them in the sternum like a coupling slamming home at speed:

*For the Thornbury line, when the water gives it up.*

Ulyana did not open it. Their hands, which could dismantle a bridge lock in the dark, hung in the air above the envelope and would not move.

Outside, the river went on talking to itself, patient, enormous, keeping whatever else it kept.

"Ilse," Ulyana said finally, and their voice came out lower than they intended. "How fast can you pack?"

She looked at the envelope, then at Ulyana's face, and whatever she saw there settled the question, because she didn't ask any of the twelve questions a sensible person would ask. "Fifteen minutes. Why?"

"Because we found nothing today. Because the crew gets paid through Friday and sent home tomorrow with a story about unstable ground. Because within a week, someone is going to come asking about an iron chest pulled out of the Sorrel, and I intend to be somewhere else when they do." Ulyana wrapped the papers back in their oilcloth, all except the thin envelope, which went into their breast pocket, over the map, against the heartbeat. "And because my father wrote me a letter, Ilse, and he has been dead to me for eleven years, and I need to know which of those things is true."

Ilse nodded slowly, the way she nodded at a span that would hold. "Fifteen minutes," she said, and went to pack.

* * *

Ulyana stood alone in the tent a moment longer, listening to the camp settle around them—the clink of mess kits, Petter bragging about the find he'd been sworn to forget, the river running high and brown with everything it had taken and given back. On the map of closed lines, spread open on the camp table, the River Sorrel ran like a vein, and Ulyana traced it upstream with one finger, past the last surveyed station, into the mountains marked in faded ink with a notation their father had written before they were born: *the Karst line — closed by order — ask no one.*

The telegram had promised a lucrative contract. The river had delivered something else entirely.

From the direction of the mess tent came the sound of laughter, ordinary and human, and beneath it, farther off, the groan of the night freight pulling through on the mainline, headed south. Ulyana folded the map, buttoned the coat over the letter, and stepped out into the dark to stop moving for the very last time in a while—or so they told themselves, knowing even as they said it that the map had never once let them choose.
