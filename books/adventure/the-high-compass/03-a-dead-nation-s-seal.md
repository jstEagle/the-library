## 3. A Dead Nation's Seal

I did not open the chest that night. I want that on the record, because everything that came after—the running, the burning, all of it—might be traced back to a single hour of weakness in a survey tent on the Sorrel, and I would like history to know that for one whole night I was sensible.

The chest sat under my cot with a tarpaulin thrown over it, and it sat there the way drowned things sit: patient, certain of its own weight. Every few minutes I felt it through the canvas floor of the tent, or imagined I did, a pressure against my spine like a hand.

Around midnight I gave up pretending to sleep and lit the lamp.

Here is what I knew by then, from two days of looking and not touching:

It was iron, riveted at the corners, the kind of work you don't see anymore because nobody builds things meant to outlast their owners. Salt water had scaled the lower third white, but above the tide line the metal was only rusted brown, and stamped into the lid—struck twice, once deep and once shallow, as if the die had been applied by two different hands—was a seal: two four-pointed stars, one above the other, joined by a compass rule.

Vessmark. The double star. Any schoolchild in three countries could tell you what it was, the way they can tell you about the plague or the flood of '09—a thing that used to exist and now doesn't. Vessmark had been a nation for six hundred years and then, forty years ago this autumn, it had stopped being one, dissolved between two treaties and an annexation so bloodless that the histories called it *an administrative consolidation*, which is the language men use when they want a word for something they're ashamed of.

And the lockwork—

I knelt beside the chest and put my lamp close to the escutcheon plate, and there it was, unmistakable, the thing that had made me claim custody before Marek's cousins could get a crowbar near it. A seven-lever mechanism with a false gate on the fourth lever and a detent that required the key to be turned past resistance, held, and turned again. My father called it "the polite lock," because it asked you to pause. He built them. He taught me to build them, summers, in a workshop that smelled of oil and charcoal, when I was small enough that his hands covered mine entirely on the file.

There are perhaps five people alive who could pick that lock without destroying it. I am one. My father is another, if he is alive, which forty years of silence says he is not.

I sat back on my heels and looked at the seal for a long time.

Then I got my tools.

* * *

The first letter was not in the chest. It was on the chest—pasted inside the lid, under a sheet of oiled silk gone brittle as moth wing. I lifted the silk away with a knife tip and found paper, heavy rag stock, watermarked with the double star. The ink had faded to the color of weak tea but every word stood sharp.

I copy it here exactly, because I have learned that if I don't write these things down immediately I begin to doubt them, and doubt is a luxury I can no longer afford.

---

*To the custodian, whoever you are, whenever you are:*

*If you have opened this lawfully, you hold the key and the name, and I need say little. If you have opened it otherwise, stop, close it, and take it to the nearest office of the Vessmarki Survey Corps, where you will be paid and asked nothing further.*

*If neither of those things is possible—if the offices are gone, if the Corps is gone, if the nation itself is gone—then read on, and forgive us the trouble we have stored up for you.*

*You are holding the final surveys of the Karst Traverse. There is a pass. The maps in circulation show none, and the treaties of 12 March rely on that absence. Four borders were drawn along the assumption that the Karst range is impassable between Mount Serel and the Grey Needles. It is not impassable. We made it passable. Twenty-two years of work: three tunnels, two viaducts, a switchback road cut into the north face, surveyed to the rod and graded to the half-percent. A railway could run through it. An army could march through it in nine days.*

*We finished the surveys in the spring of the last year. By autumn there was no Vessmark to build it in.*

*Understand what we decided, and why we decided it, and understand that we were not cowards. Four governments want this pass to remain a rumor. One people wants it to be real again. Both cannot be satisfied, and whichever way you move, someone will call it theft.*

*The documents are yours now. That is the terrible part. Not the carrying—they weigh almost nothing—but the deciding.*

*Below you will find the names of the signatories. If any live, they retain authority over the chest's disposition. Seek them first. Decide second.*

*—R. Aldous Vay, Chief Engineer, Vessmarki Survey Corps, Final Custodian*

---

I read it twice by lamplight with my hands steady, which surprised me, and then a third time with my hands not steady at all, which didn't.

A pass through the Karst. I knew the range the way I know most ranges—from the outside, from closed lines. Three branch railroads had been abandoned against the Karst foothills in my father's lifetime; two appeared on my map in faded ink, lines I'd traced a hundred times wondering who had drawn them and why they stopped where they stopped, short of nowhere, ending against rock. I'd assumed engineering failure. Floods, subsidence, the ordinary deaths of railways.

They hadn't failed. They'd been *stopped*. Stopped at the treaty line, on purpose, because what lay beyond them was a secret worth four borders.

I looked at the map pinned to the tent pole—my map, my father's map, the map I had never explained to anyone—and saw, for the first time, that its dead ends weren't scattered the way failures scatter. They clustered. Along the Karst. Along the old Vessmarki frontier. Like fingers pointing.

* * *

Beneath the letter, in fitted compartments that my father might have designed himself—everything in that chest fit together with the same fussy, loving precision—lay the surveys themselves. Forty-one sheets of drafting linen, each one a masterpiece and each one a weapon. I unrolled the first three and understood within minutes that whoever R. Aldous Vay had been, he had been better than good. The tunnel profiles ran true to the grain of the mountain; the grade calculations were annotated in a tiny, patient hand; the viaduct drawings showed a span across the Grey Needles gorge that made my breath do something embarrassing. Ilse would have wept. Ilse, whoever she turned out to be—I was getting ahead of myself even then, and I knew it.

Four treaties. I counted them off like a man counting stairs in the dark. The Halvern Accord. The Sorrel Convention. The Treaty of the Two Rivers. And the Annexation Instrument itself, the document that had erased Vessmark from the atlas—all of them drawn on the premise of an impassable range. Open the pass, and every line redrawn in forty years of diplomatic ink became negotiable. Not automatically—treaties are stubborn animals—but arguable, and argument at that scale is called war.

I rolled the linens back up with more care than I have ever shown anything, and I told myself: burn them now. Right there. Lamp, linen, done. No one would ever know what the river gave up.

I even struck a match.

I held it until it burned my fingers, and then I blew it out, because of the last compartment. Because of the envelope at the bottom, weighted under a brass theodolite mount, addressed in a hand I recognized before I could stop myself from recognizing it.

Not to me. To *the Thornbury line.*

* * *

*My child, or my child's child, or whatever the years have left you—*

*I write this knowing the odds. A letter placed in a sealed chest and given to a river has perhaps one chance in a hundred of reaching its address, and less than that of reaching it while the reader still has a father to be angry at. But your mother always said I planned badly and hoped completely, and she was right on both counts, so here goes.*

*By the time you read this you will know what the chest carries, because Vay's letter will have told you, and you will have worked out the rest, because you were always quicker than me at seeing the shape of a thing before its parts. So let me skip to what Vay couldn't know.*

*I was the one who hid it.*

*The Corps voted to publish. Vay argued for it to his last day—openly, honorably, believing that truth about geography belongs to everyone, and I loved him for it. But I had spent thirty years building locks, and a lock is just a decision you make physical, and I had already made mine. A pass like that doesn't liberate anybody. It gets taken. Whoever holds the Karst holds every valley below it, and the valleys below it are full of the same farmers and stationmasters and children who were full of them before, and they will be full of soldiers instead. I have seen what a border looks like after it moves. You have too, if you're reading this, though I pray the version you saw was only on paper.*

*So I delayed the publication order. Twice. Then the country ended, and the question solved itself, and I took the chest south with two trusted men and we gave it to the Sorrel at the ferry crossing, in a crate weighted to sink and sealed to survive, because rivers keep what nations cannot. The river forgets nothing and tells no one. It seemed the right archive.*

*Then I went north, because there was a second matter, and it is the reason for this letter, and I ask you to believe that I did not choose it lightly.*

*The chest has a sister. A smaller one. In it is my journal—not the surveys, those are dead weight, burn them if your judgment says so and I will haunt you gently—but the route to Judge Amalia Vess, the last living signatory of the Charter, the only person whose word still carries any legal weight in the matter of Vessmark's borders. She is old, she is hidden, and she is owed a choice. The judge decides; you deliver. That was always the design. I only ever meant to carry the question to her door, not answer it for her.*

*About the map. You'll have found it by now, or you drew it yourself, which amounts to the same thing. I'm sorry for the years you spent thinking it was a riddle with no answer. It wasn't a riddle. It was a record. Every closed line on it is a place where somebody decided a truth was too dangerous to leave open, and I walked them all, checking the seals, keeping the quiet. You thought you inherited a habit of leaving. You inherited a circuit. I never stopped moving because I was restless, my love. I stopped being able to arrive.*

*Don't be like me. Be like the river instead: keep everything, tell no one, and stay exactly where you are.*

*Or don't. You're a Thornbury. You'll decide, and it will cost you, and you'll pay it standing up.*

*All my love, longer than the lines,*
*Your father*

*P.S. — The polite lock opens easier if you thank it. This is not superstition. It is pacing.*

* * *

I sat in the lamplight until the wick gutted, and then I sat in the dark.

Outside, the Sorrel ran low and clear over its new channel, rearranging the world it had flooded, and somewhere downriver the survey crew slept in their own tents, dreaming, most of them, of salvage money. They thought the chest was cargo. They thought anything heavy and sealed was cargo. Half of me envied them.

The rest of me was doing arithmetic.

A father alive as of forty years ago, walking closed lines north with a second chest. Chalk marks, maybe. Cipher marks. A trail, if he'd wanted one, and the letter said he'd left a route—to the judge, to the sanatorium, to wherever a hidden woman kept the last legal ghost of a dead country. Which meant the map on my tent pole wasn't a grief object anymore. It was instructions.

Which meant other things too. If my father had carried a secret across three borders for decades, others had carried its opposite. Governments keep archivists for exactly this purpose; I'd heard of the ministry men who traveled light and asked questions in flat voices, though I'd never met one and had planned to keep it that way. And Vessmark had exiles—engineers' families, royal households, whole towns shipped east after the annexation—who had spent forty years telling their children about a nation that used to exist. Some of those children would be my age now. Some of them would kill to hold what was sitting under my cot.

I had maybe days. Perhaps hours, once the salvage crews started talking, and salvage crews always talk.

I repacked the chest in the dark, by touch, thanking the lock as I closed it because my father had told me to and because it was the closest thing to answering him I would ever get to do. Then I pulled my map down from the tent pole, spread it on the drafting table, and did the thing I had sworn since childhood never to do in front of witnesses.

I began to mark a route.

North, along the closed lines. Through the borderlands, through the towns that had scraped their signs clean, up toward the Karst and whatever waited past it—a judge, a journal, a decision I hadn't made yet and already dreaded. My pen moved fast, and my hand shook, and both facts seemed fine.

Near dawn, one of the crew's runners ducked into the camp, mud to the knees, and said a government man had come down on the morning freight asking after salvage from the flood. Neat coat. Flat voice. Carrying a ministry satchel.

He was asking, the runner said, specifically about iron.

I folded the map into my coat, woke Ilse's telegram address from memory, and started packing.
