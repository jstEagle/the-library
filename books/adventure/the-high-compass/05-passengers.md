## 5. Passengers

I left the survey camp at four in the morning with the chest wrapped in a canvas groundsheet and strapped to my back like a coffin I'd been asked to carry uphill.

The camp was still asleep. Cook fires banked, theodolites stowed, the Sorrel running low and brown in its new channel where the flood had carved it a week ago. I'd paid off my crew the evening before—generously, which bought silence for about twelve hours—and left a note on the mess tent table that said *survey incomplete, returning by rail, do not follow*. Nobody follows an engineer who writes like that. They've all worked for someone who wrote like that before.

The chest rode against my spine. Forty pounds of iron and forty years of somebody else's country. Every step down the river trail it shifted, and every shift felt like a hand on my shoulder asking whether I'd made up my mind yet.

I hadn't. But I knew one thing: I couldn't open it here, and I couldn't be found with it here, and those two facts together meant motion. Motion was the only skill I'd ever fully trusted.

* * *

Ilse Brandt was drinking coffee outside the freight depot at Kessel Halt when I came up the embankment, and she looked at me the way she looks at everything—like she's already calculated the load rating and found it wanting.

"You're leaving," she said.

"I'm leaving."

"With that." She nodded at the bundle on my back. She'd seen the crew dig it out of the riverbank two days ago. She'd held the lantern while I scraped the mud off the double-star seal, and she hadn't said a word since, which from Ilse is a paragraph.

"It's mine," I said. "Family property."

"Your family's been dead longer than I've been alive, Thornbury."

"Not all of it."

She drank her coffee. She was a big woman gone soft at the edges, sixty if she was a day, hands like bridge cable, and she had built eleven spans across three countries before the Karst Viaduct collapse took her name off every register on the continent. The inquiry said her calculations were wrong. Ilse says the steel was wrong. The steel company says nothing, because the steel company bought the inquiry.

"The southbound freight comes through at six," she said finally. "It stops eight minutes for water. You're going to need someone who knows what a trestle can carry when you get into the hills, because everything up there is forty years rotten and you know it."

"I didn't ask."

"No," she agreed. "You didn't." She stood, drained the cup, and picked up a tool roll I only then noticed sitting at her feet, packed and buckled. Packed before dawn. Maybe packed before I'd even decided to go. "Eight minutes," she said. "Don't waste them standing there looking noble."

* * *

We rode in the second-to-last wagon, among crates of agricultural lime that got into everything. Ilse slept against the wall with her tool roll as a pillow. I sat with the chest between my knees and read the letter again by the light leaking through the slats.

*To the Thornbury line,* it began, in a hand I would have known anywhere, because I'd spent nine years of my childhood copying it under a man's supervision until he pronounced my figures acceptable, which was the closest he ever came to saying he loved me.

*If you are reading this, then the river has given up what I could not bury deep enough, or you have found the other caches, in which case you already know more than I can tell you here. Either way: the documents are real. That is precisely the problem.*

My father. Aldous Thornbury, custodian of a dead nation's last secret, surveyor of lines that appeared on no map but mine. He walked out of our house in Vell when I was fifteen, carrying a leather case and wearing his good coat, and he never sent word, never died in any record I could find, never stopped being the first thing I checked for in every town I passed through. For twenty years I told myself he'd chosen his work over me. It took an iron chest to suggest the work might have been choosing him.

There was a sound at the end of the wagon. Small. Deliberate—the kind of small deliberate sound a person makes when they want you to know they're there without startling you.

I put my hand on the wrench in my coat pocket.

"Don't," said a voice, young, cracking on the edge of it. "Please. If you throw me off at speed I'll break something, and I've already broken enough this year."

A boy unfolded himself from behind the lime crates. Fifteen, maybe sixteen, hollow-cheeked, wearing a railwayman's jacket two sizes too large with the sleeves rolled six times. He had dust in his eyelashes and a book clutched to his chest like a breastplate—an actual bound book, water-stained, printed in a script I couldn't read.

"That's Vessmarki," I said.

"Yes." He hugged it tighter. "I'm Tomas. I've been riding your trains since Kessel. Well—not your trains. Trains. I don't—" He stopped, restarted. "I heard you talking at the depot. To the bridge woman. About the seal. And I can read it. The old script and the modern both. My mother taught me before—" He stopped again, and this time didn't restart.

"Before what?"

"She was from there. From Vessmark. She used to say nobody stays a refugee forever, they just become from somewhere that isn't anywhere." He shrugged, too fast, a boy's shrug doing a man's work. "I read anything they give me. Documents. Inscriptions. I worked two seasons for a salvage firm translating grave markers until the firm went under. I'm cheap. I'm quiet. I'm useful."

"You're a stowaway."

"I'm a passenger who hasn't purchased a ticket yet," he said, with the ghost of a grin. "There's a difference. Stowaways hide. Passengers negotiate."

I looked at Ilse. She was awake—I'd have bet the chest on it—and watching us through half-closed eyes, saying nothing, letting me make my own mistakes.

"What's the book?" I asked.

Tomas opened it carefully. A primer, school-age level, the kind of thing printed by the thousand and burned by the ten-thousand after annexation. On the flyleaf, in pencil, someone had written a name and a date. His mother's, I guessed. The date was the year of the border closures.

"It's the only sentence of hers I still own," he said. "The rest of her is in a cemetery in a town that changed its name so nobody would have to remember why the church had two stars on it."

The train swayed. Lime dust hung in the light like weather.

"Can you read survey notation?" I asked.

He blinked. "Better than I can read anything else. It's numbers. Numbers don't change countries."

"They do," I said, "more often than words." I shifted the chest aside and patted the planking next to me. "Sit down, passenger. We'll discuss terms at the next stop."

* * *

The next stop was Brannoch Siding, which is not a stop at all anymore—it's a water tower, a collapsed coal shed, and a junction where three lines meet and none of them run. We had a two-hour wait for the mountain local, and we spent it in the shadow of the shed, and that was where Marek found us, though found is a generous word for a man who had clearly been following us since Kessel and simply ran out of patience.

He stepped around the corner of the shed with his hands visible and open, which is either honesty or training, and in Marek's case I later learned it was both.

"Ulyana Thornbury," he said. Not a question. "Daughter of Aldous."

Every hair on my arms stood up. "Nobody living knows that name."

"Somebody living wrote it in a letter forty years ago," he said, "and letters travel, engineer. Especially letters that pass through exile communities, where people collect names the way other people collect stamps." He was perhaps fifty, lean, gray at the temples, dressed like a man who owned one good suit and wore it for occasions, and this was apparently an occasion. "My name is Marek Ostrev Vess. My grandfather was chief of bridges to the royal engineering corps. I am the third of his line to keep a photograph of a country that no longer exists, and the first to see its seal come out of the ground."

"You followed me from the river."

"I followed the rumor of you. Then I followed you." He smiled thinly. "Exiles learn to move quietly. It's the one inheritance that never devalues."

Ilse had come up on his flank without appearing to move, which is a thing she does. "And what does the grandson of a royal engineer want with a box of old paper?"

"The same thing you want, madam, whatever you tell yourself it is." He turned back to me. "I want it delivered. There is a signatory alive—Judge Amalia Vess, last officer of the charter, living in retirement under another name in the high country. The letter in that chest will name her. I know where she is. You, I suspect, do not."

"And if I just take the chest and go?"

"Then you will spend the rest of your life being followed by people worse than me." He said it without heat, which made it worse than heat. "The ministry has archivists whose entire profession is making sure that chest stays lost. The moment it surfaced on the Sorrel, they knew. You have perhaps days. Possibly less. I am offering you the route, the contacts, and the language. In exchange—"

"There it is," Ilse muttered.

"—in exchange, the documents reach daylight. Publication. Restoration. A nation remembered is a nation arguable, engineer. Four treaties were signed against a map that lied. I have spent my whole life watching people pretend the lie was peace."

I looked at the chest. My father's lockwork gleamed dully under the grime, and I thought about the letter, the part I hadn't shown anyone: *the documents are real; that is precisely the problem.* A man who hid a truth doesn't usually mean for it to be published. But a man who hides a truth also doesn't usually leave a route to a judge.

"We'll talk terms on the train," I said.

Marek inclined his head, satisfied, and went to see about tickets—which was how I learned he had money, and money, I was learning, was its own kind of cargo.

Ilse leaned close. "You trust him?"

"No."

"Good. Me neither. But I trust you to watch him, and that'll do." She settled back against the shed wall. "For what it's worth, girl, I'm not here for your father's secrets. I'm here because there's a viaduct over the Greywater that I designed when I was thirty-five and haven't seen in twenty years, and it's on the way, and I'd like to look at it once before somebody else's bad steel brings it down." A pause. "Also the bridge woman thing was a lie. I'm here because you dug that box up and didn't flinch, and I've decided that's worth following."

"That's the nicest thing anyone's said to me in years, Ilse."

"Low bar," she said, and closed her eyes.

* * *

The mountain local came at dusk, one engine and two carriages, running a line that officially did not exist anymore and unofficially ran twice a week because the garrison towns still needed feeding. We took the rear carriage. Marek bought four tickets with cash and tipped the conductor enough to make him forget our faces, and by full dark we were climbing through pine country with the chest on the seat between Ilse and me and Tomas asleep against Marek's shoulder, which neither of them acknowledged and both permitted.

I spread my map on my knees. The map of closed lines, inked and re-inked over twenty years, every dead branch and abandoned spur in three borders' worth of country, each one marked with the date it closed and, where I knew it, the reason. It was the only inheritance I'd ever claimed openly, and now it turned out it wasn't mine alone either. My father had walked these lines. Had hidden things along them. Had drawn, somewhere, some version of this same web, and left it to me the way you leave a debt.

Four heads instead of one. A disgraced builder, a boy with a dead language, an exile with a claim, and an engineer with a box. None of us trusted the others. All of us needed them. That's not a family, exactly. But it's the material families get made from, and I'd seen it happen on night freights before, strangers becoming load-bearing for each other without ever discussing the specifications.

"Tomas," I said quietly.

He surfaced from sleep instantly, completely, the way children of hard years do. "Yes."

"When we stop tomorrow, I'm going to open the chest properly. There are documents inside. Surveys. I need them read, sorted, and understood—and I need you to understand that whatever they prove, they stay between the four of us until I decide otherwise. Can you do that?"

He considered it with a seriousness that made him look older. "My mother said secrets are just truths that haven't found their timing." Then: "Yes. I can do that."

"Good." I folded the map. "Because there's a man coming behind us—a government archivist—and I'd very much like to be boring and unfindable by the time he gets interesting."

The train climbed. Somewhere below and behind, down in the dark along the river, I imagined offices lighting up, telegraph keys chattering, a careful man in a basement learning my name. Ahead, the line bent toward the mountains, toward the passes that would close with the dry season, toward a judge I'd never heard of and a choice I could already feel waiting for me like weather.

The chest sat warm between us, ticking with nobody's clock but everyone's.

At midnight, at a water stop called Mile 212, the conductor knocked on our carriage door and said, apologetic, that there was a telegram waiting for the Thornbury party.

None of us had told anyone we were the Thornbury party.

I looked at Marek. Marek looked at Ilse. Ilse looked at me, and said, "Well. Open it, girl. Bad news travels faster than freight."
