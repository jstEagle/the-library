## 6. The First Closed Line

The engine was older than Ilse, and she meant that as a compliment.

She'd found it in a weed-choked shed at the edge of Ketter's Yards, under a tarp that had rotted into lace, and she'd spent two days coaxing it back to something like life while Ulyana handled the paperwork nobody would ever check. A narrow-gauge shunter, forty years past its last inspection, built in Vessmark by a firm whose nameplate had been pried off but whose stamp remained in every casting: a small double star, worn nearly smooth.

"It'll hold," Ilse said, when Ulyana came to watch her work.

"Everything holds," Ulyana said, "until it doesn't. That's what my father used to say about bridges."

"What did he say about engines?"

Ulyana considered. "That they're just bridges that got ambitious."

Ilse laughed before she could stop herself, and the sound surprised her—she hadn't laughed in a shed in a long time, hadn't laughed much since the Meridian Span came down and took her license and her name with it. She'd spent four years being a woman who had built the thing that failed. Now she was a woman rebuilding a dead nation's engine in the dark, and somehow that felt closer to honest.

They left Ketter's Yards an hour before dawn, three of them in the cab and Tomas's dog—Tomas had acquired a dog somewhere, nobody asked—in the tender among the coal. Ulyana at the throttle because they wouldn't surrender it. Ilse on the fireman's side, watching the track ahead the way she'd once watched a span under load: not with fear, exactly, but with the professional humility of a person who knew that steel and timber fail in the same instant they succeed, and the difference is only a matter of attention.

Marek rode in the cab's corner with the chest between his boots and his hand resting on it, and Ilse didn't like that. Not because she thought he'd steal it. Because he touched it the way a man touches a thing he believes is his, and belief is heavier than any lock.

The branch line ran north through country that had been Vessmark once and was now three other names on three other maps. The rails were rusted to a dull plum color but the ballast was sound, and the culverts had been cleared—recently cleared, Ilse saw, the cut faces of the timber still pale.

"Someone's been maintaining this," she said.

Ulyana nodded, eyes on the track. "I noticed at mile nine."

"Maintaining it for what?"

"For us," Marek said quietly, "I'd hope. Or for whoever comes next."

Nobody answered that. The engine clattered on through birch and bracken, and the sun came up behind them and laid their shadows out ahead on the track like a warning.

* * *

They made forty miles the first day, hiding in a cut during the midday hours because Ulyana had seen a telegraph lineman on the ridge and didn't trust the wires. Ilse slept badly in the cab, one hand on the injector, and woke at dusk to find Ulyana sitting up in the dark, map spread across their knees, tracing the line with one finger.

"You're not sleeping either," Ilse said.

"I sleep on trains."

"You're not on a train now."

"I'm on a train that's pretending to be a rock," Ulyana said. "Close enough."

Ilse climbed down and sat beside them on the ballast, and for a while neither of them spoke. The mountains were visible now, a blue-black wall with the last light caught in the snowfields, and somewhere up there was a pass that four governments had spent forty years pretending didn't exist.

"Tell me about the Meridian Span," Ulyana said.

Ilse's whole body went still. It was the reflex of four years—every time, the flinch, the waiting for the question that was really an accusation.

"You know what happened," she said.

"I know what the inquiry said. I've read enough inquiries to know they're written by people who need an answer more than they need the truth."

Ilse looked at her hands. "The ice load was three times the rated figure. The report acknowledged that. And then it said I should have designed for it anyway." She turned a stone over between her fingers. "Maybe I should have. That's the thing about failure, Ulyana. Everyone wants it to be a villain, and usually it's just arithmetic."

"Usually," Ulyana said, "it's a river."

Ilse looked up.

"Rivers move," Ulyana said. "Maps don't. That's the whole tragedy of engineering, and maybe of everything else. You build for the world as it was, and the world has already left."

Ilse wanted to say something clever and didn't. What she felt instead was something warmer and more dangerous: the sense of being understood by someone who had no reason to bother. She'd been a curiosity since the Span—a cautionary tale in a hard hat. Ulyana had asked her about it the way you'd ask a colleague about a difficult site, and Ilse had answered, and the sky had gotten dark, and neither of them had moved.

"We should sleep," Ulyana said finally. "Long grade tomorrow."

But they sat a while longer, shoulder almost touching shoulder, watching the mountains take the last of the light.

* * *

The trestle was at mile sixty-one.

Ilse saw it from two hundred yards out and her stomach dropped before her mind caught up. It crossed the Vell gorge—a hundred-foot span of timber trestle, tall as a church, and even at that distance she could see what was wrong with it. The geometry was off. The towers stood plumb but the stringers sagged in a curve no load had put there, a curve that came from below, from the piers, from something shifting in the gorge.

"Stop the train," she said.

Ulyana already had the brake. The engine groaned to a halt a hundred yards short, and the silence after was enormous.

Ilse walked the last hundred yards alone, because that was her job and had been her job since before any of this. She walked the near approach, checked the rail joints, knelt at the first tower and put her hand flat against the timber. Sound. Dry. She climbed—there were ladder rungs in the legs, old Vessmarki standard, spaced for shorter people—and worked her way up to the deck level, and that was where she found it.

The lower chords had been cut.

Not rotted. Not failed. Cut—three of the seven main stringers on the far tower, sawn two-thirds through and then dressed over with pitch and dirt so the cuts would read as age. Someone had done careful, patient, expert work. Someone who understood exactly where a trestle carries its load and exactly how much you can remove before the removal announces itself.

She climbed back down and stood at the foot of the tower for a long moment, doing the arithmetic she'd sworn off four years ago. A light engine might cross. Might. If the cuts were where she thought they were and the timber was as sound as it looked, the deck would flex, the chords would tear the rest of the way, and the far tower would go into the gorge with the engine, the chest, and everyone she—

Everyone aboard.

She walked back. They were all standing by the engine, waiting, and she told them plainly, because that was the only way she knew to tell anything.

"It's been cut. Three stringers on the far tower. It's meant to look like rot. It isn't."

"Can you repair it?" Ulyana asked. No hesitation, no despair. Just the next question.

"Not tonight. Not alone. I'd need to sister the chords—bolt new timber alongside the cuts—and I need timber, and I need daylight, and I need to not be doing it while someone watches from the ridge."

"Someone cut it," Marek said. "Which means someone knew we'd come this way."

"Or someone cut it months ago for anyone who came this way," Ulyana said. "Either way, they knew the route. This is the old Vessmarki military spur. It's on maybe four maps in the world, and one of them is mine."

Tomas had gone pale. "Vale?"

"Vale's people don't sabotage," Ulyana said slowly. "Vale buries things. Sabotage is loud. This is—" They stopped, and Ilse watched them arrive at the same conclusion she had. "This is someone who wants the chest stopped but not destroyed. Or wants whoever's carrying it stopped, and doesn't care who."

"Or," Marek said, "it's someone who wants us to turn back and take the valley road, where it's easier to watch us."

The wind came up the gorge and moved the trestle, very slightly, and Ilse saw Tomas flinch.

"Then we don't turn back," Ulyana said. "Ilse?"

She'd been doing the arithmetic the whole time they talked. "There's a work camp two miles back—mile fifty-eight, a maintenance siding. There'll be timber in the shed if the shed's still standing. Bolts I can make from the spikes and a fire. Give me the night and one pair of strong hands who does what I say exactly, and I can make it hold for one crossing at walking speed."

"One crossing," Ulyana said.

"One crossing. After that, I burn the trestle behind us myself, because I'm not leaving it standing for whoever cut it to repair."

Something moved through Ulyana's face then—something that might have been relief, or admiration, or the beginning of the thing Ilse was trying very hard not to name. "You'll cross it first," Ulyana said. "On foot. Before the engine goes."

"Obviously."

"That's not a request I get to make. I know."

"You don't get to make it," Ilse agreed, "but I'd have made it anyway. That's the difference between us. You keep the map. I keep the bridges."

* * *

They worked through the night by lamplight, and it was the best night Ilse had had in four years.

The shed at mile fifty-eight still stood, and it still held timber—Vessmarki pine, seasoned forty years in the dry mountain air, better wood than she'd ever been issued in her licensed life. Ulyana hauled it. Tomas worked the fire and the spike-forge with a focus that surprised everyone, hammering spikes flat and drilling them rough, and Marek—Marek carried, and held, and did not speak, and Ilse caught him once looking at the cut stringers with an expression she couldn't read and didn't try to.

She bolted new pine alongside every severed chord, twelve inches of overlap past each cut, double-bolted, and then she did the thing the inquiry had said she should have done on the Meridian: she built in more than the load required. She overbuilt it, out of pride and spite and something else she wasn't ready to call hope.

Ulyana worked beside her the whole night without being asked, and somewhere around three in the morning, wedged in the tower legs with a lantern between them, Ilse realized she was happy. Not comfortable—her hands were torn and her back was a single complaint. Happy. There was a problem with a clear shape, and she was solving it, and the person handing her timber understood exactly why each piece mattered, and the mountains stood around them like a jury that had decided to wait.

"You're smiling," Ulyana said.

"I'm sistering a chord."

"You're smiling at a chord."

"It's a very good chord." Ilse drove the last bolt home and rested her forehead against the timber, and the timber held, and that was the whole of her religion. "My father built fences," she said. "He wasn't an engineer. He built fences around fields, and he used to say a fence is just a promise you can see. I think that's why I build bridges. Same promise. Bigger fields."

Ulyana was quiet a moment. "My father built locks," they said. "The kind you open, not the kind on canals. He used to say every lock is a question, and the key is the only honest answer."

"Where is he?"

"That," Ulyana said, "is the question I'm following."

The lantern light moved between them. Ilse wanted to say: then I'll help you find him. She wanted to say: I've been looking for a line worth riding since mine ended. She said none of it, because the night was already carrying more weight than a night should, and some things you say on the far side of a trestle or not at all.

* * *

They crossed at first light, walking speed, as promised.

Ulyana drove, because they would surrender the throttle to no one, and Ilse rode the deck ahead of the engine on foot, testing each panel, feeling the flex come up through her boots. The sistered chords took the load with a sound like a held breath released slowly. At the far tower—the cut one—she stopped and put her hand on the new pine, and the engine came over her shoulder at four miles an hour, and the whole hundred-foot structure stood up under it and did not fail.

On the far side, everyone got off and stood in the sun and nobody said anything clever, and Ilse found that her eyes were wet and blamed the wind, and Ulyana looked at her a moment too long and said nothing at all, which was somehow better than anything.

Then Ulyana soaked the trestle in lamp oil from the tender and lit it, and they watched forty years of Vessmarki pine go up in the gorge, and the smoke stood straight up in the still air like a column marking the exact place where they had stopped being people who could turn back.

"Whoever cut it will know we crossed," Tomas said.

"Whoever cut it will know we repaired it," Ulyana said. "That's worse. For them." They turned to Ilse. "You built a signature into that repair. Anyone who reads it will know a bridge-builder did it, and a good one."

"Is that good or bad?"

"It means Vale will know exactly who you are by tomorrow," Ulyana said. "And it means whoever else is on this line will know there's an engineer worth recruiting or worth stopping." They looked north, where the line bent up into the foothills and the mountains stood waiting. "We've announced ourselves."

The dog barked once, at nothing, at the ridge.

Ilse followed its line of sight and saw, for half a second, a figure on the skyline—too far to make out anything but the shape of a person standing very still, watching them, and then gone.

"Someone's been ahead of us the whole way," she said.

"I know," Ulyana said. Their hand went to the map roll at their shoulder, and their voice, when it came, was quieter than the wind in the gorge. "And I think I know who it is. There's a signal box at mile seventy. My father's chalk marks, if he's alive, will be in the cipher he taught me when I was nine." They didn't look at her when they said it. "If the marks are fresh, then everything I've believed about the last twenty years is wrong, and I don't know yet whether that's a thing I want."

Ilse reached out and took their hand. She didn't decide to; her hand simply went, the way a chord takes a load, because the structure required it.

"Then we'll find out together," she said.

Ulyana's fingers closed around hers, and they stood like that a moment, three travelers and a dog and a burning bridge behind them, on the first closed line either of them had ever been glad to open.

Then the whistle of a distant train—far off, wrong direction, but a whistle—came down the gorge, and Ulyana's grip tightened.

"That's not a freight," they said. "Freights don't run this line."
