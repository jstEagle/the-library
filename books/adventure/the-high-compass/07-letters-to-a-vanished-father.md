## 7. Letters to a Vanished Father

The signal box stood where the map said it would, which was the first strange thing.

Ulyana had learned long ago not to trust their own hand-inked lines too completely. Rivers moved. Landslides ate cuttings whole. A line marked closed in one decade could be scraped clean of rails by the next, its ballast hauled off to pave some minister's driveway. But there it was at the mouth of the Sorrel valley, exactly as drawn: a two-story wooden tower on a stone plinth, its windows blind with grime, its lever frame long since stripped for scrap. The Vessmarki railway authority had built it eighty years ago to guard the junction where the valley branch met the main line north. Now it guarded nothing but wind and the memory of trains.

"Cozy," Ilse said, dropping her pack against the plinth. She said it the way she said everything—flat, practical, already calculating load paths. "Roof's intact. Better than last night."

Tomas climbed halfway up the external ladder and hung there, reading the building like text. "There are dates scratched on the boards. Old ones. And—" He went quiet.

"And what?" Marek asked.

"Chalk." Tomas looked down at them, and his face had gone careful, the way it did when he wasn't sure he was allowed to know something. "Fresh chalk."

Ulyana climbed past him without a word.

The signal box's operating floor was a single room around a square hole where the levers had once risen through to the frame below. Dust lay everywhere in a grey felt thick enough to write in, and nothing had written in it recently—no footprints, no scuffs, only the small drifts and runnels of years of neglect. Except on the north wall, beside the window that faced up the valley toward the mountains, where someone had drawn four small marks in white chalk:

**‖ ⊕ ‖ ⊖**

Ulyana stood very still.

The dust proved no one had come inside. Which meant the marks had been made from outside, through the glass—or before the dust settled, and preserved somehow, which was impossible—and either way it didn't matter, because Ulyana's hands had gone cold and their heart was doing something embarrassing and childish, hammering like a boy's.

It was the Thornbury tally. The counting cipher. Their father had invented it at a kitchen table in a rented house in Kolvary when Ulyana was nine, drawing bars and circles and half-circles in flour spilled on the wood because paper cost money. *A bar is five*, he'd said. *A circle means turn the count around—read backward. A half-circle means skip the next mark; it's a lie.* It wasn't a cipher for hiding state secrets. It was a cipher for leaving messages to each other across distance: *I came this way. I am ahead of you. Meet me where we slept last summer.*

Four marks. Two bars, a circle, a bar, a half-circle.

Read forward, nonsense. Read backward, per the circle: half-circle, bar, circle, bar, bar. The half-circle voided the next mark—the first bar—leaving: bar, bar. *Two.*

*Two days ahead of you.*

Ulyana put a finger on the chalk. It smudged faintly under their skin, still soft, still recent. Not weathered. Not months old. Days.

"Ulyana." Ilse's voice, low. "You've gone white. What is it?"

"It's a message," Ulyana said. Their voice came out wrong, cracked at the hinge. They cleared their throat. "It's my father's cipher. And it says he passed through here two days ago."

* * *

The kitchen in Kolvary had a stove that smoked and a table that wobbled, and their father fixed neither, because their father fixed locks instead, and clocks, and anything else with an interior you could open. Ulyana was nine, and bored, and he set a padlock in front of them between the salt dish and the bread.

"Open it," he said.

"With what?"

"With your ears first. Then your fingers." He turned the lock over so they could hear the shackle rattle. "Every lock is a question. The key is just the answer written in brass. Your job is to learn the question by touch."

He guided their fingers into the keyhole—too big, both of them, they could barely fit one fingertip—and had them press the pins. *Feel how that one fights? That one wants to move but can't until its neighbor does. Locks have manners. Learn them.*

Later, older, twelve maybe, he taught the chalk cipher on the station platform at Drensk while they waited for a freight that never came. He drew the marks on the platform edge with a stub of white stone and made Ulyana encode a sentence, then decode it walking away and looking back, because, he said, *you won't always read these standing still. Sometimes you'll read them running.*

"Why do I need it?" Ulyana had asked. "We're together."

He had smiled then, or something near a smile—he was not a man who spent smiles freely—and looked north along the dead track toward the mountains, toward Vessmark, though he never said the name.

"Because someday we might not be," he said. "And because a person who can leave a message that only one other person alive can read is never entirely alone. Remember that, little compass. It cuts both ways."

*Little compass.* He called them that because even at eight they could not sit still in a town, could not sleep in the same bed twice without itching, and he'd laughed the first time and said, *you'll want your own map someday,* and Ulyana had thought it was a joke.

The last time Ulyana saw him was a platform too—a different one, fourteen years ago, in a border town whose name had since been sanded off the timetables. He'd been carrying a leather case and wearing the expression of a man carrying a decision he hadn't finished making. He'd kissed the top of their head and said, *if the lines go quiet, don't follow me north.* Then he'd stepped onto a train bound for the Karst, and the train had gone, and the lines had gone quiet, and Ulyana had spent fourteen years obeying him by never once going north—until a river receded and handed up an iron chest stamped with a double star, and obedience stopped being possible.

*Don't follow me north.*

The chalk said: he was two days ahead. Moving north. Leaving messages.

Against every better judgment Ulyana owned—which were considerable, hard-won, and load-bearing—hope lit in their chest like a lamp in a locked room.

* * *

They camped that night in the signal box, because the roof held and because none of them suggested moving on. Marek built a small fire in a rusted brazier that had probably warmed some long-dead signalman's tea, and Tomas read aloud from the chest documents by firelight, translating the old Vessmarki script in his halting, self-taught way, while Ilse cleaned her tools and Ulyana sat by the north window and did not look at the wall and looked at nothing else.

"The gradient figures are extraordinary," Tomas said. "Whoever surveyed the pass used a method—I don't know the word in our language. Something like *telling the mountain its own height*. They measured without climbing. How?"

"Mirrors and patience," Ilse said. "Old trick. You bounce light off a peak and measure the angle twice from known points. My grandfather did it for bridge towers." She paused. "Vessmarki engineers taught it to him, actually."

"Of course they did," Marek murmured, and there was pride in it, and grief, braided tight.

Ulyana barely heard them. They were turning the chalk marks over in their mind like a lock in the fingers, pressing each pin.

Two days. On foot or by the ghost of the valley branch, two days north of here lay the derelict yards at Skarn Hollow, and beyond that the first true climb into the Karst foothills. If their father was leaving a trail, he was leading someone along it. Leading them? Or leading whoever else knew the cipher—and the thought arrived cold and unwelcome—who else had he taught?

The letter in the chest had named him *last custodian*. Custodians hand things on. Custodians get replaced. And a man who had spent forty years keeping a nation's most dangerous secret alive in a world that wanted it dead would not travel carelessly. Would not travel unwatched. The sabotage on the trestle two valleys back had proven someone else was moving on these lines—someone who wanted the route closed to everyone.

Unless the saboteur and the chalk-writer were the same man, laying a trail with one hand and burning bridges behind himself with the other.

*You'll want your own map someday.*

"You haven't touched your supper," Ilse said, settling beside them at the window. Close enough that their shoulders nearly touched. Outside, the valley darkened in stages, the peaks going last, holding the light like coals.

"I'm working," Ulyana said.

"On what?"

"On whether I'm about to be a fool." They told her, then—all of it. The flour on the kitchen table. The platform at Drensk. *Little compass.* The last platform, the leather case, the instruction that had ruled their life for fourteen years. When they finished, the fire had burned down and Tomas had fallen asleep mid-sentence with the survey folio open on his chest, and Marek was pretending to sleep with his eyes closed in a way that meant he was listening.

Ilse was quiet for a while.

"My father left too," she said finally. "Different direction. Sea, not mountains. He was a harbor engineer, and after the collapse he took contracts overseas and wrote letters that got shorter every year, and then stopped." She turned her tin cup in her hands. "I used to draft letters back to him that I never sent. Whole pages. Do you know why I stopped?"

"No."

"Because I realized I wasn't writing to him. I was writing to the version of him I kept in my head, and that version answered everything, agreed with everything, forgave everything. He was easier than the real man ever was." She looked at Ulyana. "Be careful which father you're following. The chalk, or the memory."

"That's the cruelest thing anyone's said to me in years."

"I'm a bridge-builder. Cruelty is load-bearing sometimes." Her mouth twitched. "But I didn't say don't follow. I said be careful. There's a difference, and I'm hoping you can feel it."

Ulyana could. It felt like the difference between a door that was locked and a door that was merely closed.

* * *

They rose before dawn. Ulyana took a stub of chalk from the box's supply drawer—signalmen had kept chalk for logging train movements, and this drawer still held three, dry as bone—and knelt beneath their father's marks and added their answer below them:

**⊕ ‖ ‖**

Read backward, per the circle: bar, bar. *Two behind you. We are coming.*

Then, because fourteen years of silence deserved more than arithmetic, they added a third row, breaking the terse grammar of the trail-cipher for the first time since childhood, spelling out slowly, mark by mark, a sentence their father alone could read:

*I found the chest. I have the letter. I am bringing it north. Don't disappear again.*

Their hand shook on the last group. Chalk is a coward's medium—you can wipe it away, deny it, let rain finish the argument. But it was also the only medium they had ever shared.

Outside, the sky was going from iron to ash to a thin, cold gold. Ilse was already checking the trestle notes for the valley branch. Tomas was folding the folio. Marek stood at the foot of the ladder, looking north, and Ulyana caught his expression before he smoothed it—not ambition, not the bright nationalist fever he carried like a coal under his coat, but something rawer. Recognition. As if he too had once left chalk marks for someone who never came.

"We make Skarn Hollow by nightfall if the branch holds," Ulyana said, coming down the ladder. "After that we're off the maps entirely."

"Off the maps," Tomas repeated, with the particular terror-delight of a sixteen-year-old saying a forbidden thing.

They shouldered packs. Ulyana went last, pausing once at the doorway to look back at the wall—their father's four marks, their own rows beneath, three generations of a conversation in chalk on the wall of a dead country's watchtower.

Memory was a compass. Memory was also a trap. Their father had taught them both lessons in the same breath, all those years ago, and Ulyana had finally understood the second one only now, standing in the trap's open door: the trap wasn't the past. The trap was believing the past was finished.

Someone had passed this way two days ago. Someone knew the cipher. And someone else—this they felt in the base of the spine, the old animal ledger—was following.

Far down the valley, on the southbound track they themselves had ridden yesterday, a thread of smoke rose against the morning. A train, crawling where no timetable sent trains, stopping where nothing waited.

Ulyana watched it for a long moment, then turned north and began to walk.
