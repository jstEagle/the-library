## 9. The Town That Forgot Its Name

The town had no name on any map we carried, which was the first strange thing about it.

Ilse found it on her survey sheets as a junction marker—*KM 214, water tower, passing loop*—and nothing else. Ulyana's hand-inked map of closed lines had a small circle there and a word in pencil so faint I had to hold the page to the lantern to read it: *Vessmarki once.* Not a name. A warning, maybe. Or an epitaph.

We came down out of the hills at dusk with the chest wrapped in tarpaulin on a handcart Ilse had salvaged from a maintenance shed two stations back. My job was the cart. I'd been promoted from stowaway to mule somewhere around the second day, and nobody asked if I minded. I didn't. Pulling something heavy in a straight line is easier than thinking.

"Rules," Ulyana said, before we reached the first house. They crouched by the cart, adjusting the rope knots with quick fingers, not looking up at us. "We're a survey crew. Bridge assessment, flood damage, north line. Nobody mentions Vessmark. Nobody opens the chest. Nobody asks anyone their grandmother's maiden name." They glanced up then, and their eyes found mine and stayed a half-second longer than the others'. "Especially you, Tomas."

"I know how to keep quiet."

"You kept quiet for three days in a freight car. This is different. Here, quiet is a language everyone speaks fluently, and they'll know if your accent is wrong."

I wanted to say my accent wasn't wrong anywhere—I'd grown up learning four languages because orphanages on the border don't get to choose—but Marek was already nodding like a man agreeing to terms he intended to break later, so I just said, "Understood," and took up the cart handles.

The town smelled of coal smoke and river mud. Maybe four hundred people, maybe fewer. The houses were built low and close, stone at the bottom and timber above, the way they build where winters argue with summers. And every single one of them was blank.

That sounds like nothing until you see it. No carved dates over doorways. No painted names. No shop signs except numbers—*3*, *11*, *27*—stenciled in plain black. The church had no saint over the door, just a smooth rectangle of lighter stone where something had been chiseled off, and below it another rectangle, and another, three of them stacked like empty picture frames. Somebody had gone down that wall with a hammer and a great deal of patience.

Marek stopped walking when he saw it. I heard his breath change.

"Don't," Ulyana said quietly, without turning around.

"I'm not doing anything."

"You're breathing loudly. Breathe like a surveyor."

Ilse laughed under her breath, one short note, and Marek shut up, but his eyes kept moving over those empty rectangles the way a man reads a letter he isn't supposed to have.

* * *

The inn was called Inn. Actually called that—the sign over the door said GUESTHOUSE in trade-tongue letters, and inside, the woman who ran it said the town didn't need a name because "everyone who matters knows where it is." She said it pleasantly, pouring tea, and I understood it was a sentence she'd said many times, maybe every time a stranger came through, which was probably not often.

We took two rooms and the stable corner for the cart. Ulyana paid in coin from the middle of their purse, not the top—old habit, Ilse told me later, so you never show a stranger how much you carry—and then we sat down to a supper of barley soup and dark bread while the landlady's grandson, a boy of six or seven, stared at me from the kitchen doorway with the frank rudeness only children are allowed.

"You're not from here," he announced.

"No," I said.

"Your coat is Vessmarki."

The spoon went very loud against the bowl. Or it did in my head. Outwardly, I set it down and said, "It's a work coat."

"It's got the double star stitched inside the collar. My granda says people used to stitch stars inside their collars so the soldiers couldn't see them but God could."

"Tomas." Ulyana's voice was mild as milk. "Show the boy your boots. He likes boots."

I showed him my boots. He examined the stitching with the gravity of a customs officer, decided they were acceptable, and left. Under the table, my hands were shaking, and I pressed them flat against my knees and thought about the collar of my coat, which I had never once looked at since I'd stolen it off a drying line in a rail yard four years ago.

Grandmother's stitching. Probably. Whoever she'd been. The woman who'd owned this coat before me had someone who loved her enough to sew a star where only God could see it, and I'd been wearing her love without knowing, the way you wear everything the dead leave behind.

Ulyana watched me across the table. They didn't say anything. That was its own kind of mercy.

* * *

I couldn't sleep. Big surprise. I went down to the stable to check the cart—not because the chest needed checking, but because standing near it made me feel useful, and feeling useful is what I do instead of sleeping—and that was where I found the old woman.

She was sitting on an upturned crate beside the cart like she'd been waiting for me specifically, which, it turned out, she had. Small, dry as a seed pod, wrapped in a shawl the color of wet ash. Her eyes were the pale gray of old ice, and they were fixed on the tarpaulin with an expression I couldn't read until she spoke, and then I could.

"You brought it through my town," she said. "In the night. Like thieves."

"We're not thieves."

"No." She looked at me properly then. "Thieves want money. You want something worse. You want the past." She tapped the cart with one knuckle, twice, like knocking on a door. "What's under there has the seal on it. Don't insult me by saying no. The boy told me."

"The boy told you? Your grandson?"

"My grandson tells me everything. It's why I keep him." She smiled without any warmth in it at all. "Sit down, Vessmarki boy. You'll want to hear this sitting down."

"I'm not—" I started, and stopped, because arguing about my blood with a woman who'd lived through annexation seemed like a fight I'd lose on technicalities. I sat.

"My name was Marta Vessik," she said. "Before. Do you understand what 'before' means? Before, this town was called Sorrelgate, and it had a fountain with two stars on it, and a school where children learned two alphabets, and a railway office where my father worked as a signalman for thirty-one years. After, the fountain was broken up for gravel. The school taught one alphabet. The railway office burned, officially by accident, along with all its records." She said it evenly, like reading a list she'd memorized to keep herself from screaming it. "And the name. The name went last. They made us erase our own name. Did you know that? They didn't send men with paint. They sent men with lists, and they stood in the square while the mayor climbed the ladder himself and scraped the letters off the station sign. Because if strangers did it, we could hate them. If we did it ourselves—" She shrugged. The shrug held forty years.

"They're still here, though," I said. "The people. You're still here."

"We're here." She turned her pale eyes on me. "Boy, do you know what happens to a town that forgets its name? Nothing happens. That's the horror of it. We planted wheat. We raised children. We buried our dead under new stones with no dates before a certain year. Life goes on, and it goes on fine, and that is the trap. Forgetting doesn't hurt. That's why everyone recommends it." She leaned forward, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "But I remember. I'm the last one here who remembers, and when I die, Sorrelgate dies with me, and no one will ever grieve it, because you can't grieve what you've successfully forgotten."

The chest sat between us under its tarpaulin, and I swear the stable got colder.

"So burn it," she said.

"What?"

"Burn it. Whatever's in there—papers, seals, claims—it's a match thrown into dry grass. You think the ones who erased us will let it stand? You think they'll negotiate? They'll come with soldiers, and they won't come for the chest. They'll come for everyone who ever touched it. Every town on your route. Every name in every ledger. They'll finish the job." Her hand closed on my wrist, and her grip was stronger than her size promised. "I watched them erase a country once. I am begging you, child, as one of the erased: don't give them the ink to write us out again. Burn it, scatter the ash in the river, and walk away. Let us rest."

I should tell you what I felt, and what I felt was: she was right. Completely right, probably. I'm sixteen and I grew up in border orphanages and I know exactly what governments do with inconvenient paper, because I've been inconvenient paper my whole life—a birth record that vanished when the province changed hands, a name spelled three ways in three registries, a mother listed as *deceased, location unknown* in a file I once stole a look at and never found again. I know what forgetting costs, because I'm the receipt.

But I also knew what was in the chest besides treaties and borders. There was a letter addressed to the Thornbury line, and inside that letter, folded smaller than small, there was a page in my father's handwriting—I'd seen it when Ulyana opened the lockwork, a list of cipher keys in a slanted, careful script, and next to one key, in the margin, a tiny drawing of a signal lamp. Just a doodle. The kind of thing a man does while thinking.

My father had held that page. My father, who walked out one morning to check a culvert and never came back, leaving me a name and a notebook and nothing else. Somewhere on these dead lines, he had passed, carrying or guarding or dying near the thing under the tarpaulin, and the old woman was asking me to burn the only trail he'd ever left.

"I can't decide that," I said finally. "It's not mine."

Whose is it, then?" she asked. "A dead nation's? Dead nations don't inherit. Only the living do, and the living always pay."

She released my wrist and stood, unhurried, gathering her shawl. At the stable door she paused.

"There's a man in the taproom," she said, not turning around. "Came in on the evening coach. Says he's a wool buyer. He asked the landlady whether the north branch line was passable, and she said no one's asked that in twenty years, and he laughed and bought her a brandy." Now she turned, and the lamplight caught her pale eyes. "He has soft hands and hard boots, and he's asking after a survey crew. So whatever you decide about burning, decide it fast."

Then she was gone into the dark, and I was alone with the chest and my heart going like a telegraph key.

* * *

I woke Ulyana. Of course I woke Ulyana. I stood over their bedroll in the little back room and shook their shoulder and they came awake the way they always did—all at once, silently, hand already finding the knife under the canvas—which is terrifying the first ten times and comforting after that.

"Wool buyer," I whispered. "Taproom. Asking about the north branch."

Ulyana listened without moving, and then dressed, and we went down together, and I'll say this for them: they didn't waste a second on panic. They stood in the shadow of the stairwell and looked into the taproom through the gap in the door, and there he was—a broad, comfortable-looking man in a good coat, laughing at something the landlady said, a brandy glass loose in his soft hand. Everything about him said harmless. Except his boots, which were military, and the way his eyes swept the room every few seconds without his head moving, counting exits, counting faces.

"Vale's people move faster than I credited," Ulyana murmured. "Or that's not Vale's. Either way, we're leaving tonight."

"Marek won't like—"

"Marek can like it or walk." They turned to me, and in the dark their face was mostly shadow, but their voice was steady as rail. "Tomas. The old woman talked to you. What did she say?"

I hesitated. Lying to Ulyana had never once occurred to me as a live option, but the truth was complicated—burn it, child, let us rest—and I was suddenly aware that what I said next might matter more than anything I'd ever said.

"She said her town used to be called Sorrelgate," I said. "And that forgetting doesn't hurt, and that's why everyone recommends it." I swallowed. "And she begged us to burn the chest."

Ulyana was quiet a long moment. Down the hall, a floorboard creaked—Marek's room, or Ilse's—and outside, the wool buyer's laugh rose again through the taproom door, warm and easy and wrong.

"And what do you think?" Ulyana asked me. "You're the one who reads Vessmarki. You're the one whose father—" They stopped themselves, which was its own kindness. "What do you think we should do?"

Nobody had ever asked me that. Not once, in four years of orphanages and rail yards. Adults told children what to do; that was the whole architecture of the world. And here was Ulyana Thornbury, wanted by a ministry and hunted by exiles, asking a stowaway in a stolen coat to help decide the fate of a nation's ghost.

I thought of the old woman's grip on my wrist. I thought of the empty rectangles over the church door. I thought of a signal lamp doodled in a margin by a hand I couldn't remember anymore.

"I think," I said slowly, "that if we burn it, they win twice. Once when they erased the country, and once when they convinced the last people who remembered to be afraid of remembering." My voice shook and I let it. "My father carried this thing instead of running. The least I can do is carry it a little farther."

Ulyana looked at me for a long moment. Then they nodded, once, the way they approved a route on a map.

"Then we go tonight, and we go fast," they said. "Wake Ilse. Tell her the north branch, the old viaduct route. And Tomas—" they paused at the door, half in shadow, "—when this is done, whatever's done, I'm going to help you find out what happened to him. That's a promise. I don't make many."

I stood alone in the dark hallway with that promise in my chest, heavier than the chest itself, and went to wake Ilse.

Behind us, in the taproom, the wool buyer ordered his second brandy, and began, casually, to ask the landlady about trains.
