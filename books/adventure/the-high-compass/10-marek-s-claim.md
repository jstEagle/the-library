## 10. Marek's Claim

Marek had rehearsed the confession for eleven days, and in all eleven versions of it, he had been brave.

In the versions, he said it plainly, over a fire, with his hands steady: *I am not carrying this chest to a judge. I am carrying it to the world.* In the versions, Ulyana listened, nodded slowly, and understood—because how could they not? How could anyone hold a nation's last testament in their hands and choose to let it rot?

The reality was a wet morning in a derelict freight shed outside the town that had forgotten its name, and Marek's hands were not steady at all.

They had stopped there because Tomas's feet were bleeding through his boots and because Ilse had found a crack in the shed's east wall wide enough to shelter in and narrow enough to defend. Rain came off the hills in gray sheets. The chest sat between them like a fourth passenger, its double-star seal dulled with river silt, its lockwork—Thornbury lockwork—still holding fast.

Marek watched Ulyana across the small fire they'd allowed themselves, smokeless, fed with dry larch. Ulyana was mending a strap on their pack with the same unhurried competence they brought to everything, and it occurred to Marek, not for the first time, that he had been traveling with a person who treated the fate of a nation like a broken buckle. Something to be fixed and moved past.

That was unfair. He knew it was unfair. Ulyana had bled for this chest. Ulyana had buried a crewmate's fever dream and outwalked Vale's agents and climbed onto a sabotaged trestle without hesitation. But unfairness had been growing in Marek for weeks now, feeding on the thing he hadn't said, and unfairness, left long enough, becomes conviction.

"Tomas," he said. "Walk the perimeter. Ilse—"

"I know where the perimeter is," Tomas said, but he went, pulling his collar up, and Ilse followed him with a look at Marek that said she already understood what the perimeter was for.

Marek waited until their footsteps faded into the rain.

Then he said it. Not bravely. Not plainly. He heard his own voice come out thin and defensive, the voice of a man reading a statement into a record: "I need to tell you what I intend to do with the documents."

Ulyana's hands stopped on the strap. "You intend to deliver them. To Judge Vess."

"That was the letter's instruction. The letter was written by your father." Marek turned his palms up, an old habit from the exile halls of his childhood, where you learned early that open hands were the only argument permitted. "But the letter isn't the only voice in that chest, Ulyana. There are four hundred pages of survey in there. There is a pass through the Karst that four governments signed treaties pretending didn't exist. There is a nation in that box. And I am one of the last people alive who can speak for it."

Ulyana set down the strap. Slowly. The way they did everything, as if speed were a vice they'd sworn off. "Say the rest."

"I intend to publish it." The words were out now, and having escaped, they grew louder, the way words do when they've been caged. "The surveys, the seals, the signatory record—all of it. To every chancellery and every newspaper from here to the coast. Vessmark existed. Vessmark was erased. And this—" he touched the chest's cold lid, "—this is the proof that the erasure was not just murder but theft. They took a country and then redrew the map so no one could even point to where it had been. I am going to point."

The rain filled the silence.

"Say the rest," Ulyana said again, and Marek hated how well they knew him.

"The rest," he said, "is that when Vessmark's borders are restored—and they will be, because no treaty survives its own fraudulent foundation—the royal engineers' charter revives with it. The Corps of Vess. My family's corps. And someone will have to be its voice." He lifted his chin. "I don't intend to be a footnote in the restoration, Ulyana. I intend to be an architect of it."

* * *

He had expected anger. What he got was worse.

Ulyana stood, unhurried still, and walked to the chest, and put one boot on its lid—not hard, just resting there, the way you'd rest a foot on a coffin you were deciding whether to open or nail shut.

"Do you know what my father's letter asks for?" they said.

"It asks for delivery to Judge Vess. The last signatory. I've read it forty times."

"It asks for *judgment*." Ulyana's voice stayed level, and that levelness was its own kind of fury. "Not publication. Not restoration. *Judgment.* My father spent twenty years as custodian of those documents, Marek. Twenty years moving them, hiding them, burying caches, teaching lockwork to a child who didn't understand what the lessons were for. And at the end of it, he wrote one page. One. Do you know what a man writes on the last page of twenty years?"

Marek said nothing.

"He writes: *Let the one who holds this decide as I decided—slowly, and with the whole weight of it.* That's the sentence. I've had it memorized since the night we opened the chest. Slowly. With the whole weight of it. And you've had it for six weeks, and you've spent those six weeks composing a coronation speech."

"That's not—"

"Four treaties." Ulyana stepped closer, and now the levelness cracked, just at the edge. "Four treaties, Marek. Do you know how many soldiers are garrisoned along those borders? I do. I've surveyed the lines. There are depots on every one of them. The moment your newspapers print that pass, every one of those governments has to choose between admitting the fraud and defending it. And governments—" Ulyana's jaw tightened, "—*always* defend it. You won't be restoring a nation. You'll be handing four armies a reason they've been waiting forty years for."

"And doing nothing hands them the other thing!" Marek was on his feet now too, and the shed seemed suddenly small, the fire suddenly loud. "Doing nothing lets them finish it! Another generation and Vessmark isn't a stolen country, it's a *mistake* someone once made. My grandmother died in a resettlement town with her corps insignia sewn into her coat lining because wearing it outside was a crime. My father taught me the double-star by drawing it in condensation on a window and wiping it away before the neighbors saw. You want me to take the one object in the world that says *we were real* and carry it to an old woman in a sanatorium so she can put it in a drawer?"

"Yes," Ulyana said.

The word landed like a dropped rail.

"Yes," Ulyana said again, softer, which was somehow harder. "Because that's what my father chose. And he knew more than either of us. He knew the surveys, and he knew the governments, and he knew the grief—God, Marek, he *was* the grief, he built locks for a country that no longer existed—and at the end of all of it, he chose to let one judge decide. Not the mob. Not the chancelleries. One woman, with the whole weight of it. And you're telling me you know better than the man who kept this thing alive long enough for you to find it."

"I'm telling you I know better than a dead man what the living owe."

The silence after that was the worst sound Marek had ever made.

* * *

Ulyana turned away. Marek watched them stand at the shed's cracked wall, watching the rain, one hand flat against the boards as if checking the country's pulse through them. When they spoke again, the fury was gone, and what remained was something Marek had no defense against at all.

"I trusted you," Ulyana said. "Not with the chest. With the *route*. Every camp we've made, every fork in the line, I've shown you the map. You could have copied it. You still could."

"I would never—"

"You'd never steal the map, but you'd steal the *meaning* of everything on it." Ulyana turned. Their face in the firelight was older than Marek had ever seen it. "That's the thing you don't understand about me, Marek. I don't care about Vessmark. I don't care about the treaties, or the corps, or your grandmother's coat lining, and I'm sorry, I am genuinely sorry that I don't, because you clearly need me to. What I care about is a letter that names my father, and the fact that it's the only thing in forty years that has. This chest is the closest I will ever come to hearing his voice. And you want to take the thing that's mine—*mine by blood and by lockwork*—and spend it on a country."

"Spend it," Marek repeated. "You said spend it. Like it's coin."

"It is coin. That's all it is, to anyone who'd use it. To your chancelleries and your newspapers and Vale's ministry, it's coin, and the only question is whose hand it lands in. My father understood that. He understood that the moment those pages are read by anyone with an army, they stop being a nation's memory and become a nation's *ammunition*." Ulyana crouched by the chest, and their voice dropped. "You asked me once why I never sleep in the same town twice. You never asked me what I'm running from. I'll tell you now, since we're confessing. I'm running from exactly this. From people who love something so much they'll burn the world to give it a grave marker."

"That's not what I—"

"It's what all of us are. Me. You. Vale, probably. The whole sad caravan." Ulyana looked up at him, and there was no cruelty in it, only exhaustion, and that was the cruelest thing of all. "The difference is that some of us know it."

* * *

Marek walked out into the rain because he could not stay in the shed one more minute.

He found the perimeter without meaning to—Tomas had walked it into the mud, a neat oval around the freight shed, and Marek followed the boy's footsteps like a rail line, one tie at a time, until his anger burned down to the coals underneath, and the coals were not anger at all.

They were grief. He knew that. He'd known it since the night he'd first seen the double-star seal and felt his grandmother's hand on the back of his neck, teaching him to draw the stars in condensation. He was not an architect of restoration. He was an orphan of a country, and the chest was the largest surviving piece of it, and he wanted to hold it up to the world and say *look, look, we were here* the way a child holds up a drawing.

And Ulyana was right that it was ammunition. That was the unbearable part. Both things were true, and both things could not be true, and somewhere between them the expedition had quietly broken.

He heard footsteps and didn't turn. Ilse came to stand beside him in the rain, arms crossed, watching the gray hills.

"Heard most of it," she said. "Walls are thin."

"I meant every word."

"I know you did. That's the problem." She was quiet a moment. "Ulyana's right about the armies. You're right about the forgetting. And you're both wrong about the chest belonging to either of you." She looked at him sideways. "It belongs to the letter. And the letter says *judge*."

"The letter was written by a man who'd spent twenty years being afraid."

"So were you," Ilse said. "Difference is, he noticed."

She walked back toward the shed, and Marek stayed in the rain a long time, and when he finally came in, dripping, Ulyana was awake by the fire with the map spread open on their knees. They didn't look up. Neither of them spoke. Tomas slept against the chest with his arms around it like it was a large and stubborn animal, and Ilse sat sharpening a tool that didn't need sharpening, and the fire burned down, and no one added wood.

Marek lay down with his back to all of them and did not sleep.

Because there was one more thing he had not said in the shed, one more version of the confession, the eleventh version, the true one, and it was this: he had already begun. Three nights ago, at the signal box, while Ulyana traced chalk ciphers and Ilse stood watch, Marek had sat with the chest open under his coat and copied. Not everything. The pass coordinates. The seal impressions. The signatory record. Forty pages, folded flat against his ribs, warm as a second heartbeat.

He had told himself it was insurance. Against Vale, against fire, against Ulyana's judgment. Insurance, he'd told himself, was prudence.

Lying in the dark with the rain on the roof and Ulyana's breathing slow and even ten feet away, Marek finally admitted what it was. It was a second chest. A chest no letter could reach, no judge could weigh, no burning could destroy. He had made himself the custodian his father-figure had spent twenty years making sure the world would never again have.

And in the morning, when they broke camp and took the valley road toward the high country, Marek carried the copies against his ribs and the guilt beside them, and he understood that from here on, the expedition had two destinations.

Ulyana knew it too. He saw it in the way they watched him load the chest—watched his hands, specifically, the way you watch a lock you no longer trust.

Neither of them said a word. The rain stopped. The mountains came up out of the mist ahead, white-shouldered and waiting, and the party walked toward them in a silence that had stopped being companionship and become a countdown.
