## 11. Night on the Viaduct

Ilse had built bridges for eleven years and destroyed one in nine seconds, and she knew which of those facts had followed her out of every hiring hall since. So when Ulyana stopped the party at the mouth of the viaduct and said, "We cross tonight, in this," she understood it was not recklessness. It was arithmetic.

The fog had come up the valley with the dusk, thick as wet wool, swallowing the far end of the structure entirely. What remained visible was the first hundred meters of deck: iron rails furred with rust, ties gone soft as bread, and below, where the parapet had long since fallen away, nothing at all—just the sound of a river a thousand feet down, patient as bookkeeping.

"You can't see the gaps," Tomas said. His voice did a poor job of pretending he wasn't afraid, which Ilse found more honest than bravery.

"That's the point," Ulyana said. "Vale's people can't either."

"Neither can *we*."

"I can." Ilse said it before she'd decided to. Three faces turned to her. She shrugged, hating herself a little for what came next, which was honesty. "I've walked decks like this blind. You learn to read a bridge through your boots. Rotten tie sounds different under weight than sound ones. Loose rail hums. I can hear this bridge better than I can see it, and right now that's worth more than eyes."

Marek shifted his pack higher on his shoulder. He'd been quiet all day, quieter than usual, and Ilse had noticed him touching the chest strap of his satchel every few minutes like a man checking a wound or a wallet. "And if you're wrong?"

"Then we'll have a very short conversation about it."

Ulyana almost smiled. In the last week Ilse had catalogued that almost-smile the way she used to catalogue load tolerances—carefully, privately, with a growing suspicion that the whole structure of her depended on it.

They roped themselves together at fifteen-foot intervals, Ulyana leading with the lantern shuttered down to a coin of light, then Ilse, then Tomas, then Marek. The rule was simple: no one moved until the person ahead called the footing. Ulyana went first because Ulyana would not be argued out of it, and Ilse let them win that argument because she intended to win the next one.

The fog closed behind them within fifty steps. The world reduced itself to the circle of lamplight, the rope's small live tension against her waist, and the deck speaking under her boots. *Sound, sound, sound.* Then, at perhaps two hundred meters: *hollow*. She put her hand on Ulyana's shoulder and they stepped wide together, over a tie that had rotted through its heart, and kept going.

This was the part of engineering nobody wrote ballads about—the walking, the listening, the thousand small negotiations between a body and a structure. Ilse loved it anyway. Loved it the way she had once loved drafting rooms and crane signals and the moment when a span took its own weight for the first time and stopped being an idea. She had lost all of it in nine seconds on the Kestrel Narrows bridge, and she had told herself for three years that she deserved the loss, and then Ulyana Thornbury had appeared at her door with a map full of dead railroads and asked, without asking, whether Ilse still knew how to read a bridge.

She did. God help her, she did.

* * *

At the halfway point there had once been a passing loop—a widened section where maintenance carts could yield—and its planking had mostly gone to rot and pigeons. They stopped there to rest because stopping anywhere else meant standing on the running deck, and standing was worse than walking. The rope slackened. In the dark, breathing sounded enormous.

Tomas counted under his breath. He did that now when the fear got loud; Marek had taught him some Vessmarki counting rhyme, and it came out of him in a whisper like a rosary. Ilse listened to it and thought about how children were supposed to inherit bridges and nations and instead inherited whatever the adults left lying around—dead languages, sealed letters, half-ruined viaducts across a country that no longer admitted it had existed.

"How much farther?" Marek asked.

"A third," Ulyana said. "Maybe forty minutes if the deck holds."

"And if Vale's agent is already at the far end?"

Ilse felt Ulyana go still beside her. It was a good question. It was the question. They had picked up signs of pursuit since morning—horse prints too fresh at the water stop, a glint on the ridge road that might have been glass or might have been nothing—and none of them had said aloud what all of them were thinking, which was that Vale didn't send one man when he could send three, and that whoever waited at the far end of this bridge wouldn't need to fight them at all. He'd only need to wait. A mile of rotten trestle was a weapon that aimed itself.

"He won't shoot," Ulyana said finally. "Not in the open, not with witnesses he can't account for. Vale wants the chest intact and no report to write. He'll try something quieter."

"Quieter than a mile of fog and gravity?"

"There's always something quieter."

They started again. The deck worsened. Ilse called footings in a low monotone—"solid, solid, gap-left, step-wide"—and Ulyana repeated them forward and Tomas passed them back to Marek, so that the five of them became a single instrument being played along the dark. Ilse stopped thinking about Vale. She stopped thinking about anything but the next ten feet. This was the trance she remembered from construction season, the blessed narrowing, and she sank into it gratefully, letting the bridge talk to her through her boots.

Which is why it took her too long to understand that one of the sounds was wrong.

It wasn't underfoot. It was ahead, off the deck entirely—a faint rhythmic knock, metal on iron, somewhere out in the fog beyond the rails. Once. Twice. Then silence. Then, closer, once more.

Someone else was on the viaduct.

"Stop," she breathed, and the word traveled back down the rope and the line froze. She strained into the dark. The fog ate everything past twenty feet, but sound came through it strangely well, flattened and close. There. Again. A boot sole on a rail joint—or a hand testing the same joint. Ahead of them. Between them and the far end.

"Ulyana." Her mouth was at their ear. "Company. Forward, maybe sixty meters. One person. Moving careful."

She felt Ulyana absorb it—not flinch, absorb, the way a truss absorbs load—and pass it back along the line in hand-signals they'd invented in the first week and never needed until now. *Off the deck. Down. Quiet.*

There was nowhere to go down to. But at intervals the old viaduct had service platforms—narrow timber balconies hung under the deck edge for inspectors—and Ilse had been counting them the way she counted everything. Two lay behind them. The next one, if the bridge matched its sister spans up the valley, was thirty feet ahead and six feet below the rail bed, reached by an iron ladder rung into the outside girder.

They crawled. That was the only honest word for it—on hands and knees along the running boards, rope paid out hand over hand, the chest strapped flat to Marek's back making him move like a beetle. The knocking ahead had stopped. That was worse. Ilse kept her ears on it while her hands found the ladder rung, cold and slick, and fed Tomas onto it first because he was lightest and fastest, then Marek, then herself, then Ulyana last, pulling the rope down after them like a tail disappearing into a burrow.

The platform was four feet by eight, rotted at one corner, holding. Below them the river made its vast indifferent noise. Above them, the fog. Somewhere out in it, whoever had been knocking was now also not knocking, which meant they were listening too.

They waited. Ten minutes. Twenty. Ilse sat with her shoulder against Ulyana's shoulder and discovered she could feel the other woman's heartbeat through two coats, quick and disciplined, slowing itself by will. Tomas had stopped counting. Marek's breathing was very controlled, the breathing of a man doing arithmetic of his own.

Then the fog above them spoke.

"Thornbury." A man's voice, mild, almost apologetic, pitched to carry exactly as far as the platform and no farther. "I know you're off the deck. The rope gave you away—I watched it pay out. I'm alone, and I'm not here to fight. I'm here to make you an offer, and then, depending on your answer, I'm here to do my job."

Ilse's hand found Ulyana's wrist in the dark. Squeezed once. *Don't answer.*

Ulyana squeezed back. *I have to.*

"What's the offer?" Ulyana called up, low.

"The archivist respects what you're carrying. More than you know. Leave the chest on the deck at the halfway loop tonight. Walk north. In the morning there'll be papers waiting in Havelmünd clearing you and your companions of every charge that exists and several that don't yet. That's the offer."

"And the job?"

A pause. When the voice came again it had lost its mildness, just slightly, the way ice loses surface shine before it goes.

"The dry season ends in eleven days. My instructions after tomorrow are no longer negotiable, and I don't write them. Take the offer, Thornbury. I'd rather file a clean report than the other thing."

Silence. The fog held it. Then footsteps, unhurried, moving away along the deck toward the far end—and a new sound beneath them, fainter, from *below*, from the darkness under the viaduct where no man should be: a second set of boots on the lower inspection walk, moving toward them.

Two of them. Alone had been a lie, or the lie of a man who believed his own cover story.

Ilse understood the geometry in one cold flash: the talker above was the distraction, sent to hold their attention and pin them to a platform with exactly one exit. The second man was coming up the ladder. And the platform's corner—the rotted corner—was about to become the most important fact in the world.

"Ulyana," she whispered. "Ladder. Northeast stringer. Thirty seconds, maybe less. We can't fight him here—we'd go off the edge, all of us, it's four feet wide."

"Then we don't fight him here." Ulyana's voice in the dark was perfectly level, and that levelness frightened Ilse more than the boots did, because she recognized it. It was the voice of someone spending a resource they couldn't replace. "Can you get us across? Deck, blind, fast?"

Every calculation Ilse owned said no. Every calculation she owned also assumed she had a choice. "If I call every step. Nobody thinks. Nobody stops. If I say jump, you jump."

"Then call."

* * *

They went up the ladder into the fog like divers surfacing, and the deck received them, and Ilse began to run the bridge.

"*Solid—solid—gap-left, wide—solid—*" Her voice became the only world. Behind her the rope sang with four bodies' urgency; ahead of her the fog held whatever waited at the far end; below and behind, boots rang on iron rungs as the second man gained the rail bed and understood, and began to run.

The deck answered her boot by boot, and the bridge lied to her twice. Once at a rail joint that looked sound and dropped a half-inch under her, throwing her stride—she recovered, called "*broken rail, hop!*" and heard four hops travel down the line like a pulse. Once worse: a stretch of thirty meters where the fire damage from some old sabotage had annealed the ties into charcoal that *sounded* solid and was not, and she felt the deck flexing under them like a springboard, and called "*light steps, no rhythm!*", and they crossed it in a scattershot ghost-run that broke any pursuer's aim.

Because that was the other thing she understood, in the animal back room of her mind where engineers keep their fear: the runner behind them wasn't shooting because shooting on this deck in this fog was as likely to drop his own man as theirs. He was trying to catch them. Which meant he would follow them onto the bad sections. Which meant—

"*Hollow! Wide left!*" —and then, as the second man's boots hit the charred stretch behind them, she heard the sound she had been listening for all night and dreading: the deep wooden groan of a structure finding its limit.

The viaduct did not collapse. Bridges built by her trade rarely grant enemies such clean endings. But a twenty-foot section of the burned decking let go—she heard it, a soft structural sigh, timbers going into the dark—and the running boots stopped abruptly, and a man's voice said something short and professional and furious in a language Ilse didn't know, and then said, louder, across the fog, in the region's plain tongue:

"She's good, Thornbury! Whoever built her route knew bridges!"

And then nothing. The fog took the rest.

They did not stop running until the deck ended—at the far abutment, where the viaduct met the mountainside and the old line plunged into a tunnel whose mouth stood open like a held breath. Inside, out of the wind, they finally allowed themselves to lean against stone and shake.

That was when Ilse saw her hands.

Her tool roll was gone. It had been strapped across her back, and somewhere in the charred stretch—when she'd thrown herself flat over a gap, when she'd rolled under the low handrail clearance—it had caught and torn away, taking with it every wrench, every gauge, every caliper her father had given her at nineteen, the kit she'd carried through disgrace and unemployment and three years of sleeping in stations. Nine pounds of steel and history, lying somewhere in the dark a thousand feet above a river, or in the river, or in the fog, and she hadn't even heard it go.

She said, "My tools," and her voice cracked on the second word, and she hated it, and couldn't fix it.

Ulyana turned. Even in the dark Ilse could see the exact moment they understood—saw them start to speak, saw the apology assemble itself and get discarded, apology being useless, both of them knowing it was useless. Instead Ulyana crossed the two feet of tunnel floor, took Ilse's face in both cold hands, and rested their forehead against hers.

"We'll buy new," Ulyana said quietly.

"They were my father's."

"I know."

"You don't—you can't—"

"Ilse." And there it was again, that voice spending something unrenewable. "When we get down off this mountain I will build you a workshop with my own hands and fill it with whatever you want. But I can't give you back those tools, and I'm done pretending I can fix things I can't. All I can tell you is what's true, which is that tonight you ran a mile of dying bridge in fog with three lives on your voice, and I have never—in my entire life of watching people do brave things—been so afraid for anyone, or so certain they'd bring us through. I love you. I've known it since the trestle. I'm telling you now because we might not get a polite moment later, and you deserve to hear it in daylight, even if the daylight is only this."

The tunnel dripped. Tomas, tactful as a struck bell, had led Marek fifty feet deeper into the dark, their lantern a rumor.

Ilse laughed—one broken note, half sob. "You're declaring this *now*? On the worst night of the expedition?"

"I'm an engineer's daughter. I declare things when the load is highest. It's the only honest test."

"Your father was a lockmaker."

"He'd have made an exception."

She kissed them then, there in the black cold throat of the mountain, with her father's tools somewhere below in the dark and a government's patience eleven days from running out, and it was not gentle and it was not brief, and when it ended she pressed her mouth to Ulyana's ear and said, "I built bridges so things would stay up. Don't make me regret the metaphor. I love you too. I have since you showed me the map and didn't explain it. That's when I knew—when you trusted me with a silence."

From deeper in the tunnel, Tomas's voice floated back, carefully casual in the way of a sixteen-year-old who has learned exactly how much adults pretend not to notice: "There's a side gallery up here. Chalk marks on the wall. Old ones—but Ulyana, there are *new* ones too. Fresh chalk. Over old chalk."

The lantern came bobbing back toward them, and Ilse felt Ulyana go still against her—the particular stillness of a woman looking at handwriting only one dead man had ever taught her to read.

"My father's cipher," Ulyana said. "But the fresh marks aren't mine. And they weren't here when I came through three years ago."
