## 13. Halfway and Hunted

The garrison at Kolvach Junction had been a grain depot before the annexation, and it still smelled like one—dust and old wheat and the sweet rot of spilled barley gone to sugar in the walls. Corin Vale stood in the checkpoint office with its single window facing the tracks, and he thought, not for the first time, that his ministry had a genius for posting him in places that smelled of things slowly going bad.

The stationmaster had given up his desk. The telegraph key sat under a dust cloth like a caged bird. On the wall behind it hung a framed photograph of the Director of Archives shaking hands with a regional governor, both men smiling the smiles of men who had never once been cold or hungry or unsure. Vale had taken the photograph down and set it face-first on the shelf. He told himself this was tidiness.

Four days out from the survey camp. Four days of riding third-class carriages through country that got poorer and more stubborn the further south it went, reading Ulyana Thornbury's trail in the way he'd learned to read everything: in ledgers, in manifests, in the small bureaucratic debris a party of five leaves behind when it moves fast and pays cash. A bridge engineer's tools crated at a freight office in Marrow Fen—crated twice, because the first crate was too small, which meant someone who cared about her tools. Three tickets bought and a fourth refunded, then four bought again, which meant a stowaway promoted. Feed for two mules at a livery that also sold rope, and rope was interesting, because rope meant trestles, and trestles meant they were taking the old Vessmarki branch lines.

They were following the dead country's spine north. Which meant they were following the map Vale kept locked in the drawer beneath his bunk—the one his grandfather had drawn in exile, in ink faded to the brown of old tea, showing a nation that no longer existed as though it were still going somewhere.

He had not slept properly since Marrow Fen. In the glass of the window his reflection looked like a man who had been argued with for a long time and had begun, dangerously, to lose.

The telegram from the ministry had come at dawn. He read it again now, though he knew it by heart:

*THORNBURY PARTY LOCATED APPROACHING KOLVACH JCT STOP GARRISON COMMANDER COOPERATIVE STOP DETAIN AND RECOVER CHEST BY ANY MEANS NECESSARY STOP DIRECTOR'S OFFICE EXPECTS COMPLIANCE WITHOUT FURTHER INCIDENT.*

*By any means necessary.* Four words that did all their work in the last one. He had spent eleven years in the basement archives learning what happened when men said *by any means necessary* about paper. Paper burned. People near paper burned. He had seen the requisition orders from forty years ago—the ones marked *disposal, complete*—that had turned a nation of twelve million into an administrative footnote. He had filed them himself, cataloguing them, and every night some part of him understood that filing a thing is not the same as being innocent of it.

"Your prisoners are coming up the valley road," said Sergeant Dray, the garrison's second-in-command, a broad young man with a soldier's gift for making other people's emergencies feel routine. "Commander's put six men on the platform. Wants your word on how you want it done."

"How I want it done."

"You're the ministry man. Commander figures it's your arrest."

Vale looked out the window. The valley road ran along the river, and the river ran silver in the late-morning light, and coming up it, small with distance, were five figures and two mules: a tall engineer in a dusty coat, a broad-shouldered woman carrying a tool chest like it weighed nothing, a boy, a lean man walking slightly apart, and—

Vale blinked. Looked again. Counted.

Five figures. Marek Voss was supposed to be among them. The reports from the pass country said four now, not five; something had split the party somewhere above the viaduct. He filed that away. Splits were useful. Splits meant leverage.

"They'll be tired," Vale said. "Hungry. They've been climbing for days. We do this quietly. No rifles on the platform. I'll talk to them alone."

Dray raised an eyebrow the way soldiers raise eyebrows at civilians. "Alone, sir?"

"I'm an archivist, Sergeant. My weapon is paperwork." He almost smiled. It didn't take. "Six armed men surrounding a tired girl and a boy makes noise, and noise makes witnesses, and witnesses make this a story instead of a file. I'd rather have a file."

What he did not say—what he had been not saying to himself since Marrow Fen—was that he wanted to see her face when she understood what he was offering. He needed to see whether she was what the file said she was: a courier, an opportunist, a woman selling a dead nation's ghost to the highest bidder. Because if she wasn't—if she was something else—then the order in his breast pocket was aimed at the wrong target entirely, and Vale had built his whole careful life on never aiming at the wrong target.

* * *

Ulyana Thornbury came into the checkpoint office the way water comes into a dry channel—no wasted motion, no sound, filling the space exactly. Up close she was thinner than her file photograph, sunburned across the cheekbones, hands nicked and rope-scarred. Her eyes went around the room once: the window, the door, the sergeant by the door, the second exit behind the coal stove. Then they settled on Vale with an expression he recognized because he had seen it in mirrors his whole life—the look of someone doing arithmetic about you.

"You're the archivist," she said.

"Corin Vale. Ministry of Interior, Office of Records and Disposition."

"Disposition." She almost laughed. "That's an honest word for it."

"It's the word we agreed on forty years ago, Ms. Thornbury. It's the only honest word anyone signed." He gestured to the chair across the desk. She didn't take it. She stayed standing, weight on the balls of her feet, a woman in a room full of soldiers refusing to be seated among them. "You've been hard to catch."

"I've been easy to follow. There's a difference."

"There is." He opened the folder he'd brought—not the operational file but the one he'd assembled himself, in the nights on the train, by lamplight, the way he used to assemble histories back when history was beautiful to him. "I'm going to make you an offer, and then I'm going to tell you something true, and then you can decide what kind of person I am. Fair?"

"Archivists aren't fair. They're accurate."

"That too." He laid the papers out. "Amnesty. Full amnesty, for you and everyone traveling with you—the boy included, the bridge-builder included, all charges dissolved. Your survey contract honored and paid. A stipend. In exchange, the chest comes to me, unopened by any authority but mine, and goes into deep storage where no one will ever see it again. No trial. No disappearance. You walk away rich and free and nobody ever speaks of Vessmark in your presence again."

"And the documents inside."

"Deep storage means deep storage. Not destroyed—destroyed would be a lie, and I don't offer lies—but sealed. Catalogued. Buried so thoroughly that in fifty years even the people who buried them won't remember why."

She studied him. Outside, a freight ground past on the main line, and the windows buzzed with it, and neither of them looked.

"You've read what's in the chest," she said.

"I've read the summaries. The originals were destroyed—or so the record claims." He let that hang. "A pass through the Karst. A border drawn along watershed lines instead of treaty lines. Four treaties invalidated. Yes. I know what I'm hunting."

"Do you know what happens if it comes out?"

"I know what happens if it comes out *wrong*. A journalist in a border town, a minister with an election, a general with an ambition—and suddenly there are armies in the Karst passes and the maps my grandfather bled to draw get redrawn in blood again." His voice came out lower than he intended. He steadied it. "I am not the villain of this file, Ms. Thornbury. I'm the man who reads the endings."

"The endings are written by whoever holds the pen."

"Yes," Vale said. "And I'm offering you mine."

For a long moment she said nothing. Then she reached into her coat—not fast, and Dray's hand moved toward his holster and Vale stopped it with one lifted finger—and brought out a letter. Old paper, soft at the folds, addressed in faded ink. She didn't hand it over. She just held it flat on her palm between them, like a card played face-up.

"This came out of the chest," she said. "It names my father. It names him as custodian of that pass—a man who spent his life keeping a secret so heavy it ate him, until he walked into the mountains one autumn and never came back. I spent thirty years not knowing whether he left because of me. Now I know he left because of *this*, and you're asking me to hand it to the same ministry that erased the country he died serving." Her voice stayed level, which was somehow worse than shouting. "So here's my answer, Mr. Vale. No. Not for the amnesty. Not for the money. And not because I doubt you'd keep your word—I think you might be the only man in your ministry who would. But because some things aren't mine to bury. They're mine to carry, and I intend to finish carrying them."

Vale looked at the letter on her palm. He could see the watermark in the paper—Vessmarki rag stock, the double-star seal pressed into it, the same watermark as the sheet in his own breast pocket. His grandfather's last letter, written from a resettlement camp, three lines long, ending *keep something of us, even if it is only the habit of grief.*

He had carried that habit for fifty years. It had made him excellent at his work and very quiet at dinner parties and unable, lately, to sleep.

"Then I'm required to detain you," he said. "And I will be required to use the six men outside, and the garrison has orders from the Director's office to recover the chest *by any means necessary*, and I want you to understand precisely what that phrase costs before you refuse me twice."

"I already refused you once."

"I noticed."

* * *

Afterward Vale could never fully reconstruct the next ten minutes. Memory kept the pieces and lost the order: the way Ulyana stepped back from the desk; Dray's whistle; boots on gravel; the commander's voice rising on the platform; Ilse Brandt setting down her tool chest with the deliberate care of a woman choosing which weapon to pick up; the boy Tomas moving to stand beside Ulyana, chin up, terrified and immovable; the mules shifting; the light going strange and gold as cloud crossed the sun.

And Vale standing in the doorway of the office, holding the telegram, watching six rifles begin to organize themselves around five exhausted people, and hearing his grandfather's letter say *keep something of us* in a voice like dry paper.

"Sergeant," he said. "Stand down."

Dray looked at him. "Sir?"

"Stand the men down. All of them. Now."

"Commander's orders—"

"The commander's orders are subordinate to the ministry's representative, and the ministry's representative is telling you to stand down." His voice did not rise. It did something worse; it flattened, became the voice he used in the basement when a director's aide tried to walk out with documents in his coat. "Now, Sergeant."

They stood down. Rifles came off their shoulders and went to parade rest, which was a different posture and everyone knew it. On the platform, five people who had braced to die stood breathing in the gold light, not running, because running would give the moment back to the rifles.

Vale walked out onto the boards. The sun came out from behind the cloud and lit the whole valley—the river, the rails, the white shoulders of the Karst far south, the passes that were, right now, in this exact week of the dry season, beginning to close.

"Twelve hours," he said to Ulyana. His mouth felt like he was speaking someone else's words. Maybe he was. Maybe his grandfather was speaking through him, or maybe just the younger man who had once loved maps for their beauty. "The telegraph line east is down—storm damage, I'll confirm it myself, and it will stay down. The garrison's relief column doesn't move without fresh orders, and fresh orders can't arrive for twelve hours. That is how long you have to reach the switchbacks. After that, Ms. Thornbury, I have no choice left, and neither do you."

Ulyana looked at him for a long time. Whatever she saw in his face, she didn't name it aloud. She picked up her pack.

"Why?" was all she asked.

Vale took the folded telegram from his pocket—the order, *by any means necessary*—and held it up where she and the sergeant and the whole gold-lit platform could see it. Then he tore it in half. Then in half again. He dropped the pieces on the boards and watched the wind off the river worry them against the planks.

"Because forty years ago men like me erased a country and called it disposition," he said. "And I have spent my life filing the evidence, and I am tired, Ms. Thornbury. Tired down to the ledger-line. Go."

They went. Down the valley road, five figures and two mules, smaller and smaller, toward the white passes. The boy looked back once. The engineer did not.

Sergeant Dray came and stood beside him. "They'll call that treason, sir."

"Yes," Vale said. "They will."

"What do we tell the commander?"

Vale watched the road until it emptied. Somewhere south, in the high silence, a judge was waiting for a letter, and a man named Marek Voss—who was not among those five, who had gone ahead with copies, with intentions Vale could only guess at—was climbing toward the same summit from the other side. Twelve hours. It was either mercy or the most elaborate self-destruction of his career, and by nightfall he would know which.

"We tell him the truth," Vale said. "The telegraph is down. Nothing else arrived."

He turned and walked back into the office that smelled of rotting grain, and began, with steady hands, composing the report he would send when the line came back up—a report in which Corin Vale, acting on intelligence received, attempted interception at Kolvach Junction and regretted the party's head start due to circumstances beyond his control.

Halfway through writing it, he stopped. Took out his grandfather's letter. Read the three lines again, the last one worn soft as cloth from fifty years of thumbs.

Then he took a blank sheet of Vessmarki paper from the chest's packing straw—evidence, technically; a relic, honestly—and wrote at the top of it, in his neat archival hand, a date and a single sentence:

*Kolvach Junction. Let them climb.*

He folded it away, next to his grandfather's letter, and finished the report.
