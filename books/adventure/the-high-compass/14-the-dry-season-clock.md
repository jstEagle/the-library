## 14. The Dry Season Clock

The wind changed on the third morning in the valley, and I knew before anyone said a word.

It came down off the Karst ridge like something escaping—a hot, mineral-smelling breath that rattled the loose panes of the abandoned station house where we'd sheltered and carried with it the smell of dust that hadn't seen rain in months. Ilse stood in the doorway with her face turned into it, and I watched her jaw tighten the way it did over bad steel: not fear exactly, but recognition.

"That's foehn," she said. "Dry wind off the high rock."

"I know what it is."

"Then you know what it means." She turned to look at me. "The passes will close early this year. Weeks early. Maybe more."

I had known. I had woken at dawn with the taste of it already on my teeth, and my first thought had not been of the passes but of the letter folded against my chest—my father's careful hand, his route to Judge Amalia Vess, the sanatorium above the snowline that would be unreachable once the wind did its work. We had planned for a dry season. We had not planned for this one.

Tomas came out of the station house carrying our food sacks the way he always did, slung across both shoulders like a man hauling guilt. He set them down on the platform between us without being asked. He had started doing inventory unprompted two days ago, and neither of us had commented on it, because commenting would have meant admitting what the inventory was telling us.

"Bread's done," he said. "Dried meat for six days if we're honest, four if we're hungry. Tea. Salt. Half the sugar went when we crossed the ford." He paused. "And the fruit Marek traded for in Vellin. Whatever that was."

"It was apricots," Ilse said. "Bad ones."

"They were an experience," Tomas said, and for one moment we all almost smiled, and the almost was its own kind of ration—something you portion out because you don't know when you'll get another.

I spread the map on the platform boards and weighted the corners with stones. The valley route ran east along the old mainline, gentle grades, water every day, a garrison town at the far end where Vale's word would already have arrived if his letters moved faster than we did—which they did; paper always traveled lighter than people. Ten days on the valley route, if nothing went wrong.

The Karst switchbacks went up. Straight up, through tunnels that had been collapsing since before any of us were born, along ledges cut by engineers who had died believing their nation would live forever. Five days, if the rock held. No water after the second spring. No way back once you committed past the third tunnel.

Nobody said any of this out loud, because we'd all read the same map.

"The valley," Tomas said finally. "Safer."

"The valley has soldiers in it," I said. "Vale's telegram reached the garrison towns days ago. We'd walk into a checkpoint with a chest full of treason and no way to run except along a rail line they control."

"So we go up." He looked at the mountains the way a boy looks at something he's decided to be unafraid of. "Through the switchbacks."

"We go up," I said, "if everyone chooses it. Nobody gets dragged into the Karst because I decided for them."

Ilse snorted softly. It wasn't quite a laugh. "You said that at the trestle too. And at the viaduct. You keep offering us exits we both know we won't take."

"I keep meaning it."

"I know." She crouched beside the map, traced the switchback line with one blunt finger, counting the hairpins under her breath the way she counted loads on a bridge—out loud, precise, as if arithmetic could hold the mountain accountable. "Eleven hairpins. Two tunnels I wouldn't send a goat into. And here—" her finger stopped at a jagged mark I'd inked myself, years ago, from a surveyor's report older than either of us— "a rockfall zone three hundred meters long with no cover. If the wind's up, we cross it blind in dust."

"Then we cross it at night," I said. "Cold rock doesn't move."

"Cold rock doesn't move," she agreed. "But cold hands drop things." She looked up at me then, and there was something in her face beyond the engineering—the thing that had been growing between us since the fog on the viaduct, quiet and load-bearing, like a member neither of us had drawn on the plans but both of us leaned on. "Six days of food for a five-day climb. That's a margin of one. One delay, one injury, one wrong turn, and we starve on a ledge with a beautiful view."

"One margin is still a margin."

"That's the most engineer thing you've ever said, and I've heard you defend a culvert."

Tomas laughed outright this time, short and startled out of him, and the sound bounced off the empty platform and died fast, like laughter does in dead places.

We voted with our feet. By noon the packs were repacked and the chest rode in its sling between Ilse and me, and we left the valley behind and started up the first of the eleven hairpins while the foehn wind combed the grass flat and the sun climbed like something with an appointment.

* * *

Marek had been quiet since Vellin. Quieter than quiet—he had gone past silence into some interior country of his own, and I caught glimpses of him there: the way he touched the chest strap when he thought no one saw, the way he recited, under his breath, fragments of a language only Tomas could have confirmed and Tomas never let on that he heard. On the second hairpin, while Ilse scouted ahead and Tomas dozed against a cut-stone abutment, Marek sat beside me on a fallen rail and said, "Tell me about him again. Your father."

"There's not much to tell."

"You told me he built locks that opened to a poem instead of a key."

"He taught me cipher-lockwork when I was nine. Said a lock should ask a question only the right person knows how to answer." I kept my eyes on the trail. "Then he taught me that some questions shouldn't be answered by anyone, and disappeared to prove it."

"And you hate him for it."

"No." The word came out faster than I intended, which is how I knew it was true. "I understand him now. There's a difference, and the difference costs about forty years."

Marek nodded slowly. In the hard light his face looked carved, all planes and shadow, exile features—his people had worn that face so long it had become geology. "My grandmother used to say the Karst engineers were the last honest men in Vessmark. Everyone else lied with words. They lied with borders." He smiled, thin. "She'd have liked your father."

"She'd have hated what he did with the pass."

"She'd have understood it. Same difference, forty years." He stood, brushed grit from his knees, and offered me a hand up—an old courtesy, courtly in a way that sat strangely on a man who owned nothing but a name he might be making up. His grip was warm and brief. "Five days, Thornbury. Then we give the judge her choice, whatever it is."

"Yes," I said. "Whatever it is."

He walked ahead toward the next pin, and I watched him go, and I noticed—for the first time, though I would tell myself later that I'd known—that the shape of his pack had changed since morning. Bulkier at the flap. Heavier in the way of paper.

I said nothing. That is the sentence I have turned over most often since. I said nothing, because five days from judgment, with one margin of food and a party held together by habit and hope, I chose the expedition over the truth. Every consequence I can trace back to that platform decision starts there: not with Marek's copying, but with my silence about it.

* * *

That night we camped inside the mouth of the fourth tunnel, because the wind had risen to a steady moan and the open ledges were streaming with dust fine as flour. Ilse rigged the tarp into a windbreak with a competence that bordered on tenderness. Tomas built a fire the size of a cupped hand—just enough to heat water, just enough to feel like civilization—and read aloud from a Vessmarki school primer he'd found in a station cupboard, translating badly and proudly, teaching us the word for *bridge* and the word for *home* and discovering, with visible delight, that they were nearly the same word.

*Most,* he said. *Almost home.* "They had one word for both. Isn't that something?"

"Islands do that," Marek said from the dark end of the tunnel. "Places surrounded by what wants to erase them."

I lay with my head on my pack and listened to them trade words across the fire, and I felt the thing I felt on every expedition since I was nineteen: that these accidental families are the truest countries I've ever held papers in. And beneath that feeling, colder, the clock. Six days of food becoming five by tomorrow's accounting. The judge at the top of the mountain getting older by the hour. Vale somewhere below us, or behind us, or ahead of us, being dutiful.

Ilse lay down beside me, shoulder to shoulder, the way we'd learned to sleep in cold places—sharing warmth without announcing it. For a long time neither of us spoke. Then, very quietly, so the others couldn't hear:

"When this is done. After." She stopped. Started again, more Ilse-like: "There's a gorge outside Brast, two hours from anywhere, with a river that needs a bridge. Nothing grand. A crossing for farmers. I've had the design in my head for six years and no reason to build it."

"What's the reason now?"

"You," she said, simply, as if reporting a load calculation. "You're the reason. I want to build things again and have you standing on them."

I turned my head in the dark and found her eyes. "Ask me properly, then."

"After," she said. "When we've given the old woman her choice. When we come down off this rock alive. I'm asking properly then, and I expect a proper answer, and I'm telling you now so you can't pretend to be surprised."

"I'm never surprised by good engineering," I said, "only by how long it takes people to commit to it."

Her hand found mine in the dark and closed around it, callus to callus. Above the tunnel mouth, through the smoke-haze, the stars over the Karst burned with the pitiless clarity of high dry air, and somewhere in that black the passes were tightening like a fist around the season, and I held her hand and let myself, for exactly one minute, want the future she was describing—a bridge, a river, a town small enough to know us.

One minute. Then I slept, because tomorrow we crossed the rockfall zone, and cold hands drop things.

* * *

The rockfall took four hours of the fifth day and everything we had.

We crossed it in the blue hour before dawn, roped together, moving through dust so thick the headlamps returned nothing but a brown wall. The mountain groaned around us—not dramatically, nothing so honest as a roar, but a long grinding complaint deep in the rock, like a giant shifting in a sleep it might wake from angry. Twice the slope shed small rivers of stone that hissed past our boots and vanished downward. Once a slab the size of a cart body cracked loose fifty meters above and went through the space where Marek had been standing three seconds earlier, and none of us said anything about it, because there was nothing to say that the sound hadn't already said better.

On the far side, in the first sunlight, Tomas counted us twice by touch, like a mother animal, before he would speak.

Rations after that crossing: four days of meat, split thin. The spring Ilse had promised was a seep, barely enough to fill bottles, and we waited an hour for it and drank the hour too. The eighth hairpin revealed the seventh had partially collapsed behind us—not blocking the way down, but ending any thought of retreat, which I noted in my head with the strange calm of a person reading their own diagnosis.

And it was there, on the eighth hairpin, waiting for the others to come up the pitch, that I saw it.

Marek sat apart on a ledge, hunched forward, and the light through the dust haze fell across his knees, and on his knees was a page—one of the survey sheets, the border-changing ones, the ones that lived in the chest and nowhere else. He held a pencil stub. His lips moved as he worked, tracing lines, committing numbers to a notebook small enough to disappear into a coat pocket, copying my father's inheritance onto paper that could outlive any fire we might make of the original.

He didn't see me. The wind covered my step. I stood in the shadow of the cut with my heart going like a locomotive and my hand resting on the cold iron of the chest sling, and I understood, with total clarity, that the mission had two destinations and always had: the judge at the summit, and whatever Marek intended to do with those pages after.

Below us, faint but unmistakable, drifting up the switchbacks on the hot wind, came a sound I had hoped not to hear until the lowlands and had feared hearing here since Vellin.

A whistle. Three notes, repeated. The army signal for *column halt, advance and search*—the signal Vale's garrison troops used, the signal every soldier in four nations learned, and it was coming from the valley we had abandoned, climbing toward us through the hairpins, closing the route behind us the way the dry season was closing the sky.

Marek's pencil stopped. He had heard it too.

"Tomas," I called down the pitch, keeping my voice level, keeping my eyes on Marek. "Get Ilse. Now."
