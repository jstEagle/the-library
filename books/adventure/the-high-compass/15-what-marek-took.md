## 15. What Marek Took

I wasn't looking for anything. That's the part I keep coming back to, later, when I try to decide whether what I did was right or just convenient.

We'd made camp in the mouth of an old maintenance tunnel off the Karst switchbacks, the kind with a rusted gate that swung on one hinge and a floor of gravel that had once been ballast. Ilse had the fire down to coals. Ulyana was up top somewhere, checking the sky, because for two days the wind had been coming out of the east and dry, and dry wind in the Karst meant the passes were closing ahead of schedule. Marek was supposed to be sleeping.

I was supposed to be sleeping too. Instead I was awake the way fifteen-year-olds are awake, which is to say my body was exhausted and my head was running like a locomotive with the brake cut. So I went looking for my boots. That was all. My boots, because I'd kicked them off near the tunnel mouth and the gravel was cold and I wanted them before I tried to sleep again.

Marek's rucksack was next to them. He'd left it open.

I want to say I didn't look. I want to tell you I zipped it shut and went back to my bedroll and slept the sleep of a person who trusted the people he'd walked a hundred miles beside. But the truth is smaller and worse than that: I saw the corner of a page, and the page had lines on it, and the lines were drawn in the careful hand of a surveyor, and I knew — the way you know a thing before you know you know it — that those lines had come out of the chest.

I pulled the packet out.

* * *

There were eleven pages. Copies — good ones, done in pencil, pressed hard enough that you could feel the ridges on the back like braille. The Vessmarki survey sheets, the ones Ulyana had counted and recounted every night since the second cache, the ones that proved the pass through the Karst and unraveled four treaties like loose thread. Marek had traced them. Every contour. Every elevation mark. Every notation in that old, precise hand, including the seal at the bottom of each sheet, which he'd rendered with a care that made my stomach hurt, because you don't draw a dead nation's double-star that carefully unless you mean to use it.

There was something else folded behind them. A letter, half-written, in Marek's own hand. I read it before I could stop myself.

*To the editor of the Continental Review —*

*Enclosed are authenticated survey documents of the Vessmark Engineering Corps, recovered from the Sorrel flood, demonstrating a navigable pass through the central Karst. The implications for the Treaty of Aldenmere, the Sorrel Accords, and the annexation instruments of 40 years past are —*

It stopped there. The rest was blank. But the address on the envelope was filled in, and the stamp was already stuck on, and that was the thing that turned my cold. He'd been ready. He'd been ready for days, and he'd smiled at us over the fire, and taught me Vessmarki words, and told me stories about the royal engineers like they were bedtime stories, and the whole time his hands had been moving in the dark with a pencil.

I heard gravel shift above me and I did the coward's arithmetic. I put the pages back the way they'd lain — not exactly, I'd learn later, not quite exactly — folded the letter in behind them, and stood there holding my boots in my hands like an idiot while Marek came down into the tunnel.

He looked at me. He looked at the rucksack. Something moved across his face, quick as a bird's shadow, and then he smiled the warm smile, the one that had gotten me to teach him my father's name and my mother's grave and every other thing I'd never told anyone.

"Couldn't sleep either?" he said.

"No," I said. "Couldn't sleep."

I put my boots on. I went back to my bedroll. I lay there listening to him settle in across the coals, and I thought about loyalty, and what it weighed, and who had ever actually earned mine.

* * *

Here is what you need to know about me, so you can judge me fairly, or unfairly — people usually do one or the other and it doesn't matter much which.

I was born in a border town the year after the annexation, which means I was born into a country that had already finished pretending Vessmark had never existed. My father spoke Vessmarki under his breath, only, like a man praying in a language the walls might report. My mother sang the lullabies in it and called it "the old songs," as if they'd been old the way mountains are old and not the way murdered things are old. They died in the cholera year. I went into the rail yards after that, because the rail yards took anybody who could carry a lantern, and I taught myself to read Vessmarki from a water-damaged grammar book I found in a signal box, and I never told anyone why it mattered to me.

Until Marek. Marek was the first person who ever said a sentence to me in that language and waited, without laughing, for me to answer in it. He called me *mladý inženýr* — young engineer — and I would have walked into fire for him. I think I mostly did.

So when I lay awake in that tunnel with the copied pages burning a hole in the shape of themselves into my memory, I wasn't choosing between honesty and betrayal. Not yet. I was choosing between two kinds of loyalty, and nobody had warned me they could point in opposite directions.

* * *

Morning came up gray and dry. The wind had a sound to it now, a thin whistle through the tunnel gate, and Ulyana stood outside with their collar up, looking at the mountains the way you look at a clock.

"We move in an hour," they said. "The valley route's done. It's the switchbacks or we winter in a country that isn't on anyone's map anymore."

Nobody argued. Ilse was already breaking down the fire. And I stood there with my hands in my pockets, around the folded letter I'd taken — just the letter, just the half-written one, because I'd needed proof and I'd needed it out of his reach — and my heart was going so hard I was sure everyone could hear it.

I tried three times to get Ulyana alone. The first time, Marek was with them. The second time, Ilse was. The third time, the wind knocked a rock loose off the switchback above us and we all spent twenty minutes making sure it hadn't started anything bigger, and by then we were moving, climbing, and the trail was too narrow for conversation and too loud with gravel for confession.

It's funny, the things you tell yourself. I told myself I was protecting him — that if I gave him a chance to confess, to put the copies back, we could go on, the five of us, the way we'd been. I told myself that a lot of the trouble in the world comes from people reporting things too fast, and that I, of all people, knew what it was to have your private language dragged into the light by someone who meant well.

But underneath that, in the honest cellar of it, I was scared. Scared of his face when he found out I'd looked. Scared of losing the one person who spoke my mother's language. Scared that I'd already lost him, the moment I'd seen the stamp on the envelope.

We made maybe six miles before the trail widened at a collapsed switchback house, a stone hut with half its roof peeled back like a tin lid, and Ulyana called a rest, and Marek went ahead around the corner to look at the path, and I walked up to Ulyana with my hands shaking and gave them the letter.

* * *

Ulyana read it standing up. That's the detail I can't shake — they didn't sit, didn't lean against the hut wall, didn't do any of the things a person does when the ground is going out from under them. They just stood, in the dry wind, and read Marek's half-letter twice, and then folded it along its existing creases, very precisely, the way their father must have folded things, and put it inside their coat.

"Tomas," they said. "When did you find this."

"Last night. His rucksack was open. I wasn't—" I stopped, because *I wasn't looking* was a lie of exactly the size I'd decided not to tell anymore. "I looked. I'm not going to pretend I didn't. There are eleven pages, Ulyana. Traced copies. The whole survey. He's got an envelope addressed to a newspaper and a stamp already on it."

Ulyana closed their eyes for one breath. When they opened them, they weren't angry. That was worse. They looked like a person doing arithmetic they'd been avoiding for a long time.

"Say nothing," they said. "Watch his pack. When we camp tonight, we take the copies back quiet, and he wakes up lighter and we're all still walking the same direction."

"No."

I said it before I knew I was going to. Ulyana turned.

"No," I said again, and my voice cracked in the middle of it like I was eight years old instead of almost grown. "Because that's what everyone does to us, don't you see? That's what the whole world did to Vessmark. Quiet. Things taken back quiet. People wake up lighter and pretend nothing happened. I've watched him, Ulyana. He's not doing it for money. He's doing it because he thinks we're burying his country a second time. You can't fix that by stealing his pages while he sleeps. You have to say it out loud. You have to let it be loud."

The wind came through the gap in the roof and lifted the dust between us.

"You understand what you're asking," Ulyana said quietly.

"Yes," I said, and my eyes were burning and I hated it. "I understand exactly what I'm asking. I'm asking you to break him in front of me. Because I can't carry it anymore."

* * *

They didn't wait for camp. Later I understood why — Ulyana had spent a lifetime around secrets and knew the specific gravity of one going bad — but in the moment it felt like being thrown off a moving train.

They called Marek back from the head of the trail. He came around the corner with that easy stride, brushing stone dust off his sleeves, and he saw the letter in Ulyana's hand, and I watched the warm smile come up onto his face and hold there, trembling, like a bridge under too much weight.

"Tomas," he said. "What did you do?"

"What did *I* do?" Something in me came up hot and fast, all the weeks of it. "You traced eleven pages of the survey and addressed an envelope to a newspaper, Marek. I read it. I read all of it."

"Tomas." He said my name the way my father used to say it, gently, in the old language first and then in ours, and I felt it land like a hook. "You went through my bag."

"Your bag was open and your letter was *about us*." My voice was doing things I couldn't control. "You sat at our fire. You taught me words. You knew what the chest meant to them—" I pointed at Ulyana, at the letter, at the whole buried history of it— "and you were going to make it a headline."

"And what else was I going to do with it?" And there it was — the crack, right down the middle of him, the thing I'd half-seen for weeks and refused to look at straight. His voice went up and up. "Put it back in the box? Carry it to some judge in a sanatorium so she can lock it in another room for another forty years? My grandfather *died* on those switchbacks, Tomas. My whole family walked out of Vessmark with nothing but their names, and half of them had to leave the names behind too, because names got you stopped at garrisons. And you three want to carry the one true thing my country ever built up a mountain and *burn it* because it's safer. Because it's *tidier*."

"It's not ours to publish," Ulyana said. "It's not yours either."

"Whose, then?" Marek spread his hands at the mountains, at the dead country all around us. "The treaties'? The ministries'? The archivist chasing us with his little stamped forms? A nation that doesn't exist can't own its own survey, Ulyana. That's the whole point. That's the whole *tragedy*. Somebody has to speak for the people who can't be in the room anymore, and if it isn't me, it's nobody."

"It isn't paper that keeps them," Ulyana said. "It's this." And they touched their coat, where the letter was — not the survey, not the pass, just the letter, the one addressed to the Thornbury line. "You want to resurrect a country out of a border dispute. That's not resurrection, Marek. That's just finding a new way to spend the dead."

For a long moment nobody moved. The wind whistled through the tin roof. Then Marek looked at me, and it was the worst part of the whole day, worse than the shouting, worse than the letter. He looked at me the way you look at a station platform after your train has left it.

"You could have come to me," he said. "One word, Tomas. In our language. You could have said one word."

"I'm saying it now," I said, and my voice broke all the way through this time, and I let it. "That's why I did it this way. Loud. Out loud. So you'd know I wasn't doing it *to* you."

He nodded, slowly, like a man accepting a verdict. Then he swung the rucksack off his shoulder, and before any of us could move, he had the packet of traced pages out and pressed against his chest with both hands, and he backed up two steps onto the upper trail.

"I'm sorry about the letter," he said to Ulyana. "I'm not sorry about the rest."

"Marek—" Ilse started, and she'd come up behind us somewhere in the shouting, and her face was doing something complicated, because she'd shared a rope with this man on the viaduct, she'd trusted him with her weight in the fog.

"Twelve miles ahead there's a fork," he said. "Left goes to the sanatorium. Right goes over the summit shoulder. I'm taking the right." He looked at me one more time. *Mladý inženýr*, he said, and it didn't sound like a compliment anymore. It sounded like a door closing. "Don't follow me. One of us should get to say what happened to Vessmark."

Then he turned and went up the switchbacks, fast, sure-footed, a man who had walked this country in his head his whole life, and the dry wind took him, and he was gone around the hairpin before Ilse finished saying his name the second time.

* * *

We stood there. Four of us, then — no. Three. I keep doing that, counting him in.

Ilse looked at Ulyana. "We go after him. He can't climb the summit shoulder alone with the wind coming up like this."

"He's not alone," Ulyana said. "He's got a newspaper and a stamp." They looked at the fork in the trail, far below us now, the left hand and the right hand of it, and I watched them weigh it — the judge, the deadline, the dry-season clock ticking in the wind — and I saw them understand, maybe for the first time, that the mission had grown a second destination, and that the second one was uphill.

"Tomas." Ulyana turned to me, and I braced for it, for the blame, because I'd earned some, I'd looked, I'd taken the letter, I'd made it loud.

But all they said was: "You did the harder thing. Most people never learn it. I'm sorry it had to be this one."

I nodded, because I couldn't get any words through. Somewhere above us, small with distance, a figure moved along the summit shoulder, heading for the pass, carrying eleven pages of a dead nation in his coat.

Ilse shouldered her pack. "Then we'd better move," she said. "Because if he reaches that summit, and Vale's soldiers reach that summit, and a journalist reaches that summit — all in the same week the wind closes the passes —"

"Then everything we carried this chest for happens without us," Ulyana said, and started up the left-hand trail, toward the sanatorium, toward Judge Amalia Vess, at a pace that told me they'd already stopped mourning and started racing.
