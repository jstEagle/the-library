## 16. The Karst Switchbacks

The wind came up out of the south at dawn, dry as paper and hot as an apology, and Ilse knew before anyone said it that the valley route was dead.

She stood on the last intact platform of the Karst Valley station—a roofless shed where the timetable board still hung, its enamel face blistered by forty summers—and watched the dust move through the gorge in long brown ropes. In the lowlands this would have been nothing. Up here it meant the passes were closing early. It meant every day of safe travel had been spent already.

Ulyana crouched over the map with a pencil stub, drawing and redrawing the same line. "Valley road runs along the riverbed," they said. "By tomorrow noon there'll be no riverbed. Just water."

"Then we go up." Ilse said it before she'd decided to say it. Three faces turned to her—Ulyana's, Tomas's, and the empty space where Marek should have been standing. The space was four days old now and none of them had stopped looking at it.

"The switchbacks," Ulyana said.

"The switchbacks."

Tomas shifted the chest's carrying harness higher on his shoulders. He'd grown into it over the weeks; the boy who'd stowed away outside Sorrel Junction would have buckled under it. Now he just set his jaw the way Ulyana did when they were about to do something stupid and necessary. "How bad?"

Ilse looked up at the mountains. The old military road climbed out of the valley in a series of hairpins cut into the limestone, and above the hairpins ran the tunnels—the Vessmarki engineers' masterpiece, eleven of them bored straight through the shoulders of the Karst so that supply trains could reach the high garrisons without ever exposing themselves to artillery from across the border. She'd read about them in a monograph she'd once loved and later pretended not to own. Bored through living rock, unlined in places, abandoned mid-century with the timber still green.

"Bad," she said honestly. "The tunnels have been shedding their skins for forty years. We go single file, we test every ceiling, and if I say run, you run."

"And if you say run and it's too late?" Tomas asked.

"Then it was always going to be too late," Ilse said, "and I'd rather be wrong running than right standing still."

Ulyana almost smiled. It was the kind of thing they said to each other now—not tenderness exactly, but a shared grammar of risk that felt more intimate than tenderness. Four days since Marek had gone up into the mountains alone with his stolen copies, and the expedition had shrunk to its truest core: three people, one chest, one letter, and whatever it was between the bridge-builder and the engineer that neither of them had time to name properly.

They started climbing while the wind was still cool enough to breathe.

* * *

The first tunnel was the easy one, or seemed so—eighty meters of bore through gray stone, its floor drifted with fine talc-like dust that rose around their boots in pale clouds. Ilse went first with a hammer borrowed from the chest's tool tray, tapping the ceiling every few paces. Solid, solid, solid, then a note like a struck coffin lid, and she detoured wide around that span without slowing, because slowing was how fear got organized.

Behind her came Ulyana with the map case strapped across their back, then Tomas with the chest. The iron box rode against his spine and Ilse could hear him breathing in rhythm with it, a young man learning the weight of other people's history.

At the tunnel's far mouth the world opened onto the first hairpin ledge, and Ilse stopped breathing for a different reason.

The ledge clung to the cliff face like a shelf nailed up by someone in a hurry. Below it the valley floor was already half a kilometer down, the river a bright thread stitching through brown haze. Above, the second tunnel mouth showed as a black slot in the rock, and the only way between them was this ribbon of broken masonry barely wide enough for the chest and its carrier.

"Forty years," Ulyana said quietly beside her. "Nobody's maintained this."

"Nobody's needed to." Ilse knelt and tested the outer edge with her hammer. A slab the size of a dinner plate tipped off into the void and made no sound at all for a very long time. "Vessmark built this to move armies. Then Vessmark stopped existing, and the road kept the shape of its purpose and lost everything else."

"That's most things," Ulyana said.

They crossed the ledge in the order of least weight to greatest: Ilse, Ulyana, then Tomas, unharnessed from the chest, which Ulyana hauled across the worst span on a rope with Ilse belayed below. It took two hours to cover what the engineers had designed trains to cover in ninety seconds. Nobody complained. There was a particular silence that fell over people working above a long drop, and Ilse had learned to love it in her bridge-building days—it was the sound of everyone agreeing to trust each other whether or not they'd earned it.

In the third tunnel, Tomas found the chalk marks.

They were fresh—that was the terrible part. Not his grandfather's cipher, which Ulyana had taught him to read at the signal box a week ago, but simpler: an arrow, a date in the Vessmarki style, and beneath it the double-star seal drawn freehand, slightly lopsided.

"Marek," Tomas said. "He came through here."

"Two days ahead of us," Ulyana said, touching the chalk. Their fingers left prints in the dust. "And moving faster, because he isn't hauling this."

Ilse watched Ulyana stare at that lopsided star for a long moment. Marek had betrayed them, yes—but he had also eaten beside them, argued with them, laughed once, terribly, at Ilse's impression of a ministry clerk. Betrayal by someone you love doesn't remove the love; it just makes every memory load-bearing.

"We're not chasing him," Ulyana said finally, to no one and everyone. "We're going where we're going."

But they walked faster after that.

* * *

The seventh tunnel killed the afternoon.

It began well—wider than the others, lined with brick for its first hundred meters, the workmanship so confident it felt almost mocking. Then the lining ended and the raw rock began, and the raw rock had been listening to forty years of freeze and thaw, and somewhere above them the mountain had decided, in its slow mineral patience, that it was finished holding this particular shape.

Ilse heard it before she saw anything: a sound like a giant clearing its throat, deep in the stone.

"Run," she said.

They ran. The tunnel was four hundred meters long and they had three hundred to go, and the mountain came apart behind them in a rolling roar, dust swallowing the lamplight, slabs the size of carts hitting the floor hard enough to feel through their boots. Ilse ran with her hammer hand trailing the wall, counting mouths of side-drifts as they passed, and the far entrance stayed a gray smudge ahead that refused, insultingly, to get closer.

A secondary fall caught the ceiling twenty meters from daylight.

Ilse heard Ulyana shout—something about Tomas, *go, go*—and then the light behind her died all at once, and the roar doubled, tripled, became a pressure she felt in her teeth, and when the dust began to settle and her lamp found the tunnel again, the space where Ulyana had been was a wall of broken stone from floor to ceiling.

"TOMAS! Get clear of the face!" Her own voice sounded tiny under the settling grit. "Ulyana!"

Nothing. Dust drifted through the beam of her lamp like snow inside a globe. Somewhere beyond the new wall, muffled to almost nothing, Tomas was shouting her name, and beneath his voice—so faint she might have invented it—another voice, lower, saying something rhythmic and calm.

Counting. Ulyana was counting.

Ilse pressed both palms flat against the rubble. Warm dust, still shifting, grains trickling through gaps like sand through fingers. The collapse had come down as a wedge, she realized—the ceiling had failed along a joint plane and dumped its load in a fan, and fans had edges, and edges meant air on the far side, and air meant—

"Ulyana!" she shouted. "If you can hear me, stop counting and tell me where you are!"

The faint voice stopped counting. Then, muffled, absurdly level:

"Pocket. Left wall. About ten meters in. Ceiling's holding. Don't dig like a miner—dig like an engineer."

Ilse laughed, or sobbed, or both. "Of course you'd have opinions about technique."

"I always have opinions about technique."

She turned to find Tomas at her shoulder, lamp shaking, face gray-white with dust except where tears had cut clean tracks. "Is she—"

"She's alive. She's talking. That means the pocket has air and the pocket has room." Ilse was already stripping off her coat, tying the lamp to a fallen timber so its light fell on the face of the collapse. "Which means we don't panic and pull the wrong stone. We work from the top of the fan, small pieces, and we keep the wedge stable. Do you understand me? The mountain is balanced right now, and our job is to disagree with it gently."

Tomas wiped his face with a dusty hand, making it worse. "Tell me what to do."

That, at least, was simple. Ilse pointed. "You're my hands. I'm your eyes."

* * *

They dug through the night.

It went like this: Ilse read the pile the way she'd once read blueprints, tracing the logic of the failure, finding the keystone slabs and the filler, calling each piece before Tomas touched it. *Left shoulder, hand-sized. Now the plate under it, ease it, don't pry.* The dust was total—they breathed it, ate it, wept it. They worked in shifts of twenty minutes because longer than that the hands stopped obeying, and in the rest shifts they sat back to back against the tunnel wall, passing a single water bottle, saying nothing, because there was nothing to say that the work wasn't already saying.

From beyond the wall, Ulyana talked to them.

Not continuously—at first just status reports, delivered in that infuriating surveyor's cadence. *Air's fine. Ceiling's fine. I've got room to lie flat.* But as the hours ground on and the reports ran out of content, Ulyana started filling the dark with other things. The names of bridges they'd both admired. An argument about whether the great viaduct at Hallow Ford was overbuilt. The recipe, detailed and completely unnecessary, for the plum brandy Ulyana's father used to make.

"He'd bury a crock in the riverbank in spring and dig it up at first frost," Ulyana said through the stone. "Said a thing wasn't done until it had been forgotten on purpose. I think about that a lot lately."

"Keep talking," Ilse said, hauling a slab the size of a loaf aside.

"You think I'm doing it for you."

"I think you're doing it for you. Keep going."

A pause, filled with distant grit-fall. Then, quieter: "When I was buried under the Sorrel bank as a kid—just to the waist, an hour, maybe less—it wasn't the weight that frightened me. It was the quiet. Nobody knew where I was. I swore I'd never be anywhere nobody knew where I was. That's why I never stopped moving. Every town I slept in twice would start knowing where I was." Another pause. "I know where you are, Ilse. Right now. Two meters of rock away, being stubborn with a hammer. It's the safest I've felt in thirty years."

Ilse sat back on her heels. Her arms were trembling and her throat was full of dust and something larger.

"Dig," she said, because her voice would have failed her otherwise. "Save your breath for digging."

But she smiled in the dark, and she thought: *when you're out of there, I'm telling you. No fog, no viaduct, no ambush. Just telling you.*

Around midnight the pocket opened.

It happened fast at the end, the way collapses do—one keystone eased free and the whole upper fan sighed and settled a handspan, and suddenly there was a gap at the base, knee-high, breathing cool air into their faces, and through it a lamp beam swung wildly and Ulyana's voice, no longer muffled at all, said: "Well. Took you long enough."

"Come through headfirst," Ilse ordered. "Slow. If anything shifts—"

"Nothing will shift. You've made it honest." And Ulyana did come through—flat on their belly, dragging themselves through the gap with their arms, dusty as a ghost, one sleeve torn to the elbow, blinking in the light like something exhumed. Tomas grabbed their collar and pulled the last meter, and then all three of them were on the same side of the stone, and nobody spoke, and nobody let go.

After a while Ulyana said, into Ilse's shoulder, "You dug all night."

"You counted all night."

"Somebody had to keep the books."

Tomas was crying again, silently, furiously, the way boys cry when they've discovered the adult version of it. Ilse put a hand on the back of his neck. "You did the work of a builder tonight," she told him. "Both of you. Remember that. Whatever else happens on this mountain—you're builders now."

Above them, through some crack in the world, the first gray suggestion of dawn found the tunnel mouth.

* * *

They came out of the ninth tunnel at midmorning into wind that tasted of iron and altitude, and the view took what little breath the night had left them.

Below, the valley was a brown sea of dust with the river invisible somewhere in it. Above and ahead, the switchbacks climbed toward a col between two peaks, and beyond the col—if the journal was right, if the father's route held—the high plateau where a hidden sanatorium held the last living signatory of a dead nation's charter.

One more day. Perhaps two, if the weather broke wrong.

Ulyana stood at the ledge edge consulting the journal, and Ilse stood close enough that their shoulders touched, and neither of them moved away. Something had been settled in the tunnel that didn't need declaring anymore—or rather, had been declared, in the oldest language either of them knew: *I know where you are. I will always come digging.*

"Look," Tomas said.

He was pointing back along the cliff face, down-slope, at the sixth tunnel mouth they'd passed through yesterday. Chalk marks showed fresh against the black of the bore—newer than Marek's, drawn higher, visible from the ledge.

An arrow pointing up. A date. And beside it, in a hand none of them recognized, a single word in Vessmarki that Tomas read aloud slowly, like a man handling something fragile:

"*Hurry.*"

Ulyana folded the journal closed. The wind pulled at their coat, at the dust still in their hair, at everything.

"They're expecting us," they said. "Someone is expecting us at the col. Either help—" They glanced at Ilse, and the unfinished sentence hung between them in the dry ringing air: *or soldiers.*

Ilse tightened the strap on her ruined hammer, the only tool she had left, and nodded at the switchbacks climbing into the white.

"Then let's not disappoint them," she said.
