## 17. Judge Amalia Vess

The sanatorium did not appear on any map, which by now felt less like a coincidence and more like a signature.

We found it the way my father had intended us to find it: by the locks. The last switchback of the Karst route ended at a rusted gate set into a hillside of pine, and the gate's lock was a Thornbury pattern—his pattern—the tumblers arranged in a sequence I had known since I was six years old and he first put a pick in my small hands and said, *Listen before you force. A lock is a conversation.* I stood in the cold morning wind with the pick trembling between my fingers, listening the way he taught me, and the lock opened like an old man remembering his own name.

Beyond it, a path descended through the trees to a low stone building with white shutters and a bell tower that no longer held a bell. Smoke rose from one chimney. Laundry snapped on a line. Somewhere behind the building, someone was chopping wood with slow, patient strokes.

Ilse put her hand on my shoulder. "Is this it?"

"This is what he left us," I said. "Whether she's still here or not."

Tomas read the plaque by the door aloud, translating the old Vessmarki in a whisper, as if the words might wake something. "House of Rest. Established under charter." He looked up at me. "Under whose charter?"

"We're about to find out."

* * *

She was smaller than I expected. Judge Amalia Vess sat in a wheelchair by a south-facing window, wrapped in a shawl the color of river stones, her hands folded on a lap blanket as though she had been waiting so long for visitors that she'd learned to make waiting look like rest.

A nurse—a broad woman with a woodcutter's forearms—took one look at our dust-caked clothes and Ilse's bandaged arm and said nothing, only led us in, poured tea from a pot that was already warm, and left. As if she'd been told, decades ago, that someday three travelers would come off the mountain and they should be fed without question.

"You have his chin," the judge said to me, before anyone had spoken a word.

My cup stopped halfway to my mouth.

"Edvard Thornbury. He came here in the spring of '61, and again in '63, and once more the year before the annexation, when we both knew there would be no more visits." Her voice was dry paper but steady, a voice that had spent a lifetime ruling on things too heavy for other people to carry. "He always sat in that chair. You're sitting in his chair."

I hadn't noticed which chair I'd chosen. "You knew him."

"I signed his papers. And he signed mine." She studied me with eyes gone pale as winter water but not, I decided, gone dim. "And you've brought me something he never brought himself. I can see it in how you're holding your pack. Nobody holds a bag that carefully unless it matters more than their spine."

I set it down between us and drew out the iron chest, stamped with the double-star seal of a country that had been erased from every atlas for forty years. The judge looked at it for a long moment. Then she closed her eyes.

"So it survived," she said quietly. "All these years, I let myself hope it had drowned."

"It almost did," Ilse said. "The River Sorrel kept it until this spring."

"The Sorrel was always loyal to Vessmark," the judge said, with the ghost of a smile. "It just took its time about it."

Tomas shifted beside me. He had barely spoken in two days—not since Marek went up into the mountains with his copied pages and our trust went with him. But now he leaned forward, and I saw him looking at the seal the way he looked at the few photographs he had of his parents: hungry, careful, afraid to touch.

"My mother's family was from Vessmark," he said suddenly. "Before. I don't even know which town. They burned everything when—" He stopped. "There's nothing left. No records. My grandmother used to say the country didn't die, it was *filed away*."

The judge turned her chair toward him, and something moved across her face that I recognized because I'd spent my whole life carrying its twin: grief for a thing you were too young to save.

"Come here, boy," she said. "Let me look at you."

* * *

It took her most of an hour to open the chest. Not the lock—I opened that for her, and watched her watch my hands do it with an expression I couldn't read. It was the documents inside. She lifted each folio out with both hands, the way you lift a sleeping child, and read every page entire, though she plainly knew what they said. The final surveys. The pass through the Karst. Elevations, gradients, tunnel bearings, all of it drawn in the clean confident hand of Vessmarki engineers who believed, forty years ago, that the future would need what they were building.

And beneath them, sealed in wax that had gone amber with age, the letter addressed to *the Thornbury line.*

She held it out to me. "This is yours by blood. But I'll tell you what's in it first, because I'm still a judge, and I don't hand down verdicts unread."

She spoke slowly, and I wrote every word of it into myself.

"In 1959, four nations signed border treaties at a table in Meridian City. Those treaties are built on one assumption: that the Karst range cannot be crossed by rail above the valley line. Every boundary, every garrison, every tariff agreement north of the Sorrel rests on that assumption like a house on a foundation. This survey"—she touched the folios gently—"removes the foundation.

"If these pages are published, three governments will claim the pass within a year. Two of them will send soldiers. The treaties won't be renegotiated; they'll be torn up, because a treaty is only paper until one side finds it has been sitting on a lie. There will be war in the highlands. Not because the pass is evil—it's the finest piece of engineering I ever certified—but because men with armies do not share doors."

"And if it stays buried?" I asked.

Her hands stilled on the folios.

"Then Vessmark dies twice," she said. "Once in fact, and once in memory. Because those pages aren't just a pass. They're proof. Proof that we existed. That we *built*. That the people who were scattered and renamed and forbidden to speak their own language in school made something the world will still be using the bones of in two hundred years. Burn them, and you give the annexing powers exactly what they wanted: a country that never was." She looked up at me, and her pale eyes were wet and absolutely clear. "Publish, and you kill strangers by the thousand. Bury it, and you murder a people's memory. That is your choice, Ulyana Thornbury. Your father refused to make it, and it broke him. I refused to make it, and it has kept me alive long past any decency."

The room was very quiet. Down the hall, the nurse was washing dishes. Outside, the wind came down off the Karst with the first dry bite of the season in it.

"Why me?" I said finally. "Why did he leave it to me? I didn't ask for any of this."

"No," the judge agreed. "That's rather the point of inheritance." She folded her hands again. "He told me about you, you know. The night train game. Never sleep in the same town twice. He was proud of it and frightened of it in the same breath, because he knew why you ran. You ran along his lines. All of you Thornburys run along each other's lines, generation after generation, like current through wire, trying to find where the circuit breaks."

"I'm not running anymore," I heard myself say. And it surprised me, because I hadn't known it was decided until I said it out loud—hadn't known that somewhere back on the viaduct, in the dark, with Ilse's blood on my hands, the circuit had finally broken.

The judge smiled, faint and tired. "No. I don't believe you are. Which is why he chose right after all." She pushed the letter across the blanket toward me. "Open it."

* * *

The wax cracked like thin ice.

My father's handwriting filled one page, cramped and slanting, the hand of a man writing fast by bad light:

*If you are reading this, then you found the chest, which means you found my locks, which means you listened instead of forcing. Good. That's all I ever taught you, and it turns out it was enough.*

*I am sorry for the weight. I carried it thirty years and could not put it down or lift it high. The truth is I never knew what should be done, only what must not—and cowardice dressed as caution is still cowardice. Judge Vess will tell you the stakes better than I can; she has carried them longer and straighter than I ever did.*

*One thing only I ask. Whatever you decide, decide it yourself. Not for me, not for the dead, not for the ones who want the pages for their own fires. Decide it the way you open a lock: listen first. Then act.*

*And when it's finished—stop moving, Ulyana. Find a town. Stay. The map was never meant to be a life. It was meant to be a search.*

*Your father,
E. Thornbury*

I read it twice. The second time aloud, because Tomas asked, in a small voice, whether he could hear it. Ilse sat close enough that her knee pressed against mine, and neither of us pretended it was an accident.

When I finished, the judge was watching me with the patience of a woman who had waited forty years and could wait out another minute.

"Well?" she said. "What does the Thornbury line decide?"

I looked at the folios spread across her blanket—the beautiful, murderous, irreplaceable work of dead engineers. I thought of Marek, somewhere above us on the mountain, carrying his copies toward the summit and a journalist and history. I thought of Vale's troops, twelve hours behind us and closing, and of the dry wind rising, and of a town near the Sorrel that I had never let myself look at directly until this week.

"I don't know yet," I said honestly. "But I know who has to hear the answer besides me. And I know we have maybe two days before everyone who wants a vote arrives at the same place at the same time."

The judge nodded slowly, unsurprised, as if verdicts had always arrived at her door in weather like this.

"Then take the tea with you," she said, "and get up the mountain. And Ulyana—" her hand caught mine, brief and strong as a rail spike driven home, "whatever you choose up there, choose it as yourself. The dead have had their turn."

We went out into the wind. Above the tree line, the Karst peaks stood sharp against a hard blue sky, and I thought I could see, just barely, the notch in the ridge where a nation's last secret waited with Marek and his copies and whatever was coming up the valley behind us.

Ilse checked her watch. "Soldiers will reach the sanatorium by tomorrow noon," she said. "Marek's summit camp by tonight, if he pushes."

"Then we climb tonight," I said, and started up.
