## 18. Marek at the Pass

The wind at the top of the world had a sound Marek had been chasing his whole life without knowing it.

It came up the Karst face in long, low exhalations, moving through the abandoned switchback tunnels like breath through a flute, and where it found the pass itself—the notch of sky between two gray shoulders of stone—it sang. Not a melody. A chord. One note held by a hundred mouths of rock, all of them carved by Vessmarki engineers who had known, forty years ago, that even a mountain could be made to speak if you cut it correctly.

Marek stood in that chord and let it move through him.

Below and behind him, the old road climbed out of the fog in a series of hairpins so tight they looked from here like the fingerprints of a giant. The rail bed was gone—ripped up in the annexation, every tie burned in garrison stoves—but the grade remained, and the drainage culverts, and the parapets of dry-laid stone that had not shifted a centimeter in four decades. His grandfather's work. His great-uncle's. Men whose names were on no monument, whose nation was on no map, whose graves—if they had graves—were dug by other people's governments.

He had carried them all the way up. He could feel them now, standing at his back like a congregation.

"You're crying," said the journalist.

"I'm not."

"You are." Sef Aldous lowered his camera—not photographing, for once, just looking at Marek with those quick, hungry eyes that had made him famous in three capitals and unemployable in two. "Good. Crying photographs badly. You'd ruin my composition."

"Then don't compose me."

"I never compose anyone, Marek. I just stand where the truth already is and try not to get in its way." He resettled the camera against his chest and turned to face the pass. "How long?"

"Dawn. Maybe an hour before. Ulyana travels fast when they're angry, and they'll be furious by now."

"And the soldiers?"

"Behind everyone. Vale moves carefully. That's his whole tragedy—he moves carefully." Marek checked his watch, an heirloom with a cracked crystal he'd wound every morning since he was nine years old. "We have time to do this properly."

Sef snorted. "Properly. You mean illegally. You mean we're about to publish classified survey documents from a dissolved state on the summit of a mountain inside another country's borders, while its army climbs toward us. Properly."

"Yes," Marek said. "Properly."

* * *

They had come up the east approach in the dark, the way his grandfather's field notes described it: through Tunnel Seven, which was really half a tunnel now, its crown collapsed into a ramp of shattered limestone you could still climb if you respected it; then along the gallery ledge above the cloud line, where the parapet came only to the knee and the drop went down into white nothing; then the last stair, cut directly into the mountain's spine, sixty-two steps that his great-aunt had numbered in her own hand, chiseled small, so that climbing them you counted your way into Vessmark.

Sixty-two. Sixty-one. Sixty.

At the top, the pass opened like a held breath finally let go.

On one side, the valleys of the Republic, terraced and smoke-staked, the country that had swallowed his homeland whole and called it peace. On the other side, nothing but more Karst, range after range going blue and then violet and then a color with no name, the high country where Vessmark had kept its summer pastures and its observatories and, according to the documents folded against Marek's chest, a pass that would have put a rail line over the mountains twenty years before anyone thought it possible.

That was what he carried. Forty pages of it, copied in Marek's careful hand during three nights in the valley while Ilse slept and Tomas dreamed and Ulyana stood watch and trusted him. Forty pages proving that the mountains had a door in them, and that the door had been built by people whose country no longer existed, and that four treaties signed in good faith by men who never knew the door existed were built on sand.

Publish it, and the treaties cracked. Publish it, and the borders moved—or at least the argument about the borders began again, and arguments were how dead nations stayed alive. Publish it, and somewhere in some ministry archive, a clerk would pull a folder marked VESSMARK and find it wasn't dust after all. It was a fuse.

"They'll call you a traitor," Sef said. He had set up his tripod on the western lip, framing the stair, the ledge, the notch of sky. "Both countries will. They'll argue about whose traitor you are, which is at least something."

"I know what they'll call me."

"Do you know what I'll call you?"

Marek looked at him. Sef Aldous, fifty-three years old, a man who had photographed famines and coups and one royal wedding that ended in gunfire, and who had told Marek on the second night of their climb, in the dark of a shepherd's hut, why he'd agreed to come: because he had been born in Vessmark too, in a town whose name was now a suburb, and he had spent thirty years photographing other people's disappearances and never once his own.

"What will you call me?" Marek asked.

"A witness," Sef said. "The last profession left to us."

* * *

Dawn came up the eastern valleys first, filling them gold from the bottom like cups being poured, and Marek laid the documents out on the flat stone at the pass's center—the surveyor's table, his grandfather's notes called it, a slab of granite worn smooth by a century of instruments—and weighted the corners with stones, and read.

He read aloud. All of it. The elevations, the grades, the tunnel specifications, the load calculations for a bridge across the Sorrel gorge that would have been the longest span in the world. He read in Vessmarki, though Sef was the only one who understood, and then he read it again in the Republic's language, because the point was not comprehension. The point was that the words should touch this air. That the mountain should hear its own name spoken by its own engineers' descendants.

When he finished, the wind took the last syllables and scattered them east and west, into both countries at once, and Marek felt lighter than he had since he was a child—since the day his grandmother had stopped speaking Vessmarki in the house, mid-sentence, forever, because soldiers had knocked and she never picked the language up again.

"Now," he said. "Photograph everything. Then we wait."

"For Ulyana?"

"For Ulyana." He sat down on the surveyor's table, next to his grandfather's resurrection, and pulled his coat tight. "They'll come up the west stair. They always take the harder line—it's how they think. And they'll arrive ahead of the soldiers, because Ulyana Thornbury has never in their life arrived anywhere second."

Sef worked in silence for a while, the shutter clicking, the light going from gold to white. Then, without turning: "And if they ask you to stop? If they beg?"

Marek watched the clouds pour through the pass below them, a slow white river running uphill and downhill at once.

"Then I'll listen," he said. "I've learned that much from them. But listening isn't obeying, Sef. My whole life, people listened to us and then did nothing. Listening is what you do instead of acting. I'm done being listened to."

* * *

Ulyana came up out of the fog at mid-morning, exactly as predicted, alone, moving fast, and Marek's heart did something complicated and painful at the sight of them—relief and dread braided together, the exact feeling of the chest coming open.

They stopped ten paces away. Wind pulled at their coat. Their face was wind-scoured, exhausted, and absolutely awake, and Marek saw them take in the scene in one sweep: the documents on the stone, the camera, himself.

"You copied them," Ulyana said. No greeting. No anger yet. Just the fact, laid down between them like a rail.

"I did."

"Tomas told you to?"

"No one told me anything. That's rather the point of me." Marek stood. "You hid them. In a cache. With a cipher. You meant to decide their fate yourself, the way your father decided it himself, the way every custodian in this chain has decided it alone. I simply declined to be decided for."

"You could have killed us all. Those nights you were copying, we were in garrison territory. If you'd been caught with those pages—"

"I wasn't caught."

"This time."

"Yes," Marek said. "This time. That's the only time any of us ever gets, Ulyana. This time. Your father got his this time and he chose silence. Judge Vess got hers and she chose to hand the weight to a stranger. I'm choosing mine, and I choose the light."

"The light," Ulyana repeated, and there was something terrible in how gently they said it. "Do you know what happens when it breaks? Four treaties. Four. Do you know how many divisions sit along those treaty lines right now, Marek? How many garrisons are one document away from a reason? You're not publishing a pass. You're publishing a war with a forty-year fuse already lit."

"I'm publishing a people!" It came out of him louder than he intended, torn loose, and the wind grabbed it and threw it off the mountain into both countries. "Forty years, Ulyana. Forty years of my grandmother's mouth closing mid-sentence. Of towns renamed, of gravestones turned around, of children—me—who learned our own history as contraband. You want to know what I'm publishing? I'm publishing the fact that we existed. That we were brilliant. That we built things so well the mountains still sing through them. And if the price of that is making comfortable men in comfortable ministries nervous—" his voice cracked, and he hated it, and he kept going— "then let them be nervous. Nervous is honest. Silence is the lie they've been living on."

The chord of the pass filled the space where his shout had been. Ulyana didn't answer at once. They walked forward, slowly, to the surveyor's table, and looked down at the forty pages, at the elevations and grades and the beautiful, impossible bridge over the Sorrel gorge, and Marek watched their face and saw—not fury. Grief. The same grief he carried, wearing different clothes.

"My father wrote me a letter," Ulyana said quietly. "It's the only thing I've kept. He wrote that a map is a promise you make to strangers, and that the highest duty of an engineer is knowing which promises not to make." They touched the edge of the top page, lightly, the way you touch a sleeping animal. "He saw this same data, Marek. He understood it better than either of us. And he spent his whole life burying it, moving, hiding, erasing himself from every record—because he loved these lines too much to let them be used as weapons. That's not cowardice. That's the hardest engineering there is."

"It's also surrender dressed as virtue."

"Maybe." Ulyana looked up, and their eyes were wet, and they didn't hide it. "But here's what I've learned since the river gave up that chest: I've spent my whole life running from grief, and you've spent yours running toward it, and we're both exhausted, and neither of us is wrong. That's the trap of it. That's why Vale can't solve this either. There's no correct answer on this mountain. There's only what we can live with."

"Then live with this," Marek said, and stepped aside from the table. "Read them. All forty pages. Not as a custodian—as an engineer. Read what we were. And then tell me, to my face, that the world is safer never knowing."

Ulyana read.

Marek watched them read, page by page, the wind turning the corners, and saw the exact moment—page nineteen, the gorge bridge—when professional awe broke through everything else. He knew that moment. He'd had it in the flood mud, holding the chest, recognizing his own inheritance. Enemies, he thought. We were supposed to be enemies, and instead we're the same person standing on opposite sides of one grave.

* * *

It was Sef who saw the soldiers first.

"Smoke," he said, from the western lip, glasses raised. "Down the switchbacks. Torch smoke—they're burning the brush off the grade as they climb. Military habit. They'll reach the base of the stair within the hour."

Marek felt the hour land on his shoulders like a pack. "Then we finish this now. Sef—how long to transmit?"

"Once I'm down to a telegraph office? Hours. Once I'm down to any town with a wire? Tonight. The story writes itself, Marek. Dead nation's lost masterpiece found on its own mountain. Every editor in three countries runs it by morning."

"Then run it."

"Wait." Ulyana straightened from the documents. "Wait. Before anyone runs anywhere. Marek—one question. Answer it honestly, because I'll know if you don't." They crossed to him, close enough that he could see the salt dried on their collar. "If publishing starts the war you swear it won't—if the divisions move, if the garrisons mobilize, if the first shells fall on some border town full of people who never asked for any of this—will you still believe you were right? Five years from now? Ten? Standing in whatever's left?"

Marek opened his mouth.

Nothing came out.

Because the honest answer rose in him like water into a lock, unstoppable, and it was: I don't know. I don't know, and I have never known, and every certainty I've climbed this mountain on has been scaffolding around that one hollow room. He had told himself it was duty. It was grief. He had told himself it was justice. It was his grandmother's unfinished sentence, repeating, repeating, and he had mistaken the echo for a voice.

"I don't know," he said. And the saying of it broke something open in his chest, and he sat down heavily on the surveyor's table, next to his grandfather's life's work, and put his face in his hands. "God help me, Ulyana. I don't know. I just—I couldn't carry the silence anymore. Forty years of it. I thought if I could just make the world say the word Vessmark out loud, one more time, at volume—"

The wind moved through the pass. Somewhere below, faintly, the sound of many boots on stone.

Ulyana sat down beside him. Shoulder to shoulder, the way crews sit at the end of a shift. For a long moment neither of them spoke, and the documents lay between them, and the smoke rose on the switchbacks, and the mountain sang its one held note.

"I know," Ulyana said at last. "Believe me. I know exactly what you couldn't carry. I've been carrying the same thing since I was nine years old, in a different container." They were quiet a moment longer. Then: "There might be a third option. It's not clean. It's not what either of us wants. But it might be the only thing on this mountain that doesn't end in fire or forgetting."

Marek lifted his head. "What third option?"

Ulyana looked west, toward the smoke, toward the soldiers climbing, and their expression was one Marek had seen once before—in a photograph of his grandfather, standing at this same pass, the year before the world ended.

"Burn them," Ulyana said. "Here. Now. Both of us. Before the soldiers arrive, before Sef transmits a word—burn the surveys, keep the letter, and let the mountain keep its secret. But we do it together, in front of witnesses, and we say the names. All of them. Every engineer, every town, every sentence my grandmother never finished. We give Vessmark a funeral instead of a weapon. The world hears us—we make sure of it, Sef, you make sure of it—but what it hears is mourning, not marching."

Silence, except the chord.

"And that's enough?" Marek whispered. "A funeral?"

"It's the only ceremony anyone's given them in forty years."

Marek looked at the pages. At the bridge that would have been the longest in the world. At the numbers his grandfather had bled into existence, and his own careful copyist's hand beside them, and the smoke rising through the golden air, closer now, and the boots on the stone, and Ulyana's face waiting beside his, open, terrified, certain.

Somewhere below, a whistle blew—a soldier's sharp triple note, ordering the column to halt.

They had minutes.

"Marek," Ulyana said softly. "The matches are in your pocket. They've been there since the valley. I've known since the viaduct."

Marek reached in, slowly, and closed his hand around the little cardboard box, and found that his fingers were shaking, and that beneath the shaking, underneath everything, in the place where the grief lived and the love lived and they were somehow the same place—

—he wanted, desperately, terribly, to say yes.
