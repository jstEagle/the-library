## 21. The Last Line Open

The spur line had been dead for eleven years, and on the morning they rode it back toward the lowlands, it smelled like a birthday.

That was Ilse's word for it—birthday—and she didn't apologize for it, even when Ulyana raised an eyebrow. Creosote and cut grass and hot iron and the green smell of weeds crushed under wheels that hadn't turned since before the annexation. A crew of six men from the river towns had spent nine days replacing ties and spiking rail, working off a government contract that Corin Vale himself had signed, and when the little inspection engine finally rolled out of the Karst foothills with three passengers and one battered trunk, the foreman stood at the edge of the ballast and took off his hat like he was watching something be born.

Ilse rode in the open air car with her elbows on the rail and her face into the wind, because she had earned the right to be sentimental about machinery and she intended to spend that right freely.

Below the track, the Sorrel ran wide and slow through its flood-scoured valley, already forgetting what it had done. Willows stitched themselves back along the banks. Somewhere upstream, men were rebuilding the survey camp that had started all of this, and Ilse found she could look at the river now without her chest tightening. That was new. That was maybe two weeks old.

"You're grinning," Ulyana said beside her. They leaned against the tender, map-case slung across their body out of habit, though everyone knew what was left inside it: one letter, folded twice, and nothing else worth stealing or dying for.

"I'm inspecting," Ilse said. "Track gauge looks true. Ballast's fresh. Somebody spiked the joints on the curve back there by the book." She paused. "I checked."

"Of course you did."

"Somebody has to admire good work in this country. It doesn't admire itself."

Ulyana laughed—a real laugh, low and unguarded, the kind Ilse had heard maybe five times since the viaduct, and every one of them felt like a structure she'd built herself and would defend with tools if necessary.

Tomas sat up front with the driver, learning the air brakes. He'd been up there since dawn, asking questions in his quick, hungry way—the compression ratios, the sand dome, why the driver feathered the throttle on the downgrade—and the driver, a patient man named Oskar who had once worked the Vessmarki expresses and never thought he'd feel rails move under him again, answered all of them. Ilse watched the boy's hands as he talked. They'd stopped shaking somewhere around the switchbacks. They'd come down off that mountain with soot in their lungs and grief in their pockets, and the boy had walked every step like someone paying off a debt, and now here he was leaning over a live brake valve like the future was a thing you could grip.

Marek wasn't there. Marek was in Merano, or Trieste, or some other city whose name changed depending on which empire's stamps were on your papers, and the first anyone heard of him was—

But that came later. First there was the bridge.

* * *

They stopped for water at Kessel Halt, a town of forty houses and one grain elevator, where the spur crossed a creek on a timber trestle that made Ilse wince openly.

"That," she said, "is a confession of sin."

The trestle sagged in the middle like a tired animal. Two bents had rotted to the waterline; the stringers had been patched with lumber that had no business holding a locomotive. The engineer brought the train across at walking pace, and everyone breathed again on the far side, and Ilse stood in the caboose door looking back at it with an expression Ulyana clearly recognized, because Ulyana said, "No."

"I haven't said anything."

"You're drafting repairs in your head. I can hear it."

"It's a public hazard."

"It's a public hazard forty miles from anywhere with a budget."

Ilse turned around. She'd been waiting for this conversation for days, actually, turning it over on the switchbacks and again in the sanatorium's garden while Judge Amalia Vess slept in her chair in the sun, and she found that now, riding a living railroad through a country that had forgotten them both, the words came easily.

"So we build bridges," she said.

Ulyana looked at her.

"A firm. Small. You and me. Survey work, line assessments, bridge repair—there are dozens of trestles like that one between here and the lowlands, and half the lines your map knows are closed not because they're broken but because nobody remembers how to fix them. Nobody kept the knowledge. It went into exile with everything else." She gestured at the valley sliding past. "We have the knowledge. We have—" she hesitated, then said it plainly, because she was done being careful with this person, "—we have each other, and apparently that survives rockfalls and ambushes and burning state secrets, so a timber trestle shouldn't frighten us."

Ulyana was quiet for a long moment. The wind pulled at their collar. Ilse watched them do the thing they always did—check the horizon, check the exits, calculate the distance to the next town—and then watched them stop doing it. Deliberately. Like setting down something heavy.

"Ravensworth & Thornbury," Ulyana said at last. "Bridge-builders."

"Thornbury & Brandt."

"You want top billing?"

"I want alphabetical order. It's the only fair system left in this world."

And Ulyana smiled—not the laugh, but the smaller thing underneath it, the one Ilse liked best—and said, "Brandt & Thornbury, then. Bridge-builders and line surveyors. We'll need a shop."

"We'll need a shop, a ledger, and a rule that neither of us sleeps in the same town twice unless we choose to."

That landed differently than Ilse intended. She saw it land. Ulyana looked away, out at the water, and after a moment said, quietly, "I've been thinking about that rule."

"I know."

"How do you know?"

"Because you stopped checking the timetable at every station. You used to read them compulsively, like scripture. You haven't touched one since the pass."

Ulyana said nothing, but their hand found hers on the rail, and stayed there, and the trestle at Kessel Halt receded behind them like a problem they would absolutely come back and solve.

* * *

Tomas signed his apprenticeship papers on the tailgate of a freight wagon in the yard at Lowmark Junction, using Ilse's knee as a writing surface because there was no table.

She'd drawn up the terms herself, on the back of a survey sheet: room and board, wages beginning modestly and rising with competence, instruction in structural drawing, materials science, and "the honest mathematics of loads," four years' term, breakable by mutual consent. At the bottom, where a guardian's signature would normally go, Ilse wrote *self-emancipated, witnessed,* and signed beneath it, and Ulyana signed as second witness, and Tomas read the whole document twice with the fierce concentration he gave to Vessmarki texts and then signed his name in careful block letters, pressing hard enough to tear the paper slightly.

"There," Ilse said. "You're indentured to a bankrupt profession run by two people with criminal-adjacent reputations. Congratulations."

"What's my first assignment?"

"Learn the names of every tool in the chest, in order, and what each one is for. If you hand me the wrong chisel tomorrow, I'll know you skipped ahead."

He grinned. Then, more seriously: "Is it true you were struck off the register? In Durenberg?"

It was true. She'd been struck off eleven years ago for signing off a span she hadn't personally inspected—a shortcut taken in grief, after her brother's funeral, and the span had held, but the principle hadn't, and the board had been right to do what they did. She'd told him the story once, on the mountain, in the dark, when honesty was the only currency they had.

"It's true," she said. "Which means you'll learn the version of engineering where you never sign what you haven't seen. Consider it tuition."

"And Ulyana? What do I call them? Boss?"

"You'll call them Ulyana, and you'll learn locks and ciphers and how to read a landscape like a sentence, because that's what they know, and a bridge-builder who can't read the ground under her piers is just a carpenter with ambitions." Ilse glanced over to where Ulyana stood talking with the stationmaster, pointing at something on the reopened spur's timetable—actually reading it, actually standing still. "And you'll learn that people are load-bearing structures too. Treat them accordingly."

Tomas considered this. "Did you make that up just now?"

"Yes. But it's true anyway. That's how the best rules work."

* * *

The postcard came a week later, forwarded through three addresses before it caught up with them at the rented workshop in Lowmark—an old grain warehouse with a north-facing wall big enough for drafting tables and a loading door tall enough for timber.

The picture side showed a harbor: cranes, ferries, a skyline that belonged to none of the four treaty nations. On the back, in Marek's upright, deliberate hand:

*To Brandt & Thornbury, Bridge-Builders — I confess I laughed when I heard the name. You burned a nation and founded a company in the same month. That is either madness or the healthiest grief I have ever witnessed.*

*I am in Merano, teaching mathematics to children who have never heard of Vessmark, which is the kindest fate I can imagine for the name. I keep the copies still. Not to use—to remember. There is a difference, and I learned it on a mountain, from people who were willing to die for a piece of paper and then burn it in front of me. I hated you for that. Then I understood it. I am not sure which happened first.*

*Tell Tomas the cipher book he left in my saddlebag has been returned to the library at Merano, catalogued under local history, where any child may read it. That seemed right.*

*Tell Ilse her knots saved my life twice and I never thanked her properly. Consider this the thanks.*

*Tell Ulyana: the letter they kept is worth more than everything they burned. I know they don't believe that yet. They will.*

*There is a bridge over the Adige here, iron, built by engineers whose names were struck from the plaques after the annexation. The city has voted to restore the original names. I have been asked to write the historical text for the plaque. I am going to write: THEY BUILT IT WELL. Nothing else. It seemed sufficient.*

*Yours in exile, which is less lonely than advertised,*
*Marek*

Ilse read it aloud at the workbench while Tomas reorganized the tool chest for the third time and Ulyana sharpened pencils with a knife, and when she finished, nobody spoke for a moment.

"They built it well," Tomas repeated. "That's a good plaque."

"It's the only plaque," Ilse said. "Everything else is argument."

Ulyana put down the knife. They crossed the workshop—they still moved like someone counting exits, but slower now, like someone who had begun to trust the doors—and took the postcard, and read it again silently, and Ilse watched their thumb press flat against the words *they will believe it yet* as if testing whether the ink would hold.

"We should frame it," Tomas said.

"We should answer it," Ulyana said.

"With what?"

Ulyana looked around the workshop—the drafting tables Ilse had built from scaffold planks, the tool chest, the north light, the boy, the woman—and then at the map of closed lines, which hung rolled above the door, waiting for a wall.

"With a photograph," Ulyana said. "Of the first bridge we finish. And underneath it we'll write: *IT HELD.*"

* * *

They broke ground on the Kessel Creek trestle in the last week of the dry season, when the water was low and honest about its bed.

Ilse drove the first survey stake herself, and let Tomas drive the second, correcting his angle by a degree and saying nothing else, because that was the whole apprenticeship in miniature. Ulyana walked the banks with the old hand-inked map spread on a board, marking not closed lines now but crossings—every place the water and the iron might be persuaded to meet again.

By evening the stakes stood in a row, and the three of them sat on the bank eating bread and hard cheese while the creek went on talking to itself below.

"One more thing," Ilse said. She'd been carrying it since the sanatorium. "Judge Vess sent something. For you. I was supposed to wait until we were settled."

Ulyana took the small parcel, unwrapped the oilcloth, and went very still.

Inside was a single page of Vessmarki survey paper—the same stock Vale had kept a blank page of, though neither of them knew that yet—ruled and headed in the old style, and across the top, in the judge's trembling but unmistakable hand, a commission:

*To the firm of Brandt & Thornbury: authority to survey, assess, and reopen such lines as serve the people who live along them. Signed under the last lawful seal of Vessmark, which seal hereby passes to the keeping of the map.*

"She's deputizing us," Tomas said. "With a dead country."

"She's doing the opposite," Ulyana said slowly. "Look at the wording. *Lines that serve the people who live along them.* Not the nation. Not the treaties. The people." They looked up, and Ilse saw something settle in their face that she had been watching for since the burning—some final stone finding its place in an arch. "She's telling us the map was never a border. It was always a list of places somebody loved enough to draw."

The creek ran. The stakes stood. Somewhere south, a train they couldn't hear was carrying mail that included, among other things, a ministry report declaring an iron chest a hoax, filed by an archivist who had chosen grace over duty and told no one.

Ilse leaned her shoulder against Ulyana's shoulder, and took out her pencil, and on the margin of the judge's commission drew a small, precise line—from the word *Kessel* to the word *home*—and labeled it, in her neat draftsman's hand:

*Open.*
