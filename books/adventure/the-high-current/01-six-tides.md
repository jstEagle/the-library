## 1. Six Tides

The fog came off the interior in long grey tongues that morning, and Quentin Coldharbour flew through it the way they did everything—alone, precisely, and without looking back.

At six hundred feet the Cessna was a small dark thought moving through a larger grey one. Below, the land unspooled in folds of spruce and muskeg, black water threading through it like veins through a leaf. There were no roads worth the name out here. The coast ended and the country began, and everything past that line belonged to the mail plane, the floatplane, the occasional desperate helicopter. It belonged to Quentin.

"Medicine run," Quentin said to no one, because saying it aloud made the morning feel official. "Six stops. In and out."

The crates were lashed behind the second seat: insulin in cold packs, antibiotics, a case of saline that rattled with every bump like polite applause. A supply list folded into the door pocket, written by Sister Agnes at the mission clinic in her cramped, patient hand. Quentin had never met Sister Agnes face to face more than twice, and both times she had tried to feed them, and both times Quentin had declined on the pretense of a schedule. There was always a schedule. Schedules were how you stayed free of things.

Seven years now. Seven years of flying other people's necessities into places where nobody asked your name unless they needed something carried out. It was a good arrangement. It required nothing but fuel and weather sense, and Quentin had plenty of both. The miles accumulated in the logbook like sand filling an hourglass, and if you never flipped the glass over, you could pretend time wasn't passing at all.

* * *

The first stop was Kettle Harbour, which wasn't a harbour so much as a wide spot in a river where three families had decided, against all sensible advice, to stay. Quentin set down on the brown water, taxied to the dock where a boy of maybe ten waited with the solemnity of a customs officer.

"You're late," the boy said.

"I'm early," Quentin said, cutting the engine. "Your watch is fast."

The boy considered this. "Mama says you fly like you're being chased."

"Mama should mind her nets."

But the boy grinned, and Quentin found, to their mild irritation, grinning back before they could stop it. They unloaded the crate together, the boy insisting on carrying the lighter end as though this were a great partnership of equals, and when Quentin climbed back into the plane the boy stood on the dock and waved until the turn put him out of sight.

Quentin did not look back. That was a rule, one of several, though the rules were less commandments than habits worn smooth from use: don't linger, don't owe, don't learn anyone's birthday. The coast rewarded people who stayed rooted; Quentin had spent seven years making sure their roots stayed packed in a go-bag under the seat, along with two thousand dollars in small bills and a passport under a name that wasn't quite right anymore.

The next four stops blurred the way routine blurs—fuel drums at a fish camp, a dentist's chair crated for a village that had been promised one for eleven years, seed potatoes, a dog with a torn ear riding shotgun to a vet who owed Quentin a favour. Each landing and takeoff was its own small argument with gravity, won and re-won, and each departure left a little wake spreading behind the floats like a sentence being erased.

By mid-afternoon the sky over the interior had gone the colour of pewter, low and even, no features to it, the kind of ceiling that made distance impossible to judge. Quentin flew by feel and dead reckoning, counting rivers, counting ridgelines, the compass card steady in its bowl of light. This was the part of flying nobody romanticized—the hours where nothing happens except not crashing—and it was the part Quentin loved best. Nothing happening meant nobody needing anything. Nobody needing anything meant nobody getting close enough to be lost.

That last thought arrived uninvited, the way such thoughts do, and Quentin evicted it the way they always did: by checking the fuel gauges, then checking them again, as though arithmetic were a form of peace.

* * *

The coast came up out of the murk around five o'clock, the way it always did—suddenly, all at once, the flat grey sea asserting itself against the flatter grey sky until there was no horizon, only a single vast element with the coastline stitched through it like a scar. Quentin turned south for home, following the shore because instruments lied near iron-bearing rock and the old-timers trusted their eyes over anything with needles in it.

The tide was going out. You could see it in the kelp lying flat and shining, in the mudflats emerging along the bays like the sea slowly exhaling, showing what it kept. Low tide on this coast revealed things: wrecks of skiffs, bones of whales, the occasional rusted boiler from some steamer that had misjudged the fog a century ago. The locals called it the ocean's laundry day. Quentin called it none of anything, because thinking about tides meant thinking about cycles, and cycles meant time, and time was the thing they were outrunning.

They were abeam of Cutter's Reach—a stretch of cliffs the charts showed as featureless granite—when the light changed.

It wasn't dramatic. The sun, low and somewhere behind the cloud deck, threw a band of pale gold across the water, and the cliff face lit up for perhaps thirty seconds, and Quentin saw it sideways, out of the corner of their eye, the way you see a stranger watching you in a café mirror.

A cave mouth. Black, tall, sitting maybe eight feet above the current waterline, exposed by the ebb like a doorway in a house everyone said was empty.

Quentin's hands did the pilot things automatically—wings level, altitude held, eyes flicking to fuel, to clock, to horizon—while the rest of them went very still.

There was no cave on the chart. Quentin knew these charts. Had memorized them, in fact, during the first year, back when knowing every rock and shoal felt like a way of belonging to somewhere without having to belong to anyone. Cutter's Reach showed as sheer wall, twelve fathoms at the base, no indentation, no cleft, nothing. Yet here was an opening easily wide enough to fly a floatplane through, if a person were mad, or desperate, or both.

Quentin banked. Not sharply—that would be curiosity, and curiosity was a debt you paid later—but a lazy, just-checking-the-coastline drift that fooled nobody, least of all themselves.

The cave mouth sat in shadow, deeper than it looked, and the water inside it was calm in a way open water never was. Sheltered. Hidden. At high tide the entrance would be drowned entirely, or nearly—which meant the chart surveyors had come through at the wrong hour, or the right one, depending on whose secret it had been.

Whose secret it had been. The phrase snagged in Quentin's chest like a hook in a sweater.

Because there was a shape inside. Deep in the dark of the entrance, half-submerged in the still green water, something angular caught the last of the gold light and threw it back—metal, corroded, the unmistakable compound curve of an aircraft tail assembly, listing hard to port, its skin furred with barnacles and weed.

Quentin pulled out of the turn too fast and knew it, the old girl bucking once in protest. Their heart was doing something complicated against their ribs, and they told it to stop, and it didn't.

They knew that tail. Not that tail—no one could know a specific tail after thirty years underwater—but that *type*. High-wing transport, twin radial engines, the distinctive upswept aft fuselage of a machine built to carry mail and hope into places roads couldn't reach. A Meridian-class airframe. The kind of plane that had opened up this whole coast, back in the days when the mail mattered more than the men flying it.

The kind of plane their father had flown.

"No," Quentin said aloud, to the empty cockpit, and the word came out smaller than intended.

The fog was thickening again, folding the cliff back into anonymity, and Quentin flew south with their jaw set and their knuckles white and the image printed on the inside of their eyelids: a black doorway in a grey wall, and something waiting inside it that had been waiting a very long time.

* * *

Home was a float shed on the edge of Harrow Bay, population—depending on who was counting and how generously—somewhere north of two hundred souls who had collectively decided Quentin was either shy or rude and hadn't yet settled on which. Quentin fueled the plane, wiped her down, checked the tie-downs twice, and walked the boardwalk to the co-op store without speaking to anyone, which took effort, because Harrow Bay specialized in effortful silence-breaking.

"Evening, Coldharbour!" called Marta Voss from the porch of the chandlery—not *that* Voss, Quentin reminded themselves, different family entirely, or so everyone insisted—"You look like you seen a ghost."

"Just tired," Quentin said, and kept walking, and heard Marta make a small sound of unconvinced sympathy.

In the room above the co-op that served as quarters—cot, stove, chart table, one window facing the water—Quentin spread the coastal chart across the table and weighted its corners with coffee mugs. Cutter's Reach. There it was, in faded violet ink: sheer granite, twelve fathoms, no feature whatsoever. The chart was twenty-two years old, surveyed the year Quentin turned nine, the year before—

Before.

Quentin pressed their palm flat against the paper, over the place where the cave ought to be, the way you'd press a hand over a wound.

The tide tables hung beside the chart, and Quentin consulted them now with the mechanical calm of someone performing first aid on themselves. Tomorrow's low tide: 4:47 in the morning. The one after: late afternoon. After that, the sequence stretched out ahead like a countdown—each ebb a little lower than the last as they slid toward the spring tides at week's end, when the moon would drag the sea all the way back and hold it there, exposing the coast down to its oldest secrets.

Six tides. The number surfaced in their mind unbidden, the way numbers did, and refused to leave.

Outside, the foghorn on the point moaned its slow two-note complaint, and somewhere below, the town went about its evening—lamps coming on, dogs arguing, somebody laughing in the way people laugh when they have people to laugh with. Quentin listened to all of it from behind the window glass, separated from it by a pane and seven years and a surname nobody here connected to anything, and told themselves the tightness in their chest was just fatigue.

Then they got out the tide table again and drew a small pencil circle around tomorrow's 4:47, and another around the one after, and another, six circles marching down the page toward the springs, and looked at what they'd done as though someone else had done it.

Below Cutter's Reach, in a cave no chart admitted existed, a Meridian-class tail assembly stood in forty feet of green water, and Quentin Coldharbour—who had spent seven years perfecting the art of not coming back—understood, with the particular dread of the sleepless, that tomorrow they would go back.

And the tide, patient as everything else on this coast, would be waiting to show them what else the water had kept.
