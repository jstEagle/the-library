## 3. The Lighthouse Keeper's Ledger

Quentin told themselves it was a supply run.

That was the story, anyway, the one they rehearsed on the forty-minute flight down the coast from the float base at Kestrel Narrows: fuel, coffee, a new aneroid for the altimeter, and if it happened that the general store sat three doors from the old lighthouse road, well, geography was nobody's fault. The Cessna 180—*Sparrowhawk*, in peeling letters on the cowl—rode a low ceiling of cloud south along the shore, and Quentin flew it the way they did everything, alone and precisely, hands light on the yoke, eyes moving in the old rhythm: horizon, instruments, horizon, water. Below, the sea lay like hammered pewter, and the tide was out so far that the beaches showed their bones—black rock shelves, ribbed sand, the dark veins of channels that would be drowned again by evening.

Seven years of flying this coast and Quentin had never once landed at Harrow Point. They fueled at Kestrel, bought groceries by mail order, kept their transactions with the world short and paid in cash. It wasn't shyness. Shyness implied you wanted something you were afraid to reach for. This was more like ballast management: every attachment jettisoned before it could shift in rough air.

But the cave had changed the arithmetic. A wreck that no chart admitted existed, sitting in a cave no survey recorded—that was not a thing a careful person could sit on alone. Careful people needed information, and information on this coast lived in exactly one kind of head: the old ones. The ones who'd been here before the charts were printed, and who remembered what had been erased from them.

And there was only one lighthouse left standing between Kestrel Narrows and Cape Verity, and only one keeper retired out of it who hadn't moved away.

Quentin set *Sparrowhawk* down on the long swell off Harrow Point's sheltered hook, taxied to the public dock with the economy of someone trying to take up as little of the world as possible, and tied off with knots they'd learned from a man whose name they still didn't say out loud, even alone, even in their head. Then they stood on the dock in the wind and looked up the hill at the lighthouse, white tower gone grey at the seams, gallery rail freckled with rust, and felt the peculiar pull of a place they'd spent seven years flying past.

The town was small enough to cross in four minutes and arranged, like all these towns, around what the water allowed: a dock, a cannery long converted to a boatyard, a general store with a woodstove going, a church that had become a library that had become mostly a place to post notices. Quentin bought the aneroid, the coffee, a bag of oranges they didn't need. The storekeeper, a broad woman named Mrs. Adeyemi whose name tag had faded to *MRS.*, rang everything up without asking a single question, which Quentin appreciated until she said, conversationally, "You'll be wanting the lighthouse road, then."

Quentin went still over their change. "Why would I?"

"Only person ever walks up that road anymore is somebody wanting Ede Okonkwo." Mrs. Adeyemi counted the change into Quentin's palm without looking at it. "And you've been looking at that hill since you tied off. You're not the first pilot to do it. But you're the first one who's actually walked." She smiled, unbothered, and turned to the next customer, and Quentin understood that on this coast, being seen was not something you could opt out of. It was weather. You just dressed for it.

The lighthouse road climbed through salal and wind-bent spruce, gravel gone soft under moss, and the wind came off the water carrying salt and kelp-rot and the faint diesel sweetness of the boatyard below. Quentin walked slowly, telling themselves the slowness was caution. It was not caution. It was the feeling of approaching a door you suspected had been waiting for you, and waiting changes the weight of a door.

Ede Okonkwo was in the garden beside the keeper's cottage when Quentin came up the last rise—a narrow woman in her seventies with white hair cropped close and hands black to the wrist with soil, kneeling among potato hills with the unhurried authority of someone who had spent forty years making things grow where the salt said nothing should. She didn't look up right away. Quentin had time to notice the garden itself: rows planted in defiance of the exposure, a rain barrel, a windbreak of driftwood stakes, and beyond it the tower rising sheer against the low sky, its lamp room dark, its paint peeling in long curls like birch bark.

"You're standing in my light," Ede said, without turning. "Either come in or go past, but don't hover. Hovering blocks the sun for the whole row."

"I'm sorry. I—" Quentin's prepared opening, the casual question about wrecks and tides, evaporated. "I'm looking for information. About the coast. Old stuff."

"Old stuff." Ede sat back on her heels and looked at Quentin properly for the first time, and her eyes were the pale grey-green of shallow water over slate, and they did not blink enough. "People who want old stuff usually want one old thing. Sit down." She nodded at a bench made from a hatch cover, sanded smooth, facing the water. "I'll make tea. You look like a man who hasn't been offered tea in seven years."

"I'm not—"

"Sit," Ede said, already moving toward the cottage, and Quentin sat.

From the bench, the view ran the whole sweep of the bay: the hook of the point, the drowned channel showing as a darker line in the sandflats, and far to the north, barely visible, the black slot in the cliffs where the sea went in and did not come back out except twice a day. Quentin's chest tightened. From up here you could see how the tide worked the entrance—how at low water the reef bared its teeth and the current funneled through the gap like water through a throat. No wonder no one dived it casually. No wonder, maybe, no one had found—

The cottage door opened and Ede came out with a tray: teapot, two mismatched mugs, a plate of oatcakes. She poured with steady hands, handed Quentin the chipped blue mug, and settled onto the bench with the sigh of a woman taking her rightful seat after a long absence.

"Now," she said. "Ask me."

"I told you. Old stuff. Local history. There's a—there are some caves along this stretch, north of the point. I wondered if there was any record—"

"There's no record," Ede said. "That's rather the point of them."

Quentin's mug stopped halfway to their mouth.

Ede watched them over the rim of her own tea, and something moved behind her eyes—not surprise, Quentin realized. Recognition. The particular stillness of a person watching a prediction come true.

"You fly a yellow-and-white One-Eighty," Ede said. "Floats, cargo pod, patched cowling on the left side where you ground-looped her two winters ago on the Kestrel ice. You do the medicine run Tuesdays and Fridays, mail run when the weather allows, and you land nowhere you can't leave within ten minutes. You buy your fuel at Kestrel because the man there doesn't talk. And your name—" she set her mug down on the bench between them, deliberately, like placing a stone on a grave or a bet, "—is Coldharbour. Quentin Coldharbour."

The wind filled the silence. Somewhere below, a gull laughed its harsh idiot laugh.

"How—" Quentin started, and stopped, because there were a hundred answers and none of them mattered. Small towns knew things. Lighthouse keepers especially; forty years of watching every hull that moved on this water, logging lights and shapes and silhouettes, a ledger of comings and goings maintained in the head if nowhere else.

"Your father refueled here," Ede said quietly. "August, September, October of that last year. Eleven times. He always took tea. He always sat on that bench. He always asked about you."

Quentin's hands had gone cold around the warm mug. "I don't know what you're talking about."

"No," Ede said. "You know exactly what I'm talking about. That's why you haven't touched your tea." She turned to face the water, giving Quentin the mercy of not being watched. "Eleven times he sat where you're sitting. And eleven times he asked me if I'd seen his kid lately, and eleven times I told him the truth, which was no. And the twelfth time, the plane never came, and I have spent seven years knowing something ended on my watch that I couldn't see, because I was watching the water and the ending came out of the sky."

"I'm not—" Quentin's voice came out wrong, scraped. They tried again. "I'm not here about him. I'm here about a wreck. A plane in a cave. That's all."

Ede was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had changed—lower, steadier, the voice of a woman who had kept a lamp burning through storms and knew the difference between weather and catastrophe.

"A plane in a cave," she repeated. "North of the point. Where the chart shows nothing."

"Yes."

"And you came to ask me if there was any record of it."

"That's what I said."

"Mm." Ede rose, joints cracking like small branches, and looked down at Quentin with an expression that was not quite pity and not quite triumph but some older, harder thing between them. "Then you'd better come inside, Quentin Coldharbour. Because there is no record. There is only my ledger. And I have been keeping a page open in it for seven years, waiting for somebody to ask me exactly that question—and I confess I always thought it would be you."

* * *

Inside, the keeper's cottage smelled of woodsmoke, dried herbs hanging in bundles from the beams, and paper—the deep, sweet dust-smell of old paper, which Quentin recognized with a start as the smell of their childhood, of the chart table in another house by another stretch of this same water. Bookshelves lined every wall, but between them, filling an entire case floor to ceiling, were ledgers. Dozens of them. Cloth-bound, spine-numbered, dating back—Quentin tilted their head to read the oldest visible—more than fifty years.

"The station log," Ede said, following Quentin's gaze. "Official copies went to the Coast Guard office in Port Anselm. These are mine. Every vessel that passed the light, every entry and departure from the anchorage, every oddity, every silence where there should have been a signal. Forty-one years." She crossed to the case and ran one soil-dark finger along the spines without pulling any out, the way you'd touch the keys of a piano you weren't ready to play. "When they automated the light in '89, they gave me a form to sign saying I surrendered all records. I signed it. I never surrendered anything. Paper burns, but a keeper learns to keep."

She took down a ledger from 1987—no. Quentin saw the year stamped on the spine and something in their stomach dropped through the floor of them. Not '87. The spine read **1998**, and beneath it, in Ede's small square handwriting, a title: *Meridian Star — inquiries.*

"You knew the name," Quentin said. "Before I said anything. Before I—"

"I knew the name the day she didn't arrive." Ede carried the ledger to the kitchen table, cleared a space among the seed catalogs and the radio manuals, and opened it. The pages were dense, ruled, immaculate—columns of dates, hull names, notes in that same square hand. She turned pages with care, and Quentin saw that near the end of the volume the entries changed character. Fewer routine passages. More notes, longer, pressed hard into the paper. And then a page marked with a ribbon of braided wool, faded red.

"Read it," Ede said. "Out loud. I've read it alone long enough."

Quentin bent over the page. The handwriting was steady, unhurried, the hand of someone writing by lamplight with all the time in the world—or none, and refusing to show it.

*Oct 14. Meridian Star departed Kestrel Narrows 0640, bound Harrow Point and points south. Mail load heavy — two full sacks plus registered parcel, unusual for this run. Pilot in good spirits. Asked after Q. Told him the truth again. He laughed and said, "Stubborn as her mother." Said he had one more delivery to make and then he was coming to collect what he was owed.*

Quentin stopped. The words swam. *Asked after Q.* Their own initial, sitting in a stranger's ledger for twenty-five years, patient as a stone.

"Keep going," Ede said softly.

*Oct 14, later. Weather building from the southeast, faster than forecast. Meridian Star passed the light southbound 1712, low, running the inside channel ahead of the front. Did not stop. Signal acknowledged. Note: pilot waved. Not the usual wing-rock. A wave. Like a man done with work.*

*Oct 15. Storm. Light held. No contact from Meridian Star. No arrival logged at Port Anselm. Search commenced 0900. Searched nine days. Wreckage: none. Debris field: none. Oil slick: none. Conclusion of official search: aircraft lost at sea, position unknown, presumed destroyed on impact with dispersal of wreckage by current. Filed. Closed.*

Below that, separated by a rule line, a final entry in different ink, bluer, added later:

*They searched open water. He would never have flown open water in that front. He hugged the coast his whole life — I used to tell him he flew like a man afraid of losing sight of home. If there is a cave between here and the cape that holds her, then someone put her there, or someone let the sea keep her quiet. I will keep this page open until the sea gives her back or I am dust. — E.O.*

The cottage was very quiet. The stove ticked. Outside, the tide was beginning to turn; Quentin could feel it, the way you feel a change in pressure, the whole coast inhaling.

"He hugged the coast," Quentin said. Their voice sounded far away to themself. "He used to say the open ocean was just a way of getting lost with extra steps."

"I know," Ede said. "He told me. He told me a great many things across eleven cups of tea, Quentin. Most of them were about you. Some of them were about the mail." She laid her hand flat on the ledger, on the page, as if warming it. "Do you understand what the Meridian Star was carrying? Not the registered parcel—I mean the sacks. Two full sacks of mail that never landed. Letters from families up and down this coast. Some of those families had been scattered by the storm of '79, the one that broke the cannery at Little Sound and sent half the town away looking for work. People wrote to each other for years after. Apologies. News. Names of children born in exile. And your father carried those letters because he was the only pilot willing to fly the little strips and the tide flats and the fog-bound passes for what it paid, which was almost nothing. He called himself a postman with wings. He was proud of it in a way he was proud of nothing else."

Quentin's throat had closed. They managed, barely: "What does that have to do with a cave?"

"Everything." Ede finally pulled out the chair opposite and sat, and leaned forward, and her pale eyes held Quentin's with a force that had kept ships honest for four decades. "A plane doesn't hide itself in a cave, child. Caves like that—sealed at high water, known to maybe three living people—are not stumbled into. Either the Meridian Star went in there deliberately, or she was put there deliberately, and either way it means the last flight was not what the search report says it was. And the only man who could tell us is the man who made it." Her voice dropped. "And the only person alive who might find what he left behind is sitting in my kitchen drinking tea out of my dead husband's mug, pretending they came here about local history."

Quentin looked down at the ledger. At *asked after Q*. At the wave, *like a man done with work*. Seven years of not saying the name, and here it was everywhere—in a stranger's careful hand, in a widow's blue ink, in the smell of old paper that was also the smell of home.

"I can't tell you where it is," Quentin said at last. It wasn't refusal, exactly. It was the sound of a door opening one inch against a strong wind.

"I'm not asking," Ede said. "Not yet. I waited seven years; I can wait for the tide." She closed the ledger gently, both hands, like closing a casket or a hymnal. "But I'll tell you what I know that isn't written anywhere, since you came all this way. Three people on this coast knew that cave existed. Your father was one. I was another—I drew him a sketch of the entrance once, years before, when he asked me about safe anchorages in a southeast blow, and I never asked him why he wanted it, because I trusted him. And the third—" she paused, and for the first time since Quentin had arrived, something flickered across her face that was not grief and not hope but fear, quick and cold as a fish turning under the surface, "—the third was a man who used to run salvage out of Port Anselm. Young, then. Clever. The kind of clever that counts other people's losses as his inventory. He went away for a while. And this spring, Quentin, a barge anchored two bays south of here, and a man came into the store asking after tide tables, and Mrs. Adeyemi says he signed the book *Voss*."

The name meant nothing to Quentin. The word *salvage* meant everything.

Outside, the light was failing, and far down the coast, invisible in the dusk, the sea was sliding up the sandflats to seal the cave for another twelve hours. Quentin stood, legs stiff, and found they could not leave—not yet, not with the ledger sitting closed on the table like a held breath.

"Ede," they said. Her first name, unbidden, and she didn't correct them. "The mail sacks. If the plane's intact, the mail might be intact. Paper keeps, underwater, cold and dark. I've seen it."

"I know," Ede said. "Paper keeps. So do promises." She rose and began, without ceremony, to clear the tea things, and over the sound of water running she said, in the tone of a woman lighting a lamp against a storm she could already hear: "Come back tomorrow. Bring whatever you're willing to bring. And Quentin—if a barge crew comes asking you questions, and they will, because Voss counts tides the way I count vessels—you remember that on this coast, the difference between a stranger and a neighbor is whether they ask or whether they take."

Quentin walked down the lighthouse road in the dark with the ledger's words lodged in them like shrapnel. *One more delivery to make and then he was coming to collect what he was owed.* At the dock, *Sparrowhawk* rode the black water, faithful and small, and Quentin untied her with cold hands and did not, for a long moment, climb aboard.

Behind and above them, on the point where the old light had stood dark for thirty years, a single window in the keeper's cottage burned steady against the night—someone keeping watch, the way keepers do, over a page still open.

And two bays south, past the headland, past the reach of any window's glow, the riding lights of a barge hung motionless on the water like a row of patient, unblinking eyes.
