## 4. Rumors Travel Faster Than Planes

The radio at Harrow Point was a relic from another decade, its casing cracked like dried mud, its dial glowing a sickly amber that made every voice sound like it was calling from the bottom of a well. Quentin had chosen it for exactly that reason. A good radio carried your words out into the world; a bad one made you think twice about sending them.

They had thought twice now for three days.

The cave sat in their mind like a stone swallowed whole—always there, a weight behind the ribs, impossible to ignore and impossible to dislodge. Three times they had flown the coastline at low tide, banking wide over the headland where the sea pulled back its grey curtain, and three times they had seen it: the black seam of the cave mouth, the water sliding out of it in long silver tongues, and inside, if you knew what to look for, the pale suggestion of metal. An aircraft's tail, standing out of forty years of silt like a grave marker someone had forgotten to carve.

*Meridian Star.* The registration half-eaten by corrosion, but the type was unmistakable to anyone who had grown up around a man who loved one particular airplane the way other men loved saints. Quentin had stood in the ebb wash with seawater soaking through their boots and felt the past reach up through the shingle and take hold of their ankle.

Now, on the fourth morning, with fog pressing against the hangar windows like something begging to be let in, Quentin finally keyed the mic.

"This is Coldharbour, five-seven-niner. Anyone got eyes on marine traffic between Cape Ferrow and the Narrows? Over."

Static. The long, patient static of an empty channel. Quentin let it run ten seconds, twenty, telling themselves the silence was the answer they wanted. Then:

"Coldharbour, five-seven-niner." A woman's voice, warm and slightly frayed at the edges—Marta Reyes, who ran the fuel dock at Little Saltee and knew everything about everyone, whether or not they consented to be known. "Quiet as church out there. Why d'you ask? You planning a trip somewhere interesting? Over."

"Just checking weather windows. Over."

"Since when do you check *traffic*, Quentin? Since when do you check anything? You fly like a man crossing a frozen river—quick and quiet and don't look down. Over."

Quentin closed their eyes. "Appreciate the report, Marta. Five-seven-niner out."

They set the mic down too carefully, the way you set down a thing you'd rather throw. That was the trouble with the coast. It looked empty. It sounded empty. But the airwaves were a web, and every strand hummed, and somewhere in that web a question as small as *anyone got eyes on marine traffic* could travel faster than any plane Quentin had ever pushed through sky.

They should have known better. They did know better. Seven years of knowing better, and still the mouth had moved before the mind caught up.

* * *

Wren Okafor arrived the way Wren always arrived: without announcement, without permission, and without any apparent awareness of either. Quentin heard the hangar door scrape open at half past seven—the kid had learned which board stuck and stepped over it—and then the particular shuffle-slap of sneakers worn down to their opinions.

"You're out of coffee," Wren announced from the shadows. "Also your left tire's soft. Also I fixed your oil pressure gauge, you're welcome, it was reading high because the sender unit was corroded, not because your engine hates you."

"My engine doesn't hate me."

"It definitely does. I've heard how you talk to it."

Quentin came around the nose of the Cessna with a rag in one hand and found the kid already halfway up the workbench, legs swinging, holding a mug of the coffee that did not exist. Sixteen years old, all elbows and alertness, hair cropped short and sun-bleached at the crown, eyes the color of shallow water over sand—quick, bright, missing nothing. Ede Okonkwo's granddaughter, though nobody used the word *granddaughter* within Wren's hearing; Wren preferred *apprentice*, or on ambitious days, *crew*.

"I'm working," Quentin said.

"You're staring at a wrench."

"I'm thinking about the wrench."

"Mm." Wren drank from the empty mug with great commitment. "Ede says come for supper tonight. She says if you say no she's going to start leaving food on your doorstep like a cat lady, and she'll keep doing it until you eat it, and then you'll owe her, and owing Ede is worse than eating with her. Her words."

"I have a schedule."

"You always have a schedule. Your schedule is: fly, fix, don't talk to anybody, sleep. It's a terrible schedule. There's no supper in it anywhere." Wren hopped down, wandered along the fuselage trailing two fingers across the aluminum the way other people touched piano keys. "So. What's out at Ferrow Head?"

The rag stopped moving.

"What?"

"Ferrow Head. The Narrows side. You asked Marta about traffic there this morning—I was at the dock getting gaskets for her, I heard you on the dock set." Wren turned, and there it was, that unblinking curiosity that would get the kid killed someday or make them famous, possibly both. "You never ask about traffic. You told me once the only traffic worth knowing about is the kind that can kill you, and you can see that with your own eyes."

"I said that?"

"Word for word. You also said—" Wren pitched their voice low, doing an impression that was frankly insulting and accurate, "'—the coast is full of people who will tell you things you didn't ask to know.'"

"And yet here we are."

"So what's at Ferrow Head?" Not pushy. Patient. That was the worst part. Wren could wait out a tide.

"Nothing," Quentin said. "A survey. Chart correction stuff. Boring."

"Boring," Wren repeated, tasting the word and finding it counterfeit. But the kid shrugged, dropped the subject the way they dropped everything—lightly, without letting go entirely—and said, "Supper's at six. Ede's making the fish thing. The fish thing is non-negotiable," and scraped back out through the sticking door into the fog.

Quentin stood alone under the hanging lamps and listened to the building tick and settle, and told themselves the lie they were best at: *nobody noticed anything.*

* * *

By noon, the lie had drowned.

It happened the way these things always happen on the coast—not as a leak but as a tide, invisible until it's everywhere. Quentin flew the afternoon mail run north to Tern Harbour, two hours of low cloud and drizzle, hands busy, mind circling the cave like a bird that wouldn't land. They were back on the ground by four, taxiing toward the hangar, when they saw Wren running down the apron toward them, and Wren did not run unless something was wrong or something was wonderful, and the expression on the kid's face was neither. It was fear wearing apology as a coat.

Quentin shut down and climbed out. "What."

"Okay. So. Don't be mad."

"That sentence has never once worked in human history."

"The *Vespera*." Wren was breathing hard, hands moving as if shaping the news out of air. "The salvage barge, the one anchored off Gullane Bay—you know, the fleet, Voss's people—they moved this afternoon. Two of the small boats came up the Narrows at slack water. Marta saw them. Half the dock saw them. They were sounding the bottom right off Ferrow Head, Quentin. Right off *your nothing*."

The world went very still and very loud at once, the way it does underwater.

"How," Quentin said. Just that. Quietly.

Wren's shoulders dropped. "I maybe—when I was at the dock, Marta asked why you'd called about traffic, and I said you were doing some chart survey thing out at Ferrow Head, and I didn't think—it was *nothing*, it was just an answer, people talk, that's what the dock *is*—" The kid's jaw tightened. "And then Old Tam heard it, and Tam radios everybody, because Tam thinks he's the coast guard, and by the time I got back to the lighthouse Ede had already heard it twice, once from Tam and once from the ferry guy, and then Deke Halloran—he runs crab out of Saltee—he called in and said the *Vespera*'s tenders were already on station, and—" Wren stopped, ran both hands through the bleached hair, and finished, smaller: "I'm sorry. I didn't know it was a secret. You said it was boring. Nobody guards boring things."

Nobody guards boring things. Quentin stood on the wet apron with the propeller ticking cool behind them and understood, with a clarity that felt almost physical, that the sentence had been true in every direction. They had guarded nothing, labeled nothing, and so the whole coast had handled the truth with bare hands and passed it along.

"It's not your fault," Quentin said, and meant it, mostly, and watched the kid's face unclench by a single degree.

"Who's Voss?" Wren asked. "People say the name like it's weather. Like it's something you just dress for."

Quentin looked south, past the hangar, past the tree line, toward the far grey seam of Gullane Bay, invisible from here but present anyway, the way a bruise is present through a sleeve.

"Voss is a man who buys wrecks," Quentin said. "All of them. Anywhere there's water. He doesn't dive himself. He hires people who don't ask what they're lifting, and he sells what they bring up to buyers who don't ask where it came from. He's been working this coast for two seasons, picking at the old war debris, the fishing boats, whatever the sea gives back." A pause. "He's never had anything worth his attention before."

"And now?"

"Now he's sounding the bottom off Ferrow Head," Quentin said, "because a sixteen-year-old answered a question honestly at a fuel dock."

Wren flinched. Quentin heard the cruelty of it a half-second after saying it, and hated themselves for it with a familiar, practiced efficiency.

"Get in the truck," Quentin said instead. "We're going to see your grandmother."

* * *

The road to the lighthouse climbed through salal and wind-bent spruce, the fog thickening as they rose until the world was reduced to a corridor of wet gravel and the smell of salt and resin. Wren rode in silence, arms crossed, radiating the specific misery of a young person who has been forgiven but not absolved. Twice they opened their mouth. Twice they closed it again.

At the top, the lighthouse stood white and patient above the churn, its lamp dark in the daylight, its keeper waiting on the step as though she'd been expecting them since dawn. Ede Okonkwo was seventy-something and built like the geography itself—broad, weathered, unmoved by argument. She took one look at their faces and said, "Inside. Kettle's on. Whatever it is, it'll keep for four minutes."

Inside was warmth and order: a kitchen that smelled of woodsmoke and ginger, charts rolled in tubes along one wall like scrolls in a library, a ledger shelf that Ede dusted more often than her own windows. She poured tea without asking preferences, set the pot on a trivet shaped like a whale, and sat, folding her hands.

"Now," she said. "Tell me who Voss is and why my granddaughter looks like she's been caught smuggling."

Quentin told her. Not all of it—never all of it—but enough: the cave, the tail section, the registration, the tenders sounding the Narrows. Ede listened the way she did everything, completely, without interruption, and when Quentin finished she was quiet for a long moment, looking at the middle distance where, presumably, forty years of coastal memory was being consulted like a ledger.

"The Meridian Star," she said at last, softly, and the name in her mouth sounded less like a ship than a person. "You found the Meridian Star."

"You know it."

"Everyone on this coast knows it, or knew it, or lost somebody to it." Her eyes came up and settled on Quentin with a weight that made the tea go cold in the cup. "Including you. Including your family. Don't look surprised—I've known who you are since the day you landed here seven years ago with a duffel bag and a chip on your shoulder the size of a pontoon. Coldharbour. I kept my mouth shut because you clearly wanted it kept shut, and grief gets to choose its own silence. But Quentin." She leaned forward. "If that plane is sitting in a cave at Ferrow Head, and men like Voss are already dropping sounders over it, then the time for your silence is nearly done. Because those letters—"

"Letters?" Wren said.

But Ede was still looking at Quentin, and Quentin was looking at the table, and somewhere south of them, beyond the fog and the headlands, in the grey water of Gullane Bay, a barge sat at anchor with its lights coming on one by one as the afternoon died—and aboard it, Quentin knew with the certainty of a man who has heard a clock begin to strike, someone was drawing lines on a chart, calculating tides, and scheduling.

Six days, maybe. Maybe fewer.

"Kettle's cold," Ede said, rising. "I'll put it back on. And then, I think, you're going to want to see my ledgers."

Outside, the fog pressed close against the windows, and far off, so faint it might have been imagination, came the sound of an engine working water—patient, methodical, closer than yesterday.
