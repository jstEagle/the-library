## 5. The Fleet at Anchor

The *Corvina* rode at anchor in the lee of Sable Head like a thing that had always been there, and would always remain. Marcus Voss stood at her rail with a cup of coffee gone cold in his hand, and watched the light come up over the water the way other men watched ledgers open.

He did not drink the coffee. He never did. It was a prop, nothing more—a small ceramic argument that he was a man at leisure, a broker of no particular urgency. In truth he had not been at leisure in thirty-one years, since the morning in Valparaíso when he'd understood, at fourteen, that the sea gives nothing back and that everything worth having must be taken from it on a schedule.

The bay south of the headland was called, on the charts, Little Ferrow, and it was called that on no chart at all, by the handful of people who lived along its shingle and mudflats. There were eleven houses, a cannery that had closed the year of the big storm, and a church whose steeple leaned like a man listening. Voss had surveyed all of it in the first two days. Eleven houses meant eleven possible informants, four usable boats, and one road that turned to soup in rain. The cannery's derelict pier would take his barge at high water if the piles held, and he'd had Okafor—no, not Okafor, that was the lighthouse woman—*Renk*, the salvage master, sound the bottom. Fourteen feet at mean low. Enough.

Coasts were inventory. Voss had learned this from his first employer, a Dutchman named Verhoeven who had salvaged half the Java Sea and buried the other half in paperwork. Verhoeven used to say that a coastline was just a balance sheet drawn in water, and every year the tide redrew it, and the man who read it fastest got rich. Voss had been reading coastlines for three decades now. He had read them in the Hebrides and the Coral Sea, off Labrador and below the typhoon latitudes, and he had learned that the reading never changed: somewhere along every mile of shore, the water was holding something someone wanted, and the water was patient, and men were not.

So you did not fight the water. You scheduled it.

He went inside to the chart table.

* * *

The *Corvina*'s chart room smelled of diesel, brass polish, and old varnish, a smell Voss found more honest than any church. The room was small and he kept it that way; a chart room that felt cramped made a man think in tight, efficient lines. On the table, weighted at the corners with brass fittings he'd unscrewed from a drowned tug off Ushant, lay the coast chart—official edition, last survey twenty-two years old—and beside it, the tide tables for the current lunar fortnight, which his second, Renk, had transcribed in pencil because Voss did not trust printed tables the way he trusted a man's handwriting. A man's handwriting could be questioned. Print simply sat there, smug.

He pinned the tide curve first. It rose up the page like a slow mountain range: two highs, two lows, each a little later than the last, the whole rhythm sliding forward through the days the way a drunk slides down a wall. He had watched that slide ten thousand times. Most men saw the tide as an enemy. Voss saw it as a timetable that happened to be published by the moon.

Now the other element. He laid a second sheet over the chart—tracing paper, and on it, in his own small, level hand, the intelligence.

It had come in the night before, at 2340, by single-sideband, from a fisherman he paid two hundred a month to listen to the radio net and remember what he heard. The fisherman was not clever. That was why Voss paid him. Clever men editorialized. This one simply repeated, in a flat voice, what he'd heard: a bush pilot out of the interior strips, callsign registered to one Quentin Coldharbour, had keyed the net asking after wrecks in the cliffs north of the old light, and had used the word *mail plane*, and had then gone quiet in a way the fisherman described as *like a man who'd said too much*.

Mail plane. North of the light. A pilot asking questions who had never asked questions before.

Voss had sat with that for a long while after the transmission ended, the radio hissing, the cabin lamp swinging gently with the swell. There were forty wrecks on that stretch of coast that a fisherman might mean. But there was only one mail plane that mattered, and Voss had spent four years and a great deal of money learning that it had gone into the water somewhere between the light and the headland, and that the official record—a search that lasted nine days in weather that grounded the searchers for six of them—had closed the file with the word *lost*. Voss did not believe in lost. He believed in unfound. Unfound was simply an asset awaiting survey.

And now a pilot with no history on this coast had found something the charts didn't show.

He drew the cave. He could not know its exact position—Coldharbour had been careful, or lucky, or both—but the geology narrowed it. The cliffs north of the light were limestone for two miles, then basalt. A cave large enough to swallow an aircraft existed only in the limestone, and the tide tables told him the rest: a cave that revealed itself at ebb was a cave whose mouth lay between the four-foot and nine-foot curves. That left a working window, and the window moved forward each day with the tide's slow stagger.

He began to calculate.

* * *

Renk found him there at dawn, as he'd known Renk would. The salvage master was a broad, grey man with hands like mooring line and the permanent expression of someone listening to a hull listen to the sea. He stood in the chart room doorway, working a toothpick, and waited, because he had learned that Voss spoke when the arithmetic was finished and not one word before.

"Six days," Voss said.

Renk came to the table and looked at the tide curve. "For what?"

"For everything." Voss laid his pencil against the paper. "Look. Today's low is at 0612, minus one point three. Tomorrow, 0704. The window shifts forty to fifty minutes a day, and it shrinks—the neaps are coming, see, here, day five and six, the range drops to under seven feet. If the cave mouth needs a good ebb to open, we may get one clean working day at the end of the window and not know it until we're in it." He tapped the curve. "So. Day one, we move the barge and the *Petrel* north after dark, anchor behind the headland, out of sight of the light. Day two, divers in at slack, run a line search along the limestone at minus two. If the pilot's found it, they'll find his anchor trash—maybe a mooring, a marker. Day three, we confirm the mouth. Day four and five, we work it. Day six, we're gone, or we're legal, or we're both."

"And the pilot?"

"What about the pilot?"

Renk's toothpick moved to the other side of his mouth. "Fisherman says Coldharbour flew the interior strips for seven years. No record anywhere else. Man like that finds a wreck, he doesn't radio about it unless he wants something. Wants a partner, or wants a price."

Voss considered this. He had a private taxonomy of people he met on coasts. There were the hungry, who could be bought. There were the frightened, who could be moved. There were the righteous, who could be waited out, because righteousness was expensive and nobody subsidized it forever. And there was a fourth kind, rarer, that he thought of privately as the vanished—the ones who had already paid their price somewhere else and were living on the interest. The vanished were the hardest. You could not buy a man who had nothing he wanted. But you could offer him the one thing he'd structured his whole life around.

"Everyone wants something," Voss said. "The pilot wants the wreck to himself, or wants it gone, or wants whatever's in it. It doesn't matter which. What matters is that a man alone on a coast is a man with no claim, no witnesses, and no lawyer. If he's smart, he'll know that. If he's smart, he'll want to deal before the water decides for both of us." He made a small mark on the tracing paper—a circle, around nothing yet, the position to be filled in. "I'll talk to him myself. Day three, maybe day four. Whiskey, not threats. Threats cost you twice—once when you make them and again when you have to keep them."

"And if he won't deal?"

Voss looked up at Renk, and for a moment the lamp swung and the shadows moved on his face like water moving over a hull. "Then we work the window. The sea doesn't ask permission, Renk. It never has. Everything else that asks permission—courts, communities, pilots—that's just weather. And weather," he said, "is a scheduling problem."

Renk nodded slowly, the way he nodded at everything, and went forward to wake the divers.

* * *

Alone, Voss did what he did every morning of his working life, in every port, in every season: he stood at the chart table and made the sea small.

That was the secret no one understood about him, the thing the coast would get wrong in the days to come, calling him ruthless, calling him a pirate, calling him worse. He was none of those things. Ruthlessness was a passion, and Voss had no passions. What he had was a method. The sea was vast, and indifferent, and it drowned men and swallowed planes and erased whole towns from the charts without malice, and the only way to live beside a thing like that was to shrink it. To turn its tides into a column of numbers. To turn its drowned dead into tonnage and registry and salvage value. To turn its grief into inventory.

He had a file on the Meridian Star that was two inches thick. He had the builder's plate number, the engine serials, the last known fuel uplift, the insurance settlement, the name of the underwriter's clerk who had processed the claim and retired to a bungalow where he now grew tomatoes and answered questions for the price of a decent lunch. He knew the plane carried mail—thirty-one sacks, by the manifest, plus registered freight whose declared value had always struck Voss as a lie told in the wrong direction, too low, the way people lowball a thing they don't want noticed. He knew the pilot's name. He had read the pilot's file twice, and both times had lingered on the same line—the next of kin—and had felt nothing at all, which was how he knew the line mattered.

Somewhere under that grey water was thirty-one sacks of other people's unfinished business, and unfinished business, Voss had learned, was the only cargo that appreciated. Gold was gold. Letters were leverage. A mother's handwriting in a drowned man's mailbag could open doors that gold could not, and close others that gold could not buy shut. He did not know yet what he would do with the letters. That was the beauty of inventory. You did not have to know. You only had to hold it.

He rolled the tide tables, squared the tracing paper, and went out on deck.

The morning had come up clean and cold, the water in the bay lying flat as poured metal, and the rest of the fleet lay at their moorings in a loose crescent: the barge with its crane folded like a sleeping insect, the workboat *Petrel*, the dive tender, a longliner he'd chartered whose crew believed, on this trip, that they were running crab gear. Eleven houses on the far shore, smoke rising from three of their chimneys. People waking up. People who thought the day was about weather, and fish, and the small mechanics of their own lives.

By day six, Voss thought, they would understand that the day had been about him all along. Not because he was cruel. Because he had read the water first.

He raised his binoculars and swept the headland, the cliffs, the pale seam of limestone, and the grey line of the sea beyond it, patient as a ledger, holding what it held.

Day one began tomorrow, at 0704.

But the sea, being the sea, had already begun without him—and forty miles north, in a cave the charts denied, the water was at that very moment falling away from a corroded tail section, exposing it to the cold air inch by inch, and somewhere above the cliffs a light burned in a lighthouse where an old woman was, at that same hour, opening a ledger she had kept for seven years against exactly this dawn.

Voss lowered the binoculars. He did not know any of this. He only knew the arithmetic, and the arithmetic said six days.

He went below to schedule them.
