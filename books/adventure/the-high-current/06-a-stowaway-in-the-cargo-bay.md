## 6. A Stowaway in the Cargo Bay

The weather came down off the hills the way it always did in October—without apology, without warning, and without any interest in what Quentin had planned for the day.

They'd loaded at dawn: flour and diesel filters, a crate of antibiotics for the nursing station at Kettle Reach, two drums of stove oil lashed tight, mail sacks that smelled of damp and ink. Routine. The kind of flight Quentin had made four hundred times, maybe five hundred, each one identical to the last, which was exactly how they liked it. Identical meant safe. Identical meant nothing new could find you at twelve hundred feet over a grey sea.

The Otter lifted off the strip at Greywater Point into a sky the color of wet slate, and Quentin flew with one hand loose on the yoke and the other resting on their knee, watching the coast unspool beneath them—the black rock, the white wrack lines, the little harbors tucked into the folds of the land like secrets people kept telling themselves they'd get around to someday.

Wind out of the southeast, building. The barometer on the hangar wall had been falling all morning, and old-timers' knees had been aching on every dock from here to the light, which was as reliable a forecast as anything the government put out. Quentin had considered scrubbing the run. Had considered it for about four seconds, then loaded anyway, because Kettle Reach needed its antibiotics and because flying in bad weather was still easier than sitting still in good weather, listening to the town breathe around them.

Forty minutes out, crossing the open water between the point and the first chain of islands, the engine note changed.

Not much. A stutter, a hesitation—like a man clearing his throat before saying something he'd rather not say. Quentin's hand went to the throttle, feathering it, listening with their whole body the way you learn to when the machine beneath you is the only thing between you and very cold water. The engine caught itself. Held. Then did it again, three seconds later, and this time there was a shudder through the airframe that had nothing to do with turbulence.

"Come on," Quentin murmured. "Don't do this to me."

There was nowhere to land. That was the arithmetic of it, quick and brutal: open water below, swells already whitecapping, the nearest strip forty minutes behind them and fifty minutes ahead. If the engine quit, they'd ditch, and ditching an Otter in a rising sea was a coin toss weighted badly against you.

Quentin ran the checks. Mixture, fuel flow, magnetos—switching from both to left, left to right, hunting the stumble. It smoothed on the right mag, roughened again, smoothed. Carb ice, maybe. They pushed the heat full on and watched the gauge and waited, and the engine grumbled and thought about it and finally settled into something like honesty, running rough but running, and Quentin let out a breath they hadn't known they were holding.

That was when the cargo shifted behind them.

Not the drums—they were strapped and cinched, and Quentin had checked the tie-downs twice. This was lighter. A soft, muffled thump, followed by a sound that no crate or sack ever made in the history of aviation.

A sneeze.

Quentin went very still, hands on the yoke, eyes flicking to the mirror bolted above the cargo bay door. In its smoky rectangle, between the flour sacks and the medical crate, something moved. Something small. Something wearing, as far as Quentin could tell, an orange rain jacket with the hood up, pulled tight, as if that would help.

"You have got," Quentin said aloud, to the mirror, "to be kidding me."

* * *

The plan, such as it was, involved landing at the next inlet, dragging a stowaway out by the collar, and putting them on the first boat back. It was a good plan. It was a sensible plan. It survived contact with reality for approximately eleven minutes, which was how long it took Quentin to reach the inlet and see what the weather was doing there.

Nothing good. The squall line had arrived ahead of schedule, dragging curtains of rain across the water, and the wind sock at the inlet dock stood straight out, snapping like something in pain. Landing in that was possible. Taking off again afterward was not guaranteed. And somewhere behind Quentin, in the cargo bay, the stowaway was making small, determined noises of a person trying very hard not to be sick.

Quentin weighed it. Forty minutes more to Kettle Reach, where there was a roof and a stove and a woman named Marta who owed Quentin three favors and asked no questions. Or a landing now, in weather like this, with a plane that was already misbehaving.

The engine stuttered again, as if casting its own vote.

"Fine," Quentin said. "Fine. But you're walking home."

They climbed instead of descended, riding the rough air up to fifteen hundred feet where the ride steadied somewhat, and set course direct for Kettle Reach, and only then did Quentin unbuckle, wedge themselves half-backward between the seats, and pull the hood of the orange rain jacket down.

Wren looked up at them with the expression of a person who has rehearsed this moment all night and discovered, upon arrival, that none of the rehearsal helps.

"Hi," Wren said.

"Don't 'hi' me." Quentin braced a hand on the seat frame as the plane bucked. "How long have you been in my cargo bay?"

"Since you loaded."

"Since I—" Quentin closed their eyes briefly. "You were in the hangar. At dawn. In the dark."

"I'm quiet," Wren said, with a certain pride.

"You're a stowaway. There's a difference, and it's about six feet of jail cell." Quentin hauled themselves back into the pilot's seat, wrestled the plane level through a pocket of sinking air, and tried to assemble the speech they'd been assembling since the sneeze. It kept falling apart, mostly because every version of it ended with *and I'm turning this plane around*, and they couldn't turn the plane around. The admission sat in Quentin's chest like a swallowed stone: the kid had picked the one morning, the one flight, when there was no clean way out. Whether by luck or by calculation—and knowing Wren, Quentin was beginning to suspect calculation—the timing was perfect.

"Put your belt on," Quentin said instead. "Shoulder harness. Now."

Wren scrambled to comply, threading the straps with hands that shook slightly—not from fear, Quentin noted, but from cold. Of course. The cargo bay of an unheated Otter at altitude in October. How long had the kid been sitting back there in the dark among the crates, breathing on their hands?

"There's a blanket under the survival kit," Quentin said. "Green bag, aft bulkhead. Get it. Wrap up. Don't touch anything else."

Wren found the blanket and burrowed into it until only their face showed, and for a while the only sounds were the engine's rough song and the rain starting to tick against the windshield, fine at first, then harder.

"Why?" Quentin said finally. Not looking back. Watching the grey water slide past below.

Silence. Then, quietly: "Because if I stayed, I'd have to be at the dock at four o'clock, and I couldn't do it."

"Do what at four o'clock?"

Another silence, longer. When Wren answered, the voice had changed—flattened out, the way voices go when someone is reciting something they've gone over so many times it's worn smooth. "My uncle's taking me to the mainland. Enrolling me in school there. He says—" A breath. "He says I need structure. He says hanging around the hangar learning engines isn't a life. He's got the boat booked and everything. Four o'clock."

"So you ran away onto a plane instead of onto a boat."

"The plane goes farther."

Quentin almost laughed. Almost. It came out as a grunt that could have been mistaken for one. "Kettle Reach has eleven buildings and a nurse. It's not exactly farther."

"It's not the mainland." Wren leaned forward until their chin nearly rested on the back of Quentin's seat, and Quentin could feel the words against their neck. "Look—I know you don't want anybody around. Everybody knows. Seven years, nobody's even seen you eat at the chowder place. But you fly. Every day. You go places. And I can load, and I can tie down, and I know the difference between avgas and jet fuel, which apparently some grown adults don't, and I fixed the compressor at Halvorsen's without being asked twice, and—" The voice cracked, just slightly, and recovered. "I just need somebody to not put me on a boat today. That's all. One day."

The rain hammered. The engine droned its imperfect drone. Quentin stared through the windshield at the wall of weather ahead and felt something move in their chest that they resented deeply—the particular ache of recognizing yourself in someone else. Seven years ago there had been a boy—no, a young man, though he'd felt like neither—who had looked at a horizon and decided that anywhere was better than the place where everyone knew his name and his business and his failures. Seven years of proving it, one anonymous flight at a time.

And here was a sixteen-year-old who had done the math differently: who had looked at all that empty distance and run toward the one person known for having none of it.

"You understand I can't keep you," Quentin said. "This isn't— I'm not in the business of keeping things. Or people."

"I'm not asking you to keep me. I'm asking you to not deliver me."

"That's the same thing."

"It's really not," Wren said, with the serene confidence of youth confronting experience. "Delivering is easy. You just hand somebody over and fly away. Keeping is hard. I'm offering you the easy option."

Quentin gripped the yoke and said nothing, because the alternative was saying something true, and they had spent seven years getting very good at not doing that.

* * *

Kettle Reach materialized out of the rain all at once, the way it always did—a gray smear of shoreline, then the gravel bar, then the cluster of roofs with smoke standing straight up from the chimneys in the dead air. Quentin landed long and smooth on the wet strip, taxied to Marta's dock, and shut down, and for a moment the silence after the engine was enormous, filled only by rain on aluminum and the drip of water off the struts.

Marta came out under an umbrella, took the medical crate, counted the antibiotics twice out of habit, and squinted past Quentin at the small figure climbing stiffly down from the plane in a green blanket.

"New co-pilot?" she asked.

"Stowaway," Quentin said.

"Mm." Marta had raised four children and buried one husband and had long since stopped being surprised by anything younger than sixty. "Well, there's soup on. Stowaways eat too, or they don't, their choice." She carried the crate off toward the nursing station, umbrella bobbing, leaving the question of what happened next lying on the wet gravel between Quentin and Wren like something dropped.

Wren stood there, dripping, blanket clutched around their shoulders, and did not ask for anything. That was the thing Quentin noticed, the thing that lodged under their skin like a splinter. The kid didn't plead, didn't argue, didn't deploy whatever arsenal of persuasion they'd used in the cargo bay. They just stood in the rain and looked at the plane with an expression Quentin recognized from mirrors—a person measuring the exact size of the world they were about to be handed back.

"The tide'll be wrong for the mainland boat till tomorrow anyway," Quentin heard themselves say. Their own voice sounded strange, like it belonged to someone slightly less careful than usual. "Weather's shutting everything down tonight. Nobody's going anywhere."

Wren turned. Said nothing. Waited.

"So you might as well sleep somewhere dry," Quentin went on, staring hard at the middle distance, at the rain, at anything but the kid's face. "There's room in the hangar at Kettle Reach. Marta keeps it. There's a cot. Tomorrow we'll figure out—there's a floatplane runs back down-coast on Thursdays, the pilot owes me, we can put you on it, and he can take you wherever it is you're supposed to be delivered."

"Okay," Wren said softly.

"And you're sweeping the hangar floor before we leave. And checking my tie-downs. And if you ever come near my cargo bay again without me knowing, I will personally—"

"Yeah," Wren said, and the grin that broke across their face was sudden and crooked and entirely unrepentant, and Quentin turned away from it quickly, before it could do any further damage.

Soup was eaten. The storm arrived in earnest after dark, rattling the windows of Marta's kitchen, and Quentin lay awake in the spare room listening to it and cataloguing, with clinical precision, all the ways this was a mistake. The kid would slow them down. The kid talked. The kid knew people—knew everybody, apparently, in the way of sixteen-year-olds who grew up under floats and wings, and knowing people was dangerous, knowing people meant information moving, and Quentin had spent seven years making sure information about Quentin Coldharbour moved nowhere at all.

Except.

Except there had been a moment, somewhere over the open water, when the engine had stumbled and Quentin's whole body had gone cold with the old animal knowledge of mortality, and the first thought after *not today* had been, absurdly, *there's a kid in the back*. Not *I'm alone*. Not *no one will even miss me for days*. Just: *there's a kid in the back*, and the weight of that had been—what? Not heavy. The opposite of heavy. Like setting something down that had been carried so long its absence felt like vertigo.

Quentin fell asleep angry about it, and woke up still angry, and was pouring coffee in Marta's kitchen at dawn when Wren shuffled in, blanket around their shoulders like a cape, hair flattened on one side, and said:

"So I've been thinking about your cave."

Quentin's cup stopped halfway to their mouth. "What cave?"

"The one you radioed about Tuesday night. To the survey office. Low tide, north side of Gullwrack, mouth in the cliff—you told them to check their charts because it wasn't marked." Wren poured cereal with the calm of a person laying cards on a table. "Half the coast monitors that frequency. My uncle's scanner picks it up in the kitchen."

The coffee went down cold. Outside, the storm was dying, and the sea beyond the window was flattening into long steel-colored swells, and somewhere south of all that water, Quentin understood with sudden terrible clarity, other people might have been listening too.

"How many people," Quentin said carefully, "have scanners?"

Wren shrugged, and for the first time since the cargo bay, looked genuinely uncertain. "On this coast? Everybody."
