## 7. Letters That Never Landed

Ede had kept the ledger in the same drawer for thirty-one years—the bottom one, beneath the tide tables and the spare wicks and a tin of buttons that belonged to no one she could name. She did not open it often. Once a year, on the anniversary of the storm, she would take it out and read the first page only, then put it back, because the first page was enough to remember everything that came after.

Tonight she took it all the way out.

Quentin Coldharbour sat across her kitchen table with a mug of tea going cold between their hands, and Ede watched them the way she watched weather coming off the water: patiently, reading the pressure in the set of their shoulders, the way their eyes kept moving to the window as if the cave were visible from here, as if the sea might give up its secrets twice in one lifetime just because a pilot asked nicely.

They hadn't told her why they'd come. They'd knocked at dusk with some story about chart discrepancies, about a cave that didn't appear on any survey they owned, and Ede had let them talk for four full minutes before she said, "You have his chin, you know. Eliot Coldharbour's chin. He used to sit exactly where you're sitting and pretend he wasn't worried about something."

That had silenced them better than any question.

Now she laid the ledger on the table between them. It was a ship's logbook once—she'd bought it surplus from a chandlery in Port Anselm the year she took over the light—and its cover was the color of old rope, swollen at the edges from decades of salt air. Across the front, in her own younger handwriting, faded to the brown of dried kelp: *MERIDIAN STAR — MAIL MANIFEST — UNDELIVERED.*

"What is this?" Quentin asked. Their voice had gone careful, the way voices go when someone senses a floor giving way beneath them.

"An accounting," Ede said. "Of everything that never arrived."

* * *

Quentin looked at the ledger and felt the tea turn to lead in their stomach.

The cover alone would have been enough. But it was the word *undelivered* that did it—that plain administrative word, the kind of word that belongs to post offices and insurance forms and has no business carrying this much weight. Undelivered. As if the letters were simply late. As if forty years weren't long enough for a letter to be considered lost rather than delayed.

"You knew," Quentin said. It came out harsher than intended. "About the wreck. You've known."

"I knew a plane went down." Ede's hands rested flat on either side of the ledger, dark hands gone thin with age, knuckles like driftwood knots. "I knew what it carried. I did not know where it lay until you walked into my kitchen tonight wearing your father's face and asking after caves that don't exist."

"The charts don't show it."

"No," Ede said. "They don't. And there's a reason for that, which we'll come to." She opened the cover. "First you need to understand what's down there. Not gold. Not salvage. Mail."

The first page was a manifest in three columns, written in a hand that wasn't Ede's—younger, quicker, slanting hard to the right. Quentin recognized it before they understood they recognized it, the way you recognize a voice through a wall.

Their father's handwriting.

*Bundle 1 — Okonkwo, A. — 14 items.*
*Bundle 2 — Ferreira, M. — 9 items.*
*Bundle 3 — Voss Holdings c/o Anselm Trust — 22 items.*
*Bundle 4 — Coldharbour, E. — 1 item.*

One item. Bundle four. One item addressed to Eliot Coldharbour, and Quentin's eyes snagged on it like a hook in cloth.

"That last one," they said, and their voice betrayed them, cracking on the second word.

Ede turned the page without answering. "In the spring of '89 there was a storm—not a hurricane, nothing with a name, just three days of northeast wind that took the coast road out at Miller's Bend and cut half these communities off for a season. And while the road was out, there was a scandal. The Anselm Trust—which held deeds, mortgages, fishing licenses, whole livelihoods in paper—was found to have been selling families' debts to mainland buyers. Quietly. Families who thought they owed the bank in town discovered they owed strangers two thousand miles away, strangers who wanted cash instead of work, who wanted the houses empty and the waterfront clear."

She turned another page. The manifest continued, bundle after bundle, each entry annotated in red ink in Ede's hand: *delivered by hand, 1991.* *Delivered, 1994.* *Addressee deceased—held.* *Held.* *Held.*

"When the scandal broke, the Trust burned its local records—or tried to. But the mail kept moving. People wrote to each other. Affidavits, testimonies, letters between cousins about who signed what, a widow's account of the man who came to appraise her house while her husband was dying. Evidence, some of it. Grief, most of it. All of it was supposed to go out on the Meridian Star's last run—out to the mainland, to lawyers, to newspapers, to family." Ede's thumb moved along the edge of the pages without turning them. "The plane never made the first waypoint."

"And the mail stayed in it," Quentin said. "Forty years."

"Forty-one." Ede closed the ledger gently, as though the pages might bruise. "I was keeper then. I saw the Star go out that morning in weather I'd have called marginal, and I logged my objection, and I was overruled by a man from the Trust who said the schedule mattered more than the sky." Her jaw tightened around something old. "When it didn't arrive, they searched for nine days and called it lost with all hands. And the families—" she exhaled through her nose, slow, controlled— "the families waited for letters that explained everything. Who owed what. Who had lied. Which of their neighbors had sold them out. Some of them died not knowing. Some of their children are alive right now, running boats out of harbors you land at every week, still not knowing why their grandparents lost everything."

Quentin sat very still. The stove ticked. Somewhere outside, the ebb was drawing the water back off the flats, that vast slow exhale the coast made twice a day, and Quentin thought of the cave filling with cold green silence, of mail sacks rotting in the dark, holding words that people had died waiting for.

"Why keep the manifest?" they asked. "Why not tell someone? The authorities, the papers—"

"Tell them what? That a plane is missing somewhere in two hundred miles of coastline?" Ede shook her head. "I spent eleven years looking, Quentin. I walked beaches. I paid fishermen to drag their pots along the reefs. Nothing. And then your father disappeared too, seven years ago now, and I stopped looking for the plane and started waiting for the person who'd find it." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and absolutely steady. "Because I always knew someone would. The sea gives things back. It was only ever a question of who would be standing there when it did."

* * *

Afterward, Quentin stood out on Ede's gallery with the wind coming off the water and tried to make their hands stop shaking.

It wasn't fear. They knew fear well enough to know its shape—cold in the forearms, quick in the breath. This was something else, something heavier, sitting behind the sternum like ballast. Inheritance. The word arrived uninvited and refused to leave.

Through the kitchen window they could see Ede at her table, copying entries onto fresh paper with a fountain pen, unhurried, precise. Preparing. For what—for distribution, for proof, for whatever came next—and Quentin understood suddenly that the old woman had been preparing for decades. That the ledger wasn't a record. It was a promise she'd made to people who were mostly dead, and she'd kept it alone all this time, and now she meant to hand it over.

To them. To the child of the pilot who'd carried the mail and never landed.

Quentin's father had flown the Meridian Star. That fact had lived in Quentin's body for years like shrapnel—too deep to remove, too close to bone to ignore. Every wrecked aircraft of that type they'd ever seen in a hangar or a museum had made their chest tighten. And now the actual plane lay in a cave six miles north, and its manifest listed a bundle addressed to Eliot Coldharbour personally. One item. One letter, in a sack at the bottom of the sea, addressed to a man who had then vanished himself seven years ago without a note, without a body, without so much as a forwarding address for his own child.

Unless he hadn't vanished. Unless—

No. Quentin shut the door on that thought the way they always did, leaning their weight against it. Seven years of practice. Their father had left. Whatever else was true, that was the load-bearing fact, and Quentin had built a whole life on top of it: the solo runs, the unlisted number, the habit of leaving towns before anyone learned their middle name. You don't build a life on sand. You build it on the hardest thing you know.

But the ledger had other names in it. Names that weren't Coldharbour. Ferreira. Okonkwo. Whole bundles of them, dozens of items, families scattered by storm and scandal, still waiting. And those names didn't care what Quentin had built or why. Those names were just lying in the dark, patient as stones, waiting for someone with a boat and a dive light and a reason.

Quentin had a reason. They just didn't know yet whether it was the right one.

The truth they wouldn't say aloud, even to themselves, even alone on a dark gallery with nobody to hear: part of them had looked at that manifest and seen money. Salvage rights on a historic wreck, recovered mail as artifacts, the story itself—exclusive, sellable, worth more than the gold everyone assumed was down there. Enough to vanish forever. Enough to finally be free of the past instead of merely outrunning it. The thought had risen in them clean and cold as a fish rising to a fly, and they'd let it swim.

And another part of them—one they liked less, because it demanded things—kept seeing Ede's face when she'd said *some of them died not knowing.* Kept hearing the tick of the stove and thinking: forty-one years. Someone could have had an answer for forty-one years, and it was sitting in a cave, and Quentin was the only person on this coast who knew where.

Freedom, Quentin had always believed, meant owing nothing to anyone.

But the ledger owed. The ledger had been owing for four decades, and debts like that don't dissolve—they transfer. They wash downstream and come ashore wherever the current leaves them, and the current had left them here, in a kitchen that smelled of woodsmoke and cardamom, in the keeping of a woman who had waited seven years for a stranger with her friend's chin to knock on her door.

Behind them, the kitchen door opened. Ede stepped out with a folded page in her hand—not the ledger, a copy, ink still shining faintly.

"Bundle four," she said quietly. "Your father's. One item." She held it out. "I copied the entry line for you. I thought you should carry it yourself."

Quentin took the paper. Under the porch light the handwriting leapt out at them again, that hard rightward slant, urgent even in a manifest line:

*Bundle 4 — Coldharbour, E. — 1 item — personal correspondence, sealed. Note: addressee instructed carrier to hold for delivery "when my kid comes home."*

The wind moved through the spruce behind the house, and somewhere far down the coast, so faint it might have been imagination, Quentin heard the low diesel thrum of engines that did not belong to any fishing boat—a fleet at anchor, two bays south, counting tides.

"When your kid comes home," Ede repeated softly. "He wrote that on the manifest, Quentin. Forty-one years ago. Before you were born, maybe. Before anything." She searched their face. "What do you suppose he meant?"

Quentin couldn't answer. The paper trembled in their hand, and the engines thumped closer to hearing, and the tide below the gallery was already beginning its long turn toward the flood.
