## 8. Tide Two

The tide turned at 4:17 in the morning, and Quentin was awake to watch it, because Quentin had been awake since two.

They lay in the bunk of the Beaver's cabin with the propane lamp off and the window cracked, listening to the water drop away from the hull in long, wet sighs. Seven years of sleeping on water taught you its grammar. The slap of a rising tide against the pontoons said *stay put*. The slow peel of an ebb said *go*. And underneath both, tonight, there was a third sound—a low grinding hush from the direction of the headland, where the sea was pulling back off the rocks like a hand withdrawing from a wound, uncovering again that black slot in the cliff no chart admitted existed.

The Meridian Star.

Quentin had not slept for thinking about it. Not about the gold—though the gold was there, folded into the arithmetic like a banknote pressed between pages. What kept them staring at the cabin ceiling was the registration number they'd read by flashlight on corroded aluminum: half-eaten, but legible. CF-MSR. A Mark IV transport, twin-engine, high-wing, the workhorse their father had flown until the day he didn't come home. The same airframe type. The same paint scheme, what was left of it. Seeing it had been like opening a door in their own chest and finding a room they'd boarded shut years ago, still furnished, still waiting.

*You don't have to go down there,* Quentin told themselves, the way they'd been telling themselves things for seven years. *You could log the coordinates, sell them clean, be gone before anyone knows.*

But the tide was going out, and the plane was fueled, and the sea was giving something back whether Quentin wanted it or not.

They got up and lit the lamp and began checking gear with the ritual precision that had replaced prayer: mask, fins, weight belt, the old dive knife with the bone handle, the lift bags rolled tight as sleeping snakes. Two tanks, filled yesterday at the dock compressor while Wren watched over their shoulder and asked questions in the relentless way teenagers ask—*how deep, how cold, how long can you stay down, what happens if you run out*—and Quentin had answered each one flatly, professionally, refusing to hear the other question underneath all of them, which was *take me with you.*

"You're staying on the surface," Quentin had said. "That's final."

Wren had grinned the way Wren grinned at everything, which was to say like the world was a hangar door waiting to be slid open. "Sure," they'd said, in the tone of someone agreeing to nothing.

Now, in the grey before dawn, Quentin loaded the skiff alone. The water in the bay was the color of hammered pewter, breathing slowly. Mist sat on it in ragged layers. Somewhere out in the fog a loon called, that ancient wavering note that sounds less like a bird than like the coast remembering something sad.

Quentin did not think about the fact that they'd told no one where they were going. They were very good at not thinking about things. It was, in fact, the one skill they had practiced daily for seven years.

* * *

The cave mouth at low water was bigger than it had looked from the air—a black arch maybe four meters across, its lip fanged with barnacled rock, exhaling a smell of cold stone and old salt and, faintly, impossibly, avgas gone to varnish. Inside, the passage rose steeply after the first few meters into an air pocket, so that a diver could slip under the lip and surface inside the mountain itself. Quentin had mapped it yesterday from the skiff with a lead line and patience, and the chart they'd drawn in their notebook showed a chamber beyond the throat: a flooded cathedral where the tail section of the Meridian Star lay canted against a rock shelf, its fuselage running down into deeper dark toward the bow.

The plan was simple. Simple plans were the only kind worth having. Dive the throat at slack water—the brief stillness between tides when the current through the gap went quiet. Locate the mail compartment aft of the wing root. Rig one lift bag to test the rigging. Surface. Come back tomorrow with more hands, more bags, more sense.

Slack water was at 6:40. Quentin anchored the skiff off the mouth at 6:15 and sat watching the water in the throat, which boiled and eddied even now, white threads spinning off the barnacles like the rock was unraveling.

"Twenty minutes," Quentin said aloud, to hear a human voice in all that grey. It came out smaller than intended.

At 6:38 the boil began to smooth. At 6:41 it was glass, or near enough—dark water standing in the archway like a pupil. Quentin pulled on the wetsuit in the skiff, cursing the cold neoprene, spat in the mask and rinsed it, checked the gauge twice, three times. 2200 psi. Regulator breathed dry on the test. Knife. Light clipped to the harness. Weight belt buckled.

The last thing they saw before rolling backward off the gunwale was the sky, low and pearl-colored through the mist, and the silhouette of the headland, and—far up the ridge, a speck of movement they took for a gull and forgot immediately.

Then the sea closed overhead and the world became green silence and the sound of their own breathing, which was the sound Quentin knew best in all the world: in, out, in, out. Alone is a place you can breathe.

The throat was colder than expected. Cold climbed the arms and settled in the shoulders like a passenger. Quentin swam the passage by feel as much as sight, one hand trailing the wall, the light beam swallowed after six feet by suspended silt that hung in the water like breath held too long. Down and through, counting kicks, and then the passage opened and Quentin rose into the air pocket.

It was vast. The light beam swept across a ceiling of wet stone ribbed like the inside of a shell, and the echo of their own regulator exhaust came back doubled, tripled, a congregation of ghosts answering. The air smelled of mineral and deep time. When Quentin pulled the mouthpiece and spoke—"hello"—the word flew out across the black water and came back changed, older.

Below, the wreck waited. Quentin could see it even without the light: a pale suggestion of structure down the slope, the tail fin rising like a grave marker.

They went back under.

* * *

Later—in the hospital, in the weeks after, in every retelling—Quentin would try to reconstruct exactly how it went wrong, and would always arrive at the same answer: everything, slightly, everywhere, at once.

The silt was the beginning of it. In the open sea, silt drifts; in a cave, it hangs, and worse, it waits. Quentin's fin tip brushed the slope near the tail section—not hard, barely a touch—and thirty years of settled sediment lifted off the bottom in a single silent bloom, and the world went from green to brown to nothing. Absolute nothing. The light beam, which should have cut through anything, lit only a wall of swirling particulate inches from the lens, like shining a lamp into smoke, into milk, into blindness.

Quentin stopped. Breathed. Felt for the guideline—

There was no guideline. There was supposed to be a guideline; they had written *RUN LINE* in the notebook in their own square pilot's handwriting, because Quentin Coldharbour did not enter overhead environments without one, ever, not once in seven years of careful solitary competence. But the reel was sitting in the skiff. They had watched themselves pack the gear last night and had somehow, in the fog of a sleepless night spent thinking about a registration number, stepped over it.

Panic does not announce itself. It arrives as arithmetic. *Air: 1900 psi. Visibility: zero. Exit: unknown bearing. Current: slack, but slack dies.* Quentin breathed and the numbers got worse and the walls of the calculation began closing, and somewhere beneath the arithmetic a much older voice started speaking, a voice from a childhood storm cellar, from a night of sirens, saying *you are buried, you are buried, you are buried—*

They moved. That was the mistake. Blind, heart slamming, they swam—and swimming blind in silt means swimming without reference, and without reference the body lies to you. The inner ear insists you're level when you're inverted, ascending when you're descending. Quentin swam hard for what felt like the exit and was, in truth, deeper into the chamber, down along the fuselage, and when the gauntlet of the wreck's torn aluminum caught the tank strap and held—when Quentin pulled and it held harder, and the mask flooded with cold, and the regulator hose snagged and tugged—they felt the panic finally break its banks and come through.

They would remember fragments. Kicking. Tearing. The gauge showing numbers that fell too fast, 800, 600, the needle trembling like a pulse. Pulling the backup regulator and getting a mouthful of silt-water that tasted of iron and rot and decades. The light beam strobing wildly, illuminating nothing, illuminating the tail wheel of the Meridian Star turning slowly past like a moon—so close, so terribly close, close enough to touch the aircraft their father had flown, and unable to reach anything else.

And then, at 400 psi, with the darkness pressing in like deep water pressing on a hull, Quentin thought—with perfect clarity, with the strange calm that comes at the exact center of drowning—*this is it, then. This is the room I've been flying toward for seven years. All those miles, and the destination was here, ten meters underwater, alone.*

The body disagreed.

That was the thing Quentin would carry out of the cave along with their life: the body, given no vote for seven years, voted. Every muscle tore against the entanglement at once. The hands found the snagged strap and ripped it free with strength that had no origin in decision. The legs drove upward through blackness. The lungs burned and the vision sparked and the body clawed for the surface of the air pocket with the dumb, magnificent, animal insistence of a thing that wants to live even when its owner hasn't decided to.

Quentin broke into the pocket air coughing, gagging, sucking a breath that tasted of stone and survival, and heard—impossibly, absurdly—the echo of their own gasp multiplied into a crowd, and thought for one lucid second: *even my drowning has witnesses.*

Then the arm came out of the dark and hauled them onto rock.

* * *

Hands. Small hands, strong, shaking. A headlamp beam swinging wild. A voice cracking on the edge of tears and fury at once.

"Breathe—just breathe—you're okay, you're okay, oh god, you're okay—"

Quentin was on the sloping shelf of stone at the pocket's edge, on hands and knees, vomiting seawater and silt, and Wren was there—Wren, sixteen years old, soaked to the collar, kneeling in the dark of a sea cave they had absolutely been ordered never to enter, holding Quentin's harness with both fists like the rock might take them back.

"How," Quentin rasped. It was all they could manage.

"I followed you." Wren's voice was raw. "From the camp. I saw your skiff leave and I—I paddled the kayak, I've been sitting outside the mouth since before dawn, I wasn't going to come in, I swear, I was just going to watch, and then I heard—" Their voice broke. "I heard the tank. Your spare bottle, banging on the rocks inside. And then I heard nothing."

"You dove in." Quentin stared at them. "On one breath. Through the throat. In the dark."

"You weren't coming out." Wren said it simply, wiping their face with a sleeve already drenched, leaving streaks of cave mud. "What was I supposed to do?"

Every answer Quentin owned to that question—*mind your own business, I don't need anyone, I fly alone*—dissolved somewhere in their chest before reaching their mouth. Because the true answer was lying on the stone in front of them, shivering and alive: *the body wants to live, and apparently so do I, and neither of them consulted me.*

They sat against the cave wall while the silt below slowly settled, and Wren produced, from the waterproof bag they'd somehow carried through a submerged passage on a single lungful, a thermos of sweet tea, because of course they had, because Wren prepared for everything except permission. Quentin drank it with hands that would not stop trembling, and hated the trembling, and could not stop it, and understood suddenly that this was what gratitude felt like when it arrived uninvited: like humiliation with warmth inside it.

"I'm going to be sick," Quentin said, and was, and Wren held their shoulder through it without comment, the way family does, the way nobody had done for Quentin in seven years.

Above them, through some fissure in the rock, the first real light of morning came down in a thin grey column, touching the black water, touching the pale shape of the tail fin below, touching the two of them sitting side by side on the shelf like survivors of a country that no longer existed.

"The current turns in forty minutes," Wren said quietly. "We should get out while the throat's still calm."

Quentin looked at them—at this kid who had disobeyed every instruction, who had held their life in two small hands, who had more courage in their stubborn teenage body than Quentin had managed in seven careful years—and heard themselves say the sentence they'd sworn never to say:

"Help me up."

* * *

The paddle back across the bay was made in full daylight, the mist burning off, the water gone soft and silver. Neither spoke much. Wren towed the skiff behind the kayak with Quentin's gear stowed and Quentin's pride stowed deeper, and the Beaver sat at her mooring growing small behind them, and ahead the town woke along the shore—smoke from chimneys, the thump of the fish plant, Ede Okonkwo's lighthouse standing white against the brightening east.

Quentin watched it approach and felt something shift in the ballast of the chest. Not peace. Nothing so finished as peace. More like a course correction—a degree or two of heading, barely visible on any instrument, that over a thousand miles becomes another country entirely.

At the dock, hands helped them up the ladder, and Wren's voice, steadier now, said what Quentin was already dreading and already knowing was inevitable:

"So. Tide Three. You're not doing that alone again."

Quentin opened their mouth to refuse—and found the refusal wouldn't come, drowned somewhere back in that black chamber alongside the old self who'd tried to die politely in the dark.

"We'll need more than a kayak," was all they said.

Wren smiled like a hangar door sliding open. "I know exactly who to call."
