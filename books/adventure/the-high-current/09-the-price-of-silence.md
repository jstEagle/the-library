## 9. The Price of Silence

Voss had a rule about negotiations: never hold them in a place you didn't own. Not legally—ownership was a paperwork fiction, and paperwork could be bought—but in the deeper sense. A man was easiest to move when he sat on unfamiliar ground, among unfamiliar things, breathing air that already belonged to someone else.

So he chose the *Corvid's Rest*, the salvage fleet's lead vessel, anchored two bays south of the lighthouse, and he sent the invitation by hand: a card, cream stock, his name printed in small grey type, no title, no company letterhead. *Mr. Coldharbour. Drinks at eight. I believe we have business.* That was all. Men like Quentin Coldharbour did not respond well to more than that. More than that smelled like a trap, and bush pilots were animals that lived by their noses.

Voss dressed carefully, which is to say expensively but quietly—a charcoal sweater under an oilskin jacket, nothing that gleamed. He had learned long ago that gold on a man read as threat; wool read as patience. He wanted to be read as patient. He poured two fingers of the Talisker into the decanter in the saloon and set it where the lamplight would catch its amber, because good whiskey was a kind of argument all by itself. It said: *I am not a man who cuts corners. I am a man who pays for quality. Imagine what else I pay for.*

At seven minutes past eight, boots sounded on the deck plates—unhurried, slightly uneven, the gait of someone who lived in one plane and walked like the ground might not hold. The door opened and there he was. Quentin Coldharbour. Younger than Voss had expected, or perhaps just worn differently; the face had the weathered flatness of someone who had spent years looking into distance rather than at people. Eyes the colour of slate water. Hands that stayed near pockets.

"Mr. Coldharbour." Voss gestured to the chair across from him. "Thank you for coming."

"You said you'd pay for my fuel if I came," Quentin said. "I came for the fuel."

"I said I'd *consider* it." Voss smiled and let the smile do its small work. "Sit. Please."

Quentin sat the way cats sit—front half committed, back half ready to leave. Voss poured without asking, slid the glass across the mahogany, and watched the pilot look at it without touching it. Good. Suspicion was a form of respect. He had no use for men who drank what strangers poured.

"Do you know what this boat cost me?" Voss asked, resting a hand on the table's edge. "Not the figure. The figure's vulgar. What I mean is: she cost me eleven years. Eleven years of bad weather, bad partners, worse ports. Every rivet in her is a decision I made when quitting would have been cheaper." He turned his glass a quarter-turn. "I mention this only so you understand I'm not a hobbyist. I don't chase wrecks for romance. Romance sinks ships. I chase them because they're assets, and assets are honest. They sit where they sit, they're worth what they're worth, and they don't lie about wanting something from you."

"And the letters?" Quentin said. "Are those assets too?"

Voss let a beat pass, unhurried. So the pilot knew about the mail sacks. Ede Okonkwo's ledger, then, or the old woman's talk. It hardly mattered. Information on this coast moved like fog—everywhere at once, impossible to trace.

"Paper burns," Voss said mildly. "Metal doesn't. There's a phrase in my trade: *high-value density*. Gold is dense. Instruments are dense. Paper is—" he made a small gesture, a wave going out with the tide, "—buoyant, eventually. I'm told there are families who'd want them. Families are not my department."

"They're mine," Quentin said, and Voss noted the flatness of it, the way a man states a fact he hasn't fully agreed to yet.

"Then we may have less to negotiate than I feared." He opened the leather folio beside his glass—not a contract, not yet, just a single sheet with figures in his own handwriting, because printed numbers looked like other people's numbers and handwritten ones looked like a confidence. "Here's the shape of it. You give me the coordinates. Accurate to within fifty metres—that's all I need; my side-scan does the rest. In exchange: exclusive recovery rights, which I'll document through channels that make the claim unassailable, and a finder's fee structured so that no one—no tax authority, no creditor, no *person*—ever needs to know your name was attached to it."

He watched Quentin's eyes go to the figures. Watched the pupils widen a fraction, then narrow, the whole involuntary arithmetic happening behind them. The number was large. Voss had calibrated it large. Not absurd—absurdity insulted—but large enough that a man who had been running for seven years would see, in it, a door.

"That's enough money," Voss said quietly, "to disappear properly. Not the way you've been disappearing. I've done some reading, Mr. Coldharbour. Seven years, four names—I'm guessing at the count, correct me if I'm wrong—three provinces, a string of strips nobody flies to twice. That's not disappearing. That's *loitering*. Disappearing costs money. Real disappearing means a boat in the South Pacific, or a strip in Patagonia where the nearest neighbour is a hundred miles and doesn't speak your language. It means never checking over your shoulder again, because there's nothing left behind worth turning around for." He leaned back. "You've been paying for half a disappearance for seven years. I'm offering to fund the rest of it. In full. Tonight."

The lamp hissed faintly. Somewhere above them, the deck creaked as the tide turned, and both of them heard it—the coast keeping its own time, indifferent to the conversation beneath.

Quentin picked up the glass at last. Held it. Did not drink.

"Why?" the pilot said. "You could find the cave yourself. You've got the sonar, the divers, the barge. Why buy coordinates from me?"

"Because time is the one asset I can't salvage." Voss spoke plainly now; the performance had served its purpose, and he respected Coldharbour enough to drop it. "My survey says the cave floods fast and drains faster. My barge draws too much water to approach before tide six, maybe five if the weather holds, and the weather will not hold. You, however, know the cave. You've been inside it. You know the currents in that throat of rock better than any diver I could hire in a week. If I take the cave by force—if I simply anchor over it and start pulling metal—you'll fight me. You'll radio the fisheries office, the historical society, whoever writes angry letters in this part of the world. I'd still win, but I'd win slowly, and slow is expensive." He tapped the folio once. "This way, everyone wins quickly. You get your ocean. I get my wreck. The town keeps its stories, which cost nothing and were never going to feed anyone."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I say thank you for the whiskey, and I start planning around you." Voss held his gaze, mild as milk. "I don't threaten, Mr. Coldharbour. Threats are for men with short ledgers. I schedule. And my schedule has room in it for either version of the next six days. Only one of them ends with you rich."

Quentin set the glass down, untouched, and stood. For a moment the two of them regarded each other across the lamplit table—one man who had spent his life buying other people's pasts, and one man who had spent his trying to outrun his own—and Voss thought, with the detached interest he brought to all his acquisitions, *he wants to say yes. He's already halfway gone. He just hasn't found the door handle.*

"I'll think about it," Quentin said.

"Of course." Voss rose too, extended his hand; after a pause, the pilot took it, brief and dry. "There's no deadline. Well." A thin smile. "There's always a deadline. But I won't pretend it's shorter than it is. Tide six. Think of it as a horizon, not a wall."

Quentin nodded once and went out. Voss listened to the footsteps cross the deck, fade down the ladder, and become the sound of an outboard coughing awake in the dark. Then he collected the untouched glass, poured its contents back into the decanter—waste offended him—and stood at the saloon window watching the single running light move north along the black water.

He took out his notebook and wrote, in his small precise hand: *C. — tempted. Will not decide alone. Something holds him here. Find what it is.*

He underlined the last sentence twice, closed the book, and went up to check the tide tables.

* * *

The flight back was eleven minutes and Quentin flew none of them honestly—hands on the stick, eyes on the water, mind somewhere in a leather folio on a mahogany table.

*Enough money to disappear properly.*

That was the poison of it. Not the number—though the number was real, Voss wasn't a bluffing kind of man, that much had been clear—but the *shape* of the thing being bought. Seven years Quentin had been running on fumes of it: enough fuel to reach the next strip, enough cash to reach the next month, enough distance to make the last name unusable. Half a disappearance, Voss had called it, and the words had landed like a hook finding cartilage. Because it was true. Every year the past got heavier and the money got thinner and the strips got lonelier, and somewhere in the back of the skull a voice kept saying *one big score, one clean break, and then you're free*—and here was a stranger in a charcoal sweater holding out exactly that, gift-wrapped, no violence required.

Just coordinates. Just a signature. Just the Meridian Star, sold by the pound.

Quentin brought the plane down on the dark water off the town dock harder than intended, the hull slapping, and sat for a moment after shutdown with hands loose in their lap, listening to the swells knock against the pontoons like something asking to be let in.

The dock lights were on. That was wrong—it was nearly ten, and the dock lights went off at nine since the fish plant cut its shifts—and sure enough, Wren was sitting on a mooring cleat with a thermos, swinging one leg, wearing the expression of a person who had been waiting long enough to compose several speeches.

"You met him," Wren said, before Quentin's boots even touched the planks.

"How—"

"The *Corvid's Rest* put her launch in at six. Marta saw it from the cannery window. Whole town knows Voss asked you for a drink." Wren hopped off the cleat, thermos swinging. "So? What'd he offer?"

"Nothing. Whiskey."

"You didn't drink it."

"How do you know I didn't drink it?"

"Because you're walking straight," Wren said, "and because you look like somebody handed you a live wire and told you it was a jump rope." The kid fell into step beside Quentin along the dock, and for once didn't fill the silence, which was somehow worse. At the top of the ramp, where the path split toward the hangar and toward Ede's cottage light burning yellow above the tideline, Wren stopped. "Whatever he offered you—whatever number—he's lying about one part of it. There's always one part."

"There is," Quentin admitted. "But it isn't the part you'd guess."

"What part?"

Quentin looked out at the bay, at the black seam where the cave lay under forty feet of cold water, holding a plane, and mail sacks, and a logbook with handwriting Quentin hadn't seen in seven years and could still draw from memory.

"He's not lying about the money," Quentin said. "That's the worst part. The money's real. Everything else is the lie." A breath. "Go home, Wren. It's late."

"It's not late, it's barely—"

"*Home,*" Quentin said, gentler than intended, and Wren went, glancing back twice.

Quentin did not go to the hangar. Quentin walked the other way, up the path to the lighthouse, because there was a question that had been forming since the saloon, and it wasn't about coordinates or fees or tides. It was simpler and much heavier: *what was in that cockpit besides instruments?* Voss had talked about high-value density, about paper burning, about families not being his department—and every word of it had slid off Quentin like rain off waxed canvas, because underneath the fear and the temptation ran a colder current: the last entry in a father's logbook might be a line with Quentin's name in it. An apology. An explanation. A curse. Anything. Everything.

Ede Okonkwo answered the door before the second knock, as though she'd been standing behind it for hours, and looked at Quentin the way lighthouse keepers look at weather.

"He made his offer," she said. It wasn't a question.

"This morning the whole town knows about the launch. Now you too. Does anything stay secret on this coast?"

"Nothing," Ede said, stepping aside to let Quentin in. "That's the point of a coast. Water carries everything eventually. Best learn to decide what you want carried." She poured tea without asking, set the cup down, and folded her hands. "Now. Tell me what he wrote on that piece of paper. All of it. And then tell me what you're thinking of doing—because I've watched you fly out of here for seven years, Quentin Coldharbour, and I have never once seen you land. Before you sign anything, I'd like to know whether you finally intend to."

Outside, far down the bay, the lights of the salvage fleet burned in a patient row, and the tide began its long, unhurried climb toward the cave mouth—tide three of six, and counting.
