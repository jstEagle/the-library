## 10. Hangar Nights

The hangar smelled the way Wren imagined old churches must smell—oil and dust and something older underneath, something patient. She'd been sneaking into it since she was eleven, back when Quentin Coldharbour still locked it and she still had to come in through the gap where the weather stripping had rotted off the north door. Now the door stood open half the evening because Ede had decided it should, and because nobody, apparently, argued with Ede.

Wren sat cross-legged on a crate of aircraft bolts that rattled every time she shifted, holding a rivet gun she wasn't supposed to be holding, watching Quentin work.

Quentin worked the way water worked. No wasted motion. The pontoon patch went on in slow, deliberate passes, sealant laid like mortar, each rivet set with a tap-tap-tap that echoed off the corrugated roof and came back down softened, almost gentle. Outside, the light was going the color of weak tea. Gulls argued over something on the breakwater. Somewhere down the dock, the *Kestrel's* engine turned over once, twice, and died again—old Marta Okafor's boat being stubborn about bedtime.

"You're staring," Quentin said, without looking up.

"I'm learning."

"By staring."

"It's how I learned everything else." Wren swung her feet. "Nobody on this coast teaches you anything official. You watch. You steal time with machines when their owners aren't looking. You break things quietly and put them back before anyone notices."

"That's not learning. That's burglary."

"Burglary with a maintenance schedule."

Something happened at the corner of Quentin's mouth. It wasn't quite a smile—it never was—but Wren had been cataloguing those near-smiles for two weeks now, the way other people collected stamps or sea glass, and she was fairly sure this one counted. Number nine. Maybe ten. She kept the ledger in her head the way Ede kept hers: dates, conditions, what the tide had been doing.

Quentin set the rivet gun down and flexed their fingers, and for a moment they just looked at the plane—the *Meridian* work had left the old bush plane looking like a quilt, new aluminum bright against panels gone dull as pewter—and Wren watched them look at it, and understood without being told that Quentin was seeing something else entirely. A different plane. A different sky. Seven years ago, maybe, or seven days.

"My grandmother used to say," Wren offered, because silence in this hangar had started to feel less like privacy and more like a hole in the floor, "that the coast keeps its history in people, not books. Books burn. People just forget slowly, which is nearly the same thing but takes longer."

"Mm."

"She said if you wanted to know what really happened to the *Alder Queen*—the packet boat that went down off Sable Neck in '61—you don't go to the county archive. You go sit with Old Tomas Ferreira until he decides to tell you. Took him four visits. She brought bread the first three."

"What changed his mind?"

"The fourth visit she brought nothing. Just sat there. He said—" Wren pitched her voice low, doing the gravelly old-man impression she'd practiced in the mirror, "—'Girl, you brought me bread like I'm a story you're buying. Come back when you want to listen instead.'"

Quentin did look up then. The near-smile flickered and died, replaced by something more careful. "And did she?"

"Did she what?"

"Listen instead."

"For six hours." Wren grinned. "She said he talked about the *Alder Queen*, then about his brother, then about a woman in Port Anselm, then about the war, then about herring. By the end she knew everything about herring and nothing about the woman in Port Anselm, and she considered it a fair trade."

This time the smile arrived properly. Small, crooked, gone fast—but real. Eleven, Wren decided. Definitely eleven.

* * *

Ede arrived at seven, the way she'd arrived at seven the night before and the night before that, carrying a basket covered with a cloth that meant food, because a basket covered with a cloth always meant food, and because Ede Okonkwo had apparently decided, somewhere in the last fortnight, that two people living out of a hangar and a lighthouse respectively were not eating properly, and that this was a problem within her jurisdiction.

She didn't ask. That was the thing Wren kept noticing. Ede didn't ask if they wanted dinner, didn't ask what they liked, didn't ask permission to take over the scarred workbench at the back of the hangar, sweep it clean with three strokes of her forearm, and begin unpacking. She simply cooked for three, the way the tide simply came in, and if either of them had objected she would have looked at them with those flat, patient eyes and said nothing at all, which would have been worse than any argument.

"There's a pot under the loft stairs," she said now, by way of greeting. "Bring it. And the blue enamel one, if it hasn't grown legs."

It hadn't. Wren fetched both, and watched Ede build a stew out of things that should not have made a stew—salt fish, a heel of cabbage, potatoes going soft at one end, a tin of tomatoes dented like it had survived a war—and turn them into something that made the whole hangar smell like somebody's childhood. Not Wren's childhood; her childhood had been gas-station noodles and her mother's boyfriend's cigarettes. But somebody's. Somebody somewhere had a childhood that smelled like this, and standing in the warm edge of it, Wren felt a homesickness for a home she'd never had.

"Your grandmother," Ede said, stirring, not looking at Wren, "was Adaeze Ferreira's girl, yes? Before she married into your line."

"Tomas Ferreira was my great-uncle, actually. The herring one."

"Hm." Ede's mouth did something complicated. "Then you have listening in your blood. Good. It skips generations in most families."

Quentin had stopped working. They were standing very still by the pontoon, wrench in hand, wearing the expression of a person who has walked into a room and realized everyone in it knows something they don't. Wren had learned to read that expression too. It was becoming one of her favorites, mostly because it appeared so often.

"Eat first," Ede said, to no one and everyone. "Talk after. Stew doesn't wait and neither do I."

They ate sitting on crates and an upturned dinghy, bowls balanced on knees, steam rising into the rafters where two barn owls had taken up residence despite everyone's objections. The owls objected to nothing, actually. They just blinked down at the humans with the serene contempt of landlords.

"This is good," Quentin said. It came out rough, like a word in a language they were relearning.

"I know," said Ede.

Wren laughed into her bowl, and after a moment—she heard it clearly, unmistakably—so did Quentin. One short breath of a laugh, startled out of them like a bird flushed from cover. Wren filed it away. First laugh. She'd remember the date.

* * *

Afterward, Ede washed the pots herself, refusing help with a look that could have moored a freighter, and Quentin went back to the fuselage seam they'd been chasing all week—a hairline crack above the cargo bay, the kind of thing that wouldn't ground a plane anywhere with a proper mechanic but would absolutely ground it here, forty miles from anything with a proper mechanic. Wren handed tools. Quentin drove rivets. The lamp threw their shadows huge against the wall, two shapes bent over the same small work.

"Tell me the one about the light," Wren said eventually. "The night it burned."

She'd asked before, weeks ago, back when asking Quentin Coldharbour for anything felt like reaching into a trap. Back then the question had bounced off. Tonight, with stew in them and the day's work nearly done, Quentin's hands slowed on the rivet gun.

"You've heard it," they said. "Everyone here has heard it."

"Not from someone who was there."

"I wasn't there."

"You flew supply runs through that storm. Marta says you brought insulin in over the Neck with the wind reading sixty knots."

"Marta talks too much."

"Marta says you landed on the road."

"The road was there. I used it." Quentin set the gun down. Rolled their shoulders. Wren held her breath and waited, the way you wait at a seal haul-out, motionless, letting the animal decide. "It wasn't the fire that was the story," Quentin said at last. "Fires happen. It was the chain. Lighthouse keeper's sick, can't climb, so the fishing fleet forms up offshore and flashes their running lights in sequence so the ships coming down the strait see a broken pattern and know something's wrong. Forty boats. Nobody organized it. Nobody decided. Marta just started flashing and everybody else joined in, and by midnight there's this—" Their hand moved, tracing it in the air. "This stuttering constellation where a lighthouse used to be. And the freighter saw it. Turned out to sea. That's the whole story. Forty people did a thing without being asked, and it worked, and nobody ever got a medal or even thanked each other properly. They just went back to fishing in the morning."

Wren was quiet for a moment. "That's why you stayed, isn't it. After. Even though you keep saying you're leaving."

Quentin's head came around sharply. For a second Wren thought she'd overreached, broken some rule she hadn't known existed. But Quentin only looked at her—really looked, the way you look at a chart when the coastline has changed since the last survey—and said, "You're sixteen. How do you see things like that?"

"I live here. We keep our history in people, remember? I'm collecting."

"Collecting what?"

"Everything." Wren shrugged, suddenly self-conscious, fiddling with the rivet gun's safety. "Everyone's stories. Before they forget them. Tomas is eighty-three. Marta's seventy-six. Half the names in Ede's ledger are already just names. Somebody should write it down while the mouths that know it are still talking." She risked a glance up. "Somebody should fly it places, too. Stories travel faster than planes, my gran said, but planes carry more."

The hangar was very quiet. The owls shifted overhead. Out past the open doors, the tide was making, a long silver whisper against the shingle, and the first stars were pricking through over the water.

"Hand me the sealant," Quentin said finally, voice not quite steady, and Wren understood that the conversation was over, but also that it hadn't been refused—that it had been *received*, logged, entered into whatever ledger Quentin kept behind their eyes. She passed the tube. Their fingers brushed. Neither of them mentioned it.

They worked another hour. At some point Ede came back from wherever she'd gone—checking her light, she said, though the light had been automated for twenty years—and sat on the dinghy with her hands folded, watching them work, and began, unasked, to tell the story of the winter the harbor froze and the mail came in by dogsled and her father traded a barrel of diesel for a piano. Nobody had asked for the story. Everybody listened anyway. That was how it worked here, Wren thought. That was the whole mechanism. Things given without asking, received without demanding, the whole coast running on a currency nobody printed and everybody honored.

By ten the crack was patched, sealed, curing under a heat lamp rigged from a car battery. By ten-thirty Ede had gone, taking her empty basket and leaving behind a jar of something pickled and a stern instruction about eating breakfast. By eleven it was just Wren and Quentin and the plane and the sound of the tide.

Wren unrolled her sleeping bag in the loft—she'd stopped pretending she was going home at night; her mother was three towns away and glad of it, and everyone in town had silently agreed to consider the hangar arrangement temporary in the way that permanent things are temporary—and lay listening to Quentin below, moving through the shutdown ritual: fuel caps checked, pitot cover on, master off, keys in the coffee can. The same sequence every night. A liturgy. She'd memorized it the way she memorized everything.

Then footsteps on the ladder, and Quentin's face appeared over the loft edge, lamplit from below.

"Wren."

"Yeah?"

A pause. In the pause, Wren heard the tide, and a dog barking twice down in the village, and the creak of the hangar doors breathing in the night wind.

"When we get the mail sacks out," Quentin said, "the letters. When they're distributed." Another pause, longer. "I'll need someone who knows whose family is whose. Someone who's been collecting."

Wren's heart did something complicated and enormous. She kept her voice level, mostly. "I'm your girl."

"Good." Quentin nodded once, and the face withdrew, and the lamp clicked off below.

Wren lay in the dark grinning at the rafters like an idiot, and might have fallen straight asleep if she hadn't heard, a few minutes later, the north door slide open—the one with the rotten weather stripping, the one she'd used for years—and soft footsteps cross the concrete, and stop.

Not Quentin's footsteps. Quentin was in the loft stairwell; she could hear them breathing.

Someone else was in the hangar.

Wren sat up without a sound. Below, in the thin wash of moonlight through the doorway, a figure stood by the plane—not moving toward it, just standing, close enough to touch the fresh patch, one hand raised slightly, palm out, the way you'd approach a horse. And as her eyes adjusted, Wren caught the shape of him: broad, stooped, work jacket, rubber boots.

Dock lights. Dock boots. Dele, the dockhand who'd been helping load their supplies all week, standing alone in the dark beside the plane, his hand hovering an inch off the aluminum, not touching, trembling.

He whispered something. Too low to catch. Then he pulled his hand back like it had been burned, turned, and walked out into the night, and the door slid shut behind him, and Wren lay back down with her heart hammering, telling herself it was nothing, telling herself it was grief, telling herself it was a man visiting a wreck the way people visit graves—

—and finding, underneath all of it, a cold little stone of doubt that would not dissolve: *why does a dockhand cry over a plane he's never flown?*
