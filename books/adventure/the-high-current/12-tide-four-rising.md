## 12. Tide Four, Rising

The fog came in off the water at dawn like a held breath, and Quentin stood on the dock at Little Kestrel watching it swallow the bay whole.

Three days since Tide Two. Three days since Wren had hauled them half-drowned out of the current, and Quentin's ribs still ached where the rocks had caught them, a deep bruised throb that flared every time they reached for a coffee mug or turned the yoke too hard. The body kept its own ledger, Ede had said, pressing a poultice of something bitter-smelling into their side. It remembers what you'd rather forget.

Quentin had slept badly. That was nothing new—sleep had been a country they only visited, never settled in, for seven years—but the dreams had changed. No longer the falling, the fire, the argument that replayed in their skull like a skipping record. Now it was the cave. The green-black water. The tail of the Meridian Star rising out of the silt like a hand reaching up through a grave.

And now, standing on the dock with the fog thick as wool, Quentin felt it again—the prickle at the base of the neck, the animal certainty of being watched.

The bay was empty. That was the trouble. It was too empty.

The *Kestrel* fleet should have been out by now. Marta Osei's crab boat, the two skiffs the Halvorsen boys ran, the old gillnetter that belonged to nobody in particular and everybody in general. On a fog morning like this they'd be running slow, running sound, the whole bay a conversation of engines and bells. Instead there was nothing but the water lapping at the pilings and somewhere far off a loon calling its one cracked note.

Then Quentin saw it. Or rather, heard it.

An engine. Not the loose, familiar stutter of a local two-stroke but something smoother, heavier, a diesel with money behind it. It came threading through the fog from the south, and a moment later a boat resolved out of the grey: a hard-chined sport fisher, thirty feet of white gelcoat and polished stainless, the kind of boat that cost more than the entire Kestrel dock. Two men stood in the cockpit. One of them held a rod. Neither of them was fishing.

Quentin watched them run the channel—too fast for the fog, too fast for the crab pots that everyone in Kestrel knew were strung across the eastern shallows, and they missed every one of them. They missed them the way you miss things when you already know exactly where they are.

*They've charted this bay,* Quentin thought. *They've charted it from somewhere else.*

The boat slowed off the point below the old cannery, and the second man did something with his hands at the console—something small and deliberate—and Quentin's stomach dropped through the dock planks into the cold water below. Because Quentin knew that gesture. You made that gesture when you were watching a screen, when you were reading numbers off a display, when the water in front of you wasn't water anymore but a profile, a contour, a shape with a name and a depth and a value.

A depth sounder. A side-scan, maybe, or something better. Running the bottom of the bay like a man reading a letter addressed to someone else.

Quentin turned and walked, then almost ran, up the dock ramp, past the fuel shed, up the gravel switchback toward the hangar, and the fog closed behind them like a door.

* * *

Wren was already at the hangar when Quentin burst in, cross-legged on the concrete with the Cessna's port wheel spat open around them, greased to both elbows, a wrench in one hand and half a sandwich in the other. The kid had appointed themself chief mechanic sometime around Tide Three and nobody had had the heart or the sense to stop them.

"Your brake line's weeping," Wren said, not looking up. "I'm bleeding it. Also your left magneto's—"

"Wren."

The kid looked up. Whatever they saw in Quentin's face made them set down the wrench.

"There's a boat in the bay," Quentin said. "White hull, twin diesels, sport fisher. Two men. They're not fishing."

Wren was on their feet before Quentin finished, sandwich abandoned. "Sounder?"

"Looked like it. They were parked off the cannery point, right over the—" Quentin stopped. Right over the approach channel to the cave. Right over the only water deep enough to hide a plane.

"Okay." Wren was already moving, grabbing the binoculars off the workbench, wiping the lenses on their shirt. "Okay, okay. The fog's burning off. We can see them, they can see the town, but they can't see the cave mouth from outside the point unless the tide's below two feet and it's not, it won't be till—" they checked the tide table pinned to the wall, a habit Ede had drilled into them, "—four this afternoon. Low slack at 4:12. We've got time."

"Time for what?"

"Time to not look like we know." Wren said it with a flatness that didn't belong on a sixteen-year-old face, and Quentin was reminded, not for the first time, that this kid had grown up in a town that survived by reading water. "If they're Voss's people, they're not here to dive today. They're here to confirm. So we let them confirm nothing. We go about our day. Loudly. Stupidly. We fish."

"I don't fish."

"Today you do." Wren shoved the binoculars at Quentin's chest. "Marta's skiff is tied at the north float and she owes me three favors. Get the skiff. I'll get the gear. And Quentin—" they paused, and for a second the bravado thinned and something older looked out through the kid's eyes, "—don't go out there alone. Please."

It was the *please* that did it. Quentin took the binoculars.

* * *

They came out of the fog together an hour later, Marta's skiff running slow and stupid in the way Wren had coached—engine loping, one rod dragging a spoon behind them on a line that had never touched a fish, two people in oilskins who had no business being on the water and every appearance of enjoying it.

The white boat was still there. It had moved, though. It was working a grid now, slow and methodical, back and forth off the point in legs half a mile long, and through the binoculars Quentin could read the name on the transom in flaking gold leaf: *MERIDIAN ROSE*.

The name went through Quentin like a fishhook.

"Subtle," Wren muttered, watching Quentin's face. "Real subtle."

"They're telling us they know."

"They're telling us they don't care if we know." Wren took the binoculars, studied the boat, and their jaw tightened. "The guy at the console. See him? He's not watching the screen anymore."

Quentin took the glasses back. The man had turned. He was facing the skiff. Even at three hundred yards, even through fog-thin sunlight, Quentin could feel the weight of his attention, patient and unhurried, the way you'd look at a piece of equipment you were deciding whether to buy or scrap.

Then the man raised one hand—not a wave, exactly. An acknowledgment. A *we see you too*. And beside him, the other man reached into the cockpit and lifted something into view. Held it up. Let it catch the light.

A camera. Long lens.

He held it for a long five-count, deliberate as a signature, and then set it down.

"They're documenting us," Quentin said. Their voice came out low and strange. "They're building a file."

"Then we give them a file worth nothing." Wren turned the skiff in a wide, lazy arc, exactly the movement of a bored kid showing off, and hollered across the water with theatrical misery: "HEY! YOU'RE IN OUR CRAB GROUNDS!"

The white boat did not answer. It turned one more leg of its grid, unhurried, and then, as the fog burned off the headland and the sun came through in ragged shafts, it turned south and ran—smooth, fast, expensive—back toward the bays where the salvage fleet lay at anchor.

Quentin watched it go and felt no relief at all. The water where it had been lay flat and innocent, and underneath that water, under forty feet of green cold, a plane full of letters waited, and now the men who wanted it had a map of the bottom drawn in their own instruments, and every hour that passed was an hour they were spending it.

* * *

Ede was waiting at the dock when they tied up. Quentin hadn't radioed her. Quentin hadn't told anyone. But there she stood at the head of the float in her oilskin coat, silver hair pinned back, and her face had the look of a woman who had read the weather in a room with no windows.

"The Osei girl saw the boat from the ridge," she said, before Quentin could speak. "Radioed the co-op. Half the town knows a survey boat ran the bay this morning." She looked at Quentin, then at Wren, then back. "How good were their instruments?"

"Good enough," Quentin said.

Ede nodded slowly, and Quentin watched her do the arithmetic—the same cold, patient arithmetic Voss would be doing, the same numbers, arriving at the same sum. "Then he knows the shape of the bay now, if not the cave. He'll need one more pass to find the mouth, and he'll want to make it on a low tide, because the mouth only opens below two feet." She turned and looked south, toward the headland, toward the grey line where the fleet waited. "Low slack today is 4:12. Tomorrow, 5:40. The day after, past seven. He'll come tomorrow evening."

"Or tonight," Quentin said.

"Or tonight." Ede's voice didn't waver, but her hands, folded at her waist, tightened on each other. "Quentin. The mail sacks. How much can you lift in one dive?"

"One. Maybe two, if the current's kind. It wasn't kind last time."

"Then two dives isn't enough, and ten dives is too many, and we have—" she glanced at the tide clock nailed to the co-op wall, its brass hand pointing toward the afternoon, "—one tide we can trust and one we can't."

Quentin stood on the dock and felt the old reflex rise in them like water in a bilge: *run*. Take the Cessna, take whatever you can carry, take Wren if the kid will come, take nothing else, vanish the way you've always vanished, let the town and the wreck and the whole drowned past go to the men with the white boats. It would be easy. It would be exactly what seven years of practice had trained them for.

But Wren was standing at the skiff's stern, coiling the line with careful hands, and Ede was looking at Quentin with seven years of patience in her face, and down the dock, Quentin could see Marta Osei walking toward them with her two grown sons, and behind them the Halvorsen boys, and behind them, strung along the shore road like beads on a wire, other figures coming—drawn not by radio calls or meetings but by the oldest network on the coast, the one that runs on worry and neighborliness and the sight of a stranger's boat where a stranger's boat should not be.

They were coming to Quentin. Not for answers—Quentin had none worth having. They were coming because it was their bay, and something was in it, and that was all the reason a coast ever needed.

"Tonight," Quentin said. "We go tonight, on the falling tide, before he's ready. All of us. Every boat, every light, every pair of hands." They heard their own voice and barely recognized it—no longer the flat, transactional voice of a pilot negotiating with the world, but something rougher, something that had been waiting seven years for permission to exist. "He wants to schedule this. So we unschedule it."

Ede's eyes were steady. "That's not a plan, Quentin. That's a gamble."

"It's a tide," Quentin said. "It's the only one we've got."

And out past the headland, far to the south, the fog was lifting off water that had already begun to fall.
