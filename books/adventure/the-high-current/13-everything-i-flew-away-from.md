## 13. Everything I Flew Away From

The storm came in off the water at dusk, and Quentin did not sleep.

They told themselves it was the wind against the hangar roof, the tin complaining in its old language, the rain ticking like a telegraph key nobody had learned to read. They told themselves it was the coffee at midnight, the third cup, the one they'd sworn off. But the truth sat in the corner of the hangar where the mail sacks now lay stacked under a tarp, damp and patient and smelling of iron and old paper, and the truth was this: the wreck had opened something in them that had been sealed for seven years, and what leaked out did not leak gently.

They sat on an oil drum with a flashlight between their boots and did not look at the sacks. Looking at them was like looking at a door you knew had a room behind it.

The rain thickened. Somewhere down the hill, Ede's lighthouse turned its white arm through the murk, once every seven seconds, the rhythm Quentin had fallen asleep to as a child without ever admitting it. Wren had gone home hours ago, protesting, promising to be back at first light with a borrowed compressor. The hangar was Quentin's again, wholly theirs, the way they had wanted it to be for seven years. And it had never felt so much like a cell.

They slept, finally, near dawn, and the dream took them where the waking mind would not go.

* * *

Seven years ago the coast had been in the grip of a September that would not end. The light came off the water gold and low, the kind of light that makes everything look like a memory even while it's happening, and Quentin Coldharbour, twenty-four years old and certain of everything, had come down the gravel path to the house above the tideline with their duffel over one shoulder and a ticket to the interior in their breast pocket.

Their father was in the yard, washing the Meridian Star's propeller blades with a rag gone grey from years of the same work. He washed that propeller the way other men said grace. He didn't look up when Quentin's boots crunched the shell path, but his shoulders changed—Quentin saw it, the small settling, a man bracing for weather he'd been forecasting for weeks.

"You're really going," Elias Coldharbour said. Not a question. He said it the way he read altimeter readings: flat, factual, already calculating the descent.

"There's a job. Charter work, up past the divide. Good money."

"Good money." He stood, knees cracking, and looked at Quentin with those eyes the color of the water over the shoals—grey, but with green in it if the light came right. "There's always good money somewhere that isn't here."

"That's not fair."

"No," he agreed, wiping his hands. "It's not. Fair's got nothing to do with it."

The quarrel, when Quentin tried to reconstruct it in the years after—and they had reconstructed it hundreds of times, in cockpits, in motel rooms, in the long white noise hours between radio fixes—never quite held its shape. It was less a fight than a structure collapsing, load-bearing walls coming down one after another. Quentin had said the coast was a place where people stayed because they were afraid to leave. Their father had said that leaving was just staying, somewhere else, with better weather for hiding. Quentin had said the words they could never take back—the words that lived now under their sternum like a swallowed hook: *You love this wreck of a coast more than you ever loved having a family worth staying for.*

Their father had gone very still. The rag hung from his hand. And then he had done the thing that Quentin had replayed more than any other moment of that day, more than the slammed door, more than the duffel thrown into the truck: he had nodded. Slowly. As if Quentin had confirmed something he'd long suspected about himself, and found the confirmation bearable, and that was the unbearable part.

"The Star flies the north run tomorrow," he'd said. "Weather window's short. I'll be back by dark the day after."

"Fine."

"You could come. One last run. You always loved the north run."

And Quentin—twenty-four, certain, already halfway gone in their own mind—had said, "I've got a plane to catch," and meant the other plane, and their father had nodded again, and washed the last blade of the propeller, and that was it. That was the whole of it. No raised voices at the end. Just a man asking his child to fly one more route beside him and a child too busy leaving to hear what the asking was made of.

The Meridian Star went down the following night, somewhere between the last light and the first buoy, with a hold full of mail and a man who washed his propeller like a prayer. They never found it. They never found him. The coast grieved in its closed way—flowers on the breakwater, the flag at the light at half-mast for a month—and Quentin had not come back for the funeral. Had not come back for the year after, or the year after that. Had flown medicine and mail and strangers into places with no names on the charts, seven years of miles laid down like a wall, brick by brick, between themselves and a grey-green sea.

* * *

Quentin woke with the dream still on their skin like salt.

The hangar was grey with early light. Rain had stopped. Through the open door the tide was making, sliding up the flats in long silent sheets, and the cave—Quentin could see the headland from here, a black shoulder under the paling sky—would be closing its mouth again, taking the Meridian Star back down into the cold.

They made coffee they didn't drink. They sat on the oil drum again and looked at the tarp and the shapes beneath it.

One bundle, Ede had said. One bundle in the recovered sacks bearing Elias Coldharbour's handwriting—his narrow, forward-leaning script, the script on every checklist Quentin had ever copied as a kid, the script that had taught them their letters before any school did. Quentin had seen it through Ede's magnifying glass and had felt the floor of the world tilt a degree, just enough.

The mail was undelivered. That was the thing Ede kept coming back to, turning over like a stone looking for what lived under it. The Meridian Star had been carrying mail that never landed. Families on this coast, scattered by a scandal and a storm two generations back, had written to each other for decades, and the letters had gone into the mail sacks and the mail sacks had gone into the Star, and the Star had gone into the water, and every word in every letter had been waiting down there in the dark, patient as sediment, for seven years and sixty.

*What could my father have been writing?* Quentin thought. *To whom?*

The question had teeth. It worried at them all through the morning while they checked the Beaver's tie-downs and wiped the windshield that didn't need wiping and listened for Wren's truck on the gravel that didn't come.

* * *

It came back to them, in the middle of the morning, in pieces—the way the wreck had come back, tide by tide, not all at once.

Their father at the kitchen table the night before the last flight, writing. Not the logbook—Quentin knew his logbook habits, the terse entries, times and headings and fuel states, a man who wrote to the regulations and no further. This had been different. A long letter, pages of it, and when Quentin had come into the kitchen for the coffee tin, Elias had turned it over, unhurried, and put his hand flat on it like a man keeping a chart from blowing off a table in wind.

"Who's it to?" Quentin had asked, and their father had smiled—that smile, the one that gave away nothing and somehow gave away everything—and said, "Someone who's owed a long answer."

"You could just call them."

"Some things you write," Elias said. "Calling's for arranging. Writing's for meaning it."

Quentin had taken the coffee tin and gone. They remembered the exact weight of the tin. They remembered the smell of the kerosene lamp, though the house had electricity; their father liked the lamp for writing, said the light was older and told the truth. They remembered the sound of the pen, the scratch of it, going on and on after Quentin's door closed.

And they remembered, with the terrible clarity that only arrives years too late, that their father had looked at them across that kitchen table with an expression Quentin had spent seven years refusing to name. Not disappointment. Not hurt. *Hope.* The expression of a man who had written something long enough and true enough that he believed, against all evidence, that his child might still be reachable. That the letter in his hand and the son in his doorway were both addressed to the same silence, and that either one of them might still get through.

The Star had gone down with the letter aboard. Quentin had gone out with the silence. Between them, the sea kept both.

* * *

Ede found them at noon, or maybe Quentin found her; afterward neither could have said. She came up the hill with a basket—bread, smoked fish, a jar of her pepper sauce that could strip paint—and found Quentin sitting in the hangar doorway with the flashlight in their lap, turned on in broad daylight, as if it might show them something in their own hands.

"You look like a man who's been talking to ghosts," she said, setting the basket down.

"Is that what this is?" Quentin's voice came out rough. "Talking to ghosts?"

Ede lowered herself onto the second oil drum with the economy of a woman who had climbed a hundred lighthouse stairs and knew exactly what her body would and wouldn't do. She looked at the tarp. She looked at Quentin.

"You remembered something."

"I remembered the night before he flew it. He was writing. A long letter. He wouldn't say who it was to." Quentin turned the flashlight over and over. "Ede, if the Star went down on purpose—if that's what the survey charts say, if someone erased that cave from the records to protect something—then he was carrying that letter down with him. He wrote it and then he flew it into the water."

"Or," Ede said gently, "he wrote it and the water took him, and the two facts are only neighbors, not a sentence." But her hands, folded in her lap, had gone still, and stillness in Ede Okonkwo was a kind of speech. She had been a keeper for forty years. She knew the difference between a light that failed and a light that was put out. "There's a way to know," she said. "The sacks we brought up. The paper down there—it's cold, it's dark, it's anoxic. Paper keeps. Better than people do."

"I can't read them."

"No," she agreed. "Not yet."

"Not ever, maybe. That's the point of mail, Ede. It's not addressed to me." Quentin heard their own voice climb and hated it. "Every one of those letters belongs to somebody else. Somebody who's been waiting sixty years without knowing they were waiting. Who am I to—"

"Who are you," Ede said, "to be the one who found them?" She let that sit in the hangar's cool air. "You've been asking the wrong question since you were a boy, Quentin Coldharbour. Not *what do I deserve.* The sea doesn't deal in deserving. The question is *what was given to you to carry.*" She nodded at the tarp. "Your father carried mail his whole life. Never once opened a sack that wasn't his. But he read every address like it was scripture. He told me once—this was before you were born—he told me the whole job was just this: making sure what people meant to each other didn't get lost on the way."

Quentin's throat closed. The flashlight clicked off under their thumb, finally.

"He was writing to someone," Quentin said, when they could. "The night before. Someone who was owed a long answer. Ede—what if it's in there? What if it's in the sacks?"

"Then it's in the sacks," Ede said, "and the sea kept it for you, and kept you for it. That's not a punishment, child. That's just the tide." She rose, took the basket into the hangar's little back room, and began, without asking, to make tea, because that was what Ede did: she arrived, and things became livable.

Quentin stayed in the doorway. The tide was high now. The headland's black shoulder wore a collar of white. Down there, in the dark that had held for seven years, a letter waited with their father's forward-leaning script on the envelope, and Quentin understood at last that the treasure everyone whispered about—the gold, the salvage, Voss's arithmetic—was a misunderstanding so complete it was almost funny. The wreck held one thing of value, and it was addressed, in a hand Quentin would know blindfolded, to them.

* * *

They were still sitting there when Wren's truck finally came up the gravel, too fast, the way Wren did everything, and the kid came around the hangar corner with a look on their face that stopped Quentin's heart before a word was spoken.

Wren's eyes went from Quentin to the Beaver to the road behind them, quick as a depth sounder.

"Quentin," Wren said, breathless. "You need to come look at your plane."
