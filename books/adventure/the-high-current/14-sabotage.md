## 14. Sabotage

Wren woke to the sound of gulls arguing over something that wasn't fish.

That was the first wrongness. Gulls at the harbour fought over fish, over chips, over the soft parts of anything dead. They did not fight at five in the morning over nothing, in that flat, aggrieved tone they used when the world had broken some unspoken rule. Wren lay in the loft above the hangar office—Ede's old cot, a wool blanket that smelled of camphor and decades—and listened until the sound resolved into something worse than gulls.

Silence from the fuel shed.

Every morning since Quentin had started flying the recovery runs, the little pump motor in the shed had coughed itself awake before dawn, a stuttering two-stroke heartbeat Wren could set a clock by. This morning: nothing. Just the gulls and the slap of water against pilings and, beneath it all, a hissing whisper that didn't belong to any engine Wren knew.

They were down the ladder and out the side door before their boots were fully on, one heel folded flat, running the gravel spine of the dock with their breath smoking in the grey.

The pontoon was bleeding.

Not blood—fuel, but it looked like blood in the half-light, a rainbow-slicked ribbon unspooling from the *Kestrel*'s port float and drifting out on the ebb, iridescent, obscene. And the float itself—Wren dropped to their knees on the planking without feeling the cold go through them—one long gash opened along its belly like something had tried to gut the plane and lost its nerve halfway. The fabric of the hull hung in fronds. Water lapped inside the wound with each small swell, patient as a tongue.

The hiss was coming from the fuel drain valve under the wing root. Open. Fully open, the little lever flipped to its detent, and the tank had been emptying itself into the harbour all night, a slow dark confession.

"No," Wren said, out loud, to nobody, because saying it made it less true. "No, no, no."

They slammed the valve closed with hands that wouldn't stop shaking. The hiss died. The silence after was enormous.

* * *

By full light there were six people on the dock, which in Harrow's Reach constituted a crowd, a crisis, and a committee all at once. Marta Oduya had brought a roll of gaffer tape she had no use for and no intention of leaving behind. Old Silas stood at the far end with his arms crossed, radiating the opinion that this was what came of stirring up sunken things. Ede had arrived without being seen arriving, the way she did, and stood beside the *Kestrel* reading the gash in the pontoon the way she read weather—silently, thoroughly, with her whole face.

Quentin crouched at the waterline and did not touch the plane.

That frightened Wren more than the sabotage had. Quentin Coldharbour touched everything—checked everything twice, three times, ran their thumb along control cables like a mother counting fingers. Now they knelt an arm's length away, hands hanging, staring at the cut as if looking directly at it might make it worse.

"Clean blade," Marta said. She'd gone down on the skiff and come back with it; now she leaned close over the pontoon. "One pass. Somebody knew where to cut—followed the seam. Didn't hack at it."

"Somebody knew planes," Silas called from the end of the dock, and let that sit in the air like smoke.

Wren's stomach dropped through the boards and kept going.

Because it was true. It took knowledge to open a drain valve in the dark without spilling a drop on your boots. It took knowledge to know that a pontoon's seam was its spine, that one long cut along it would write off the float entire. And of everyone within thirty miles who had that kind of knowledge in their hands—

"—and I suppose we're all wondering," Silas said, not loudly, not cruelly, which somehow made it worse, "who exactly has been spending their evenings elbow-deep in other folks' aircraft."

The dock went quiet in the specific way a room goes quiet when everyone has thought the same thing and no one wants to be first.

"I sleep here," Wren said. Their voice came out higher than they wanted. "I sleep *here*, Silas, you can see my—"

"You slept here last night, then."

"Yes!"

"Anyone see you?"

Marta looked at her boots. Silas looked at Wren. The tide breathed against the pilings. And Quentin—who had said nothing through all of it, whose face had been doing the arithmetic of the cut seam and the open valve and the six days Voss needed—Quentin finally stood up and turned around.

And looked at Wren.

It lasted maybe two seconds. It should have lasted zero. But in those two seconds Wren watched Quentin's eyes do the thing they'd watched them do to strangers at the fuel dock a hundred times: the quick downward flick, the weighing, the *can I trust this person with the thing that keeps me alive*. Wren had spent three weeks earning their way past that look. Three weeks of pre-flights done right, of radio calls copied perfectly, of showing up early and staying late and never once asking for anything. Three weeks, and it took one slashed pontoon for the look to come back.

"I didn't say anything," Wren said. Quieter now. "I told you. That night, when you asked—I told you I'd talked to Kai about the depth sounder, and that was a mistake, and I've hated myself for it every day since. But I never—" Their throat closed. They pushed through it anyway, because if they stopped they might not start again. "I would rather cut off my own hands. You know that. You have to know that."

"I don't know anything," Quentin said.

It wasn't cruel. That was the terrible part. It was just tired, and level, and true, the way Quentin was true the way a knife is true—honest about its edge regardless of where it pointed. And it landed in Wren's chest like a dropped anchor.

"Right," Wren said. "Right. Okay." They heard themselves laugh, one dry note with nothing under it. "Seven years you've been here, and nobody's ever once accused you of anything, because nobody knows enough about you to try. Three weeks I've been here and I'm already the suspect. Maybe that's the trick, huh? Maybe that's how you stay safe—just never let anybody close enough to blame."

"Wren." Ede's voice, low, from beside the plane. Not a warning. An invitation to stop.

But the door was open now and the whole night's hurt was coming through it. "You want to check someone, check the visitor log at the hotel. Check who's been asking about chart depths at the chandlery. Check the *tide tables*, Quentin, because whoever did this knew the ebb would carry the fuel away before dawn so there'd be nothing to trace—they planned it around the *water*—" and Wren stopped, because they heard their own voice crack, and hated it, and finished anyway, barely above the gulls: "I found it. I'm the one who found it. I ran down here in the dark alone because somebody had to, and the first thing you did was look at me like I did it."

Nobody spoke. A herring boat puttered out past the point, absurdly ordinary, trailing its smell of diesel and salt.

Quentin opened their mouth. Closed it. Whatever was behind their eyes—the guilt, or the fear, or whatever it was Quentin carried instead of either—went behind shutters, and when they spoke it was only logistics, only survival, the language Wren had once loved them for and suddenly couldn't stand.

"We need a patch kit from Port Merrow. Fabric and dope won't hold a cut that size—we need a whole replacement float section. That's a day round trip by road, two by sea." They were talking to Marta now. To the dock. To anyone who wasn't Wren. "Voss's scouts will know by tonight that we're crippled. We fly nothing until that float's fixed, which means the mail sacks stay in the cave through at least one more tide cycle, which means—"

"Which means you need to decide," Ede said, "who you believe."

The words landed on the dock like a dropped tool.

"Because I'll tell you what I see," Ede went on, and her voice had that quality it had when she read from the ledger—old, unhurried, immovable. "I see a child who has slept in my late husband's cot for three weeks and never once locked the door. I see someone who found the damage and raised the alarm before dawn, alone, instead of going back to bed and letting us discover it at a civilized hour—which is what anyone guilty would have done. And I see a grown man who is so practiced at suspecting everyone that he does it in his sleep, even to the people who—" she paused, chose the word with care, "—who are trying to help him."

Quentin's jaw worked. For a moment something moved behind the shutters—not anger. Something rawer. "You weren't here seven years ago, Ede. You don't know what it costs when you—"

"When you what? Trust?" Ede stepped closer, and even Wren felt the temperature change. "I kept a light burning over this water for forty-one years, Quentin Coldharbour. I know exactly what it costs. I paid it every single night. What I never learned is what it costs to keep it *dark*—but I expect you could tell me."

The silence that followed had weight, and grain, and history. Out on the ebb, the last of the fuel slick thinned and broke apart and drifted toward the open sea, taking its rainbow with it.

Quentin looked at Wren. Really looked, this time—past the two seconds, into the long second underneath, the one where Wren stood very straight and refused to wipe their face because wiping it would mean admitting there was something to wipe.

"I'm sorry," Quentin said. It came out rough, like something dragged over gravel. "I shouldn't have—that look. I shouldn't have given you that look."

"Yeah," Wren said. "You shouldn't have."

"It doesn't mean I'm not—" Quentin stopped. Started again, slower, as if translating from a language they hadn't spoken in years. "Trusting people is not something I know how to do anymore. That's not an excuse. It's just the truth. And apparently I do it badly enough that it looks like suspicion." A breath. "I'll drive to Port Merrow myself. I'll take the truck. Wren—you know the part numbers better than I do. Write me the list."

Something in Wren's chest loosened half a turn. Not all the way. Half a turn.

"And while I'm gone," Quentin added, and now their voice changed, dropped into the register Wren had only heard once before, the night they'd spotted the scout boat's depth sounder—flat, fast, certain, "somebody needs to figure out who *did* do this. Because it wasn't us. Which means it was someone who walked onto this dock in the dark, past Ede's window, past the harbourmaster's shack, and knew exactly what they were looking at."

* * *

They found the footprints at noon, or rather Ede found them, because Ede noticed things.

The shingle below the high-tide line, behind the fuel shed, held the ghost of prints pressed deep by someone carrying weight—two loads, by the spacing. Jerrycans, Wren realized. Empty ones carried in, full ones carried out. The thief hadn't wasted the drained fuel; they'd siphoned what remained into cans and walked it out along the shingle to where the ground firmed up past the boat ramp, where the prints ended in the crescent shadow of tyre tracks. A vehicle. Small. Narrow tyres.

And pressed into the mud beside the ramp, half-filled by seepage, a boot heel with a distinctive worn crescent across the tread—the mark left by a steel toe cap gone slightly proud of the sole.

Wren crouched over it for a long time.

They knew that boot. Everyone at the harbour knew that boot. It belonged to Tam Beckett, who worked the winch on the *Sea Holly*, who'd fixed Wren's bicycle chain twice without being asked, who'd taught them to gut a fish standing up so the blood ran clear. Tam, who'd been drinking at the Anchor three nights running and telling anyone who'd listen that salvage money was real money and real money didn't care whose feelings got wet. Tam, whose little sister had married out to the mainland years ago after the storm scattered everyone, and whose mother's name—Wren remembered it suddenly, coldly, completely—was on Ede's list of undelivered letters. Beckett. One of the bundles still lying in the dark of the cave, forty feet down, wrapped in oilcloth, waiting.

Tam hadn't sold them out for money. Or not only for money. Tam had sold them out because Voss's people had probably shown him something—a photograph, a promise, a way to get his family's letter back ahead of everyone else—and Tam, drunk and broke and hungry for anything of his own, had said yes.

Wren stood there in the noon light with the print filling slowly with seawater, and understood something that sat in their stomach like swallowed stone: enemies were just neighbors with worse options. And Harrow's Reach was full of neighbors.

They were still crouched there when Quentin's truck came back over the hill, hours early, horn sounding one long note across the water—an alarm, not a greeting. Wren ran for the dock and reached it as the truck slewed to a stop and Quentin got out holding a folded sheet of paper, their face gone the colour of the morning fog.

"The chandlery," Quentin said. "The man at the chandlery. Somebody bought out his whole stock of marine epoxy yesterday afternoon. Paid cash. Every tube he had." They unfolded the paper with hands that weren't quite steady. "He wrote down the truck plate. Wren—it matches the tyre tracks. It's the same vehicle."

"Whose?"

Quentin looked at them, and this time there was no weighing in their eyes at all—only the flat bright certainty of someone whose last comfortable theory has just caught fire.

"It's not Tam's," Quentin said. "Tam's truck is still parked outside the Anchor—it hasn't moved in two days. Someone used Tam. Someone *bought* Tam." They smoothed the paper flat against the dock post, and Wren leaned in and read the plate, and read it again, and felt the morning's whole wounded argument fall away beneath something much larger and much colder.

Because Wren knew that plate too. Everyone at the harbour knew that plate. It had been tied up at the visitors' buoy for a week, polite as Sunday, riding easy on the swell.

It belonged to the *Sea Holly*.
