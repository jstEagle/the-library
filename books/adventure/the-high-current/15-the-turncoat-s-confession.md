## 15. The Turncoat's Confession

The rain came in off the strait the way it always did in October, sideways and without apology, and Dell Hargrove stood under the fish plant awning with a cigarette he wasn't smoking and an envelope in his jacket that felt heavier than it had any right to be.

Three hundred dollars in twenties. Voss's man on the *Corvid* had paid him Tuesday, counted the bills twice like Dell was stupid, then said the same thing they'd all been saying since the fleet dropped anchor two bays south: *just keep your ears open, Hargrove. You hear coordinates, you hear names, you call the number. That's all.*

That's all. Dell had been telling himself that for eleven days now, and for eleven days it had worked, right up until the night he'd walked out to the fuel shed with a hose and a can and drained Quentin Coldharbour's tanks into the dark, and then knelt in the wet gravel with a knife against the pontoon fabric and found his hands wouldn't move.

He'd cut anyway. He was good at doing things his hands didn't want to do. That was most of what being poor on this coast taught you.

But here was the thing nobody warned you about sabotage: it wasn't the act that got you. It was after. It was standing at the dock the next morning watching Wren Okafor—the kid who'd waxed his skiff last spring for nothing but the use of his tools—staring at that slashed pontoon like somebody had cut her open instead of the boat. It was Ede Okonkwo crossing the street rather than pass him, which she never did, because Ede Okonkwo had fed half this town through two bad winters and did not cross streets to avoid anybody. It was the silence that followed him down the dock like a smell.

And underneath all of it, older and colder than any of it: the letter.

His mother's name was Ruth Hargrove. She had written forty-one letters in her life, and thirty-nine of them were still in the house she died in, unsent, because postage cost money and pride cost more. The fortieth went into the Meridian Star's mail sacks on a morning in November twenty-six years ago, addressed to a sister in Kettle Harbour with three words of apology in it and a photograph folded inside—a photograph of Dell, age six, gap-toothed, holding up a salmon nearly as long as he was tall.

The forty-first letter she wrote on her deathbed. It went nowhere. There was no plane anymore to carry it, no sister who hadn't already decided the silence was answer enough.

Dell had done the arithmetic a hundred times since the wreck came up. The salvage money Voss promised—if it ever came, if it ever came through clean, which men like Voss's money rarely did—was four thousand dollars. Maybe six. Enough to leave. Enough to start over somewhere the dock didn't know his name.

A letter from his dead mother was not worth anything. He knew that. He knew it the way you know a stone sinks.

He stood there anyway, under the awning, in the rain, holding three hundred dollars he couldn't make himself spend, and finally he understood that he had been standing on the wrong side of a line for eleven days, and that the line had never been about money at all.

* * *

He almost turned back twice.

Once at the head of the Coldharbour road, where the gravel went white with fog and every sensible nerve in him said *turn around, Hargrove, you're walking into a hangar full of people you betrayed.* And once at the door itself, hand raised, hearing voices inside—Ede's low and steady, Wren's quick and bright, and beneath them both the particular silence of Quentin Coldharbour listening, which was its own kind of weather.

Then he thought about the photograph. Gap teeth. Salmon. Rain on the roof of a kitchen where his mother hummed while she wrote apologies she never mailed.

He knocked.

It was Wren who opened the door, and the look on her face when she saw him was so exactly what he deserved that he nearly lost his nerve entirely. She stepped back without a word—not welcoming him, just clearing the sightline—and behind her, in the yellow light of the hangar's work lamps, Quentin Coldharbour stood up from a workbench scattered with dive gear, slow and deliberate, and Dell watched seven years of solitary caution come awake in the man's whole body at once.

"Dell," Ede Okonkwo said, from her chair by the stove. Not cold. Just level. "You'll take tea."

It wasn't a question, and somehow that undid him more than an accusation would have. He nodded. His voice had gone somewhere without him.

Quentin didn't sit back down. They stood by the bench with their arms crossed, and the lamplight caught the salt-scars on their hands, and when they spoke their voice was flat as the water before wind. "You've got until the tea boils to tell me why you're here."

So Dell told them.

He told it badly, the way confessions go—all sideways, starting with the money and circling back to the knife, the hose in the tank, the number he was supposed to call, the envelope still in his jacket because he hadn't been able to buy so much as a coffee with it since Tuesday. He told them about the *Corvid*, and the tender crew, and the man named Brice who paid him and smiled like a closed drawer. He told them the schedule.

That was the part that made the room go still.

Voss worked tides the way other men worked calendars. Dell had heard it repeated over the radio in fragments he wasn't meant to understand, and he'd written it down one night on the back of a fuel receipt because writing things down was the only power a poor man had. Now he unfolded the receipt and laid it on the workbench between them, smoothing it flat with two fingers like he was laying out something dead.

"Barge comes round the headland on the fifth day," he said. "They want the big ebb—spring tide, lowest of the month. Divers first, then the lift bags, then the crane barge sits over the cave mouth and they pull the fuselage whole. Voss doesn't send divers into caves unless he means to take everything." He swallowed. "They've got a lawyer too. Paperwork drawn up. Salvage claim filed under a company name out of Vancouver. By the time anyone here knows what happened, the letters will be in a warehouse being catalogued for auction."

"The letters aren't worth anything," Wren said. "Gold's worth—"

"They don't know that." Dell looked at her, and then he made himself look at Quentin, and then at Ede, whose face had gone very quiet in the way of someone watching a wound reopen. "Or maybe they do and they don't care. But I care."

"Why?" Quentin asked. One word. No softness in it.

And this was the part Dell had rehearsed on the walk up and forgotten entirely once inside, so it just came out raw, the way true things do.

"My mother," he said. "Ruth Hargrove. She put a letter on the Star the morning it flew. Last thing she ever tried to fix—her and my aunt, fell out twenty years before that over a debt neither of them would name, and Mom wrote her an apology and a photograph of me and never knew whether it landed." His throat worked. "There's a picture in that wreck of a six-year-old kid holding a fish bigger than he is, and it's the only proof left anywhere that my mother was ever sorry about anything, or glad about anything, or—" He stopped. Pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes for a second. When he lowered it, he finished plainly. "You can't auction that. Whatever Voss thinks is down there, some of it belongs to people who never got to say goodbye, and I helped the man who'd sell it. So. That's why."

Nobody moved. The kettle climbed toward its whistle and Ede took it off the heat before it screamed, and the small ordinary kindness of that—of being handed tea by a woman he had wronged—was worse than any blow.

"You slashed the pontoon," Wren said at last. Her voice shook. Not with fear—with something hotter. "I patched it for two days. I thought—I thought maybe I'd said something, or people would think I did, and Quentin—" She cut herself off, jaw tight, sixteen years old and furious and trying very hard to be fair, which was more than most grown men managed. "Why should we believe any of this?"

"Because he brought the schedule," Ede said quietly.

"Because he brought the schedule," Wren repeated, unconvinced. "Maybe it's fake. Maybe this is how they get us to move the mail sacks somewhere they can watch."

"If I wanted to feed you false information," Dell said, tired beyond anger now, "I'd have called the number. It would've been easier. It would've paid better." He set the envelope of bills on the workbench next to the fuel receipt. Three hundred dollars in twenties, counted twice by a man who thought Dell was stupid. "That stays here. Consider it the first payment on what I owe. The rest I'll work off. I've got a boat, a winch, and two hands that have hauled cod since I was nine. I know you've got no reason to take either offer."

Quentin picked up the receipt. Read it. Their eyes moved across Voss's tide figures and something shifted in their face—not trust, nothing so generous—but recognition, the look of a pilot reading weather and finding it worse than forecast and at least finally *knowable*. They set it down.

"Five days," Quentin said. "Spring tide's the fourth. Barge arrives the fifth. We've got less than that, because scouts will come ahead of the barge and I'm not fighting them on open water again." They looked around the hangar—at Ede, at Wren, last and longest at Dell—and Dell watched something happen in that face that he suspected almost never happened: a decision to stop carrying things alone. "Ede, the chart room. Tonight. Wren, radio the net—quietly, person to person, no broadcasts. If Voss hears anything, it'll be that the harbor's busy, nothing more." A breath. "Dell."

"Sir?"

"Don't call me sir." Quentin's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "Go home. Sleep, if you can. Be at the dock at first light with your winch boat. And Dell—if you're lying, there won't be a second conversation."

"I know," Dell said. And he did know, and the strange thing, the thing he'd carry out of that hangar along the fog-white road like a coal cupped in both hands, was how much lighter he felt knowing it. Eleven days of double life, and all it had taken to end was one knock on a door and the truth spoken ugly and plain.

At the threshold he stopped. Turned halfway back.

"The letter," he said. "When you get the sacks up. My mother's would be—" His voice failed on it, briefly. "Her handwriting leaned to the left. Like it was hurrying. Everything she ever wrote looked like it was in a hurry to be forgiven."

Ede rose from her chair, crossed the hangar floor, and stood before him—an old woman looking up at a guilty man—and touched his arm once, lightly, the way you'd steady someone on a ladder.

"We'll find it," she said. "All of them. Every last hurried word."

Dell Hargrove walked home through the rain, and for the first time in eleven nights he slept.

* * *

The hangar lamp burned past midnight.

Ede spread the old survey charts across the workbench, weighting the curling corners with socket wrenches and a tin of screws, and the three of them bent over the erased coastline together—Quentin tracing the blank space where the cave ought to be, Wren copying tide tables into her notebook in a schoolgirl's careful hand, Dell's fuel receipt propped against the lamp base like scripture.

"Five days," Quentin said again, but softer now, working it aloud. "One plane, one winch boat, one dive window per tide. We can't lift the fuselage—we don't need to. Sacks, cockpit, logbooks. Anything else Voss can have." A pause, and then, quieter, almost to themselves: "Almost anything else."

Wren looked up. "What's the plan?"

Quentin straightened, and the lamplight moved across their face, and for the first time since the cave mouth had opened in the falling tide, they looked not like a person running but like a pilot with a heading.

"Tomorrow we ask this town for help," they said. "All of it. Boats, hands, rope, everything. And then—" they tapped the fuel receipt, the tide numbers, Voss's own arithmetic, turned against him at last— "we beat him to the water."

Outside, the rain kept coming off the strait, and five tides away, the spring ebb waited at the bottom of the sea.
