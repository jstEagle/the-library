## 16. Five Tides Left, One Plane

The tide table hung on Ede's kitchen wall the way other houses hung photographs of grandchildren. Quentin had never paid it much attention before—tides were weather to them, something that happened to you. Now they stood in front of it with a grease pencil in their hand and five circles drawn on five dates, and understood that the water had stopped being weather and become a clock.

"Say it again," Ede said from behind them. "Slowly. Like you're not ashamed of it."

Quentin's jaw worked. Asking for help had a taste to it, coppery and strange, like biting down on a cold fillings morning.

"I need boats," they said. "I need a winch that can pull four hundred pounds of wet mail sack up a rock chute against a current. I need hands—more than mine, more than Wren's. And I need all of it in the water before the fifth tide, because on the sixth one Voss's barge comes around Serrated Head with a crane arm long enough to reach into my cave and take everything I—" They stopped. Corrected themselves. "Everything *we* found."

The word *we* came out crooked, like a wing feather bent wrong. Ede heard it anyway. She always did.

"Well," she said, and untied her apron. "Then we'd better start with the ones who owe me favors. Sit down, child. That list won't write itself."

* * *

**WREN**

By noon Wren had flown three circuits of the harbor in Marsh's old Cessna 140, the one with the door that rattled like a tambourine, dropping weighted notes onto decks and into dories because the coast's radio net was half gossip and half static and both traveled at the speed of rumor. The notes were written in Ede's handwriting, which was the whole trick. A note from Quentin Coldharbour would have been read twice and doubted. A note from Ede Okonkwo was read once and obeyed.

*Recovery operation. Meridian Star. Five tides. Boats, winches, strong backs. Dock meeting at six.*

Wren banked over the fish cannery and watched old Halvard Brandt come out onto his deck, read the note, and stand very still for a long moment with his hat in his hands. Then he went inside and came back out with his two sons, and all three of them were looking up at her plane, and Wren felt something turn over in her chest that she didn't have a name for yet.

They were coming. Not because of treasure—though some of them surely thought about treasure, everyone did—but because the Meridian Star was theirs. It had carried their letters. Their mothers' handwriting. Their fathers' apologies, sealed in kraft paper and lost for thirty years under forty feet of green water. You did not let strangers with cranes dig through your family's mail.

She landed on the strip behind Marsh's hangar, taxied in, and found Quentin already elbow-deep in the Beaver's engine cowling with grease to the elbows and a look on their face Wren had never seen before. It took her a second to identify it.

"You're scared," she said.

"I'm doing arithmetic," Quentin said. "It looks like fear."

"No." Wren leaned against the strut. "Arithmetic is what you've been doing this whole time. Miles and fuel and tides, everything by yourself. This is different." She pointed at the grease, at the open engine, at the spare parts laid out on a tarp like organs on a tray. "You're fixing your plane so other people can depend on it. That's not arithmetic."

Quentin looked at her the way people look at a chart when it shows water where they thought there was land.

"Go get some sleep," they said finally. "You'll be no use to anyone running on nothing."

"I'm sixteen," Wren said. "Sleep is for people who don't have a wreck to save." But she went, because even she could hear the tremor underneath the bravado, and pride only works if nobody notices it's holding you upright.

* * *

**EDE**

Ede ran the dock meeting the way she had once run the light: precisely, without flourish, trusting that the machinery would do what it was built to do if she gave it power enough.

Thirty-one people came. She counted them out of habit—the same way she'd counted fog signals, counting meant you cared whether something was missing. Halvard Brandt brought his trawler, the *Kestrel*, and offered her engine room as a workshop. The Okonkwo name still opened doors, though Ede had spent thirty years insisting she was no relation to anybody important; the town had decided otherwise and she had learned to spend the currency they minted for her.

Quentin stood at the edge of the lamplight with a charcoal sketch of the cave on a piece of chart backing, and Ede watched them do the hardest thing she had ever seen anyone do on this dock, harder than diving, harder than drowning nearly: they waited to be asked.

It was Halvard who asked. "Show us the entrance again."

Quentin turned the sketch around. "High-water mark is here. The mouth is eight feet above that, exposed maybe ninety minutes each ebb, less in swell. Inside, the passage drops. The fuselage sits in a silt bowl at about twelve feet, bow-in, nose down maybe twenty degrees. Current runs through on the flood—it's why the tail section survived, actually, the flow scoured the silt off it. On the slack we might get twenty minutes of workable water. Maybe less. I'm not going to lie to you: it's a bad dive. Cold, dark, tight."

"And the sacks?" said Marta Ilves, whose grandmother's sister had written letters that never landed, letters about a farm sold out from under her during the scandal years. Marta had asked that question every day since the news broke, sometimes aloud, sometimes just standing on the seawall looking south toward Serrated Head.

"Eleven sacks recovered so far, dry in the tail compartment. There are more forward, in the flooded section. Wet but sealed. Wax-impregnated canvas, the old postal issue. Some of it will be readable. Some of it won't." Quentin's voice caught on that, just barely, and steadied. "We get what we can. We get it before the barge does."

A silence, then Halvard's younger son, Petter, who was twenty-two and built like a capstan, said: "My father has a hand winch off the *Kestrel*'s old boom gear. Three-quarter ton rated. If we anchor it on the ledge above the mouth—"

"We'd need to drill into rock," Quentin said. "In surf surge."

"We've drilled into worse," Halvard said mildly, and someone laughed, and the meeting changed key. After that it wasn't a plea anymore; it was a plan assembling itself out loud. Boats assigned by draft and capability. The cannery's generator for lights. Divers—three of them, God help them, including a retired abalone poacher named Silas Fenn who had spent more hours underwater than most fish—and rope teams, and signal watches on the headland to give warning when Voss's scouts moved.

Ede wrote it all in her ledger, the same ledger that held thirty years of weather and ship sightings, and thought: this is what a lighthouse is for. Not the lamp. Never really the lamp. It's for the night when everybody shows up.

At the end, when the crowd was draining away into the blue dusk, Quentin lingered. Stood beside her at the dock rail like a person who had forgotten how to loiter and was relearning it badly.

"Why," Quentin said. "Why are they doing this? Half of them haven't spoken to me except to buy fuel."

Ede looked out at the water, which was going the color of hammered pewter. "Because you asked," she said. "Seven years, you never asked anybody for anything. People notice that kind of thing even when you think they don't. Tonight you asked, and it turns out the whole coast was waiting to be asked." She patted the rail once, twice. "Go home, Quentin. Sleep. Tomorrow we drill rock."

* * *

**VOSS**

Two bays south, Aldous Voss received his scout's report at 2200 hours, standing at the rail of the *Meridian's Reckoning*—he had named the barge himself, and enjoyed the irony the way he enjoyed most things: quietly, and alone.

"They're mobilizing," the scout said. "Trawler, skiffs, a generator on the dock. Looks like a salvage op."

"It is a salvage op," Voss said. "Ours. They're simply confused about ownership." He unfolded his tide table—he preferred paper, paper couldn't be hacked or lost—and made his small marks. Slack windows. Weather forecast: building northwest swell in three days, which would close the dive window entirely after tide four unless it eased. His schedule and the sea's schedule were converging on the same narrow throat of time, which meant the contest would be decided not by equipment—he had more, better, heavier—but by whoever wanted it more.

Voss considered the question of wanting. He had wanted things his whole life and gotten most of them, and had noticed somewhere along the way that wanting was a renewable resource, that it replenished itself precisely because it was never satisfied. The pilot out there—Coldharbour—wanted differently. Voss had seen the type. They wanted an ending. And people who wanted an ending would spend anything to get it, including themselves.

"Keep the scouts out," he said. "Passive only. No contact." He paused. "And find out how many divers they have. Names, certifications, anything."

The scout left. Voss stayed at the rail a while longer, watching the distant smudge of lights along the far shore, and felt the old familiar pleasure of a schedule tightening around other people's lives.

Five tides, he calculated. He would need every one of them.

**QUENTIN**

They drilled rock for eleven hours and Quentin learned the names of everyone in town.

That was the thing nobody warned you about, asking for help: it came with introductions attached. By midmorning of the first day they knew that Halvard took his coffee black and bitter "like his opinions," that Marta Ilves could splice line faster than anyone and talked constantly while she did it, that Silas Fenn had a laugh like a bilge pump starting and had been diving these waters since before Quentin was born. By afternoon they knew that Petter Brandt sang hymns underwater in his head to pace his breathing, and that Wren had appointed herself quartermaster and ran the supply skiff with the imperiousness of a much older person, and that Ede stood on the ledge above the cave mouth the entire time, watching the water like it owed her money.

The winch went in on the second day. Drilling into the ledge rock in surge surge was exactly as miserable as Quentin had promised, and twice the anchor bolts spun loose in soft seams and had to be reset deeper, and at one point a wave came through the cave mouth below and exhaled spray thirty feet up the cliff face, soaking everyone, and Silas Fenn laughed his bilge-pump laugh and said, "Well, she's awake now," and they kept drilling.

On the third day they rigged the haul system: main line, backup line, tag lines, a lowering bridle for the divers, a lifting frame for the sacks. Quentin drew it all on the chart backing and then, for the first time in seven years, handed the drawing to someone else and let them check the math. It was Halvard who found the error—a chafe point where the main line would rub basalt on the lift—and they rerigged, and Quentin lay awake that night thinking about the chafe point, thinking: *seven years, that's what I was. A chafe point. Rubbing against my own life until something wore through.*

On the fourth day, the northwest swell arrived early.

It came in at dusk as a long low pulse, nothing dramatic, just the sea clearing its throat, and everyone on the ledge felt it through their boots. Ede checked her watch against the tide table and did not say anything, but Quentin saw her jaw set, and went and stood beside her.

"How much time does that cost us?"

"Tomorrow's slack shortens," Ede said. "Day after, the mouth surges too hard to work. So." She turned and looked at them, and in the last grey light her eyes were very steady. "One good window. Tide five. Everything we have, all at once."

"One shot," Quentin said.

"One shot." Ede folded the tide table closed. "Get some sleep, pilot. Tomorrow you'll want your whole self—every hour of it, every mile." A pause, and then, gentler than the wind coming off the water: "Every mile you ever flew away. Spend it all tomorrow. Bring them back."

Below them, in the dark, the cave breathed its slow tidal breath—in and out, in and out—like something sleeping, like something waiting to be woken. Twelve feet down in its silt bowl sat a fuselage full of undelivered words, and somewhere inside it, in a cockpit sealed for thirty years, a logbook with a final line addressed to a name Quentin had spent seven years refusing to answer to.

Quentin stood at the edge a moment longer. Then they turned and walked back toward the lights of the town, and for the first time since they'd landed on this coast, they did not walk alone.

Dawn would bring tide five. Dawn would bring the dive.
