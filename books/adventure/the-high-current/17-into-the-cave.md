## 17. Into the Cave

The tide turned at 4:17 in the morning, and by six the whole cove had organized itself around the effort like a body around a heartbeat.

Quentin stood on the gravel shelf at the cave mouth with a regulator between their teeth and the cold climbing their legs, watching the town do something Quentin had spent seven years making sure they would never need. Marta Reyes's gill-netter rode at anchor off the shelf, her deck crowded with air tanks borrowed from the fire station and dive gear older than Wren. Two dories held position outside the headland as pickets, radioing every ten minutes that the sea beyond was still empty—still empty—still empty. Ede Okonkwo sat in the bow of the netter wrapped in a wool coat she refused to button, holding the ledger against her chest the way other people held Bibles.

And Wren.

Wren stood waist-deep in the shallows in a wetsuit two sizes too big, sleeves rolled three times, checking the straps of a lift bag for the fourth time. Sixteen years old and vibrating with purpose. They had argued about this until midnight, and Quentin had lost every round.

"You need someone small," Wren had said, not looking up from the gear. "The gap between the fuselage and the wall is maybe sixty centimeters. I've been through tighter spaces under the dock since I was twelve. You're too big to fit and too proud to say so."

"I'm going in," Quentin had said.

"First sack, yes. But somebody has to run the line inside while you haul, and it can't be me hauling because my hands are—" they'd held them up, those rope-burned hands from Tide Two, "—not what they were. So we trade. You take the first bag out. I go in for the rest."

It had made sense. That was the trap of it. Everything Wren proposed made sense, delivered in that flat, certain voice, and Quentin kept agreeing because agreeing was easier than admitting what was actually happening—which was that Quentin Coldharbour, who had arranged an entire life so that no one could ever be lost to them again, was about to send a sixteen-year-old into a flooded cave with their father's wreck.

"You breathe when I tell you to breathe," Quentin said now, one last time, mouth around the words badly because of the regulator strap. "You follow the guideline. You touch nothing that isn't yours to touch. And if anything shifts—anything—you come straight back down the line. Not up. The line. Promise me."

Wren looked at them, and for a second the cocky hangar-rat mask slipped and Quentin saw the kid underneath—the one who'd stowed away because home was somewhere they couldn't stay either—and said, "Promise."

The water inside the cave was green-black and utterly still, a held breath. Quentin went first, pulling themselves down the guideline into the throat of rock, and the world narrowed to the circle of their torch beam and the rasp of their own breathing, which sounded enormous and animal in the silence. The Meridian Star's tail came out of the dark exactly as it had seven days ago—upright, improbable, furred with a decade of silt and pale with barnacle, the registration letters half-eaten but legible if you already knew them. C-GMSK. A type Quentin could identify by silhouette alone, the way you identify your own name called across a room.

They worked. That was the mercy of work: it left no room for anything else. The first mail sack came free of the cargo bay with a soft tearing sound and a bloom of sediment, and Quentin got the lift bag under it, clipped it, sent it rising along the guideline toward the light where Marta's crew waited with ropes. One. Eleven more inside, according to the manifest numbers Ede had reconstructed from the ledger. Twelve sacks, four hundred and eleven pieces of undelivered history, sealed in canvas and rust.

Wren slid past them into the fuselage gap, small and quick, torch beam sweeping. Quentin watched the light move deeper into the wreck and felt something tighten behind the sternum that had nothing to do with the harness.

Then they hauled the first sack into daylight, and the day took over.

* * *

By noon the sun was high enough to throw a blade of light straight down the cave mouth, and the water inside turned briefly, treacherously beautiful—gold shafts in green dark, silt drifting like weather. Quentin had made four trips. Four sacks on the gravel shelf, black with silt, being carried up the beach by a chain of people Quentin mostly knew by reputation: the Reyes brothers, old Sam Teller, the dockhand Halloran, whose face had been strange all morning, tight with something he wasn't explaining. Nobody spoke much. There was a rhythm to it, a working silence, and Quentin understood suddenly why people built lives inside communities: because the silence of people who know what you're doing is a different substance entirely from the silence of being alone.

Fifth trip. Quentin went down the line with empty hands and rising hope, and hope, they would think later, was the mistake. Hope made you fast.

Inside, Wren had freed three more sacks and stacked them in the cargo doorway for lifting. "Four left," Wren's voice crackled through the cheap comm units, tinny and young. "They're wedged under the frame. I need to go in past the bulkhead."

"No," Quentin said. "Send them out one at a time, I'll haul, we do it from outside."

"There's a brace fallen across them. From out here it's a two-person lift. From in there it's one person and a lever." A pause, and then, gentler than Quentin had ever heard them: "I've got room to work. You don't. Come hold the light."

So Quentin did the thing they would spend the rest of their life being grateful and horrified by: they put their shoulder against the corroded frame of their father's last aircraft and held it steady while Wren wriggled past, torch in teeth, into the space where the cockpit used to be.

The sacks came free one by one. Bag five rose along the line. Bag six. Bag seven.

On bag eight, the Meridian Star moved.

It was not dramatic. That was the horror of it—no groan of metal, no warning. Ten years of corrosion had been holding the fuselage together the way frost holds a stone, and the frost chose that moment to let go. Quentin felt it through their shoulder first, a shiver in the airframe like a horse shifting weight, and shouted into the comm—"OUT, OUT NOW"—and shoved backward off the frame, and the tail section settled twenty centimeters with a sound like a giant closing a book, and the silt came down.

The water went brown. Total brown. Torch beams became rumors. Quentin hung in the cloud with their heart slamming, counting seconds, listening.

"Wren. Talk to me."

Static. Then, thin and wrong: "I'm—I'm stuck. My leg. The frame came down on my leg, Quentin, I can't—"

"Pull."

"I'm *pulling*—"

The comm hissed. Somewhere in the murk, the guideline still ran, taut, toward the light. Quentin's own body voted before their mind convened: fins kicking, hands on rock, swimming blind back into the wreck they had spent seven years pretending didn't exist.

* * *

Underwater time does not behave. Ninety seconds later—Quentin would learn it was ninety seconds; it felt like a season—they found Wren by the glow of a dropped torch wedged in the wreckage, and found the problem by feel: the collapsed section of frame had come down across the fuselage floor like a gate, and Wren's right leg was through the gap to the knee, pinned at the calf where the corroded strut had folded over it. Wren's mask was fogged, their breath loud and fast, bubbles pouring off them in panicked strings.

Quentin got a hand on the back of Wren's neck—grounding, the oldest rescue trick, the thing you do before language exists—and squeezed once. Then twice. Then pressed their forehead, absurdly, against the kid's mask, because there were no words underwater except the ones you said with your hands.

*Breathe. I have you. We are doing this slowly.*

Wren's breathing slowed by degrees. Not calm—nothing like calm—but controlled, the way you control a skid. Quentin's mind, that cold instrument seven years in the tuning, laid out the arithmetic with terrible clarity: Wren had maybe eight minutes of air. The strut pinning the leg was steel, corroded, immovable by hand. Above it, the fuselage was settling by millimeters, the way ice settles before it goes. The exit gap was now forty centimeters, maybe less. Quentin's shoulders would not fit through with Wren. Quentin's shoulders might not fit through alone.

There was a hacksaw in the tool bag clipped at Quentin's hip—one of the six things they'd brought on the theory that preparation was a substitute for prayer. A corroded strut, half its thickness eaten away. Maybe four minutes of cutting. Maybe six. Underwater, in zero visibility, in a collapsing tomb, with a panicking teenager bleeding into green water.

Quentin thought—clearly, calmly, with a kind of disgust at their own clarity—*you could go for help.* Out the gap. Up the line. Thirty seconds. People above, divers, jacks, a proper rescue. And every second away was a second the fuselage settled, and the math on whether Wren's air outlasted a proper rescue was math Quentin could not make come out right, and they knew it, and their body knew it, and their body had already decided.

Seven years. Seven years of fuel stops without conversation, of charts without annotations, of a life trimmed of every tether heavy enough to drown. Seven years of the same private creed, never spoken because spoken creeds can be challenged: *no one gets to be lost because of me.*

Their hands found the hacksaw. Their body spent the creed like a coin it had been saving.

The first stroke of the blade shrieked through the water, a sound Wren felt rather than heard, and Wren went rigid, then deliberately, heroically loose. Quentin cut. Silt swirled. The strut gave in flakes of orange rust and bright metal, and Quentin counted strokes because counting was the only way to keep the panic in its cage—*two hundred, three hundred, four*—and between counts they checked Wren's gauge: past half. Past half, and the cutting had barely started.

Something shifted overhead. Not the fuselage this time—the water itself, a long slow pulse, the distant ocean turning over as the tide began its afternoon climb. Quentin felt it in their inner ear, a pressure change like a door opening far away, and understood with a lurch that the clock had just acquired a second hand. The cave filled on the flood. In ninety minutes, the exit gap would be gone, and everything in here—wreck, mail, pilot, kid—would belong to the sea again, permanently.

*Cut faster.*

The blade snapped at stroke six hundred.

The sound of it—a tiny, brittle *tick*, swallowed instantly by water—was the loudest thing Quentin had ever heard. They held the useless stub up to their mask. Half the blade gone. Enough left, maybe, for a hundred more strokes. The strut needed three hundred.

Above them, faintly, the guideline trembled. Someone topside feeling the tension change, wondering. Quentin thought of Ede on the netter with her unbuttoned coat, of the ledger against her chest, of four hundred eleven letters waiting on a gravel shelf in the sun. Of a logbook somewhere in that cockpit, addressed to no one yet, to someone, to *them*.

No. Later. Everything later. Right now there was only this: a strut, a stub of blade, a kid's leg, and a gap forty centimeters wide that had to be an exit.

Quentin repositioned, braced both feet against the fuselage frame, hooked the remaining blade into the deepest cut, and pulled with their back, their arms, their whole furious history. Metal screamed. Rust snowed down. Wren had stopped trying to pull free and instead held the frame above themselves with both hands—taking load off the strut, buying clearance, thinking like a pilot even pinned and drowning-adjacent, and Quentin loved them then with a ferocity that frightened them more than the water did.

The strut parted at stroke ninety-one.

It came away with a lurch that dropped the fuselage another few centimeters, and Quentin did not think—thinking was for people with time—just seized Wren by the harness and hauled, and the leg came free with a scraping agony Quentin heard through the comm as a single high breath, and then they were both moving, Wren first through the gap because Wren was smaller and because Quentin's hands were on their back pushing, and the fuselage groaned above them like a thing deciding, and the gap was *tight*, Quentin's tank kissed the frame, scraped, held—

—and then green water, open water, the guideline in one fist and Wren's wrist in the other, and they were rising, both of them, along the line toward a blade of gold light that widened as they climbed like the pupil of an eye opening, and Quentin broke the surface gasping in the cathedral echo of the cave with the tide-light falling on them both and Wren coughing and laughing and crying all at once, saying something, saying "*told you* I'd get the sacks out," insane, absolutely insane, alive.

Quentin got them onto the gravel shelf and stripped the kid's fin and ran thumbs down the calf, finding blood, finding bone intact, finding a pulse hammering like a bird in a closed fist. Above, voices—Marta's crew hauling Wren up the rocks, blankets, the whole shorebound machinery of care descending. Hands pulled Quentin after, and Quentin let them, which was itself a kind of miracle, letting hands.

It was Ede who reached them first at the top of the shelf. She took Quentin's face in both her weathered hands, looked hard into their eyes, and asked only: "How many?"

"Eight," Quentin rasped. "Eight sacks out. Four still inside." They looked back at the cave mouth, where the blade of sunlight was already shortening, the tide sliding in beneath it, patient as arithmetic. "And the cockpit's still sealed. The logbook's still in there."

Ede followed their gaze to the shrinking light. When she spoke, her voice was quiet and certain and terrible.

"Then you'd best rest," she said, "because the next tide is the last one deep enough—and Voss's barge just cleared the headland."
