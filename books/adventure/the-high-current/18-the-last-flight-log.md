## 18. The Last Flight Log

The water came for him the way debts come—slowly, then all at once.

Quentin felt it first as a change in pressure against his ears, a deepening hum in the flooded dark of the cave. Then a cold tongue licked at the back of his neck, and he knew. The tide had turned. Somewhere beyond the rock, beyond the grey curtain of the ebb, the sea was drawing its long breath back in, and every second he lingered in this drowned throat of stone was a second borrowed from a lender who never forgave.

*Wren.*

He turned toward the sound of the water churning behind him—the place where Wren had gone under, where the fuselage had shifted with a groan like a dying animal and dropped a wall of twisted aluminum and silt across the passage. His dive light swept the murk and found nothing but brown-green cloud, particles turning in slow galaxies around him. He'd pushed Wren clear. He'd felt them go, felt the small strong hands shove off his shoulder, felt the moment of contact break—and then the hull had come down between them like a door slamming shut on seven years of bad decisions.

He hammered on the metal with the heel of his fist. Once. Twice. The sound came back flat and dead, swallowed by the cave. No answer. No tapping from the other side. Either Wren had made it out into the open channel where Ede's boat waited, or—

No. He wouldn't finish the thought. He put it down the way you put down something hot, before it burned all the way through your hands.

His gauge said eleven minutes of air. His depth said twenty-two feet and rising, because the water in the cave rose with the tide outside, climbing the walls in invisible inches. The exit behind him was sealed. Ahead, deeper in, the passage opened the way it had opened on the survey maps Ede had shown him—upward, toward the dry chamber at the heart of the cave, the one that existed only at low tide and only on paper that had been deliberately erased.

He swam.

The light found the Meridian Star again as he moved forward, and even now, even with death counting down somewhere behind his teeth, Quentin felt the old awe move through him like current. The mail plane lay on its belly in the silt of decades, wings folded against the rock like a bird that had chosen to land here and nowhere else. Its skin was furred with barnacle and rust. Its tail rose above him, and there—half-eaten by corrosion but still legible to a son's eye—was the registration. He had known it before he read it. He had known it the way you know your own name.

His father's airplane. His father's last flight.

The passage narrowed, tilted upward, and Quentin kicked through it with his shoulders scraping stone, air bubbling out of him in silver strings. The water grew colder, then shallower, then—suddenly, shockingly—he broke the surface.

Air. A pocket of it, a low stone chamber no bigger than a chapel, ceilinged with dripping rock. His light bounced off wet walls and found the far end of the space, where a shelf of pale stone rose out of black water like an altar.

And on the altar: a seabag, canvas, rotted at the seams but whole. And beside it, wrapped in oilcloth tied with wire, a shape he recognized before his mind could name it—a flight log, bound in leather, swollen with salt but intact.

Quentin hauled himself onto the shelf, and the rock took his weight the way the coast takes everything: without comment, without mercy, without letting go.

He got his mask up on his forehead and his regulator out of his mouth and just breathed for a moment, ragged, the sound enormous in the little chamber. Above him, water dripped from the ceiling with the patience of centuries. *Drip. Drip.* Each drop meant the tide was coming. Each drop meant the chamber would fill. He had minutes—not many—but he had come seven years and six tides to this room, and he was not going to leave without opening the bag.

His fingers shook. Not from cold. From the thing underneath the cold, the thing he had been outrunning since he was twenty-four years old and had walked away from a hangar door without looking back.

He cut the wire with his knife. The oilcloth fell away in sheets.

The logbook lay in his palms like a sleeping bird.

* * *

Elias Coldharbour had kept logs the way other men kept diaries—religiously, obsessively, in a small square hand that slanted forward as if always leaning into weather. Quentin knew that hand better than his own. He had grown up watching it move across fuel slips and grocery lists and the margins of navigation charts pinned to the kitchen wall. He had spent seven years trying to forget it and had failed at the job completely.

The early pages were routine. Dates, routes, hours, wind. *Meridian Star. Cessna 208. Dep 0640. Vis 3 mi improving.* The plain music of a working pilot's life, and reading it, Quentin could hear his father's voice saying the words aloud, could smell the hangar coffee and the avgas, could feel the particular quality of coastal morning light that came through the big doors in bars of gold.

Then, three-quarters through, the handwriting changed.

Not the letters themselves—the pressure behind them. The entries grew longer. They stopped being about weather and started being about men.

*Two strangers at the dock asking after cargo manifests. Said they were surveyors. Surveyors don't wear shoes that new.*

*Voss's people back again. Offered me a finder's fee to "report anything unusual." Told them the only unusual thing on this coast is what they charge for fuel. They laughed. I didn't.*

*They know about the mail run. All of it. The undelivered sacks from the storm year, sitting in the depot because nobody would claim them and nobody would burn them. Voss wants them. Not for the letters—for the salvage rights. A wrecked mail plane full of "historical material" is worth more than the plane ever was. He told me so to my face, smiling, like we were partners. Like the families waiting on those letters were inventory.*

Quentin's breath went thin. The drip of the ceiling seemed louder. He checked his watch without meaning to—six minutes of surface time before the chamber began to close—and made himself keep reading.

*I've been flying these sacks for two months now. Every trip, a few more bundles, moved quiet, stored at the lighthouse under Ede's floor where no manifest will ever find them. She asked me once why I didn't just deliver them. I told her the truth: some of these letters are forty years late. Delivering them opens doors people nailed shut a generation ago. But nailing doors shut is how this coast got sick in the first place.*

*So I'm going to do something stupid instead.*

The next entry was dated the day before the crash. Quentin's hands had gone very still.

*Quentin and I fought tonight. He's right and he's wrong. He says I should sell the route, sell the plane, get out while the getting's good—that men like Voss always win, that the smart play is to be gone before they arrive. Seven years old and already talking like a man who's been chased. I asked him who taught him that. He didn't have an answer. He just looked at me the way he does when he's decided something, and I thought: there goes my boy, learning to leave before he can be left.*

*I told him tomorrow's flight is the last one. That after tomorrow it won't matter. He didn't believe me. He slammed out of the house and I let him go, because some things a father has to finish alone, and some apologies take longer than a night to write.*

*Tomorrow I fly the last of the sacks out over open water and I am not going to come back. The Star will go down in the cave where the old charts say no cave is, and the mail will sleep with her, and Voss will spend thirty years looking for a wreck that isn't on any map. By the time anyone finds her, the letters will be too old to hurt anyone—or exactly old enough to heal them. I'll let the tide decide which.*

*I'm not doing it because I'm brave. I'm doing it because I finally understand that you can't outrun a debt. You can only pay it.*

Quentin stopped reading.

The chamber was smaller than it had been. Or the water was higher—he could hear it now, a soft encroaching whisper at the edge of the shelf, licking at the stone below his boots. His watch said four minutes. The exit passage behind him was already a black seam of rising water, and somewhere beyond it, sealed behind the fallen fuselage, was the way out, and somewhere beyond that was air, and sky, and a boy named Wren who might be alive because Quentin had shoved them clear with the last selfish strength he had.

He should stop reading. Every survival instinct he owned, every hard-earned rule of the man he'd built himself into—*never stay, never sink roots, never look back*—was screaming at him to stuff the log in his jacket and swim.

He turned the page.

The final entry was short. The handwriting had changed again—not weaker, but quieter, the way a voice goes quiet when it has stopped performing.

*If anyone finds this, tell my son:*

*Quentin. You were right about men like Voss. You were right that they always come, and right that running works, for a while. But you were wrong about one thing, and I need you to hear it, even if I'm not there to say it twice: the thing you're running from isn't them. It's the night you walked out, and the words you didn't say, and the fear that I died thinking you'd chosen a stranger's money over your own father. I never thought that. Not for one hour. Not for one minute. I knew why you left—I'd taught you how.*

*You don't owe me an apology. I owed you one, and I'm paying it the only way I have left, in the only currency this coast understands: I'm staying. Right to the bottom. So when you find this—and you will, because you're my son and you were born finding things—don't fly away. Land.*

*The letters are yours now. Not the gold everyone thinks is down here. The letters. Give them back.*

*That's the whole inheritance. That's everything I flew away from, so that you wouldn't have to.*

*—Dad*

The water was at his knees.

Quentin sat on the stone shelf in the drowning heart of a cave that did not exist, holding his father's last words, and something in his chest that had been clenched for seven years—some fist of guilt and grief and shame he had mistaken for his own heartbeat—finally, slowly, opened.

He wasn't forgiven by a voice from the past. There was no voice. There was only ink and salt and a dead man's careful hand, and yet the forgiveness arrived anyway, complete and posthumous and absolute, the way rain arrives on ground that had stopped believing in it. *I never thought that. Not for one minute.* Seven years of self-exile, of measured miles and unclaimed beds and a name he almost didn't use—all of it built on a fear his father had dismantled in four sentences, decades too late and exactly on time.

"I'm sorry," Quentin said aloud, to the chamber, to the water, to the man sleeping in the wreck below him. His voice cracked the silence like a wing against glass. "I'm sorry I didn't stay."

The water didn't answer. The ceiling dripped. And Quentin Coldharbour, who had spent half a lifetime perfecting the art of leaving, folded the logbook into the waterproof pouch against his chest, zipped it over his heart, and stood up to face the rising sea.

Because there was no time to grieve. The chamber was filling. The passage out was submerged and blocked. His air was a number shrinking toward zero. And somewhere on the other side of a wall of twisted aluminum, a sixteen-year-old stowaway was either alive or not, and Quentin had finally learned the difference between running from something and going toward it.

He pulled his mask down. Clamped the regulator between his teeth. Took one last look at the little stone chapel where his father's confession had waited twenty years for its only reader.

Then he stepped off the shelf into the black water, and dove.

* * *

The fuselage wall loomed out of the murk like a shipwreck's gate. Quentin followed his light along its corroded face, hands searching for what his eyes couldn't find—a gap, a tear, a seam weak enough to give. His gauges ticked. Eight minutes. The silt hung around him in blinding clouds stirred up by his own fins, and every instinct said *surface, surface*, but the surface was stone, and the only way out was through.

His glove caught on something near the wing root—a split in the skin, jagged, forced outward from the inside. Fresh metal gleamed at the edges of the tear.

Fresh. Bright. Recent.

Someone had already been through here. Someone small, someone desperate, someone who had not waited to be rescued.

Quentin laughed—a burst of bubbles, wild and disbelieving—and squeezed through the gap shoulder-first, tearing his sleeve, flooding his cuff with cold, caring nothing. On the far side the water cleared and his light stabbed ahead down the sloping passage toward the cave mouth, where the distant grey of open water glimmered like a promise.

And there, silhouetted against that grey, kicking hard for the light with a dive bag dragging at one shoulder and the unmistakable silhouette of the mail sack strapped across their back—was Wren.

Alive. Swimming. Heading out.

Wren must have heard his bubbles, because the small figure stopped, turned, and the beam of their own light found him and held—two seconds, three—long enough for Quentin to see the grin behind the mask, wide and furious and alive.

Then Wren pointed past him, back into the dark, back toward the wreck, and made the gesture every diver knows and dreads: *air—low—go, go.*

They went together, side by side through the collapsing blue, past the wing of the Meridian Star, past the tail with its half-eaten registration, out through the cave mouth into the channel where the tide was turning and Ede Okonkwo's boat was cutting a white wake straight toward them.

But even as he kicked for the light, even as the cold closed over him and his lungs burned and the surface rushed down to meet him like an open hand, one line from the logbook beat in Quentin's chest with every stroke, steadier than his pulse:

*Don't fly away. Land.*

Behind them, deep in the drowned dark, the Meridian Star settled another inch into the silt—her mail delivered at last, her pilot finally forgiven, her secret no longer hers alone.

Ahead, rounding the headland with the morning sun glinting off its deck cranes, came the barge.
