## 19. Out on the Ebb

The water let them go the way a fist lets go of something it has held too long—slowly, grudgingly, finger by finger.

Quentin felt it first as a loosening around their chest. The current that had been dragging at them since they entered the cave, shoving them sideways into rock, tangling the safety line around Wren's ankle like a fisherman's knot tied by a drunk—it slackened. The ebb was turning. Somewhere far above them, beyond sixty feet of green-black water, the sea was drawing its breath back out through the cave mouth, and everything inside it was being pulled toward that opening with the patience of something that had done this ten thousand times before and would do it ten thousand more.

They came up hand over hand along the line Quentin had rigged from the mail sacks, Wren's grip locked around Quentin's forearm hard enough to leave bruises neither would mention later. The sacks rose behind them in a slow silver constellation, each one buoyed by the lift bags Ede's nephew had sewn from tarpaulin and prayer. The fuselage stayed where it had settled after the shift—the great groaning movement that had nearly taken Wren with it—and Quentin did not look back at it. There was nothing left to look back for. Everything worth carrying out was rising with them now.

Except one thing. One small thing, flat and oilcloth-wrapped, riding against Quentin's ribs where they'd strapped it beneath the harness, where it had pressed against their heartbeat for ninety minutes of the longest dive of their life.

*Later,* Quentin told themselves. *Get out first. Then.*

The surface came up to meet them the way surfaces do at the end of a long dive—not suddenly but all at once, the black ceiling of the world splitting open into grey light and rain and air that tasted of kelp and diesel and cold iron. Quentin broke through it gasping, tore the regulator from their mouth, and heard—before anything else, before the slap of water or Wren's whooping cough beside them—the sound of engines.

Not one engine. Many.

"Quentin." Wren spat salt and pointed north, toward the headland, and Quentin turned in the water with their arms already going numb and saw what the sound meant.

Voss's barge had rounded the point.

It came on like a statement: a hundred and forty feet of rust-red steel with a crane arm folded along its spine like a sleeping heron, pushing a bow wave that flattened the swell for half a mile in either direction. Behind it came two workboats, low and black, and behind those, riding the chop with the arrogant ease of money that has never once been inconvenienced, a white-hulled cabin cruiser with radar domes bristling off its top like the antennae of some deep-sea insect.

And between the barge and the shore—between Voss's fleet and the mouth of the cave, which even now was exhaling the last of the ebb tide around their shoulders—was the town.

Quentin counted them without meaning to. It was an old pilot's habit, counting things, because numbers were steadier than hope. Eleven boats. Twelve. The *Marta June*, Halvorsen's forty-year-old gillnetter with the patched wheelhouse. The crab skiffs, three of them, running without pots. The water taxi, which had never once in its life been used as a weapon and was being used as one now, positioned broadside-on directly over the cave mouth like a cork in a bottle. A dory so old its planks wept at the seams. Two kayaks—kayaks—paddled by people whose faces were too distant to see but whose postures said everything posture needed to say: *we are here, we are small, we are not moving.*

"They came," Wren said. Water ran off the kid's hood, off their chin, and there was something in their voice Quentin hadn't heard before—something that wasn't fear or triumph but a kind of stunned recognition, like waking in a house you thought you'd dreamed. "I radioed when you went under, just—just in case, and I didn't think—" They stopped. Started again, quieter. "Ede must have rung every number in that ledger."

"She rang every number in the county," said a new voice, and Quentin twisted in the water to find the dockhand—Rourke, his name was Rourke—holding the gunwale of the skiff that had come gliding up alongside them, keeping the boat steady while they hauled themselves and Wren and four hundred pounds of undelivered history aboard. His hands were cracked and steady. "She started calling yesterday afternoon. By midnight she had lawyers on a ferry from the mainland. By dawn she had everyone else."

The mail sacks came up last, hauled dripping over the transom one at a time, and Rourke put his palm flat against the nearest one the way a man touches a door he hasn't opened in thirty years. He didn't say anything. He didn't have to. Quentin watched him and understood, finally, what Ede had tried to tell them in the chart room with her lamplight and her erased maps: this wasn't cargo. It had never been cargo. Every sack was a room in a house, a voice at a table, a sentence someone had written down because writing it down was the only way to make it true.

"Get them covered," Quentin said. Their voice came out scraped raw, half water, half rust. "Saltwater's already in them. We dry them tonight, page by page, or we lose them."

"We won't lose them." Rourke threw a tarp over the sacks with the tenderness of a man tucking in children. "Not twice."

Then the barge sounded its horn—one long blast, the international signal for *I am underway and I do not intend to alter course*—and the morning stopped being about mail.

* * *

From the deck of the skiff, two hundred yards off the cave mouth, Quentin watched Voss conduct his approach the way other men conducted conversations: precisely, pleasantly, and entirely without doubt.

The barge slowed a quarter mile out. The two workboats fanned wide, flanking, and the white cruiser came on alone, cutting the distance with the unhurried confidence of something that had never been told no by anything smaller than itself. Through the glasses Rourke handed over, Quentin could see figures on her aft deck—men in orange survival suits, professional, bored-looking—and at the rail, alone, a tall shape in a charcoal coat that could only be Voss, hands clasped behind him, studying the ragged line of fishing boats between him and the shore as though reading a document he intended to amend.

"He'll hail us first," Rourke said. "He's polite. That's the worst part of him. He makes you feel rude for objecting."

"I've met him." Quentin lowered the glasses. Rain beaded on the barrels. "He offered me whiskey and exclusivity. I didn't say yes."

"You didn't say no, either." Rourke said it without accusation, which somehow made it worse. "But you dove anyway. And you're here. So maybe the answer got made somewhere between."

Wren, wedged between the mail sacks with a bailer working steadily at the bilge water, looked up. "What happens if they just—push through? They've got steel. We've got—" They gestured at the flotilla. At the *Marta June*. At the kayaks.

"Then it gets ugly," Rourke said, "and ugly is expensive, and Voss is a man who counts. But don't mistake the arithmetic. Those boats aren't a wall. They're witnesses. You can't salvage what you can't reach, and you can't ram eleven neighbors in front of a mainland lawyer and call it maritime operations. He knows that better than anyone. It's why he schedules instead of threatens."

On the horizon east of the headland, right on cue, a ferry slid into view—white superstructure, blue hull, running lights still lit against the rain—and Quentin felt something loosen in their chest that had nothing to do with the tide.

"Ede's lawyers," they said.

"Ede's everything," Rourke answered. "You should've seen her this morning. Standing on the fuel dock at five a.m., ledger under one arm, phone in the other, telling some junior partner in Vancouver that if his firm couldn't produce an injunction by noon she'd take her business to his competitor, and she knew his competitor's mother, and she'd say so at the church social." He almost smiled. "That woman waited seven years for this fight. She brought reinforcements."

The cruiser's engine dropped to idle fifty yards off the skiff's beam. A figure appeared at her rail with a hailer, and even before the amplified voice rolled across the water, flattening the rain-sound, Quentin recognized the cadence—unhurried, courteous, each word set down like a coin on a counter.

"*Skiff at the cave station. This is the vessel Meridian Recovery, registered out of Prince Rupert. We have a salvage claim on file for this grid and a schedule to keep. I'm asking you to clear the area.*"

Silence, except the rain and the idling engines and the slap of water against hulls. Then, from the *Marta June*, Halvorsen himself stepped out onto his wheelhouse roof—a big man gone soft at the edges, wearing rubber boots and a slicker older than Wren—and raised his own hailer, and his voice came across the water rough as a gull's cry:

"*This is a community anchorage. You're in our water. Clear YOURS.*"

"*Our claim is registered with the provincial ministry—*" Voss's man began, and then Voss himself took the hailer, and his voice changed—not softer, exactly, but closer, the way a chess player leans in.

"*Mr. Coldharbour. I can see your skiff. I'd like a word, if you're willing. Nothing more than a word.*"

Quentin stood. The skiff rocked; the mail sacks shifted under the tarp like sleeping animals; Wren caught their elbow, steadying them, and did not let go until they nodded.

They took the cruiser's thrown line and crossed the fifty yards of cold water standing upright in the pitching skiff, and when they stepped aboard the white deck of the *Meridian Recovery*'s tender-cruiser, their boots left wet prints that a crewman moved to wipe away and then, catching Quentin's eye, decided to leave.

Voss received them under the aft canopy with two chairs and, unbelievably, a thermos of coffee. No whiskey this time. Quentin noted the change and understood it: whiskey had been for negotiation. Coffee was for aftermath.

"You got them out," Voss said. Not a question. His eyes went past Quentin to the skiff, to the tarp-covered shapes, and something flickered there—not disappointment, Quentin realized. Confirmation. Like a scientist whose experiment returned the predicted result. "Ninety minutes on the bottom, in a collapsing wreck, against the turn. I've had divers quit for less and I paid them well."

"What do you want, Voss."

"To watch you count." He poured coffee into both cups, offered one, and when Quentin didn't take it, drank from it himself. "Your injunction arrives in"—he checked a plain wristwatch—"forty minutes. My lawyers will read it, find it airtight, and advise me that continuing costs me more than withdrawing. I will withdraw. The barge turns south by evening." He said it all lightly, a man reciting tomorrow's weather. "But understand something, Coldharbour, before you stand on that dock tonight and let them applaud you. I never wanted the gold."

"The gold is real."

"The gold is heavy." Voss set down his cup. "Gold sits in a vault and appreciates at a rate I could earn before lunch. Letters, though. Letters are leverage. Undelivered mail means unfinished business, and unfinished business means claims—estates unprobated, names unwitnessed, apologies that certain families would pay a great deal to bury or to hear. I've sold worse things to better people." He smiled, thin and correct. "So no, I'm not angry. I'm curious. You're holding forty years of other people's unfinished business in a fishing skiff. The question I sailed two bays to see answered—" and here he leaned forward, and for one moment the pleasantness fell away and something old and tired and honest looked out of his face like a lamp through fog— "is what you'll do with yours."

Quentin's hand went, without permission, to their ribs—to the flat oilcloth bundle strapped beneath the suit, to the last flight log of Elias Coldharbour, to the final line written in a dying man's hand that Quentin had read by dive-light sixty feet underwater with the sea closing overhead:

*Tell Q the tide was always going to turn. Tell him I stayed so he wouldn't have to run.*

"I know what I'll do with mine," Quentin said.

Voss studied them a moment longer, then nodded once, as if a figure had come out right. "Yes," he said. "I rather think you do. That's the difference between us, and it took me twenty years and this whole coast to learn it." He rose, extended a hand, and when Quentin didn't take it, withdrew it gracefully. "The barge will be gone by dark. Give my regards to Mrs. Okonkwo. She was kind to my mother once, years ago, on this same coast. Neither of us ever forgot a kindness here. We only forgot how to ask for one."

* * *

The injunction arrived at 11:52, eight minutes ahead of schedule, carried up the dock steps by two exhausted mainland lawyers in city shoes that slipped on wet planks, and Ede Okonkwo met them at the top like a general receiving dispatches she had personally ordered and personally expected.

By then the flotilla had closed ranks around the skiff, and the skiff had reached the dock, and the mail sacks were coming ashore hand to hand—Rourke to Halvorsen to the water-taxi captain to three teenagers including, prominently, one soaked sixteen-year-old who refused all offers to go home and change. The sacks passed down the human chain the way buckets pass at a fire, and nobody spoke much, and everybody touched them, briefly, the way you touch a railing of a house you grew up in.

Quentin stood at the end of the chain and handed each sack to the next pair of waiting arms and did not let go of any of them one second longer than necessary. Except the last. The last one they carried themselves, small and flat, and set down apart from the others on the dock boards, and covered again with their own jacket.

Wren came and stood beside them, dripping, shivering, radiant. "You went back in for me," the kid said.

"You'd have gone back in for me."

"Yeah, but I'm sixteen, I'm basically indestructible." Wren bumped their shoulder against Quentin's arm. "You spent seven years making sure you never owed anybody anything. And then you swam into a collapsing wreck for me. That's math I want to see the work on."

Quentin looked out at the harbor—at the barge already swinging its bow south, at the workboats falling in behind it like ducklings, at Voss's cruiser standing off, patient, watching, taking its last measurement of them all—and at the town spread along the waterfront with its lit windows beginning, one by one, to show faces.

"There's one more delivery," Quentin said. "It's not in the sacks."

Wren followed their gaze down to the jacket-covered bundle. "Whose is it?"

Quentin crouched, lifted the corner of the jacket, and let the kid see the oilcloth, and the string, and the name block-lettered on the tag in handwriting Wren had seen a dozen times now—in the ledger margins, on the old survey maps, on the hangar wall where three names were destined for paint.

"It's addressed to me," Quentin said. "And I think—" Their voice caught, and they let it. On this dock, in front of this town, with the tide turning clean and cold below them, they found they were done hiding the sound. "I think it's time to open it. Will you sit with me while I do?"

Wren sat down on the wet boards without a word. Above them the gulls wheeled, the ferry horn sounded, and Ede Okonkwo came down the dock steps slowly, carrying the ledger against her chest like a heart she had finally gotten back, and stopped a few feet away, and waited—she, who had waited seven years—with her hands folded and her eyes bright, for the tide to finish giving back what the water had borrowed.

Quentin untied the string.
