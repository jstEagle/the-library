## 20. Voss Counts His Losses

The barge came around the headland at slack water, riding low and patient as a whale that had decided to die there. Voss stood at the rail with his hands folded behind his back and watched the flotilla form up in the narrows—fourteen boats by his count, then sixteen, fishing skiffs and a gillnetter and someone's ancient cabin cruiser with a church banner strung across its wheelhouse. They were not armed. They were not even angry, most of them. They were simply *there*, arranged across the water like punctuation, and behind them the injunction lawyers were already ashore in Ede Okonkwo's kitchen, no doubt drinking her tea and filing papers on her tablecloth.

His operations manager, Brandt, came up beside him holding a tablet against the wind.

"Salvage counsel says we're done," Brandt said. "Judge granted the stay an hour ago. Heritage designation pending on the wreck site. We can't touch it without triggering federal review, and federal review means eighteen months of—"

"I can read the weather, Brandt."

Brandt shut up. He was good at his job mostly because he knew when to shut up.

Voss looked out over the water. The morning was the kind the coast did so well it had become a cliché: grey light laid flat on grey water, the headland a smudge of black-green, gulls hanging motionless in the updraft off the cliffs as if pinned there. Somewhere beneath that flat pewter surface, forty feet down in a cave that did not exist on any chart, sat a mail plane he had spent eleven years and four fortunes learning how to find.

He had lost. That was the arithmetic of the morning, and Voss did not flinch from arithmetic; it was the only language he had ever trusted. Assets: one salvage vessel, three months of charter, a dive team now sitting in the galley playing cards, a fuel bill that would make a small nation wince. Liabilities: all of the above, plus a reputation that would survive this—the coast forgave ambition faster than it forgave failure, and Voss had never failed publicly before—but which would carry a new asterisk. And gains?

He turned it over the way he turned everything over, looking for the hidden weight.

"Glasses," he said.

* * *

They drank the concession whiskey on the afterdeck, because Voss believed losses should be acknowledged in the open air where the wind could take some of them. He poured two fingers each into heavy tumblers and sent Brandt ashore in the tender with a message for Quentin Coldharbour: *Mr. Voss requests the pleasure. No tricks. Bring whoever you like.*

He half hoped they'd bring the girl. Wren. He'd watched her through binoculars twice now—once standing on the dock with her arms crossed like a foreman, once aloft in Coldharbour's beat-up amphibian doing something with the rudder that made the old girl crab sideways through a gust in a way that was either terrible piloting or genius, and Voss genuinely could not tell which. There was a hunger in the way she moved around aircraft that he recognized the way you recognize a scar pattern. He'd been that kid once. Before the ledgers got him.

Quentin came out alone.

Of course he did. Voss watched the floatplane cross the bay and thought: *of course.* Seven years of flying alone, living alone, landing alone. A man who treated other people like weather—something to be monitored, endured, occasionally flown around. Voss knew the type intimately. For a long time he had been the type.

Coldharbour stepped onto the deck with the particular stiffness of a man who has rehearsed hostility and found the script insufficient. He was soaked to the waist still, or had been recently—there was salt dried white along the seams of his trousers, and his hands had the raw look of hands that had been in cold water longer than cold water permits.

"You cut it close," Voss said, offering the glass. "The tide yesterday. Another twenty minutes and I'd have been reading about two drownings instead of one successful theft."

"It wasn't a theft."

"No?" Voss smiled and let it sit. "Then let's call it salvage. Everyone else does. It's the same act either way—taking what the sea surrendered and calling the taking righteous. I've built a career on the distinction. It's a very comfortable distinction." He raised his glass slightly. "To the Meridian Star. She was a good ship. Better than the man who flew her into the ground, maybe. Or worse. Depends on the day."

Quentin took the glass but didn't drink. "You're being very calm about losing."

"Am I losing?"

"You brought a barge. You brought divers. You burned—" Quentin gestured at the whole apparatus of him, the cranes and compressors and the satellite dish bolted to the wheelhouse roof, "—all of this. And you're leaving with nothing."

"Mm." Voss walked to the rail. Below them the flotilla held its line, engines idling, and he could see faces turned up toward the barge—not hostile, exactly. Watchful. The coast had decided how this story went, and the coast's version had him cast as the weather: large, impersonal, ultimately survivable. "Do you know what I actually came here for, Coldharbour?"

"The gold. Half a million in mint coin, if Ede's ledger is accurate. I've seen your manifests."

"The gold, yes. On paper, yes. But gold is just money that takes effort, and effort is the one expense I never stop auditing." He sipped. The whiskey was decent; he'd stopped buying exceptional things years ago because exceptional things made people sentimental. "I'll tell you something, and you may believe it or not. I knew about the letters before I ever anchored in this bay."

Something moved behind Quentin's eyes—a quick dark flicker, like a fish under ice.

"Eleven years ago," Voss went on, "I bought a defunct airfreight company in Vancouver. Assets, liabilities, the usual corpse. Among the liabilities was a stack of insurance claims from a storm season thirty years back—one of those coast-wide blows that scattered communities like seed. Whole families relocated, mail service collapsed, undelivered sacks warehoused and forgotten. One last flight was chartered to clear the backlog. The Meridian Star. Pilot named Coldharbour." He watched Quentin's jaw tighten. "The claim file included the cargo manifest. Coin in the hold, yes. But also ninety-one pounds of personal correspondence. Letters that never landed. And here's the thing that made me sit up at my desk at one in the morning like a man who'd seen a ghost: the insurance company had written the letters off as *total loss*. Not delayed. Not recoverable. Total. Which meant somewhere in that paperwork was a signature authorizing their destruction—and a reason for the signature. I've spent eleven years finding out whose."

"And?"

"And I found the cave before you did, Coldharbour. Six years ago. I had a survey team run the coast with side-scan sonar under cover of a fisheries contract. They flagged an anomaly in this bay—an intrusion in the limestone, tide-dependent, uncharted. I filed it away. I could have come for the plane then." He swirled the whiskey, watching the light fail to get through it. "I didn't."

"Why not?"

"Because I ran the numbers on the letters, and the numbers said they were worthless. Ninety-one pounds of paper. No market. No collector pays for other families' grief—you can't display it, can't liquidate it, can't leverage it. The gold was the asset. The letters were ballast. I came for the coin and I planned, from the very first schedule I drafted, to leave the sacks in the hull." He shrugged. "Ballast keeps a wreck stable. Let the paper rot where it lay."

Quentin stared at him. "You're lying."

"Almost certainly men have told you that before, in worse circumstances. But ask yourself—if I'm lying, why am I telling you? What do I gain? The injunction stands whether you believe me or not. The flotilla stays whether you believe me or not. I have nothing left to buy here except, apparently, an honest conversation, which is the one currency this coast doesn't accept." He set his glass down on the rail cap with a small precise click. "So. Believe me or don't. Here's what I actually know, and then I'll be gone by tomorrow's tide."

* * *

Voss had grown up in a house where love arrived as inventory.

His father ran a marine chandlery in a harbour town not unlike this one—smaller, meaner, further north—and kept the books the way other men kept diaries. Voss learned to read columns before he learned to read stories. By nine he could reconcile a ledger. By twelve he understood that his mother's illness appeared in the accounts as a recurring debit labeled *medical, misc.*, and that when the debit grew too large his father began selling things quietly: first the good china, then the boat, then, one grey Tuesday without discussion, the piano. Voss had come home and the corner where the piano stood was just a rectangle of unfaded wallpaper, and nobody mentioned it, and dinner happened, and the ledger balanced.

That was the lesson, and he had taken it whole: *everything is countable, and whatever cannot be counted will be taken from you anyway, so learn to count.*

He had left that town at nineteen with a duffel bag and his father's second-best adding machine, and he had never once gone back, and he had built a life in which every attachment was priced on entry. Ships, contracts, crews—he hired well and paid fairly and felt nothing, because feeling was the debit that never reconciled. People asked him, sometimes, usually late and drunk, why he did it. Why the accumulating. And he had a stock answer—*because I'm good at it*—which was true the way a mask is true.

But there was another answer, older, that he kept below the waterline where cargo like that belongs: once, when he was fourteen, his mother had written him a letter. He'd been away at school for eight months, hating it, starving in ways the meal plan couldn't fix, and one afternoon an envelope came in her handwriting, and he held it for a full hour before opening it because he already knew what it would cost him to read it. It said, among other things, *come home when you can, the water's high this spring, you'd laugh at your father arguing with the tide tables.* Ordinary words. Devastating ones. He still had it. It lived in a fireproof box with his passports and title documents, filed between assets like everything else, and in thirty years he had taken it out perhaps six times, and every time he put it back feeling robbed.

That was what letters were. He had known it since he was fourteen. Ninety-one pounds of them, sunk in a cold cave—that wasn't ballast. That was ninety-one pounds of the only thing on earth that could make a man like him feel robbed.

And that was why, six years ago, having found the anomaly, having drafted the schedules, having the barge nearly booked—he had closed the folder and let it sleep. Because some part of him understood, in the wordless way he understood balance sheets, that if he opened that hull he would eventually have to open the sacks, and somewhere in ninety-one pounds of other families' grief there would be a letter addressed to a boy who never came home, and Voss did not trust himself with that arithmetic. So he'd left it. Filed under *anomaly, unresolved*. The only entry in thirty years of ledgering that he had deliberately refused to total.

Until Coldharbour flew over the cave mouth and the whole account reopened itself.

* * *

"So here is what I want you to understand," Voss said aloud, because the silence between them had gone on long enough and Quentin Coldharbour was not a man who filled silences for other people's comfort. "I didn't lose to you. I want to be precise about this, because precision matters to me more than almost anything. I lost to a decision. Yours."

"I didn't decide anything. I just—"

"You surrendered a claim worth, conservatively, five hundred thousand dollars, to a community you spent seven years refusing to join, in order to deliver mail that is legally worthless. Do you know what I would have done with that claim?" He didn't wait for an answer. "I'd have sold it. Quietly, through three intermediaries, to a buyer who'd never surface, and I'd be on a plane tonight with the number in a Cayman account and no forwarding address. That was always the exit. That was the entire design of my life—acquire, convert, vanish. Freedom is just distance with good accounting." He looked at Quentin steadily. "You had the same door. Same coordinates. Same lock. And you didn't take it."

Quentin's hand had gone to his breast pocket—reflex, unconscious—and Voss saw it and understood, and something in his chest shifted like ballast settling.

"There's a letter in there for you," Voss said quietly. "In those sacks. I'd bet the barge on it. I've known since I read the manifest—the pilot requested a private pouch be added at the last minute, unsigned authorization, the kind of thing that gets a man noticed. Whatever's in that wreck that matters to you, Coldharbour, it isn't the coin, and it isn't even the history. It's one envelope with your name on it. Am I right?"

Quentin said nothing, which was answer enough.

"When I was young," Voss said, "a letter reached me once, at the exact moment it could have changed what I became. And I filed it. Put it in a box with my documents, between the passport and the deeds, and told myself it was safe there, which is a lie men tell about the things they're afraid of." He picked up his glass again, and this time he drank, properly, and set it down empty. "You were handed the same choice I had. Keep the past in a box, or give it away and see what grows. I chose the box. Thirty years later I own four ships and speak to no one at Christmas." He gestured, a small economical motion, at the flotilla, the lighthouse, the smoke rising from chimneys in the town that had turned out in sixteen boats for a man who used to buy his fuel there and leave before anyone learned his name. "You chose the other thing. And watching it happen from this rail has been—" He stopped. Recalibrated. Voss did not say things like *the most interesting*, or *moving*, or any word that admitted the ledger had a column for this. "—instructive," he finished.

"That's it? You're just leaving?"

"I'm leaving at tomorrow's ebb. My crew gets paid through the month regardless—they earned it, they worked clean, and clean work should always pay. And I'm releasing my survey data on this bay to the heritage registry. Charts, sonar imagery, tide models. The site will be protected properly. Consider it—" he almost smiled, "—a write-down. Sometimes you clear a loss off the books and the ledger breathes easier."

Quentin stood there a moment longer, and Voss watched him try to fit all of it into the categories he'd built for men like Voss—predator, vulture, broker—and fail, and Voss felt no triumph in that. Only the strange lightness of a man setting down a case he'd carried so long his shoulder had grown around its shape.

"One more thing," Voss said, as Quentin turned to go. "The girl. Wren."

"What about her?"

"She flies like someone being chased. You'd know the style." He let that land. "Whatever she's running from, it'll catch her someday, the way yours caught you. When it does—don't let her solve it the way I solved mine. Counting isn't living, Coldharbour. I'm the proof. I'm the proof with four ships."

* * *

He watched the floatplane lift off an hour later, climbing hard into the grey, banking once over the flotilla—over Ede's lighthouse with its fresh paint, over the dock where the whole town seemed to have assembled for no reason anyone would ever put on a manifest—and heading inland toward the cave.

Voss stayed at the rail until the aircraft was a speck, then a rumor, then gone.

Brandt appeared beside him with the departure checklist. "Tide turns at oh-four-forty. We'll be clear of the headland by dawn."

"Good."

"Sir—" Brandt hesitated. In eleven years Voss could count Brandt's hesitations on one hand. "There's a question going around the crew. About why we're really walking away. Some of them think there's a play we haven't seen yet."

"There is a play," Voss said. "We're watching it." He turned from the rail and looked, for the last time, at the town—at the lights coming on early against the grey, at the hangar where a boy was probably teaching a bush pilot something about belonging, at the lighthouse that had waited seven years for a question and finally gotten it. "Tell the galley to break out the good beef tonight. We lost to the only thing on this coast I couldn't have bought at any price." He paused at the wheelhouse door, and the wind took his voice and gave it back smaller. "A home."

Below decks, in the fireproof box in his cabin, between the passport and the ship's articles, a thirty-year-old envelope waited in its sleeve, corners soft with handling, and for the first time since Vancouver Voss found himself calculating—not profit, not tides, not tonnage—but hours. How many hours to the nearest post office that could trace a dead woman's forwarding address. How many hours until morning.
