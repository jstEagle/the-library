## 21. Deliveries

The letters came ashore on a Tuesday, in the hour when the tide was so far out the harbour floor lay bare and shining like a coin someone had dropped.

Wren watched from the hangar roof, where they'd dragged a crate to sit on and a thermos of Ede's chicory coffee to hold. Below them, the whole town had turned out—not the way towns turn out for disasters, all noise and elbows, but the other way, the quiet way, the way people gather when something sacred is about to be handled. The mail sacks sat stacked on a trestle table borrowed from the church hall, still stiff with salt, still smelling of the cave: cold stone and old iron and, beneath it all, faintly, impossibly, paper.

Forty-one years those letters had waited. Wren did the math again because they'd done it every night since the recovery—forty-one years longer than Wren had been alive—and it still didn't fit inside their head. The Meridian Star went down when Ede Okonkwo was younger than Wren was now. Some of the senders were certainly dead. Some of the addressees too. And yet here it all was, drying on lines strung between the hangar beams like laundry, page after page of handwriting going soft and brown at the edges, held flat under clothespins while the wind off the water moved through them like it was reading over everyone's shoulder.

Quentin stood at the table with the manifest, calling names.

Not many words, as usual. Just the name, and a pause, and whoever the name belonged to coming forward through the crowd like a swimmer through surf. But Wren had learned to read Quentin's silences the way other people read weather, and there was something different in this one. Something almost gentle. Each time Quentin handed a bundle across that table—the last thing their father ever carried, given away piece by piece—they stood straighter, as if setting down weight.

* * *

The first bundle went to the Aldous family.

Old Man Aldous came up in his good coat, the one he wore to funerals, and when Quentin said "Aldous, Thomas, sender," the old man's hands shook so badly his daughter had to steady them. The letter was from his brother, lost in the interior since before the storm, presumed gone north. Forty-one years of not knowing, folded into three pages.

He didn't open it at the table. He carried it back through the crowd holding it out from his body with both hands, the way you carry a lamp through wind, and he sat down on the seawall and just looked at it for a long time before he broke the seal.

Wren couldn't hear what he read. But they saw his shoulders change. Saw him press the pages flat against his chest like a compress against a wound, and his daughter knelt beside him on the wet stone and didn't ask, and the tide kept sliding further out, patient as anything.

* * *

By midmorning the table was half empty and the stories were multiplying faster than the deliveries.

A woman named Corinne Baptiste found a letter from a fiancé she'd been told had abandoned her—drowned himself rather than marry, the town had whispered for four decades—and instead the letter said what it actually said, which was that he'd taken work up-coast to buy her a ring and been caught in the storm on the way home. She read it standing, then sitting, then lying down on a bench with her hat over her face while her granddaughters fanned her with junk mail. When she finally spoke, her voice came out scraped clean. "He didn't leave me." That was all. "He never left me."

An envelope reached the Petraglia brothers, who hadn't spoken to each other in nine years over some argument nobody could anymore remember the shape of, only its cost. They arrived separately, on opposite sides of the square, each pretending not to see the other. The letter was from their mother, written the week of the storm, asking them both—for once, asking them plainly—to stop being fools. They ended up reading it together, shoulder to shoulder, because neither could let go of an edge of it, and by the end the elder one was laughing and the younger one was crying and neither would say afterward which of them was which.

Word traveled up the coast road all afternoon. Cars came in from the next inlet. A man arrived by boat with his two sons and read a letter from his grandfather aloud right there on the dock, voice carrying over the water, and nobody minded, because that was the strange arithmetic of the day: the more the letters were shared, the more there seemed to be of them.

* * *

Ede Okonkwo worked the table beside Quentin all morning, ledger open, checking each bundle against entries made in her own young hand forty-one years ago. Her penmanship hadn't changed. Wren noticed that—same narrow upright letters, same little hooks—and thought it was like seeing the same lighthouse photographed twice, decades apart, still standing in the same weather.

It was Ede who insisted on order. Sender first, then addressee, then date. It was Ede who caught the mislabeled bundle before it walked off in the wrong pocket. And it was Ede who, near noon, set down her pen and looked out at the crowd and said, quietly enough that only Wren and Quentin heard it:

"I used to think my job was keeping the light burning."

She turned a page. The ledger's spine cracked like a knuckle.

"But it was this. All along. I just didn't know the light could wait this long to be delivered."

* * *

The dockhand came at low afternoon sun.

Marek Solano—he'd been in town eleven months, big quiet man with rope-burned forearms and a way of watching everything sideways, and it was Marek who'd drained Quentin's tanks and slashed the pontoon for Voss's money, and Marek who'd brought Voss's tide schedule back across the line the moment he understood what was sleeping in that cave. He'd spent the days since the injunction working twice as hard as anyone, hauling and winching and saying nothing, paying in labor what he couldn't pay any other way.

Now he stood at the edge of the crowd with his cap in his fists, and Wren watched him not move for a long time. Watched him want to come forward and be unable to. Watched Quentin look up from the manifest, find him in the crowd without searching—which meant Quentin had known he'd come, had maybe saved it for now—and say the name into the hush:

"Solano. Inés. Sender."

The crowd did not part so much as make room, the way water makes room.

Marek crossed the boards like a man walking a gangway in bad weather. At the table his voice failed him twice. "My mother," he managed. "She wrote to her sister. Before—" He stopped. Everyone knew the rest. Before the family scattered. Before the sister took the children east and the letters stopped and the silence calcified into a kind of law.

Quentin slid the bundle across. Didn't say anything. Wren had learned that about Quentin: they knew exactly when words would help and when they'd only get in the way.

Marek held the packet a moment. Then, instead of retreating with it the way the others had, he did something no one expected. He untied the string right there. He drew out the single folded page. And he unfolded it against the trestle and began, in his rough accented voice, to read aloud.

*"My Leta—"*

His voice cracked on the second word and he steadied it and went on.

*"My Leta. By the time this reaches you the storm will have taken the road, and maybe by then it will have taken my courage too, so I am writing tonight while I still have both..."*

The square went absolutely still. Even the gulls seemed to bank away to listen. Marek read his dead-or-gone mother's words to her estranged sister in front of the whole town, and the words were ordinary words—about the garden, about the price of flour, about how the boy Marek had lost a tooth and buried it under the fig tree so it would grow another—but they landed like scripture, because they proved what everyone secretly feared was untrue: that love keeps writing even after the mail stops, that it goes on addressing envelopes to people who may never read them, faithfully, absurdly, the way a lighthouse keeps turning.

At the end his mother had written: *"Tell Marek the fig tree grew. Tell him I never doubted him. Not for one day."*

Marek lowered the page. His eyes were wet and he didn't hide it. Then he laughed—a short, broken, astonished sound—and said, mostly to himself, "I buried that tooth. I remember. I remember the fig tree."

And Wren, up on the hangar roof with their cold coffee, felt something turn over in their chest like a plane coming about.

* * *

The light went long and gold across the emptied harbour. The tide had begun its return, creeping up the mudflats in silver veins, reclaiming inch by inch what it lent out twice a day. One by one the bundles left the table until only a few remained, and the crowd thinned into clusters on the seawall, trading fragments of what they'd witnessed, building the day already into story.

Wren climbed down and drifted toward the table, the way they drifted toward everything Quentin-related these days—sideways, pretending otherwise.

The last bundle sat alone in the center of the boards.

No label. No entry in the manifest beyond a dash and a question mark in Ede's hand. Wren knew what it was before Quentin touched it. Had known for days, honestly, since the sacks came up out of the dark and Quentin went very still over this one packet, wrapped in oilcloth inside the oilcloth, tied with wire instead of string.

"You could keep it," Wren said. Their voice came out smaller than they intended. "Nobody would—I mean, it's yours. Legally. Emotionally. Probably every -ally there is."

Quentin turned the packet over once. The wire glinted.

"For seven years I thought if I got far enough away, nothing could reach me." Quentin's thumb traced the edge of the oilcloth. "Turns out the thing that reaches you can sit at the bottom of the ocean the whole time. Distance doesn't do what I thought it did."

"So don't give it back."

"I'm not giving it back." Quentin looked up, and in the slanting evening light Wren saw something they'd never seen on that face—not grief exactly, and not peace either, but the ground between them, newly plowed. "I'm delivering it."

"To who?"

Quentin glanced across the square, to where Ede stood at the seawall with the ledger closed at last under her arm, watching the water come home.

"To everybody," Quentin said. "That was always who it belonged to."

But they didn't take it to Ede. Not yet. They untied the wire themselves, right there at the trestle, with Wren close enough to breathe the smell of the cave rising off it—stone and iron and patience—and drew out a small book bound in stained canvas. A flight log. The logbook. Wren had heard the story of the cockpit, of the rising water, of the ninety seconds Quentin had spent being braver than seven years of running; everyone had heard it by now, told and retold until it had already gone smooth around the edges.

Quentin opened it to the last written page.

They didn't read it aloud. They read it with their lips moving slightly, and Wren watched their face the whole time—the way you watch a sky you've been flying under your whole life suddenly change—and what crossed it wasn't pain. It was something older than relief and quieter than joy. It was the look of a person being forgiven across forty-one years and seven thousand miles of running, all at once, by ink.

Then Quentin closed the book, and held it out.

Not to Ede. To Wren.

"Log says the route, the weather, the load. Everything a pilot needs to fly it again someday." Quentin's voice was level but their eyes weren't, quite. "You're the pilot in this town who hasn't decided yet what she's afraid of. Seems like it should live with you."

Wren took it with both hands. The canvas was cold from the deep and heavy past its size, the way true things are.

"I don't—" they started.

"You do," Quentin said. "That's the whole point. It's not treasure. It never was. It's a flight plan." A breath. "Finish the delivery."

Across the square, Ede raised one hand—not waving, beckoning—and the last light came down the water like a runway lighting itself, and somewhere out past the headland the tide turned for the night, and Wren understood that tomorrow there would be a flight, and that it would begin here, on foot, with a book passed hand to hand, the way everything worth carrying always is.
