## 22. Home Field

The morning fog had not yet lifted off the harbour when Quentin Coldharbour rolled the Cessna out of the hangar and into the pale light, and for the first time in seven years, the act of rolling an airplane out felt less like departure and more like opening a door.

The air smelled of salt and spruce resin and, faintly, of Ede's woodsmoke drifting down from the light station on the point. The harbour was doing its slow morning arithmetic—gulls counting, water counting, the tide sliding out with the patience of something that knew it would be back. Quentin stood with one hand flat against the cold aluminum of the fuselage and let all of it arrive: the sound, the smell, the particular grey-gold quality of a coastal dawn that they had once treated as scenery to be escaped and now understood to be home.

Seven months. That was how long it had been since the cave. Since the ebb tide that had given up the Meridian Star and then, like a thing embarrassed by its own generosity, taken the wreck back under. Seven months since Quentin had surfaced coughing beside Wren, both of them alive in a way that felt newly invented, while the town's flotilla held its ragged line between them and Voss's barge.

Seven months of landing here.

Quentin still flew the medicine run. Same route, same grey interior, same gravel bars and logging strips and the little settlement at Tidewater Bend where Mrs. Aldous waited every second Tuesday with her list of needs written in pencil on the back of a calendar page. The route hadn't changed. What changed was the last leg. There used to be no last leg—there was only onward, the next strip, the next bay, the next place far enough away that nobody could learn your face. Now there was always the turn for home, the headland with the white tower on it, the long glide down over the harbour with the whole town visible and unhidden from.

Wren came around the corner of the hangar at a jog, hair tied back, flight bag over one shoulder, sixteen going on seventeen and carrying it like a co-pilot carries hours—proudly, and not quite believing it yet.

"You said seven," Wren said, breathless. "It's seven-oh-four."

"The plane doesn't care."

"I care." Wren dropped the bag, ran a preflight with the speed of someone who'd done it four hundred times now, and paused at the strut the way they always did—a two-second touch, palm to metal, a superstition borrowed from Ede and defended as science. "Mrs. Aldous's insulin is in the cooler. I checked it twice."

"Then we're ahead of schedule." Quentin swung up into the left seat, and Wren took the right one, because that was where Wren sat now, had sat for five months, ever since Ede had looked at the two of them across her kitchen table and said, in the tone she used for tides and other unavoidable facts, *that child is going to fly whether you teach her or someone worse does.*

The engine caught on the second try. Quentin taxied out past the dock, past the fish boats, past Halvorsen's skiff where the dockhand—Marek, whose name had finally settled into the town's mouth like a stone finding its place in a wall—lifted a hand without stopping his work. Not a wave exactly. An acknowledgment. It was enough. It had taken Marek three months to stop flinching when Quentin's shadow crossed the dock, and another month after that before he'd read his mother's letter aloud at the end of the pier, voice cracking on the last line, the whole harbour pretending not to listen and everyone listening.

They climbed out over the water, banked south around the point, and the lighthouse flashed once through the thinning fog—Ede's signal, the old greeting, one pulse meaning *I see you; you are seen.* Quentin tipped a wing in answer, as they did every time, and heard Wren laugh softly in the right seat.

"One day I'm going to get her to tell me why one flash means that," Wren said.

"She'll tell you when she tells you. Asking just moves the date."

The interior swallowed them the way it always did—grey ridges, brown rivers braiding and rebraiding, mist pooled in the valleys like milk nobody had stirred. Quentin flew it by memory now instead of by avoidance, which was a different kind of flying entirely. The same mountains. But the mountains were no longer a wall behind which a person could disappear. They were just mountains, beautiful and indifferent, and Quentin could look at them without looking for exits.

At Tidewater Bend, Mrs. Aldous was waiting on the gravel strip with her calendar-page list and a jar of crabapple jelly "for the pilot," which meant for both of them, because Mrs. Aldous had decided some things were not negotiable.

"You look thinner," she told Wren, pressing the jar into their hands.

"I look exactly the same."

"That's what thin looks like at your age. Tell Ede I want my recipe back."

In the right seat on the way home, Wren worked through the logbook with a pencil stub, adding the leg, the time, the landing count. Wren kept the books now. Had insisted on it. *You log everything,* Ede had said once, *because everything you do to the airplane you're also doing to yourself, and it's good to have it written down somewhere honest.*

"Sixteen forty-two total," Wren announced. "For me. You're—" the pencil moved, stopped, moved again. "Huh."

"What?"

"You logged the same number last month. Hours flown this season. We're tied."

"We are not tied. I've been flying since before you were born."

"In hours this season, though." Wren held up the book, grinning. "Tied."

Quentin took the corner around the headland a little faster than strictly necessary, mostly so Wren would have to hold on to the yoke and stop smiling quite so much. It didn't work. Nothing worked on that smile. It was the smile of a person who had stowed away in a cargo bay once and been found and, instead of being put ashore and forgotten, had been kept.

The harbour came up gold-grey beneath them. The hangar roof. The dock. Two planes now inside that hangar—the Cessna and the old Piper Cub the town had dragged out of Halvorsen's barn and rebuilt over the winter, all of them, a dozen pairs of hands, because Wren needed a trainer and the town had apparently decided that what Wren needed, Wren got. On the hangar's inside wall, freshly painted in the spring, three names in Ede's careful hand:

*COLDHARBOUR. OKONKWO. WREN.*

Not surnames. Just names, the way a crew signs a ship. Quentin had watched Ede paint them, standing in the doorway saying nothing, feeling something in the chest go loose and strange, like a knot finally untied by someone else's patient fingers.

The float touched down soft, the way it did now, most days, because Quentin had stopped landing like a person leaving and started landing like a person arriving, and it turned out the hands knew the difference even when the heart was slow to catch up.

* * *

Evening came down over the harbour the way it did in late summer—slow, unhurried, the light lying down on the water like a tired animal. Ede cooked for three without being asked, because she had been cooking for three for months now and the asking had become unnecessary, which was its own kind of language.

They ate on the gallery above the light station: chowder, bread, the last of Mrs. Aldous's jelly on everything. Below them the harbour turned amber, then copper, then the deep bruised blue of a coast putting itself to bed. The Meridian Star's mail sacks were gone now—delivered, every bundle, every letter, the work of a whole autumn—but the story of them lived in the town the way stories do, retold at the dock, embroidered, argued over, until the wreck itself had become less history than inheritance.

"Do you remember," Ede said, ladling chowder with the gravity of a woman administering medicine, "the first time you came to my door? Seven years you lived on this coast, and in all that time you never once knocked."

"I knocked."

"You *tapped*. Like a bird that might change its mind." She set the bowl down in front of Quentin. "I had the kettle on for years, waiting. Waste of good firewood, all that boiling."

"And now?"

Ede sat, folded her hands, considered the horizon with the expression of a woman who had spent fifty years reading water and had earned the right to read anything else she pleased.

"Now the kettle's never off," she said. "That's what it costs. That's what it gives."

Wren, mouth full, said: "Voss sent a letter. Came on the supply boat Tuesday."

Quentin's spoon stopped.

"He did?"

"Asks how the Cub's engine rebuild is coming. Asks if we need parts. Says he has 'a line on a good carburetor.'" Wren shrugged. "He does this now. Every couple of months. Polite little letters, like a man checking on an investment he lost on purpose."

"What did you write back?"

"Nothing yet." Wren looked at Ede, then at Quentin, and there was something older than seventeen in the look. "What do we write back?"

Quentin thought about Voss—about the man who counted wrecks as assets and coasts as inventory, who had stood on his barge that last day and conceded with unsettling grace, who had said, *I wanted to see what you'd choose,* as if Quentin's choice were the final entry in a ledger Voss himself had once abandoned. A dark mirror. A man who had chosen the fleet, the schedule, the arithmetic, and had spent decades watching the water to see if anyone would ever choose differently.

"Tell him the carburetor's handled," Quentin said slowly. "And tell him—" They stopped. Looked out at the light beginning its evening rotation below them, steady as a heartbeat. "Tell him the kettle's on. If he's ever passing."

Ede made a small sound that might have been approval or indigestion; with Ede it was often both.

"That," she said, "is either very generous or very dangerous."

"It's probably both too."

* * *

Later, alone in the hangar, Quentin stood before the wall with the three names and opened the logbook on the workbench.

The last page of the old logbook—the father's logbook, recovered from the cockpit, salt-stained, half the ink ghosted but the final lines legible—was tucked in a plastic sleeve at the back of Quentin's own book, where it had lived since the night Quentin first read it. The last line still did what it always did. It still landed like a float touching water.

*If you're reading this, Q—come home. The flying was never the running. Come home, and land.*

Below it, in Quentin's own hand, fresh ink, tonight's entry. And Quentin noticed—as if noticing for the first time—that the entry had changed shape without any decision having been made. No miles. For months now, no miles. Just hours.

*Sept 14. Two legs, both smooth. Insulin delivered, jelly acquired. Student soloed the bay circuit—clean, a little showy, will be a better pilot than me. Kettle on at Ede's.*

Quentin capped the pen. Stood a moment in the lamplight with the names on the wall and the planes quiet in the dark and the tide turning outside, coming in, faithful, unhurried, certain of its welcome.

Hours, not miles. Arrival, at last, without leaving.

Outside, far down the channel, a single running light appeared—small, steady, moving toward the harbour through the dusk. Too big for a fishing boat. Too slow to be in a hurry. Quentin watched it a moment longer, pen still in hand, and wrote one more line before closing the book:

*Something coming up-channel. We'll know by morning.*
