## 1. Six Fathoms of Quiet

The sea off Tanah Merah was the color of old pewter that morning, and Cyrus Kavanagh loved it the way you love something that has never once loved you back.

He was in the water before six, alone, which was how he preferred it. The Perdita rode at anchor four hundred meters astern, her engine ticking as it cooled, Devi's voice already in his earpiece with the flat cadence of a woman reading numbers she trusted more than people.

"Bottom time starts now. Depth call every five minutes. You know the drill."

"I wrote the drill," Cyrus said.

"Then say it back to me like you respect it."

He smiled behind his regulator, a small private thing no one could see. "Depth call every five minutes. Ascent rate under ten meters a minute. Safety stop at three for three. Any current over half a knot, I come home."

"Good boy," Devi said, and cut the channel to standby.

The wreck sat in nineteen meters of green-grey water, a decommissioned coastal patrol boat the Malaysian maritime authority had scuttled two years back as an artificial reef. Officially. Unofficially, someone had forgotten to strip her engine room properly, and the harbor authority wanted an inventory before the salvage scrappers came through with their torches and their indifference. The Perdita took these jobs because they were legal, boring, and paid just enough to keep diesel in the tanks and rice in Auntie Bee's galley. Ten years ago Cyrus would have found the work insulting. Now he found it restful, and he had stopped asking himself what that said about him.

He descended along the mooring line hand over hand, letting the water close over him in layers—warm at the surface, then cooler, then that deep neutral cold that felt less like temperature than like silence given weight. At eight meters he equalized without thinking about it, the way he breathed, the way he checked his gauges: left, right, console, contents. Left, right, console, contents. His body ran the sequence on its own clockwork, ten years of repetition worn so smooth it had become a kind of prayer.

Below him the patrol boat materialized out of the murk bow-first, listing gently to port, her lines still crisp under the soft grey fur of new growth. Fish had already claimed her. A school of fusiliers poured through the wheelhouse windows like smoke running backward, and a fat grouper—the same grouper from last month's survey, or one exactly like her, or maybe they all looked alike out here—hovered near the anchor winch with the territorial patience of an old landlord.

Cyrus hung there for a moment and let the scene settle into him.

This was the part nobody understood when he tried to explain it, back when anyone bothered to ask. People thought diving was about adventure, adrenaline, treasure. It wasn't. Diving was about the quiet. Down here there were no debts, no contracts, no names spoken in low voices in harbour bars. There was a task list and a gas supply and a bottom timer counting down toward the surface where all the other things lived. The water didn't care who you'd been. The water only cared whether you were methodical.

He was methodical. He had made himself methodical the way a man makes himself a new spine.

*Survey transect one,* he thought, and swam forward.

* * *

The hull came up slowly out of the haze, and Cyrus began his work the way he always did: at the bow, moving aft in a grid, photographing everything, touching nothing he didn't have to. His slate was strapped to his wrist. He wrote in block capitals because underwater handwriting dissolved into spider tracks if you hurried, and Cyrus Kavanagh never hurried.

*Bow fair condition. Growth light. No hull penetrations fwd of frame 12.*

He moved along the port side. A lionfish regarded him from a bollard with its feathered disdain. Below it, the hull plating showed the clean seams of the scuttling charges—opened deliberately, valves and seacocks arranged, a ship put down by her own crew with something approaching tenderness. He'd seen ships die other ways. Ships torn open from outside, punched inward, holed in ways that told you a story about violence and speed and people who had not been ready. Those were the wrecks he didn't take contracts on anymore.

Those were the wrecks he didn't think about anymore, either. Both statements were lies, but they were well-maintained lies, load-bearing, and he kept them painted and caulked like any good deckhand keeps a working vessel.

Transect two. The superstructure. He rose two meters, finned over the rail, and dropped into the wheelhouse through the open starboard door.

Inside, the quiet changed quality. Wrecks had acoustics—you felt them against your skin, the way enclosed spaces pressed sound back at you even when there was no sound. The wheelhouse was small and brown with silt and shadow, the helm pedestals stripped, cables hanging like cut harp strings. Someone's glove lay on the deck plates, silted in, fingers pointing at the companionway as if it had been trying to tell him something for two years and he was the first person patient enough to listen.

Cyrus photographed it. Logged it. *Misc debris, whlsh, item 7.*

His gauge check was automatic. Nineteen meters. Eleven minutes elapsed. Two hundred forty bar. All fine. All fine. He said it to himself sometimes, the words bubbling uselessly inside the second stage, a superstition he'd deny under oath: *all fine, all fine.*

Through the forward windows, the light came down in long grey columns, shifting with the swell ten fathoms above. Six fathoms of quiet, Elias used to call it. *Anything past six fathoms is just the world holding its breath, lad. Don't be standing next to anything expensive when it lets it out.*

Elias Kavanagh had said a great many things in his life, most of them in that tone—not quite joking, not quite serious, calibrated to make you laugh while some part of your brain filed the sentence away for later. Cyrus had spent ten years filing those sentences away for later, and the file had gotten so heavy he could barely lift it, and he had never once opened it.

He finned out of the wheelhouse and continued aft.

* * *

Halfway down the survey, he found the thing that wasn't on the task list.

It was nothing, really. That was what he told himself first, the way you tell yourself anything inconvenient. A length of chain draped across the afterdeck, trailing over the side into the sand. Chain was normal on a wreck. Chain was everywhere on wrecks. Except this chain was bright—newer than the boat, newer than two years of immersion should allow—and it ran taut over the gunwale and down into the grey, angling away to the northeast, toward deeper water, toward whatever lay beyond the edge of this survey grid.

Cyrus hovered above it for a long moment, his exhaust bubbles climbing past his face in silver ropes.

The contract covered frames zero to sixty-two. The contract covered this boat. The chain was not attached to this boat's business. And Cyrus Kavanagh had built his whole life on a simple architecture: do the job, log the job, don't look past the edges of the job. Curiosity was how divers died. Curiosity was also, said a smaller and older voice, how men like Elias ended up owning charts with annotations in pencil that no one was ever supposed to read.

He checked his gas. Two hundred ten bar. Plenty for the job, plenty for a short excursion, not plenty for both.

Devi's voice came up soft in his ear, as if she'd heard him thinking. "You've stopped moving, skipper. Transect three is waiting on you. Everything okay down there?"

"Chain," he said. "New chain, running off the afterdeck. Heading northeast."

A pause. He could picture her in the wheelhouse, one eye on the sonar, one eye on the tide table, both eyes somehow also on him. Devi Okafor missed nothing; it was her most irritating feature and the reason everyone aboard was still alive.

"Off-contract?" she asked.

"Off-contract."

"Then it's not ours, is it." Not a question. Devi didn't ask questions she already owned the answers to. "Log it, photograph it, leave it. We're a survey boat this week, remember? Boring and legal. Your favorite."

"My favorite," Cyrus agreed, and photographed the chain anyway, three frames, then followed it with his eyes to where it vanished into the murk, and marked the bearing on his slate in block capitals, because that was what methodical meant, and because some part of him—a part he kept below the waterline, where nobody could inspect it—already knew he'd want the bearing later.

He finished the transect. He did it perfectly. Every frame sharp, every measurement logged, every valve and seam recorded for the harbor authority's clipboard people. The grouper watched him work with what he chose to interpret as professional approval. Near the stern he found the nameplate, half-fouled, and cleaned it gently with his thumb: KD *Perantau*. Patrol boat, displaced some four hundred tons, thirty-one years in service, two years as a reef. A whole biography in brass, and the fish swimming through her portholes knew none of it and needed none of it.

There was a lesson in that, probably. Cyrus declined to learn it.

* * *

The safety stop was at five meters, three minutes, in the bright shaken-glass light of the surface overhead. Cyrus hung on the line and did what he always did on safety stops, which was nothing. Absolutely nothing. It was the only discipline harder than diving and the one he practiced most.

Ten years ago, on another line, in another sea, he had hung at a safety stop beside a diver named Rook, and Rook had been telling a joke—he could no longer remember the joke, only that Rook had been mid-sentence, hands moving, eyes crinkled behind his mask—and then Rook had gone down again. Just that. A decision made somewhere below the level of words. Later there would be explanations involving narcosis and a stuck low-pressure inflator and a current that hadn't shown on any forecast, and all of those explanations would be true and none of them would be sufficient. Sela went down after him, because that was what dive partners did, and the sea accepted them both with the terrible courtesy it extended to everyone eventually.

Cyrus had hung on that line until his computer screamed, watching two sets of bubbles rise without divers attached, and Elias had been the one to haul him out, and Elias had never once spoken of it again, and neither had Cyrus, and the silence between them had become the largest room in the ship they both lived in.

Three minutes. He ascended.

* * *

The surface took him back the way it always did—noise first, the slap and chuckle of swell against the hull, then light, then the smell of diesel and salt and, faintly, frying garlic riding out from the Perdita's galley on the morning air. Auntie Bee cooked at volume regardless of conditions. It was her form of navigation; the crew steered by their stomachs.

Tomas Reyes leaned over the rail and hooked his arm out for Cyrus's gear, hauling tank and harness aboard with the easy strength that made everything he did look casual. "Any treasure?" he asked, grinning. His grin had survived things that should have killed it. "Please say treasure. I've got a plan for treasure."

"You've got a plan for a lottery ticket too."

"And I stand by both plans."

"No treasure. Chain, though." Cyrus rolled onto the deck, pulled his mask down, and let the sun hit his face. Above the waterline the world resumed all its weight: the tide tables, the fuel bill, the invoice due to the harbor authority, the slow arithmetic of a business that stayed solvent by never quite being interesting.

"Chain?" Tomas crouched, interested despite himself. Tomas was constitutionally incapable of not being interested; it was his best quality and the reason Cyrus kept him on deck instead of in the engine room, where curiosity got people killed.

"Bright chain, off the stern, running northeast. Off-grid."

Tomas's grin dimmed by one degree, which for Tomas was equivalent to another man going pale. "Northeast's deep water."

"Northeast is deep water," Cyrus agreed.

Devi appeared at the wheelhouse door with a towel and the survey tablet, handing over the first and studying the second. "I'll pull the charts tonight," she said. "If there's something sitting off our grid, I want to know what it is before someone tells us we're not allowed to know."

"We're not going to—" Tomas started.

"We're not going to do anything," Cyrus said. "We're going to finish the survey, get paid, buy fuel, and eat whatever Bee's making, which smells like she's feeding an army." He said it clearly, firmly, the way Elias would have said it, with exactly the right weight to close a subject. It worked the way it always worked, which was completely, except on the people who knew him best.

Devi looked at him a moment longer. She had known him ten years. Then she nodded once and went back inside, and Cyrus sat on the deck of the Perdita with salt drying white on his forearms and let the morning be ordinary.

* * *

They finished the survey by early afternoon. Cyrus logged the final frames, packed the cameras, signed the slate summary with the date and his certification number in block capitals, and stood at the rail while Devi weighed anchor and brought the tug around for the run back to Tanah Merah. The island slid past on the port beam, green and indifferent, its little harbor town already waking—fish boats coming out, laundry lines, the tin roofs catching light.

That was when the patrol boat found them.

She came around the southern headland at cruising speed, grey hull, naval ensign, no name visible at this range. Standard posture for these waters. But she altered course directly toward them, which was not standard, and closed at a speed that turned courtesy into arithmetic.

Devi saw her first. "Contact off the port quarter. Coming our way."

Cyrus lifted the binoculars. Patrol craft, thirty-some meters, deck mounts covered, bridge glass glinting. He watched her approach with the detached attention he gave weather: assess, categorize, prepare. In ten years of grey-zone work he had been boarded twice, inspected nine times, warned off more times than he could count, and the pattern was always the same—loud, official, slightly bored.

This one wasn't bored. This one came straight at them without a single wasted degree of turn, and when she was eight hundred meters out she called them on channel sixteen with a voice so polite it sounded rehearsed.

"Vessel Perdita, vessel Perdita, this is KD *Satria*. You are conducting survey activity in proximity to a restricted area. Advise your intentions."

Cyrus took the handset. Beside him, Devi had gone very still over her chart table, and Auntie Bee had appeared in the wheelhouse doorway wiping her hands on her apron, and Tomas had stopped coiling line halfway through a coil.

"KD Satria, Perdita. We're contracted for artificial reef survey on the Perantau, coordinates logged with the harbor authority. We're four hundred meters inside our grid and heading home."

Another pause. Longer than radio etiquette required. Long enough that Cyrus lowered the binoculars and looked at the ship herself, at the way she sat in the water, at the antenna arrays, at the launch cradled on her davits.

"Understood, Perdita," the voice said finally, smooth as poured concrete. "Complete your transit. Be advised the area to your northeast is closed to survey, salvage, and diving activity of all kinds. Further queries may be directed to the regional office."

"Acknowledged, Satria. Northeast is closed. We're heading southwest. Good day."

The patrol boat held station until they'd cleared the grid, then turned—not back the way she'd come, but northeast, into the deep water, toward the bearing of a bright chain running off the stern of a dead ship, and Cyrus stood at the rail and watched her go with the feeling, cold and familiar as pressure at depth, that the sea had just let out a breath it had been holding, and that something expensive had been standing very close to him the entire time.

Behind him, Devi said quietly, "Skipper. You're going to want to see the chart."
