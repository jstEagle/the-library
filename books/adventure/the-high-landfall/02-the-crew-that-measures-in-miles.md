## 2. The Crew That Measures in Miles

Devi Okafor could tell the state of the whole world by the sound of the Perdita's engine.

Ten years of listening had taught her the vocabulary. A clean, even thrum meant flat water and a captain who wasn't pushing her. A stutter at the top of the stroke meant fouled intake—jellyfish season, usually, or plastic bags that drifted out from Tanah Merah like grey jellyfish of their own kind. And a high, thin whine in the reduction gear, barely there, like a mosquito behind a wall, meant a bearing was dying and Cyrus Kavanagh would pretend not to hear it for another three weeks because money was money and bearings cost money.

The bearing was singing tonight.

She lay on her back in the engine room with a work light clipped to a pipe above her head and let the sound wash over her, cataloguing it the way Auntie Bee catalogued spices: by weight, by age, by how much trouble it was going to cause. The Perdita was forty-one years old, a harbour tug built for a life of dignity that she'd never gotten to live, sold surplus, patched, re-engined once badly and once well, and now spent her days doing work that no honest vessel would admit to. Devi loved her the way you love a difficult relative. Not despite the flaws. Through them.

"Devi." Tomas Reyes's voice came down the engine-room ladder ahead of him, the way it always did, half a second before his boots. "Bee wants to know if you're eating."

"Tell her I'm eating."

"She said if you say you're eating, tell her you're lying."

Devi smiled at the underside of the engine. "Tell her the bearing's singing again."

There was a pause while this information travelled up through the deck plates, and then Bee's voice arrived directly, projected down the ladder with the force of a woman who had raised three children and one salvage crew and saw no meaningful difference between the two. "Then you'll eat while it sings. Food first. Grief after. That's the rule."

"I'm not grieving," Devi called back. "I'm engineering."

"Same posture," said Bee, and her footsteps receded toward the galley, satisfied.

Tomas came down the ladder anyway. He was twenty-six, all elbows and restless hands, and he crouched beside her without being asked, holding the other end of the work light so it stopped swinging. He'd learned that without being told, too. Ten years. He'd been sixteen when Elias Kavanagh fished him off a dock in Puerto Sagrado with a forged seaman's card and a lie about his age, and he'd never once mentioned leaving.

"Bearing?" he asked.

"Number two. It'll last the season if nobody asks anything heroic of it."

"When does anybody ever ask anything heroic?"

"That's what worries me about us."

He laughed, quiet, and they stayed there a moment in the smell of diesel and salt rust, the engine talking its steady talk between them. This was the part of the job nobody put in brochures—the hours under the deck plates, two people not saying much, the machinery filling in the conversation. Devi had worked with engineers who needed radio silence and crews who needed constant chatter, and she'd decided long ago that the best crew was the kind that could share a small space with a dying bearing and feel no need to fill the quiet.

The quiet had cracks in it, though. It always did. There were certain silences aboard the Perdita that weren't comfortable at all, and everyone knew where they were located the way you know where the soft ice is on a frozen lake. One of them lived around the name of a ship none of them said aloud. Another lived around the tenth of October. Another, smaller one, lived around whatever had happened between Cyrus and Maren Voss back in certification school, which Tomas found endlessly fascinating and Devi found exhausting, mostly because Cyrus's face did something complicated whenever the name came up and complicated faces were bad for morale.

"You heard from the office?" Tomas asked, which was what he called it when he wanted to ask about money without asking about money.

"The office being our bank balance?"

"The office being the fact that we've got fuel for six weeks and contracts for zero."

"We've got the patrol boat survey tomorrow. Tanah Merah. Legal, boring, pays on delivery."

"Legal and boring," Tomas repeated, tasting the words. "Our two favourite flavours."

"It's what keeps us honest."

"No," Tomas said mildly, "what keeps us honest is Bee standing over us with a ladle. What keeps us solvent is everything else."

She reached up and smacked his ankle without looking. He grinned. Above them, through the open hatch, the evening came down over the anchorage in layers—first the heat breaking, then the light going amber, then the fishing boats coming home with their running lights strung out across the water like a string of beads someone had dropped and couldn't be bothered to pick up.

* * *

Dinner was rice, mackerel in a tomato-and-lime stew that Bee refused to call by any name but "stew," and fried plantain that appeared on the table in quantities suggesting Bee believed the four of them were convalescing from something. They ate in the mess, which was really just the aft cabin with a table bolted down and a bench on each side, and which held, in its corners, ten years of their lives: a dartboard with no darts (lost overboard, story disputed), a photograph of the Perdita younger and uglier, a shelf of paperbacks swollen with salt air, and, bolted above the doorframe where nobody had asked for it, a horseshoe that Bee swore came off a lighthouse tender and Tomas swore came off a donkey.

Cyrus ate the way he did everything—methodically, completely, without appearing to notice the food. He sat facing the door. He always sat facing the door. Devi had stopped finding that strange around year three.

"So tomorrow," Cyrus said, when the plates were half empty, which was when business got done, because Bee insisted on food before business and Cyrus respected exactly one authority on the boat. "Patrol boat sits in eight meters off Tanah Merah. Survey's a formality—insurance company wants photos and a hull report before they scrap her. In, dive, out, paid."

"And then?" Tomas asked.

"And then nothing. We go on the careening list and wait for something else."

"Nothing," Tomas said, "is expensive."

"Nothing is rest," said Bee, refilling his plate without asking whether he wanted it, because the answer was always yes and they both knew it. "You're twenty-six. You think rest is expensive. Wait until you're my age and rest is the only luxury left worth stealing."

"You stole plenty of luxuries," Tomas said. "Don't act retired."

"I stole necessities," Bee said with dignity. "Luxuries are for people who don't know the difference."

Devi watched them go at it, the old rhythm, and felt the thing she felt most evenings aboard, which was a warmth with a hairline fracture running through it, visible only if you looked straight at it, so mostly nobody did. Ten years. Rook had been aboard five of them. Sela, three. The bench on the port side still had a gouge in it from Rook's dive knife, and nobody had sanded it out, and nobody ever would, and the horseshoe above the door had been hung by Sela, who'd claimed it was good luck, and the luck had run out the way luck does, suddenly and without paperwork.

Nobody said their names. That was the rule Elias had made the night he came back alone, and the rule had outlived him by four years now, which Devi sometimes thought was the strangest monument anyone had ever built: a silence, maintained by four people who didn't even remember agreeing to it.

"Cyrus," she said, before she could stop herself. "We should talk about the careening yard. The slipway at Batu Lima quoted me—"

"Send them the deposit," Cyrus said.

"—and the number two bearing—"

"Is fine."

"It sings."

"It's harmonizing with Bee's radio."

Bee pointed the ladle at him. "My radio is a treasure."

"Your radio is a crime scene," said Tomas.

And that was dinner, more or less. Afterward Tomas washed up—his night, and he took it seriously in the way he took everything seriously, scrubbing like the plates had feelings—and Devi went topside to check the anchor watch and found Cyrus already there, leaning on the rail, looking at nothing in particular with the particular intensity of a man looking at something specific.

She stood next to him. Neither of them spoke for a while. The anchorage breathed around them; somewhere across the water a bar was playing music too cheap for its speakers.

"The bearing," Devi said finally. "I'm ordering it. Don't argue."

"I wasn't going to argue."

"You were going to make a face."

"I make excellent faces."

"You make expensive faces." She turned her forearms on the rail. "Cyrus. Six weeks of fuel, one boring contract, and a bearing that's dying. We're fine. But we're fine the way a diver at fifty meters is fine. Everything nominal until it isn't."

He was quiet a moment. "I know."

"Do you? Because you've been doing that thing since the navy boat passed us Tuesday. The thing where you look at the horizon like it owes you an explanation."

That earned her a glance, quick and unreadable. "What navy boat?"

And there it was. Devi kept her face pleasant and her voice level, the way you kept your breathing steady at depth. "The one that crossed our stern Tuesday afternoon. Grey hull, pennant number painted over. You watched it for eleven minutes. I timed you."

"You timed me."

"Somebody has to keep track of you. Bee does the food. Tomas does the optimism. I do the counting."

Cyrus looked back out at the water. For a second—one second, maybe less—something moved behind his eyes, some calculation or memory, and then it was gone, filed away wherever he filed things, in that locked locker of a man that even Bee's ladle couldn't pry open.

"They were polite," he said.

"Navy boats aren't polite."

"This one was. Courteous as a hotel manager." He pushed off the rail. "Get some sleep, Devi. Early start tomorrow."

She watched him go below, and she stood there a while longer with the count running in her head, the way it always ran: fuel, money, bearing, weather, crew. Four people. One tug. Six weeks of margin.

Everything nominal.

Except that Cyrus Kavanagh had lied to her just now, smoothly, about a thing so small it shouldn't have needed a lie, and in ten years Devi Okafor had learned that the small lies were the load-bearing ones. You could survive a big confession. It was the little evasions that quietly took the keel out from under you.

Below her feet, faint through the deck plates, the number two bearing sang its thin song into the dark, and the Perdita rode the tide, patient as an old woman who knows more than she says.
