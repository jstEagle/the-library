## 3. A Wreck the Navy Won't Discuss

The survey site was forty minutes east of Tanah Merah, past the fish farms and the rusting spar of an old navigation light that leaned out of the water like a drunk leaning on a wall. Cyrus ran the Perdita at eight knots because there was no reason to run her faster, and because Devi had asked him—politely, once—to stop treating the engine like a rental car. Eight knots gave him time to think, or to not think, which was often the same thing.

He'd taken the contract from a marine archaeology outfit in Singapore that existed mostly as a letterhead and a bank account. Side-scan survey of a grid square nobody cared about, three days' work, paid in full up front. The kind of job that kept the lights on between the jobs that kept the lights on. He didn't ask why a letterhead company wanted sonar imagery of open water. Asking questions was how you ended up in conversations you couldn't leave.

The morning was flat and grey, the sea the color of unpolished steel. Monsoon season was still six weeks out, but the light already had that bruised quality to it, like the sky was keeping something in reserve. Cyrus stood at the wheel with a thermos of Auntie Bee's coffee—too sweet, always too sweet, and he'd never once told her so—and watched the depth sounder tick down from ninety meters toward sixty.

"Contact in four hundred yards," Devi called from the electronics bench she'd built herself out of salvaged shelving and stubbornness. She had headphones pushed up into her curls and a pencil behind her ear for no reason he'd ever identified; she did all her math on paper, by hand, like the machines were witnesses who might talk. "Small. Hard return. Sitting proud off the bottom."

"Proud how?"

"Like it's standing up." She frowned at the screen. "That's not right for anything modern. Modern stuff falls over."

Cyrus brought the Perdita around in a slow arc, letting the towfish paint the target from a second angle. He'd learned survey work from Elias Kavanagh, who'd learned it from a hydrographer in another life, and the first thing either of them taught was patience: the wreck would tell you what it was if you stopped guessing first.

The second pass came back clean. A hull form, maybe fifty meters, sitting upright on its keel in sixty-two meters of water, deck structures intact, superstructure mostly whole. Too whole. Ships that sank in this stretch of coast got dragged by currents, broken on reefs, scattered across sand in pieces that took years to map. This one looked like it had been set down.

"That's a patrol boat," Devi said quietly. "Coastal class. Look at the bow profile." She tapped the screen where the sonar shadow showed a raked stem and the stub of a gun mount, long since stripped. "Decommissioned, I'd guess. Ten, fifteen years ago."

"Sunk?"

"Or placed." She said it carefully, the way she said most things that mattered. "There's no debris field, Cy. No scatter pattern. Either she went down slow and straight, or somebody put her here."

Cyrus stood looking at the screen for a long moment. Sixty-two meters. Upright. Intact. In his experience—and his experience was the only education he trusted—the sea did not leave things neat. Neatness was a human signature.

"Log it," he said.

"Already logging." Devi's pencil moved. Coordinates, depth, heading, time, the shape of the return. Her handwriting was small and exact, like everything else about her. "You want me to flag it for the client?"

"No," Cyrus said. Then, because the word felt too naked on its own: "Not yet. Let's finish the grid first."

Devi looked up at him over the rim of her headphones. She had one of those faces that made silence feel like a question. He ignored it professionally and drank his too-sweet coffee.

* * *

They worked the grid through the afternoon, and the patrol boat sat in the corner of every fourth screen-pass like a man standing at the back of a group photo. By sixteen hundred they had a decent mosaic: the wreck, the surrounding seabed, and—this was the part Cyrus kept coming back to—a scour trench along her port side that suggested she'd settled recently enough that the current hadn't finished tucking sand in around her hull.

Recent. Or maintained. He didn't let himself finish that thought, because the thought after it was worse.

Tomas came up from the foredeck where he'd been re-stenciling fenders, which was Tomas's way of being useful when there was nothing to fix and nothing to catch. He was twenty-six and built like a dock bollard, with a laugh that carried across water and a habit of reading over people's shoulders that had survived three years of everyone telling him to stop.

"What's the thing?" he asked, chin at the screen.

"Patrol boat. Decommissioned."

"Navy?"

"Probably."

"So we're mapping a navy boat for a company that doesn't exist." He grinned. "Normal Tuesday."

"Tomas."

"I'm just saying. It's a normal Tuesday for us." He leaned closer to the mosaic, and his grin faded the way it did when something actually landed. "She's clean, though. Look at her. Like somebody parked it."

"That's what I said," Devi murmured.

"That's what worries me," Cyrus said, before he could decide not to say it.

The radio crackled at 1637. He remembered the time afterward, exactly, because his mind did that—filed timestamps against his will, a habit from a decade of needing to know when things happened so he could testify to himself later that they had happened at all.

"Motor vessel Perdita, motor vessel Perdita, this is naval patrol vessel Sri Gemilang, calling on channel one-six."

Cyrus set down the thermos. He picked up the handset the way he'd been taught to pick up anything that might be a weapon: firmly, without hurry.

"Perdita copies, Gemilang. Go ahead."

"Perdita, be advised you are conducting operations within a designated survey area under active naval administration. Request your business here."

Every word was correct. That was the first thing wrong with it. In ten years of working grey water, Cyrus had been hailed by patrol boats maybe two dozen times, and the hails came in flavors: bored, hostile, lazy, corrupt, occasionally friendly in the way that preceded a request for cigarettes or cash. They did not come in this flavor. This flavor was a man reading from a card.

"Perdita is conducting commercial side-scan survey under contract," he said. "Happy to provide particulars."

A pause. Long enough to count. On the electronics bench, Devi had gone very still, pencil suspended.

"Perdita, be advised the survey area is subject to restriction pending further notice. Recommend you conclude operations and depart the area. Acknowledge."

"Copy, Gemilang. Can you give me a reference number for the restriction? For our paperwork."

Another pause. Longer. Somewhere in the background of the transmission, a voice said something too low to resolve, and someone else shushed it.

"Reference will be issued through proper channels, Perdita. Departure is recommended."

Recommended. Not ordered. An order created paperwork, obligations, a record that could be quoted back. A recommendation was a gift—you could refuse a gift. Except that gifts from navies were never gifts, and both of them knew it, and the courtesy was the message. We are asking nicely because asking nicely costs us nothing and tells us whether you're smart.

"Acknowledged, Gemilang," Cyrus said. "We'll wrap our lines and clear the area."

"Perdita, Gemilang. Thank you for your cooperation. Safe passage." And the channel went quiet, which was somehow louder than the hail had been.

Tomas exhaled through his teeth. "That was polite."

"That's what I said," Devi said again, and now her voice had an edge of something that wasn't quite fear and wasn't quite excitement, and Cyrus recognized it because he'd spent ten years keeping his own ration of it buried.

* * *

They cleared the area, because Cyrus Kavanagh was many things but stupid wasn't one of them, and the first rule of staying invisible was doing what you were told the moment someone bothered to tell you. He ran south for an hour, put a headland between them and the grid square, and anchored in a fishing anchorage where a dozen wooden boats bobbed and nobody asked anybody anything.

But the survey gear was still streaming data to the recorder, and the recorder didn't know it was supposed to be invisible.

He waited until dark, until Auntie Bee had fed them rice and sambal and a fish Tomas swore he'd caught that morning though they hadn't had lines in the water for a week, until the anchor watch was set and the deck was quiet except for the slap of water against the hull. Then he went to the electronics bench alone and pulled the day's files.

The mosaic glowed on the monitor in the darkened wheelhouse. Fifty meters of hull, upright, patient. He zoomed in on the scour trench, then on the superstructure, then on a detail he'd noticed and dismissed and now refused to dismiss: a rectangular void amidships, just forward of the engine space, where the sonar return went strange—not the soft shadow of an open hatch but a hard-edged absence, as if a section of the ship returned *less* than empty.

Sealed. Compartmented. Something inside the hull that didn't read like machinery.

He thought about Elias. He thought about Elias more often than he admitted, usually in the gaps—at anchor, at night, in the minute between starting a task and starting it—but tonight the old man arrived without invitation and stood in the wheelhouse the way he used to stand at the rail: silent, watching the water, radiating the particular disappointment of a man who has seen you about to do something foolish and has decided to let you.

*Don't look at things that look back,* Elias used to say. *The sea's full of holes. You don't need to go poking yours.*

Except Elias had poked plenty of holes in his time. Everyone knew it and no one said it. There were wrecks in Elias's chart book marked with a small notation—a circle with a slash—that meant *we don't work this one*, and the reasons were never written down, and Cyrus had spent his entire adult life not asking, because the not-asking was part of what Elias had left him, along with the boat and the method and the crew.

He opened the chart drawer instead of thinking about it. Elias's charts lived there, rolled in oilcloth, annotated in a hand so precise it looked printed. Cyrus found the sheet covering the survey grid—coastal approach, depths in fathoms converted to meters in the margin, current arrows drawn with a draftsman's care.

And there, in the corner of the grid square, a notation he had never seen on any other chart.

A small circle. No slash through it. Just the circle, and beside it, in letters two millimeters high:

**62.**

Sixty-two meters. The exact depth of the patrol boat.

Cyrus stood very still. Outside, the anchorage muttered and clinked. Somewhere aft, Tomas laughed in his sleep, a short bark, dreaming of something better than this life or worse.

Elias had marked this spot. Not crossed it off—marked it. Marked it with a number, the way you'd mark a waypoint you intended to come back to, or a place you'd already been.

He photographed the chart with his phone, closed the drawer, and sat down at the bench where Devi's logbook lay open to today's page. Her entry was complete: times, headings, contacts, the hail from the Gemilang transcribed word for word, because of course Devi had transcribed it word for word. At the bottom, under *Disposition*, she'd written: *Cleared area per naval recommendation. Client notified? — pending.*

Pending. Because he'd told her not to send it yet.

Cyrus picked up the pencil. He added a line beneath hers, in his own hand, in the column where the logbook expected nothing more important than fuel figures:

*Grid square 14-K. Contact: coastal patrol vessel, ~50m, upright, intact, 62m. Naval restriction advised, no reference given. Position logged.*

Then, after a moment, because the logbook was the closest thing the Perdita had to a conscience and because some part of him wanted the record to exist even if he never acted on it:

*Also: E.K. chart notation, same position. Unexplained.*

He capped the pencil. The mosaic still glowed on the monitor, the wreck sitting in its rectangle of hard-edged absence, sealed and waiting, and Cyrus understood—with the calm, detached clarity he brought to diving and to almost nothing else—that he had just crossed a line so fine he couldn't see it behind him. Navies didn't warn off survey boats over nothing. Letterheads didn't pay for grids over nothing. Dead captains didn't mark coordinates with numbers instead of slashes over nothing.

Three nothings, stacked. Which made a something.

He should delete the file. He should wipe the log entry, notify the client, take the money, and spend next week mapping a pipeline route somewhere shallow and boring and safe. That was the method. That was the decade of discipline. Stay small, stay dull, stay alive.

Instead he copied the day's sonar data to a drive, labeled it with a date and nothing else, and locked it in the drawer with Elias's charts.

In the morning, he'd tell Devi to finish the grid—they'd been paid for the grid, and Cyrus Kavanagh honored contracts down to the last square kilometer, especially when honoring them bought him another day of legal, deniable reason to point a sonar at a hole the navy didn't want looked at.

And in the afternoon, he'd run into Tanah Merah for stores, which was true, and for fuel, which was also true, and to sit at the harbour bar for one drink, which was truest of all, because Maren Voss drank there when she was in port, and Maren Voss always seemed to know when something interesting had sunk.

He told himself it was coincidence. He was good at telling himself things. It was, after all, the family method.
