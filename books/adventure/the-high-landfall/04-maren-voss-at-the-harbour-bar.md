## 4. Maren Voss at the Harbour Bar

The Harbour Bar at Tanah Merah had exactly three virtues, in Maren Voss's estimation: it was close to the water, it was loud enough that conversations died two tables away, and its owner owed her a favor she had never asked to be repaid. Everything else about it—the sticky ceiling fan, the rum that tasted like it had been filtered through a fishing net, the clientele of dockhands and customs clerks who stared too long at her boots—was negotiable.

She'd arrived forty minutes early, which was not like her. Maren Voss was a woman who arrived precisely when she intended to arrive, never before, because early meant eager and eager meant weak. But tonight she'd taken the corner booth with its back to the wall at seven-fifteen for a meeting set at eight, and she'd spent the extra forty-five minutes doing something she despised: rehearsing.

Not the pitch. The pitch was clean. The pitch had been clean for two years, polished in hotel rooms and charter cabins across three seas, waiting for the one man alive who could make it work. She could deliver the pitch in her sleep, and on some nights she had, waking with the numbers already arranged in her mouth like teeth.

What she was rehearsing was the moment he said no.

Because he would say no. That was the problem. Cyrus Kavanagh had a talent for no that she'd been admiring and cursing since they were both twenty-three years old and broke in a decompression chamber in Subic Bay, sharing an oxygen scrubber and a grudge. He said no the way other men said yes—calmly, completely, with a finality that made argument feel like shouting at a sea wall. In fifteen years she had watched him turn down contracts that would have made lesser salvagers rich for a decade. He turned them down the way he did everything else: without theater, without apology, without leaving a crack you could drive a wedge into.

She needed a crack tonight.

The fan turned. The rum sat untouched in front of her, because she never drank before a negotiation, and because the rum was genuinely terrible.

At eight-oh-four, the door opened and Cyrus Kavanagh came in out of the heat.

He hadn't changed, which annoyed her. Fifteen years and he still moved like a man walking a deck in a following sea—economical, unhurried, reading the room the way he read water, for current and obstruction. He was leaner than the certification photos, grey coming in at the temples like frost on a rail, and he wore the same expression he'd worn at twenty-three: a face that had decided, long ago, to give away nothing for free.

He saw her. He did not smile. He crossed the room and sat down across from her without asking, which meant he'd already decided how long this conversation would last, and it would last exactly as long as he permitted.

"Maren."

"Cyrus." She let a beat pass. "You look terrible."

"I've been diving a patrol boat since dawn." He signaled the owner with two fingers. The owner, to his credit, brought a beer without being asked and left without being thanked. "You look expensive."

"I am expensive." She turned her glass a quarter rotation on the ring it had left. "That's rather the point of this meeting."

"Is it a meeting? I thought it was a drink between old friends."

"We were never friends, Cyrus."

"No." He drank. "We were never that."

There it was. The old arithmetic between them, laid on the table like a chart. She'd spent years trying to decide whether she hated him for it and had concluded, eventually, that she didn't—she hated that he was right. Friends was a word for people who didn't keep score. She and Cyrus kept score the way tide tables kept time.

"Tell me about your week," she said.

"Why?"

"Because I'm curious."

"You're not curious. You're building something." He set the beer down. "You always build. I can hear the scaffolding."

She laughed—genuinely, briefly, before she could stop it. That was the other thing about him. Fifteen years, and he could still make her laugh when she'd come armed. It was intolerable.

"Fine," she said. "Here's what I know. You spent this week on a decommissioned patrol boat off Tanah Merah, a job that pays maybe four hundred a day split four ways. Before that, a cargo hatch recovery for a shipping agent who paid you late and short. Before that—" she let the pause do its work— "nothing for six weeks, because the monsoon's coming and nobody with a legal contract wants a grey-zone crew anywhere near their liability."

"Your intelligence is good."

"My intelligence is expensive. It's the only thing I buy without haggling." She leaned forward, and lowered her voice, and watched his face the way a navigator watches a barometer. "And here's what I know that you don't."

"Go on."

"There's a ship going down in the Sunda Trench sector. A salvage vessel. Big one. Corporate money, naval escort arrangements, the whole apparatus. They're staging at Pulau Sepat as we speak, taking on mixed gas and ROV spares." She let him absorb that. "They're looking for a wreck. And they are going to find it, unless someone who knows these waters better than their charts do gets there first."

"Every wreck hunter in the region is looking for something."

"Not this something." She reached into the leather satchel at her hip—slowly, letting him watch, because sudden movements near Cyrus Kavanagh got you catalogued—and laid a single folded sheet of paper on the table between them. She did not open it. "I'm going to say a name now, Cyrus, and I want you to hear the whole sentence before you stand up and walk out. Because I've watched you walk out of rooms before, and I know you can do it without spilling your beer, and I'd rather you didn't."

His expression didn't change. That was the maddening thing about him; his face was a sea state of zero. But his hand stopped moving toward the bottle, and that was its own kind of tell, if you'd spent fifteen years learning to read him.

"Say it," he said.

"The Corvane."

The fan turned. Somewhere behind the bar a bottle broke and nobody apologized for it. Cyrus Kavanagh looked at the folded paper on the table as though it had begun to smoke.

"No," he said.

"You haven't heard the—"

"No." He said it the way she'd remembered, the sea wall, complete and load-bearing. He reached for his beer, drained it, and set the empty down with the care of a man putting something away for good. "Whatever this is, Maren—whatever they're paying you, whoever's paying you—the answer is no, and it was no before you finished saying the name, and it'll be no tomorrow and next monsoon and at your funeral."

"Cyrus."

"That ship is a grave."

"The Corvane is a *contract*," she said, and heard her own voice sharpen, and made herself soften it, because sharpened was wrong, sharpened was losing. "The Corvane is a wartime transport that went down in '09 with a sealed manifest, a sealed strongroom, and a passenger list that somebody with a great deal of money and a great deal of influence has spent fifteen years making sure stays at the bottom of a trench. The fleet I told you about is going to raise her in the next three weeks, and when they do, whatever's in that strongroom gets lifted out by crane and disappears into a corporate archive, and no one—no one, Cyrus—will ever see it again."

"Then it stays buried. That's not my problem."

"It could be your retirement."

"I don't have a retirement. I have a boat and a crew and a season, and none of them need whatever you're selling badly enough to go near that name." He started to rise.

"Two hundred thousand."

He stopped. She'd known he would. Not because Cyrus Kavanagh was for sale—God, no, that was the entire tragedy of him—but because two hundred thousand was the number that made the *Perdita's* hull repairs, Devi's bad knee, Tomas's sister's debts, and Auntie Bee's leaking roof all stand up in a row and look at him. She'd done the arithmetic. She'd had people do the arithmetic. She knew his crew's books better than he thought she did, because knowing things was the only currency she'd ever trusted.

"Upfront," she said. "Half on signature. Half on location. You don't even have to dive her. Find her, fix her position, hand me the coordinates, and walk away. Your crew never touches the wreck. Your name never touches the paperwork. You get paid for knowing something the fleet doesn't."

"And what do you get?"

"That's my business."

"It's always your business." He sat back down, which she noted with a small cold satisfaction, and which she noted even more carefully was not the same as saying yes. "Who's behind the fleet?"

"Does it matter?"

"It matters."

"A consortium," she said. "Shipping money. Old money, some of it, with war years in its ledger."

"And you? Who's behind *you*?"

"Backers."

"Named backers."

"Cyrus." She smiled at him, and she made it the smile that had opened doors in Singapore and closed deals in Jakarta, the smile that said *we're on the same side of the table, you and I, against the whole gray ocean.* "You're doing the thing you do. The interrogation. The slow no. I've sat across from you for twenty minutes and you've said no four times and drunk one beer and asked me nothing about the money, which tells me the money interests you and frightens you in equal measure, and I understand that, I do. But I need you to listen to the shape of this and not just the name."

"The name is the shape."

"The name is a scar," she said. "I know. I know what that ship cost you. I was in the region that year, Cyrus. I knew Rook. I drank with Rook at this exact bar, two months before." She let that land, and watched it land, and hated herself a little for how precisely she'd aimed it. "I'm not asking you to dive her. I'm asking you to find her. There's a difference, and the difference is two hundred thousand dollars and your crew's next ten years."

He was quiet for a long time. She let him be quiet. Silence was a tool she'd learned from him, ironically, back in the chamber in Subic, when he'd sat with a busted eardrum and said nothing for six hours while she filled the dark with talk, and at the end of it she'd realized she'd told him everything and he'd told her nothing, and that was the day she'd understood what kind of man she was dealing with.

"You said the fleet stages at Sepat," he said finally. "How do you know that?"

"Because I pay for information, as established."

"You know their dive depth ratings?"

"Better than they do. They're surface-supply and ROV. They can't work her efficiently past fifty meters, and she's deeper than that. They'll burn weeks on survey before they touch her. You—" she tapped the table once— "have charts nobody else has. Elias's charts. Everyone in this trade knows the Kavanagh surveys were the best in the archipelago, and everyone knows where they ended up."

Something moved behind his eyes then. Not anger. Something older and quieter, and she realized, with a small internal lurch, that she'd misstepped—that Elias Kavanagh was not a lever she should have touched this early, or possibly at all.

"You've done your homework," Cyrus said. His voice had gone flat in a new way. Not the sea wall. Something beneath the sea wall.

"I always do."

"Then you know Elias is dead."

"Three years. I sent flowers."

"You sent a card with no return address."

"I sent what I send everyone." She lifted her glass, found it empty, and set it down again. The fan turned. She was aware, suddenly and unpleasantly, that she was the one filling silence now, and that he had learned the trick too, and was using it on her, and that fifteen years ago in the chamber she had taught him how.

"Here's my problem, Maren," he said. "You came to me. You. Not a broker, not a cutout—although I assume the liaison officer you've got living at the Marina Inn is a cutout of some kind, and I assume she's been photographing things, because that's how you operate. You came in person, which means this matters to you personally, which means there's something under the money, and I don't dive on things under money. That's the whole school of hard knocks in one sentence. That's the *Perdita's* entire safety culture."

"Then don't dive on it. Find it. Walk away."

"And you walk into the trench with whatever crew you've got and whatever's down there, and I read about it in six months, or I don't read about it at all, which is more likely." He shook his head slowly. "Why me, Maren? There are other crews with old charts. Half as good, sure, but they'd take the money and not ask the questions I'm asking. Why the one captain in the archipelago with a reason to hate that ship's name?"

And there it was. The question she'd been steering around for twenty minutes, the reef directly under her keel, and she felt the hull touch it and hold.

Because she'd rehearsed forty-five minutes of answers and every one of them was a lie, and Cyrus Kavanagh could hear a lie the way he heard a change in engine note—instantly, and with his whole body.

"Because you're the best," she said.

"Try again."

"Because your charts are the only ones that show the approach channel."

"Closer."

"Because—" and her voice did something she hadn't authorized, dropped half a register, lost its polish, and for one unguarded second Maren Voss sat in a sticky booth in a bad bar and was simply a woman in her forties who was tired, "—because if anyone can get there before that fleet and keep what's in that strongroom out of their hands, it's you. And what's in that strongroom shouldn't be theirs. That's the truth. That's the whole truth I can give you tonight, and if you push me past it I'll lie to you, and we'll both know it, and then you'll say no forever, and I will have burned the only card I have left."

He studied her. The fan turned. She had the distinct, vertiginous sense that he was reading her the way he read a wreck on sonar—sweep by sweep, building the image out of returns, and that the image he was building included the thing she'd just shown him whether she'd meant to or not.

"You're frightened," he said.

"Don't be absurd."

"You are. Right now. Underneath it." He leaned forward. "Fifteen years, and I've seen you scared twice. Once in the chamber, when the scrubber failed. Once now. So whatever the Corvane is to you, it's not just a contract, and it's not just money, and you came to the one man who'll notice that." He sat back. "I'm not saying yes."

"I know."

"I'm saying I'll look at the numbers. I'll look at your paperwork. I'll talk to my crew, and they'll say no, and then I'll probably say no, and you'll have wasted an evening and a very good lie about Rook."

"It wasn't a lie. I knew Rook."

"Everyone knew Rook. Rook bought the whole harbor drinks." He stood, put a bill under his empty bottle—enough for both their tabs, she noticed, which was its own small unbearable courtesy—and looked down at her. "The folded paper. What is it?"

"Coordinates," she said. "Partial. From a 1980s survey. The fleet has them too. It's not the prize, Cyrus. It's the bait. I'm being honest with you about that, at least."

"First time for everything." He picked it up, and she watched him fold it into his jacket without looking at it, and understood that she'd just moved something into his possession that he would now have to either use or deliberately destroy, and that both options cost him, and that she'd known that when she put it on the table. She was not a good person. She had never claimed to be a good person. She was a thorough one.

"Cyrus," she said, as he turned to go. "The fleet's survey window opens in nineteen days. After that, the monsoon closes everything for four months. If you're going to look at the numbers, look at them fast."

He paused at the door, backlit by the sodium glare of the quay, and for a moment he looked exactly like Elias—same silhouette, same stillness—and the grief of it went through her sideways, uninvited, like water through a bad seam.

"I'll be in touch," he said, and was gone into the heat.

Maren Voss sat alone in the corner booth for a long time after that, turning her empty glass, listening to the fan, and doing the thing she never did, which was admitting to herself, in the privacy of her own skull, that her hands were not quite steady.

She'd seen the fear flicker across him when she said the name—saw it and catalogued it and planned around it. What she hadn't planned for was the fear in herself, the one that had nothing to do with the fleet or the money or the monsoon. It had to do with the passenger list. It had to do with a name on that list, a name she had not said aloud in fifteen years, a name that was currently sitting in Cyrus Kavanagh's jacket pocket in the form of a folded sheet of paper she'd told him was coordinates.

It was not only coordinates.

She finished his abandoned beer, because it was there, and because she'd earned it, and because tomorrow she would have to be flawless again.

Outside, the tide was turning, and somewhere out past the breakwater, the *Perdita* rode at anchor with her lights burning low, and a man who had just said no four times was carrying her paper home to his crew—and Maren Voss, who had built a career on knowing what would happen next, discovered she had no idea at all.
