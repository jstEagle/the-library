## 5. The Sworn-Off Job

Devi Okafor heard the name before she saw Cyrus's face.

She was in the engine room of the *Perdita*, elbow-deep in the port generator's cooling system, when the word came down through the deck plates the way bad news always did aboard a small boat—by osmosis, by the way footsteps changed overhead, by the quality of silence in the wheelhouse. The *Corvane*. Maren Voss had said it out loud at the harbour bar like it was just another hull number, and now it was sitting in the boat with them, dripping salt water on the deck.

Devi wiped her hands on a rag that had been grey since the Obama administration and climbed the ladder into the afternoon light.

Cyrus was alone in the wheelhouse, which was how she wanted him. He stood at the chart table with Maren's contract folder open in front of him, not reading it so much as staring through it, the way he stared through weather reports he didn't like.

"You haven't signed yet," she said.

"No."

"That's not an answer."

"It's a fact." He didn't look up. "Facts are what I have right now."

Devi pulled the door shut behind her. On the *Perdita* there were no private conversations, only conversations with the volume turned down and the door closed, and everyone aboard understood the difference. She took the folder from under his hand—not gently—and read the first page herself. The figure at the bottom made her blink twice. It was more than they'd cleared in the last three years combined. It was, if she was honest about the arithmetic she'd been running since the harbour bar, enough to retire the boat, settle Tomas's mother's medical debt in Cebu, put Auntie Bee somewhere with air conditioning for the rest of her life, and buy Devi a machine shop where nobody ever asked her to make a part out of scavenged truck axle again.

"Obscene," Devi said. "That's the word Tomas used."

"Tomas uses a lot of words."

"He also asked me this morning whether we'd need the deep rig. I told him no such job existed." She set the folder down. "So let's stop pretending the name on page two is just a name."

Cyrus finally looked at her. Ten years of diving together meant she could read his face the way she read pressure gauges—small deflections meaning large things underneath. What she saw now wasn't surprise. It was the look of a man who'd been waiting for someone to say the thing out loud so he wouldn't have to be the one to say it.

"The *Corvane*," she said. "Wartime transport. Went down off the Serrano shelf eleven years ago—no, ten. Ten years and four months." She watched his jaw tighten at the precision. "You know exactly how long. So do I."

"Devi."

"Rook and Sela," she said.

The name went into the wheelhouse like cold water into a warm engine block. Somewhere below them, the generator thumped its steady idiot rhythm, and above them Auntie Bee was singing something in Tagalog while she cooked, and the world continued being a normal world, and inside the wheelhouse it had stopped being one.

"We don't talk about that," Cyrus said.

"Elias's rule. I remember the rule." Devi crossed her arms. "I also remember the night he made it. You want to tell me I'm wrong? Go ahead. Tell me I've spent ten years misremembering the worst night of my life."

He didn't tell her she was wrong. He sat down slowly, the way older men sit down when their body has opinions their pride hasn't approved, and rubbed both hands over his face.

"I was twenty-six," he said. "It was my third season on the boat."

"I was thirty-one and I'd already rebuilt that generator twice. I remember my own history, Cyrus."

"Then you remember I wasn't on the dive."

"No," she said quietly. "You weren't. You were topside with Elias. Rook and Sela went down together because Sela insisted the survey line needed two sets of hands and Rook never told her no about anything." Devi had rehearsed none of this and it came out anyway, ten years of held breath escaping all at once. "And then the shot line went slack. And then the comms went to static. And Elias stood at the rail for six hours doing decompression math out loud, over and over, like if he got the numbers right they'd come back up. And they didn't come back up. And the next morning he called us all into the wheelhouse and said—" Her voice caught on the exact words, because some sentences are load-bearing. "He said: *That wreck is closed. We don't take work near it, we don't discuss it, we don't log it. Anyone who asks why gets off at the next port.*"

"And you stayed," Cyrus said.

"I stayed because he was Elias. Because he'd pulled me out of a burning yard in Surabaya and given me a bunk and a wage and never once made me feel like cargo. Because when a man gives you everything, you don't audit his reasons." She leaned on the chart table across from him. "But I'm auditing them now. Because Maren Voss just walked into our lives with a contract for the one wreck Elias sealed shut, and she knew. Cyrus. Look at me. *She knew.* Why else pick us? There are forty salvage outfits in this archipelago. Half of them would sign for half that money. She picked the crew of the *Perdita*."

"She said it was our reputation."

"Our reputation is for finding things people don't want found. That's true. But it's not why." Devi tapped the folder. "I ran the numbers on her offer last night. Not the money—the shape of it. She fronted us a retainer before we even agreed. She named a search box that happens to overlap the Serrano shelf. She wants us moving inside two weeks, ahead of monsoon, ahead of this corporate fleet. That's not a client hiring divers. That's someone who already knows where the wreck is and needs us to get there fast and quiet. Which means the only thing we're actually selling is—" She stopped, because saying it made it real. "The charts."

Cyrus went very still.

Elias Kavanagh had kept his charts the way monks kept relics: locked, annotated in pencil shorthand only he could read, updated obsessively for waters most captains never entered. When he died—heart, in his bunk, three years ago, between one tide and the next—he'd left the locker to Cyrus and the rest of the boat to the crew, and nobody had ever quite worked up the nerve to ask what was in the charts that he'd guarded harder than his money.

"You think he mapped it," Cyrus said. His voice had gone flat, careful, the voice he used on dives when the plan stopped matching reality. "The approach channel."

"I think a man who forbade his crew from a wreck might still have been unable to stop thinking about it. Or—" and here Devi made herself say the second thing, the worse thing, the thing she'd turned over at three in the morning while the generator ticked, "—or he mapped it because he'd been there before she ever went down. Because he knew the water. Because he knew exactly what she'd hit and when."

"That's insane."

"So is losing two divers and banning the subject forever instead of holding a memorial. So is keeping a sealed locker for three years that neither of us has opened." She softened, because the point wasn't to wound him; the point was that they were standing on the same cracked deck. "I loved him too. Don't do the thing where you think you're the only one grieving. I rebuilt his engines for a decade. He taught me navigation because he said every engineer should be able to bring the boat home if the wheelhouse goes dark. I have spent ten years being loyal to a dead man, Cyrus, and I am asking you one question and I need an actual answer."

"Ask it."

"What was he protecting?" She held his eyes. "Us—or himself?"

The question hung there. Through the wheelhouse windows Devi could see Tanah Merah harbour going gold with late light, fishing boats coming in, the ordinary commerce of an ordinary coast. Below them, faintly, Tomas laughed at something Auntie Bee said, and the sound came up through the deck like warmth through a floor.

Cyrus looked at the contract folder for a long time.

"When Rook and Sela didn't come up," he said finally, "Elias dove himself. Against every protocol he ever taught us. Single tank, no standby, straight down the shot line at dusk." Devi hadn't known that. Something in her chest folded. "He was down maybe twelve minutes. When he came up he wouldn't speak for a day. And when he did speak, it was the rule. And I asked him—I was twenty-six and stupid and I asked him *why*, and he looked at me like I'd hit him, and he said—"

Devi waited. The generator thumped.

"He said, *Because the truth down there costs more than they can pay, and I've seen the invoice.*" Cyrus exhaled. "I thought he meant the depth. The gas bill. Diver math. I was twenty-six."

"And now?"

"Now I think he meant something else entirely." Cyrus pushed the contract folder away from him, then pulled it back, then left his hand flat on top of it as if pinning something down. "Maren Voss offered us obscene money for a wreck our captain swore off the day after he saw whatever he saw. Either she doesn't know what happened down there—which makes her reckless with our lives—or she knows exactly what happened, and that's the actual product she's buying."

"Which one do you believe?"

"I believe she flinched," Cyrus said. "At the bar. When I hesitated. For about half a second she looked genuinely afraid, and it wasn't fear of losing the contract. It was fear of me saying no." He shook his head slowly. "People aren't afraid of a no unless the yes matters more than they're admitting."

Devi straightened up. Outside, the light was going copper, and Auntie Bee's singing had stopped, which meant dinner was close, which meant the whole crew would be in one room within the hour, and whatever was decided in this wheelhouse would either be told to them or hidden from them, and Devi already knew which one she could live with.

"Here's where I stand," she said. "Tomas doesn't know the full story. He was nineteen, he remembers the rule but not the night. Auntie Bee knows everything—she always knows everything—but she'll follow your lead, because she followed Elias's lead for twenty years and old habits hold better than welds. So this lands on you. You're the captain now, whatever Elias's paperwork says about ownership. You decide, and we live with it."

"That's not fair."

"No," Devi agreed. "It isn't. Neither was the rule. Neither was dying with your questions still in your mouth." She moved toward the door, then stopped with her hand on the latch. "One more thing. If you sign that contract without telling them what the *Corvane* is—if you spend their loyalty like it's yours to spend—then Elias protected himself after all, and so will you. Think about which man you want to have been. Dead men don't get revisions."

She went down the ladder and out into the gold light, and behind her she heard nothing, which from Cyrus Kavanagh was the loudest sound there was.

* * *

Dinner was rice, grilled mackerel, and a tension you could have cut samples from.

Auntie Bee served it all with the serene ferocity of a woman who had fed crews through typhoons and funerals and one memorable police raid, and she watched everything over the rim of her tea mug. Tomas talked enough for four people—something about a girl at the fuel dock, something about a YouTube video of a container ship losing stacks in heavy weather—filling silence the way young men pump water out of a bilge, constantly and without much strategy. Devi ate and answered when addressed and kept her eyes off Cyrus, which was itself a kind of signal, and she could see him registering it.

It was Tomas who broke first, because Tomas always broke first; it was practically his function on the boat, the sacrificial awkwardness that let everyone else stay quiet.

"So," he said, pushing fish skin around his plate. "Are we actually talking about the Voss job, or are we doing the thing where we all pretend the wheelhouse meeting didn't happen until somebody explodes?"

"Tomas," Auntie Bee murmured.

"No, I'm serious. Devi came out of there looking like she'd found a fault line in the hull. Cap looks like he's doing dive tables in his head. Somebody say something or I'm going to start guessing out loud and my guesses get weird."

Devi set down her fork. She looked at Cyrus. *Your call,* she didn't say, and said it anyway with her eyebrows.

Cyrus put his back against the bulkhead and looked around the table—at Tomas, at Auntie Bee, at Devi—and Devi watched him arrive at the decision in real time, watched the moment land in his shoulders.

"The job is the *Corvane*," he said.

Tomas's fork stopped. Even he, nineteen years old and three seasons aboard, knew the shape of that silence; the rule had soaked into the boat's timbers the way diesel soaked into everything, unmentioned and permanent.

"The transport?" Tomas said carefully. "*That* Corvane?"

"That Corvane."

"But Elias—" Tomas glanced at Auntie Bee, caught himself, glanced away. "I mean. The rule."

"The rule was Elias's," Cyrus said. "Elias is dead. And Maren Voss is offering us more money than this boat has earned in a decade to find that wreck before a corporate fleet does." He laid it out flat, no varnish: the retainer, the timeline, the monsoon window, the search box. Devi gave him credit—he didn't dress it up. He told them the price and let it sit on the table like a landed fish.

Nobody touched it for a moment.

"Ten years," Auntie Bee said at last, softly, in the tone she used for announcing course changes. "Four months."

"You knew the date too," Devi said.

"I know every date on this boat, anak. Grief keeps books." Auntie Bee refilled her mug though it was half full, which was what she did with her hands when her mind was elsewhere. "Elias forbade that name and I obeyed, because obeying was easier than asking. But I cooked for that man for twenty years and I watched him not-talk about that wreck every single week. You don't forbid a subject unless the subject owns you." She looked at Cyrus over the steam. "What did he say to you, the night he made the rule? The real thing, not the rule."

Cyrus hesitated. Then: "He said the truth down there costs more than they can pay. And that he'd seen the invoice."

Auntie Bee closed her eyes briefly, the way you do when a long-feared diagnosis is confirmed. "Ay," she breathed. "That man."

"Auntie Bee." Tomas leaned forward. "You know something."

"I know Elias Kavanagh came back from that dive looking like a man who'd read his own obituary," she said. "I know he burned papers in the galley stove at two in the morning, and I pretended to sleep because he was my captain and burning papers was his business. And I know that for ten years he paid for masses—for two names he never said aloud—in a church in Puerto Verde, cash, every month, regular as tide." She opened her eyes. "You don't pay monthly for an accident, anak. Accidents you mourn once. Monthly is penance. Penance means sin."

The cabin was very quiet. The generator thumped below, faithful and oblivious.

"So we don't take the job," Tomas said. "Right? Whatever's down there, it's not ours. Guy bought his silence, let him keep it."

"He's dead, Tomas," Devi said. "His silence isn't keeping anyone safe anymore. It's just keeping us ignorant."

"Ignorant is *fine*. Ignorant is how we've stayed alive and out of prison for ten years!"

"Ignorant is how Rook and Sela died," Devi said, and immediately wished she could pull it back, because it wasn't true and wasn't fair and landed on Cyrus like a dropped wrench.

But Cyrus didn't flinch. He looked at his hands for a moment, then around the table.

"Here's what I know," he said. "Maren Voss knows more than she's telling. The money is bait or it's hush, and either way we're the tool, not the client. Elias left a locker I've never opened, charts I've never fully read, and a rule that smells less like grief every year and more like fear. And tomorrow I have to give Maren an answer." He stood, collected his plate, and paused at the doorway to the aft passage. "I'm going to open the locker tonight. All of it. Before I sign anything, I want to know what my captain was hiding—and from whom."

He went aft. A minute later they heard the wheelhouse hatch, then the creak of the bench locker, then nothing.

Tomas looked at Devi. "Should we—"

"No," Devi said. "Some doors you open alone."

Auntie Bee poured the last of the tea, four cups though only three of them remained at the table, and set the fourth at the empty place out of twenty years of habit, and no one remarked on it, and no one drank it.

* * *

Devi couldn't sleep, which surprised her not at all.

Around midnight she gave up, dressed, and climbed to the wheelhouse intending to sit with the radar and the hum and her own thoughts. The hatch was open. Cyrus was at the chart table under the single red lamp, and spread out in front of him—not the contract, not the logbooks—was one of Elias's charts, weighted at the corners with socket wrenches, and he was holding a magnifying glass over the Serrano shelf like a man reading scripture by lightning.

Devi came up beside him and looked.

Elias's annotations crawled along the reef lines in his tiny engineer's hand: depths, currents, bottom composition, all normal, all the meticulous record of a master navigator. But along the approach channel to the Serrano shelf—along the exact corridor any ship would steer toward the anchorage where the *Corvane* had gone down—the annotations changed character. Tighter. Newer ink. And beside one particular bend in the channel, where the reef narrowed to a throat, Elias had written a single notation, dated **four years before the sinking**:

*Channel clear at 14m. Wide enough for a transport hull if she comes in dark.*

Devi felt the floor of the world tilt a degree.

"Cyrus," she said. "When did he write that?"

Cyrus's voice came out level and terrible. "Four years before she sank." He lowered the magnifying glass. "Devi—he mapped her approach channel before she ever sailed. He knew these waters. He knew exactly what a ship coming in dark would hit." He looked up at her, and in the red lamplight his face was ten years older. "The question isn't what he was protecting anymore."

Down in the engine room, the generator thumped on, steady as a heartbeat, steady as a countdown.

"The question," Cyrus said, "is what he was paid for."
