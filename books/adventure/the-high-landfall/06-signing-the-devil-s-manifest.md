## 6. Signing the Devil's Manifest

Maren Voss's contract arrived by courier at six the next morning, which told Cyrus she'd been awake all night, and that she wanted him to read it before the coffee wore off his judgment.

It came in a waterproof tube, of all things—the kind surveyors used for charts. Inside, a document printed on heavy stock, forty-one pages, bound with brass fasteners. No email. No digital signature. Paper you could burn, paper you could feed to the sea. She knew her audience.

He sat in the wheelhouse of the *Perdita* with the hatch open to the grey morning and read the thing twice through while the harbor woke around him—gulls arguing over the fish market, the diesel clatter of the ferries starting up, Auntie Bee's radio murmuring weather from somewhere below decks.

The numbers were obscene. Not good money. Not salvage money. *Silence* money, the kind that came with a shape to it, a purpose. Locate the *Corvane*. Fix her position to within fifty meters. Confirm structural condition and—his eye snagged here—*recover any sealed documentation containers intact and deliver to the Client's representative.* The client was named only as a post office box in Singapore. Maren's signature was already on it, ink the color of dried blood.

Payment on delivery. A bonus clause for speed. A confidentiality clause so broad it might as well have said *forget you ever read this.*

And there, on page thirty-eight, the sentence that made him set the whole thing down and look at the water for a long time: *Contractor acknowledges familiarity with the target vessel's last known position and approaches.*

She wasn't guessing. She knew he knew. She'd known when she slid the photograph across the bar table, and she'd known what she was doing when she let the silence stretch after he asked how she'd found him.

Cyrus picked the contract up again. He didn't sign it. He carried it below instead, past the galley where Auntie Bee was pretending not to watch him, and locked it in the chart drawer under the false bottom where Elias used to keep the things he didn't want found.

Then he went up on deck and waited for his crew to wake up and tell him what an idiot he was.

* * *

Devi got there first, as she always did. She came up the companionway at half seven with a mug in each hand, handed him one without asking, and stood at the rail beside him looking out at the harbor the way she did when she was assembling an argument.

"You didn't sleep," she said.

"You did the math on our debt load last month. You tell me."

"I did the math. That's how I know the numbers in that contract don't fix anything. They just move the hole." She blew on her coffee. "Forty-one pages, Cyrus. Nobody writes forty-one pages unless they're afraid of something. Fear has lawyers."

"You read it?"

"I read the parts that matter. Page thirty-eight." She turned and looked at him then, and her face had none of the usual warmth in it. "She's not hiring a salvage crew. She's hiring the one crew in this sea that has Elias's charts. She knows about the charts. Which means somebody told her, which means either she's been sitting on that knowledge for ten years, or somebody who was there gave it to her. Both of those options should scare you."

"They do."

"And?"

Cyrus looked at the harbor. A naval patrol boat was coming in on the morning tide, white and clean, moving with the unhurried confidence of something that had never been told no. He thought about the survey site, the lieutenant's rehearsed courtesy, the coordinates he'd logged and never mentioned to anyone.

"And I want to know what's down there," he said. "Ten years, Devi. Ten years of not asking. Elias took it to his grave and we let him, because asking felt like digging him up. But Maren knows something. The navy knows something. And we're the only ones standing in the dark."

"That's not a reason to sign. That's a reason to ask her questions first."

"I did ask. She gave me a photograph and a number."

"She gave you a *hook*," Devi said, "and you're measuring the hook by how good the bait looks." She drained her coffee and set the mug on the rail with a click that was louder than it should have been. "I'm not saying don't. I'm saying don't pretend it's courage. Courage is a choice you make with your eyes open. This is a choice you're making because you can't stand the dark, and there's a difference."

She went below. She didn't slam anything, which was worse.

* * *

Tomas found him in the engine room at noon, which was where Cyrus went when he wanted to think, because the noise made thinking honest. You couldn't romanticize anything down there. The engine was either running or it wasn't.

Tomas didn't say anything at first. He picked up a rag and started wiping down the housing of the port injector, which didn't need wiping, and worked beside Cyrus in the clatter for a while.

"Auntie Bee says you signed it," he said finally.

"I haven't signed it."

"She says you *will*." Tomas shrugged, unbothered. "She's usually right about people. It's unsettling."

"You have an opinion?"

"I have a question." Tomas set the rag down. "When we find her—the *Corvane*—what do we do with what we find? Because Maren's contract says we hand it over. But I've known you ten years, and I know you don't hand things over. Not things that matter."

"That's page thirty-eight talking."

"That's *you* talking. I'm just repeating it back." He leaned against the housing. "I was nineteen when Rook and Sela died. I carried their gear off the boat. I still have Sela's backup light in my kit—I've never changed the batteries, don't ask me why. If we go down there and find out why they died, I want to be the one holding it. Not Maren. Not her post office box. Us."

Cyrus looked at him for a long moment. Tomas had come aboard as a kid with nothing, all elbows and appetite, and somewhere in ten years he'd turned into a man who could say things like that without flinching.

"You'd follow me down there," Cyrus said. It wasn't a question.

"I'd follow you into a burning building if you told me there was an answer inside." Tomas smiled, and it wasn't entirely a happy smile. "That's the problem with you, boss. You only tell us the truth when it's already too late to turn around."

* * *

Auntie Bee didn't come to him at all. She made him come to her, which was her way, and had been since the day Elias first brought her aboard with a crate of spices and a lie about being a qualified navigator.

She was in the galley at dusk, making the kind of meal that meant *we need to talk*—nothing fancy, just the crew's favorites, laid out with the care of a woman setting a table for a verdict. Cyrus sat. She put a bowl in front of him. She sat across from him and folded her hands.

"Elias signed a contract once," she said.

Cyrus stopped with the spoon halfway up. "I know."

"You know the shape of it. You don't know the words." Her voice was even, the voice she used for weather reports and hard truths. "The consortium came to him eight months before the *Corvane* went down. I saw the man. I made him tea. He wore good shoes and he talked about salvage rights and survey work, and Elias listened and nodded and signed something, and after the man left, Elias burned the copy and didn't eat for two days."

"What are you telling me?"

"I'm telling you that the last time this family signed a paper we didn't fully understand, two divers went into the water and only their gear came back. And Elias spent the rest of his life paying for whatever he agreed to, and he paid in silence, and he made us pay in silence too, and I let him, God forgive me, because I thought he was protecting us." She reached across and turned his bowl so it faced him properly, a small domestic gesture that landed like a blow. "So before you put your name on that paper, boy, you ask yourself one question. Not *can we afford to*. Not *do I want to know*. Just this: when Maren Voss looks at you, does she look like a woman hiring a diver, or a woman paying a debt?"

He didn't answer. He didn't have to. She read his face the way she read weather.

"That's what I thought," she said, and stood, and started clearing dishes, and that was the end of the conversation, because with Auntie Bee it always was.

* * *

He went back to the bar the next evening.

Maren was there, in the same corner, and she'd dressed the part again—the linen, the watch, the look of a woman whose money was old and whose patience was older. But when he sat down across from her, he saw the thing under it, the thing she'd let flicker once before and controlled better now: she was afraid. Not of him. Of the calendar. Of whatever clock was running behind her eyes.

"You read it," she said.

"Forty-one pages."

"Lawyers."

"Fear has lawyers. A friend told me that." He put the contract on the table, unsigned, and laid his hand flat on top of it. "Before I sign, you're going to answer one question honestly. Not well—honestly. I'll know the difference. I've had ten years of practice knowing the difference."

Something moved behind her eyes. Calculation, mostly, and under the calculation, a kind of exhaustion.

"Ask."

"Why me? Not the charts—anyone could buy charts, you could buy the whole hydrographic office. Why the crew of the *Perdita*? Why the man whose captain swore off this exact wreck and made sure everyone who loved him swore off it too?"

The bar noise went on around them—glasses, laughter, a football match nobody was winning. Maren looked at him for a long time, and he watched her decide, in real time, how much truth the moment could carry.

"Because your captain was the last person to dive her," she said. "And because whatever's down there, he knew about it before she sank. And because I think—" she stopped. Started again, quieter. "I think Elias Kavanagh spent ten years trying to undo something. And I think you're the only person alive who might finish it."

"That's not an answer. That's a story."

"It's the true part of the answer. The rest is in the water." She slid a pen across the table. It was a cheap ballpoint, deliberately, he realized—the kind of pen that left no impression of wealth, no fingerprints of importance. "Sign it or don't, Cyrus. But if you don't, the *Halcyon Dawn* finds her in three weeks, and whatever Elias was trying to undo stays undone, and you get to spend the rest of your life the way he spent his."

He picked up the pen. He told himself it was for the debt—eight months of back wages, a hull that needed plating, Tomas's mother's medical bills that Tomas pretended didn't exist. He told himself it was pride, that no corporate fleet with borrowed charts was going to take a wreck out of his sea. He told himself it was curiosity, clean and professional, the same instinct that had him logging coordinates the navy didn't want logged.

He knew, sitting there in the bad light with Maren Voss watching him the way people watch a diver go under, that it was none of those things. It was a boy's question wearing a man's coat: *what did you do, Elias? what did you do, and why did you make us carry it with you?*

He signed. The ink came out thin and blue, unimportant-looking, and his name looked smaller on the page than it ever had in his own hand.

Maren took the contract, checked the signature, and folded it away with the efficiency of someone closing a door.

"First advance hits your account tonight," she said. "Provision fast. The monsoon won't wait for your feelings, and neither will the fleet." She stood, dropped bills on the table that covered the drinks twice over, and paused, one hand on the chair back.

"For what it's worth," she said, not looking at him, "I'm sorry it's you. I mean that."

Then she was gone through the crowd, and Cyrus sat alone with two empty glasses and the shape of what he'd done, which felt less like courage and more like stepping off a ledge in the dark and calling the falling a kind of flight.

He walked back to the harbor through the warm night, and the *Perdita* sat at her mooring with her riding lights on, patient as a dog that knows it's about to be taken somewhere bad.

Devi was waiting in the wheelhouse. She didn't ask. She just looked at his face, and nodded once, and said:

"Then we provision at first light. And Cyrus—whatever she's not telling us, we find it before she does. Agreed?"

"Agreed," he said.

Below, in the chart drawer under the false bottom, Elias's old charts waited in the dark, and Cyrus went down to get them, and his hands were steady, and that frightened him more than anything else.
