## 7. Outfitting the Perdita

The Perdita sat in the graving yard at Tanah Merah like a patient who'd been told she had a week to live and decided to spend it arguing.

Tomas Reyes stood on the dock with a clipboard he didn't trust and a coffee he did, watching Devi Okafor argue with a crane operator about sling angles. The argument had been going for eleven minutes. Devi was winning, which was inevitable, but the crane operator hadn't figured that out yet, and Tomas was in no hurry to end anyone's entertainment.

"Reyes." Auntie Bee's voice came from behind him, carrying the particular weight of a woman who had already made three decisions before breakfast. "You're on hull watch today. The yard boys patch what I tell them to patch, in the order I tell them, and you make sure of it."

"Since when do I supervise plating?"

"Since we have money for plating and no time to waste it." She pressed a laminated list into his hand. It was written in her handwriting, which meant it was law. "Portside frames nine through fourteen. The ones Cyrus says are fine and I say are lying."

"He actually said they're fine?"

"He said, and I quote, 'they'll hold.' Which is what a man says when he means *they'll hold until exactly the moment I need them not to.*" She took a sip from her thermos and looked out over the yard with the expression of a general surveying terrain she intended to own. "We're going somewhere deep, boy. Deep water doesn't forgive optimism."

She walked off toward the chandlery, thermos swinging, leaving Tomas holding the list and the strange new feeling of being trusted with something structural.

Ten years on the Perdita and he'd mostly been ballast with opinions. Deckhand, diver's tender, the guy who kept the diesel honest and the mooring lines coiled. But something had shifted since Maren Voss walked into the harbour bar wearing a white linen jacket and a contract worth more than the tug's insured value. The crew moved differently now. Faster. Like a clock someone had quietly wound too tight.

* * *

By noon the yard looked like a surgery.

The Perdita was hauled out just far enough to expose her port strake, and two welders Tomas had known since he was nineteen were laying beads along frame eleven while Devi stood underneath with a flashlight, inspecting every centimeter like a customs officer with a grudge.

"Porosity," she said, tapping the fresh weld.

"That's flux," said the welder, whose name was Anwar and who had once pulled Devi out of a fuel tank fire and therefore felt entitled to disagree with her.

"It's porosity, Anwar. Grind it and run it again."

"You want this hull done in a week or done pretty?"

"I want it done once." Devi's voice didn't rise. It never rose. That was the terrifying thing about her. "Deep water does one take, brother. No second screening."

Anwar muttered something in Malay that was probably a compliment and ground the weld back down.

Tomas crouched at the edge of the cradle and handed Devi a rag for her hands. "You slept?"

"Four hours."

"Liar."

"Three." She wiped her palms and looked up at him, and there were shadows under her eyes the color of engine oil. "I can't stop running numbers, Tomo. The gas plan alone. If we're diving past fifty meters on mixed gas, we need redundancy we don't own. Bailout bottles, stage cylinders, a proper shot line with a decompression trapeze. And the sonar—" She shook her head. "Our sonar is a fish finder with delusions. We need side-scan, real side-scan, and the only unit available on short notice belongs to a man in Singapore who charges by the day and asks no questions, which tells you everything about the questions other people ask him."

"So we're renting the good sonar from a criminal."

"We're renting it from an entrepreneur with flexible ethics. There's a difference. The difference is about four hundred dollars a day."

Tomas laughed despite himself. That was the thing about Devi — she could price the apocalypse and make it sound like a bargain. He'd learned more engineering in ten years of listening to her swear at machinery than he had in six years of technical school, and half of what he'd learned was that machines fail in patterns, and people do too, and the trick was noticing which pattern you were in before it finished.

He went back to the list.

* * *

The gear arrived in pieces, the way dubious things always do.

A crate marked AGRICULTURAL PARTS — SURABAYA yielded four steel stage cylinders, hydro-tested eight months ago, valves wrapped in newspaper from a country none of them had ever dived in. A second crate, smaller, held regulators: three sets of mixed-gas first stages, cold-water rated, serial numbers filed to smooth blankness.

Tomas held one up to the light and turned it slowly. "These are beautiful."

"They're stolen," said Cyrus, passing behind him with a coil of rope over one shoulder.

"They're *borrowed*."

"They're stolen by someone who has excellent taste." Cyrus dropped the rope into the hold and came over, taking the regulator from Tomas's hands. He checked the seat, the diaphragm, the intermediate pressure spring, all without looking, the way a musician tunes by ear. "Whoever lifted these knew what they were lifting. That's either reassuring or very much the opposite."

"Which one are you voting for?"

Cyrus set the regulator down carefully, aligning it with the others, and for a moment he had the look he got sometimes — the thousand-meter stare, like he was reading water nobody else could see. "Neither. I'm voting for functional. Everything else is decoration." Then he clapped Tomas on the shoulder, brief and hard, and was gone down the ladder toward the engine room, where he'd spent most of three days helping Devi chase a vibration in the port shaft that both of them pretended wasn't also a metaphor.

That was Cyrus. Ten years and Tomas still couldn't tell whether the man trusted any of them or simply hadn't gotten around to distrusting them yet. He ran the crew like a dive plan: everyone had a role, everyone had a depth limit, and nobody talked about the bottom.

* * *

Wednesday was the sonar.

The entrepreneur with flexible ethics sent it by ferry, packed in foam inside a case stenciled MEDICAL EQUIPMENT, with a handwritten note taped to the lid: *Calibrated Tuesday. Return in same condition or I keep your tug as collateral. — R.*

Devi installed it herself, splicing into the Perdita's power bus with a soldering iron and a patience she extended to no human being. By evening the display was painting the harbor bottom in silver-grey relief, and they all crowded into the wheelhouse to watch it like children watching television.

"There," Auntie Bee said, pointing at a smudge of return off the breakwater. "That's the *Selamat*, sunk in '09. And that bright spot near the channel marker is somebody's lost outboard, and somebody else is going to be very happy when I tell them where it is."

"You're going to charge them for it," Tomas said.

"I'm going to *generously inform* them, and then they're going to insist on compensating me, because that's how grace works." She tapped the screen. "Look at the resolution on this thing. At depth, this will show us a hull outline, deck structures, maybe even openings if the angle's right. This isn't equipment, boy. This is eyes."

Eyes for what, nobody said. They all knew the name. They'd been not-saying it for a week with the discipline of monks.

* * *

It was Thursday evening, hauling the last of the dive gear aboard, when Tomas saw the liaison officer photographing their lists.

Maren Voss's man was named Callis — a lean, sunburnt individual with a corporate lanyard and the permanent expression of someone doing arithmetic about you. He'd appeared the day after the contract was signed, ostensibly to "coordinate logistics," which so far had meant drinking Auntie Bee's coffee, staying out of the way, and asking questions shaped like small talk.

Tomas was coming up the dock with a crate of dive lights when he saw Callis standing by the gangway, phone raised, photographing the clipboard hanging by the wheelhouse door.

The clipboard held the master equipment manifest. Every cylinder, every regulator, every meter of shot line, the gas blending ratios, the decompression schedules Devi had built. Ten days of their planning, hanging in plain sight because that's how a working boat runs.

Callis lowered the phone, thumbed through something, raised it again. Took a second shot. Maybe a third.

Tomas stood very still with the crate in his arms and watched him do it, and felt something in his chest go from warm to cold in the space between one wave slap and the next.

* * *

He didn't say anything right away. That was the discipline Auntie Bee had beaten into him years ago — *observe first, report clean, never report a story.* So he finished stowing the lights, noted the time, noted the position of the sun, and then found Cyrus in the engine room, elbow-deep in the shaft bearing housing with Devi handing him tools.

"Problem," Tomas said.

Cyrus's head came around. One word from a deckhand didn't usually rate that kind of attention, but Cyrus had a diver's instinct for tone, and Tomas's tone had gone flat.

"Talk."

"Your employer's man just photographed our full equipment manifest off the wheelhouse clipboard. Twice. Maybe three times. Gas mixes, schedules, everything."

The engine room got quiet enough to hear the bilge pump cycling. Devi set down the wrench she was holding with exaggerated care, the way she did when she wanted to throw it.

"You sure?" Cyrus asked.

"I'm sure it was him, sure it was the clipboard, and sure he wasn't photographing the sunset. It was facing the wall, boss."

Cyrus climbed out of the bilge, wiped his hands on a rag that made them dirtier, and stood thinking. Tomas watched him think and recognized the process — the same slow, methodical sweep he used on a wreck site. What's here. What moved. What it means.

"Could be innocent," Devi said. "Fleet ops wants documentation for insurance—"

"Fleet ops gets documentation by asking," Cyrus said. "Nobody photographs a clipboard twice unless they're building a file." He looked at Tomas. "Did he see you see him?"

"No. I kept walking."

"Good. Don't change anything. Clipboard stays where it is." A ghost of a smile crossed his face, thin and sharp. "From tonight, though, the real manifest lives somewhere else, and the clipboard holds a slightly creative version. Devi — swap the gas mix percentages. Nothing dramatic. Enough that if someone's planning around our numbers, they're planning around fiction."

"And the schedules?" Devi asked.

"Leave those accurate. If they come down here to check on us, I want our deco profile to look survivable. People who think you'll die slower leave you alone longer."

Tomas felt the words settle into him with a weight that was almost physical. This was the part of the life the brochures never mentioned — not the diving, not the salvage, but the constant low-grade arithmetic of who was watching and why. Ten years in the grey zone taught you that every job had two cargoes: the one you were paid for, and the one someone else wanted catalogued.

"Auntie Bee should know," he said.

"Auntie Bee already knows," said Auntie Bee, from the top of the engine room ladder, where she had apparently been standing long enough to hear everything, because of course she had. She descended with the unhurried menace of a woman who owned the ladder. "I've been watching that man drink my coffee for a week. Nobody drinks that much of my coffee innocently." She fixed Tomas with a look. "Good reporting, boy. Clean observation. You're learning."

Coming from her, it landed like a medal. Tomas nodded and tried to look like he'd been cool about the whole thing instead of standing in an engine room with his heart going like a winch under load.

* * *

Friday night, the crew ate on deck because the galley was full of spare parts and Auntie Bee refused to cook around unsecured cylinders. Rice, fried fish, chili sauce fierce enough to strip paint. The yard lights glowed orange across the water. Somewhere aft, the new sonar hummed its standby note, a sound none of them had heard a week ago and already couldn't imagine the boat without.

"So," Devi said, pointing her chopsticks at the group generally. "Inventory. New sonar: yes. Hull: patched, re-plated, and better than she's been since before Elias died. Mixed-gas rig: assembled, tested, and of frankly criminal origin. Deco gear: adequate. Crew: exhausted." She looked around. "Anyone want to say the thing we're not saying?"

Silence. Even the harbor seemed to hold its breath.

"The Corvane," Tomas said.

Everyone looked at him. He shrugged, mouth full of fish. "Somebody had to. We've been doing the monk routine for a week and it's exhausting. There. The ship's name is out. Nobody's struck by lightning."

"You don't know that lightning won't wait," Auntie Bee said mildly, but she reached over and refilled his bowl, which was her way of agreeing.

Cyrus sat at the end of the bench, saying nothing, looking at the water. Tomas studied him sideways. He'd known the man ten years and had catalogued perhaps forty of his expressions; this was number thirty-one, the one that meant a decision had already been made and the rest of them were being allowed to catch up.

"We leave early," Cyrus said finally. "Ahead of schedule. Monday's tide, not Thursday's."

"Because of the photographs," Devi said.

"Because of the photographs, because the weather window's closing, and because every extra day in this harbor is a day someone smarter than Callis figures out what we're actually doing." He picked up his tea. "We're not ready. We're ready enough. There's a difference and we're going to find out what it costs."

"Cheerful," said Devi.

"Gallows humor is free," said Auntie Bee. "Everything else on this job has a surcharge."

They laughed — tired, real laughter, the kind that comes after a week of welding fumes and filed-off serial numbers. Tomas looked around the deck at these people, his people, the only family structure that had ever made sense to him: an engineer who priced catastrophe, a cook-navigator who ran the boat like a small nation, a captain who spoke in tide tables. Broken in various places, all of them, but fitted together like patched plating — the seams visible if you knew where to look, and holding anyway.

He thought about Callis's phone, and the clipboard, and the file someone was building somewhere with their names on it. Fear was there, sure. Sitting in his chest like a stone. But underneath the fear, something else had hardened this week, something he could feel when he gripped the rail: they were going to do this thing, whatever it was, whatever it cost, and they were going to do it together, and God help whoever thought a photograph of a clipboard told them who they were dealing with.

* * *

Sunday night, Tomas made his last walk of the deck before turning in. The yard was quiet. Across the water, the harbor bar threw its usual yellow light, and parked outside it, unmistakable even at distance, was the rental car Callis had arrived in a week ago.

Except Callis wasn't in the bar. He was standing at the end of the dock, phone to his ear, back turned, and as Tomas watched from the shadow of the wheelhouse, the man said something short, glanced at the Perdita, and said something else — and even across the water, even without hearing a word, Tomas understood the shape of it.

*Reporting.*

He stood in the dark a long moment, heart steady, hands steady, everything steady except the future.

Then he went below and knocked on Cyrus's door.

"Cyrus. We've got a problem, and I think we've got until morning."
