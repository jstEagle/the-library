## 8. First Tide Out

The customs launch was supposed to be at the fuel dock at 0600.

Cyrus knew this because Maren's liaison officer—a smooth, forgettable man named Calloway who smelled of hotel soap and government stationery—had told him so twice, in the same tone, on two different days. *Inspection at 0600, Mr. Kavanagh. Standard for a vessel carrying mixed-gas equipment out of Tanah Merah. Nothing to worry about.*

Nothing to worry about. In Cyrus's experience, those four words were the sound a trap made just before it closed.

So at 0417, with the harbor still black and the tide three hours from slack, Cyrus stood in the wheelhouse of the *Perdita* and watched Devi walk the deck one last time, checking lashings by flashlight, her breath fogging in the pre-dawn damp. Tomas was forward, coiling lines he'd already coiled twice. Auntie Bee had come aboard an hour ago without being asked, carrying two crates of provisions she refused to itemize, and had installed herself in the galley as if she'd never left it.

"Engines are warm," Devi said, appearing in the doorway. She didn't look at him when she said it. She'd barely looked at him since he'd signed the contract, and Cyrus understood that too. Some kinds of anger were just loyalty wearing a mask.

"Then we go," he said.

"We're supposed to wait for inspection."

"We're supposed to be inspected at 0600." He tapped the chart table, where the tide tables lay open under his thumb. "We're leaving now."

Devi finally looked up. Her eyes were red-rimmed—she hadn't slept well either, or maybe she'd spent the night doing what Cyrus had done, which was lying in his bunk staring at the overhead and running the arithmetic of the contract over and over until the numbers stopped meaning anything. The pay was obscene. That was the word everyone kept using. Obscene. And obscenity, in Cyrus's experience, was usually a down payment on something worse.

"She arranged it," Devi said quietly. "The inspection. You know that."

"I know that."

"So we're not dodging customs. We're following her schedule."

Cyrus let that sit for a moment. Outside, the harbor lights smeared across the flat water, and somewhere beyond the breakwater the sea waited, grey and patient, the way it always did. Ten years he'd worked this coast and the sea had never once lied to him. People lied constantly. The sea just killed you honestly or left you alone.

"That's exactly why we're going early," he said. "If she wants us out on her schedule, I want to be out on mine. Let her find us already gone."

Devi held his gaze for a long moment. Then something in her shoulders loosened—not agreement, exactly, but the closest thing to it she could manage. "Tomas already cast off the spring line," she said. "He's been ready since midnight."

Of course he had. Cyrus almost smiled. His crew had read him before he'd read himself. That was what ten years bought you—the ability to hear a decision before it was spoken.

* * *

They went out on the ebb, riding the last of the night's dark like a thief riding a stolen horse.

Tanah Merah harbor was a working harbor, which meant it slept lightly but not well, and the *Perdita* slipped past the moored trawlers and the sleeping fish-processing barges with her running lights dimmed to nothing. Cyrus took her out himself, hands on the wheel, feeling the old tug answer him the way she always did—a little heavy in the turn, a little stubborn in the swell, honest as a hammer. Forty years old if she was a day, re-engined twice, patched more times than anyone could count, and still the truest thing in his life.

Behind them, the customs launch sat at the fuel dock, dark and empty. Waiting for 0600. Waiting for a ship that was already a mile out and opening the gap.

"You want to log our departure time?" Tomas asked from the wing, grinning around a mouthful of bread Auntie Bee had pressed on him. "Because officially, we're still tied up."

"Log it as 0600," Cyrus said. "Let the paperwork match their expectations. Paperwork is a story you tell the government, and the government only checks whether the story is boring enough."

"That's cynical," Tomas said.

"That's survival."

Auntie Bee appeared with coffee—real coffee, hoarded from some private reserve no one had ever located—and handed mugs around the wheelhouse without ceremony. She was a small woman, wide through the hips and hands, with a face like a folded map: every route she'd ever taken written into the creases. She'd cooked on coastal freighters before Cyrus was born, navigated half of them when their officers were drunk, and buried one husband to the sea and another to a lawyer, and she spoke of both losses with the same tone of mild inconvenience.

She handed Cyrus his mug last and held onto it a beat longer than necessary.

"You didn't sleep," she said.

"I slept."

"You have the face of a man who slept the way a stone sleeps." She released the mug. "Drink. It's bad for you to steer tired, and worse to steer guilty, and you're both."

"I'm not guilty."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "You're something else. But it keeps the same hours." She turned back toward the galley, then paused at the door. "Elias used to say that. About guilt. That it keeps the same hours as the tide—you can't stop it coming in, you can only decide what it floats."

She left before he could answer, which was her way. Auntie Bee never asked a question she didn't already know the answer to; she just delivered the answer disguised as an observation and let you live with it.

Cyrus drank the coffee. It was terrible and perfect.

* * *

By full light they were clear of the harbor approaches and standing southeast along the coast, the land falling away behind them into a haze of green and woodsmoke. The weather was holding—for now. Everyone aboard had seen the satellite feeds, the great white spiral arm of the monsoon turning in the basin like a slow thought. Five days, maybe six, before the leading edge reached them. Maren's timeline. Maren's window. Everything about this job came back to Maren's numbers, and Cyrus was beginning to hate how much room they occupied in his head.

Devi ran the new sonar through its paces while Tomas tested the decompression rig in the shade of the deckhouse, checking regulators and gauges with the methodical suspicion of a man who didn't trust equipment that arrived too easily. Which was correct, because the gear had arrived too easily. Two days after the contract was signed, a crate had shown up at the yard with no invoice and a shipping label from a company that, as far as Devi could determine, existed mainly as a phone number. Good gear, though. Excellent gear. The kind of gear that cost more than the *Perdita* was worth, given away like a gift.

Nobody gives gifts like that. They give bait.

"Calloway called again," Tomas reported, coming into the wheelhouse around noon. "Wants a position update every twelve hours. Says it's for safety coordination."

"Safety coordination," Devi repeated, without looking up from the sonar display.

"That's what he said."

"He also say who else is coordinating?"

Tomas glanced at Cyrus. "No. But he asked which route we're taking. Twice. Same question, two different ways, like he was checking my answers against each other."

Cyrus felt the cold settle into his stomach, familiar as an old injury. He'd been played before—in the salvage trade, being played wasn't a risk, it was a weather condition—but there was a difference between being skimmed and being steered, and everything about Calloway's questions smelled of steering. Maren knew where they were going. Of course she did; she'd drawn the target on the map herself. So why ask about the route? Unless someone besides Maren wanted the route confirmed. Unless the position updates weren't going where Calloway claimed.

"Tell him we'll report at the intervals specified in the contract," Cyrus said. "And nothing more. If he asks about routes again, tell him the *Perdita* goes where the work is and the work goes where the charts say."

"And if he pushes?"

"Then he's telling us something, isn't he."

Tomas nodded and went out to make the call, and Cyrus stood at the wheel and watched the coastline slide by and tried to name the feeling in his chest. It wasn't fear, exactly. Fear was simple; fear was for storms and blowouts and the moment when your backup reg coughed at depth. This was something older and more uncomfortable. It was the feeling of moving fast in a direction you hadn't chosen.

Exhilaration, he realized. That was the shameful part. Ten years of small wrecks and thin margins and careful, invisible work, and here he was running ahead of a customs inspection with borrowed gear and blood money in escrow, and some traitor part of him was *alive* in a way it hadn't been since—

Since Elias. Since the last time they'd run hot on someone else's money toward a wreck nobody wanted found.

He gripped the wheel harder and put the thought down the way you'd put down a lit fuse.

* * *

They raised the outer islands at dusk, low green humps strung along the horizon like beads on a wire. The plan was to pass outside them through the night and make the deep-water approach by morning, staying clear of the shipping lanes and clearer of attention. Devi had the watch first. Cyrus should have gone below to sleep—he knew the math of it, knew that a dive supervisor who arrives exhausted makes decisions with other people's lives—but instead he stayed in the wheelhouse, drinking tea he didn't want, watching the sonar paint the seabed in soft greens and yellows.

The channel was down there. Somewhere under forty fathoms of ordinary water, hidden among reefs that official charts marked as impassable, Elias had found a way in. Auntie Bee had spent two evenings with the old charts before they sailed, tracing his annotations with one blunt finger, and she'd gone quiet in a way that Cyrus had learned, over ten years, to treat as an alarm. She hadn't said what she'd found. She'd said only: *"When we're closer. Not on the radio. Not on paper."*

Which meant whatever Elias had mapped, it wasn't just skill. Skill you could talk about.

Devi relieved herself off watch and came to stand beside him, and for a while neither of them spoke. That was the luxury of a decade together—silence that didn't need filling.

"Tomas thinks Calloway's reporting to the fleet," she said finally. "The corporate one. Halcyon Dawn or whatever they call themselves."

"Tomas thinks everyone's reporting to everyone. It's why he's still alive."

"I think it too." She crossed her arms. "Cyrus. Say it plainly, once, so we both heard it. Are we diving this wreck for us, or for her?"

The question hung in the wheelhouse air, and Cyrus looked at it honestly, because Devi deserved honesty even when he couldn't give her answers.

"For us," he said. "Whatever she wants, whatever her backers want—the reason I signed is ours. There's something down there that's been sitting in my chest for ten years wearing Elias's name. I need to know what it is. Even if it kills the memory."

Devi studied him for a long moment. "That's the first true thing you've said since the bar," she said. "It's also the most dangerous. Because men who need answers stop checking their corners."

"I know."

"Do you?" She touched the sonar display, where the seabed scrolled past, indifferent and endless. "Because I've been checking mine since we weighed anchor, and every corner has her fingerprints on it. The inspection timing. The gear crate. The route questions. We're not sneaking out of harbor, Cyrus. We're being *escorted*. We just haven't been shown the escort yet."

Cyrus said nothing, because there was nothing to say that she hadn't already said better. He watched the dark water and felt the truth of it settle over him like ballast: they were moving fast, and the speed wasn't theirs.

Below, in the galley, Auntie Bee was singing something old and low, a working song in a language Tomas didn't speak and Devi pretended not to. The *Perdita* pushed southeast through the night at eight knots, steady as a heartbeat, and sixty miles ahead of her—though none of them knew it yet, though the sonar showed nothing but clean empty water—the reef field waited, and under the reef field the trench, and in the trench a ship that had been keeping its secret for ten years, patient as the dead.

At 2300, the radio crackled. Calloway's voice, smooth as oiled teak, polite as a subpoena:

"*Perdita*, be advised—weather window closing faster than forecast. Ms. Voss recommends you press on through the night. No delays."

Cyrus keyed the mic and looked at the words a long moment before answering. *Recommends.* As if Maren were aboard. As if the decision were already made and he was merely being informed of it.

"Perdita copies," he said finally. "We'll press on."

He set the mic down and stood alone in the wheelhouse with the engine thrumming under his feet like a second pulse, and admitted the thing he'd been refusing to admit since the harbor:

Maren hadn't arranged the inspection to be dodged.

She'd arranged it to be *missed*—so that when anyone asked later how a scrupulously legal salvage tug happened to leave Tanah Merah without clearance, the record would show the *Perdita* had slipped her inspection. Had run. Had something to hide.

She was building them an alibi in reverse. A story that would hang them, if hanging became necessary.

And he'd sailed straight into it, on the first tide out, exactly on schedule.

Cyrus turned the wheel a point to starboard, off the recommended track, into water no one had asked him about, and told himself it was a small defiance. It didn't feel small. It felt like the first move of a game whose rules he was only beginning to see—and somewhere out in the dark, on a ship flying flags that matched nothing, someone was already noting the change of course.
