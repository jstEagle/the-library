## 9. Reading the Old Charts

Auntie Bee had a rule about paper: it never lied, but it never told the whole truth either. Charts were the worst kind of paper—drawn by people who wanted to be believed, corrected by people who knew better, and annotated by people who thought they'd live forever.

She spread Elias Kavanagh's charts across the Perdita's mess table at two in the morning, when the only light was the gimbal lamp and the only sound was Tomas snoring somewhere aft and Devi's radio hissing static in the wheelhouse above. She'd waited three days for this. Three days since Cyrus signed Maren Voss's contract with a face like a man signing his own autopsy, and nobody aboard had said the name *Corvane* out loud except once, in Devi's cabin, behind a closed door that Bee had pretended not to stand near.

The charts came out of the chart locker under the helm, bottom drawer, wrapped in oilcloth—the way you wrap something you mean to keep but never want to look at. Bee had known they were there for ten years. She'd dusted around them the way you dust around a photograph of someone who broke your heart.

Elias's hand was unmistakable. Big block letters for place names, because he said cursive drowned. Tiny numerals everywhere else, packed into margins like ants. He'd been a surveyor before he was a salvager, back when the government still paid him to measure water, and it showed: every line ruled true, every depth sounding placed where a careful man would put it.

Bee poured herself tea she wasn't going to drink and started at the northern sheet, the one covering the Sembilan Passage, and worked south.

The first hour was ordinary. Soundings, current arrows in Elias's neat hand, a note about a fish trap that had been there in 2011 and probably still was, because fish traps outlive governments. She found herself smiling at one annotation—"T. says reef here is 'nothing,' T. is an optimist"—and had to stop for a moment with her hand flat on the paper, feeling the smile turn into something else on its way through her chest.

Then she reached the middle sheet.

It covered the coast from Tanjung Rhu down to the deep water past the Karang Seratus reefs—a stretch of grey zone where the chart showed nothing but warnings: *unsurveyed*, *dangerous in monsoon*, *local knowledge required*. The kind of water where ships went to disappear and salvage crews went to find them.

Elias had surveyed it anyway.

Not officially—there was no hydrographic office stamp, no field-sheet number. This was private work, done on their own runs over years, and he'd built it up in pencil first and inked it later, which meant he'd trusted it. Bee knew his habits. Pencil was thinking. Ink was certainty.

And the channel he'd drawn—the deep-water approach threading between the Karang Seratus reefs, the one every published chart marked as impassable—was inked.

She sat back. The lamp hummed. Somewhere above, Devi shifted in the wheelhouse chair, and the hull ticked against its mooring lines.

Because here was the thing Bee couldn't get past, the thing that made her pour the tea out cold into the scupper rather than drink it: the soundings weren't guesses. They were dated. Small dates beside clusters of numbers, the way a proper surveyor logs a run—*14/03/09*, *02/11/10*, *19/06/12*—three separate passes over the same ground, years apart, each confirming the last. A man doesn't run the same unmarked channel three times across four years because he's curious. He runs it because he's keeping something ready.

And the final pass—the deepest set of soundings, the ones that turned the penciled channel into ink—was dated 21/04/13.

The Corvane went down on 04/05/13.

Two weeks.

Bee put both hands flat on the table to stop them doing anything foolish. She was sixty-one years old and she had buried a husband, raised no children of her own but fed half the harbor's orphans, and she had learned that the body knows a truth before the mind consents to it. Her body knew now. Something cold walked up her spine and settled between her shoulders like a hand.

Elias had mapped the Corvane's grave two weeks before the Corvane died.

*

She didn't sleep. She worked instead, because working was what she did with grief and always had been—you fed it, gave it tasks, kept its hands busy so it wouldn't start breaking things.

By four she had the whole picture laid out chronologically across the mess table, sheets overlapping, and the pattern was worse than the single fact. It wasn't just the channel. Elias had annotated everything around it. Tide tables copied out for dates in late April and early May 2013, with slack-water windows circled. Current notes: *sets east along trench after 0400, slackens toward dawn*. A remark in the margin beside the deepest hole in the whole reef field, written small and pressed hard enough to emboss the paper: *62m at MLWS. Bottom soft. Good holding.*

Good holding. As in, for a wreck. As in, a place where a ship could settle and stay put and be findable again.

Bee read that phrase maybe twenty times. Each time it meant the same thing and each time she hoped it would mean something different. A surveyor writes *good holding* about anchorages. Nobody writes it about open water unless they're thinking about what will rest on the bottom there.

There was more. On the southern edge of the sheet, half-hidden under a later correction strip, Elias had noted a rendezvous point—a set of coordinates with no explanation, just the date 28/04/13 and the word *meeting* in his block capitals. And beneath that, in pencil, so lightly it was almost an apology: *asked twice. paid half.*

Asked twice. Paid half. Half of what? Asked whom? The pencil note had been rubbed at—not erased, Elias never erased, but worried at with a thumb, the way a man worries a tooth that's gone bad.

Bee sat in the lamplight and let ten years rearrange themselves around her.

She had cooked for this crew since before some of them could legally buy beer. She had watched Elias teach Cyrus to read water the way his own father taught him, patient and exacting, a man building something. She had stood at the rail the morning after Rook and Sela died and watched Elias come up the ladder gray-faced with another man's blood on his sleeve, and she had made him tea and asked no questions, because that was the deal on boats like this: you fed the silence and it fed you back.

She had told herself for ten years that the silence was grief. That Elias carried those two divers the way a mule carries too much weight—badly, stubbornly, until it killed him. And maybe he had. But grief doesn't draw tide tables for a sinking that hasn't happened yet. Grief doesn't write *good holding* about a grave two weeks before anyone's in it.

Unless—

Unless the man who drew these charts hadn't been planning the sinking. Unless he'd been told about it. Paid half, asked twice. Unless Elias Kavanagh had known the Corvane was going to die, had mapped the best place for her to die, and had taken money for the knowing—and then, when the dying happened and it was worse than whatever he'd been promised it would be, when there were survivors in the water and divers going down and two of his own people on the bottom—he had spent the rest of his life trying to buy the knowledge back.

Bee pressed the heel of her hand against her mouth. In the galley behind her, the rice cooker ticked as it cooled, domestic and indifferent.

She thought about the night Elias forbade them to speak of it. Not suggested. Forbade—slammed his hand on this very table hard enough to jump the dishes, the only time any of them had ever seen him angry at his own crew. *That name doesn't get said on my boat,* he'd said. *Not while I'm alive.* And they'd obeyed, because he was Elias, and because obedience was easier than questions.

Now she understood the anger differently. It hadn't been grief protecting itself. It had been guilt standing guard.

*

Dawn came up gray through the portholes, the color of old dishwater, and Bee heard Devi's boots on the ladder well before she saw her. Devi came in smelling of coffee and engine grease and stopped dead when she saw the table.

Charts everywhere. Dates circled in red—the red was Bee's; she'd borrowed the marker from the nav station—and the mess table looking like the scene of an intervention.

Devi looked at the charts. Then at Bee. Then at the charts again, longer, and Bee watched her do the arithmetic—the same arithmetic, in the same order, that Bee had done six hours earlier. She watched it land.

"That's the Corvane's ground," Devi said quietly. "Karang Seratus. That's—" She touched the inked channel with one finger, not quite letting the finger land. "He surveyed this. When?"

"Over four years. Final pass two weeks before she sank."

Devi pulled out a chair and sat down slowly, the way you sit when your legs have opinions of their own. "No. No, that's—Bee, that's not right. Elias wouldn't—" She stopped. Started again. "He loved that man's memory too much" wasn't a sentence anyone finished aloud on this boat, but Bee heard it anyway in the shape of the silence.

"He drew tide tables," Bee said. "For the week she went down. Circled the slack windows." She slid the southern sheet across. "And this. *Asked twice. Paid half.*"

Devi read it. Read it again. Bee saw her jaw change, saw the engineer in her take over from the grieving woman—the part of Devi that took engines apart to find out why they'd seized.

"Paid half of what?"

"That's what we're going to find out."

"From who? He's dead, Bee. Everyone who'd know is either dead or—"

"—or hired us to go there." Bee folded her hands. "Maren Voss shows up with obscene money and a target nobody speaks of, ten years to the season after two of ours went into that water. You believe in coincidence, Devi?"

"I believe in engines," Devi said. "Engines don't lie. People do." She was staring at the embossed pencil marks, at the places Elias had worried the paper with his thumb. "You think he knew. Before. You think he knew she was going down and he—"

"I think a man can be paid for knowledge he doesn't fully understand until it's too late to return it." Bee stood and began stacking the sheets, gently, the way you handle a thing that might break—or might cut. "I think he spent ten years trying to figure out how to give it back. And I think he ran out of tides."

Devi was quiet a long moment. Above them, footsteps crossed the deck—Cyrus, up early, checking moorings the way he checked everything, twice and then once more for luck.

"There's something else," Devi said finally. "I've been meaning to tell you. The sealed locker. Under his bunk. Cyrus has never opened it—not once in ten years. Won't let anyone touch it."

Bee looked at her.

"You're telling me this now why?"

"Because if Elias left a confession anywhere," Devi said, "it'll be in there. And because somebody on this boat has to say out loud what we're all thinking." She took a breath. "We're not diving a wreck next month. We're diving a crime scene. And our captain might've dug the grave."

Bee gathered the last chart, the northern sheet, and paused. In the margin, in Elias's block capitals, half worn away, was one more line she hadn't shown Devi yet—one she'd found at five in the morning and had been carrying around since like a stone in her shoe:

*If it goes wrong, the truth stays with me. No one else carries it.*

He'd written it beside the channel soundings. Beside the grave he'd measured in advance.

"No one else carries it," Bee murmured.

"What?"

"Nothing." She squared the sheets, tied the oilcloth back over them, and looked toward the companionway where Cyrus's footsteps were coming down the ladder. "Get some sleep, girl. And Devi—when he asks, and he will ask—we show him everything. All at once. No man should have to learn his father's sins one page at a time."

Through the porthole, the eastern sky had gone the color of unpainted steel, and far out past the harbor mole, the first pre-monsoon cloud was stacking itself up like something being built on purpose.

Cyrus's head appeared in the doorway. He looked at the table, at the oilcloth bundle, at their faces, and Bee watched him understand—instantly, completely—that the morning had already changed shape without him.

"What's wrong?" he said.

Bee untied the oilcloth.
