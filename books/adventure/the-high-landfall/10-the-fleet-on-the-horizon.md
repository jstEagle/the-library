## 10. The Fleet on the Horizon

Maren Voss had learned a long time ago that the sea told the truth about people. It stripped them down to what they actually were—competent or careless, brave or merely loud—and it did so without malice. She'd built her career on that principle. You watched how a diver handled a bad vis day, how a broker behaved when a deal went sideways, and you knew everything you needed to know.

Which was why she was standing at the rail of the Perdita's aft deck with a pair of borrowed binoculars, watching the Halcyon Dawn sit at anchor off Pulau Serak, and feeling the truth of herself come up cold out of the water.

The supply island was nothing—a fuel depot, a fishing cooperative, a rust-streaked jetty where islanders sold diesel by the drum and ice by the sack. Every salvage operation in the region staged through places like it. That was normal. That was fine.

What wasn't fine was the ship.

She was beautiful, for one thing. A hundred and ten meters of white steel and clean lines, helideck aft, moon pool amidships, cranes that could lift a small building. The kind of vessel that cost more per day to operate than the Perdita grossed in a year. Maren had seen her class before—she'd even been aboard a sister ship once, in another life, drinking champagne in a conference room while men in pressed shirts explained saturation diving to her like she was a child.

But the flags were wrong.

There were three of them flying from the signal halyards: the house flag of Meridian Recovery Group, which was expected; the courtesy ensign of the territorial waters they claimed to be operating in, which was also expected; and a third, smaller, riding just below the radar reflector, that made her stomach tighten.

She knew that flag. Blue field, white saltire, a gold device in the canton that meant nothing to anyone who hadn't spent three years buried in maritime registries looking for the seams between companies.

It was the house flag of Corvane Maritime Holdings.

A company that had been dissolved eleven years ago, when its flagship transport went down with all hands and most of its paperwork.

"Maren." Devi Okafor's voice came from behind her, low and careful. "You've been at that rail for twenty minutes. Either you're admiring the view or you're counting guns."

"Both," Maren said, and handed her the binoculars. "Third flag. Tell me what you see."

Devi took her time. Maren watched her face do the work—the slight narrowing of the eyes, the way her jaw set. Devi Okafor was the best engineer Maren had ever contracted, and one of the few people alive who could read a ship's history off her paint lines. She deserved better than what Maren was doing to her.

"That's not a working flag," Devi said finally. "Working ships fly working flags. That's a statement flag. Somebody wants to be identified."

"Somebody wants to be identified by exactly the right people," Maren said. "And nobody else."

Down the deck, Tomas Reyes was coiling line with the exaggerated calm of a man listening to every word. Auntie Bee was visible through the galley port, ostensibly making lunch, actually doing the same thing she always did when something was wrong: feeding people while thinking about drowning them later. Cyrus was up in the wheelhouse with the Perdita's owner, keeping his distance from Maren the way he'd kept it since the harbour bar—professionally, completely, like a wall he'd built out of politeness.

And Callum Reeve, her liaison officer, was coming across the deck toward them with a tablet in one hand and a smile that didn't reach his eyes.

Callum was thirty-four, ex-naval intelligence, currently employed by an entity Maren referred to in her own head only as *the backers*. He wore linen shirts and expensive sunglasses and had the gift of seeming helpful in six languages. Maren had worked with his type before. They were useful until the moment they weren't.

"You're looking at our competition," Callum said pleasantly, arriving beside them. "Impressive, isn't she? DP-3 station keeping, two hundred-ton crane, saturation capability to three hundred meters. Honestly, Ms. Voss, I'm not sure why we're rushing."

"Because she doesn't need to dive deep to find the Corvane," Maren said, not taking her eyes off the ship. "She needs to find the approach channel. And once she has that, depth is just a budgeting problem."

"The channel's marked impassable on every chart in the region."

"The channel's marked impassable because nobody wanted traffic through it." *And because Elias Kavanagh drew the real chart and hid it in a dead man's locker,* she did not say. "Your employers hired me because I know things charts don't. That hasn't changed."

"No," Callum agreed. "It hasn't. But I'll pass along a professional observation, if I may?" He turned to face her fully, and for a moment the pleasantness dropped away entirely, the way a mask drops when the wearer forgets to hold it up. "You've changed course twice since we left harbor. You're burning fuel on a dogleg that adds eighteen hours. Your captain"—he nodded vaguely at the wheelhouse—"hasn't shared his route plan with me, which is within his rights but outside my comfort. And now we've stopped within visual range of the very vessel we're supposedly racing. From where I stand, Ms. Voss, it looks less like a race and more like a parade."

Maren held his gaze. Ten years of negotiating with salvors, insurers, and men with guns had taught her that the person who blinked first lost ground they never got back.

"It looks like due diligence," she said. "I stopped here because I wanted to see her with my own eyes before we commit to the final run. Now I've seen her. We weigh anchor within the hour."

"And the third flag?"

"What about it?"

"You know what about it."

"I know that flags are cheap and registries are cheaper," Maren said. "I know that half the ships in this ocean are owned by three shell companies stacked inside each other like cups. If your employers are worried about who the Halcyon Dawn really belongs to, perhaps you should ask them. Since you talk to them more often than I do."

Something flickered across Callum's face—not anger, worse than anger. Recalculation. She'd reminded him that she knew the shape of the arrangement, and men like Callum hated being reminded that their principals' arrangements had shapes.

"I'll relay your schedule," he said, and walked away, unhurried, his footsteps light on the deck plates.

Devi waited until he was out of earshot, then said quietly: "You lied to him just now."

"I delayed him. There's a difference."

"There isn't, on this boat." Devi handed back the binoculars. Her voice stayed level, but there was iron under it. "You stopped here for another reason. I've been watching you watch that ship. What is it?"

Maren looked at the Halcyon Dawn again. At the impossible flag snapping in the trade wind. At the two fast response boats hanging from her davits, each one capable of carrying eight armed men across six miles of open water in under fifteen minutes.

At the fact that the ship was anchored precisely where she would anchor if someone aboard already knew where the Corvane lay.

*They can't know,* she told herself. *Nobody knows. Elias took the chart to his grave and Cyrus doesn't even know he has it.*

But the thought arrived anyway, uninvited, wearing its own life jacket: *Unless somebody read the same documents I read. Unless the backers have sources I don't. Unless this whole contract is a leash, and I'm the one wearing it.*

"They're staging," she said aloud. "Fuel, provisions, maybe waiting on weather data. That's all it is."

"That's not what you think it is."

"No," Maren admitted. "It isn't."

* * *

She found Cyrus in the wheelhouse an hour later, after the anchor came up and the Perdita swung her bow back onto the dogleg course that would take them wide around Pulau Serak and down into the deep water east of the reef field.

He stood at the chart table with a mug of coffee going cold beside his elbow, and he didn't look up when she came in, which was itself a message. Cyrus Kavanagh communicated mostly through what he declined to do.

"We're past the island," he said. "Whatever you came to say, say it fast."

"The Halcyon Dawn carries a Corvane Maritime house flag."

That got him. His head came up, and for a second she saw past the wall—not much past it, but enough. Ten years since certification school in Fremantle, where they'd raced each other on timed wreck penetrations and she'd beaten him by four seconds and he'd never once mentioned it, and still she could read him faster than any man she'd ever known. It was infuriating. It was the reason she'd chosen him for this job, though she'd given the backers a different reason.

"That company was liquidated," Cyrus said.

"So was the truth about that ship, and look how well that's holding up." Maren moved to the chart table, keeping her hands flat and visible, the way you did with a man who trusted actions and nothing else. "Cyrus. Listen to me. Whatever you think of me—and you should think poorly of me, it keeps us both honest—you need to understand that the money behind that ship is not salvage money. Salvage money buys cranes. That flag is a message to whoever put it there, and the message is *we were always coming back.*"

"For what? The wreck's been down eleven years. Anything loose has been picked over by fishermen and trawl nets."

"Not anything." She let a beat pass. "Not everything."

He studied her, and she felt the examination like a lamp shone into a hold—methodical, thorough, looking for the thing she'd stowed where the inspectors wouldn't check. She gave him nothing. That was the discipline. That was the whole job.

"You know what she was carrying," Cyrus said. Not a question.

"I know what my contract says. Locate the wreck, secure the manifest compartment, deliver contents to specified parties. Everything else is above my clearance and below my interest."

"Liar," he said, without heat, almost gently, and turned back to his charts.

Maren stood there for a moment, feeling the wheelhouse sway beneath her, hearing the engine's steady heartbeat through the deck. She should leave. She should go topside, log the sighting, eat whatever Auntie Bee was burning, and keep her mask on until the job was done, because the mask was the only thing that had ever kept her whole.

Instead she heard herself say: "My father ran agents for a shipping consortium. Did I ever tell you that?"

Cyrus looked up slowly.

"He disappeared when I was nineteen. Officially, a heart attack in Singapore. Unofficially—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "Unofficially, nobody has ever said the word unofficially to me about it. That's how good they are. Eleven years I've been pulling threads, Cyrus, and every thread ends in a room where the lights are on and no one is home. The Corvane is the first thread that goes somewhere dark enough to matter. So yes. I know what she was carrying. And yes, I want it more than I want the money, and if that makes me a liability, throw me overboard now, because I will not stop."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below, Tomas laughed at something, the sound muffled and ordinary, and it hurt in a way she couldn't name—ten years of crew noise, of belonging she'd traded away for leverage, leaking up through the deck plates.

"You could have gone to the fleet," Cyrus said at last. "Walked down that jetty, flashed whatever credentials you actually carry, and been aboard the Halcyon Dawn by dinner. Instead you're on a rustbucket tug eating burnt rice, lying to your own handler. Why?"

Because your captain drew the only chart that matters. Because you're the only diver alive who might survive that trench. Because I need you and I hate needing anyone—

"Because the fleet doesn't find wrecks," she said. "Fleets find excuses. I need the wreck."

Cyrus held her gaze for a long five seconds. Then he tapped the chart with one blunt finger, on the empty blue water east of the reef field, and said: "Two days. Then we're on site, and whatever game you're playing with your backers, you finish it before we drop a diver in the water. I'm not running cover for you at sixty meters."

"Understood."

"And Maren." He picked up his coffee, finally, and drank it cold without flinching. "That flag. If the people who sank her are coming back for her—then whatever's down there isn't treasure, and it isn't paperwork. It's a confession. Think hard about who you're planning to give a confession to."

He turned back to his charts, and she left the wheelhouse with her heart going harder than the climb justified.

* * *

At dusk she stood alone at the stern rail and watched Pulau Serak sink behind them into the haze.

The Halcyon Dawn's running lights were visible for a long time—a white masthead, red and green wings, hanging motionless on the horizon like something patient. Like something that had all the time in the world, because it did.

Her satellite phone buzzed against her hip. Encrypted channel, the backers' prefix. She almost didn't answer. She answered, because not answering was information too.

"Status," said the voice. Not Callum. Someone further up the food chain, a woman whose accent Maren had never placed.

"Transiting to the search box. On schedule."

"Our asset reports you altered course near Serak."

*Our asset.* So Callum reported directly past her. Fine. She'd assumed as much; it was almost comforting to have it confirmed.

"I conducted a visual reconnaissance of the competing vessel. We are proceeding."

A pause, long enough to be a message in itself. When the voice came back, it was softer, which was worse.

"Ms. Voss. Let me simplify your remaining decisions. The Halcyon Dawn will arrive at the objective within seventy-two hours regardless of your progress. Your value to this operation is time—hours, not days. Deliver the manifest compartment intact and on schedule, and everyone on that tug gets paid and forgotten. Fail, and the fleet handles recovery its own way, and I promise you its way involves customs launches and frozen accounts and questions asked of your crew that they cannot answer. Do you understand?"

Maren watched the last light die off the water. Behind her, faint through the superstructure, she could hear Auntie Bee ringing the dinner bell, and Tomas shouting something about it, and the small domestic music of a family that had survived ten years in the grey by trusting each other absolutely.

A family she was leading straight into a trench full of confessions, with a knife at its back and a clock in its chest.

"Seventy-two hours," Maren said. "Understood."

She killed the call and stood in the dark, doing the arithmetic she'd been avoiding since the harbour bar. Two days to the site. Seventy-two hours until the fleet arrived. A dive to sixty-two meters that needed a surface interval she couldn't afford, on a wreck her own backers might already have coordinates for, carrying evidence she no longer intended to hand to anyone.

Because somewhere between the harbour bar and that impossible flag, the job had stopped being a contract and become a race she was running for herself—for a father in an unmarked grave and a truth with no address—and she had not yet decided whether that made her free or simply the newest kind of owned.

Ahead, low on the black water, the first heat lightning flickered along the horizon. Not the monsoon. Not yet. But the sky was clearing its throat.

Below her feet, the Perdita drove steadily east into the dark, and Maren Voss realized, with a clarity that felt like cold water closing over her head, that in forty-eight hours she was going to have to choose—and that for the first time in eleven years, she genuinely did not know which side of herself would win.
