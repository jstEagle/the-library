## 11. Squall Line

Tomas Reyes had a theory about weather that he'd never shared with anyone aboard the *Perdita*, mostly because it was stupid: the sea gave you one warning that actually mattered, and it was never the barometer. It was the smell. Two hours before the squall line hit them, the air off the water went sweet and green, like cut grass and copper coins, and his grandmother's voice rose up out of thirty years of memory to say *get the wash in, mijo* — and so he got the wash in.

He didn't call it a warning when he told Cyrus. He called it a feeling, because feelings were acceptable currency on this boat and premonitions were not.

"Green air," Cyrus said, looking at him over the chart table with that flat attention he gave everything, like the world was a wreck site and Tomas was a promising piece of hull plating. "You're sure."

"Sure as I've ever been about anything I can't prove."

Cyrus looked out the wheelhouse glass at a sky that was doing nothing wrong. Blue-white, high haze, the sea running a lazy two-foot chop behind them. Somewhere ahead, past the horizon's clean line, the monsoon was assembling itself like something being built by an enormous patient machine. The satellite feed in Auntie Bee's cabin showed the leading edge as a smear of white and orange three hundred miles wide, crawling toward them at fifteen knots, but that was days away. That was the war. What Tomas could smell was the skirmish.

"Devi," Cyrus said into the overhead mic, "batten what isn't already battened. Tomas thinks we're about to get wet."

"Your Tomas," Devi's voice came back, tinny and dry from the engine room, "has been wrong about rain exactly once, and that time it hailed instead."

"He remembers it differently," Cyrus said, and there was almost a smile in it, which for Cyrus was practically a parade.

Tomas went forward to secure the deck gear while the sky was still pretending. This was the part of seamanship nobody wrote songs about: coiling, lashing, dogging hatches, checking that every shackle and pin on the salvage crane was seated the way it had been seated yesterday and would be seated tomorrow. Ten years on the *Perdita* had taught him that survival at sea was ninety percent housekeeping done slightly earlier than seemed necessary. The other ten percent was luck, and luck favored the tidy.

The tug herself was a converted harbor tug of no distinguished lineage, seventy feet of riveted stubbornness that Cyrus had bought cheap and loved unreasonably. She rode heavy in the stern where they'd added the dive gear lockers and the decompression chamber that Devi maintained like a jealous god maintains a temple. Her freeboard was low for open water work. Everyone knew it. Nobody said it, the way nobody said the name *Corvane*.

By the time he'd finished forward, the green smell had thickened into something you could lean on, and the light had gone strange — not dark exactly, but bruised, the way fruit goes before it drops. He looked west and saw it coming.

A squall line has a shape. People who haven't seen one think of clouds, but it's more than clouds; it's a wall, a standing rampart of grey-green violence with a shelf cloud jutting out under it like the lip of a dropped jaw, and beneath the jaw, the sea simply stops existing. There's just a pale curtain where water is falling so hard and fast that the ocean itself is obscured, replaced by something that is neither sea nor sky. And ahead of all of it, running out like scouts, a wind that arrives minutes early to tell you what's coming and give you time to be afraid.

Tomas stood at the bow rail and let the scout wind hit him. It was warm — that was the obscene thing, warm as breath — and it carried spray and dust and the smell of a storm that hadn't even started raining yet.

"Auntie Bee," he said into his collar mic, "you seeing this?"

"I'm seeing it, I'm smelling it, and I'm putting lids on my pots," she said. "Get below or get tied on, boy. Your choice, but choose fast."

He chose the wheelhouse, because if the *Perdita* was going to fight, he wanted to watch Cyrus do it.

* * *

Cyrus Kavanagh drove a vessel the way he dove: no wasted motion, no drama, decisions arriving already made. He'd shortened sail — metaphorically; the tug had none, but he'd taken in the tow boom, secured the crane, and altered course twenty degrees north of their track to put the squall on the starboard quarter rather than taking it head-on. "We don't beat it," he said, half to himself, half to the room. "We let it pass through us. Fighting weather is how you lose hardware."

"Or people," Auntie Bee said from the doorway, arms crossed, her small frame planted like a bollard.

"Or people," Cyrus agreed. "Devi, I want everything you've got on the main if I ask for it, and nothing on it if I don't."

"You'll have it before you ask," Devi said. "That's the arrangement."

Tomas took the second seat, harness clipped, and watched the wall come. It moved faster than it looked like it should — that was always the trick of it, the way a squall line eats distance. One minute it was five miles off, a painting of itself; the next, the shelf cloud was overhead and the world went the color of gunmetal and the first gust hit the *Perdita* like a hand shoved flat against her flank.

She rolled. Not badly. A warning roll.

Then the rain arrived, and it wasn't rain, it was the sea being thrown at them sideways, whole tons of it, visibility collapsing from miles to yards in the space of three heartbeats. The wheelhouse windows became a solid grey blur. Cyrus killed the wipers' futility and drove by compass and feel, and the *Perdita* climbed the building swell and slammed down the far side hard enough to make Tomas's teeth click.

"Barometer's in freefall," Auntie Bee reported from the nav station, calm as a woman reading a recipe. "Wind's backed southwest and building. Forty knots in the gusts, maybe better."

"Maybe better," Tomas muttered. "I love a precise forecast."

"You want precision, marry an accountant," she said. "You want to live, hold onto something."

It got worse the way bad things at sea get worse — incrementally, then suddenly. The squall wasn't one storm but a chain of them, cell after cell strung along the front like boxcars, and each one delivered its own private insult of wind and water. Between cells there were lulls that felt like mercy and weren't, because in the lulls you could hear the next cell coming, a low freight-train rumble under the wind.

On the third cell, the lightning started. It walked down the line toward them in long silent strokes, and then it stopped being silent, and the thunder stopped being thunder and became concussion, pressure you felt in the chest. The radar screen bloomed solid red-orange. Auntie Bee reached over without asking and turned it down to a glow.

"Anyone struck by lightning on this boat gets cooked by me personally for wasting good electronics," she said.

"Noted," said Devi, over the intercom, from the engine room, where she was strapped into her chair in the roar and heat with her hands moving over the controls like a pianist who couldn't hear the audience.

Tomas realized, somewhere in the middle of the fourth cell, that he wasn't afraid. He noted this with clinical interest. Fear was supposed to be part of this, and its absence worried him more than the storm did — until he understood that it wasn't absence, it was deferral. His body had filed fear for later processing and was currently occupied with the task of staying alive, which required both hands and most of his attention. The terror would come. Terror always kept its receipts.

"Cyrus." Devi's voice, tight now. "I'm getting water in the lazarette. Bilge pump's handling it, but it's coming in faster than it should."

"How fast?"

"Fast enough that I'd like to go look at it."

"It's your funeral if you leave that chair in this."

"It's everyone's funeral if I don't."

There was a pause. Through the grey blindness of the wheelhouse glass, Tomas watched Cyrus make the calculation — the same flat, quick arithmetic he applied to everything, currents and gas loads and men's lives weighed in the same units.

"Go," Cyrus said. "Tomas, go with her. Harnesses, hard hats, and if the deck goes green, you both get inside and stay inside. I mean it."

"Aye," Tomas said, and unclipped, and went out into the screaming.

* * *

The deck of the *Perdita* in a squall was a different country. Water moved across it in sheets, ankle-deep, knee-deep, flowing aft in pulsing surges timed to the rolls, and the wind tried to peel him off the superstructure like a label. He crossed to the engine room trunk on the lifeline, hands over hands, breathing spray, and found Devi already waiting at the hatch, clipped on, hard hat strap cinched, her face lit by the strobing deck lights.

"Took you long enough," she shouted.

"You're welcome!"

They went down together into the relative quiet of the engine room, which was only quiet the way a drum is quiet compared to a gunshot. Devi pulled the inspection plate on the lazarette access and shone a light down into the void space at the stern, and the light came back glittering. Water, six inches of it, sloshing with the roll, and above it, at the after bulkhead, a weeping seam where two rivet lines met — old corrosion, old metal, old ship, opened up by the working of the hull in a sea state she'd never been asked to love.

"That's our leak," Devi said. "That's not terrible. That's manageable. I'll pack it, we pump, we live."

"Good news for once."

"Don't celebrate yet. I need to get at it from outside too. There's a doubler plate in the stores locker, port side aft. Grab it, plus the hammer and the patch kit."

"The stores locker is—" Tomas stopped. The stores locker was on deck. Open-topped, bolted to the bulwark aft of the wheelhouse, forty feet of open deck between here and there.

"I know where it is, Tomas."

"I know you know. I'm just saying it out loud so the universe hears me objecting."

"The universe doesn't take objections. Move."

He went back up the ladder and out, and the storm took him the moment the hatch closed behind him, snatched his breath, filled his mouth with salt. He clipped his tether to the jackstay and began the crossing, and it was the longest forty feet of his life, each step a negotiation with a deck that tilted twenty degrees one way and then twenty the other, water grabbing at his boots, wind leaning on his shoulders with the weight of a friendly drunk. Twice he went to one knee. Both times he got up, because the alternative was becoming a story Auntie Bee told.

At the locker he unclipped one arm, held on with the other, and wrestled the lid open against the wind. The doubler plate was a foot down under coiled rope and a crate of spare impellers. Of course it was. He dug, got the plate free, jammed it under his arm, grabbed the tool bag with his other hand, and turned back toward the superstructure.

And the sea changed its mind.

Later, he would try to reconstruct it and fail. There was no single wave he could point to, no rogue crest with a name. There was just a roll that went further than the ones before it — the *Perdita* laying over hard to port, further than she'd gone all day, the deck tilting past the angle where footing is a decision and becoming an opinion — and a wall of water coming across the bulwark amidships, green and solid, the kind they called a boarding sea because that's exactly what it intended to do.

Tomas saw Devi.

She'd come out of the engine room trunk to meet him — of course she had, because Devi Okafor did not wait to be helped, waiting was for other people — and she was halfway across the deck, clipped to the jackstay, when the water hit. It took her feet out from under her and swung her out on her tether like a pendulum, out over the leeward rail, and the tether snapped taut with a sound Tomas felt in his back teeth, and she hung there over the sea with the wave pouring off her and the next one already standing up behind it.

Her harness held. That was the first thing. The harness held, and the jackstay held, and the pad-eye held, and every piece of ironmongery that ten years of Devi's own inspections had certified held, and Tomas would remember that later too — that she'd been saved by her own standards, that the safety regime she'd bullied them all into following was the thing standing between her and the deep.

But the pad-eye was aft. The wave had swept her down-deck, and the tether had paid out to its full length, and she was hanging at the rail's edge with the sea reaching for her boots, and the clip mechanism — he could see it even at this distance, even through the rain — the clip was fouled. Twisted in the webbing. The kind of jam that takes two hands and thirty seconds and a calm surface to fix, and she had neither, and the next wave was coming.

Tomas did not decide anything. That was the thing he'd understand afterward, the thing that rearranged him: there was no decision. There was no moment where Tomas Reyes weighed options and chose courage. There was just the fact of Devi hanging over the side and his body already moving, dropping the doubler plate, clipping and unclipping along the jackstay in a sprint that wasn't brave because bravery requires deliberation and he had none to spare.

The deck rolled under him. He fell, caught the jackstay, hauled himself hand over hand through the flooding water, reached her.

She was face-down against the rail, arms hooked over the toe rail, hair plastered across her face, and when he grabbed her collar she turned her head and shouted something the wind ate whole. Her eyes were wide and white-ringed and absolutely furious, and he understood the fury — fury was what Devi did instead of panic, fury was her lifejacket — and he loved her for it in a way he filed away with everything else he filed away about Devi Okafor, in the drawer that had no key and no label.

He got his knife out. Cut nothing — first rule, you don't cut a tether unless you're holding the person — wrapped his left arm through hers and around the stanchion, locked his grip, and worked the fouled clip with his right hand while the sea hammered them both. The clip was jammed with its own webbing, twisted a quarter-turn past true. Thirty seconds, Devi had needed. He gave it eight seconds of pure animal speed, thumb breaking the webbing loop, gate snapping free —

— and the next wave arrived.

It picked them both up. For one full second Tomas was not on the *Perdita*; he was in the ocean, in the green roaring dark, Devi's weight tearing at his arm, the jackstay ripping across his ribs as the tether yanked them both back toward the steel like a fish on a line. Then the deck came up and met them, hard, and they were lying in six inches of rushing water with the rail above them and the sky above that, alive, both of them, alive.

He dragged her to the superstructure door. She was coughing and swearing in three languages, and she got the door open herself, because of course she did, and they fell through it into the passage and lay on the deck plates gasping while the storm went on without them, indifferent, enormous.

* * *

In the wheelhouse, the squall spent itself the way squalls do, all at once, like a debt finally paid. The last cell passed astern trailing ragged curtains, and the sun came through in broken shafts, and the sea settled from murder into mere grievance. Cyrus held their new course. Auntie Bee appeared with towels and tea in enamel mugs, and nobody spoke for a while, because there are moments when speech would be too small for the room.

Devi sat wrapped in a towel with her hands around the mug, and her hands were shaking. Tomas watched her notice this with visible offense, as though her own body had committed a breach of contract.

"Adrenaline," Auntie Bee said. "It does that. Let it."

"I'm fine."

"You're fine," Auntie Bee agreed placidly. "Fine people shake. Dead people don't. I prefer the shaking."

Cyrus looked at Tomas across the wheelhouse, and something passed between them that wasn't words. Cyrus had seen the deck camera footage already — they all had, the grainy replay of the wave, the pendulum, the sprint. He didn't say thank you. Thank you would have been too small too, and besides, Cyrus Kavanagh didn't spend words he could keep.

Instead he said, "The doubler plate?"

"Still in the stores locker," Tomas rasped. His voice sounded like gravel in a bucket. "Along with my dignity."

"We'll get both tomorrow," Devi said. Her voice was steadier now. "Weather window holds till evening. I'll pack the seam tonight from inside, patch it proper at first light."

"You're not going back out on that deck tonight," Cyrus said.

"I'm going back out on that deck tonight."

"Devi—"

"I nearly died today, Cyrus. Do you know what that means? It means I'm not spending the night listening to water come into my engine room." She drank her tea. Her hands had almost stopped shaking. "Besides. Somebody has to inspect that pad-eye. If my harness saved my life, I want to shake its hand personally."

Nobody laughed, quite, but everybody breathed, which amounted to the same thing.

* * *

Night came down soft behind the departing squall, stars in the wet clear air, the *Perdita* rolling gently on a long leftover swell. They stood watches as always. At 0200, Tomas came up to relieve the wheel and found Devi already there, sitting on the deck plates beside the helm with a flashlight and the pad-eye assembly disassembled in neat rows, the way she disassembled everything — problems, engines, silences.

"Shouldn't you be sleeping?" he said.

"Shouldn't you?" She didn't look up. "Bolt's worn. Look — see the elongation? Whole fitting was one season from letting go. My own inspection schedule missed it. I signed off on this piece of steel four months ago and it nearly fed me to the fish."

"So we replace the schedule."

"So we replace the schedule." She tightened the last bolt, torqued it by feel, and sat back on her heels. For a while she said nothing, and Tomas had been at sea long enough to know that Devi's silences were load-bearing, that you didn't walk on them, you waited for them to speak.

"Tomas." She still wasn't looking at him. "Today. On the deck. You didn't hesitate."

"You hesitated plenty for both of us. You were furious."

"I was furious because I was scared." She said it flatly, like reporting a gauge reading. "I don't get scared. Haven't since I was a kid. Today I got scared, and then you came through that water like it owed you money, and I—" She stopped. Picked up a bolt, put it down. "I don't have a word for what I felt when your arm went around me. It wasn't gratitude. Gratitude's too polite."

Tomas looked at her — at the top of her head, really, bent over the fittings — and felt the drawer inside him strain against its unlabeled lid. There were things he could say. He catalogued them and closed the drawer, because some cargoes shift if you open them in rough seas, and they were still in rough seas, whatever the barometer said.

"You don't need a word," he said. "The bolt's fixed. That's enough for tonight."

She glanced up at him then, and something moved across her face — acknowledgment, maybe, or the beginning of patience, the decision to let a thing stay unsaid because unsaid things, unlike ships, sometimes arrive safely anyway.

"Take the wheel, Reyes," she said, standing, gathering her tools. "And Tomas? Next time the universe objects to something, object louder. It might listen."

She went below. He took the wheel and set them on course, and the *Perdita* ran on through the starlit swell toward coordinates that no one aboard would say aloud, and in the nav station behind him, lit by its single green lamp, Elias Kavanagh's old charts lay open on the table — and Auntie Bee, who was supposed to be asleep, was standing over them with a magnifying glass, very still, reading something in the dead captain's handwriting that was making her frown.
