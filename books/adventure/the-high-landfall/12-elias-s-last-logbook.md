## 12. Elias's Last Logbook

The locker had been on the *Perdita* longer than any of them.

It sat in the aft cabin beneath the chart table, a steel box bolted to the deck plates, painted the same tired grey as everything else Elias Kavanagh had ever touched. Forty centimeters by thirty, dented at one corner where something heavy had fallen against it in some other decade, some other sea. A combination dial, brass gone green with salt. Cyrus had looked at that dial ten thousand times in ten years and never once turned it.

Not because he didn't know the numbers. He knew them. He'd watched Elias spin them once, drunk on arrack after a good season, muttering the date like a prayer — 04-19-31 — and then locking the box again as if the moment of weakness had never happened. April nineteenth. The day the *Corvane* went down. Elias had made his lock code the date of the worst night of his life, which told you something about the man that Cyrus had spent a decade refusing to think about.

He hadn't opened it because opening it would have meant deciding what to do with whatever was inside, and there were questions a man could survive only as long as he didn't ask them.

Now Maren Voss's contract sat signed in the wheelhouse drawer, and the *Perdita* was three days out from the survey grounds, running dark along a shipping lane with her transponder answering to a name she'd never been christened with, and the question wasn't avoidable anymore.

* * *

It was Auntie Bee who said it first, though she said it the way she said most true things — sideways, over food, so it could be denied if anyone wanted to.

She set a bowl of noodles in front of him in the galley at 0300, when he couldn't sleep because the sonar returns kept replaying behind his eyes even with the unit powered down. The monsoon swell was building. The hull groaned its old familiar groans.

"You're going to open it," she said.

"I haven't decided."

"You've decided. You've been walking past that locker like it owes you money for three days." She sat across from him, folded her hands around her tea mug. She was seventy-one and her hands were steadier than his. "I'm not going to tell you not to. I wanted to tell you not to for ten years, and I never did, and look how well that worked out."

"What's that supposed to mean?"

"It means I knew he kept one too." She sipped her tea. "A logbook. He thought nobody knew. A man who writes everything down in dive-table shorthand thinks he's being clever, but I cooked for that man for nineteen years, Cyrus. I know what a man looks like when he's carrying paper instead of grief."

Cyrus put his chopsticks down. "You knew he coded things?"

"Everyone knew. Devi figured out half the system before she was twenty-five. Tomas uses a version of it for his parts inventory. It's not a secret, boy. It never was. It was just a door we all agreed not to knock on." Her eyes were steady and dark and very old. "The question isn't whether the book is in there. The question is whether you can read what's in it and still be the man who signs Maren Voss's contracts."

"That's two questions."

"No," Auntie Bee said, standing, collecting her mug. "That's one question wearing a coat."

* * *

He opened it at dawn, alone, in the aft cabin, with the door shut and the sea running grey-green through the porthole glass.

04-19-31. His hands knew the numbers before his head did. The dial resisted, then gave — the sound of tumblers falling into place was small and enormous, like a bone setting. The lid came up on a smell of oiled steel and old paper, and underneath it, faintly, diesel and brine: the smell of the *Perdita*'s whole life compressed into a sealed box.

Inside: a pistol he'd never known existed, wrapped in an oilcloth so dry it crackled. A bundle of documents in a bank envelope, tied with string — he didn't touch those yet, couldn't, his attention snagging like a hook on coral. And on top, squared and neat as everything Elias had ever done, a logbook. Hardbound, navy cloth cover, the spine hand-labeled in block capitals: **KAVANAGH — FINAL SEASON**.

Final season. Not final voyage. Not last entries. Final season, the way a man labels a ledger he intends to close out.

Cyrus sat with it on his knees for a long time. Through the bulkhead he could hear Tomas singing off-key in the engine room, Devi's boots on the ladder, the ordinary music of a living crew. Then he opened the book.

The early pages were almost ordinary. Dive logs, fuel tallies, maintenance notes — all of it written in the shorthand they'd all learned at Elias's elbow, the dive-table notation repurposed into a private cipher. Depths became letters. Bottom times became numbers that weren't bottom times. Surface intervals marked pauses, decisions, payments. Devi had cracked pieces of it years ago, bored on a long transit; Cyrus had always assumed it was for hiding small things. Fuel skims. Off-book salvage. The grey-zone accounting every boat like theirs ran on.

Then he reached the entries from March of the last year, and the character of the handwriting changed.

Elias had written like a draftsman — square, unhurried, each figure placed with care. From mid-March onward the letters leaned. The spacing collapsed. Whatever the man had been recording, it had started costing him something to record it.

Cyrus decoded slowly, lips moving, translating column by column the way Devi had taught him over cards one winter, back when learning the cipher had seemed like a game.

**14 MAR.** *Met contact. Tanah Merah. Consignment fee confirmed. Route survey complete — channel clear, approach unmarked.* 

Route survey. Approach channel. For a wreck that wouldn't exist for another five weeks.

He read it again. Then a third time. The words didn't rearrange themselves into anything kinder.

**22 MAR.** *Second payment received. Terms amended. No divers below except me. Manifest seal to remain intact. If asked: mechanical failure, fire, foundering in weather.*

If asked. As if Elias had already been rehearsing the lie. As if the lie had been drafted in advance, in his own hand, in a book he kept locked under the chart table where his crew ate their meals six feet above it.

Cyrus became aware that his hands were shaking. He set the book flat on his thighs and pressed them still and kept reading, because stopping now would have been worse than anything the pages could say.

* * *

Devi found him there four hours later. He didn't hear the door. He heard nothing at all — the cabin had gone distant and underwater, the way rooms do when the floor drops out of a man's understanding of the world.

She stood in the doorway a moment, took in the open locker, the oilcloth, the logbook, his face. Then she came in without asking, sat down across from him on the bunk, and waited. That was Devi. She never pulled. She just made it possible to let go.

"He surveyed the channel," Cyrus said. His voice came out wrong, scraped. "Five weeks before she sank. He mapped the approach. He logged payments — three of them, Devi, dated, itemized — from a shipping consortium. And then—" He stopped. Swallowed. "And then the last entry."

"Read it to me."

He read it to her. The final page. Dated 18 April, the night before the sinking, the ink pressed hard enough to emboss the next three sheets:

**18 APR.** *Charges confirmed set. Six frames, port side, below the waterline. Passenger manifest sealed per contract. Sela asked me today why the Corvane sails empty of cargo and full of people. I told her—*

It ended there. Mid-sentence. The rest of the page blank. The next page blank. Every page after that blank, right up to the back cover, forty sheets of nothing where a man's account of the worst night of his life should have been.

Sela. Sela Ferreira, who had died down there with Rook, whose name the crew still didn't say aloud, whose absence had a shape they all navigated around like a shoal. Sela had asked the right question the day before the ship went down, and Elias had started to answer her, and then—

"Then what?" Devi said quietly. "What happened between this sentence and the morning?"

"I don't know." Cyrus heard his own voice as if it belonged to someone else. "I've spent ten years not knowing. I thought — God help me, Devi, I thought the *Corvane* job went bad. Bad luck, bad gas, a wreck that fought back. I built my whole life on top of that story. He lost two divers and he swore us off the site and I thought that was grief." He laughed, and it came out ugly. "It wasn't grief. It was cleanup. We were the loose ends."

Devi took the logbook from his hands — gently, the way she handled anything that mattered — and read the last entry herself. He watched her decode it faster than he had, watched her face go through the same sequence his must have gone through: confusion, translation, the pause, the drop.

"The charges," she said. "Six frames, port side. Cyrus, Tomas and I have been arguing about the blast pattern on wrecks like this since certification school. Scuttling charges leave a signature. Inward-punched plating, clean fracture lines, no fire damage consistent with foundering. If someone ever got eyes on that hull—"

"Maren knows."

"Maren knows *something*. She's paying us obscene money to get eyes on a hull the navy won't discuss, and now we know why the navy won't discuss it." She closed the book and held it against her chest like a shield. "Your captain helped sink a ship full of people. And then he hired himself to be the one diver who went down afterward to confirm the job was done clean. And Rook and Sela—"

"Don't."

"—were on that dive with him, Cyrus. Or they followed him down. Either way, they died on a wreck your captain helped make. And then he brought the *Perdita* home and locked this box and never said one word, and we spent ten years treating his silence like wisdom."

The cabin was very quiet. Through the hull, the sea made its indifferent sounds. Somewhere above them, Auntie Bee was making breakfast, and the smell of frying onions drifted down the companionway, absurd and domestic and unbearable.

"Elias taught me to dive," Cyrus said finally. "He taught all of us. He pulled me out of the water twice when I was green enough to die of enthusiasm. I named myself after his judgment. Every decision I've made on this boat for ten years, I've made by asking what he'd do." He looked at the open locker, at the bank envelope with its string bow, at the oilcloth-wrapped pistol he still hadn't examined. "So what do I do now? What does a man do when the voice in his head turns out to have been paid for?"

Devi was silent for a long moment. When she answered, her voice was level in the way hers got level before big swells — the calm that was really load-bearing.

"You finish the sentence," she said. "His or yours. But somebody finishes it."

* * *

They gathered in the wheelhouse at noon, all four of them, and Cyrus laid it out — not everything, not the bank envelope yet, but the shape of it. The payments. The route survey. The charges. The unfinished sentence with Sela's name in it.

Tomas took it hardest and showed it least, which was his way. He stood at the wheel with his knuckles white on the spokes and stared at the horizon and said, "Ten years I cooked his memory dinners. Ten years I poured him a shot on the anniversary." His accent thickened until the words nearly fused. "I need to go hit something that doesn't matter."

"Hit the generator," Devi said. "It's been misfiring for a week."

He went. They heard the wrench work begin below, rhythmic and furious, and gradually slow, and eventually become just maintenance.

Auntie Bee listened with her arms folded, and when Cyrus finished she was quiet for a while, looking out at the building swell with an expression he couldn't read — not surprise. That was the thing that frightened him. There was no surprise anywhere in her face.

"You knew," he said. "Some of it. You knew some of it."

"I knew he was afraid of that stretch of water," she said. "I knew he stopped sleeping in Aprils. I knew he sent money every year to a woman in Tanah Merah, cash, no return address, and told me it was debts." She turned to face him. "I chose not to know more. That was my part in it, and I'll carry mine, same as you'll carry yours. But listen to me, Cyrus Kavanagh." She stepped close, and her voice dropped to the register she used maybe once a year, the one that had raised three generations of sailors. "Whatever is in that envelope, whatever else he did — that man loved you like blood. Both things are true. You don't get to keep one and throw away the other. Nobody gets to."

"And if the truth costs us everything?" Cyrus said. "Maren's contract. The fleet. The navy. If we pull this thread, Bee—"

"Then we pull it together," she said, "or we cut the line right now, turn this boat around, and spend the rest of our lives knowing exactly what we chose." She held his gaze. "But we don't get to pull it halfway. Half-truths are what killed those people in the first place."

Above them, the sky had gone the bruised pewter color that preceded the squall line. The barometer on the bulkhead had fallen twelve points since dawn. Devi glanced at it, then at the radar, then at Cyrus.

"We've got maybe two days of decent weather," she said. "After that, the monsoon owns everything south of the shipping lane. If we're doing this, we decide now."

Cyrus looked down at the logbook in his hands. At the last entry, ending mid-sentence, Sela's name hanging in the incomplete grammar of a dead man's doubt. *I told her—*

Told her what? That the ship was doomed? That she should stay ashore? That he was sorry? That the money was good?

Or — and this was the thought that arrived cold and unwelcome and would not leave — had Sela already known? Had she asked her question because she'd *worked it out*, and had Elias begun his answer knowing that she had, and knowing what she might do with it?

The locker was still open behind him. The bank envelope sat inside, string-tied, patient as a mine.

He hadn't opened it yet. Some part of him understood that once he did, there would be no version of this story where the *Perdita* turned around.

Below decks, Tomas's wrench struck iron, steady as a heartbeat. Ahead, the squall line built itself along the horizon like a wall being raised. And somewhere south, in water sixty meters deep, a ship full of answers lay waiting with its plating punched inward, holding the end of a sentence Elias Kavanagh had never finished.

Cyrus untied the string.
