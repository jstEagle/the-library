## 13. Decoding the Shorthand

The logbook sat on the chart table like something that had drowned and been brought back against its will.

Devi had dried it for two days—hairdryer on low, pages fanned with tweezers, silica gel packets Auntie Bee hoarded for electronics pressed between the leaves like bandages. The paper had gone the color of old bone. The ink had held, mostly. Elias Kavanagh wrote with a fountain pen because he was a sentimental man about tools, and fountain pen ink, it turned out, outlasted everything except the truth.

She'd told Cyrus she needed three days. She'd told herself she needed three days. What she actually needed was to be alone with it, because there were things you couldn't say out loud while you still hoped you were wrong.

* * *

The first night she worked in the engine room, where the generator noise made a wall around her and nobody came looking unless something was broken.

Elias's dive-table shorthand wasn't a cipher, not really. It was a working system every commercial diver of his generation used—depth, time, gas mix, bottom intervals compressed into strings of numbers and letters so a diver could log a run without writing *I went down to forty meters for twenty-two minutes* like a tourist. Devi had learned it at nineteen, kneeling beside him in the Perdita's wheelhouse while he tapped the tables with a blunt finger and said, *Numbers don't lie, girl. People do. That's why we write them down.*

So the first pass through the book read like any salvage captain's log. Dates. Sites. Bottom times. Surface intervals. Decompression stops noted to the minute, because Elias was the kind of man who logged a five-meter stop as 5:03 when he'd lingered watching a turtle.

It took her four hours to notice the problem.

The numbers didn't balance.

A dive log is arithmetic. You go down, you come up, the times have to add up against the tables or somebody bends. Every entry in the back half of the book—the entries after the Corvane's name started appearing in the margins like a bruise surfacing—had surface intervals that didn't match the preceding dives. Too long here. Too short there. A man who logged 5:03 stops did not make arithmetic errors.

Which meant the numbers weren't all dives.

Devi sat back on her stool, engine humming through the deck plates, and felt the first cold thread of it go down her spine. She pulled a fresh notebook across the table—her own, clean, unmarked—and began copying out every anomalous entry by hand, stripping away the ones that balanced.

Forty-one entries didn't.

* * *

"Devi."

Tomas's voice through the engine room door, somewhere past midnight of the second night. She'd surfaced once for food, eaten standing up, gone back down. Her eyes felt full of sand.

"Not now."

"You said that six hours ago." He came in anyway, because Tomas always came in anyway, carrying a mug of Auntie Bee's ginger tea and wearing the expression he wore when he'd been sent as an ambassador. "Bee says if you don't sleep she's coming down here, and you know she'll bring the whole pot and sit on the toolbox."

"I'm close."

He set the tea next to her elbow and looked at the notebook pages spread across the workbench instead of asking what they were. That was one of the ten thousand small reasons she trusted him: he understood that some doors you opened yourself, in your own time, and pushing them just bent the hinges.

"What's it saying?" he asked finally. Quietly.

Devi looked at the columns. Forty-one entries, converted, cross-referenced, laid out in her own tight handwriting. And underneath them, the pattern she'd spent eighteen hours confirming and reconfirming because she kept hoping the math would break.

"It's saying Captain was keeping two logs," she said. "One for us. One for someone else."

Tomas didn't move for a long moment. Then he pulled a crate over, sat down across the workbench from her, and said, "Show me."

* * *

Here was what the code was.

Every anomalous entry followed the same skeleton: a plausible dive string, then a cluster of figures that resolved, under conversion, to a sum of money. Not directly—Elias hadn't been stupid enough to write *$40,000* anywhere—but through a chain of substitutions Devi had cracked at three in the morning, hunched over the dive tables with her heart going like a bilge pump.

Depth became denomination. A 42-meter bottom time, nonsense against the tables, was 42 units. Gas mix letters became currency markers—EAN became one thing, trimix another, and there was a third letter combination that appeared only seven times and mapped, when she checked the exchange rates from the relevant years against the dates in the margin, to US dollars.

Bottom time became quantity. Surface interval became date of payment. And the decompression stops—the sacred stops, the numbers Elias had drilled into her as gospel—became account routing fragments, split across entries so no single page held anything complete.

"He built a ledger out of dive tables," Tomas said, when she walked him through it. His voice had gone flat, which was how Tomas sounded when he was most upset. "Ten years I watched him write these. Ten years he made me check his arithmetic."

"I know."

"He taught me the tables, Devi. He used to say—" Tomas stopped. Rubbed his jaw. "What are the payments?"

That was the part she'd finished an hour before he knocked. She turned the notebook toward him.

Seven payment clusters. Spread across fourteen months, ending eleven days before the Corvane went down. The first was modest—a retainer, maybe, though she hated how naturally the word came. The middle ones grew. The last one, dated nine days before the sinking, was the largest by a factor of four.

And in the margin of that final entry, in Elias's hand, in the same fountain-pen ink but pressed harder, the way a man writes when his fingers aren't steady:

**CORVANE — SAILS ON THE 14TH — CONFIRM**

Confirm what? That was the question she'd been chewing since dawn. Confirm the sailing date. Confirm the cargo. Confirm the job. The entry gave nothing else—no site coordinates, no client name, no task. Just the ship, the date, and a word that could mean anything from *watch her leave harbor* to something Devi's mind refused to finish forming.

"There's a shipping consortium," she said. "Meridian-Halcyon. They insured the Corvane's route. When she sank, they paid out on hull and cargo both, and then the claim went quiet—no litigation, no investigation, nothing. Bee flagged it years ago and Elias shut it down. Told her insurance companies bury their own dead, leave it be."

"And now?"

"Now the routing fragments in the last three entries match a Meridian-Halcyon settlement account. I checked the public filings. It took me two hours and a satellite link I'm going to have to explain to Cyrus." She rubbed her eyes. "Tomas. Elias was being paid by the people who owned the Corvane's paper. Before she sank."

Tomas stared at the notebook. When he spoke again his voice was very careful, the way it got when he was holding something heavy over open water.

"The night Rook and Sela died. He forbade us to talk about it. Said the sea took them and grief doesn't need an audience." A pause. "You think he knew why they were down there?"

"I think," Devi said, "that I've been doing this math for thirty hours hoping to be wrong, and the math won't break."

* * *

She slept four hours that morning, dreamless and gray, and woke to find Auntie Bee sitting in the galley with the logbook open in front of her and an expression Devi had seen exactly twice in ten years: once when the Perdita's old owner tried to sell them out to port authorities in Kota Bharu, and once the day they buried Elias's effects box in the harbor office safe and Bee locked it and didn't speak for a day.

"You cracked it," Bee said. Not a question.

"Bee—"

"Don't 'Bee' me, girl. I've been reading that man's charts since before you could tie a bowline. I knew his hand better than he did." She turned the logbook around. Her finger stopped on the final entry. "Sails on the 14th. Confirm. I want to know what you think that means, and I want you to say it plain, because I'm too old to dance around it."

Devi poured coffee she didn't want, mostly to have something to do with her hands.

"I think he was paid to confirm the sailing," she said. "And I think whatever confirmation meant, it's why the Corvane is where it is, and it might be why Rook and Sela were diving her, and I think—" Her voice caught on the last part, and she let it, because Bee deserved the unvarnished version. "I think the man who taught me that numbers don't lie was lying in numbers for at least a year before he died. Maybe longer. Maybe since before any of us signed aboard."

Auntie Bee was quiet for a long time. Outside, the harbor slapped against the hull, indifferent.

"When your mother died," Bee said finally, "who sat with you?"

"Elias. All night. On the foredeck. He didn't say a word until sunrise, and then he made breakfast."

"Yes." Bee closed the logbook gently, the way you close a casket. "That's the man I knew too. Which is why this book frightens me more than any storm ever has. Because either there were two Elias Kavanaghs, or there was one who did something terrible and spent the rest of his life paying for it—and I don't know yet which one I can live with."

* * *

The third night, Cyrus found her.

She heard him coming down the ladder—she'd have known those footsteps in a crowd of thousands—and she didn't hide the notebook. There was no point anymore. Whatever happened next, it happened with the numbers on the table.

He stood in the doorway a moment, reading her face the way he read water: looking for the current underneath. Then he crossed the engine room, pulled up a crate, and sat.

"Show me."

She showed him. All of it—the substitution chain, the seven payments, the Meridian-Halcyon account match, the final entry with its hard-pressed ink. She narrated it flat and technical, engineer's voice, because if she let any other voice in she was going to shake apart, and one of them had to hold together tonight.

Cyrus didn't interrupt once. When she finished, he sat with his elbows on his knees and looked at the final entry for a long, long time.

"Sails on the 14th," he said. "Confirm."

"That's all it says."

"No." He shook his head slowly. "That's not all it says. It says my father took money from the people who sank that ship, and then took my father's crew down onto her, and then never let us say her name again." His voice didn't rise. That was the worst part. It stayed level and quiet, a depth sounding. "Ten years, Devi. He made us swear off the job and I thought—I *thought*—he was protecting us. From the navy. From whoever wanted her dark. And he was protecting himself."

"We don't know that. Not yet. We know he was paid. We don't know what for. There's a difference between taking blood money and—"

"Is there?" Cyrus looked up at her, and his eyes were dry and terrible. "Rook had a daughter in Cebu. Sela was saving for a boat of her own. If Elias pointed them at that wreck to clean up evidence for the consortium—if he sent them down to make sure nothing ever surfaced—then the difference is accounting, Devi. It's just accounting."

Devi reached across the workbench and put her hand over the logbook, over the final entry, as if she could press the ink back into the pen.

"Then we find out which it was," she said. "Because there's one person who hired us to go down there who's been smiling at us for three weeks and hasn't once asked what we found in that locker. Maren Voss knew the Corvane's name before we did. She knew the coordinates region. She knew enough to build a contract around it." She met Cyrus's eyes. "Ask her what *confirm* meant. Ask her what the consortium paid for. Because I've decoded everything in this book except the reason, and I think she's been carrying the reason since before we signed."

Cyrus was silent. Above them, through the deck plates, came the sound of Tomas and Auntie Bee moving around the galley, making dinner neither of them would taste, keeping the small machinery of the family running while its foundation shifted underneath.

"Three days," Cyrus said at last, standing. "You said three days."

"I said I needed three days. I was wrong. I needed three days to prove I couldn't un-know this."

He nodded once, and headed for the ladder, and stopped with his hand on the rail.

"Devi. Whatever else he was—the man who sat with you all night on the foredeck. Don't lose him yet. Not until we know."

"I'm trying," she said. "But the numbers don't lie, Cyrus. He's the one who taught me that."

He climbed out into the evening light, and Devi heard his boots cross the deck, heading aft—heading, she realized, toward the radio room, where Maren Voss's liaison officer had left a frequency card three weeks ago with instructions to call when they had news.

She looked down at the logbook one more time. Seven payments. Fourteen months. One word in hard-pressed ink.

Then she picked up the satellite phone, dialed the number Auntie Bee had slipped into her pocket two nights ago with a look that said *trust me*, and waited for a journalist named Priya Nair to pick up.
