## 14. What the Navy Paid For

Maren Voss had learned a long time ago that the best lies were told in the shape of the truth—just not all of it, and never in order. She'd been assembling this particular truth for eleven years, carrying it from harbour bar to harbour bar like contraband, waiting for the one man stubborn enough and skilled enough to carry it the rest of the way.

Now Cyrus Kavanagh sat across her chart table on the Perdita's aft deck, the signed contract between them, and she still hadn't said the word *Corvane* out loud. Not once. Not since Tanah Merah.

The tug rode at anchor off a nameless islet, engines ticking as they cooled, the crew deliberately elsewhere. Devi had taken Tomas up to check the new sonar transducer with a thoroughness that fooled no one. Auntie Bee was below, and Maren had the distinct feeling that Auntie Bee heard everything whether she was present or not.

"You've read the contract," Maren said. "Every clause. I watched you do it twice."

"I read contracts the way you read people," Cyrus said. "Looking for what's underneath."

"And did you find it?"

"I found a salvage fee that's three times market. A completion bonus that's obscene. And a confidentiality clause so tight it might as well be a gag." He turned his cup slowly on the chart table, not drinking. "Nobody pays that much for a hull. So either you're after cargo nobody's inventoried, or you're after something that isn't cargo at all."

Maren let the silence sit. This was the part she'd rehearsed for years—the part where she gave him just enough to make him hers without giving him enough to make him run.

"The Corvane wasn't lost to weather," she said.

Cyrus didn't move. Ten years of grey-zone diving had taught him to keep his face like ballast: heavy, low, stable. But she saw the flicker behind his eyes, the same flicker she'd seen at seventeen when an instructor asked him a question he already knew the answer to.

"Go on," he said.

"Official finding: structural failure in heavy seas, forty miles east of the Serrano shelf. All hands lost. Insurance paid out to the Meridian Shipping Consortium within ninety days—one of the largest marine claims of the decade." She recited it flatly, the way you recite something you've hated for a long time. "There was no storm that night. I've checked the records. Barometric pressure was unremarkable. Two other vessels passed within thirty miles and logged calm conditions."

"Then what sank her?"

"Her own crew. On orders. Scuttle charges in the engine spaces and the aft hold, set while she was still making way, while there were—" Maren stopped. Steadied herself against the table edge, and let him see her do it. That was part of it too. The visible cost. "While there were people aboard who were never meant to be counted."

"How many?"

"Passenger manifest listed sixty-one. Recovery teams pulled nineteen bodies. The consortium's insurers never asked about the difference. Nobody ever asks about the difference when the payout clears."

Cyrus looked out at the water. The channel light blinked its slow idiot rhythm on the far point. "You're telling me murder," he said. "Mass murder, dressed as an accident, cashed as insurance. And you want me to go down and find proof."

"I want you to find the manifest."

"Why? Proof of scuttling would be in the wreck itself—blast patterns, charge residue. You could hire any forensic team in the region if you had the coordinates. What you keep circling back to is paper. A passenger list." He leaned forward. "What's on a list that's worth what you're paying?"

*Careful now.* Maren had watched interrogators work, had been worked over herself by better liars than Cyrus Kavanagh, and she knew the geometry of this moment. Give him the architecture of the crime—it made her credible, made the money make sense. Keep the personal thing sealed. The personal thing was leverage only as long as it stayed hers.

"The manifest isn't just names," she said. "It's the boarding record. Who actually boarded at Port Callis versus who was listed. The Corvane took on passengers at two ports. The second manifest—the real one—was sealed in the purser's strongroom because the first one didn't match the insurance declaration. There were more souls aboard than the consortium ever declared, because some of those souls weren't supposed to exist on paper at all. Witnesses. Auditors. A junior accountant from the consortium's own firm who'd found the discrepancy and was being quietly relocated before he could talk." She shrugged. "He's on the list. Or he was."

"You're saying they scuttled her partly to bury their own paperwork."

"I'm saying the sea is the cheapest filing cabinet ever built. No warrant can reach sixty meters."

Cyrus sat back. She watched him do the arithmetic she wanted him to do: the money, the risk, the sheer scale of the lie—and beneath all of it, the thing she'd bet everything on. The name of the ship. The year. The two divers.

"Ten years ago," Cyrus said quietly. "Elias knew her. Everyone in the trade knew her name by the end of that season. He wouldn't take a contract near her. Wouldn't even discuss the site. When a survey boat offered us triple rates to run a side-scan pass over that grid, he threw the radio handset across the wheelhouse." His jaw tightened. "I always thought it was grief. Rook and Sela went down on a job that season—not that one, but close. Same waters. I thought the whole area was poisoned for him."

"And was it?"

"That's what I'm asking you, Maren. Because you knew him too. Certification days, remember? You and Elias and me, sharing a rented compressor and a bottle of rum that tasted like kerosene. You knew how he operated. So tell me why a man who forbade his crew to say the word *Corvane* is suddenly the only salvager in the region whose charts might get you to her."

There it was. The question she'd dreaded, arriving exactly on schedule, wearing his careful voice.

Maren reached into her jacket—not fast, letting him see every inch of the movement—and set a photograph on the chart table. Old, creased, salt-stained at one corner. A younger Elias Kavanagh standing on a dock beside a woman with dark hair and a laugh caught mid-motion.

"My sister," Maren said. "Lena Voss. Junior claims adjuster, Meridian Shipping. Twenty-six years old." She tapped the photo once. "She boarded the Corvane at Port Callis. Second manifest. She'd found something in the claim files she shouldn't have found, and someone above her decided the safest place for Lena Voss was a ship that was going to be at the bottom of the ocean by Thursday."

The wind moved through the rigging. Somewhere below, a pot clanged—Auntie Bee, cooking or listening, probably both.

"Elias didn't know," Maren went on, and here she had to be precise, because this was the hinge, the load-bearing lie—or maybe it wasn't a lie; she'd stopped being sure, which frightened her more than the fleet on the horizon ever had. "Not then. He found out after. After Rook and Sela. He was hired—quietly, through a broker—to run a recovery dive on the Corvane. Document retrieval. He was told it was routine. He got his team down, they got into the superstructure, and something went wrong. Rook's line. Sela went after him. Elias came up alone."

"I know how it ended," Cyrus said, very flat.

"Do you know how it started?" She held his eyes. "Because I've spent eleven years pulling that thread, Cyrus, and every time I think I've reached the end of it, there's more. The broker who hired Elias was paid by the same shell company that arranged the scuttling. Think about that. The same people who sank her sent divers down afterward—to confirm nothing was left. To confirm the filing cabinet was empty. And your captain took the job without knowing whose money it was, and it cost him two of his people, and then he spent ten years making sure no one ever went back. Including you. *Especially* you."

"You're lying," Cyrus said. But his voice had gone quiet in the wrong way, the way voices go quiet around a wound.

"I might be," Maren admitted. "Check me. That's why I'm telling you this much. I need you sharp, not loyal. Loyal men dive badly—they dive to prove something. I need you diving angry and exact." She leaned forward. "But ask yourself one question, Cyrus. If Elias was clean, why does my trail keep leading back to him? Why does every broker, every fixer, every retired harbormaster I've squeezed in eleven years flinch at his name the same way your crew flinches at the ship's?"

Cyrus stood. He walked to the rail, and Maren let him, because men like Cyrus needed distance to think and proximity to decide. The channel light swept its beam across the water, once, twice.

"If half of what you're saying is true," he said finally, "then the navy patrol that warned us off the survey site last month wasn't courtesy. It was containment. Someone's still guarding the file cabinet."

"Now you're thinking like a salvager instead of a diver." Maren rose and gathered the photograph, sliding it back inside her jacket with a care that she didn't have to perform. That part was real. God help her, that part had always been real. "The corporate fleet that's staging for this—Halcyon Dawn, and whatever navy shadow rides along with her—they're not after scrap value. There is no scrap value at sixty-two meters in monsoon season. They're after the strongroom. They're going to strip it, melt the contents, and log the Corvane as surveyed-and-empty. The last page of the story, torn out forever."

"And we get there first."

"We get there first, or nobody ever gets there at all." She came to stand beside him at the rail, close enough that the next words were only for him. "I'm not asking you to believe me, Cyrus. I'm asking you to go down and look. Blast patterns don't lie. Paper doesn't lie. Dead men's knots don't lie. Whatever Elias did or didn't do down there, the wreck will say it plainer than I ever could."

"And if the wreck says something about Elias?" He turned his head, and his eyes in the running lights were hard and old. "If what's down there makes him guilty?"

Maren had prepared answers for everything. She had contingencies nested like dive tables, exits mapped from every conversation, every relationship, every decade of her life. But that question went through all of it and hit the place where Lena lived, and for one unguarded second her answer was simply true:

"Then at least somebody finally says it out loud. My sister has been a lie for eleven years. I'd rather she be a terrible truth than a comfortable one."

Cyrus studied her for a long moment, and she understood—with the weary clarity of a gambler watching the last card leave the deck—that he was deciding right now how much of her to believe, and that whatever number he chose, he'd chosen it partly because of the crack in her voice she hadn't meant to show.

"All right," he said. "We dive your wreck. We bring up your manifest. But Maren—" he stepped closer, and for the first time since Tanah Merah she felt the full weight of him, the methodical, patient danger of a man who survived by noticing things, "—when we come back up, you're going to tell me the rest. Not the version you rehearsed. The rest. Whatever it costs you."

"It will," she said. "Cost me, I mean. It already has."

Below them, the companionway hatch opened and Auntie Bee emerged with a tray of tea nobody had asked for, setting it on the chart table with the serene authority of a woman who had been listening to every word and had opinions about all of it. Her eyes met Maren's over the cups—level, unreadable, and old in a way that made Maren feel briefly, absurdly, like the child in the room.

"Drink your tea," Auntie Bee said. "Both of you. Storm's coming early this year. My knee's been saying so since Tuesday, and my knee has never once lied to me about weather, money, or men."

She withdrew below decks. Cyrus picked up his cup but didn't drink, looking instead at the eastern horizon, where the last light was dying and the first stars were coming out over open water.

"One more thing," he said. "Your liaison officer. The one photographing our equipment lists at the yard. Who does he actually report to?"

Maren kept her face pleasant and her mind racing, because that was a question she'd been asking herself for six days without liking any of her answers.

"He reports to me," she said.

"Then you'd better hope that's true," Cyrus said, "because tomorrow we sail, and whatever he's been writing down is going to arrive somewhere before we do."

Out past the reef, far beyond the range of the Perdita's lights, a white ship with mismatched flags was already moving.
