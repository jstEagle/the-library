## 15. Five Tides

Auntie Bee had cooked through funerals, near-drownings, a cholera scare in Sulu, and the year Tomas tried to make kimchi in a galley the size of a coffin. She did not rattle easily.

But she had not slept, and she could feel it in her hands as she folded dumplings at the Perdita's little dinette table—folds crooked, pleats uneven, the kind of work she'd have sent back from any cook working under her. The dough stuck to her palms. Outside, the sea had gone that flat, held-breath grey that meant the sky was only pretending to be done with them.

The satellite printout was pinned above the chart table where Cyrus had put it that morning, and nobody had taken it down, because there was nothing to argue with. The monsoon's leading edge sat on the feed like a bruise coming up on skin—a broad smear of white and green spiraling off the southwest, its outer bands already brushing the shipping lanes two days east of them. The forecaster's annotation, printed in the navy's usual tidy hand before the navy stopped sharing these feeds freely: *Tropical depression strengthening. Expected landfall 96–120 hrs.*

Five tides. That was what Devi had said last night, tapping the glass with a pencil. Five tides until this water was undivable, unsailable, and possibly un-survivable for anything smaller than a container ship.

And the vote was tonight.

Bee set the steamer over the pot and listened to it begin to hiss, and she thought about Elias Kavanagh's chart, which lay open on the shelf beside her flour tin because there was nowhere else in this ship safe from salt and grease except next to the food, and Elias had always said the safest place aboard was next to the food.

She had been staring at it for three days.

* * *

The crew gathered at dusk, the way they always did when something needed deciding—not in the wheelhouse, where Cyrus's word carried too much weight, but around the dinette, where the table was small enough that everyone had to sit close and nobody could leave without moving somebody else's knees.

Bee fed them first. That was rule one of any hard meeting: no decisions on an empty stomach. She'd learned that from her mother, who'd learned it from a famine. She laid out the dumplings, the pickled cabbage, the fish head soup Tomas pretended not to love, and she watched them eat the way she always watched them—Devi fast and neat, engineering even in her chewing; Tomas slow, savoring, a boy at a feast; Cyrus barely at all, pushing food around the bowl like a man doing sums he didn't want to finish.

Maren Voss wasn't invited to this one. Bee had made sure of it—the liaison officer could drink coffee on the aft deck with the door dogged, and like it.

"So," Cyrus said, when the bowls were down to broth. "You've all seen the feed."

"Seen it," Devi said. "Memorized it. Dreamt about it, if we're being honest."

"Then you know what I'm going to say." He turned his cup a quarter-turn on the table. "Sixty-two meters. Marginal gas margins even in good conditions. And in five tides, those conditions are gone. If we go, we go now or never."

"And if we go now," Devi said, "and anything goes wrong down there—even small-wrong, a hung valve, a delayed ascent—we surface into weather we can't run from. There's no port inside the storm radius. We'd be riding it out at anchor behind the reef, hoping the holding ground is good."

"It is good," Cyrus said. "Elias marked it."

"Elias marked everything," Devi said, and there it was—that edge that had been sharpening in her voice for two weeks, ever since the logbook. She caught herself, pressed her lips together, but the words were out, and Bee watched them land on Cyrus like small stones thrown by someone who loved him and couldn't stop throwing.

Tomas cleared his throat. "For what it's worth. I'll go."

"You don't get to volunteer first," Devi snapped. "You're the least experienced diver aboard."

"I'm the most expendable diver aboard," Tomas said, easy as ordering noodles, "which makes me the right one to say yes first. Somebody has to break the tie, and I'd rather do it before we're all too tired to be honest."

Bee poured tea all around. Her hands were steady now, interestingly. They always went steady when things got bad. It was grief that made them shake, not fear; she'd known that about herself for fifty years.

"Cyrus," she said. "Say your piece plain. You've been circling it since breakfast."

* * *

Here is what Bee knew about Cyrus Kavanagh that he did not know she knew, or rather, that he knew she knew but neither of them had ever said aloud in twenty years of shared decks and galleys:

She had been there the night he came aboard. Seventeen years old, half-feral, a duffel bag with a broken strap and a face like a locked door, standing on the quay while Elias talked to him in that low patient way Elias had, the way he talked to frightened animals and stubborn engines. She'd fed the boy stew and he'd eaten it like it might be taken away, and she had thought, *ah, there's the wound*, though she hadn't known then how deep it went or how much of it Elias himself had made.

She'd also been there—aboard, in the galley, ten years ago—the night the Corvane came up in conversation for the last time. The night Rook and Sela didn't. She remembered the radio crackling, Elias's face going still in a way faces shouldn't go still, and then the long silence, and then Elias saying, in a voice like gravel poured slowly: *We don't dive it again. Not for money, not for bodies, not for God Himself. It's finished.* And Cyrus, twenty-three years old and bleeding from a rope burn across both palms, asking why, just once, and Elias looking at him with an expression Bee had spent a decade failing to file under any name she knew.

Now she had a name for it. It had arrived with the decoded ledger, three nights ago, when Devi spread the pages on this very table and the whole ship went quiet in a way that had physical weight.

Guilt. The expression had been guilt.

* * *

"I found the approach channel," Cyrus said now, flat, factual, the voice he used for numbers. "Elias mapped it years before the Corvane ever sailed. Bee found the dates. You've all seen the dates."

Nobody looked at Bee. Kind of them. Useless.

"He knew the channel. He knew the wreck site before she sank. And the ledger says—" Cyrus stopped. Restarted. "The ledger says he was paid. Tied to her sailing date. So either he helped sink her, or he knew she was going down and took money to be nearby, or—" His jaw worked. "Or the ledger lies, and I can't make myself believe that, because Devi checked it four times."

"The ledger doesn't lie," Devi said quietly. "The handwriting is his. I'd know it anywhere. He taught me my tide tables in that hand."

"So we go down there," Cyrus said, "and we find out which man he was. The one who set the charges, or the one who spent ten years trying to buy back something money took." He looked around the table. "That's the truth of it. This isn't salvage anymore. I need to know. And I'm asking whether you'll come with me to find out, knowing what it might be."

Silence, but the living kind, full of breathing.

Tomas spoke first, because Tomas always spoke first when silence got heavy; it was his function aboard, Bee had decided long ago, like a bilge pump. "My grandmother used to say you can't pick your family's graves. You inherit them. You go anyway." He shrugged. "Elias was family. The ugly parts are part of it."

"That's not a reason to risk your neck," Devi said.

"No. But it's a reason I can live with, which is more than most reasons give you."

Devi turned her cup the same quarter-turn Cyrus had turned his—Bee noticed these things, the ways a crew becomes one organism over a decade—and stared into her tea for a long moment.

"I ran the numbers," she said finally. "Gas, weather windows, deco schedules. If we go tomorrow on the morning slack, we get two dives. Two. Maybe three if the squalls hold off past what they should. After that, the sea closes." She looked up. "I'm not saying yes because I forgive him. I'm saying yes because I need to see it with my own eyes. Whatever's down there—charges, bones, a strongbox, nothing—I need to stand in front of it. Otherwise I'll spend the rest of my life arguing with a dead man's handwriting."

"Devi," Bee said.

"No, Auntie. Let me finish." Devi's voice cracked, just once, and she let it. "He taught me navigation. He taught me to read water. And if the same hands that did that also drowned three hundred people for an insurance payout, then I need to know what that means about everything he ever taught me. Including me." She folded her arms. "Yes. I vote yes. Against my better judgment, which has been wrong about this family before."

Three yeses, then, counting Cyrus. Bee counted heads around the table. Four places set, four bowls empty, and hers.

All of them looked at her at once, the way families do, and she understood suddenly that this was why she'd cooked first—so their mouths would be full and their eyes would have somewhere to go during the hard part. Old trick. Didn't work anymore. They were looking.

* * *

Bee had grown up in a house where the sea took people the way other houses lost them to fever or debt: regularly, without malice, as a matter of course. Her father. Two brothers. A first husband whose name she kept in a tin with his watch. She had buried more than her share, and she had learned the arithmetic of it early: the dead do not need the truth. The dead are past needing. Truth is a currency that only spends among the living.

But Elias—

Elias had been her friend before he was her captain. Before the Perdita, before any of them, back when they were both younger than Cyrus was now, running a leaky freight lighter between islands that didn't appear on honest charts. She'd known him broke, drunk, grieving, ridiculous. She'd known him the night he came back from wherever the consortium's men had found him, gray-faced, scrubbing his hands in seawater over the rail like Lady Macbeth with salt spray, and she'd asked him what was wrong and he'd said, "Nothing I can fix," and she'd believed him, because she loved him, and loving someone is the oldest excuse for believing them.

Ten years of believing him.

And now a ledger in his own handwriting said otherwise, or said worse than otherwise—said maybe, perhaps, could-have-been—and Bee found she could not vote. Not yes, not no. Because a yes said she'd decided he was guilty and wanted proof. A no said she'd decided he was innocent and wanted the proof buried with him, and both of those were verdicts on a man she'd loved for forty years, delivered by a woman who'd been wrong about him for ten.

"I abstain," she said.

The word dropped into the room like a stone into a well. No splash for a long time.

"Auntie," Cyrus started.

"No." She raised one hand, palm out—the gesture she used to stop Tomas from adding chili to everything, the gesture that had ended a thousand arguments aboard this vessel. "I've cooked for this crew through every bad night it's ever had, and I've never once refused to take a side, because taking sides is what family does. But I can't side against Elias and I can't side for him, not yet, not blind. So here's what I'll do instead." She stood, went to the shelf, and brought the chart to the table—his chart, the one with the annotated channel, the one she'd been staring at for three days—and smoothed it flat with both palms.

"Look here." Her finger found the margin notes, tiny, dense, in that handwriting Devi knew anywhere. Dates in the corners of soundings. Depth figures corrected by hand. A note in the approach channel, written small enough that you'd miss it unless you were looking: *hold fast to starboard mark — current sets NNE after dark — trust the lead line, not the echo.*

"I've been reading this for three days," Bee said. "Not the channel. Him. And here's what I see." She looked around the table, at each of them in turn. "This is the work of a man making absolutely certain he could get back to this place. Every note, every correction—it's a map home. Guilty men run away. This is a map toward."

"Could be operational planning," Devi said gently. "If he was paid to survey—"

"If he was paid to survey, why correct it every year for six years?" Bee tapped the dates. "Why keep coming back? Why annotate a channel nobody paid him to annotate, years after the money stopped?" She straightened. "I don't know what he did. Maybe I don't want to know. But I know what this is, and this—" she pressed her hand flat on the paper, "—this is a man who couldn't stay away. That's not innocence and it isn't guilt. It's something in between that hasn't got a name yet. And I'm abstaining until it does."

The galley clock ticked. Somewhere below, the engine ticked with it, off-beat, the ship's own pulse.

Cyrus reached across and covered her hand with his—his father's rope-burn scar long faded, but Bee could still find the place where it had been, some mapmaker's habit of marking old wounds.

"Abstention noted," he said quietly. "Motion carries, three to none. We dive on tomorrow's morning slack." He paused. "Bee. Whatever we find down there—"

"You'll bring it up and put it on this table," she said, "and I'll feed you first, and then we'll decide what's true. That's the order of operations. Food, then truth. Everything else is theater."

Tomas laughed—one short bark, surprised out of him—and Devi almost smiled, and just like that the fracture in the middle of the room knitted itself into something that would hold, if not heal. Families didn't heal, Bee knew. They scarred, and scars held better than original skin anyway.

* * *

She washed up alone afterward, the way she liked, listening to the sounds of the ship preparing: Devi in the engine room, coaxing; Tomas on deck, coiling and lashing; Cyrus in the wheelhouse with Maren Voss's liaison finally allowed back inside, voices low and clipped through the bulkhead.

Bee dried the last bowl, then took down the satellite printout and looked at it again. The leading edge had crept closer since morning. The forecaster's tidy line had been amended in someone's hasty pen: *accelerating — revised landfall estimate 72–90 hrs.*

Four tides, maybe. Less than five.

She unpinned it, folded it, and was turning to stow it when her eye caught the chart again—Elias's chart, still open on the table where she'd left it. And she saw, for the first time in three days of staring, the thing she'd somehow missed, tucked into the fold crease at the chart's edge, so faint it might have been a watermark:

A second set of annotations. Different ink. Older, or newer, she couldn't tell. And a single line of text beside the trench where the Corvane lay, in letters so small they were nearly nothing:

*Bee — if it's you reading this, the answer isn't in the wreck. It's in the harbor office, Tanah Merah, box forty-one. Forgive me.*

The galley seemed to tilt very slightly, the way it did in swells too small to see.

Bee stood with the folded weather report in her hands and did not call anyone. Not yet. Tomorrow they would dive into sixty-two meters of dark water chasing a dead man's guilt, and here, all along, folded into the paper she'd been sleeping ten feet from, was a dead man speaking directly to her—asking forgiveness, pointing elsewhere, saying the wreck wasn't the answer at all.

Outside, the first spit of rain touched the wheelhouse glass.

She folded the chart along its old creases, careful as folding a flag, and went to find Cyrus.
