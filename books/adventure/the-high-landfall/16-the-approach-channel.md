## 16. The Approach Channel

The moon didn't rise until ten, and when it did it was a thin paring of itself, barely enough to silver the swells. That suited Cyrus. Elias had always said the best time to run an unlit passage was on the darkest water you could trust—dark enough that your eyes stopped lying to you about distance, and the only truth left was what the old man had written in pencil sixty years before either of them was born.

Cyrus stood at the wheel with the chart book open on the ledge beside him, weighted down with a coffee mug and Auntie Bee's brass compass. The pages were soft as cloth from handling. Elias's annotations crawled up the margins in his cramped, patient hand: depths, headings, tide corrections, and once, inexplicably, a small drawing of a frigate bird.

"You're going to want to slow to four knots," Devi said from the nav station, not looking up from the screen. "Depth under the keel is going to drop fast after the second marker."

"I know."

"You know because I told you."

"I know because he wrote it." Cyrus tapped the margin. *Slow to 4 kn — bottom comes up to meet you like a bill collector.* "His words."

Devi glanced at the page, and something moved across her face—not quite a smile. "He never could resist a joke at the worst possible moment."

"He saved them for it."

* * *

They'd been running dark since sundown, no deck lights, only the red glow of instruments and the pale wash of the radar repeater. Behind them, forty miles back, the Halcyon Dawn sat anchored off the supply island, fat and certain, its charts showing nothing but a wall of reef marked IMPASSABLE in the surveyor's flat bureaucratic red. Let them stare at it. Let them run their surveys and file their reports. The fleet would come around the long way if it came at all, and by then—

By then, Cyrus thought, and didn't finish the sentence.

Ahead, the reef field spread across the water like something drowned mid-struggle. You couldn't see it in the dark, but you could hear it—a low, continuous seething where swell broke over coral, a sound like the sea whispering to itself. Every few minutes a patch of white flickered where a wave found a shallow head and died against it.

Tomas stood on the bow with a handheld spotlight he'd been ordered not to use except on command. Auntie Bee was below, ostensibly sleeping, actually sitting upright in her bunk with her reading light on and Elias's original chart—the big one, the linen-backed one—spread across her knees. She'd been doing that for three nights now. Cyrus hadn't asked what she was looking for. He suspected she'd tell him when she found it, or when she decided he was ready to hear it.

"First marker," Tomas called back, voice pitched low but carrying over the engine note. "Two o'clock. Looks like a rock, maybe two meters out of the water."

Cyrus checked the chart. Elias's pencil: *Marker 1 — twin rocks, seaward one looks like a dog's head at low water. Trust the gap between, not your eyes.*

"Come left five degrees," Cyrus said. "Hold four knots."

The Perdita eased over, her old hull groaning through the turn, and the twin rocks slid past to starboard close enough that Cyrus could hear the water sucking in the gap between them. His hands were steady on the wheel. They were always steady. It was the rest of him that felt like it was being read aloud.

Ten years. Ten years since Elias had died, and the man was still steering.

* * *

The second hour was worse than the first, because the second hour was where the channel narrowed.

Elias's annotations got denser here, the handwriting tighter, as if the pencil itself had been holding its breath. *Marker 3 — do NOT cut inside the kelp patch, there is a bommie at 1.5 m that has taken better boats than yours.* Below that, in different ink, older: *Confirmed 3x.* And below that, almost too faint to read: *Rook counted nine heads on this reef once. I have never seen more than six. Do not assume you've found them all.*

Cyrus read that line three times while the boat carried him toward it.

"Devi. Depth?"

"Fifteen meters and falling. Bottom's coming up uneven—pinnacles on both sides of the track." She looked up. "Your call, skipper. My screen says impassable. Your dead man says otherwise."

"My dead man ran a trawler through here twice a year for twenty years."

"And then he mapped the approach channel to a ship that hadn't sunk yet." Devi said it quietly, without accusation, which somehow made it worse. She'd been carrying that question since the night they found the charts, and she set it down on the wheelhouse floor between them now, gently, the way you set down something you might need to pick up again later.

Cyrus didn't answer. There wasn't an answer that fit in his mouth. Elias had annotated these waters years before the Corvane went down—he'd known the approach channel, known the depths, known the reef the way you know a room you expect to be standing in. Cyrus had spent a decade telling himself there was an explanation. Salvage men chart everything. Old captains doodle. Pencil wears strange paths.

But the annotations weren't general. They were specific to the Corvane's draft, to her likely approach lane, to the exact stretch of trench where a ship running dark in bad weather would go aground. Elias hadn't surveyed a reef. He'd surveyed a grave site before anyone was in it.

"Bottom's at eleven," Devi said. "Nine. Skipper—"

"Steady. Marker four should be a coral head breaking at half tide, thirty meters off our port bow."

The sea built suddenly ahead of them, a long glassy hump where the swell stood up over shallow coral, and then it broke—white water blooming out of the darkness twenty meters to port, close enough to feel the spray drift across the wheelhouse windows. Beyond it, just visible in the moonlight, a dark mound showed its crown above the surface.

"Marker four," Tomas called from the bow, unnecessarily. His voice had gone thin. "That was close."

"That was exactly as close as he said it would be." Cyrus heard his own voice come out flat and hard. "Come right three degrees. Then we're in the deep water for a mile, and after that it gets ugly again."

Devi watched him steer. He could feel her watching, the way you feel weather change.

* * *

They anchored in the wide pool Elias had marked halfway through the field—*good holding, sand and rubble, room to swing, nobody comes here because there is nothing here worth coming for*—and waited for the tide to turn. It was nearly midnight. The pool was a bowl of black glass ringed by the sound of breaking water, and the stars came all the way down to the reef line, doubled in the still water so that the Perdita seemed to float in the middle of the sky.

Cyrus climbed down to the aft deck, where Tomas was smoking with the cigarette cupped inside his fist like a boy hiding it from a teacher.

"You okay up there?" Tomas asked.

"No."

Tomas nodded as if that were a complete and satisfactory answer. He offered the cigarette. Cyrus took it.

For a while neither of them said anything. The reef talked to itself on all sides.

"He knew every rock," Tomas said finally. "Every single one. That gap at marker three—I've run reefs my whole life, and I wouldn't have tried that in daylight with a paid pilot. He drew it in pencil like he was writing directions to his house."

"Yeah."

"So how does a man learn a channel like that?" Tomas's eyes caught the ember-glow. "And why does a man learn a channel like that, and write it down, and seal it away, and never say a word? Not to you. Not to any of us. Ten years, skipper, and not one story about this place. A man who knows water like that talks about it. Brags about it. That's half of what old captains are."

Cyrus handed the cigarette back. "Maybe he wasn't proud of why he knew it."

"Or maybe he was protecting us from knowing." Tomas shrugged. "Either way, he kept us out. Question is whether he kept us out of danger or out of the truth."

"They're usually the same door."

Tomas laughed, short and without much humor, and flicked the cigarette into the water, where it hissed once and died. "Auntie Bee wants you below when the tide turns. She says she found something."

"What kind of something?"

"The kind she made me promise not to describe." He pushed off the rail and headed for the ladder, then paused. "Skipper. Whatever it is—whatever he was—we're through the worst of the field because of him. I just want that said out loud once. Whatever else is true."

Cyrus looked at the black water, at the reef that had parted for them like a held breath.

"It's said," he answered.

* * *

Auntie Bee had cleared the chart table in the saloon and pinned the big linen chart flat, weights at the corners. Her reading glasses were down on her nose. She looked, Cyrus thought, like a woman conducting an autopsy.

"Sit," she said.

He sat.

She turned the chart so it faced him and laid one brown finger on the approach channel—the same channel they'd just threaded, rendered in Elias's confident pencil strokes. Then she slid her finger along the annotations, one by one, and Cyrus realized she'd numbered them. Small neat numerals in her own hand, in the margin, ordering the notes into a sequence.

"Now look at the dates," she said.

"There are no dates."

"There are tides." Her finger stopped on a notation near the middle of the channel. *Springs, ebb first light, 0.4 kn cross-current at marker six.* "Elias always logged the tide state when he surveyed. Always. Thirty years of habit—you couldn't break him of it with a crowbar. So I worked backward. Cross-referenced the tide tables for the last fifteen years to find which dates match these states at these markers." She straightened up and looked at him over the glasses. "There are only four nights in fifteen years where every annotation lines up. Four nights. And the last one—" her voice caught, steadied— "the last one is eleven days before the Corvane sailed."

The saloon was very quiet. Somewhere above them, the anchor chain ticked against the hawse pipe as the tide began to move.

"He surveyed the channel eleven days before she sank," Cyrus said slowly. "On a spring tide. At night."

"At night, Cyrus. In the dark. With tide corrections for a vessel drawing seven meters." She pulled off her glasses and pressed her thumb and finger against the bridge of her nose. "I've been telling myself for three days there's a decent explanation. Coastal survey. Insurance work. Something. But you don't survey a shipping channel at night for a ship that hasn't been hired yet, unless—"

"Unless you already know where she's going to die."

Auntie Bee didn't answer. She didn't have to. The chart lay between them with all its patient pencil marks, and every mark had changed weight. What had been skill an hour ago was now evidence. What had been pride was now a question with teeth.

Cyrus stared at the annotations until they blurred. He thought of Elias teaching him to read water, Elias's hand over his on the wheel, Elias saying *the sea doesn't lie, boy, people lie and blame the sea*. He thought of Rook and Sela, whose names nobody aboard said aloud, and of the sealed locker, and of the logbook they hadn't opened yet because Cyrus hadn't been able to make himself cut the wax.

Anger rose in him like the tide—slow, total, filling every space. Not hot anger. Cold, tidal anger, the kind that moves wrecks.

"He was mapping her grave," Cyrus said. "Before she ever left port."

"Or someone paid him to," Auntie Bee said, very quietly. "And he wanted the map kept, even if he couldn't keep the money. Even if he couldn't live with it." She put her glasses back on and began, methodically, to roll the chart closed. "Men like Elias don't leave evidence, Cyrus. They leave confessions shaped like navigation."

* * *

They weighed anchor at 0300, on the making tide, exactly as the annotations prescribed. The last third of the channel was the worst—a twisting mile where the track bent three times around coral heads and the cross-current pushed them sideways at nearly half a knot, and Cyrus steered by pencil marks and faith while Devi called depths in a voice gone hoarse and Tomas hung over the bow rail staring into black water like a man counting his own heartbeats.

At 0440, the bottom fell away.

Devi watched the sounder climb—twenty meters, forty, eighty—and let out a breath she'd been holding, Cyrus suspected, for four hours. "We're through," she said. "Deep water. Open trench ahead." She turned and looked at him, exhausted, salt-crusted, lit red by the instrument glow. "He got us through. Whatever he was, he got us through."

"Yeah," Cyrus said. And then, because the anger needed somewhere to go and the wheelhouse was too small to hold it: "Now let's find out what he got us through it for."

The trench opened beneath them like a throat. On the sonar display, the seabed dropped away into blue-black nothing, and the Corvane—if Elias's numbers were honest, and everything tonight said they were—lay somewhere down in that dark, waiting under sixty meters of cold water for the one crew stubborn enough and stupid enough to come find her.

Cyrus killed the engines and let the Perdita drift, silent, over the deepest water any of them had ever worked.

"Devi," he said. "Wake the sonar tech in yourself. Paint me that bottom."

She was already reaching for the switch when the screen bloomed—and there it was. A long, hard return, lying on its side in the trench, intact, unmistakable, a hundred and forty meters of dead ship holding its shape in the dark.

Behind him, Auntie Bee whispered something that might have been a prayer or might have been a name.

Cyrus leaned closer to the screen, and the anger and the pride in his chest finally stopped fighting each other, because they had found the thing they were both aimed at.

"Hello, old girl," he said quietly. "What did he do to you?"
