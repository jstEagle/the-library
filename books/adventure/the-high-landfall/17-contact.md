## 17. Contact

Devi Okafor had a rule about sonar contacts: never fall in love with the first return. Echoes lied. Thermoclines bent them. A school of fish at the right density could paint itself as a hull, and a hull could dissolve into noise if you wanted it badly enough.

She had wanted this contact for nineteen days, and she still made herself run the pass three times before she let herself believe it.

The Perdita crawled north along Elias's annotated channel at two knots, towfish streaming forty meters astern on its umbilical, the chart table below converted into a shrine of printouts, grease pencils, and Auntie Bee's cold coffee cups. Devi sat hunched over the sonar display in the dim of the electronics nook, one earbud in, the other ear cocked to the hull above her—listening, as she always did at moments like this, for Tomas's footsteps on the deck plates, because some news was better carried by hand than by wire.

The first pass gave her a hard return off the port side of the trench line: an elongated mass, roughly a hundred and thirty meters long, sitting proud of the seabed shadow. She marked it and said nothing.

The second pass, offset fifty meters east, gave her the same mass from a different angle—and this time the shadow resolved into structure. A break in the silhouette amidships. Superstructure collapsed to starboard. The unmistakable stepped profile of a wartime transport's stern, rudder canted hard over like a man caught mid-turn.

The third pass she ran slow, gain down, every filter off, just raw return and her own eyes. The mass sat at the bottom of the trench Elias had drawn on his chart in pencil so faint it looked like regret—a trench his annotation labeled *deep water, holds cold, keep your people out of it* in his cramped engineer's hand.

Sixty-two meters to the top of the superstructure.

Devi did what she always did when the sea handed her something enormous. She got up, climbed the ladder to the wheelhouse, and told Cyrus Kavanagh in a voice that came out flatter than she intended.

"Contact. It's her."

* * *

They gathered the way they always gathered—badly. Cyrus at the plot table with his arms folded, Auntie Bee perched on the chart drawer with a dish towel over her shoulder like she'd been interrupted mid-kitchen, Tomas wedged in the doorway dripping rainwater, Maren Voss standing too close to the sonar repeater with her liaison officer hovering behind her like a paid shadow. The man's name was Ferran and he had the gift of being present without ever seeming to arrive, which Devi trusted exactly as far as she could throw the generator.

"Show me," Cyrus said.

She walked them through it. The trench, running northeast-southwest like a scar someone had tried to stitch shut. The wreck lying on its starboard side against the trench wall, bow toward deep water, stern toward the reef shelf. The blast damage aft that even the sonar shadow suggested—a concavity in the hull plating near the engineering spaces, inward-crumpled, not outward-burst.

"Depth," Cyrus said.

"Sixty-two to the rail. Sixty-eight, sixty-nine to the seabed on the down-slope side." Devi had the numbers printed and highlighted because she'd known he'd ask, and because writing them down was the only way she'd stopped her hands from shaking. "Top of the funnel is shallower. Fifty-four."

"Fifty-four," Tomas repeated from the doorway, the way a man repeats a number he'd like to argue with.

"That's past no-decompression on air by half again," Devi said. "Past anything sane. On our mix—" she pulled the gas plan she'd built three nights ago and hadn't shown anyone, because showing it would have been admitting they were going to try— "on trimix twenty-eight, bottom time's maybe eighteen minutes working, with a hang on the way up that runs close to ninety. That's if nothing goes wrong. If everything goes right, we're spending more time decompressing than diving."

"And if something goes wrong?" Auntie Bee asked quietly.

"Then the numbers stop mattering," Devi said.

Silence took the wheelhouse. Rain worked at the windows. Somewhere aft, the generator changed pitch, the Perdita's heartbeat stumbling and recovering, and Devi found her hand resting flat against the bulkhead without deciding to put it there—as if she could steady the ship by touching her.

Maren broke it first. "It's her," she said, and there was something in her voice Devi had never heard from Maren Voss at the harbour bar, across ten years of professional contempt. It sounded almost like prayer. "You're certain."

"The profile matches the Corvane's class," Devi said. "The length matches. The trench position matches the channel Elias drew. And the blast damage—" she tapped the printout, the ugly concavity— "is on the inside face. Something punched that hull inward."

Ferran shifted behind Maren, and Devi clocked it—the small recalibration of a man whose briefing hadn't included that detail. She filed him away.

Cyrus hadn't spoken since *show me*. He stood over the plot with his father's charts spread beside her sonar overlay, pencil annotations and electronic returns lying on top of each other, the dead man's hand and the living machine agreeing about where a ship full of people had gone into the water. His jaw worked once.

"Elias knew the depth," he said finally. "Look at his note. 'Deep water, holds cold.' He wrote that years before she sank. He surveyed this trench."

"He surveyed everything," Auntie Bee said. Her voice had a strange grain to it. "That's what's been eating me since the charts. He didn't map the approach like a salvager. He mapped it like a man planning a funeral."

* * *

Devi spent the afternoon doing the thing she was best at, which was turning awe into arithmetic until the arithmetic became dread.

She laid the gas plan out on the workbench in the engine room, where nobody could see it but her, and made herself be honest. Trimix 28/40 for bottom. EAN50 from thirty meters up. Oxygen for the shallow hangs. Two divers on the first descent—Cyrus would insist on that much, and Tomas would insist on going with him, and she'd already lost that argument in her head twice. Eighteen minutes on the wreck meant maybe twelve minutes of actual work after descent and locating the strongroom. Ninety-plus minutes of hanging in open blue water, watching their gauges, trusting their tables, betting their lives on deco schedules computed from borrowed software on a laptop that had survived a squall and a salt bath.

Their mixed-gas rig was marginal. She'd known it when they bought the compressor secondhand, known it when she blended their first helium mix with a flowmeter she'd calibrated against guesswork and prayer. Marginal was a word engineers used when they meant *it will probably hold*, and probably was a word the sea ate for breakfast.

Tomas found her there at dusk, sitting on an upturned bucket with the plan in her lap, not reading it.

"You've been down here four hours," he said.

"I've been down here doing math."

"Same smell." He lowered himself onto the deck beside her, back against the compressor housing, and for a while neither of them said anything, which was the language they were best at. Then: "How bad?"

"Manageable," she said, which was her worst lie, and he knew it.

"Try again."

Devi folded the plan. "Eighteen minutes bottom time. If the strongroom's easy to find, if the box is where the letter says it should be, if the hatch isn't collapsed, if nobody snags a line, if the current in that trench is slack like the tide tables promise—if all of that, we come up fine and spend an hour and a half hanging on the shot line feeling very stupid and very alive." She looked at him. "If any one of those breaks, we're doing emergency procedures at sixty meters with gas we can't spare, and you know what the survival statistics look like for that. I do. I've read them. Twice tonight."

Tomas nodded slowly, the way he nodded at engine readings that confirmed what he already feared. "And if we don't go?"

"Maren has a fleet six miles off and a navy notice giving us forty-eight hours of cover. After that, the Halcyon Dawn puts a saturation team down and takes whatever's there, and everything Elias hid stays hidden, and Rook and Sela stay names on a chart nobody reads." She unfolded the plan again, smoothed it against her knee. "I'm not saying don't go. I'm saying I want everyone to understand what the numbers cost before anyone says yes."

"You'll say yes," Tomas said.

"I'll say yes," Devi agreed. "I've already said yes. I said it when I started blending the helium in Tanah Merah. I just haven't told my mouth yet."

He laughed—short, startled out of him—and then did the thing he sometimes did that undid her completely: reached over and knocked his knuckles twice against the deck plate, old dockyard superstition, luck for the ship. For them.

"We should tell Cyrus," he said.

"Yeah." She stood, knees stiff from the bucket. "And then somebody should tell Auntie Bee, because she'll want to cook the last supper before anybody dives, and if we let her find out from Maren she'll sulk for a week and under-salt everything."

"She'd never under-salt anything."

"She'd find a way. She's that angry."

* * *

They told the others in the galley that night, over rice and fish stew that tasted better than the conversation deserved. Devi kept it clinical—depths, times, gases, margins—because clinical was how she kept her voice level, and level was how she kept from screaming at any of them for wanting this.

Cyrus listened with his forearms on the table, eyes on the middle distance where, Devi suspected, a different dive was playing: one from ten years ago, two divers who went down and didn't come up. When she finished, he was quiet long enough that the rain filled the silence.

"Bottom time's eighteen minutes," he said at last.

"Working time's less. Call it twelve once you're through the hull."

"Twelve minutes," Cyrus said, "to find a strongroom inside a ship I've never seen, in the dark, sideways, in a trench."

"Yes."

"And if we miss the window?"

"Then you blow the dive, come up empty, and live to hate yourselves. There are worse outcomes. I've listed them."

Auntie Bee set down her spoon. "You've listed them," she repeated. "Out loud? For the table?"

Devi met her eyes across the galley lamp. Auntie Bee had fed her for ten years, had taught her navigation when Devi's own charts confused her, had sat with her through the anniversary nights when Elias's name couldn't be said in the crew's hearing. Lying to her wasn't on the menu.

"There's a scenario where Tom or Cyrus doesn't come back up," Devi said. "Or both. Or me, if I go down—I'm not rated for the working dive, I know, I'm the surface tender and I hate it. The deco schedule has no fat on it. One equipment failure at depth, one missed gas switch, and the ocean collects." She turned her cup a quarter-turn. "That's the truth of the numbers. I wanted everyone to hear it from the engineer and not from the optimist."

"The optimist being Maren," Tomas muttered.

"The optimist being whoever says it'll be fine," Devi said. "Nobody's said it yet. I'm keeping score."

Maren, seated at the end of the table like a guest who'd overstayed, set her glass down carefully. "For what it's worth, I'm not asking anyone to dive beyond what they judge safe."

"No," Cyrus said. "You're asking us to beat a navy-backed fleet to a wreck in a trench. Same thing wearing a nicer coat."

Maren didn't flinch. But Devi saw her hands, wrapped around the glass, and saw the knuckles, and thought: *she knows the numbers too. Someone's given her the numbers. Maybe she's read the survival statistics herself, twice tonight.*

"Cyrus," Maren said. "My sister was on that manifest." The galley went very still; apparently she'd chosen now to say it, in front of all of them, and Devi understood it was a kind of payment—truth offered against the risk she was asking them to take. "I've spent eleven years knowing she went into the water somewhere near that trench. I'm not asking you to be careless. I'm asking you to be precise."

"Precise," Cyrus echoed.

"At sixty-two meters," Maren said, "precision is the only mercy left."

* * *

Later, alone in the electronics nook, Devi ran the numbers one final time and then, instead of closing the file, opened the sonar replay and watched the wreck scroll past the screen again. The great dark shape on the trench wall, holding its dead, holding its papers, holding whatever Elias had spent his last decade circling and never named.

She zoomed in on the stern. On the rudder, canted hard over, frozen mid-turn. Ships didn't sink turning unless something happened fast. Blast damage inward. A hull punched from within—or from a charge placed against the plating by someone who knew exactly where to set it, and had dived down afterward to confirm the job was done.

Her cursor hovered over the annotation field. She typed *CONTACT CONFIRMED — CORVANE — 62m* and dated it, and her hand paused before she hit save.

Because beneath the technical log entry, in the space where she usually noted sea state and equipment status, she found herself typing something else, something she'd deny later if anyone ever pulled the file:

*Elias, what did you do here?*

She deleted it. Saved the clean version. Climbed the ladder to the deck.

The night was black and soft with rain, the fleet's distant lights invisible but present in her mind like a headache. At the bow, Cyrus stood at the rail looking down at the water as though he could see through it—sixty-two meters down to rust and bone-white coral and a hole punched inward. Devi came to stand beside him, close enough to share warmth, far enough to leave him his silence.

"Gas plan's ready," she said.

"I know."

"I'll have the mixes blended by 0400. Slack water in the trench is 0930. That gives you a dawn brief and a morning to lose your nerve, if you're going to."

Cyrus didn't answer right away. Below the rail, the sea breathed against the hull, patient as a creditor. When he finally spoke, his voice was quiet enough that the rain nearly took it.

"Devi. The letter said the charges were set by a diver hired to confirm them. You ran the sonar. You saw where the damage is." He turned his head, and in the dark his eyes were just glints. "What if what's down there doesn't prove he was innocent?"

Devi had known this question was coming. She'd been circling it herself for days, in the engine room, in the arithmetic, in the space between the numbers where dread lived. She looked at the man who had inherited a dead captain's debt and a dead captain's shame and possibly a dead captain's crime, all bundled together with the charts that were the only reason any of them could reach this water.

"Then we bring it up anyway," she said. "We're not divers anymore, Cyrus. We're evidence."

Ahead, far off across the black water, a white light flickered once on the horizon—there and gone, deliberate, a signal from a ship that shouldn't be signaling. Neither of them moved. The light did not come again.

But Devi had spent nineteen days reading returns, and she knew a contact when she saw one.

The Halcyon Dawn knew they were here.
