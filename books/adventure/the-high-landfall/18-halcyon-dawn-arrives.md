## 18. Halcyon Dawn Arrives

Maren Voss had spent twenty years learning to read ships the way other people read faces, and the Halcyon Dawn lied with every line of her.

She came over the eastern horizon at 0600, riding low and heavy, a white-hulled leviathan with a crane arm folded along her deck like a sleeping mantis. Registry flags out of Singapore. AIS transponder chirping a name that belonged to a container ship scrapped in Alang three years ago. Paint so new it still smelled of the yard.

"Six miles," Devi said, standing at the Perdita's rail beside her, binoculars steady. "Anchoring. They're dropping the hook."

"They know we're here."

"They've known since Tanah Merah." Devi lowered the glasses. "The question is what they think we have."

What they thought she had was nothing, Maren told herself. That was the whole architecture of the plan—she'd built it that way, layer on layer, the way you built a decompression schedule: redundancy stacked on redundancy until failure became arithmetic instead of catastrophe. The contract routed through two shell companies. The liaison officer, Aldous Wren, reported to backers who reported to backers who reported to men whose names appeared nowhere. Cyrus Kavanagh knew only that he was racing another salvage outfit to a wreck worth diving. The manifest—the actual object, the reason any of this existed—was a word she hadn't said aloud in eleven years.

Except the plan had a flaw, and the flaw was her face, and right now she could feel it doing something she couldn't control.

"You should eat," Auntie Bee said from behind them, materializing the way she always did, a mug of coffee already extended. "Standing at a rail looking worried burns calories. I read that somewhere."

"I'm not worried."

"Mm." Auntie Bee pressed the mug into her hands anyway. "Drink it while it's honest."

* * *

By 0900 the Halcyon Dawn's intentions were legible even to Tomas, who came down from the mast with his shirt stuck to his back and his voice pitched carefully casual.

"She's flying a naval house flag now. Blue pennant, gold border. And there's a broadcast going out on the maritime safety channels—looped. Restricted zone, twelve nautical mile radius, centered on these coordinates." He laid a printout on the chart table. "Effective immediately. Duration forty-eight hours, pending 'survey operations of national significance.'"

Cyrus read it twice. "National significance."

"That's the phrase."

"It means they've bought themselves a legal fence," Maren said. "Anyone inside the zone after the notice is trespassing in a military exclusion area. It gives them cover to board, seize, detain—whatever they want, whenever they want it. It also gives us—" She stopped. Everyone was looking at her.

"It gives us what?" Cyrus asked.

Forty-eight hours, she wanted to say. Forty-eight hours where the same order that threatens you protects you, because a navy that declares a zone has to police it, and a fleet that paid for the declaration can't move openly inside it without explaining why a corporate survey vessel cares about a restricted military site. It gave them both a cage and a shield, and which one it became depended entirely on who moved first.

It also meant her backers were done pretending. The notice wasn't caution. It was a signature on a confession—proof that whatever the Corvane held, someone powerful enough to borrow a navy's pen wanted it to stay down there for another decade, or forever.

"It gives us a clock," she said. "That's all it gives anyone."

Wren was in the wheelhouse corner, as he'd been all morning, tablet in hand, watching the Dawn's silhouette with an expression Maren couldn't parse. She caught him once glancing at her—not the way a handler watches an asset, but the way a man watches weather he's been told won't reach him.

"Aldous," she said quietly, drawing him toward the companionway. "Your people just painted a target on this boat. I'd like to know if we're still partners or already liabilities."

His smile was professional, brief, and told her nothing. "Ms. Voss. The notice is a standard instrument. It keeps third parties clear while legitimate operations proceed."

"We're a third party."

"You're contracted first-party interests operating under pre-existing authorization." A beat. "The distinction will hold as long as results arrive on schedule. My principals are... less patient than the tide." He turned back to his tablet, and Maren understood the conversation was over, and understood something else beneath it, cold and settling: Wren wasn't lying to her. He was managing her. Which meant somewhere above him, someone had already decided how this ended, and she hadn't been consulted.

* * *

They gathered in the mess that evening because the mess was where decisions happened aboard the Perdita—around a table scarred by ten years of elbows, under a lamp Auntie Bee refused to replace because its flicker kept everyone honest.

The Dawn sat six miles off like a lit city, floodlights washing her white hull, helicopter pad glowing. Too far to matter, too close to forget.

"Here's the arithmetic," Cyrus said, and Maren watched him do what he did best: strip a problem down to weight and water. "Their dive capability tops out around fifty meters—saturation support takes days to rig, and they don't have it aboard, or they'd be deploying it tonight instead of lights. The Corvane sits at sixty-two. Our mixed-gas rig is marginal but workable. We can be on the wreck before their ROV finishes its safety checks. But—" and here he paused, and Maren saw him weigh the pause, saw him decide to say it anyway— "the restricted zone changes the risk. Before today, worst case was a competitor beating us down. Now worst case is a boarding party with a warrant and no rules worth the paper."

"So we run," Tomas said. Not a challenge—a question, honestly asked.

"And spend ten years wondering," Devi said. "No."

Auntie Bee said nothing. She sat with Elias's chart spread before her, one finger resting on the approach channel, her eyes on some middle distance where the dead kept their appointments. When the vote came, she abstained again, and Maren felt the abstention like a draft through a sealed door.

Then Cyrus looked at her. "You've been quiet."

Because she had been rehearsing, Maren thought. All day. Since dawn, since the white hull rose over the horizon wearing someone else's name. She had a speech prepared about schedules and windows and the monsoon's leading edge, technical, unanswerable, the kind of case she'd have made to a boardroom.

Instead she heard herself say: "Dive tonight."

The table went still.

"The window is real," she went on, faster now, the rehearsal burning off, the thing underneath showing through. "Not the monsoon—I mean the notice. For forty-eight hours the Dawn can't deploy divers without filing movement plans through a naval liaison, can't put boats in the water without logged authorization. They built themselves a cage and they're sitting in it thinking it's a throne. Every hour we wait is an hour they spend getting comfortable, getting permissions, getting that saturation system flown in from wherever it's parked. Tonight, they're a lit ship with a locked gate. Tomorrow they're a fortress." Her voice cracked on the last word, actually cracked, and she hated it, covered it with the mug of cold coffee, drank like it was armor.

Cyrus was studying her the way he studied wrecks—methodically, patiently, looking for the structural failure.

"Why tonight?" he said. "Really."

"Because—"

"And don't give me the tide tables. You've got a hydrographic answer for everything and none of them are why your hands shake when you look at that ship."

Her hands weren't shaking. She checked. They were.

* * *

Later—or maybe not later; maybe she walked straight up onto the aft deck because staying seated meant answering him—she found Cyrus at the stern rail, watching the Dawn's lights smear across the black water. The night was thick, pre-monsoon, the air like a wet cloth.

He didn't speak. That was his weapon, she remembered, from certification days in Subic, fifteen years ago: Cyrus Kavanagh never rushed a silence, because most people drowned in one.

She'd never been most people. But tonight—

"My sister's name is Anneli Voss," she said.

The words came out of her like blood from a cut you'd forgotten you had. Cyrus turned slowly.

"There. Now you know something true, and I'd thank you to understand what it costs me, because I have spent eleven years making sure nobody aboard this ocean knows anything true about me." She gripped the rail. "Anneli was twenty-six. She took the Corvane because it was cheap and fast and the agent in Port Cael swore the storm window was clean. She sent me a letter from the terminal—actual paper, she was sentimental like that. It's in a drawer in my flat in Fremantle. I've read it four hundred times. The last line is 'back by Thursday.'"

The Dawn's floodlights trembled in the swell. Somewhere on that ship, men were being paid by the same consortium that had scuttled a transport with passengers locked below, and one of those passengers had written *back by Thursday*, and Maren Voss had built a career, a reputation, a whole armored self on the principle that grief was a private inefficiency.

"Cyrus. I need to tell you what I actually know, because if I go down with this wreck I am not letting the truth go with me."

"Now you're scaring me."

"Good. Stay scared. Scared men check their gear twice." She turned to face him fully. "The Corvane wasn't lost. She was sunk. Charges against the plating—your own eyes found the damage pattern on the first descent, blast geometry that doesn't match any grounding or engine casualty ever recorded. The consortium insured her for triple value, sailed her into open water with a skeleton crew who got off and two hundred souls who didn't, and called it weather. There are documents aboard that prove it. A manifest. A ledger. Names, dates, signatures. Everything needed to hang the men who signed the order—and everything needed to hang whoever helped them."

She watched him absorb it. Watched his jaw change.

"Elias," he said. Just the name. Flat.

"Elias Kavanagh was hired to confirm the charges were set correctly. That's in the documents too. His initials. His certification stamp." She said it plainly because kindness would have been worse. "I found out eight years ago, from a marine court archivist who owed me. I hired you anyway. Do you understand? I could have gone to any crew in the region. I came to the one man whose captain's name is welded into those papers, because your charts are the only ones that reach the channel—and because I thought—" Her throat closed. She pushed through it. "I thought if Elias's own crew brought the proof up, it might count for something. Redemption by proxy. I told myself it was strategy. It was the closest thing to forgiveness I know how to buy."

The silence between them lasted long enough for the swell to lift and drop the tug twice.

"You let me sign that contract," Cyrus finally said, and his voice was very quiet, very controlled, the voice of a man holding a hull together with his bare hands, "knowing what my captain did."

"I let you sign it knowing what your captain *was*," Maren said. "Which isn't the same thing. I've read the edges of this, Cyrus. Payments tied to the sailing date, yes. But there's a gap in the record—a week between the sinking and his last logged payment. Nobody pays a diver for a week of silence unless the diver stopped cooperating." She shook her head. "I don't know what happened down there. I know the consortium thinks it's buried. I know my sister is at sixty-two meters with her name on a list that says she never existed. And I know that in thirty-one hours that ship's lawyers will make this reef legally radioactive, and everything down there becomes evidence seized by the state and shredded by the state, and Anneli stays *back by Thursday* forever."

She hadn't begged anyone for anything since she was nineteen years old and a harbormaster told her no. She begged now. She didn't dress it up.

"Dive tonight, Cyrus. Please. Take me down with you if you have to strap me to the stage bottle. I'll carry gear until my arms come off. I'll stay shallow, I'll tend lines, I'll do anything except sit on this deck one more night listening to their generator hum while my sister waits." Her voice broke completely then, and she let it, because the mask was already in pieces on the deck plates and stepping on the shards changed nothing. "I'm asking you to help me rob a grave. I know what that makes me. Make me anything you want afterward. Just make it tomorrow."

* * *

Cyrus looked past her, out at the lights, for a long time. Long enough that she started cataloguing exits, rebuttals, the fallback positions of a woman who had never once in her adult life been without one.

Then he said: "Devi runs the gas plan. If she says the mix supports a working descent tonight, we dive. If she says no, we wait, and you'll wait with us without argument." A pause. "And Maren—if there's a way Elias comes out of this cleaner than the papers suggest, I need you ready to believe it. Because I'm going down there looking for it either way, and I can't do that while watching you flinch at his name."

"He cut my sister's world open," Maren said. "I'll believe what the water tells us. That's the only witness I trust."

Something moved through his face—agreement, or grief, or both wearing each other's clothes.

"Get some rest," he said. "First descent party briefs at 0400. Water's calmest at slack before dawn, and if we're on the bottom when the sun clears the horizon, the Dawn's watch officers will be changing shift and drinking bad coffee while we're already inside their fence." He turned back to the rail, then added, without turning around: "You should know Auntie Bee voted with her silence tonight. On this boat, that's a louder yes than a raised hand. Whatever happens below—we surface to a family that decided, before we asked."

Maren stood alone at the stern after he left, and let herself feel, for exactly one minute by her watch, the full unarmored weight of what she'd set in motion. Then she wound the bezel forward, wiped her face dry with the heel of her hand, and went below to check her own gear twice.

At 0347, the wheelhouse light clicked on. At 0352, Devi's voice came over the intercom, flat and certain:

"Gas plan's green. Sixty-two meters, eighteen minutes bottom time. Cyrus wants you suited up, Ms. Voss. You're diving."

Above them, six miles off, the Halcyon Dawn burned through the dark like a promise someone else intended to keep.
