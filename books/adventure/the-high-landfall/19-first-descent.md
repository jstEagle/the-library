## 19. First Descent

The water at sixty meters didn't feel like water. It felt like a decision.

Cyrus hung on the shot line, checking his gauges by feel more than sight—the light down here was the color of old bruises, green-grey and thick, and his torch beam only carved a cone out of it, a tunnel of visibility that ended in nothing. Above him the Perdita's hull was a smudge, a rumor of a ship. Below him was sixty-two meters of trench and the thing he'd sworn off for a decade.

"Bottom in four minutes," Tomas said, his voice arriving through the comm with a slight underwater warble, calm as a man reading a grocery list. Tomas always sounded like that down here. Cyrus had never decided whether it was courage or a defect.

"Copy. Watch your gas. We turn at fifteen hundred bar or the first sign of trouble, whichever comes first."

"You say that like I'd argue."

"Everyone argues at depth. It's the nitrogen."

Tomas laughed, a small burst of static. "Then I'll argue with you on the surface, where it's cheap."

Cyrus let out a long breath and watched the bubbles climb, silver coins rising toward a sky he wouldn't touch again for two hours. He ran the numbers the way Elias had taught him—Elias, who used to tap Cyrus's gauge twice before every descent, a little ritual, *count your life in breaths, boy, not in dollars.* Ten years dead, and the man still ran the dive.

He didn't let himself think past that. Not yet.

* * *

The trench opened beneath them at fifty meters like a mouth.

The reef wall fell away in terraces, each one furred with life—black coral trees like burnt lace, a cloud of fusiliers parting around their torch beams, a grouper the size of a door hinged to the rock, watching them with the flat patience of something that had outlived every predator it had ever met. And below the terraces, below the last honest color, the shape.

Cyrus saw it the way you see a body in a dark room: not all at once, but as an absence the light kept falling into. Then the beam caught steel, and the absence became a ship.

The *Corvane* lay on her port side in the sand, a two-hundred-foot wartime transport settled into the trench like she'd lain down to sleep and simply never gotten up. Her hull was furred white—ten years of coral claiming her, bone-white plating, white rails, white superstructure, a ghost ship bleached by the dark. Her funnel had collapsed. Her masts lay along the seabed like fallen trees. She was, Cyrus thought, the largest grave marker he had ever seen, and someone had written nothing on it at all.

"Jesus," Tomas said quietly. "She's *big*."

"They always are, down here."

They descended the last few meters along the shot line, fins kicking slow, and Cyrus felt the depth settle into him like a passenger. The mix was doing its job, but the trench had its own gravity, its own opinion. At sixty meters, on their gas plan, every minute was borrowed. He'd planned the dive to the second and the second was already spending itself.

The hull rose out of the sand ahead of them, and Cyrus put his glove against it.

The steel was cold through his glove, and it came away in flakes under his touch—rust and coral and old paint, a decade of the sea eating her from the outside in. He swept his torch along the plating, reading the hull the way Auntie Bee read charts, and that was when he saw it.

The hole.

It was amidships, just below the waterline as she'd sat in life, maybe four meters across, and its edges were bent *inward*. Not torn outward the way a hull fails when a ship breaks up or grounds or scatters itself on a reef. Inward. A clean, punched, inward crater, the steel curled into the wreck like a fist driven through a door.

He felt it before he understood it, in his chest, in his teeth—a vibration that wasn't in the water at all.

"Cyrus." Tomas had come up beside him. His torch was on the same spot. "Tell me I'm reading this wrong."

"You're reading it right."

"Because the report said grounding. Reef strike, storm, hull opened on coral. That's the account. That's what Elias—" Tomas stopped himself. Down here, at this depth, you didn't waste breath on sentences that went nowhere.

Cyrus ran his light around the crater's rim. Coral had colonized the edges, bone-white and thick, but the steel underneath told the story plainly enough for a man who'd spent twenty years reading wrecks. There were concentric fracture lines radiating from the center. There was a petaling pattern, sharp and directional. And there was, on the plating beside the hole, a smear of soot-dark metal that no reef in the world had put there.

"Charged," Cyrus said. "Placed against the hull. Below the waterline, amidships, where the framing is lightest." He heard his own voice come out flat and clinical and was grateful for it, because the other thing in his voice would have been unbearable. "Someone sank this ship, Tomas. She didn't sink."

The fusiliers had come back, a wheeling silver band above them, indifferent. A shrimp walked across the crater's rim on legs too small to see. The trench held its breath, and so did Cyrus.

*Elias.* The name arrived without permission. Elias, who had mapped this approach channel years before the ship went down. Elias, who had lost two divers here and forbade the crew to speak of it, ever, on pain of something worse than anger. Elias, whose last logbook ended mid-sentence in a code Devi had cracked over three sleepless nights, and whose decoded entries had put a cold hand around the whole crew's throat.

*Payments received. Tied to the sailing date.*

Cyrus had told himself there was another explanation. There was always another explanation, if you wanted one badly enough. The ledger was ambiguous. Devi's decoding had been strained. The old man had been old, and frightened, and maybe confused.

The hole in the hull was not ambiguous. The hole in the hull was a sentence with one word in it, and the word was *yes*.

"Strongbox," he said, because if he kept talking about the hole he was going to start thinking about the hole, and he had a gas plan that didn't care about his feelings. "Aft of the break. Maren's survey put the strongroom two frames back from the bulkhead. Move."

* * *

They swam the length of the wreck's exposed side, torch beams sweeping the bleached plating, and Cyrus made himself look at everything, because looking was the job now and flinching was a luxury.

Portholes, some open, some shut. Coral in the shape of hands. A cargo winch hanging off its mount by a single bolt, swaying in nothing. The hull's white fur thinned as they went aft, and the steel showed through in patches, red-brown and flaking, and once—just once—Cyrus's light found a boot. Leather, mostly gone, the sole upturned in the sand like a small boat run aground. He looked at it for exactly one second, marked its position in his head for the report he would never file, and swam on.

Tomas didn't mention it. That was why Cyrus dove with Tomas.

"There." Tomas's light stabbed forward. "The break."

The superstructure's aft section had collapsed across the hull in a tangle of plates and piping, but beneath it, where the deckhouse met the cargo well, there was a gap—a dark slot in the wreck's side, wide enough for a diver, too wide for comfort. The coral grew thickest around it, as if the reef knew it was a wound and had tried to close it for ten years.

Cyrus checked his gauges. Fourteen hundred bar. Twelve minutes on the bottom, planned eighteen. He held up a fist, then two fingers: *in and out, no lingering.* Tomas mirrored it.

The slot swallowed them.

Inside, the *Corvane* was a cathedral built by drowning. Their lights moved through a silt-hazed dark full of drifting motes, and the beams came back off surfaces that had no right to exist—formica tabletops furred green, a brass fitting still bright where the current kept it polished, a doorway standing in a wall that had otherwise become rubble. Their exhaust bubbles ran along the overhead, gathering in the pockets the wreck's angle made, silver ceilings that shivered and merged. Everything was white. Everything was soft. The sea had spent ten years upholstering this place in bone.

Cyrus moved through it like a man in a museum at night, touching nothing, and the narcosis sat down beside him around the time he passed the second bulkhead. He knew the signs; he'd been briefed on them since he was nineteen, and he'd felt them a hundred times. The warmth first. Then the certainty—the low, honeyed certainty that everything was fine, that the numbers were fine, that he could stay another five minutes, ten, that there was no hurry anywhere in the world. It felt like being loved. That was the trick of it. Nitrogen felt like being loved by something that wanted to keep you.

*Count your life in breaths, boy, not in dollars.*

He checked his gauge. Thirteen hundred. The warmth tried to tell him that was plenty. He told it to shut up and kept swimming.

The strongroom door was where Maren's survey said it would be—a steel door, dogged shut, its wheel furred white. The frame around it had buckled slightly when the ship settled, but not enough to seal it; there was a hand's width of gap at the bottom, and Cyrus put his light to it and saw, in the gap, a darkness that wasn't silt. A space. A room that had stayed dry long enough, maybe, to keep something.

He took the pry bar off his harness and set it in the gap, and the work was ugly and slow and exactly the kind of work he was good at. Leverage, patience, small movements. The door groaned—a sound that traveled through the wreck like a word through a sleeping house—and gave, and gave, and opened.

The room beyond was small and mostly empty. Shelving, collapsed. A desk bolted to the deck, its drawers long since rifled by the sea or by whoever had come before. And on the deck itself, wedged against the desk's leg where it had slid when the ship rolled, a box.

It was maybe two feet long, steel, with a handle on top and a combination dial under a hinged cover, and it was the only object in the room that the coral had not claimed. The sea had gotten to everything else in the *Corvane*—softened it, whitened it, made it part of the reef. The box sat in the middle of all that bone like a tooth.

Cyrus's hands were steady. He noted that with a kind of distant professional surprise. His heart was going like a drum, his breath like a bellows, the narcosis whispering its warm nonsense—and his hands were steady, because some part of him had been waiting ten years to pick this up, and that part didn't shake.

He clipped the lanyard, tested the weight. Heavy. Heavier than an empty box had any right to be.

"Got it," he said. "Turning."

"Copy. Cyrus—" Tomas's voice did something it almost never did down here; it hesitated. "The hole. Amidships. If someone set charges on this ship, they set them from outside. From a boat. Or—"

"Or from a diver," Cyrus said.

The words went into the water and stayed there. Neither of them picked them up again. There was no version of that sentence that didn't end with a man Cyrus had loved.

They backed out of the slot into the open dark, and Cyrus took one last look along the wreck's bleached length—the white rails, the punched-in crater, the trench holding her the way the sea holds everything it's been given—and made himself turn away.

* * *

The ascent was a discipline, nothing more. Cyrus turned it into a discipline because that was all he had.

Six meters a minute to the first stop, the wreck shrinking beneath them until it was only a pale suggestion, a scar on the trench floor. Then the stops, hung on the line like laundry, three minutes here, five there, the deco schedule Devi had built running through his head in her dry voice: *you shortchange the stops, you buy the bends, and I am not recompressing either of you in a fish hold.* The ocean around them changed character as they rose—the bruise-green lifting toward blue, the cold lifting toward warmth, the trench's gravity letting go of them by degrees.

Tomas hung beside him, patient as ballast. At the thirty-meter stop he said, "You saw the boot."

"Yeah."

"You okay?"

"I'll be okay at forty bar and on deck."

"That's not what I asked."

Cyrus watched his bubbles climb. "No. But I will be. Ask me again after we open the box."

At fifteen meters the light turned honest and the water turned warm, and Cyrus felt the narcosis finally let go of him—the warm certainty draining away like a fever breaking, replaced by the ordinary weight of everything he'd seen. The crater, bent inward. The soot. The boot with its sole to the sky. The box on his lanyard, knocking gently against his hip with each breath, patient as a heart.

*Payments received. Tied to the sailing date.*

He'd spent three days telling himself Devi had read the code wrong. He'd spent ten years telling himself Elias had been protecting them—protecting *someone*—and that the silence had been a kind of love. He'd built a whole man out of that silence, a good man, a hard man who carried hard things so his crew wouldn't have to. The hole in the hull had just taken a wrecking bar to all of it, and the steel had bent inward.

At six meters he did his final stop, five minutes, watching the Perdita's keel come down to meet them, and Tomas's hand found his shoulder and squeezed once—*good dive, we're alive, almost done*—and Cyrus found that his eyes were stinging and told himself it was the salt.

* * *

They broke the surface into late afternoon light so bright it hurt, into the smell of diesel and the sound of Auntie Bee already shouting at them to grab the ladder before the swell put them under the transom. Hands pulled them aboard—Tomas first, then Cyrus, the box passed up ahead of him like a newborn, like contraband, like both.

Devi met him on the deck with a towel and a look that stripped his gauges, his gait, his pupils, everything. "Talk."

"Sixty-two meters, on plan, no issues." He set the box on the deck between them. It sat there in the daylight, streaming seawater, and everyone stopped moving. Even the wind seemed to lean in. "She's intact. More intact than she should be. And there's a hole in her hull, Devi, four meters wide, bent *inward*, with blast residue on the plating. She didn't hit a reef. She didn't break up in a storm. Somebody put her down."

Auntie Bee had gone very still by the wheel, a dish towel forgotten in her hands.

"And this," Cyrus said, "came out of her strongroom."

He looked down at the box, at the combination dial under its little hinged cover, furred with ten years of the sea but whole, sealed, waiting. Somewhere inside it was a partial manifest, Maren had said. Names. And if the hole in the hull was the sentence, the box was the signature.

Tomas crouched next to it and turned the dial idly with one finger. "Anybody want to guess the combination?"

Nobody laughed. Then Cyrus did—short, and not really a laugh at all—because he was already turning the dial, already trying the only number that made sense, the number that had been burned into all of them, the number of tides, of years, of silence.

The dial stopped. The cover popped.

Inside, dry as the day it was closed, lay a sealed oilcloth packet—and a single sheet of paper, folded once, with a name written across it in a hand Cyrus had known since he was nineteen years old.

The hand was Elias's. The name was not his own.
