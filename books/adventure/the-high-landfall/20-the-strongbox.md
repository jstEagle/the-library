## 20. The Strongbox

Devi heard them come up the ladder before she saw them—the particular rhythm of two exhausted divers, boots heavy on the rungs, the tender's winch still groaning somewhere below. She had the hot water already going. That was the rule Auntie Bee had set a decade ago and nobody had ever broken it: hot water first, talk second, because cold divers say stupid things and stupid things said cold become stupid things believed.

Cyrus came over the rail first, and Devi knew something was wrong before he'd fully cleared the gunwale.

It wasn't his face. Cyrus's face was a locked door at the best of times; ten years of running the Perdita had worn it down to something a poker player would weep over. It was his hands. He was holding the strongbox the way you hold something you're not sure is a gift or a grenade—away from his body, fingers white, like it might go off if he let himself want it.

Tomas came up behind him, hauling the gear bag, and when their eyes met Devi felt the whole deck tilt a few degrees off true.

"Hot shower's rigged," she said. "Both of you. Now."

"Devi." Cyrus set the box on the workbench amidships. It was maybe sixty centimeters long, steel gone the color of old bone, crusted in a decade of coral and rust. A brass plate on the lid had been scoured nearly blank by the sea. "We need to open this first."

"You need to get warm first. You were at sixty-two meters on a gas plan with no margin. Your judgment is currently dissolved in nitrogen."

"Devi."

She looked at him. He looked back. And she understood, in the way you understand a hull sound before the instrument confirms it—that this wasn't a request. That whatever was in the box had already gotten into him down there, in the dark, in the flooded corridor of a ship that had no business being on the bottom, and that every minute they waited was a minute it kept working on him.

She killed the water heater. "Tomas. Tools."

* * *

It took forty minutes.

The lock was a marine-grade combination unit, salted shut, and the hinges were worse—fused with corrosion into something less like metal and more like geology. Tomas worked the hinges with a grinder and a chisel while Devi ran a penetrating oil into the seams every few minutes, the smell of it sharp over the salt. Cyrus stood over them the whole time and did not help, which was how Devi knew how bad it was. Cyrus always helped. Cyrus was a man who could not watch a knot being tied without adjusting it. He stood there with his hands at his sides and his breathing carefully even and let them work.

Auntie Bee came out of the galley twice. The first time she set down coffee and left without a word. The second time she didn't leave.

"Stand back," Tomas said, and drove the chisel into the seam under the lid's rear edge, and the hinge let go with a crack that sounded, to Devi, like something a lot bigger breaking.

The lid came up.

Inside: a waterproof document case, the old military kind, rubber-gasketed, and a smaller metal tin that had rusted through at one corner. And beneath them, filling the bottom of the box, a layer of silt and—Devi's stomach dropped—a scatter of small bones. Fish bones, probably. She made herself believe fish bones.

Cyrus lifted the document case out with both hands. The gasket had held. When he unzipped it, the pages inside were dry.

"Paper towels," Auntie Bee said. "Dry hands. Nobody breathes on it."

They cleared the workbench. Devi laid out the pages in the late-afternoon light, weighted at the corners with wrenches, and the crew of the Perdita stood around them the way people stand around a body.

The top page was a manifest.

* * *

It was headed with the Corvane's name and a sailing date Devi knew without checking—everyone in the region knew the date, the way Americans knew Pearl Harbor. *S.S. CORVANE, OUTBOUND, TANAH MERAH TO PORT SAID, PASSENGER MANIFEST — PARTIAL, REV. 3.* Someone had stamped it REV. 3, which meant there had been a revision, which meant the list had changed after it was first drawn up, and Devi felt her analyst's brain start turning that over before she made herself stop and just read.

One hundred and forty-one names. Typed in columns. Ages, nationalities, cabin numbers. A clerk's neat little world: engineers, missionaries, a schoolteacher, a family of six with the surname Adeyemi, a widow traveling with her sister. Halfway down the second column, the typewriter ribbon had faded and someone had gone back over the letters by hand in pen.

Devi was scanning the names without meaning to, the way you scan a crowd for a face, and then she stopped scanning, because she saw it.

*VOSS, ILSE M. — Cabin C-12 — Age 19.*

She didn't say anything. She looked at the name for a long moment and then she looked at the pages below it, because whatever was happening in her chest had to wait, because the next document was a letter, and the letter was dated eleven days before the Corvane sailed.

It was on consortium letterhead. Devi recognized the name from the news, from the lawsuits that had gone nowhere, from the memorial plaques that had been paid for by public subscription because the consortium's insurers had tied everything up in arbitration for six years. The letter was signed with initials she didn't know and addressed to someone identified only as *K.*

She read it once at speed, the way you take a wave. Then she read it again, slowly, and felt the deck go out from under her for the second time in one afternoon.

*...as discussed, the survey fee is released in two tranches, the first on confirmation of placement and the second on confirmation of effect. Your man will need to verify the charges are set per the revised plan—below the waterline, portside aft, as marked. We cannot stress enough that discretion is the entire value of this arrangement. The vessel must be a loss. The vessel must be a loss at sea. The timing of the loss is the sole remaining variable and will be communicated separately...*

Below the waterline. Portside aft. *Verify the charges are set.*

Devi's eyes went to Cyrus, because there was only one person in the world the letter could mean, and Cyrus was already reading it over her shoulder, and he had gone the color of the coral they'd left on the hull sixty meters down.

"K," he said. His voice came out flat, stripped of everything. "Kavanagh."

"It could be anyone," Tomas said. It was the right thing to say, the loyal thing, and it fooled no one, least of all Tomas, whose voice cracked in the middle of it.

Devi turned back to the last page in the case. It was a receipt. A single typed line on a carbon copy, a payment schedule, two tranches, amounts that made her eyes water even now, ten years later, even adjusted for everything. And at the bottom, in a hand she had known for twenty years—a hand she had watched fill out dive tables and parts orders and birthday cards for Tomas, a hand she had trusted with her life in water and out of it—

*E. Kavanagh.*

Signed. Dated. Eleven days before the Corvane sailed.

* * *

Nobody spoke for a long time. The Perdita's generator chugged. Somewhere aft, the sea slapped the hull in its idiot, indifferent rhythm.

It was Auntie Bee who moved. She reached out and turned the manifest page, gently, the way she turned the pages of her recipe book, and looked at the names for a while, all one hundred and forty-one of them, and then she said, very quietly, in the voice she used for the worst news, the kind you deliver over rice:

"One hundred and forty-one. And they paid him to make sure it stayed one hundred and forty-one."

"We don't know that." Cyrus's voice. "We don't know what he did. We don't know if he—"

"Verify the charges are set, Cyrus." Devi heard her own voice and didn't recognize it. It was cold and precise and it came from somewhere below the hurt, some pressure layer she hadn't known she had. "That's what the letter says. That's what he signed for. He didn't just know. He was the quality control."

"He was our captain." Tomas, again, and now there was nothing in his voice at all, which was worse than the crack.

"And the night she went down," Devi said, "he lost two divers on that wreck. Rook and Sela. And the next morning he stood on this deck and told us never to speak of it again, and we obeyed him for ten years, because he was Elias, because he'd earned it, because—" She stopped. Her hands were shaking. She put them flat on the workbench and made them stop. "Because we thought he was protecting us from the grief."

"And if he wasn't," Auntie Bee said.

Nobody finished the sentence.

Devi looked at Cyrus. He was staring at the signature, and she watched him do the thing he always did, the thing she had loved him for and hated him for across a decade of shared bunks and shared danger: he went inward. He started building the case for the defense. She could see it assembling behind his eyes—*the signature could be forged, the letter could be planted, Maren could have staged this, the box could have been seeded*—and she watched the case collapse even as he built it, because the box had been sealed under ten years of coral, because the paper was period-correct, because the payment amounts matched a number Cyrus himself had once mentioned, drunk, one anniversary night: *he said he came into money the year Rook died, and he never said how, and I never asked.*

"You knew," Devi said. It wasn't an accusation. It was worse. It was recognition. "Some part of you always knew. That's why you never opened his locker. That's why you signed Maren's contract when every instinct you have said run. You needed to know, and you needed someone else to make you look."

Cyrus didn't deny it. He stood there with the letter in his hands and the manifest spread out in front of him and the bones of the Corvane's strongroom still wet on his boots, and he said, "He dove that wreck the night it went down. He was already there. He knew where the bodies were before the navy did."

"Because he put the charges where he told them they'd be," Devi said.

"Or because he went back for them." Cyrus's head came up. "Devi. Listen to me. The letter says verify the charges. It doesn't say what he found when he got down there. It doesn't say—"

"It doesn't matter what he found." She heard herself and couldn't stop. "He took the second tranche. Look at the schedule—there's a release date for confirmation of effect, and there's a second signature line, and it's signed, Cyrus. He confirmed. He took the money *after* they were all dead."

The silence that followed had weight. It pressed down on the deck like deep water.

Tomas sat down slowly on the bench, the way divers sit when their legs stop being reliable. Auntie Bee took off her glasses and cleaned them on her apron, a small domestic motion, and put them back on, and Devi understood that she was doing it so she wouldn't have to be seen for a moment.

It was Cyrus who finally spoke, and his voice was the old voice, the dive-brief voice, the one that came out level no matter what the water was doing.

"Her sister's on that list."

Devi blinked. "What?"

"C-12." He touched the manifest, one name, *VOSS, ILSE M., AGE 19.* "Maren's sister. Maren didn't hire us to beat a salvage fleet. She hired us because we're the only crew with Elias's charts, and Elias is the only man who ever mapped the approach channel, because Elias is the man who sank her sister." He looked up, and his eyes were dry and terrible. "She's known. She's known for years. And she picked us—she picked *his* crew—to bring it up, because she wanted us to find this. She wanted us to know what he was."

* * *

Devi walked aft after that. She didn't decide to; her legs did it. She stood at the rail with the reef shelf sliding past below, blue shading into black, and she thought about Elias Kavanagh teaching her to read a decompression table when she was twenty-three and broke and one bad charter from drowning. She thought about him standing between her and a customs thug in Tanah Merah with a smile and a bribe and never telling her what it cost him. She thought about the last thing he ever said to her, three days before the heart took him in his bunk: *Take care of the boat, Devi. The boat never lies to you.*

The boat never lied to anyone. That had always been the difference between the boat and the captain.

She heard footsteps and didn't turn. Cyrus came and stood beside her, forearms on the rail, and for a while they just watched the water, which was the oldest ritual they had.

"The fleet's going to be here inside two days," he said finally. "Maren's going to be on this deck inside two hours, and she's going to want this box, and I have to decide what to tell her before she gets here."

"You're going to tell her the truth."

"I'm going to tell her what's in it. Whether it's the truth—" He exhaled. "Devi, I need to know one thing, and I need you to say it straight. Can you sail with me after this? Knowing what's in that box, knowing I brought us here—can you?"

Devi looked at him. Ten years of grey water and grey work and one dead man's silence between them, and the floor had dropped out of all of it, and here he was asking her the only question that mattered, which was not about the money or the manifest or Maren Voss at all.

"I can sail with you," she said. "I can't sail with *him*. And he's dead, so we'll manage." She pushed off the rail. "But Cyrus—when she gets here, you ask her the question you're avoiding. Not what's in the box. Why she really picked us. Because if she knew about the payments, she knew about the second tranche, and if she knew about the second tranche, she knew there's a ledger. And a ledger has more names on it than his."

Cyrus turned. "What do you mean?"

"I mean Elias was a diver, not a consortium. Someone gave the order. Someone signed the other end of that letter." She nodded back toward the workbench, toward the pages drying in the lamplight, toward the hundred and forty-one names. "And I want to know why a navy patrol boat warned us off this exact patch of ocean with rehearsed courtesy, three weeks before we ever signed a contract."

From up forward, Auntie Bee's voice carried across the deck, calm as a weather report:

"Boat coming in. Running dark, no lights. It'll be Maren."
