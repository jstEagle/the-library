## 21. Confrontation in the Wheelhouse

Maren Voss had rehearsed this conversation for eleven years, and in every version of it she was the one holding the truth like a lantern, calm and steady while Cyrus Kavanagh came to terms with it.

In none of the versions did he come through the wheelhouse door already knowing.

He didn't knock. The Perdita's wheelhouse was too small for ceremony—Devi's chart table bolted to the port bulkhead, Auntie Bee's radio stack humming on the starboard side, the wheel itself a relic from some decommissioned ferry that Elias had salvaged and never replaced. Cyrus filled the doorway the way he filled most spaces: not large, but absolute. His dive skin was still damp at the shoulders. Salt had dried white along his jaw. In one hand, wrapped in a chamois cloth, he carried the letter they'd pulled from the strongbox—the one Devi had read aloud on deck twenty minutes ago in a voice that got quieter with every line.

The one that named Elias Kavanagh as the consortium's diver.

"You knew," Cyrus said.

Maren set down the coffee she hadn't been drinking anyway. "Cyrus—"

"Don't." He crossed the wheelhouse in three steps and put the letter down on the chart table between them, unrolling it flat, pinning one corner under the brass weight shaped like an anchor that Auntie Bee used for charts. The paper was translucent with age, the ink browned but legible. *Payment confirmed to E. Kavanagh upon verification of charge placement. Balance due upon sailing.* A signature at the bottom that Maren had stared at until two in the morning more than once over the past decade, telling herself it wasn't really his.

"Eleven years I've been reading about the Corvane," Cyrus said. "Every registry, every insurance filing, every rumor in every harbor bar from here to Singapore. And you walk into the Harbour Bar with a contract and a smile and you don't think I'd find out you knew what my captain did?"

"I didn't know what he did." Her voice came out level, which surprised her. She'd expected it to crack. "I knew what the consortium did. There's a difference."

"Is there?" He laughed, and it was an ugly sound, nothing like the dry humor she remembered from certification week in Kota Kinabalu, when they were nineteen and broke and racing each other to the bottom of a training quarry. "Because from where I'm standing, everyone who touched this ship is standing in the same water."

That landed harder than he probably intended. Maren felt it go in below the ribs, exactly where she kept the thing she'd never told him, and for a moment the wheelhouse was very quiet—the tick of the radar sweep, the groan of the hull working against its anchor, Tomas's footsteps crossing the deck above and stopping, deliberately, near the companionway. Listening. Let him listen. It would save her saying it twice.

"Cyrus." She stood up. She was half a head shorter than him and always had been, and she'd learned long ago that the only way to talk to Cyrus Kavanagh when he had his back up was to stop trying to be taller. "Sit down."

"I'm fine standing."

"Then stand there and shut up for ninety seconds, because if you interrupt me I will throw this whole contract over the rail and you can explain to your crew why their payday just went into the trench with the Corvane."

His jaw worked. But he folded his arms and leaned against the doorframe, blocking it, which was so perfectly him that she almost smiled. Almost.

* * *

Here was what Maren Voss knew, and had known since she was twenty-three years old, sitting in a claims office in Port Harrow across from a lawyer who kept using the word *unfortunate*:

The Corvane was not lost. The Corvane was *spent*.

She'd learned it the way most terrible things are learned—by accident, by paperwork, by a clerk who made a copying error and sent her the wrong file. She'd gone to the office to settle her sister's estate, such as it was. Ilse Voss, twenty-six, second-class passage, emigrating to join a fiancé in the eastern territories, one suitcase and a sealed envelope of letters their mother had written and never mailed. Ilse had been the brave one. Ilse had been the one who left first.

The file the clerk handed her contained not a death certificate but an internal memo, dated nine days before the Corvane sailed. Hull survey. Stress fractures in three frames. Repair estimate: forty percent of book value. Recommendation, in a hand that belonged to someone with a corner office: *Total constructive loss preferable. Vessel fully insured. Schedule departure as planned.*

As planned. As though the departure were the point. As though the ship had been scheduled to die the way other ships are scheduled to arrive.

Maren had read the memo four times in the waiting room, then walked out with it inside her coat, heart hammering, certain that any moment a hand would land on her shoulder. No hand ever did. Nobody cared. That was the part that had kept her awake for a decade—not the crime itself but the indifference of it. Forty-one passengers. A crew of twelve. A ship with a broken back pointed at open water because a spreadsheet said drowning her was cheaper than fixing her.

And the manifest sealed. That was the detail the lawyers always came back to, whenever she'd tried, over the years, to find anyone who would listen. Why seal a passenger manifest? Ships sank; manifests were public record; grieving families had rights. Unless the manifest proved something. Unless the names themselves were evidence—who was aboard, how many, in what class, in what state of ticket. Unless someone wanted the count of the dead to stay uncertain forever, so the number could be argued down in courtrooms, whittled from forty-one to thirty to *unconfirmed reports*, until the Corvane became a footnote and the footnote became fog.

So she'd spent eleven years becoming the kind of person who could get to a sealed strongbox at sixty-two meters. Certifications, salvage licenses, a company, a reputation, a fleet of rivals she'd outbid and outfought—and every year the wreck sat deeper in the grey zone, inside waters the navy had quietly fenced off after the war, inside a channel no commercial chart marked as passable.

Which was the problem. Which had always been the problem. She could fund an expedition. She could not navigate one. The approach channel to the Corvane's trench existed on exactly one set of charts in the world, hand-annotated, in the possession of exactly one man.

Elias Kavanagh.

Who had died before she worked up the nerve to ask him. Who had, in the years before his death, refused her calls, returned her letters unopened, and once—she'd never forgotten this—stood up from a table in the Harbour Bar when she approached and walked out without a word, leaving his own drink untouched.

And who had left behind a protégé with his same stubborn jaw and his same methodical hands and, apparently, his same talent for keeping secrets.

* * *

"So yes," she said now, into the silence. "I knew the ship was scuttled. I've known since before you grew your first grey hair. I hired you because you're the best small-crew salvage operator in these waters and because your dead captain drew the only map to the only way in. Those are all true things. Now ask me the question you actually want to ask, Cyrus, because you've been circling it since you walked in and we don't have time for you to circle it politely."

He hadn't moved from the doorframe. But something in his face had shifted during her account—he'd listened, actually listened, the way he did everything, completely—and what remained there now wasn't rage anymore. It was worse. It was the look of a man doing arithmetic.

"The passenger list," he said slowly. "You want the manifest. Not for salvage value. Not for the consortium's ledger."

"No."

"Whose name is on it?"

Maren looked at the porthole. Outside, the light was going the color of old pewter—the monsoon building itself out beyond the reef, day by day, hour by hour, a wall of weather assembling with the patience of something that had never once been in a hurry. Somewhere below all that grey water lay a steel box with her sister's name written inside it, in a clerk's careful hand, filed under *lost with vessel*.

"Ilse," she said. "Ilse Voss. Second-class passage. She was twenty-six." Her voice held. She'd have bet money it wouldn't. "My little sister. She waved from the rail in a photograph I still have. Three days later your captain took consortium money to confirm the charges were set, and the charges were set, and she went down with the ship in water she couldn't swim in, and the company collected on her life twice—once on the hull and once on the cargo, and do you know what category the passengers fell under, Cyrus? *Cargo.* Third schedule. They insured her as freight."

The words came out of her in a flood she hadn't planned, eleven years of them, and she hated every second of it—hated the wet heat behind her eyes, hated the way her hands had closed around the edge of the chart table, hated that Cyrus Kavanagh was seeing this. But she let it come, because she'd learned that too, somewhere in the long ugly middle years: the truth was the only tool that cut through him.

When she could speak again, she said, quieter: "I didn't know about Elias. I want you to believe that, because it's the only clean thing I have left. I knew the ship was murdered. I did not know the man who helped do it was the man whose charts I needed. When I figured out whose crew you were—" she gestured at the letter, at the anchor-shaped weight pinning it down, "—I told myself it didn't matter. That the past was the past and the dead were the dead and I needed that manifest more than I needed a clear conscience. That's the honest answer, Cyrus. I used you. I picked you because grief makes people predictable and yours pointed at this wreck like a compass needle, and I aimed you at it, and I let you find out everything the hard way because I was afraid that if I told you up front you'd do exactly what you're doing right now."

"And what's that?"

"Look at me like I'm the consortium."

He pushed off the doorframe. For one bad moment she thought he was going to leave—or worse, say nothing, which from Cyrus was the leaving. Instead he crossed to the chart table and stood over the letter, reading it again, top to bottom, the way he read everything: twice, completely, committing it.

"My captain took their money," he said. "Ten years I built a shrine to that man. Ten years Devi and Tomas and Bee built their whole lives around his memory, around his rules, around *never speak of the Corvane*, and it turns out the rule wasn't grief. It was a gag order." He tapped the signature. "You knew he was capable of this?"

"I suspected he was involved. There were rumors after the sinking—a diver who survived the war doing contract work, a payment trail that stopped at a shell company. Rumors are cheap. I bought them all and none of them named him. If I'd had proof, I'd have—" She stopped. What would she have done? Gone to the navy? The navy that had drawn the restricted zone? Gone to the press? She'd tried the press, twice, eleven years ago, and watched both stories die in editorial meetings for lack of documents. "I don't know what I'd have done. Probably something stupid and expensive and alone. Which is why I hired a crew instead."

Above them, Tomas's footsteps finally moved away from the companionway. Good. Some of this shouldn't reach the deck yet—though she'd known, signing that contract, that all of it eventually would. Secrets aboard a tug the size of the Perdita lasted about as long as milk in the tropics.

Cyrus straightened. When he turned to face her, the rage was gone, and what replaced it frightened her more than the rage had, because it was recognition. Two people standing in the wreckage of the same lie, looking at each other across it.

"He cut her line," Cyrus said. "Sela. Down in the escape hatch. Or he stayed with her. Devi found the knife beside her, and there's no third story that isn't worse than those two." His voice was steady in the way that cost him visibly. "I spent ten years thinking the sea took my people. Turns out my captain sold the ship and maybe killed my friend, and your sister drowned in the same water, and we both spent a decade mourning men and women who were murdered by a spreadsheet." He exhaled. "You should have told me, Maren. From the first night. You should have put the letter on the bar and said *your captain helped kill my sister* and let me walk out if I was going to walk out."

"Yes," she said. "I should have."

It cost her more than the confession about Ilse had. She watched him take it, weigh it, file it away in whatever ledger he kept behind those grey eyes.

"But you didn't," he said. "And now the fleet's six miles off with a naval warrant and a ROV, and the monsoon's four tides out, and the only reason we're still ahead of them is a channel your sister's killers don't know exists." He rolled the letter carefully, handed it back to her—handed it *back*, and she understood the gesture even if she couldn't have explained it: he was trusting her with the evidence against her own judgment. "So here's how this goes. No more versions of the truth. No more truths arriving in installments timed for maximum leverage. Everything you know, everything your backers know, everything you suspect—on this table, tonight, before Devi finishes decoding the rest of the ledger. Because if there's a second letter in this box with another name on it, Maren, I will not find out from the paper. I'll find out from you. Understood?"

"Understood."

"And when this is over—whatever *over* looks like—you and I are going to finish this conversation somewhere with better lighting."

Despite everything, despite the ache in her chest and the storm stacking itself on the horizon, Maren felt the ghost of a laugh move through her. "Is that a threat or a promise?"

"It's a tide table," Cyrus said, moving to the door. "Everything comes back around eventually. Ask the sea."

He paused at the threshold, one hand on the frame, and didn't turn around.

"For what it's worth," he said. "Ilse Voss. Twenty-six. I'll remember the name. She'll go in the water with Rook and Sela when this is done. All of them together."

Then he was gone, boots ringing down the companionway ladder, and Maren Voss stood alone in the wheelhouse with a letter naming a murderer and a window full of coming storm, and realized her hands were shaking—and that for the first time in eleven years, it wasn't fear shaking them.

On the chart table, the radio crackled. Devi's voice, tight and fast, from the engine room below:

"Maren—get up here. We've got a contact on the sonar. Something's moving down the channel toward us, and it's not the fleet."
