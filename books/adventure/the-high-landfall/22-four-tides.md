## 22. Four Tides

Devi saw it first on the thermal overlay, before anyone else knew what they were looking at: a bloom of heat six miles northeast, where the Halcyon Dawn's deck cranes had come alive in the dark.

"Contact," she said, and the wheelhouse went quiet the way a room goes quiet when someone says a word like *tumor* or *bailiff*. "They're launching something. ROV by the signature—tether spool, A-frame cycling."

Cyrus came to stand behind her chair. She felt his breath on her hair, smelled salt and old coffee. On the screen, the heat bloom resolved into movement: a slow, deliberate crawl across the seabed toward the trench where the Corvane lay at sixty-two meters, holding its dead and its secrets and, as of last night, one empty strongbox cavity that would tell the fleet exactly what kind of divers they were dealing with.

"How fast?" Cyrus asked.

"Two knots over bottom. Call it three hours if they go direct." She ran the numbers again because she wanted them to be wrong. "They'll be on the wreck by oh-three-hundred."

Tomas leaned against the doorframe with a mug he wasn't drinking from. Auntie Bee sat in the corner with Elias's charts spread across her knees like scripture she'd stopped believing in but couldn't put down. Maren stood at the aft windows, arms crossed, watching the Halcyon Dawn's lights glitter on the horizon like a carnival that had come to the wrong town.

"They can't dive it," Maren said. "Not tonight, not with their saturation schedule blown. The ROV is a recon play. They're going to film the hull, confirm the blast damage, and file it as evidence for the restricted-zone seizure."

"And then we're done," Tomas said. "Evidence gets sealed. Lawyers get rich. Wreck gets 'stabilized,' which is navy for bulldozed."

Nobody argued, because he was right. Devi watched the ROV's heat signature crawl another hundred meters and felt the old familiar arithmetic of salvage settle over her—not the romance of it, the arithmetic. Ten years she'd kept this crew alive on arithmetic: gas mixes and bottom times, weather windows and fuel margins, the cold ledger of what a body can survive and what it can't. And every calculation she'd ever run ended the same way this one did.

*We lose.*

Unless.

She pulled up the sonar feed again, then the chart of the reef shelf, then—almost without deciding to—the schematic of the Perdita's comms suite, the one she'd rebuilt twice and hot-rodded once, back when hot-rodding it had seemed like a clever thing to do rather than a confession waiting for a courtroom.

"Cyrus," she said. "Come look at this."

---

He looked at it. Then he looked at her for a long moment, and she watched him arrive at the same place she'd arrived, watch him flinch away from it, watch him come back.

"No," he said.

"You haven't heard it yet."

"I don't need to hear it. I can see your face." He rubbed his jaw. "Devi."

"Their ROV is telemetry-controlled. Fiber tether for data but the control loop runs through an acoustic modem link for redundancy—that's standard fleet kit, I've seen the spec sheets. Acoustic link means acoustic frequency. Acoustic frequency means—" She turned the screen toward him. "—I can talk to it."

"That's not talking. That's jamming."

"It's better than jamming. Jamming is noise. Noise is traceable, directional, and stupid. What I'm proposing is a spoof. I flood their link with a false return—a second wreck signature, offset two miles southwest into open sand. Their operator sees two contacts. His software flags ambiguity. He has to re-survey, re-verify, burn hours doing sanity checks while his ROV circles a hole in nothing." She heard her own voice going fast and bright, the way it did when the engineering was good and the ethics were bad, and she made herself slow down. "Or I don't spoof anything. I just degrade the link enough that his video stutters and his telemetry drops frames. He thinks it's thermocline interference. Monsoon water does terrible things to acoustic propagation. Nobody will ever prove it was us."

"Except you just proved it was us, right now, out loud, in front of four witnesses."

"Three witnesses and a woman who isn't crew," Devi said, and didn't look at Maren when she said it, which was its own kind of message.

Maren took it without blinking. "For what it's worth, I've seen Devi's comms work. If she says nobody will prove it, nobody will prove it."

"That's supposed to make me feel better?"

"It's supposed to make you feel *informed*."

Cyrus turned back to the screen. The ROV crawled. Three hours. Devi watched him do the same arithmetic she'd done, watched it come out the same way, watched him hate it.

"There has to be another option," he said.

"There is. We run. Four tides until the monsoon makes landfall and after that nobody dives anything for weeks. We take the partial manifest and the letter, we disappear into the archipelago, we sell what we have to whoever pays, and we spend the rest of our lives being careful." She paused. "That's option one. Option two is we let their ROV document the wreck, the seizure goes through, and everything Sela died carrying stays in a naval evidence locker until the people who paid for it die of old age."

"Sela didn't carry anything. Sela died in a hatch."

"You know what I mean."

He did know. That was the problem. Devi had spent ten years not saying certain things aloud—about Rook, about Sela, about the night Captain Elias Kavanagh came up alone and wouldn't tell them why—and now the words were queuing up behind her teeth like they'd been waiting for exactly this moment, exactly this cowardice of his, to break loose.

"We're grey operators," Cyrus said quietly. "That's been the whole trick. Grey doesn't attract navies. Grey survives."

"Grey survived," Devi said. "Look at the horizon, Cyrus. The grey era's over. They sent a fleet."

---

Auntie Bee spoke from her corner, and the room rearranged itself around her voice the way it always did, though she'd never once raised it.

"The lieutenant who boarded us," she said. "The polite one, with the tea habit. You remember what his paperwork said? Restricted zone, established under emergency fisheries ordinance. Not salvage law. Fisheries." She folded her glasses. "You know why that matters? Because a fisheries ordinance expires when the monsoon advisory lifts. Forty-eight hours, maybe less. After that they need a new warrant, and new warrants need judges, and judges need evidence, and evidence needs—" she nodded at the crawling heat signature on Devi's screen, "—that. That little machine down there in the dark is the whole legal architecture of everything they're doing to us. Take away its eyes and the architecture stands on air."

"You've thought about this," Tomas said.

"I've thought about everything since the day Elias died," Auntie Bee said. "I just haven't said most of it."

The silence that followed had ten years inside it. Devi let it sit, because some silences are load-bearing.

Then Cyrus said, "If we do this—if—if it goes wrong, what's the worst case? Actually walk me through it. No optimism. Numbers."

Devi gave him numbers, because that was the language they'd always spoken to each other when the truth got too heavy for words.

"Worst case: they detect the interference, triangulate it, and it points straight at us. Interference with licensed maritime communications in a declared restricted zone during an active naval operation. That's not a fine. That's a boarding, an arrest, and a trial in a jurisdiction where the consortium owns two of the three newspapers and probably the judge. We lose the boat. We lose the manifest. We possibly lose years of our lives." She met his eyes. "And that's if they're in a lawful mood."

"And the best case?"

"Best case, their ROV spends tonight filming a sandbank, their operator files a report full of thermocline excuses, and we get four tides to finish what we started."

"And the middle case? There's always a middle case."

"The middle case is they suspect but can't prove, and it changes nothing except that now they know someone skilled is down here. Which they already know, because somebody opened their strongbox with a cutting charge laid clean enough to make their own techs weep." She shrugged. "Middle case is today, continued."

Cyrus laughed—one short, unhappy syllable—and Devi realized she hadn't heard him laugh in days and hated how much better it made her feel anyway.

"Tomas," he said. "You're the vote that matters. You were the one who said we should've stayed home."

Tomas set his untouched mug on the chart table. He looked younger than his thirty-one years when he was scared, and older than his thirty-one years when he decided something, and right now Devi watched both faces cross his like weather fronts.

"I was the one who said we should've stayed home," he agreed. "And then I went down there. I put my hands on that hull. I felt the hole punched inward." He shook his head slowly. "I can't unknow it, Cap. Somebody blew that ship with people aboard and paid our captain to keep quiet about it, and now a floating city full of lawyers wants to film the proof so they can lock it in a drawer forever. I'm done being careful. Careful is how they win."

"Auntie Bee?"

"I've been an outlaw since I started cooking for this crew," she said. "The paperwork was always decorative. Yes."

Four faces turned to Maren. She unfolded her arms.

"I launched this expedition," she said. "My backers funded it. If you're asking whether I'll report the jamming plan to them—" something moved behind her eyes, quick and cold, "—my backers sold my sister's death certificate to an insurance adjuster twenty years before I understood what I was reading. They can read about this plan in the newspaper like everyone else." She paused. "Yes. Do it."

Devi exhaled. It sounded steadier than it felt.

"Then somebody get me the transducer couplant from the forward locker," she said, "because I've got about ninety minutes of calibration work and a crime to commit."

---

She worked in the comms bay with the door shut, though shut doors on the Perdita were a politeness, not a barrier—everyone could hear her muttering, everyone could smell the flux paste, and within the hour Tomas appeared at her elbow holding coffee and saying nothing, which was his way of standing guard.

The physics were simple; that was the obscenity of it. Sound traveled through seawater at roughly fifteen hundred meters per second, and every acoustic modem on earth was built on the assumption that the ocean was mostly honest—that a signal arriving was a signal sent, degraded by distance and noise but fundamentally itself. All Devi had to do was lie convincingly in a medium built for listening.

She'd cannibalized the Perdita's fish-finder transducer, wired it to a signal generator she'd built from a spare autopilot board and parts Tomas had "liberated" from a chandler in Tanah Merah who owed Auntie Bee a favor involving a poker debt and a photograph. The generator could synthesize returns across the band the fleet's modems used—she'd pulled the frequencies from a maintenance manual Maren's liaison officer had left in a photocopier, a carelessness so convenient it still made her itch—but convenience was a problem for tomorrow. Tonight it was ammunition.

"Talk to me about the geometry," she said, half to Tomas, half to herself.

"Reef shelf sits between us and the ROV's position, call it two miles off their bearing line," Tomas recited. He'd memorized her briefing faster than she'd delivered it, the way he did. "Our signal bounces off the shelf face and arrives at their hydrophone from the south. They triangulate, they get the reef. Reef reflects sound all day. It's practically a confession-proof alibi."

"It's practically a confession-proof alibi," she agreed. "Say it again slower, I want to enjoy it."

"You're enjoying this too much."

"I'm terrified," she said. "This is what terrified looks like when it's been awake for thirty hours."

His hand hovered near her shoulder—she saw it in her peripheral vision, hovering, retreating, hovering again—and finally landed, brief and warm and gone, like a bird deciding against landing and landing anyway. Neither of them mentioned it. They never did. Some bonds hold better unnamed; naming them puts weight on them.

"Status," Cyrus's voice crackled on the intercom. "Time check."

"Nineteen minutes to intercept," Devi called back. "Generator's warm. Signal path's mapped. I'm running the final gain staging now." She keyed the transmit test—into a dummy load, into the bench, nowhere near the water—and watched the waveform come back clean. "One thing left."

"Which is?"

"Somebody has to flip the switch."

Silence on the intercom. Then: "It's your rig, Devi. Your call when to fire."

"My call," she repeated, and heard what he was actually saying underneath it: whatever happens, this lands on me, not on you. Old captain's habit. Elias used to do the exact same thing, distribute the guilt downward so it pooled thinnest on top. She wondered if Cyrus knew he'd learned it. She wondered if he knew where Elias had learned it.

Probably from the consortium.

She pushed the thought away and worked.

---

At oh-two-forty-one, the Halcyon Dawn's ROV crossed the two-mile mark and began its descent toward the Corvane.

Devi watched it on the thermal feed, a small cold spark sinking into a black field, trailing its umbilical like a kite string cut loose from heaven. Somewhere above it, in a climate-controlled operations room, a technician with a lanyard and a mortgage was drinking vending-machine coffee and guiding a nine-hundred-thousand-dollar machine toward the grave of two hundred people, on instructions from people who would never say the number two hundred out loud.

She thought about Sela. She thought about the hatch. She thought about the letter in the strongbox with Elias's name in it, the name written in a stranger's hand, *the diver retained to confirm emplacement*, and how she'd read it four times hoping the words would rearrange themselves into something else and they never did.

Elias had taken the money. That part was true, and no amount of love could launder it. But Elias had also mapped that approach channel in secret, year after year, annotations in pencil in a hand that shook slightly on the last entries—as if he'd known, all along, that someday someone would need to reach that wreck and that it would have to be reached by skill instead of permission. A man can be bought and still leave a map to the scene of his crime. Maybe that was the apology. Maybe it was the only kind he was capable of making.

"Devi." Tomas, quiet. "They're at forty meters."

She keyed the generator live. The dummy load showed a perfect synthetic return: a hard-bottom contact, displacement-consistent, sitting fat and confident on the band. Two miles southwest. Open sand. A ghost.

"On your mark," she said to the room, to the crew, to whatever version of herself existed on the far side of the next thirty seconds.

"Do it," Cyrus said.

She flipped the switch.

The transducer hummed beneath the hull, below the threshold of hearing, a lie told at fifteen hundred meters per second. On her screen, nothing visible changed—the ocean kept its face blank, the way it always did. But six miles away, in a bright operations room, an acoustic modem was receiving a second contact where moments ago there had been one, and a technician was frowning, and a supervisor was being woken up, and a chain of increasingly expensive people was about to spend the rest of the night arguing with the sea about what was real.

"Oh," Tomas said softly, watching her monitor mirror-feed the fleet's command channel, which Maren's liaison had—through channels he declined to explain—made briefly audible on their receiver. "Oh, they bought it. Look at that. Contact ambiguity, requesting re-verification. They're *re-verifying*."

"Thermocline interference flagged in the log," Devi read aloud, savoring each word like a sweet she'd stolen. "'Possible multi-path return off reef structure.' Off the reef. Exactly where we aimed it." She leaned back and let out a breath that shook only slightly. "Gentlemen, ladies—the United Meridian Salvage Group is currently conducting a survey of an empty sandbank."

Cheers broke out in the wheelhouse—real ones, Tomas whooping, even Auntie Bee allowing herself a single dignified clap—and Devi laughed along, high and giddy, riding the adrenaline spike, letting it carry her.

It lasted eleven minutes.

Because at oh-two-fifty-six, the fleet's command channel went quiet, then came back different: a new voice, clipped, unhurried, senior. And the order that came across it had none of the confusion they'd manufactured. Just a course correction, calm as a scalpel.

"*All stations, Halcyon actual. Disregard contact ambiguity, maintain original track. Navigation confirms primary contact. Someone is playing games on the acoustic band. Note it, log it, find it later.*"

The wheelhouse silence afterward was total.

"They know," Maren said.

"They suspect," Devi corrected, her mouth dry. "They can't prove it. The return came off the reef, the reef eats evidence, and by sunrise the monsoon will eat everything else. But—" she swallowed. "But they know someone's here. Someone skilled. And now they're not going to leave until they've found out who."

Cyrus stood looking at the horizon where the Halcyon Dawn's lights burned steady and patient, a city that had decided to stay. When he spoke, his voice was flat and quiet and more frightening than shouting would have been.

"Then we've got four tides," he said. "And after that, they stop sending machines."
