## 23. Jamming the Ghost

Tomas Reyes had never liked machines that thought they were smarter than him.

The ROV on the Halcyon Dawn's aft deck was exactly that kind of machine—a yellow-bodied Saab Seaeye with thrusters like insect legs and a sonar pod that could paint a wreck at two hundred meters through silt. He'd watched it come off the fleet ship's crane an hour ago, watched it slip under the swell trailing its umbilical like a gutted eel, and now it was down there, somewhere in the dark above the Corvane, thinking its way toward their secret.

"You're staring," Devi said.

"I'm assessing."

"From forty feet away, with binoculars, at night."

"That's how assessment works."

She didn't laugh. Neither of them did, not tonight. The Perdita sat dark in a fold of reef shelf six miles from the trench, running no lights but the green glow of Devi's workstation, and the whole crew was crowded into the wheelhouse like people around a sickbed.

Devi had laid it out for them an hour before that: the ROV broadcast everything—sonar returns, video, telemetry, position—back up to the Halcyon Dawn on a spread-spectrum link, encrypted to hell and back. They couldn't crack the encryption. But encryption only protected the message. It didn't protect the conversation.

"We can't make it lie to them about what it sees," she'd said, tapping the screen where the ROV's signal pulsed like a heartbeat. "But if we're loud enough, we can make it deaf. And then we tell it what it heard."

Now she was building the lie in real time, three windows open on her rig: one listening to the ROV's uplink bursts, one generating a false acoustic environment, one running the replay. Auntie Bee stood behind her with tea nobody drank. Maren Voss leaned against the chart table with her arms crossed, watching Devi work with an expression Tomas couldn't read—half hunger, half dread.

Cyrus wasn't in the wheelhouse. Cyrus was in the radio shack.

* * *

The plan, such as it was, had three moving parts, and Tomas hated that he was one of them.

Part one: Devi jammed. Not crude static—that would get flagged in seconds—but targeted noise, timed to the ROV's own ping cycles, drowning its sonar returns in echoes bounced off the reef wall. The ROV would see a seafloor made of ghosts. It would report confusion. Confusion was survivable; confusion bought hours.

Part two: Devi spoofed. Once the real returns were buried, she fed the ROV's processing stack a synthetic contact—a wreck signature assembled from survey data she'd pulled from a decommissioned ferry sinking eighty miles north, massaged until it matched the Corvane's dimensions, sitting in the wrong place by four hundred meters, in water too shallow to be right. If the fleet's analysts bit, they'd spend the night repositioning over sand and rubble.

Part three: Cyrus talked. While the ROV drowned in lies, Cyrus would be on the radio, being exactly who he'd been for ten years—a small-time salvager with bad manners and worse equipment, complaining about weather, asking the fleet ship to stop shining searchlights near his anchorage, giving them a face to attach to the annoyance so that when something went wrong tonight, they'd look at the loudmouth in the tug and not at the silence underneath.

"It won't hold," Devi had said flatly, when she finished explaining. "An hour, maybe two. Their sonar techs aren't stupid. When they figure out the geometry doesn't close, they'll know someone fed them a ghost."

"Two hours is enough?" Maren asked.

"It has to be." Cyrus had said it quietly, and everyone had understood what he meant: the manifest was still in the ballast tank, but the ledger—the ledger was in the strongroom, sixty-two meters down, and Maren hadn't gotten it yet. Two hours was the difference between finished and unfinished.

Tomas had looked around the wheelhouse—at Devi's jaw set like a bulkhead fitting, at Auntie Bee's hands folded, at Maren's fear she thought she was hiding—and he'd realized there was no version of this where he said no.

So here he was, crouched in the stern lazarette with a soldering iron and Devi's hydrophone array, splicing cable in the dark while the sea tried to shake his hands apart.

* * *

"Tomas. Talk to me."

Her voice came through the dive mask comm he wore pushed up on his forehead, tinny against the slap of water in the lazarette. Sweat ran into his eyes. The splice in front of him was the ugliest thing he'd ever made and it had to carry a signal clean enough to fool a military-grade receiver, which meant ugly wouldn't do.

"Third conductor's got corrosion under the jacket. I'm re-running it."

"How long?"

"Asking makes it longer."

A pause. Then, softer: "I know. Sorry."

He stripped the wire with his teeth because the strippers were somewhere in the bilge where he'd thrown them during the squall, twisted the conductors together, flowed solder until the joint shone silver, and heat-shrunk it with a match he had to shield from the wind coming through the hull seam. His fingers burned. He didn't notice much anymore.

"Array's live," he said. "Testing."

Above him, through three decks, he heard Devi's voice relayed on the comm: "Receiving. Signal strength ninety-one percent. Tomas Reyes, you beautiful idiot, that's better than spec."

"Tell the spec I said hi." He gathered the cables, coiled them, and started the climb back up into a world where the air moved and people existed.

* * *

At 0140, the ROV began its first systematic sonar sweep of the trench, and Devi went to war.

Tomas stood behind her chair and watched her hands move—not fast, exactly, but certain, the way Elias used to tie off a line, no wasted motion because wasted motion was how people died. On her main screen, the ROV's real sonar picture flickered: a smear of hard return at the bottom of the trench, the unmistakable profile of a large hull lying over on its beam ends. The Corvane, plain as a fingerprint.

"Contact acquired," Devi murmured, mimicking the flat tone of the fleet's own systems. "And—gone."

She keyed the jamming sequence. The reef wall, primed all day by Tomas's hydrophone array, threw back a cascade of false echoes timed inside the ROV's processing window. On the screen, the clean wreck signature dissolved into a blizzard of returns—reef, rubble, fish schools, noise, noise, noise. Somewhere six miles away, a machine worth more than the Perdita twice over stared into static and blinked.

"Uplink burst," Auntie Bee said, reading the log. "They're asking it to re-acquire."

"It'll re-acquire," Devi said. "Onto my wreck."

She launched the spoof. The synthetic contact slid into the ROV's acoustic field like a knife into soft wood—a hull-shaped echo, 140 meters long, resting at 31 meters on a sand shelf northeast of the true site. She'd built it from the ferry data over two days of no sleep, and watching it appear on the feed, Tomas felt the hairs rise on his arms. It looked real. It sounded real. It had a shadow.

On the repeater showing the Halcyon Dawn's traffic, nothing happened for ninety seconds. A minute and a half of the whole ocean holding its breath.

Then the fleet ship's deck lights changed pattern—crane traversing, floodlights swinging to a new heading—and her engines came up out of idle.

"They took it," Maren breathed.

"They took it," Devi confirmed, and let out a breath that shook at the end, and Tomas saw her hands tremble once she lifted them off the keyboard, and he understood that the trembling had been waiting the whole time.

* * *

Cyrus earned his keep at 0215.

The Halcyon Dawn, repositioning toward the ghost wreck, swung wide across the Perdita's anchorage, and her bridge called channel sixteen with the crisp irritation of money inconvenienced: "Unidentified vessel on the reef shelf, this is salvage vessel Halcyon Dawn, you are inside a designated restricted operations zone, please state your intentions."

Tomas listened from the wheelhouse doorway as Cyrus picked up the handset. He'd put on his radio voice—that particular drawl he saved for customs men and harbormasters, bored and slightly aggrieved, a man with nowhere to be and no reason to hurry.

"Perdita. We're anchored. Anchoring is an intention, last I checked."

"You are advised that commercial diving and salvage activity within the zone requires a permit from—"

"We got the same notice you sent everybody, friend. Read it twice. Notice says restricted zone, notice don't say nobody's allowed to be in it. We're riding out weather on the lee side of the shelf. You want to file a complaint with the monsoon, be my guest."

There was a pause on the other end, the sound of a watch officer deciding how much he cared. "Perdita, maintain your position, monitor this channel, and be advised that boarding inspections may occur."

"Now that," Cyrus said, "sounds like a threat wrapped in a courtesy. Y'all have a good night."

He set the handset down. In the wheelhouse, nobody spoke. Auntie Bee poured tea into five cups as if the ritual itself were ballast.

"They've got our name now," Tomas said.

"They've had our name since the harbor bar," Cyrus said. "Tonight they just got my voice to go with it. That's fine. Voices are cheap. Let them listen to me complain about the wind while you—" he nodded at Devi, "—keep their robot dreaming."

* * *

It worked for fifty-eight minutes.

Tomas counted, because counting was the only thing keeping the adrenaline from shaking him apart. Fifty-eight minutes of the Halcyon Dawn hovering over empty sand, lowering lights, running her own hull sonar against a wreck that did not exist. Fifty-eight minutes of Devi feeding corrections to the ghost every time the ROV drifted, each synthetic return placed with surgical patience. Fifty-eight minutes in which Maren Voss stood at the wheelhouse window with her back straight as a mast and Tomas caught himself, twice, almost admiring her.

At 0238, the ROV's umbilical winch stopped paying out. On the traffic feed, the fleet ship went quiet—not the quiet of satisfaction but the quiet of a room where someone has just done arithmetic.

"Uh oh," Auntie Bee said.

"The geometry," Devi whispered. Her eyes moved across her screens, reading their faces the way you'd read weather. "They're comparing the ghost against their own fathometer. Sand shelf at thirty-one meters, but their charts show the shelf edge at twenty-eight. There's no room for a hundred-and-forty-meter hull. Somebody's going to notice there's no room for—"

"There it is."

The Halcyon Dawn's bridge came back on channel sixteen, and the voice was different now. Not the watch officer. Someone older, unhurried, with the particular calm of a man who had already decided what he believed.

"Perdita, Halcyon Dawn. Interesting weather we're having. Say, would your engineer happen to be on frequency? We've been having trouble with our equipment tonight—sonar returns that don't add up. Very strange interference patterns. Almost like somebody down there knows our gear better than we do."

Tomas watched Cyrus pick up the handset. Watched him consider. Ten years of grey-zone survival lived in the two seconds Cyrus took to answer, and Tomas knew—he knew the way you know a wave is going to break on you—that whatever charm was left in this night had just run out.

"Cyrus Kavanagh," Cyrus said. "And I'd tell you our engineer's asleep, but you'd check the tide tables and know I was lying. So: good evening. Whatever you think we did, we're a tug with a broken depth sounder and a crew that wants to go home."

"Mm," said the voice. "Here's my problem, Mr. Kavanagh. Spoofing a survey-grade ROV takes a hydrophone array, signal-processing horsepower, and a person who thinks in decibels. That's not a tug's toolkit. That's a professional's. Which means either you've been hiding a professional for ten years—and maybe you have—or someone put you up to this. Either way, I'm no longer curious about your depth sounder."

A pause, long enough for the rain to start ticking on the wheelhouse roof.

"I'm curious about what's down there that was worth the effort. Good night, Perdita. We'll talk again soon, and next time it won't be on the radio."

The channel clicked dead.

* * *

Nobody moved for a moment. Rain thickened against the glass, and out beyond the shelf the Halcyon Dawn's floodlights swung back toward the true coordinates with the slow, terrible certainty of a head turning.

"Well," Auntie Bee said into the silence, "that's that, then."

"They know," Tomas said. His voice came out flatter than he intended. "Not what—they know *how*. They know someone skilled is down here. That's not a warning shot, that's—"

"That's a name on a list," Devi finished. She pulled off her headset and rubbed her eyes with the heels of her hands, and for a second she looked ten years older and twenty pounds lighter, emptied out. "We bought the night. We spent it. Now we find out the exchange rate."

Maren turned from the window. In the green instrument light her face had gone sharp and pale, and when she spoke, the charm was gone entirely, and what was underneath was worse: urgency with nothing left over it.

"They'll launch a boarding team at first light. Maybe sooner—they don't need the restricted-zone pretext to be polite about it anymore. Which means the second descent happens now. Tonight. Before they re-task the ROV onto the real wreck and put cameras on the strongroom." She looked at Cyrus, and her voice dropped to something raw. "Cyrus. The gas is mixed. The stage bottles are rigged. I'm certified on this exact rig and you know it. Give me one hour of dark and I'll bring up the ledger myself if you won't come."

Cyrus stood very still, looking at the rain on the glass, at the floodlights crawling back across the water, at the place below the black surface where Sela had died and the truth had waited ten years in a flooded room. Tomas watched his captain the way he'd watch a man deciding whether to jump or climb down.

"We dive at 0300," Cyrus said finally. "Both of us. And Devi—" his voice roughened, "—if we're not back on the tag line in ninety minutes, you take the Perdita and you run. You don't wait. You don't argue. Swear it."

Devi looked at him for a long moment, and Tomas saw her decide not to fight, which frightened him more than the fleet ship ever could.

"I swear," she said.

Outside, the rain hardened into sheets, and the first far-off grumble of thunder rolled up out of the southeast—out of the direction, Tomas couldn't help noticing, where the monsoon lived.
