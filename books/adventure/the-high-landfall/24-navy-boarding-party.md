## 24. Navy Boarding Party

Auntie Bee heard the engines before anyone else did, because Auntie Bee heard everything. Twenty-two years of standing a night watch with her ear cocked toward the water had taught her the difference between a fishing boat's lazy diesel cough and the flat, disciplined thrum of a patrol launch running at speed, and what she heard at 0340 was the second kind, and it was not slowing down.

She put down the knife she'd been using to slice ginger, wiped her hands on her apron, and climbed the wheelhouse ladder without hurrying. Hurrying was a tell. She'd learned that in another life, in another country, watching men in uniform come down a street and reading in the set of their shoulders whether they'd come to talk or to take.

Cyrus was already at the glass, binoculars up. "One launch," he said. "Running dark until now. Lights just came on."

"Then they wanted us to see them coming," Bee said. "That's courtesy. Courtesy is worse than surprise."

Devi appeared from the engine trunk with a wrench still in her hand, hair tied back, grease to her elbows. Tomas came up the aft ladder two rungs at a time. Nobody said the obvious thing, which was that the Halcyon Dawn had been sitting six miles off for two days, and that a restricted-zone order was a door that swung both ways, and that they had been standing on the wrong side of it since the moment Devi jammed a military ROV into believing a rock was a wreck.

"Manifest?" Cyrus asked, and his voice was level, and Bee loved him for it and feared for him because of it.

"Ballast tank, port side, sealed in the dry bag with the desiccant," Devi said. "I did it an hour after the jam. I figured—" She stopped.

"You figured right," Cyrus said.

Bee watched him for a moment. There was a thing about Cyrus Kavanagh that people who hadn't sailed with him for a decade missed: he decided fast and then he stopped deciding, and the space where the deciding had been filled up with a stillness that looked like calm and was actually something older and harder. Elias had had it too. Bee had spent ten years telling herself that was a comfort, the resemblance. Lately it was not.

"They'll board," she said. "So we will be boarded properly. Tomas—deck wash, now. I want this boat so clean it insults them. Devi, engine log, fuel log, crew list, the salvage permit, the survey contract, all of it in the folder, the good folder. Cyrus—" she caught his eye, and held it, "—you will go below and you will not come up. You are the one they'd recognize from the harbour bar, and the one whose face does the thing your face does."

"What thing does my face do?"

"It says *ask me again and see what happens*," Bee said. "Below. Now."

He went. He was smart enough to know when the argument was already lost, which was the only kind of smart that had kept this crew alive for ten years.

* * *

The launch came alongside at 0410 with a fendering drill so clean it barely kissed the hull, and that told Bee something too: a coxswain who'd done this a hundred times, which meant a boat that did this a hundred times, which meant the restricted zone was not a line on a chart to these people but a habit.

The lieutenant who came up the ladder was young—younger than Tomas, maybe, though he carried himself older, the way boys do when the navy has spent three years sanding the boy off them. Two petty officers behind him, one at the rail, one by the ladder, both with sidearms and the particular blankness of men trying to look like they were not looking at anything.

"Good morning," Bee said, before he could speak. "You've caught us at breakfast. You'll take tea."

"Lieutenant Arun Prasad, Malaysian Maritime Enforcement—"

"Malaysian registry, yes, I saw your ensign, and a fine one it is." She was already moving, already pouring, because the first rule of hospitality as a weapon was that you never let the guest establish the shape of the visit. "Sit, sit. Tomas, another cup. The lieutenant will want the strong one; men who board boats at four in the morning always want the strong one."

"I didn't—"

"Ginger and lemongrass. It's good for the nerves." She set the cup in his hands and folded herself onto the bench across from him with her hands in her lap, a small round woman of sixty-one in a flowered housecoat, and watched him decide, visibly, over the space of three seconds, that she was nobody.

Men had been deciding she was nobody her whole life. It was the most reliable thing about her.

"Madam, we're conducting an inspection under the restricted-zone notice issued—"

"Two days ago, by the fleet office, on behalf of the Halcyon Dawn's salvage claim. Yes. Devi has the notice, the date, and the reference number. She's very thorough. She keeps us honest." Bee smiled. "You'll want the paperwork first, I expect. You look like a paperwork man."

"I look like—"

"You look like your mother taught you to check the receipts," Bee said, and something flickered across his face—amusement, quickly strangled—that told her she'd guessed right. "Good. I like a paperwork man. The last lieutenant who boarded us, two years ago off Pulau Tinggi, wanted to see everything with his own hands. Took four hours. We missed our tide. I made him lunch anyway, because that is who I am, but I'll be honest with you, Lieutenant: I liked you better in the first thirty seconds."

The petty officer by the rail made a sound that might have been a cough.

* * *

Devi brought the folder, and it was beautiful, and Bee watched the lieutenant understand that it was beautiful, that it had been built for exactly this morning, that the salvage permit was current and the survey contract was signed and stamped and the crew list matched the passports and the safety equipment inventory matched the safety equipment, item by item, flares by flare, because Devi had spent the previous evening checking flares like a woman defusing bombs.

"Independent salvage survey, contracted through Voss Maritime," the lieutenant read. "Target: unlisted."

"Unlisted means unlisted," Bee said pleasantly. "You can't list a wreck you haven't found. That's the whole business. We look where the charts are rude to us and mostly we find rocks."

"Your sonar logs will show your search area."

"Of course. Devi?"

Devi handed over the tablet without being asked twice, and Bee watched the lieutenant scroll, and watched him stop scrolling, and knew exactly what he was seeing: a week of sonar tracks that wandered the reef field in a pattern that was methodical and honest and pointed nowhere near the trench, because Devi had rebuilt the logs at two in the morning with the same care she gave her engines, and because the Perdita's actual search pattern, the one Elias's charts had dictated, existed nowhere but in the dead man's handwriting and in Cyrus's head.

"Your tracks don't approach the restricted datum," the lieutenant said.

"Should they?" Bee asked, sipping her own tea. "It's restricted."

The silence that followed had a shape, and the shape was the lieutenant understanding that he had been outmaneuvered by a housecoat, and not enjoying it.

"Madam, with respect. We've had a report of signal interference in this area. Deliberate interference with a commercial ROV operation."

"Interference," Bee said. "How terrible. The sea is full of interference. Thermoclines, monsoon static, cheap Chinese radios on the fishing fleet. Half this coast runs on interference." She refilled his cup. "But you should investigate it properly, of course. Where was the interference reported?"

He told her the coordinates. She listened with her head tilted, a woman hearing about a neighbor's roof leak, and then she said, "That's four miles north of here. We've been on anchor here for two days. You can check the anchor drag circle in the log; Devi records it every watch, because she records everything, God help us all." She patted his hand, once, briefly, the way you'd pat a nephew. "Someone is playing games with your fleet, Lieutenant. I'd wonder who benefits."

It was a risk and she knew it. Plant the suggestion and pray it grew. But she had watched this lieutenant's face while he read the folder, and what she'd seen was a tired young man on a launch at four in the morning doing a favor for a salvage company he did not respect, and tired young men doing favors for companies they do not respect are the softest target on any ocean.

* * *

They searched anyway. Of course they searched. The lieutenant had a job and a warrant and two petty officers, and Bee had tea, and tea only goes so far.

She made it go far.

"Forward cabin, please," the lieutenant said, and Bee walked ahead of them, unhurried, narrating. "Bunks. Tomas sleeps there, he snores like a landslide, you'll forgive the state of it. Head. Cyrus's cabin—my grandnephew, third crew member, he's below, he's been on watch, he's asleep, knock gently, he's short-tempered before coffee." Which was true, and which bought Cyrus thirty seconds to pull a blanket over himself and look like a man rudely woken rather than a man who had spent the last hour listening at the deckhead.

The engine room took longest. Devi stood aside with her arms crossed while the petty officer ran his light along the frames, and Bee watched him pause at the port ballast tank—the port ballast tank, of all the tanks on this boat, because Devi had chosen it with a technician's cruelty, wedged behind the fuel polishing rig where a stranger's flashlight would catch nothing but grease and shadow—and she felt the fear then, finally, the real thing, the one she'd been holding at arm's length all morning. It came up through her sternum like cold water.

Because she knew what was in that tank. Not the manifest itself—she'd refused to know the details; a crew survives on need-to-know and she had decided long ago that what she needed to know was that Cyrus and Devi trusted each other, and that was the whole of it. But she knew the weight of what they'd pulled off that wreck, and she knew what the Halcyon Dawn's owners would do to a small brown woman on a small grey boat if the wrong flashlight swung the wrong way, and for one long moment, while the petty officer's light crawled along the frame, Bee stood in her galley doorway with her hands folded and performed calm the way other women perform hymns—word-perfect, note by note, from memory, with nothing underneath.

"Fuel polishing," Devi said. "You want to see it run?"

The petty officer looked at her. Running the rig meant noise, meant time, meant a man crouched in an engine room while a woman who clearly owned every wrench on this boat watched him with an expression that dared him.

"Negative," he said, and moved on, and Bee's knees forgave her.

* * *

By six the light had gone from black to the bruised grey that passes for dawn under monsoon cloud, and the lieutenant stood in the wheelhouse with his cap under his arm and his folder closed, and Bee poured the last of the tea because a guest does not leave a hostess's boat with an empty cup, that was simply law.

"You understand we may be back," he said.

"I understand you may be back," Bee said. "You'll be welcome. I'll make better tea. This batch suffered; I was nervous." She said it lightly, and watched him decide not to ask why a cook on a survey tug would be nervous about a routine boarding, and watched him decide, also, to look at her—really look, for the first time, past the housecoat and the teapot—and what she saw in his face was not suspicion. It was recognition. The look of a young man who has spent his career boarding boats for other people's reasons and has just, this morning, in the smallest possible way, noticed it.

"Madam," he said, at the rail, "your paperwork is the cleanest I've seen on this coast."

"I'll tell Devi. She'll be unbearable for a week."

He went down the ladder. The launch pulled away, running dark again until it was a mile out, and Bee stood at the rail with her cup until the sound of it was gone.

Then she set the cup down very carefully, went to the head, and was quietly, thoroughly sick.

* * *

Cyrus found her twenty minutes later on the aft deck, watching the water. He didn't say anything. He just stood next to her, the way Elias used to, and after a while he said, "The ballast tank."

"Held," Bee said.

"The logs."

"Held."

"The lieutenant?"

Bee considered. "Held," she said. "And maybe more than held. There was a moment, Cyrus, when I thought he might ask me a question he already knew the answer to. And then he didn't. And I don't think it was because of my tea."

Cyrus was quiet. Out past the reef the Halcyon Dawn sat on the horizon like a fact, its deck cranes black against the grey.

"Three tides," he said finally. "Devi's figure. After that the window on the strongroom closes and the storm owns the reef. Maren wants a second descent tonight—her and me, past the point of no return on the gas plan. I told her I'd decide by noon."

"And what are you deciding?"

Bee didn't look at him. She looked at the water, at the long grey swell coming out of the southeast like something being pushed, and she thought about a sealed locker and a logbook and a sentence that ended mid-word, and she thought about a young lieutenant who had looked at her this morning like a man waking up.

"I'm deciding that we did not feed that boy ginger tea and lie to his face so that we could stop one dive short of the truth," she said. "Whatever the truth costs."

She pushed off the rail and went to make breakfast, because the crew would need feeding, and because her hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking, and because in the galley, in the drawer under the spice rack, there was a satellite phone that had been quietly texting a journalist named Priya Nair since the night they'd read the old charts—and this morning, for the first time, Bee had sent a message that said not *soon* but *now*.

The monsoon was coming. The truth would need a boat of its own.

She put the rice on, and waited for the tide to turn.
