## 25. Three Tides

The fire started at 0340, which told Cyrus everything he needed to know about how well Devi had planned it.

He stood at the Perdita's rail and watched the Halcyon Dawn's aft deck glow orange six miles off, smoke boiling up in fat black columns that the pre-dawn wind shredded and smeared across the water. Even from this distance he could hear the klaxons, thin and electronic, carrying flat over the swells. A generator fire, the fleet's radio traffic said. Contained to the engineering spaces. No casualties.

Devi had written that last part herself, three days ago, on the back of a dive-table card: *Generator fire, contained, no casualties. Nobody dies. We're criminals, not monsters.*

"You're staring," Maren said beside him. She had her dive harness half buckled, the straps hanging loose, and she was watching the fire the way other people watched sunsets.

"I'm checking my work."

"It's not your work. It's hers." Maren nodded toward the wheelhouse, where Devi stood silhouetted against the running lights, radio handset pressed to her ear, feeding the fleet's emergency channel just enough corroborating chatter to keep the story alive. "You just signed off on it."

"Same thing on this boat."

Maren smiled, and in the orange light it looked almost genuine. Almost. Cyrus had spent enough years around her to know the difference between her real smile and the one she used when she was afraid and wanted you to think otherwise. This was the second kind. He'd been seeing a lot of it since the Halcyon Dawn had dropped anchor.

"Comms check," Devi's voice crackled in his earpiece, cutting across the moment. "Both of you. Say something."

"Perdita two, reading loud," Cyrus said.

"Loud and bored," Maren added.

"Bored is good. Bored means your pulse is under a hundred and forty." A pause, static breathing. "Tomas has the skiff ready. Weather window is four hours, maybe five, before the outer bands start making the surface ugly. After that, I pull you out whether you like it or not."

"Understood," Cyrus said.

"One more thing." Devi's voice changed, dropped the operational flatness. "Your gas plan has you turning at forty minutes bottom time. That's the *plan*. The plan assumes you're not stupid. If you blow past the turn pressure because you found something shiny down there, I want you to remember that the thing you found will still be there tomorrow, and your bloodstream won't."

"Devi—"

"I'm serious, Cyrus. Past the point of no return on this mix, there's no sharing air on the way up. There's no buddy breathing, no emergency bottle, no clever improvisation. There's just the math. And the math doesn't care whose sister was on that ship."

The wheelhouse went quiet. So did the rail.

Maren finished buckling her harness, each strap cinched with a small, precise motion, and said nothing at all.

* * *

The skiff ride out took twenty minutes into a sea that was already beginning to think about misbehaving. Tomas drove with his usual economy, throttling down into troughs rather than fighting them, and kept his eyes on the water and his mouth shut until they were a mile from the wreck buoy. Then he looked back at them both and said, "Whatever's down there, it's been down there ten years. It'll still be there when you're thinking straight."

"You sound like Devi," Cyrus said.

"Devi's usually right. It's her worst quality."

He killed the engine over the site and the world went quiet in that particular way it did over deep water — no bottom visible, just the blue going down and down into a darker blue that wasn't a color so much as an absence. The buoy line ran off the skiff's stern and vanished after fifteen feet.

Cyrus ran his checks. Gauges. Valves. Buoyancy. The strongbox from the first descent had given them the partial manifest, the letter, the name of his dead captain written in a stranger's accusation — and it had told them, in the consortium's own accounting, exactly where the rest of the paper lived: the master's safe, aft superstructure, second deck. Maren had read that line out loud on the surface with her voice perfectly level and her hands not level at all.

"Bottom time forty minutes," he said. "Total runtime ninety-five. We're on the same gas, same schedule. You stay on my left, you stay in my sightline, and if I give you the thumb, we go up. No negotiation, no pointing at things, no *one more minute*."

"You've done this before," Maren said. It wasn't a question.

"Once. Off Luzon. A man named Ravelo stayed down nine extra minutes for a crate of silver pesos." He looked at her. "We brought up the pesos. We didn't bring up Ravelo."

Maren held his gaze for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, and put her regulator in her mouth, and rolled backward off the skiff without another word, which was, Cyrus had to admit, the most honest thing she'd done since the harbour bar.

He followed her down.

* * *

The first thirty feet were warm and bright and lied to you about everything. At sixty feet the light turned to pewter. At a hundred it went to iron. The Corvane rose out of the dark at a hundred and eighty — wait, no, he corrected himself, *two hundred and three feet*, sixty-two meters, the numbers Devi had made him repeat until they were liturgy — and there she was, the way she'd been every time he'd closed his eyes since the first descent.

A wartime transport, three hundred feet of her, lying on her starboard side in the trench like something that had been put to bed by a careless giant. Silt hung around her in permanent suspension, a fog the sea never bothered to clear. The blast damage on her port hull gaped in the dive light — steel curled *inward*, a fist-shaped wound no mine outside a hull could have left, and every time Cyrus's lamp crossed it, he thought of Elias's hands, and made himself stop thinking about Elias's hands.

Maren was already at the hull, running the line toward the superstructure. She moved well. That was the thing he kept forgetting to hold against her — she moved like a diver, not like an owner, not like someone who paid other people to take the risks. Her kicks were economical, her trim flat, her lamp discipline clean. He'd known her for twenty years and he was only now learning what she looked like when she wasn't performing.

They reached the second-deck opening at the twelve-minute mark. The superstructure door stood half off its hinges, a black mouth in the steel, and the silt inside was deep and undisturbed — ten years of it, settling in the dark, waiting for the first thing stupid enough to stir it up.

Cyrus went in first. Not because he didn't trust her. Because it was his job, and because if the deck collapsed, he'd rather it collapse on him.

Inside, the Corvane was a cathedral of rust. Passageways narrowed and branched. Their two lamps carved cones out of the black, and the silt hung in the beams like snow that had forgotten which way was down. Cyrus moved by touch as much as sight, one hand trailing the guide line, the other reading the walls — bulkheads, doorframes, a pipe rack furred with coral white as bone. The ship breathed around them in slow tidal groans, steel flexing with the swell a hundred and eighty feet above.

At the twenty-minute mark, they found the master's day cabin.

Maren's lamp found it first — the door marked with a faded stencil, half the letters gone, but the shape of them legible enough. She hovered at the threshold, and even through the mask and the hood and the dark, Cyrus could see her change. Her breathing rate climbed. The bubbles came faster.

"Maren." He said it into the comm, quiet. "Breathe."

"I'm breathing."

"You're breathing like a woman about to do something stupid. Breathe like a diver."

A pause. Her exhalations slowed, lengthened, evened out. When she spoke again, her voice had the flat calm of someone who had just picked something heavy up off the floor and set it down. "My sister would've been nineteen. She'd lied about her age to take the contract. The Corvane was supposed to be the safe ship. The boring ship." Her lamp swept the cabin doorway. "Everyone told her the boring ship was the safe one."

"I know."

"You don't know. Your captain knew. Your captain—" She stopped herself. Her lamp steadied on the door. "The safe. Aft bulkhead, behind the chart locker, if the consortium's drawings were right."

"Then let's find out if they were."

The cabin was a tomb of small things. A desk fused to the deck by corrosion. A mirror gone black. A uniform hanging in a locker, dissolved to lace. And against the aft bulkhead, exactly where the drawings said, a chart locker — and behind it, when Maren's knife pried the corroded panel away with a screech that Cyrus felt in his teeth, a steel box the size of a ship's biscuit tin, bolted down, crusted orange.

Maren went at it with the pry bar and Cyrus went at it with the wrench, and for three full minutes the only sounds in the world were their breathing, the sea groaning overhead, and metal fighting metal in the dark. The bolts gave one at a time. On the last one, Maren's hand slipped and the wrench rang off the bulkhead, and the sound rolled away down the passageway like a bell in a drowned church, and they both froze, lamps wide, listening to the ship decide whether it minded.

It didn't mind. The Corvane just went on breathing.

"Thirty-one minutes," Cyrus said.

"I saw the gauge."

"Then you know what I'm going to say."

"We're not leaving without it." She had the box free now, cradled against her chest, and her lamp was on him, and behind the mask her eyes were doing the thing they did — the calculation, the charm, the whole apparatus she used on the world. But underneath it, for the first time, something else. Something that wasn't performing anything. "Cyrus. Ten years. My whole life has been the outside of this ship. You don't get to make me leave with the door open."

He looked at her — really looked, in the way the dark allowed, in the way the depth stripped things down — and understood that they had crossed some line he hadn't noticed. Not trust. Not yet. But the place where two people admit they're in the same trap, and stop pretending the trap belongs to only one of them.

"Thirty-four minutes," he said. "Then we go, box or no box. That's not negotiation. That's the math."

"Devi's math."

"Devi's math is the only reason either of us has ever come back from anything." He took the box from her — she let him, which was its own kind of surrender — and clipped it to his harness ring. "Move."

* * *

They were at thirty-eight minutes when the deck collapsed.

Not the whole deck. A section of the passageway aft of the cabin, ten feet of corroded plating that had held for a decade and chose that moment, under the pressure of two divers' fins and the long slow flex of the swell, to give up. It went without sound at first — just the silt erupting, a brown wall swallowing their lamps — and then the groan came, deep and structural, the ship's whole skeleton shifting around them.

Cyrus's hand found Maren's arm in the zero-visibility and squeezed twice. *Hold.* They hung in the brownout, weightless in a cloud of ten years, and the silt hissed and settled, and somewhere below them something large slid and came to rest.

When the water cleared enough to see lamps through, the passage behind them was gone. Not blocked — *gone*, folded down into the deck below, a jagged skylight of torn steel where the corridor had been. Their guide line ran through the wreckage, pinched under a plate the size of a car door.

Maren's breathing had gone ragged again. Cyrus put his lamp on her gauge, then his own. Thirty-nine minutes. Turn pressure was a memory. The math had stopped caring about their plans somewhere around the moment the deck did.

He could see her running it — he could see the exact shape of her thinking, the same arithmetic he was doing, the same cold ledger. The line was fouled. Cutting it meant finding the fouling in the silt, in the dark, in a ship that had just demonstrated it could change its mind. Swimming without the line meant navigating the Corvane's guts on memory and luck, and the Corvane's guts had already eaten two of Elias's divers and, if the letter was true, a hundred and forty passengers.

Maren unclipped her backup line reel and held it up. Her eyes behind the mask said what her voice couldn't waste gas saying: *your call.*

Cyrus looked at the wrecked passage. He looked at the box on his harness. He thought about Ravelo, nine extra minutes, a crate of silver. He thought about Elias, and the knife, and whatever was still wedged somewhere in this ship that the sea had kept for ten years.

Then he tied off Maren's reel to a stanchion, took the lead, and swam them out through the ship's broken ribs by dead reckoning and the memory of a single pass — left at the pipe rack, down through the torn deck, out through the superstructure door — with her line paying out behind them like a lifeline in both senses, and the Corvane groaning overhead the whole way, and his own gauge showing him numbers he had promised Devi he would never let it show.

At the fifty-foot stop, hanging in the green-gray light with the wreck's dark shoulder rising below them like a sleeping animal, Maren took his hand.

Not a grip. Not a signal. She just took it, gloved fingers around gloved fingers, and held on for the length of one full breathing cycle, and then let go, and pointed up, and neither of them ever mentioned it afterward. But Cyrus understood that something had happened down there in the dark that had nothing to do with manifests or ledgers or the dead. Two people had gone into a drowning ship together, and the ship had tried to keep them, and they had walked out of it holding the same rope.

That was a partnership. He'd seen it built a hundred times, in a hundred ways, and it never looked like the movies. It looked like this: a hand in the dark, at fifty feet, with nothing left in the plan.

* * *

They broke the surface forty yards from the skiff, and the world was loud and bright and moving, and the first thing Cyrus saw was Tomas's face over the gunwale, and the second thing was the sky.

The monsoon's outer bands had arrived early. The clouds to the west were stacked up like a wall, blue-black, lit from inside by lightning that flickered without sound at this distance, and the wind had come around and freshened and was putting a chop on the sea that the skiff was going to hate.

Tomas hauled them aboard. His hands were shaking — Cyrus had never seen Tomas's hands shake — and his voice came out too fast. "Devi's been calling you for ten minutes. Comms went to hell. And there's more." He pointed east.

The Halcyon Dawn's fire was out. The smoke was gone. And the fleet ship was *moving* — under way, bow wave white in the failing light, coming around in a slow, deliberate arc that put her bow directly toward the Perdita's position.

"They faked the fire," Maren said, pulling her hood back, water streaming off her. "It was a diversion. For us. They let us dive."

"No," Cyrus said. He was watching the ship come, and the box on his harness, and the storm wall behind it all. "They let us dive because now they don't have to find the manifest. They just have to find *us*."

Behind them, sixty-two meters down, the Corvane settled back into its silence, keeping the rest of what it kept — including, wedged in an escape hatch Cyrus hadn't gone deep enough to reach, the answer to a question he was no longer sure he wanted asked.

The first rain hit the skiff like gravel.
