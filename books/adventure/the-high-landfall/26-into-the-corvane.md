## 26. Into the Corvane

The cargo deck swallowed them whole.

Cyrus went through the gash in the port side first, Tomas's torch beam jittering against his shoulder like a nervous hand. The hole was big enough to swim through with gear on—barely—and inside it opened into a cathedral of dark water and drifting silt. Their lights cut cones through it, and everything the cones touched was rust and shadow and the pale bloom of coral that had spent a decade turning steel into reef.

Sixty-two meters. Bottom time already bleeding away. Cyrus checked his gauges without really seeing them. He knew the numbers by heart. Twenty-two minutes of work gas if they pushed it, longer than any sane man would push it, and they had already burned eight getting down through the trench.

He tapped Tomas twice on the shoulder: forward, slow. The deckhand answered with a single squeeze on his arm. Good. No words wasted at this depth; words cost air, and air was the only currency that mattered anymore.

The cargo deck stretched ahead of them like a drowned street. Containers had broken loose in the sinking and now lay stacked against the starboard bulkhead in a tangle that looked almost deliberate, as if some giant hand had tidied up before leaving. A forklift sat on its side, wheels still turned toward a pallet that had rotted into nothing. Fish hung in the beams—small silver ones, indifferent, scattering when the light swept past.

And everywhere, everywhere, the small white shapes caught in the corners of things. Coral, he told himself. Just coral. Bone-white branching coral finding every seam in the metal.

Except the shapes had fingers sometimes.

*Don't look. Work.*

Maren came through behind him, her breathing ragged even through the regulator. She'd insisted on coming down on the second dive, and Cyrus hadn't argued, which surprised him more than arguing would have. Something had changed between them on the ascent from the first descent—something about sharing a mouthpiece at thirty meters with your lungs burning and your safety margin gone. You didn't come back from that as strangers.

She pointed left. He followed her glove to a hatchway set into the forward bulkhead, half-collapsed under a fallen container, and felt his stomach drop through the floor of him.

The strongroom. Elias's charts had marked it. *Cargo hold 2, fwd lower, adjacent to strongroom* — written in his captain's cramped hand years before the ship ever sank, annotated like a man planning a delivery route rather than predicting a grave.

They'd found the strongbox yesterday on the outer hull side, wedged in debris near where the blast damage punched inward—a hole blown from outside, against every official account of an internal boiler explosion. But the box had been partial. A fragment of manifest. A letter that named names but not all of them. The full ledger, Maren said, the complete passenger list, would be in the strongroom itself. Where a consortium's courier would have locked it. Where a hired diver would have been sent to make sure it never surfaced—or to make sure it did.

Cyrus swam toward the hatchway and tried not to think about what else might be in there.

The collapsed container had left a gap barely wide enough for one diver. He went first because it was his name on the contract, because it had always been his name on the contracts Elias signed, because ten years of diving wrecks the navy preferred forgotten had taught him that the person who hesitated at the gap was the person who died in it. He pulled himself through, torch held high, and the light fell into the strongroom like water into a cup.

Steel shelving. Rusted lockers. A door on the far wall hanging open on black nothing. Silt lay over everything in a soft grey shroud, thick enough to swallow sound, and their bubbles broke against the overhead in slow silver beads that rose and vanished.

The safe was where the chart said it would be—floor-mounted, old naval pattern, door sprung. Whatever had been inside had been taken long ago or never secured, because the interior held only a drift of sediment and one dead flashlight, its casing furred with growth.

But the ledger wasn't in the safe.

It was in a document tube lashed to the underside of the nearest shelf, wrapped in oilcloth, tied with wire that had rusted to lace. Someone had hidden it deliberately. Someone had known the safe could be forced and chosen the one place a searcher wouldn't think to look. Cyrus's hands were steady as he cut the wire—Diving Master hands, Elias had called them once, the only compliment the old man ever gave—and the oilcloth came away in a cloud of silt, and beneath it was a sealed tube stamped with the Corvane's registry and the consortium's logo, and beneath the stamp, in marker that time hadn't fully eaten:

*DO NOT OPEN. RETURN TO PORT OFFICE. E.K. HAS BEEN COMPENSATED FOR SECURE DISPOSAL.*

E.K.

Cyrus read it three times. The letters didn't change. The water didn't rush in. His heart kept beating with the dumb persistence of muscle, and around him the wreck went on being a wreck, and somewhere above the storm was counting down the hours until it erased all of this forever.

*Tomas*, he signaled, and passed the tube back through the gap. Tomas took it, clipped it to his harness, and gave him a look through the mask that Cyrus couldn't read and didn't try to. There would be time for looks later. Or there wouldn't.

Fourteen minutes. Maybe twelve of usable work.

Maren tapped his shoulder and pointed deeper into the strongroom, toward the far corner where an escape hatch let into the compartment aft—the emergency route, the way out if the main passage flooded. Standard wartime layout. Elias had taught him the layout of a dozen ships this age, walking him through blueprints in the Perdita's wheelhouse with coffee going cold between them, saying *always know two ways out, boy, because the sea only leaves you one.*

Cyrus turned toward the escape hatch to check it—habit, discipline, the litany of survival—and stopped moving entirely.

She was wedged in it.

Not floating. Not drifted. Wedged—shoulders jammed in the frame, body folded by the current that must have pushed her there in the last minutes, or the last seconds, of the flooding. Her dive gear was long gone, rotted off her, but the wetsuit fragments clung in strips, and the coral had done what coral does, and after ten years there was very little left that you could call a person. But there was enough. There was a harness frame fused to the ribs of her. There was a dive knife on her belt, civilian pattern, and beside her—dropped, or placed, resting against her hip in the angle of the hatch frame—

Elias's knife.

Cyrus knew it the way you know your own handwriting. Six-inch blade, brass bolsters worn smooth on one side where a right-handed man's thumb worried them for thirty years, the tip re-ground after the Java job because Elias had pried open a container seal with it and snapped a centimeter off and never let anyone forget whose fault that had been. His own. He'd said so every time he sharpened it.

That knife. Here. Ten years down, in a flooded strongroom sixty-two meters inside a wreck the whole coast pretended didn't exist.

The implications arrived one after another, each worse than the last, each arriving with the slow inevitability of water filling a boot.

Sela had made it inside. She'd gotten through the blast damage, through the cargo deck, into the strongroom—maybe chasing the same tube Cyrus now carried, maybe just running from the water. And she'd died here, wedged in the escape hatch, and Elias's knife had been beside her when she died.

Which meant Elias had been here too.

Which meant Elias had come back down after the disaster—not once, but enough times to leave his knife, unless he'd never left at all, unless he'd been aboard when she died—

Or.

The other thought. The one that had lived in Cyrus's chest for ten years like a splinter working toward the heart. The letter in the strongbox had named Elias as the diver hired to confirm the scuttling charges were set. Paid. Compensated. The tube in Cyrus's hand said it again in faded marker: *E.K. HAS BEEN COMPENSATED.*

And Rook and Sela had gone down the night after, on a dive Elias authorized, on a wreck Elias had mapped years early, and only Elias had come back up.

Had he cut her line? Had the money bought more than silence—had it bought the kind of thoroughness that leaves no witnesses? Was that what the knife meant? Not a keepsake. Not grief. Evidence. The tool of the thing, left where it finished.

Or had Elias stayed? Had he gone down into the flooding dark after her, past any sane depth, past any survivable timeline, and stayed until the sea closed over both of them, and surfaced alone because the sea gives back only what it wants to—and left the knife because he couldn't carry the weight of it and his own guilt besides?

Two stories. One knife. No way to choose, down here, in the cold, with the silt drifting like ash.

Cyrus heard his own breathing, harsh and fast in his ears, and made himself slow it. Four counts in. Four counts out. Elias's rule. The old man's voice in his head, patient as tide: *Panic is just arithmetic done badly, boy.*

Except Elias had taught him arithmetic while signing receipts from men who killed a ship full of passengers.

His eyes stung and he couldn't tell if it was salt water or something else, and at sixty-two meters it didn't matter because either way he was losing his mind by degrees and the gauge said nine minutes.

Maren had seen. Of course she'd seen. She hung in the hatchway gap behind him, motionless except for the tremor of effort that lives in every diver's limbs at depth, and her torch was fixed on the white shape wedged in the steel, and her shoulders were shaking in a rhythm that had nothing to do with cold.

Her sister. The name from the partial manifest. Cyrus had known it since chapter twenty-something of this nightmare—since the wheelhouse, since the confrontation, since she'd told him in a voice scraped raw why she'd really hired the one crew with the charts. And now here was the answer to a question Maren had carried twenty years, lying in a hatch frame wearing coral for a shroud, and no answer at all to the question underneath it, the question Cyrus could see forming behind her mask even from here:

*Did she suffer? Did someone help her? Did my sister die reaching for a hand, or flinching from a knife?*

There was no way to tell her. There was no way to tell anyone. That was the unbearable part—not the bones, not the rust, not even the knife. It was that the truth had come down to two possibilities and the sea had kept the deciding fact for itself, the way the sea kept everything, and no amount of salvage would ever dredge it up.

Cyrus reached out.

Slowly. Deliberately. The way you move around remains, the way Elias taught him—*you don't disturb the dead for salvage, boy, you take what they offer and you say thank you.* His gloved fingers closed around the brass bolster of the knife, and it lifted free of the silt with a soft whisper, and it weighed almost nothing, and it weighed everything.

He clipped it inside his harness, against his chest, where he could feel it with every breath.

Then he looked at Sela one more time. What was left of her face was turned slightly upward, toward the surface, toward the distant impossible light. Ten years she'd lain here pointing home. Nobody had come. Elias had sworn them all off this wreck, forbidden the very mention of it, and Cyrus had obeyed for a decade because loyalty was easier than questions, and all that time she'd been here, waiting in a doorway, holding the exit shut with her body.

*I'm sorry*, he thought, and knew how worthless it was, and thought it anyway.

He worked the hatch frame gently, testing. She wasn't blocking anything—the hatch beyond was jammed anyway, seized solid with corrosion, another dead end. But he couldn't leave her wedged like something discarded. He braced his feet and eased her back from the frame, careful, respectful, until she settled against the bulkhead in a posture that looked almost like rest. Almost like someone who'd lain down on purpose.

Maren helped him. Neither of them spoke—couldn't speak—but their hands moved together in the dark with a coordination that needed no language, arranging what remained, and when it was done she pressed her glove flat against the bulkhead beside the body and held it there for three full breaths. A touch. A goodbye. The only kind available.

Seven minutes. The numbers were screaming now. Cyrus checked Tomas through the gap—clipped tube secure, torch steady, breathing rate acceptable—and gave the signal: *ascend.*

They rose out of the strongroom the way they'd entered, backward through the gap, and Cyrus went last because he always went last, because Elias had drilled it into him—*the dive master is the anchor, the anchor comes up last*—and at the mouth of the gap he stopped, one hand on the rusted frame, and looked back.

The torchlight held the compartment for one more second. Shelving. Sediment. The small pale shape resting against the wall, finally un-wedged, finally at ease, keeping her watch in the deep.

Then he killed the memory of it as best he could, kicked for the gash in the hull, and rose into the grey.

* * *

The ascent took forever, the way ascents do when your margin is gone. They hung at the stops like ornaments on a line, three bodies swaying in the blue-black, trading gases from the staged bottles Auntie Bee's cousin had sourced from a decommissioned commercial outfit of dubious paperwork. Cyrus breathed and counted and breathed and counted and felt the knife against his sternum the entire way up, a small cold rectangle of brass and questions.

Tomas watched him across the stop line with the particular attention of a man who has seen his captain go quiet in a specific dangerous way. At six meters, in the last long hang, Tomas drifted closer and put a hand on Cyrus's tank valve—steadying him, or checking his gas, or just touching base with a human being. Through the masks, Cyrus met his eyes.

Later, Tomas seemed to say. Everything, later.

Cyrus nodded. Later.

The surface took them like a slap—rain already falling in fat warm drops, the swell running higher than it had been two hours ago, the sky to the southeast the color of a bruise going bad. The Perdita swung at her anchor beyond the reef shelf, smoke thin from her stack, and Devi was in the launch that came for them, and Auntie Bee was in the wheelhouse window with binoculars, and none of it was normal, all of it was wrong, because the horizon had changed character while they were below.

Devi hauled Tomas over the gunwale first, then Cyrus, then Maren, and the launch driver—Bee's youngest deckhand—had his eyes wide and fixed on Cyrus's face.

"Skipper," the kid said. "You need to hear this from Devi."

"What."

"The Halcyon Dawn's tender launched forty minutes ago. Not toward us." The kid swallowed. "A navy frigate came over the horizon with her. Flying the consortium's house flag alongside the ensign. They're anchoring inside the restricted zone boundary." He paused, and the rain ran down his face. "And there's a second boat. Unmarked. It went quiet on our radar twenty minutes ago, heading for the channel. Somebody's coming through the reef, Skipper. In this weather. Toward the wreck."

Cyrus climbed aboard the Perdita with the knife against his chest and the tube in Tomas's arms and the storm eating the horizon, and Devi met him at the rail with the logbook already open in her hands, and her expression told him before she said a word.

"I finished decoding," she said quietly. "The last entries. Cyrus—" She stopped. Started again. "He wasn't paid to sink her. He was paid to stay silent about who did. And the final entry—it's not a log. It's instructions. For whoever found this."

Rain hammered the deck. Somewhere south, through the squall, an engine note neither of them recognized was threading the reef channel in weather no sane pilot would attempt.

"Read it," Cyrus said. "All of it. Now."
