## 27. The Full Manifest

The strongroom door was a slab of steel gone the color of old teeth, and it had been waiting for them for eleven years.

Maren Voss hung in the flooded corridor of the Corvane's cargo deck with her lamp beam steady on the hinge line, and she thought—not for the first time, but more honestly than ever before—that everything in her life had narrowed to this one rectangle of rusted metal. Behind her, Cyrus Kavanagh held position at the corridor junction, running his light along the overhead plating the way he did everything: methodically, without flourish, as if the sea were a room he'd been hired to clean.

*Two minutes on the door,* Devi had said topside. *Cutting charge is small. You'll feel it more than hear it.*

She'd felt it. A soft concussion through the water, a shiver of silt, and then the door had sagged inward like a tired man leaning on a wall.

Maren swam through.

The strongroom was small—maybe three meters by four—and the water inside was different somehow, stiller, as if it had been holding its breath since the night the ship went down. Her light swept across shelving bolted to the bulkheads, across a filing cabinet whose drawers had fanned open in the sinking, spilling paper that had long since dissolved into grey felt. Across a deck safe, its handle fused with corrosion.

And there, lashed to a stanchion in a waterproof document tube—the kind shipping companies used for bills of lading, the kind that were rated for exactly this kind of abuse—was what she had crossed an ocean and burned every bridge she owned to find.

Her hands were shaking. She made herself stop. Ten years of certification drills, of cold-water work off Tromsø, of learning to make her fingers obey her when her mind wanted to scream—all of it funneled into this moment, and she would not let narcosis or grief or hope ruin it.

The tube's end cap fought her. She worked it with the flat of her dive knife, careful of the blade near the tube wall, and felt the O-ring give with a squeak only she could hear through the water. Inside: two bundles wrapped in oilcloth, tied with wire that had gone to lace.

She pulled them free and held them up to her light.

The first bundle was heavy card stock, laminated pages in a plastic sleeve. Even through the film of silt she could read the header: *S.S. CORVANE — FINAL PASSENGER MANIFEST — COMPLETE.* Not partial. Not the fragment from the strongbox they'd raised on the first dive. The whole list. Two hundred and six names. She didn't need to look for the one that mattered. Her eyes went to it the way a compass needle goes north.

*VOSS, ILSE MARIE — Cabin C-14.*

Her sister had beautiful handwriting. Ilse had filled out her own embarkation card in that handwriting, in the ferry terminal at Port Callas, and Maren had teased her about it—*you sign your name like you're signing a treaty*—and Ilse had laughed and said someone in the family should look official. That was nine days before the Corvane sailed. Nine days before the sea took her and kept her.

Maren's vision blurred behind her mask and she blinked it clear, because crying at thirty meters inside a wreck was how people died, and she refused to die before she finished this.

The second bundle was thinner. A ledger bound in green board, corporate stationery, the consortium's crest embossed on the cover—a stylized wave over a keel, the emblem she'd seen on executives' lapels at the memorial service where men who had signed her sister's death warrant shook her hand and said *our deepest condolences*. She opened it. The pages were protected by the same oilcloth discipline, and the ink had survived, and there it all was in columns: dates, disbursement codes, authorization initials. Payments. Insurance valuations filed six weeks before the sailing date. A hull survey noting "structural deficiencies beyond economic repair." And a final entry, dated the day before the Corvane left port, authorized by a signature she recognized from news broadcasts: *Special contingency — confirmation of charges placement — external contractor.*

External contractor. She knew the name behind that euphemism now. They all did. It had been sitting in Elias Kavanagh's logbook for ten years like a splinter working toward the heart.

*Maren.* Cyrus's voice in her earpiece, low and even. *Time check. We're at twenty-two minutes bottom time. Devi wants you moving.*

*Getting it now,* she said, and her voice came out steadier than she deserved.

She stowed both bundles in the lift bag's mesh pouch, double-clipped it, checked the clips twice, and gave him the thumbs-up. He returned it, and for half a second his light crossed her face and she saw something in his eyes that she couldn't afford to name—not here, not at depth, not with her lungs full of helium and her chest full of her sister's name.

They ascended out of the cargo deck together, through the corridor that had once been a passageway where passengers walked to dinner, past the hole punched inward in the hull plate that no torpedo account or storm report had ever explained. Maren looked at it one last time. Inward. Charges set against the inside of the skin, below the waterline, placed by a diver who had been paid to confirm the work and had spent the rest of his life drowning in it.

Then the wreck fell away beneath them, and the blue opened up, and they began to climb.

* * *

The first hundred meters of ascent were routine. Depth ticked down through the deco schedule Devi had built—stop at fifty-four, stop at forty-eight, stop at forty-two—and Maren let the numbers carry her, because numbers were safe. Numbers didn't have sisters on them.

Cyrus rose beside her, slightly above and slightly seaward, the way he'd positioned himself on every dive of this expedition. She'd told herself it was professionalism. Standard buddy protocol. But watching him now, silhouetted against the brightening water, she understood it was something else. He'd been standing between her and the deep the entire time, and he'd never once said so.

At thirty-six meters they hit the long stop, and the world simplified: their bubbles, the reef shelf a pale suggestion below, the grey-green nothing in every direction. Maren checked her gauges. Pressure fine. She checked Cyrus's, because he'd taught her on the first dive to check her partner's even when he checked his own. Fine.

She was thinking about the ledger. About which page she'd photograph first. About whether Ilse's name would be legible in the scans, whether Priya Nair would print it, whether somewhere in the world a man in a good suit was at this moment feeling the first cold draft of consequence.

That was why she almost missed it.

A sound—or not a sound. A change in the texture of her own breathing. The easy rasp of the regulator went thin, went whistling, went—

Nothing.

One breath, and the second stage delivered vapor instead of air. She exhaled on instinct, pressed the purge button, got a stutter of pressure and then a sound like a dying kettle. She reached for her octopus, and her hands—her trained, disciplined, expensive-certification hands—fumbled the clip.

Panic arrives faster than thought. That was the first thing any instructor ever told her, and it was true, and knowing it didn't stop it. The dark closed in at the edges of her vision. Her diaphragm seized. Somewhere very far away, logically, she knew she had maybe fifteen seconds of useful consciousness, and logically she knew the solution was simple, and logic was drowning faster than she was.

Then Cyrus was there.

He didn't swim to her; he arrived, the way weather arrives, already in motion. His hand closed over hers on the jammed octopus clip, freed it, and shoved the regulator into her mouth in one motion—his backup, his gas, the margin he was supposed to keep for himself. His eyes behind his mask were calm in a way that seemed almost insulting, and he held her gaze and tapped his watch twice: *breathe slow. We have time.*

They did not have time. She took the breath anyway, because the body does not negotiate, and the air came sweet and real, and the dark receded from the edges of the world.

He switched to his own primary, took a breath, handed it back—buddy breathing, the old way, the way divers had done it before everyone carried redundant everything, the way you did it when the math stopped working. Share. Exhale. Pass. His movements were unhurried, deliberate, a rhythm he was imposing on both of them, and slowly her heartbeat came down to meet it.

*Devi,* he said into the mask comm, voice muffled around the shared mouthpiece. *Maren's reg failed. Thirty meters. I'm sharing gas. Recalculate us for the worst case—we're burning my back-gas reserve.*

Static. Then Devi's voice, tight and fast: *Copy. Come up slow, Cyrus. Slow. I mean it.*

They came up slow.

It was the longest ascent of Maren's life, and she had once spent forty minutes hanging on a shot line in the North Sea with a cramping calf. Stop at twenty-seven. Stop at twenty-four. At each one Cyrus checked her gauges and then his own, and she watched him do the arithmetic behind his eyes and say nothing, and she understood what he wasn't saying: the safety margin they'd planned with was gone. Spent. Every minute of deco from here on was being paid for out of gas that was supposed to be insurance against exactly this.

He was giving her his insurance. He hadn't hesitated for even one breath.

At eighteen meters her own octopus—she'd finally gotten it unclipped, tested it, found it flowing—gave her back independent gas, and Cyrus waved it to her mouth with an economy of gesture that meant *use yours, save mine*, and she obeyed, and they kept climbing through the stops like two people climbing a staircase in a house that was slowly filling.

Somewhere around nine meters, in the long grey pause of the last deep stop, she looked at him.

He was watching the depth gauge, watching her, watching the water, doing all three at once the way he seemed to do everything at once. There was a scratch of coral dust along his jaw strap. His breathing rasped gently in the shared silence. And Maren Voss—who had built a career, a reputation, and a fortress out of knowing exactly what to say to every person in every room—discovered that there was nothing in her vocabulary for this.

*Thank you* was too small. It was a word for borrowed pens and held doors. What he had done was spend a piece of his life on hers, calmly, without invoice, and she knew—Ilse's death had taught her—that life was the only currency that didn't come back.

She wanted to say: *I hired you because you were the only ones with the charts, but I would have hired you anyway. I wanted it to be you. I've wanted it to be you since we were twenty-three years old and you beat me on the salvage exam by four points and bought me a beer and said "next time don't sandbag the navigation section."*

She wanted to say: *I'm sorry I used your captain's ghost as leverage. I'm sorry I let you find out about the payments from a ledger instead of from me.*

She wanted to say: *When your regulator fails someday, I will be there. I will arrive the way weather arrives.*

None of it would fit through a mouthpiece at nine meters. So she did the only thing she could. She caught his eye, held it, and put her bare hand flat against her chest—over her heart, over the dry suit, over everything—and pressed once. Then she pointed at him.

His expression didn't change. But something behind it did, some gate swinging on a rusted hinge, and he nodded once, slow, and looked away first—which, she suspected, from Cyrus Kavanagh, was its own kind of answer.

They broke the surface into grey light and the smell of rain coming, and Auntie Bee's hands were on their tanks before their fins cleared the water, and Tomas hauled them aboard the Perdita like they were cargo worth saving, and Devi was talking—fast, relieved, angry-relieved—about regulator maintenance and intermediate pressures and *I told you we should have serviced those second stages in port*, and the ordinary machinery of the tug closed around them, warm and loud and alive.

Maren sat on the deck plates with the lift bag between her knees, the manifest and the ledger sealed in their pouch, dripping seawater onto steel. Two hundred and six names. One of them was Ilse Marie Voss, cabin C-14, age twenty-nine, occupation teacher, next of kin: sister.

She'd spent ten years imagining this moment as triumph. It felt like holding a stone.

Cyrus lowered himself onto the deck beside her, pulling off his hood, hair plastered flat, looking older and younger than his age at once. He didn't ask if she was okay. He sat with her in the rising wind and let the question stay unanswered, which was, she was beginning to understand, the most honest thing anyone had ever done for her.

"The ledger," she said finally. Her voice came out scraped. "Page forty-one. The authorization signature. That's the man who signed the order."

"I know," Cyrus said. "Devi flagged it during the decode. He's on the consortium board. He gave a speech at the tenth-anniversary memorial."

"He shook my hand."

"I know that too." He turned the regulator over in his hands, the failed one, examining it with the detached attention he gave everything. "Diaphragm split. Eleven-year-old part. Could've happened to either of us."

But it happened to her, and he'd paid for it, and they both knew the deco tables didn't care about gratitude. She watched him rack the gear without being asked, check the cylinders, start the log entry in his blocky handwriting—*dive 6, bottom 24 min, max 62 m, incident: reg failure at 30 m, gas shared, all hands recovered*—and she thought about how Elias Kavanagh must have written logs just like this, in just this hand's steady predecessor, while taking money from men who sank ships with people in them.

"Cyrus." He glanced up. Rain was starting to speckle the deck, fat warm drops, the monsoon clearing its throat. "What I said down there. The—" she touched her chest again, briefly, unable to complete the gesture in daylight. "I meant it."

He held her gaze for a long moment. Water dripped off the boom overhead. Somewhere forward, Auntie Bee was already putting the kettle on, because Auntie Bee believed tea solved things that God wouldn't touch.

"I know," he said. And then, quietly, almost to himself, looking out at the darkening horizon where the Halcyon Dawn's lights would be: "Get below and get warm. Storm's early. Devi says we've got maybe two days of window left, and after what the fleet saw on their sonar last night—"

He stopped. Maren saw it land on him, the thing he'd been carrying since the ROV spoof: the knowledge that somewhere out there in the rain, skilled people were recalculating, and this time they weren't guessing who.

"—they're going to come at us with everything," he finished. "Both barrels."

From the wheelhouse, Devi's voice cut through the rain, tight and flat in a way Maren had learned, over one hard week, to fear:

"Cyrus. You need to see this. Radar's got a contact coming out of the squall line—and it's not the Dawn."

Maren stood, the manifest clutched to her chest, and followed him into the wheelhouse, where the screen glowed green against the grey afternoon—and where a contact that should not exist was closing on the Perdita at twenty knots, flying no transponder at all.
