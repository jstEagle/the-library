## 28. Two Tides

The cove had no name on any chart worth trusting, which was exactly why Bee had picked it eleven years ago and never told a soul since. A notch in the limestone coast of an island the surveyors had misspelled twice, ringed by mangroves that grew right down into the water like they were drinking it, with a sand shelf shallow enough that at low tide you could walk out fifty meters before it went past your waist. The Perdita sat at anchor in four fathoms, engines cold, riding a swell so small it was more suggestion than motion.

Bee stood in her galley and listened to the boat breathe.

She'd been doing that for ten years—listening to the Perdita breathe. The hull ticked as the sun warmed it. The rigging hummed in the offshore breeze. Somewhere below, Devi's compressor cycled down after filling the last of the deco bottles, and the silence that followed had a shape to it, a held-breath quality Bee didn't like.

They were two tides from the monsoon. Maybe less. And somewhere in the strongroom of the Corvane, sixty-two meters down in a trench the charts called impassable, there was a ledger with names in it, and one of those names belonged to a man she'd cooked for every day for nine years.

Bee put water on for tea because her hands wanted something to do that wasn't shaking.

* * *

Cyrus came up from the foredeck where he'd been stowing the dive gear, moving slow, the way men move when their bodies have been to sixty meters on gas mix that was already marginal and their blood is still paying the bill. His face was salt-streaked and grey around the eyes. Maren Voss followed him, wrapped in a blanket Bee had thrown at her without being asked, looking smaller than Bee had ever seen her. The great Maren Voss, who used to outbid them on wrecks just to watch Cyrus's jaw tighten, sitting on Bee's own bench with her hands around a mug like it was the only solid thing left in the world.

The full manifest lay on the chart table between them, sealed in a waterproof case, along with the ledger pages Maren had brought up from the strongroom. Plastic sleeves. Laminated edges. Forty years old and dry as the day they went down.

Bee set the tea down and didn't sit. She never sat for the hard conversations. Sitting made you part of the furniture, and furniture didn't get a vote.

"Devi's asleep," Cyrus said. "Tomas is on watch."

"Then we say it once now and once again when they're up," Bee said. "Because I'm not having this decided by whoever's awake."

Cyrus looked at her. He knew what she was doing. He'd known her long enough to know when she was building a wall he'd have to go through.

"Bee—"

"Sit down, Cyrus Kavanagh."

He sat.

Maren's tea steamed untouched in front of her. Her eyes kept going to the case on the table, then away, then back, like a woman checking on a sleeping thing that might wake up angry. Bee had watched enough people carry secrets to recognize the posture. Maren wasn't guarding the manifest. She was guarding what it meant.

"You knew," Bee said. It wasn't a question, and she didn't make it one. "When you walked into the Harbour Bar with that contract and that smile. You knew what Elias did."

Maren's chin came up. To her credit—and Bee would grant her credit, grudgingly, in private, never aloud—she didn't flinch. "I suspected."

"Suspected." Bee laughed, and it came out ugly. "My husband—" She stopped. Corrected herself, the way she always did, the way she'd done for nine years. "*Our* captain took money from a consortium to confirm charges were set on a ship with passengers aboard. Then he spent the rest of his life teaching us how to read water and telling us the sea doesn't lie. And you sat across a bar from his boy and offered him dirty money to go dig up the proof."

"He didn't know," Maren said quietly. "That was the point."

"That was the *point*?" Bee's voice cracked across the galley and she let it. "You used him. You used all of us. You used Elias's charts—the ones he drew with his own hand, God rest him—to get to the place that killed Rook and Sela, so you could dig up the thing that would break him open if he were alive to see it."

"Yes," Maren said.

The word hung there. Bee realized she'd expected anything but yes. Excuses. Deflections. The lawyer's shuffle. Not this flat, exhausted honesty from a woman who hadn't slept either.

"I've spent fifteen years thinking my sister drowned when a ship broke apart in a storm," Maren said. "Two weeks ago I learned she drowned because men scuttled her for an insurance payout and paid a diver to keep quiet about it. So yes, Bee. I used your captain's charts. I'd do it again tomorrow. But I never lied to Cyrus about what we'd find. I only lied about why I needed it found."

Bee turned away and looked out the porthole at the mangroves, because looking at Maren Voss while she said things like that was more than Bee's heart had budgeted for today.

Anahera Voss. That was the sister's name. Bee had read it off the partial manifest two nights ago, in Devi's cabin, by lamplight, while Devi decoded and Tomas pretended to sleep and Cyrus stood his silent watch. Anahera Voss, age 24, occupation listed as teacher. Bee had written the name down on the back of a shopping list and then burned the shopping list in the galley stove, which was a stupid thing to do and she'd done it anyway, because some names you can't have lying around the boat.

* * *

By mid-morning the whole crew was crammed into the saloon, and the heat of six bodies and a tropical sun through the portholes made the air thick enough to chew. Devi sat with her arms crossed, dark circles under her eyes from three nights of code-breaking and one night of not sleeping after the second dive. Tomas leaned in the doorway, half in, half out, the way he always positioned himself when he thought a conversation might turn into a fight—he liked to have an exit mapped. Maren sat at the far end of the bench, and nobody had invited her and nobody had told her to leave, which in the grammar of the Perdita amounted to a provisional visa.

The manifest case sat in the middle of the table like a guest of honor nobody had seated.

"Three options," Cyrus said. He'd clearly rehearsed it, standing at the head of the table with his hands flat on the wood. "One. We sell. Maren's backers pay out, we disappear for a few years, everybody retires comfortable. Two. We bury it. Seal the case, drop it back on the wreck or sink it in the trench, and the Corvane keeps its secret another forty years. Three."

He stopped. Bee watched him stop. She'd raised him, near enough—not in blood, but in rice and salt and years—and she knew the difference between Cyrus pausing for effect and Cyrus pausing because the next word cost him.

"Three," Devi prompted, flat.

"We give it away," Cyrus said. "To Priya Nair."

Silence. The kind that has weight.

"Priya Nair," Devi repeated. "The journalist."

"The journalist," Cyrus confirmed.

Bee kept her face very still. Since the harbour, since the night they'd slipped customs and run east, she had been sending signals—quiet ones, deniable ones, the kind an old woman could pass off as sentiment. She'd mentioned Priya over dinner twice. She'd left a printout of one of Priya's old investigations on the nav desk where Cyrus would find it, a story about a fishery collapse and the ministers who'd profited from it, a story that had gotten three people arrested and one journalist exiled and had changed the law anyway. She'd said, once, in front of everyone, watching the horizon instead of their faces: *"Somebody should write all this down someday. Somebody who knows how."*

Nobody had picked up the thread. Nobody ever did. People assume the cook talks to hear herself.

But now Cyrus was saying the name himself, and Bee felt something loosen in her chest that she'd been carrying tight for days.

"Why her?" Tomas asked from the doorway. "Why not sell it ourselves? Why not shop it to a paper and take a cut?"

"Because a cut makes it a transaction," Cyrus said. "And transactions get renegotiated. You sell to one outlet, their competitors come after the story to discredit it, and the first thing they discredit is the source. Us. Our whole history goes under the microscope—the grey jobs, the wrecks we shouldn't have touched. Everything we've built gets used to bury what we found." He looked around the table. "Priya Nair publishes from outside every jurisdiction that has fingers in this. She's done it before. She's lost things doing it. She'll know what it costs going in, and she'll take it anyway, and she won't trade our names to soften the blow."

"And what does it cost?" Devi asked. Quietly. Devi always asked the real question quietly, like she was afraid loud questions would scare the answer off.

Cyrus didn't answer right away. Bee answered for him, because someone had to say it plain.

"Everything," Bee said. "It costs everything. Warrants. Blacklisting. Every port that takes consortium money closes to us. The navy that's been politely ignoring us for ten years stops being polite. We are done, boys and girls. Done as salvors, done as divers, maybe done as free women and men depending on how vindictive it gets." She looked at each of them in turn. "I want that said out loud, once, in daylight, so nobody votes pretty and regrets it in the dark."

Tomas shifted in the doorway. "Option one," he said. "Sell it. Take the money. We could be in Valparaíso by Christmas with new names and a boat that doesn't leak."

"The money comes from the same people who paid Elias," Devi said.

Tomas winced. "I know what it comes from. I'm saying it out loud so nobody votes pretty and regrets it in the dark." He threw Bee's own words back at her, and she almost smiled. Almost.

"It's blood money," Devi said. "Rook and Sela died on that wreck. Sela died *inside* it, wedged in that hatch, and Elias—" Devi stopped. Looked at Cyrus. Cyrus gave her a small nod: say it. "Elias was there. Or he sent her down. Either way, the ledger in that case is the reason she's dead, and I am not taking a paycheck signed by the same pen."

"So we bury it," Tomas said. "Drop it back in the trench. Nobody profits, nobody hangs, we sail home poor and whole."

"And Anahera Voss stays a storm statistic forever," Maren said. Her voice was rough. "And the consortium writes off one transport and collects on the insurance, and the families keep lighting candles for an act of God." She looked down at her hands. "I'm not neutral here. You all know I'm not neutral. My sister is in that case. But I'll tell you what I told Cyrus in the wheelhouse: I don't care what you do with it, as long as it isn't sold back to them. Bury it, publish it—I'll live with either. Sell it, and I'll burn the boat myself."

"Dramatic," Devi muttered.

"I'm a dramatic person."

"You're a person who's been dramatic *at* us for fifteen years."

"Devi." Cyrus's voice was tired, not sharp. Devi subsided, but Bee saw the corner of her mouth twitch, and was grateful for it. Even now. Especially now. A crew that couldn't snipe at each other was a crew that had already stopped being one.

Bee poured more tea. It was a ritual and she performed it deliberately, moving around the table, topping up mugs, letting the ordinary motion settle the room the way ballast settles a hull. When she reached Maren, she hesitated half a second longer than she meant to, and then filled the mug anyway. Maren looked up at her with something startled in her face. Bee said nothing. There would be time, or there wouldn't. Tea was not forgiveness. Tea was tea.

"Let me ask it different," Bee said, returning to her spot at the head of the table. "Not what do we take. What do we *tell*." She set the pot down. "Ten years ago, Elias forbade us to speak the Corvane's name. Told us some things belong to the water. And we obeyed him, because he was Elias, and because obeying was easier than asking why his hands shook when he said it." She laid her palm flat on the table, next to the case. "We've been quiet for ten years. Rook's mother still doesn't know where he died. Sela's people got a letter saying *lost at sea, circumstances unclear*. Circumstances unclear." Her voice caught and she pushed through it. "I wrote that letter. Elias dictated it and I wrote it, and I have regretted my handwriting every day since. So no. I'm done with burying. Whatever else happens, somebody is going to be told."

Cyrus was looking at her with an expression she couldn't read—or could, and didn't want to, because it was the expression of a man realizing the ground under him had been shifting for years without his noticing.

"You've been pushing for Priya since the harbour," he said.

"I've been pushing for the truth since 1998," Bee said. "Priya's just the only person I know who can carry it without dropping it."

* * *

They voted the way they did everything—out loud, in order of seniority, oldest first, because Elias had insisted on it years ago and the habit had outlived him. *Oldest speaks first so the young ones aren't anchored to it,* he used to say. *And youngest last, so they speak at all.*

"Tomas," Bee said.

Tomas came fully into the saloon for the first time and sat down, which was itself an answer of sorts. He turned his mug in his hands. "I came onto this boat to get rich," he said. "Everybody knows that. Ten years, and I'm not rich, and I stopped expecting to be about year four, and I stayed anyway, and I've never once asked myself why until this week." He looked up. "I stayed because of you lot. And you lot don't sell each other out, and we'd be selling out Rook and Sela and every name on that manifest if we cash this in. So. Publish. Give it to the journalist. All of it, our names included. If we're going to burn, let's at least be warm."

"Poetic," Devi said.

"Shut up, Devi."

"Devi," Bee said.

Devi had her arms crossed again, but her foot was tapping, which those who knew her understood to mean the decision had already been made and the body was just catching up. "I spent three nights decoding a dead man's crimes," she said. "And then one night deciding whether I hated him. I landed on hating what he did and loving what he tried to fix, and I don't think those cancel out, I think they're both true, and I think the truth deserves better than a trench." She unfolded her arms. "Publish. And I want the logbook in the story too. All of it. The payments and the atonement. People should get the whole man, not the convenient half."

"Maren," Bee said, because seniority on this boat had suddenly become a flexible concept.

Maren blinked. For a moment she looked genuinely unmoored—Maren Voss, who commanded salvage fleets and bought harbours, being handed a vote on a leaking tug by a cook. "I don't get a vote. This is your crew."

"You're on my boat eating my food," Bee said. "You get a vote."

Maren looked at the case. At Cyrus. Back at Bee. "Publish," she said. "And if your journalist needs funding for lawyers afterward, my accounts are about to be very interesting to several governments, but whatever's left is hers."

"Cyrus," Bee said.

And the saloon went quiet, because everyone knew the vote had really been waiting on him the whole time, and everyone knew why. It was his captain. It was his wound. It was his name that would be in the story, next to Elias's, tied together forever in print: *the diver and the man who hired him.*

Cyrus stood. He walked to the porthole and looked out at the mangroves for a long moment, and Bee watched his shoulders, and read them the way she'd read them since he was nineteen and seasick and too proud to admit it.

"My father taught me that the sea keeps honest accounts," he said finally, not turning around. "Every debt comes back on the tide. I believed that right up until I found out he'd been cooking the books." He turned. His eyes were red-rimmed and steady. "Elias ended his last log entry with a sentence somebody finished in different ink. *If you're reading this, finish it.* I think he wrote that for whoever came after him. I think he hoped it would be someone who'd do what he couldn't." He put his hand on the case. "Publish. Everything. The manifest, the ledger, the logbook, the blast damage, Sela—all of it. Let Priya decide what the world can stomach. Our job is to get it to her."

"Carried," Bee said, before anyone could dilute it with discussion. "Unanimous. Four votes and a visa." She nodded at Maren, who almost smiled.

"Now the hard part," Devi said. "How do we reach her? We're in a hidden cove with the monsoon coming and a fleet ship six miles offshore pretending it isn't."

Bee reached into her apron pocket and set a folded piece of paper on the table beside the manifest case. She'd been carrying it for nine days.

"What's that?" Tomas asked.

"A PO box in Colombo," Bee said, "and a frequency, and a phrase to say on it that means *I have something you'll want to cross an ocean for.*" She met Cyrus's eyes. "I've had it since before the Harbour Bar. I wrote to her the night Maren's liaison photographed our equipment lists, because I've been on this water long enough to know when a job smells like more than money."

Cyrus stared at her. Devi started laughing—a short, disbelieving bark. "You've had contact with her this whole time? You let us vote blind?"

"You didn't vote blind," Bee said. "You voted true. I just made sure the door was unlocked." She tapped the paper. "Question is whether we send it. Once that message goes out, it's real. No burying it after. No selling it after. The moment Priya Nair knows what we have, the world starts knowing too."

Outside, far off across the water, thunder spoke—low and unhurried, the monsoon clearing its throat beyond the horizon. The light in the portholes dimmed a shade, then recovered.

Bee picked up the paper and held it out to Cyrus, palm steady, the way she'd held out ration cards in bad seasons and Elias's last letter in worse ones.

"Your captain started this," she said. "His boy finishes it. Send it or I will—but one of us sends it before the next tide turns."

Cyrus took the paper.
