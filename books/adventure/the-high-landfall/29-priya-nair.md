## 29. Priya Nair

The fishing boat came in on the last of the ebb, riding low and ugly against the green water of the cove, and Priya Nair stood at its rail with her arms crossed and her weight on the balls of her feet the way she always did when a story was close enough to smell. Twenty-two years in the trade had taught her that the smell was never quite what you expected. Sometimes it was money. Sometimes it was fear. Most often it was both, layered like paint on a harbour wall.

She'd come out from Bandar Serani on a boat called *Selamat Pagi*, which meant Good Morning and was captained by a man who had not said good morning to anyone in a decade. Three days of diesel fumes and dried fish and one very suspicious customs skiff that had circled them twice off Cape Rusa before losing interest. She had spent those three days doing what she always did: reading.

She'd read everything there was to read about the Corvane, most of it redacted into lace. She'd read about Elias Kavanagh, dead eight years now, a salvage captain with a clean record and an oddly quiet tax history. She'd read about the Perdita — a hundred-ton tug with a patched hull and a crew list that read like a family tree someone had shaken too hard. And she'd read the eleven messages Auntie Bee had sent her over five weeks, each one more careful than the last, each one hiding its real meaning inside weather reports and recipes until the final message, sent four days ago: *Come now. Bring the good antenna. Bring your brave face. We have something the sea kept for you.*

Priya had learned long ago to trust grandmothers with satellite phones. They were the only source class that never lied for fun.

*Selamat Pagi* bumped the Perdita's hull, and lines went across, and Priya looked up at the tug's rail where five faces were already gathered, watching her the way people watch a stranger carrying something fragile through a crowded room.

"Ms. Nair," said the woman at the gangway. Grey-streaked hair, forearms like dock rope, an apron over her shirt despite the fact that no cooking was happening. "Auntie Bee."

"Madam Bee." Priya took the offered hand. It was warm and it did not let go quickly. "Your rendang recipe is excellent, by the way. The turmeric quantities are criminal, but the principle holds."

Something eased around the old woman's eyes. Good. The recipe messages had been the authentication protocol; she'd guessed as much from the start. You didn't build a five-week cipher out of spice measurements unless you were a woman who understood that trust is a thing you test slowly, like wire rope.

"Come aboard," Auntie Bee said. "Mind the third step. Tomas keeps meaning to fix it and Tomas keeps meaning a lot of things."

*

They put her in the wheelhouse because it was the only room on the Perdita with a door that closed, and they gave her tea in a mug that said WORLD'S OKAYEST FISHERMAN, and then Cyrus Kavanagh set a hard case on the chart table, unlocked it, and stepped back.

Priya had imagined this moment maybe forty times on the boat down. In most versions she felt triumph. In two or three she felt fear. What she actually felt was neither. She felt the specific, professional stillness that arrives when the thing in front of you is exactly as heavy as it looked from far away.

Inside the case, packed in dive-weight foam, were two objects. A corroded steel document tube, salt-welded shut along half its seam but opened cleanly along the other. And a ledger bound in oilcloth, its pages warped into soft waves by ten years of seawater, the ink miraculously legible where the paper hadn't bloomed.

"Partial manifest first," Cyrus said. His voice was flat, controlled, the voice of a man who had rehearsed being calm about this. "Corvane, sailing under consortium charter, registered passenger count two hundred and fourteen. The official record says one hundred and six survived, thirty-one lost." He turned a laminated photocopy toward her — they'd photographed everything within hours of opening the box; the originals stayed sealed. "This page alone lists sixty-one names. Cross-check the dates of birth against the memorial registry published after the sinking. I'll save you the night's work: forty-four of these sixty-one are not on any casualty list. Not among the dead. Not among the survivors. Nowhere."

"Erased," Priya said.

"Worse than erased." He tapped the ledger without touching it. "Payment schedule. Consortium disbursements to a shell company, quarterly, for six years prior to the sailing date. Then a final payment, triple the quarterly rate, dated nine days *after* the Corvane went down. The memo line says 'survey confirmation.' Survey confirmation for what, on a ship already at the bottom?"

Priya didn't answer immediately. This was the discipline: when the story handed you its heart, you held it carefully and asked the boring questions first, because boring questions were load-bearing. "Chain of custody," she said. "From the wreck to this table. Who's touched it?"

"Me, my engineer Devi Okafor, my deckhand Tomas Reyes, Auntie Bee, and Maren Voss." A muscle worked in his jaw at the name. "Voss recovered the full ledger herself, second descent. Everything since has been photographed, hashed, and copied twice. Devi can walk you through it. She thinks in chains of custody the way other people think in sentences."

"And the original documents stay aboard this vessel."

"They stay aboard this vessel."

"No," Priya said.

The word landed in the wheelhouse like a dropped wrench. Cyrus went very still. Behind him, near the doorway, a broad-shouldered woman — Devi, she placed her — stopped pretending to check a cable run.

"You don't keep originals on a boat," Priya continued. "Boats sink. Boats get boarded. Boats get bought. I've had three sources lose originals to bilge water and one to a fire that the insurer paid out on suspiciously fast. The originals go into a bank vault in a neutral jurisdiction within seventy-two hours, under names we'll discuss, and everyone works from copies from that moment forward. That's not negotiable, Mr. Kavanagh. That's the difference between a story that survives and a story that drowns with its witnesses."

She watched him weigh it. He was not a man who liked being told things — she could see that in the set of his shoulders, in the way his thumb found the scarred edge of the chart table and worried it. But he was also, visibly, a man who had spent ten days watching a navy fleet sit on his horizon like a held breath.

"Auntie Bee," he said finally, without turning. "You heard the lady."

"I heard her," Auntie Bee said from the doorway, and there was something in her voice that made clear she'd been hearing Priya correctly for five weeks of recipes.

*

They fed her dinner in the galley — actual rendang, turmeric crimes forgiven — and it was there, over rice and the low rumble of the generator, that Priya earned her fee.

Not by promising anything. By doing the opposite.

Devi sat across from her, a compact woman with burn-scarred knuckles and eyes that hadn't slept properly in a week. Tomas hovered at the galley's edge, young-ish, watchful, a man built like he'd been assembled for emergencies. Maren Voss was elsewhere — deliberately elsewhere, Priya noted; the woman had introduced herself with a handshake like a contract clause and had not reappeared since.

"Before anyone shows me another document," Priya said, setting down her fork, "I'm going to tell you what happens if I do my job well. All of it. No varnish."

The galley went quiet except for the generator.

"There's a version of this where everything works," she said. "I file from Singapore or Reykjavik or wherever my lawyer decides is least flammable this month. The story runs. Reuters picks it up, then everyone picks it up, the consortium's stock loses a billion dollars of market cap in a day, two executives resign into 'consulting,' a commission is formed, hearings happen, some mid-level men go to prison for fraud while the architects retire to lakeside properties. That's the *good* version. That's the version where the machine mostly works."

"And the bad version?" Devi asked.

"The bad version is the same, plus you." Priya looked around the table, meeting each pair of eyes in turn. Old habit. You delivered consequence face-to-face or it didn't land. "Let me be precise, because vague warnings don't help anyone plan. Cyrus Kavanagh: your salvage licenses in four jurisdictions become unsalvageable. Insurance won't touch you. Every port master who owes a favour to someone who owes a favour to the consortium will find a reason to inspect your hull for a week. You will be unemployable above board and radioactive below it. The grey zone you've lived in for a decade closes. There is no more legal-adjacent diving for you. There is Perdita-as-lifeboat, or there is Perdita-at-the-bottom."

Tomas shifted. She kept going.

"Devi. You're the best marine engineer I've never heard of, which tells me someone has been keeping it that way, which tells me you have a past you'd prefer stayed past. Publication drags every thread of that past into daylight, because that's what investigation does — it doesn't discriminate between relevant and convenient. Your certifications, your visas, whatever country you left and why. All of it becomes ammunition."

"I know how investigations work," Devi said quietly. "I've been on the other end."

"I believe you. That's why I'm saying it out loud instead of letting you find out later." She turned to Tomas. "You're the youngest and the most exposed and nobody in this conversation has mentioned that yet, so I will. You're the one they'll approach first. Not with threats — with offers. Money, papers, a fresh start somewhere the Perdita isn't. They will make it easy to leave and expensive to stay, and the offer will come at 2 a.m. on a bad night, and you should decide *now*, sober, in front of witnesses, what your answer is."

Tomas's jaw tightened. "And if my answer is yes?"

"Then tell me before you take their money and I'll structure the publication so nothing traces to you. Whistleblower hygiene. I've buried braver people than you in safer footnotes." She let that sit. "But you won't say yes. I've watched you for four hours. You're the type who stays."

"You don't know me."

"No," Priya agreed. "But I've interviewed two hundred people who thought about running, and I can hear the ones who aren't going to. It's in how you stood between Devi and the door during the boarding-party story Auntie Bee told me. You weren't guarding the door. You were guarding her. People who guard people don't sell."

Tomas looked at Devi. Devi looked at her tea. Nobody denied it. Priya filed that away — not for print, never for print, but for understanding whose story this actually was beneath the manifest.

"And Auntie Bee?" Devi asked. "What does publication cost her?"

Priya hesitated. Honest cost accounting meant naming the price even when the customer flinched. "Her peace. She's spent ten years sitting on the knowledge that something was wrong about the Corvane — I could hear it in every hedged sentence she ever sent me. Publication means admitting she waited ten years to act. Grief makes strange accountants. Some part of her believes staying silent was loyalty to Elias Kavanagh, and this breaks that belief open in public." She paused. "Also, she's seventy-one years old and about to become a named source in the biggest maritime scandal of the decade, which means her quiet life ends. Full stop."

"She doesn't have a quiet life," Tomas said. "She has us."

"That," said Auntie Bee from the galley doorway, where she had apparently been standing long enough to hear her own autopsy, "is the truest thing anyone's said on this boat all week." She came in and refilled Priya's mug without asking. "Go on, girl. Finish the bill. We're not a family that hides prices."

Priya nodded once, and finished it.

"Maren Voss. Her backers hired her to recover these documents for reasons she hasn't fully disclosed to you — and before you bristle, Mr. Kavanagh, note that I'm not accusing her, I'm describing her. Voss is a broker, and brokers get sold out; it's practically the job description. When this publishes, whoever hired her will need a scapegoat with a signature on your contract, and she's standing closest to the flame. She knows that. Watch whether she stays anyway. That'll tell you more about her than any confession would."

"And you?" Cyrus said. He'd been silent through all of it, arms folded, watching her the way he probably watched sonar screens. "What does it cost you?"

The question surprised her, mostly because it shouldn't have. "Me? Nothing you'd respect as a cost. I've been sued twice, detained once, and shot at zero times, which in my profession counts as a lucky career. My outlet will protect me or I'll publish independently and they can chase a byline with no address." She shrugged. "The truth is I'm the cheapest person at this table. I've already paid my dues. You're all paying yours starting the day I hit send."

Silence. The generator thumped. Somewhere aft, water slapped the hull in the rhythm of a cove that felt safe and wasn't.

"So," Cyrus said at last. "You're telling us not to publish."

"I'm telling you nothing of the kind." Priya leaned forward. "I'm telling you to publish with open eyes. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference. People who publish to hurt their enemies get sloppy. People who publish knowing exactly what it costs them — what it costs *everyone* — write carefully, store properly, name precisely, and survive. My job tonight isn't to scare you off. It's to make sure that when you say yes, you're saying it the way divers say it: having checked your gas, your depth, and your route back up." She spread her hands. "So. Check your gas. Do you want this story or not?"

It was Devi who answered first, which surprised no one who'd been paying attention. "We voted already. Four to one, one abstention." A glance at Auntie Bee, who inclined her head fractionally — the abstention acknowledged, its meaning still sealed. "We want it. We just needed to know what we're handing you."

"What you're handing me," Priya said, "is the only copy of the truth about two hundred and fourteen people, forty-four of whom the world was told never existed. Which means from this moment, I am not a guest on your boat. I am cargo. Treat me accordingly."

Cyrus almost smiled. It was a small, tired thing, but real. "Auntie Bee. Set another place. Our cargo eats with the crew."

*

Later — much later, past midnight, when the cove had gone black and the anchor light burned its lonely eye above the water — Priya sat in the wheelhouse with her satellite uplink humming softly on the chart table, running transmission tests she didn't need to run, because running them calmed her hands.

The door opened. Cyrus. He carried two mugs and set one beside her without ceremony.

"Can't sleep either?" he said.

"I sleep fine. I just work at night." She killed the uplink's test cycle. "You want something, Mr. Kavanagh. Ask it."

He looked out the dark glass at nothing. "The letter. The one from the strongbox that named Elias." His voice stayed level; his hands didn't. "In your professional judgment. When it publishes. How does he read? Monster or accomplice or —"

"Or a man who took dirty money and spent ten years trying to buy his way back out of it?" She'd read the logbook excerpts that afternoon, decoded entries laid beside the ledger's payment dates like two halves of a wound. "Mr. Kavanagh, I've written obituaries for saints and exposés about monsters, and in twenty-two years I have met perhaps four people who were entirely either. Your captain will read as what he was: a man who did an unforgivable thing and then did a decade of unforgiven penance. Some readers will hate him. Some will recognize themselves. Both reactions are correct. That's what honest stories do — they refuse to hand you a single feeling and call it closure."

He absorbed that in silence, the way he seemed to absorb everything — fully, slowly, like ballast taking on weight.

"One more question," he said. "Timeline. Once you file. How long until it's everywhere?"

"Uplink to my editor in Singapore, verification pass, legal review — forty-eight hours if nothing panics anyone. Then live." She studied his profile against the dark glass. "Why? You're planning something."

"The Halcyon Dawn's been quiet for eighteen hours. Too quiet. Fleet ships don't do quiet unless they're waiting for something, and the only thing worth waiting for out here is us getting careless." He drained his tea. "Storm window opens in two days. If we're moving your documents ashore, we move them inside it, and if your story goes live—" he glanced at her, and in the instrument glow his eyes were the flat grey of deep water, "—I'd rather the world knew what we're carrying *before* someone comes to take it. Noise is armor. You taught me that yourself tonight."

Priya felt the old familiar click behind her sternum — the moment a story stops being a story and starts being a countdown. She'd felt it in Lagos, in Jakarta, outside a courthouse in Manila. It never got lighter.

"Forty-eight hours after I file," she said. "Give me the word, and I'll give the world the Corvane."

Outside, far off across the black water, a light that wasn't a fishing boat blinked twice and went still — holding station, holding breath, six miles out where the big ship lay anchored.

Cyrus saw it too. He set his empty mug down on the chart table, next to the case that held everything.

"Then we'd better hope," he said, "that the storm gets here first."
