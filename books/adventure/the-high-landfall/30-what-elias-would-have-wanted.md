## 30. What Elias Would Have Wanted

The cove held them like a cupped hand. Two tides left, maybe less, and the Perdita swung at anchor in water the color of bottle glass, her engines cold, her decks littered with the archaeology of a week that had gone too deep and come back changed.

Cyrus hadn't slept. He'd tried—Devi had all but ordered him into his bunk after the surface interval began—but sleep was a country he couldn't reach from here. Every time he closed his eyes he saw Sela's remains wedged in that escape hatch, and beside them, resting on the frame like it had been placed there, Elias's knife.

So instead he sat in the wheelhouse with the logbook open on the chart table, under the one working lamp, and read his dead captain's handwriting for the hundredth time.

The final entry was dated ten years ago, the night of the disaster. It was coded in dive-table shorthand, the same cipher Devi had cracked over three sleepless nights, and it ended mid-sentence. That was the detail that had haunted Cyrus since they'd found it. Elias Kavanagh did not leave sentences unfinished. The man had annotated tide tables to the second and corrected other captains' chartwork in letters to hydrographic offices. A sentence that simply stopped was not an accident. It was a wound.

*Charges confirmed at frames 14 through 22. Placed by my own hand per contract. Sela wants to go back for the manifest but I told her no, we're done, we take the money and we never—*

And there it ended. Ink dried mid-word, the pen stroke trailing off as if the hand holding it had been pulled away.

Cyrus turned the page. Blank. He'd checked it a dozen times. Blank, blank, blank—the rest of the logbook empty, because Elias had died three days later of a heart attack in a harbour bar with two whiskeys in him and the name *Corvane* on his lips, or so the story went. The story Cyrus had built his whole adult life around: that the old man had carried something unbearable down with him, some grief about Rook and Sela, and that the job was cursed and sworn off and never to be spoken of.

He had never once asked the question Devi had asked him in the wheelhouse two nights ago, in a voice stripped of everything soft: *What if he wasn't grieving? What if he was guilty?*

He turned back to the final page and held it closer to the lamp.

That was when he saw it.

At first he thought it was a shadow, a fold in the paper, a trick of the light through the salt-hazed lamp glass. But the light moved when he tilted the book, and the marks didn't. Faint impressions, pressed into the page from writing done above it—except this was the last written page, so nothing could have been written above it. Which meant the impressions were older than the ink. They were indentations from a page that had once sat on top of this one and been removed.

Someone had written something on a sheet laid over this page, pressing hard enough to score the paper beneath, and then torn that sheet out.

Cyrus's heart was doing something slow and enormous in his chest. He went to the galley, woke Auntie Bee without apology, and asked for a pencil. She looked at him—red-rimmed eyes, hair bound up for sleep, a woman who had spent two days staring at Elias's charts and saying almost nothing—and she gave him the stub of carpenter's pencil she kept tied to the spice rack without asking why. She followed him back to the wheelhouse anyway.

"Shade it," she said. "Sideways. Light as you can."

He shaded. Graphite whispered across the page, catching on the ridges, skipping the valleys, and the sentence rose out of the paper like something surfacing from deep water.

*If you're reading this, finish it.*

Different hand? No. He knew that slant, that hard downstroke on the F, the way Elias crossed his T's like he was cutting line. Same hand. Different ink—or no ink at all, just pressure, which meant it had been written later, after the logbook was closed, after the last entry, written blind on a sheet laid over the finished page and then destroyed.

Elias had known someone would find this book. Had known it might take years. And he had left a message that even a casual search would miss, because he hadn't trusted ink, hadn't trusted locks, hadn't trusted anything except the patience of whoever came after him.

"Finish what?" Auntie Bee said quietly.

"The truth," Cyrus said. His voice came out rough. "He wanted the truth finished."

She put her hand flat on the page, careful not to touch the graphite, and stood there a long moment. Then she said, "Read me the last entry again. All of it."

He read it. *Charges confirmed at frames 14 through 22. Placed by my own hand per contract.* The words he'd read fifty times since Devi decoded them, and which had landed each time like a fresh blow. Their captain. Their Elias, who taught Tomas to splice, who fed Auntie Bee's recipes back to her with compliments, who had held Cyrus's certification card in front of the exam board and said *this boy reads water better than I do*. Placed the charges himself. Took the consortium's money. And Sela—Sela had wanted to go back for the manifest, and he'd told her no.

"He wasn't finishing a confession," Auntie Bee said. "He was starting one."

*

Cyrus took the logbook up onto the foredeck, where the light was better and the air moved. The eye-passage calm wouldn't hold forever; the southern horizon already had a bruised look to it, a low grey smudge that wasn't cloud so much as weather deciding what it wanted to become. Two tides. Maybe one and a half, if the monsoon came early, and the monsoon always came early.

He sat against the windlass and read the whole logbook again, front to back, now that he knew how to read it.

It was a different book, this time. Before, every entry had been evidence of betrayal—a decade of trust converted overnight into ash. Now he saw the shape underneath. The payments started small: survey fees, consulting fees, a salvage rights dispute settled suspiciously in their favor. Elias recording each one in shorthand, dates and amounts, meticulous as a man keeping receipts for a crime he intended someday to answer for. And threaded between the payment entries, other notes. Dive-table numbers that weren't dive plans. Channel soundings for waters they never worked. References to a ship name rendered only as *C——*, always abbreviated, as if even in code he couldn't make himself write it whole.

The Corvane. Elias had been mapping her approach channel years before she sank. Auntie Bee had found that in the old charts, and Cyrus had felt the floor tilt beneath everything then. But now, cross-referenced with the ledger entries, the sequence resolved into something worse and more human than conspiracy. Elias hadn't known she'd sink. Elias had been paid to prepare the ground—to survey the channel, confirm the route, flag the hazards—and then, when the consortium decided to scuttle her for the insurance and bury the passengers' testimony along with her, they'd called in the man who knew the water. Called in a debt. And Elias, who owed them, who had taken their money for years, who had convinced himself right up until the end that it was survey work, salvage support, nothing that would cost lives—

He'd said yes. That was the whole of it. One yes, given by a man who thought he'd already sold his soul in installments and might as well get it over with.

And then he'd stood on the Corvane's deck, or hung above it in the dark, and set the charges with his own hands, and told Sela no, and taken the money, and spent the rest of his life unable to finish the sentence.

Except he had finished it. Just not in ink. *If you're reading this, finish it.*

Cyrus pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. Ten years. Ten years of swearing off the job, of forbidding the ship's name aboard, of flinching every time the navy's patrol boats got courteous, and Cyrus had read all of it as grief. As trauma. As a captain protecting his crew from a cursed wreck. He'd built his own identity on top of that reading—the methodical diver, the man who measured everything twice, who kept his feelings in a decompression schedule—because he'd believed Elias's silence was wisdom.

It wasn't wisdom. It was shame wearing wisdom's clothes.

And the worst part, the part that made his throat close, was realizing how much of Elias's last decade had been aimed at this moment. The charts annotated with the approach channel—not erased, not burned, but kept. The logbook sealed in a locker Cyrus had never been ordered to open but never quite dared to, either, and Elias knowing, knowing, that the boy who read water would eventually not be able to help himself. The knife. God, the knife. Left beside Sela's body in the escape hatch, forty meters down in the dark, where only someone who dove the wreck would ever find it. Not dropped. Placed. Elias had gone back—had to have gone back, on some later dive, alone, past all safety margins—and left his knife at the hatch where Sela died, the way a man leaves flowers. Or leaves a confession.

Or the way a man stays. The ambiguity was still there, still unbearable, and Cyrus understood now that it would never resolve. Either Elias had cut her line in panic or negligence, or he had stayed with her at the end rather than surface alone, and had placed the knife afterward on some return dive as penance. Both readings required a version of Elias Cyrus couldn't reconcile. Both readings were probably true at once. People drowned in both directions.

But the message cut through all of it. *Finish it.* Not forgive me. Not forget. Finish. Elias hadn't asked for absolution. He'd asked for completion—as if the truth were a dive profile he'd been forced to abort, and the only thing that mattered was that someone, someday, ran it to the bottom and came back up with the answer.

"You look like a man arguing with a ghost," Maren said.

Cyrus hadn't heard her come up. She lowered herself onto the foredeck hatch across from him, knees drawn up, a mug of tea in both hands. In the storm-light she looked older than her charm usually let her appear, the calculation worn thin, the fear showing through like bone through a graze.

"I'm trying to figure out what he'd have wanted," Cyrus said.

"You've figured it out. You just don't like it." She blew on her tea. "My sister's name is on that manifest, Cyrus. Amara Voss, age twenty-six, seat 14-C. I've had ten years of people telling me what she would have wanted. The navy telling me she died in a navigational accident. The consortium sending a condolence letter with a settlement check. My mother telling me to stop asking." She looked at him over the rim of the mug. "You know what they all had in common? None of them actually knew her. Wanting things for the dead is how the living hide."

"That's not what I'm doing."

"No," she agreed, surprisingly gently. "You're doing something worse. You're finding out he was guilty and loving him anyway. That's the part that costs."

Cyrus looked at the logbook in his lap. The grey smudge on the horizon had thickened. Somewhere out beyond the reef, the Halcyon Dawn was waiting out the same weather with a warrant and an offer, and somewhere south of that, Priya Nair was coming north on a fishing boat with a satellite uplink and a history of getting people killed by telling them the truth.

"Elias took their money for ten years," Cyrus said. "And then he spent ten years leaving a trail. The charts. The locker. The knife. The message under the message. He couldn't say it out loud—couldn't even write it in ink—but he made sure it could be found. By us. Specifically by us." He shook his head slowly. "I used to think he swore us off the job to protect us from the wreck. He was protecting us from himself. From what he'd done. And then, near the end, I think he decided we deserved to know anyway."

"And?"

"And I think—" Cyrus stopped. Started again. "I think forgiveness isn't mine to give. Sela's family gets to decide that. Rook's. Your sister's. What's mine to give is the finishing. He asked for the finishing."

Maren was quiet for a while. Out on the water, a frigate bird wheeled high and motionless, riding the calm before the weather broke it.

"When Priya publishes," she said, "your captain's name will be in it. Not as a victim. As a hired hand. The divers who brought up the proof will be heroes; the man who trained them will be the villain of the piece. Are you ready for that?"

"No," Cyrus said honestly. "But I don't think I get to choose the version of him that survives. He didn't get to choose what happened to Sela either. Or maybe he did, and that's the point." He closed the logbook. "He left the sentence unfinished because he couldn't face ending it. So we end it for him. All of it. The payments, the charges, the name. Everything."

Maren studied him for a long moment, and whatever she saw in his face seemed to settle something in hers. "Then we'd better survive long enough to publish," she said. "Because the fleet won't wait for the monsoon, and neither will my backers."

As if the sea had been listening, the wind arrived.

It came down the cove in a single long gust, flattening the water, setting the Perdita's halyards snapping, carrying the smell of rain that was still twenty miles out but closing. Auntie Bee appeared at the wheelhouse door with the satellite feed printout in her hand, and Cyrus didn't need to see it to know. He could read weather in his bones the way Elias had taught him to read water.

"Landfall's early," Auntie Bee said. "Twelve hours, maybe less. And Cyrus—" she hesitated, and he saw that behind her, in the wheelhouse, Devi and Tomas had stopped what they were doing and were looking at him. Waiting. The whole crew, gathered without being called, the way families gather. "There's a boat on the horizon. Fishing hull, running lights wrong for a fisherman. Bee says it's her contact. Says the journalist's aboard."

Cyrus stood, logbook under his arm, and looked south toward the bruised horizon where the monsoon was making up its mind—and where, somewhere inside that greying wall of rain, Priya Nair was coming to ask them the only question that mattered.

Not whether they'd sell. Not whether they'd bury it.

Whether they'd finish it.

"We give her the meeting," Cyrus said. "Before the weather closes. Everyone votes after they hear what she has to say—but Elias already voted, ten years ago, in pencil pressure on a page nobody was supposed to find." He tapped the logbook against his palm once, twice, like a man knocking on a door he'd finally decided to open. "We're just catching up to him."

The first drops hit the deck as the fishing boat's lights steadied on the horizon, holding position at the mouth of the cove, waiting.
