## 31. One Tide

Maren Voss had spent her whole life learning to read the weather, and the monsoon's outer bands arrived exactly as promised: a bruised horizon, a wind that smelled of iron and drowned things, and a pressure drop she felt in her teeth before the barometer confirmed it. She stood on the Perdita's aft deck and watched the first grey curtains of rain march across the water toward them like something being deployed rather than fallen.

Behind her, inside the wheelhouse, Auntie Bee was arguing with the radio.

"—repeat, all vessels in sector seven, advisory upgraded to warning, expect sustained winds of forty knots with gusts to sixty by nightfall—"

"Sixty," Tomas said from the doorway. He said it the way a man reads his own diagnosis. "Gusts to sixty."

"We've seen sixty," Devi said, not looking up from the engine room hatch she was cleaning. "We didn't enjoy it."

"We were tied to a dock when we saw it."

Maren let their voices wash over her. It was a strange thing, listening to a crew argue about weather the way other people argued about dinner. Ten years she'd built her operation on the opposite principle—weather was leverage, weather was cover, weather was a tool you used on people who loved their boats more than their margins. The Perdita's people talked about the storm like it was a relative coming to visit. Unwelcome. Inevitable. Family anyway.

Her satellite phone buzzed against her hip.

She'd been expecting it for six hours, ever since the Halcyon Dawn's tender had withdrawn to the fleet ship at dusk and the ship itself had come about, closing to four miles and anchoring with its work lights blazing—a statement, not an anchorage. Nobody lit up a salvage vessel like a carnival unless they wanted to be seen.

She stepped to the rail, out of the wind as much as the rail allowed, and answered.

"Maren." The voice belonged to Aldous Wren, and hearing it was like touching a live wire you'd been assured was dead. Her principal backer. Her architect. For eleven years, the man who'd turned her salvage company from a borrowed skiff into a fleet. "You're well, I hope."

"You sold me out."

"I restructured," Wren said pleasantly. "There's a difference, though I understand it may be difficult to appreciate from where you're standing. Are you somewhere you can talk freely?"

"I'm on the deck of a tug in a building storm. Free is the operative condition."

A pause. On the horizon, lightning stitched inside the cloud bank, silent at this distance, patient.

"The consortium's counsel has been in contact with the naval authority," Wren said. "There's a warrant now. Formal. Your name is on it, along with the Kavanagh crew's. Interference with a restricted maritime site, theft of cultural property, and—" he paused, savoring it or perhaps just reading it, "—conspiracy. The Halcyon Dawn has boarding authority. They're going to come for you tonight, Maren, while the weather keeps your options honest."

"I know."

"Of course you know. You've always known things early. It's why I invested in you." A sound that might have been a sigh, or wind bleed across his end. "Here's what I can still do. The warrant names the manifest and the ledger. Not the divers. Hand over what you recovered—through me, not through them—and the charges against you evaporate before they're filed. You walk away with two million. Retained. Plus your vessel, your licenses, your name. In eighteen months this is a story someone else tells at a conference, and you'll be richer for having survived it."

"And the crew?"

Another pause. Longer.

"The crew is collateral language," Wren said. "I'm sorry. I fought for a carve-out. I got one for you because you are the asset with institutional value. They are—" he searched for the word and found it without difficulty, "—expendable."

Maren looked back through the wheelhouse glass. Cyrus stood at the chart table, one finger resting on Elias's annotated channel, talking quietly with Auntie Bee. Devi had gone below. Tomas was coiling line on deck with the automatic grace of a man who'd stopped thinking about his hands years ago. They were, all of them, doing the small necessary work of staying alive, and none of them knew yet that she'd been weighing their lives against a number since before dawn.

"They're not expendable," she said.

"Everyone is, Maren. That's not cynicism, it's bookkeeping. The only question worth asking is whether you're on the asset side or the liability side of the ledger when the audit comes." His voice softened into something almost paternal, which was how he'd always delivered his worst ideas. "Take the offer. You were never one of them. You know that. You hired them the way you'd hire a drill bit—because they could reach something you needed. Don't confuse proximity with loyalty. It's the mistake sentimental people make, and you've never been sentimental."

That was the sentence that did it. Not the warrant, not the two million, not even *expendable*. It was the ease with which he described her—the fluency of a man who had known exactly what she was for eleven years and had simply never bothered to say it aloud until it was useful.

Because he was right. That was the obscene part. She *had* hired them as a drill bit. She'd found the one crew with the charts, dangled money at a man grieving his captain, and steered them all into a trench. Every step of it had been calculation dressed as partnership, and she'd told herself the dressing made it true.

And then she'd shared Cyrus's gas at thirty meters. She'd watched him give her his last safety margin without hesitation, without arithmetic, and she had understood—in the cold green silence, mouth to mouth, his eyes steady on hers—that there was a category of person who did that, and a category who didn't, and that she had spent eleven years on the wrong side of the line telling herself the line wasn't real.

"Maren?" Wren said. "Are we agreed?"

* * *

The rain arrived ten minutes later, the leading edge of it, fat warm drops that hit the deck like thrown gravel and then thickened into a hissing wall. Maren came into the wheelhouse dripping, and five faces turned toward her—Cyrus, Auntie Bee, Tomas, and Devi up from below with grease still on her knuckles. Priya Nair had gone back to the fishing boat at dusk; the journalist was anchored in the cove's lee, waiting on a window that might not come.

Maren put the phone on the chart table where everyone could see it.

"That was Aldous Wren," she said. "My principal. The money behind the Halcyon Dawn contract. He's also the consortium's man, and he has been from the beginning. There's a warrant. It names all of us. The Halcyon Dawn has boarding authority and they're coming tonight, in this, because the storm is their ally—it grounds us, scatters us, makes us easy to take one boat at a time."

Nobody spoke. Rain drummed the wheelhouse roof like applause from a hostile crowd.

"He offered me a way out," Maren went on. "Two million, charges dropped, my name clean. The price is the manifest and ledger, handed over through him—and the rest of you left holding the warrant alone."

"And you called us together to tell us this why?" Devi asked. Her voice was flat, but Maren had learned to read flatness as armor. "Contract says the find belongs to your backers. Contract says you decide. So decide."

"The contract's void. My backers sold the contract's own objective to the consortium before we ever found the wreck. I've been working for the people who killed those passengers since the day I signed with Wren." She heard her own voice crack on *passengers* and did not try to hide it. "I told myself I was using them. Turns out the usage ran both directions and I was too well paid to check."

Cyrus hadn't moved. He stood with his arms folded, watching her with the same expression he'd worn on the descent—measuring, unhurried, a man reading depth gauges in the dark.

"You could still take it," he said. "Walk down the ladder, take the launch, be on the Halcyon Dawn before the squall line hits. Two million buys a lot of new beginnings."

"Yes," Maren said. "It does."

"So why are you standing here?"

"Because at thirty meters you gave me your gas and didn't ask me anything first." She made herself hold his eyes. "And because I watched Sela's hatch, same as you, and I know what it costs to leave people in the water. Wren taught me the accounting. You taught me the other thing. I'm choosing the other thing. That's the whole speech. I don't have a better one."

The rain intensified. Somewhere forward, a mooring cleat groaned.

Auntie Bee broke the silence by pouring tea. She did it deliberately, loudly, setting five cups on the chart table and pushing one toward Maren with a look that contained neither forgiveness nor rejection—only the assessment of a woman deciding whether a guest was staying the night.

"You'll want to sit," Auntie Bee said. "Storm decisions go better sitting down."

* * *

They sat. The plan assembled itself out of pieces they already owned, the way good plans always did.

Devi laid it out with a grease pencil on the chart. The cove was sheltered but exposed to the channel; once the sustained winds topped forty, the Perdita couldn't hold anchor and couldn't run the reef except through Elias's annotated passage, which no one had ever navigated in a cyclone's outer bands. Priya's fishing boat was smaller, shallower-drafted, and could slip out through the mangrove cut on the cove's far side—a route the fleet's charts wouldn't mark because the fleet had never bothered mapping a fisherman's shortcut.

"So the transfer happens here, tonight, in the worst window we get," Devi said. "Or it doesn't happen."

"And the Halcyon Dawn?" Tomas asked.

"Four miles off, in this sea state. Their big ship can't maneuver close. They'll send the fast tender with a boarding team—six, eight men, maybe the lieutenant again. Twenty minutes to reach us in these seas if they push it, longer if they're careful." Devi tapped the cove's entrance. "Which means our real problem isn't the boarding team. It's the twenty minutes. We need the transfer done and Priya gone before they arrive, and we need them arriving to feel like a waste of their time."

"A decoy," Tomas said.

"A decoy," Devi agreed, and looked at Maren. "Your launch. It's registered to your old company. If it's sitting at the Perdita's mooring when they board, they'll waste their warrant on an empty skiff and a lot of paperwork."

"It's a good launch," Maren said.

"It's a decoy," Devi said again, gently. "Everything's a decoy tonight. That's the job."

Maren thought about the launch—her launch, the first asset she'd ever owned outright—and found, to her mild surprise, that letting it go cost her nothing at all. Eleven years of acquisition, and the only things she couldn't surrender were a sealed strongbox, a dead sister's name, and the memory of shared air in the dark.

"Take the launch," she said.

Cyrus finally unfolded his arms. "One more piece. When they board, they'll search. The manifest can't be aboard either vessel when they do."

"It won't be," Auntie Bee said. All of them turned. She had the waterproof case on her lap, both hands resting on it, and her face wore the serene expression she brought out for customs inspections and funerals. "Priya takes it through the mangrove cut. But she doesn't take it from us at the dock, in the open, under their lights. We pass it on the water. Heaving line, mid-cove, engines idling, rain for cover. I've moved rice through worse."

"In sixty-knot gusts," Tomas said.

"In sixty-knot gusts," Auntie Bee agreed, "which is why we'll do it twice if we have to, and why the case is lashed to a float. You don't throw documents in a storm, children. You throw a line, and the line throws the truth. There's a difference, and the difference is that you can pull a line back."

Devi was already sketching the approach angles. Cyrus pulled the tide tables. And Maren sat with her cooling tea and understood that something had finished dying in her and something else had quietly started—the sensation less like relief than like decompression, painful and correct, bubbles rising through places that had been pressurized so long she'd mistaken the pressure for structure.

Wren would call again. She'd let it ring.

* * *

The call came at 2100, as the barometer fell through the last comfortable number.

She took it alone at the rail, rain hammering her shoulders, the Halcyon Dawn's work lights smeared into long yellow ghosts by the wet air.

"Maren. My window closes at midnight." Wren's voice had lost its warmth; the storm had gotten into it somehow, or maybe the storm had just stripped the varnish. "After midnight the warrant executes and I can't protect you from what follows. This is the last generous act of your career. Take it."

"Aldous," she said, "do you remember the Corvane's passenger list? The real one, the one the consortium buried?"

A silence with weight in it.

"There were ninety-one names," she said. "My sister was the eleventh. Ana Voss. Age nine. You signed the insurance filings—I've read them. Your signature is on the document that declared her drowning an act of God." The rain ran down her face and she let it. "So no. There's nothing you can offer me that costs you more than it costs me. Come take the empty launch. Bring paperwork."

"Maren—"

She ended the call and stood at the rail until her hands stopped shaking, watching the lightning walk closer across the water, counting the seconds between flash and thunder the way her father had taught her when she was small enough to be afraid of storms. Five seconds. Four. Three.

Behind her, the wheelhouse door opened. Cyrus came out, ducking against the rain, and stood beside her without speaking for a while. Two people at a rail in a storm, which was, she supposed, the most honest configuration she'd ever occupied.

"She was nine," Maren said.

"I know."

"Elias knew what was in that hold. Maybe he set the charges himself. And I spent eleven years becoming the kind of person Wren could make that phone call to." She shook her head. "I keep waiting to feel like I've chosen the right side. I just feel wet."

"That's how it feels," Cyrus said. "The right side is mostly just wet, and tired, and still there in the morning." He nodded toward the black water. "Squall line's twenty minutes out. After that, the transfer window opens and closes fast. You ready?"

"No," Maren said honestly.

"Good. Ready people freeze." He turned back toward the wheelhouse. "Come on. Auntie Bee's rigged the float, and Devi wants to argue about knot types before it's too late to argue."

Maren followed him inside, and behind them the first true band of the monsoon came ashore over the reef like a door slamming on the whole world, and out beyond the cove's mouth, running lights low and engines loud, the Halcyon Dawn's tender began its run through the dark.
