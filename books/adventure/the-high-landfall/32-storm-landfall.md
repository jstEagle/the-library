## 32. Storm Landfall

Devi Okafor felt the storm before she saw it—felt it in the deck plates, a low tremor through her boot soles, like the sea clearing its throat.

She was in the engine room at 0340, hands inside the auxiliary generator's guts, when the vibration changed pitch. Ten years of listening to the Perdita had taught her the difference between a machine complaining and a machine warning. This was a warning. She wiped her hands on a rag, climbed the ladder two rungs at a time, and came out into a night that had gone the wrong color.

The horizon south of them was flickering. Not lightning, not yet—just a bruised, pulsing grey where the stars should have been, and above it clouds stacked so high and so fast that they seemed to be building a wall rather than weather. The barometer on the bulkhead had fallen eleven millibars since midnight and was still falling, the needle twitching like something trapped.

"No," she said, to no one.

The monsoon wasn't due for another two days. Every model Auntie Bee had pulled off the satellite feed, every forecast Priya's people had relayed, had put landfall at dawn the morning after next. Devi had built the whole plan around that window. The transfer to Priya's fishing boat. The handoff. The forty miles of open water between the reef and the cove. Two days of margin, which in monsoon season was already nothing.

The sea had decided not to honor the appointment.

* * *

She found Cyrus at the wheel, already awake, already dressed, one hand flat on the chart table like he was holding the boat down by force.

"Tell me I'm wrong," Devi said.

"You're not wrong." His voice was flat and steady, the voice he used when the numbers were bad and he'd already finished being afraid about them. "It's coming in early. Bee's been on the uplink for an hour. The leading edge is moving at twenty-two knots, and it's going to pass directly over us in—" he glanced at his watch, "—five hours. Maybe four."

"Four hours." Devi gripped the back of the chart chair. "The transfer—"

"Has to happen now. Tonight. Inside the window before the outer bands hit, or Priya's boat can't make the rendezvous at all, and after the storm there won't be a rendezvous point left. The cove will be a washing machine for three days minimum. By then the Halcyon Dawn will have whatever's left of its boarding teams organized, or the navy will, or both."

Devi did the arithmetic she didn't want to do. "Priya is forty miles south."

"Thirty-eight. She's already running. Bee called her an hour ago." Cyrus looked up, and in the red glow of the binnacle light his face looked carved out of something older than the man she'd known for a decade. "She's coming into the same storm we're trying to outrun, Devi. She's doing it in a thirty-six-foot wooden hull."

Devi thought about Priya Nair—small, wiry, with the permanent squint of someone who'd spent twenty years reading documents instead of horizons—and tried to picture her in a cyclone's leading edge and failed. "She knows what she's sailing into?"

"She knows. She said—" and here Cyrus's mouth did something that was almost a smile, "—she said the story doesn't file itself."

* * *

By 0430 the whole crew was up and the Perdita had become a single organism with one purpose.

Devi ran the engine flat out—nine and a half knots, which for a converted harbor tug of the Perdita's age was less a speed than a sustained act of violence. The old Gardner diesel hammered below her like a heart that had forgotten how to slow down, and she knelt beside it with a stethoscope and her palm flat on the block, listening for anything that would quit on them in the next six hours. The bearings were hot but holding. The fuel line she'd patched off Tanah Merah was weeping slightly at the fitting; she tightened it, marked it with chalk, and told it, out loud, in the engine room's roar, "You will not die tonight."

Tomas was on deck, lashing down everything that wasn't already lashed, working with the frantic economy of a man who'd grown up on small boats in bad weather and had never entirely left them behind. He'd rigged double lifelines with jacklines the length of the deck, and he clipped Devi's harness tether himself when she came up, his hands quick and sure at the carabiner, not looking at her face.

"Storm rules," he said. "Nobody on deck alone. Nobody off the tether, ever. You go down, you shout once and I come."

"I know the rules, Tomas. I wrote half of them."

"You wrote them for squalls." He finally looked at her, and the wind was already in his hair, and behind him the southern sky was starting to flicker in earnest now, long silent pulses of heat lightning inside the cloud wall. "This is the real thing. I need you to agree out loud, because if I have to come find you mid-storm, I want to remember you promised."

Devi looked at him—this skinny, stubborn deckhand who'd pulled her back from the rail during the squall line three weeks ago and had never once mentioned it since—and she said, "I promise. Storm rules. All of them."

He nodded once and went back to the lashings, and she felt the promise settle over her like the tether itself: not comfort, exactly. Structure.

* * *

Auntie Bee had the chart table covered in printouts and the satellite feed up on the old laptop, its screen the only bright thing in the wheelhouse. She'd drawn the storm's projected track in grease pencil—a fat black arrow aimed at the coast like an accusation—and the leading edge was a smear of red that was going to swallow their position in four hours.

"Here's the truth," Bee said, when Cyrus, Devi, and Maren were crowded around the table. She said it the way she said everything, in the voice of a woman who'd fed sailors for forty years and had stopped softening anything. "The window is small. The outer bands start hitting us around oh-eight-hundred—squalls first, then sustained wind building through the morning. The real core makes landfall on the coast around noon. Between now and oh-eight-hundred, the sea is merely angry. After that, it's murderous."

"The rendezvous point," Cyrus said.

"The deep water anchorage behind the northern reef, twelve miles from here. Priya can make it by oh-six-hundred if her engine holds. We can make it by oh-five-forty-five. That gives us—" Bee tapped the grease pencil against the table, once, twice, "—maybe forty minutes of transfer time before we're all running for shelter. The transfer has to happen at first light, in growing wind, between two small vessels, in open water."

"And if we miss the window," Maren said quietly.

Bee looked at her. Maren Voss had been aboard nine days now, and Devi had watched her go from employer to ally to something more complicated, but she'd never seen her look like this—pale in the binnacle light, her hands flat and still on the chart like she was afraid they'd shake if she let them move.

"If we miss the window," Bee said, "we run for the cove and we shelter through the storm with the manifest aboard, and the Halcyon Dawn knows where the cove is, and after the storm there are warrants with our names on them and nowhere left to hide. Or we run south ahead of it and pray our fuel and our hull and our luck hold longer than the storm's patience." She folded her hands. "I have been through eleven monsoons on this water. I do not recommend the second option to anyone I love."

The word *love* sat in the wheelhouse for a moment. Nobody picked it up. Everybody held it.

"Then we make the window," Cyrus said.

* * *

Devi spent the next hour doing everything an engineer does when the sea is about to try to kill her boat.

She pumped the bilges dry and then dry again. She secured the battery bank with an extra strap. She checked the steering hydraulics twice, then a third time, because a tug that loses steering in a cyclone is not a boat, it's a piece of wreckage with opinions. She lashed the spare fuel drums in the hold, drained a cup of water from the primary fuel filter that shouldn't have been there, and changed the element with hands that were steadier than she felt.

The fear, she noticed, was a strange thing tonight. It wasn't the screaming kind. It was quiet and total, and it had stripped everything down to essentials. She wasn't afraid of the warrants, or the consortium, or the Halcyon Dawn, or the ruin of everything they'd built in ten years of grey-water salvage. None of that existed right now. There was only the engine and the hull and the sea, and the hands—hers, Tomas's, all of theirs—and whether the hands were good enough.

She surfaced from the engine room at 0510 and found Tomas in the galley, where Auntie Bee was producing thermoses of sweet tea with the grim efficiency of a woman provisioning a siege. Tomas was checking the heaving lines—three of them, coiled and flaked, the throw bags repacked so they'd pay out clean even when soaked.

"Throwing lines between two boats in thirty knots," Devi said, sitting down across from him, "is how people lose fingers."

"I know."

"And we're doing it anyway."

"We're doing it anyway." He cinched a knot, tested it, undid it and tied it again. His hands were beautiful when he worked—quick, certain, no wasted motion. Devi had spent years not noticing that. "Flemish flake on the transfer line so it runs without kinks. Bee's sewing the manifest case into a canvas sling with a float collar. One throw, one catch, one pull. If the first throw misses, we fish it out and throw again. If the second misses—"

"Nobody's missing the second one," Bee said from the stove, without turning around. "Because I'm going to be standing next to whoever throws it, and I have raised children and crew, and I have never once missed a head with a thrown object."

Despite everything, Tomas smiled. Despite everything, Devi did too.

* * *

They raised the northern reef anchorage at 0538, in a light that didn't know what it wanted to be.

The sky to the south was black-green and boiling, shot through with silent lightning, and the wind had already come up to twenty knots and was backing around the way Bee said it would, swinging from southeast to east as the storm's circulation reached for them. The sea inside the reef line was choppy but survivable—short, steep, confused waves that slapped the hull and threw spray across the wheelhouse windows. Outside the reef, Devi could see the swell already building, long dark lines marching in from the deep water like an army arriving early.

Priya's boat was already there.

She'd anchored in the lee of the reef, her little wooden hull rising and falling, a single riding light showing, and when the Perdita's floodlight swept across her, Devi saw Priya herself at the helm, one hand on the wheel, the other raised—not waving, exactly. Acknowledging. *I see you. I'm here.*

"Thirty-eight miles in four hours in that," Maren said softly, at the window. "In a wooden hull."

"She wanted the story," Bee said. "Now the story wants her back."

Cyrus brought the Perdita around to anchor upwind of the smaller boat, close enough for a line throw, far enough that neither hull would come together in the chop. It was delicate, ugly work—two anchors, engines holding them against the wind, the distance between the boats breathing in and out with the swell, forty feet, then sixty, then forty again.

"Window check," Cyrus said.

Bee looked at the sky, then the barometer, then her watch. "Ninety minutes. Maybe less. When the first real squall line comes through, we stop, no matter what."

"Devi—engines ready?"

"Both engines ready. Steering's good. Bilges dry." Devi clipped her tether to the jackline and stepped out onto the deck, where the wind hit her like a living thing, shoving at her chest, trying to take her voice. Tomas was already at the rail with the first throw bag, and Auntie Bee had the canvas sling in both hands—the manifest and the ledger sealed in the waterproof case, wrapped, slung, floating, the whole truth of the Corvane in a package the size of a bread loaf.

Priya's boat had come up on its own anchor and was holding, and Priya had moved to her bow with a boat hook and a line of her own, and even at this distance Devi could see the set of her shoulders: small woman, enormous stubbornness.

"First throw!" Cyrus shouted from the wheelhouse. "On my mark—make it count!"

The two boats rose and fell, out of phase, the gap yawing between them. The wind screamed through the rigging. Somewhere south, the sky flickered and flickered and did not stop.

Devi put her hand on Tomas's shoulder and felt him gather himself, and she thought: this is what it comes down to. Not the ledger, not the consortium, not the dead captain and his terrible secret. A rope, thrown through wind, between two small boats, caught by a stranger's hands. Everything they'd risked, everything they'd learned about what they were, hanging on an arc of wet line and a stranger's grip.

The swell lifted the Perdita. The gap began to close.

"Mark!" Cyrus roared.

Tomas threw.

* * *

The line went out flat and fast, the throw bag tumbling end over end across the gap—and the wind took it. Devi watched it happen in horrible slow motion, the bag drifting right, drifting wide, and Priya lunged along her bow rail with the boat hook extended, stretching, the hook's tip chasing the bag across two feet of black water—

—and caught it. Clean. The line came taut, Priya hauled it in hand over hand, and the sling with the case came across the gap on the second heave, slapping against the Perdita's hull, then swinging free, then being guided in by Priya's hook and pulled over the little boat's rail.

Gone. The manifest. The ledger. Ten years of a dead man's secret, in a stranger's arms, on a wooden boat, in the teeth of a coming storm.

Devi realized she'd been holding her breath since the throw and let it out in a whoop that the wind tore to pieces. Tomas was grinning, wild and soaked. Auntie Bee had both fists in the air.

Then Bee's face changed.

Devi followed her gaze south, and the words died in her throat.

The squall line was coming early. It stood across the whole southern horizon—a wall of rain and wind miles high, moving faster than any forecast had promised, its leading edge already white with the sea it was tearing off its own waves. And behind it, faint through the rain, the first real lightning was no longer silent.

"That's not an outer band," Bee shouted, over the rising roar. "That's the storm. That's the whole storm, it's come forward—"

The Perdita's radio crackled. Priya's voice, thin with static and wind, calm as a surgeon: "*Perdita, I have it. I'm running for the river mouth now. You need to be behind that reef in twenty minutes or you won't be behind any reef. Go. Go now.*"

Cyrus's hand hit the engine telegraph. "Devi—all ahead full. Get us out of this anchorage and behind the northern point before that wall hits."

Devi was already moving, already down the ladder, already sliding into the engine room's roar with her hands out for the throttles. The fear was back, but it was clean now, stripped to nothing but hands and lines and trust—her hands on the levers, the lines holding the deck gear, the trust that the old boat under her would hold together for twenty more minutes because she asked it to.

She opened the throttles all the way.

The Perdita surged forward into the darkening water, racing the wall of wind for the shelter of the reef—and Devi, watching the engine gauges climb into the red, saw the oil pressure needle begin, very slightly, to tremble.
