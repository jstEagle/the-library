## 33. The Transfer

The monsoon didn't arrive so much as fall on them.

One minute the Perdita was running before a hard southeast wind with her rails already wet; the next the sky came down like something dropped from altitude, and Cyrus lost the horizon entirely. Rain in sheets, so dense it made its own weather inside the weather. The barometer had been falling all morning—Devi had called it at 0900, said they had maybe four hours of window before the leading edge became the whole storm—and Priya Nair's fishing boat was supposed to be waiting off Tanjung Rhu point.

*Supposed to be.*

"Contact!" Tomas shouted from the bow, one arm hooked through the lifeline, rain hammering his oilskin hood flat against his skull. He was pointing into the grey and Cyrus squinted until his eyes watered and then he saw it: a smudge where the smudge shouldn't be, a boat riding to a sea anchor with her bow into the swell.

"That's her," Auntie Bee said from the wheelhouse doorway, cup of tea welded to her hand as if the storm were an inconvenience to be outlasted rather than survived. "Priya runs a tidy boat. Look how she's holding station."

Cyrus held station too, closing the distance in slow increments, because this was the part that would kill them if they rushed it. Two vessels in forty-knot gusts, hulls rising and falling out of phase, sometimes three meters apart vertically when one was in a trough and the other on a crest. He'd done transfers in weather before. Never with this cargo. Never with this much depending on a throw of a line.

The case sat between his boots on the deck, lashed down with two turns of shock cord. Grey Pelican case, waterproof to whatever depth the manufacturer claimed and beyond anything it would see today, the manifest and the ledger sealed inside in vacuum bags, and beneath them—the thing Devi had insisted on—a copy on a microSD card taped inside a film canister, because redundancy was the only religion Devi practiced.

"Maren," he said over his shoulder.

She was braced against the wheelhouse bulkhead, hair plastered dark across her face, watching the fishing boat come up out of the rain. She'd been quiet since the cove. Since the Halcyon Dawn's boarding teams had mustered on their deck under floodlights and her own backers' warrant had been read aloud over the radio while she stood there listening to men she'd trusted for six years agree to sell her out for a bonus clause. Quiet in the way a sea goes quiet just before it changes direction.

"I'm here," she said.

"You don't have to be on deck for this."

"Yes I do." She wiped her face with the back of her glove. "I've been running from this story for ten years, Cyrus. I'm going to watch it leave my hands."

He nodded once and turned back to the water. There wasn't time for anything else anyway. The window was shrinking around them like a fist closing.

* * *

They came alongside at fifty meters—too far for comfort, close enough to work—and held it, both helmsmen fighting the same confused sea from opposite ends of it. Priya's boat was smaller than the Perdita, a converted longliner with a wheelhouse the size of a closet, but she handled herself well, and the woman at her rail was small and wiry and had tied herself on with what looked like climbing rope.

"Priya Nair!" Cyrus bellowed through the megaphone, and even the megaphone sounded thin against the wind. "You sure about this?"

"If I weren't," she bellowed back, "I'd have stayed home!"

He liked her immediately. Auntie Bee had been signaling her since the reef channel—quiet messages buried in weather reports and fish-market chatter, a code older than any of them—and now here she was, having crossed open water ahead of a cyclone because someone she'd never met told her the truth needed carrying. That took a particular kind of person. The kind who understood that some stories weigh more than the people telling them.

"Tomas! Line!"

Tomas had the heaving line coiled and ready, weighted end wrapped in tape so it wouldn't sting when it landed. He waited for the roll—waited for Priya's boat to rise toward them, closing the vertical gap—and threw.

It was a good throw. It was a beautiful throw, arcing flat through the rain and landing across Priya's foredeck, and the journalist had it in her hands before the coil finished paying out, fastening it to her forward bollard with hands that didn't shake.

"Case!" Cyrus knelt, freed the shock cord, and lifted the Pelican case. It weighed almost nothing. Ten years of silence, two dead divers, a captain's ledger of shame, and it weighed almost nothing. He passed it to Maren, who clipped it to the messenger line they'd rigged through a block on the halyard, and together they ran it out along the heaving line, the case swinging free above the water between the two boats, rising and falling in the gap like a pendulum keeping some terrible time.

Halfway across, a gust hit.

It came off the squall line in a flat white wall, and the Perdita surged forward on the wave face, and the case swung wide, and for one full second it hung directly over open water with nothing beneath it but the kind of sea that doesn't give things back.

Nobody breathed. Cyrus watched the case swing, watched the line hold, watched Priya's crewman—somebody's nephew, by the look of him, nineteen if he was a day—reach out from her rail with a boathook and catch the messenger on the second try and haul it home against the wind.

Then Priya had the case in her arms, and she looked across the water at Cyrus, and even at fifty meters in a monsoon he could read her expression: *this is real now. There's no version of tomorrow where this isn't real.*

"Go!" she shouted, and pointed south, at the black wall of the storm front coming down the strait like a moving coastline. "Get clear! We'll run for the islands!"

"Copy!" Cyrus raised his hand, and Priya raised hers, and that was the whole ceremony. No speeches. The sea didn't hold with speeches.

"Cast off the line," he ordered, and then the world ended sideways.

* * *

Later, reconstructing it, Cyrus could never get the sequence straight. What he knew for certain was the sound: a horn, deep and wrong, not from Priya's boat or his own, and then the shape of the Halcyon Dawn's fast boarding launch materializing out of the rain to the north, running at speed through seas no launch should have been running in, because the fleet had finally decided that a warrant was worth more than a weather report.

They'd launched it from the Halcyon Dawn's davits despite everything—despite the storm, despite the truce in the cove, despite their own captain standing down after reading the ledger. Or perhaps not despite. Perhaps someone aboard the Dawn had never agreed to stand down. Warrants had a way of outranking captains.

The launch came in fast and low, four men visible in the cockpit, and it never had a chance. A gust caught it abeam as it crested a swell, and Cyrus watched—helpless, the way you watch anything at sea—as the hull lifted, tripped, and went over.

Not slowly. Not cinematically. Just *gone*, inverted in half a heartbeat, four shapes flung into the water, and the launch's engine died with a cough, and then there were just heads in the grey, and the grey closing over them.

"Man overboard," Tomas said quietly, uselessly, because the men in the water belonged to the ship that had come to take everything from them.

For three seconds nobody moved. Cyrus felt those three seconds in his chest like a held breath. Every rational calculation said *run*. The window was closing. The Perdita had a crew of five and a hull full of evidence and no obligation on God's green ocean to the men in the water. The men in the water had boarded them under arms eleven hours ago. The men in the water would testify against them if they lived.

Auntie Bee said nothing. Devi's hand hovered over the throttle. Maren stared north with an expression Cyrus couldn't parse and didn't try to.

"Turn us around," Cyrus said.

"Cyrus." Devi's voice was careful. "That's the fleet."

"I know whose it is." He was already moving, pulling the rescue sling from the rail locker, checking the carabiner with fingers that worked on their own momentum. "Turn us around."

And Devi—bless her, bless her stubborn engineering heart—pushed the throttle forward and put the Perdita about.

* * *

The maneuver took four minutes that felt like four hours. Coming upwind to a drift, positioning so the wrecked launch and its survivors fell onto their leeward side, because you always approach from upwind or you'll drift over the people you're trying to save. Cyrus stripped to his thermal layer, clipped the sling to his harness, and stood at the rail while Tomas paid out the tether line.

"You're not diving in this," Devi said.

"I'm not diving. I'm swimming." He looked at her. "Someone has to go in the water, and I'm the best swimmer aboard, and we all know it."

"It's forty knots."

"It's forty knots and four men are drowning." He pulled his hood tight. "Rook and Sela drowned in calm water, Devi. Nobody went in for them either."

That landed. He saw it land, saw her jaw tighten, and then she did the thing that defined her: she stopped arguing and started solving. "Tomas on the tether. Bee on the engine room telegraph. Maren—" a beat, the first time she'd ever given Maren a job, "—spotter. Call the swells for us. You've got eyes; use them."

Maren blinked. Then she climbed to the rail and planted herself there like she'd been crew for years.

Cyrus went over the side.

The water hit him like a slap. Warm—it was always warm, that was the obscenity of these latitudes—but violent, churned white, visibility nothing, the noise of the storm suddenly everywhere and inside everything. He swam for the first head he could see, a man clinging to the launch's overturned hull, and got the sling around him and hauled twice on the tether and felt the Perdita's winch take the load.

The sailor was young, maybe twenty-five, lips already blue with cold-shock despite the warm water, and he stared at Cyrus with pure animal terror—not of the sea, Cyrus realized, but of *him*, of the pirate diver from the warrant, of the man he'd been sent to arrest.

"Easy," Cyrus shouted into the wind. "You're on my line now. My rules. Breathe."

The man breathed.

They got him up. Then Cyrus went back in for the second one, and the second one was worse—he'd gone under twice already, and Cyrus found him by luck and a scream, grabbed a handful of collar, and got the sling on him while the man clawed at his harness hard enough to bruise. "Stop fighting me or we both die!" Cyrus roared, and the man stopped, and the winch brought them home.

Two more sailors had made it to a life raft that had auto-deployed from the sinking launch. The Perdita recovered the raft on a towline, dragging it into the lee of the hull, and then there were four fleet sailors on Cyrus's deck, wrapped in blankets, vomiting seawater, staring at the manifest crew as if the world had been rearranged while they were underwater.

And the fourth—the last one Cyrus hauled aboard himself, the one who'd been clinging to the raft's painter when they found him—looked up from the deck with rain streaming off his face, and Cyrus knew him instantly.

Lieutenant Aris Thorne. Chapter and verse. The boarding party from the reef anchorage, the one Auntie Bee had disarmed with tea and impeccable paperwork while Cyrus hid the manifest in the ballast tank. The officer who had found nothing because he hadn't looked very hard—or hadn't wanted to—and who had signed the inspection log with a neat hand and said, at the gangway, quietly enough that only Cyrus heard: *"Whatever you're carrying, Mr. Kavanagh, I hope it's worth what it costs."*

Now he lay on the Perdita's deck in a fleet blanket, coughing the strait out of his lungs, saved by the man he'd been sent to break.

Thorne looked at him for a long moment. Around them the storm screamed, and the overturned launch sank stern-first into the grey, and somewhere south the monsoon proper was making landfall on a coast full of people who would never know how close the truth had come to drowning today.

"You pulled us out," Thorne rasped.

"You were in the water," Cyrus said. "That was the whole calculation."

Thorne laughed—a short, broken sound, half cough. "My orders were to recover the manifest. By force if necessary." His eyes went to the wheelhouse, to the grey case-shaped absence where the case had been, already fifty miles gone in Priya's arms. "I'd say you've made that difficult."

"The storm made it difficult," Cyrus said. "We just declined to drown you in it."

Something moved behind the lieutenant's eyes—something that might, in a calmer sea, on a calmer day, have been the beginning of a decision. He pulled the blanket tighter and said nothing more, and Cyrus let him keep his silence, because there would be time for whatever Thorne decided later, and right now there was a cyclone coming ashore and a reef field between them and open water, and Elias's charts were folded in the chart table, and Devi was already shouting for everyone to brace.

"Hold on!" she yelled from the helm, wrestling the Perdita's bow around to the southeast, into the teeth of it, toward the only channel through the reef that anyone alive had ever run in weather like this. "Next stop is the other side or the bottom—everyone find something to hold!"

Cyrus found the rail. Found Maren's shoulder beside him, solid and unyielding. Found, absurdly, Auntie Bee pressing a mug of hot sweet tea into the lieutenant's shaking hands, because hospitality was a religion too, and she had never once abandoned the faith.

The Perdita drove into the storm, and the storm received her.
