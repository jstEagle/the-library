## 34. Running the Reef in a Cyclone

The barometer had been falling all night, and by dawn it fell like something pushed.

Devi Okafor stood at the wheel of the Perdita with both hands wrapped around the spokes and her weight braced against a deck that pitched fifteen degrees to port, then twenty to starboard, then gave up on angles altogether and simply dropped out from under her boots. Rain came sideways in sheets so dense they erased the horizon. The wind was reading forty knots steady with gusts into the fifties, and the gusts didn't come one at a time anymore—they came in trains, three and four together, each one shoving the tug's stern around like a hand testing a door.

"Channel marker should be dead ahead," Tomas shouted from beside her, one fist locked around the rail stanchion, the other holding the chart board flat against his chest under a plastic cover that was already failing at the corners.

"There is no channel marker," Devi shouted back. "There hasn't been a channel marker since before we were born. There's Elias's handwriting and there's my eyes and there's the sounder, and right now two of those three are lying to me."

She wasn't being fair to the handwriting. She knew that. But fear made people cruel to the dead, and she was afraid in a way she hadn't been since the squall line off Vellai Atoll—afraid past the point where fear sharpened you, down into the place where it just made your hands stupid.

Elias Kavanagh's annotations ran along the chart in his small, patient capitals, ink gone brown as old blood: *KEEP THE GREEN TO STARBOARD UNTIL THE WATER GOES FROM TEA TO MILK. THEN HARD PORT AND TRUST THE DEEP SPOT. IT'S ONLY 4 FATHOMS BUT IT HOLDS.* Below that, almost an afterthought: *IF RUNNING IN WEATHER, COME IN ON THE BACK OF A SET. NEVER ON THE FACE.*

*Never on the face.* Devi had read that line a hundred times over ten years without understanding it. Now she understood it. The sets rolling off the open ocean were coming through the outer reef like freight trains through a tunnel, walls of green water eight feet high, and if you met one head-on it would pick the Perdita up and introduce her to the coral at whatever speed the sea preferred.

So she waited for the back of a set. That was the whole art of it, Elias had written—the waiting. Anyone could steer. The skill was in knowing when not to.

"Sounder!" Cyrus called from the bow, clipped to the jackline, rain hammering his oilskin hood flat against his skull.

Devi looked down at the depth display, its numbers swimming behind a film of spray inside the wheelhouse glass. Fourteen meters. Twelve. Ten. The bottom rising fast beneath them, the reef shelf shouldering up out of the dark like something surfacing to look.

"Not yet," she said, mostly to herself. "Not yet, old man."

Maren Voss was wedged into the corner of the wheelhouse with Auntie Bee, both of them braced against the chart table, and Devi could feel Maren watching her the way passengers watch a pilot in bad weather—with a hunger for confidence that Devi couldn't afford to feed. Maren had spent three days being the smartest person aboard, the one with the contacts and the plan and the money. None of that worked here. Out here the only currency was hands on the wheel and years in the water, and Maren had neither.

"How far to the cove?" Maren asked.

"Six miles," Devi said. "Through four miles of reef that every chart in the world calls impassable."

"And you can do this?"

Devi let the question sit through one full roll of the deck. Then: "Elias did it twice. In worse than this. And he taught me everything he knew about reading water, which was more than he taught anyone, because I asked more questions than anyone." She adjusted the wheel a few degrees, feeling the rudder answer through the hull like a muscle. "So don't ask me if I can. Ask me when the next set comes, because that's the only thing that matters."

* * *

The truth Devi hadn't said aloud—and wouldn't, not in this weather, maybe not ever—was that she'd been dreading this passage for a different reason than the reef.

It was the charts themselves.

For ten years the annotated charts had lived in the drawer under the nav station, taken out on quiet nights, traced with fingers, argued over. They were Elias the way nothing else aboard was Elias—not the knife, not the logbook, not even the way Cyrus sometimes stood at the rail with his weight on one foot exactly the way the old man used to. The charts were his mind, preserved. Every note was a sentence he'd never gotten to finish saying.

And now she was going to run them through a cyclone, and either the notes would save them or the notes would fail, and if they failed—if the Perdita went onto the coral with all of them aboard and the manifest in the ballast tank—then the last thing Elias ever gave the world would be the thing that killed his family.

She'd told Auntie Bee this once, years ago, drunk on festival rice wine in the galley. *What if his maps are wrong? What if he made a mistake?* And Bee had looked at her with those old, unbothered eyes and said, *Then he made a mistake, and we forgive him, same as he forgave us for everything. That's what family is. But Devi—that man did not make mistakes on water. He made mistakes on land, with money and men and promises. On water he was clean.*

Clean. Devi held onto the word like a handhold as the next set came through.

She saw it building a quarter mile out—a long dark shoulder where the rain momentarily thinned, the sea stacking itself up against the barrier reef and then pouring through the gap they'd been aiming for since midnight. It came at them with a low grinding roar, the sound of several thousand tons of water being squeezed through a hole too small for it.

"Back of the set," she said. "Tomas, engine ready?"

"Engine's been ready since yesterday," Tomas called back, and there was something in his voice beyond the shout—something steadying, aimed at her specifically, and she grabbed onto it the way you grab a line. He'd pulled her out of the water once already this trip. Some part of her brain had apparently decided that meant something permanent, and that part was talking now, calm as a lighthouse: *he's watching, do it right.*

The wave passed beneath them. Devi felt the hull lift, hang at the top of the world for one suspended second with the whole grey chaos of the ocean laid out below, and then the back of the set took them and she put the wheel hard over and drove the Perdita down the face of the retreating water, straight into the gap.

The sounder went nine meters. Seven. Five.

Coral rose on both sides—she could see it now through the churn, pale branches and dark bommies flashing past close enough to touch, close enough that a single degree of error would open the hull like a tin. The tug accelerated wildly as the following sea picked her up, twelve knots, fourteen, the wheel going light in her hands, and every instinct she owned screamed at her to turn away, to run for deep water, to give up.

*Trust the deep spot,* Elias had written. *It's only 4 fathoms but it holds.*

"Hold," she whispered. "Hold, hold, hold."

Four fathoms. Twenty-four feet. For a vessel drawing eleven, it was a corridor of air thinner than a coffin lid, and Elias had found it, measured it, memorized it, and written it down in brown ink for a crew he'd never meet, running a storm he'd never see, carrying a truth he'd paid for with two divers and ten years of silence.

The sounder read 7.3 meters and steadied.

It held. It *held*. The deep spot opened under the keel like a hand cupping them, and Devi felt the bottom fall away to nine meters, then eleven, and the roar of breaking water moved astern, and they were through the outer line and into the lagoon, where the sea was still angry but no longer trying to kill them—just shaking them, the way a dog shakes a rat.

Tomas whooped, a raw animal sound torn out of him. From the corner, Maren Voss exhaled a breath she must have been holding for two full minutes. Auntie Bee reached over and patted Devi's arm once, twice, saying nothing, which from Bee was a parade with brass band.

Devi kept her eyes forward and her hands on the wheel and let herself shake later.

* * *

The inner channel was its own education.

Inside the barrier the swell couldn't reach them, but the wind could, and the wind in a cyclone's leading bands came in squalls that arrived without announcement—one moment forty knots, the next sixty, driving rain like gravel and visibility down to the length of the boat. Between squalls the air went strangely, horribly still, the pressure dropping until Devi's ears popped and the water lay flat and oily, revealing the reef heads beneath them with terrible clarity: gardens of coral standing in eight meters of gin-clear water, beautiful as cathedrals, patient as graves.

She navigated between squalls. That was the method she built, minute by minute, improvising on top of Elias's foundation: run hard during the calm windows, fix position on anything—a reef head Bee called out from the bow, a color change in the water, the shadow of the barrier wall to starboard—then heave to when the squall hit, engine just enough to hold the bow into it, everyone hanging on, waiting it out.

They did this seven times. Devi counted. Seven times the world dissolved into white noise and violence, and seven times it reassembled, and each time she put the nose back on the line Elias had drawn thirty years ago in a hand steadier than hers would ever be.

During the fourth calm window, Cyrus climbed back into the wheelhouse, dripping, and stood beside her at the wheel without touching it. That was the etiquette; the wheel was hers. He watched the water ahead for a while.

"You know what he'd say right now?" Cyrus said.

"He'd say I'm steering too much. Less rudder, more anticipation."

Cyrus huffed something that was almost a laugh. "He said that to me on my first solo run. I was sawing at the wheel like I was churning butter." He was quiet a moment. Rain ticked against the glass. "You're doing it right, Devi. He'd be—" His voice caught, just slightly, on the word he'd been avoiding for ten years and three thousand miles. "He'd be proud."

Devi nodded, because speaking would have cost her something she needed for the next squall. But she filed the words away in the place where she kept things that mattered, next to Bee's rice-wine sermon and Tomas's hand closing around her wrist in the green water off the stern.

*Proud.* From a man who'd spent a decade unable to say his captain's name without his jaw tightening. The manifest had done that—pulled the splinter out, even though the wound was still open and bleeding everywhere. Cyrus had surfaced from that final dive with the ledger against his chest and something rearranged behind his eyes, and in the days since he'd started saying *Elias* again, plain, like a man setting down a weight one joint at a time.

The fifth squall hit harder than the others. It came with a waterspout visible for one awful moment off the port beam—a grey rope connecting sea to cloud, wandering drunkenly across the lagoon a half mile away, and Devi held the Perdita into the wind and watched it and thought, with a strange detached clarity, *I am forty-one years old and I am watching a tornado from the deck of a hundred-and-twenty-foot tug in a cyclone, and my main emotion is that it's blocking my view of the reef head I need.*

Bee appeared beside her with tea in a thermos cap, somehow, impossibly, having made tea in a galley that was pitching like a funhouse. Devi drank it one-handed without taking her eyes off the water. It was scalding and oversweet and perfect.

"Your hands are steady," Bee observed.

"They're not."

"I've known you since you were nineteen and green as spring leaves. I know what your steady looks like and I know what your scared looks like, and right now you're both, which is exactly what you should be." Bee tucked the thermos back under her arm. "Elias used to say the best helmsman is the one who's frightened enough to respect the water and calm enough to think. You're the first helmsman I've sailed with who managed both at once."

"Elias said that?"

"No. I'm making it up. But it sounds like him, doesn't it." And she patted Devi's shoulder and went aft to check on Maren, who was quietly, thoroughly terrified and pretending very hard otherwise.

* * *

Two miles from the cove, the Halcyon Dawn came back into their lives.

Tomas spotted her first—a shape on the northern horizon where no shape should be, grey-white through the rain curtains, big. He raised the binoculars, swore with impressive creativity for a man raised by nuns, and handed them to Devi.

The salvage ship was standing off the seaward side of the barrier, hull-down but unmistakable, her crane silhouette unmistakable even at that distance. Holding position. Waiting.

"She can't come in," Devi said. "Her draft alone—she'd ground on the outer bar before she got within a mile of the gap. Her own charts mark this whole reef system as unnavigable."

"So why is she sitting there?"

Devi studied the shape. The ship wasn't maneuvering. Wasn't launching boats. Just... holding, bow into the seas, in water far too exposed for comfort, like a dog tied outside a shop.

Then she saw the launch.

It came off the Halcyon Dawn's starboard side as they watched—a rigid-hulled inflatable, maybe two of them, small black shapes dropping into the maelstrom and beginning to move, bouncing white water, heading south along the outside of the barrier. Heading for the gap. Or for the coast road that led to the gap. Or for wherever the fleet's boarding teams had decided the Perdita would emerge, because of course they'd figured it out—of course they knew the tug had to come out somewhere, and there was only one somewhere.

"They're going to wait for us at the exit," Cyrus said flatly. He'd come up behind them without a sound. "Or follow us in. Either way."

"They can't run this channel," Devi said. "Not in this. Not in anything. Their coxswains are harbor-trained; they'd fold an RIB on the first set."

"They don't need to run it. They need to be at the mouth when we come out, with a warrant and a boarding team and a navy lieutenant who owes somebody a favor." Cyrus lowered the binoculars. His face in the grey light was unreadable, except it wasn't, not to Devi, not after ten years. Underneath the calm he was doing arithmetic and not liking any of the sums. "How long to the cove?"

"Forty minutes, if the squalls cooperate."

"And how long does Priya need alongside?"

"Ten minutes to transfer. Five if the sea state lets us use the heaving line instead of coming bow-to."

"Then we go in, we transfer, and we're back out through the gap before they've finished their tea." He said it like it was simple, and Devi loved him for saying it that way, and hated him a little too, because it wasn't simple and they both knew it. "Devi. Can you get us out through that gap in this?"

She looked north, at the barrier wall where the sets were still marching through in endless freight-train lines, bigger now than they'd been on the way in, the cyclone feeding itself fat on warm water. She looked at the chart, at Elias's brown capitals, at the little arrow he'd drawn pointing *out*, with the note beside it: *OUTBOUND IS EASIER. THE SEA WANTS YOU GONE. LET IT HELP.*

Let it help. Thirty years old and it sounded like he was standing at her shoulder.

"Yeah," she said. "I can get us out."

* * *

The cove, when they slid into it, was a miracle of ordinary geography: a hook of sand, a stand of wind-bent palms bent nearly horizontal, water gone suddenly, shockingly calm in the wind shadow. Priya Nair's fishing boat was already there, riding to a single anchor, a small woman in a yellow rain jacket standing in its bow with a waterproof case strapped across her chest like a bandolier.

What happened next happened fast and happened well—lines thrown, the case passed hand to hand across six feet of green water, Priya shouting last instructions over the wind (*the uplink goes live at midnight, jurisdictional filing's already done, don't contact me, don't wait for me, just survive*)—and then the fishing boat was moving, threading back out through a side passage Bee had found on an old survey sheet, and the manifest and the ledger were gone from the Perdita forever, traveling toward midnight and a satellite dish and the end of ten years of silence.

Devi turned the tug for home.

They came back to the gap on the back of a set, exactly as the inbound, exactly as written, and it was on the outbound run—in the middle of the deep spot, keel clearing coral by three meters, the whole crew silent with concentration—that the sounder did something it had never done before.

It spiked. Hard. Nineteen meters, then twenty-two, then a hard return at twenty-six that repeated twice and vanished, a shape where no shape should be, sitting in the middle of Elias's deep spot like a stone someone had dropped in a doorway.

Devi stared at the display. Ran the memory back. Played it again.

"Tomas," she said slowly. "Get the sonar picture up. Full history."

He did. And there it was on the screen, ghostly and undeniable: a hard contact, stationary, roughly eighty meters long, sitting in the trench on the seaward approach—the deep water just outside the gap where the fleet's ships had been loitering all day.

Not an RIB. Not a launch.

"That's her," Tomas breathed. "That's the Halcyon Dawn. She's moved. She's—" He checked the plot again, as if the numbers might apologize and change. "Devi, she's inside the five-fathom line. She's *on the outer bar*. She's grounded."

Outside, through a tear in the rain, Devi saw it with her own eyes: the great grey ship, listing hard to starboard, her bow buried to the anchor hawse in the coral bar that every chart—including Elias's, including hers—said was impassable. Surf was already breaking against her flank in long white smashes. And along her decks, tiny in the distance, lights were moving. People. Lots of people.

The wind gusted fifty knots and shoved the Perdita's stern around, and Devi brought her back without thinking, hands and wheel and water all one thing, Elias's voice in her head saying *let it help*—and then Cyrus's voice cut across it, hoarse:

"Devi. Stop the boat."

She looked at him. He was staring at the grounded ship, at the lights, at the sea full of wreck debris already starting to trail downwind, and his face had gone through disbelief and anger and landed somewhere she'd only seen once before, the night he'd decided to pull two drowning sailors out of a cyclone instead of running for shelter.

"They're going in the water," he said. "All of them. Right now, in this."

The Perdita hung in the seaway, engine idling against the storm, three miles of killing reef and forty knots of wind between them and the people who had hunted them across a hundred miles of ocean to take everything they had.

Devi put the wheel over and turned toward the wreck.
