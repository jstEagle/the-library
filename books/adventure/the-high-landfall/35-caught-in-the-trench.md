## 35. Caught in the Trench

Tomas Reyes had grown up on stories of the sea that all ended the same way: someone in the water, someone with a rope, and not enough time. His grandmother told them like warnings. The sea takes what it wants and asks no permission. He'd spent ten years aboard the *Perdita* learning how much of that was true, and how much of it was just an excuse people made for each other.

He learned the rest of it at 0340, in a cyclone, watching a hundred-and-forty-meter salvage vessel try to kill herself on a reef.

* * *

It started as a shape on the radar that shouldn't exist.

Devi had them running the outer channel with Elias's annotations spread across the chart table, her finger tracing the dead captain's pencil marks like she was reading scripture. Forty-knot gusts, gusting fifty in the squall bands. Rain came sideways, hard enough to sting through oilskins. Auntie Bee had wedged herself against the wheelhouse bulkhead with a pot of ginger tea lashed to the counter beside her, because God forbid anyone drown dehydrated.

"Contact," Devi said. "Bearing two-seven-five. Big."

Tomas leaned over her shoulder. The return was fat and slow-moving, plowing straight toward the reef field instead of around it.

"That's the *Halcyon Dawn*," Cyrus said from the doorway. He was soaked to the skin, having just come off deck check, and he didn't even bother wiping his face. "She's following us."

"She can't follow us," Devi said. "Her charts mark this whole approach as impassable. She doesn't have the channel."

"She has our wake," Cyrus said.

And that was the thing about the corporate fleet, Tomas thought later. They'd spent three weeks throwing money and warrants and ROVs at the problem, and when none of it worked, they'd done the one thing money couldn't fix: they'd gotten impatient. The *Halcyon Dawn*'s captain—some ex-navy man with a charter to seize a tug full of divers—had looked at the storm, looked at the radar, and decided that if the little tug could run the reef, so could he.

Except the little tug had a dead captain's hand-drawn soundings and a pilot who'd spent ten years learning to read them.

The *Halcyon Dawn* had a wake and arrogance.

"They're going to hit," Maren said quietly. She stood at the aft end of the wheelhouse, arms crossed, watching the radar like it might change its mind. "They're going to ground themselves on Corvane Reef in a cyclone."

"Good," said Devi.

Nobody answered her. Nobody needed to. They all felt it—the small, ugly satisfaction of it, and the horror arriving right behind it, holding hands.

Because a grounded ship in a cyclone wasn't a victory. It was four hundred tons of steel breaking up in seas that would swallow a life raft like a bottle cap, and it was however many men the fleet had put aboard, most of whom had never heard of the *Corvane*, never seen a manifest, never done anything except take a contract that paid well and asked no questions.

Tomas knew exactly how that worked. He'd been that man once, more or less. Everyone aboard the *Perdita* had been, in one way or another. That was the whole point of the grey zone: you got there by degrees, and every degree felt reasonable at the time.

"Contact's turning," Devi said. "No—" Her voice changed. "Contact's slowing. Contact is—" The return stuttered, smeared, went strange. "Oh, hell. Hard aground. Northern shoulder of the reef, right where Elias marked nine fathoms over coral."

The wheelhouse went very quiet except for the wind screaming against the glass and the rain hammering the roof like gravel thrown by an angry god.

Then the radio crackled. Mayday. Pan-pan first, then mayday, then nothing but static and a voice repeating coordinates in a flat, professional tone that was trying very hard not to shake.

* * *

Here is what Tomas knew about abandon ship in a cyclone, and he knew it the way you know things after you've rehearsed them a thousand times in your head at three in the morning: the life rafts would deploy but not stay deployed. The davits would be useless in those seas. The crew would go into water at twenty-nine degrees Celsius that would still kill them in hours, churned into foam and driving rain, in a debris field of their own ship coming apart around them.

The *Perdita* was eighty feet of tug with a hull Devi had patched twice and an engine room that Auntie Bee called "temperamental" the way other people call volcanoes "active."

"We're not equipped for mass rescue," Devi said. It wasn't cowardice; it was arithmetic. "We have maybe capacity for twenty extra souls if we strap them to the deck, and we're fighting for our own lives out here."

"I know," Cyrus said.

"The fleet sent those men to arrest us. To ruin us. Maybe worse—Maren's backers sold her out, remember? Those boarding teams weren't carrying citations."

"I know," Cyrus said again.

"Cyrus." Devi turned from the radar. Rain-light strobed across her face through the wheelhouse windows. "Say what you're thinking so we can vote on it properly."

Cyrus looked at the radar. At the smear where a ship used to be. At the radio, still hissing coordinates.

"Tomas," he said. "Get the rescue gear topside. Every line, every ring, the Stokes basket, both heaving lines. Devi, bring us about—we'll stand off to leeward of the wreck inside the channel, where the reef breaks the worst of it. Auntie Bee, hot drinks, blankets, everything below that can hold a human being warm. Maren—" He paused. "You can sit this one out. Nobody would blame you."

Maren Voss, who had lied to him, used him, and lost her sister to the same lie that had hollowed out his own life, unbuckled her harness clip and started pulling flares from the locker.

"They're my backers' men," she said. "My name signed the charter. Get me on the searchlight."

Tomas was already moving. That was the thing nobody understood about moments like this, he thought—there was no decision in it, not really. The decision had been made years ago, in a hundred small ways, by a dead captain who taught a salvage crew that you went back for people. Even Rook and Sela's names were proof of it: Elias had gone back for them too late, and it had broken something in him that never healed, and the crew had inherited the break along with the boat.

You went back. That was the whole religion. Everything else was commentary.

* * *

The sea out there was not water so much as moving architecture—walls of it, black and white-capped, folding down on themselves with a sound like buildings collapsing. The *Perdita* pitched and rolled in ways that made Tomas grateful for every hour Devi had spent re-welding the deck rails. He clipped himself to the jackline and worked forward with the searchlight, sweeping the water on the leeward side of the reef where the current would carry survivors if it carried them anywhere.

The *Halcyon Dawn* was a nightmare by flare-light. She'd grounded bow-first on the coral shoulder and the sea was working her amidships, bending her like a man snapping kindling. Her stern section still showed lights. Her life rafts—two of them, orange blisters in the dark—were already away, and already wrong, dragging toward the open sea where no one would ever find them.

"There!" Maren's voice, raw from shouting. The searchlight caught movement on the reef itself—a cluster of figures clinging to the exposed coral shelf where the surf hadn't fully claimed it. Men in immersion suits, some of them, others just in whatever they'd been wearing when the order came. One of them had a line. One of them was trying to throw a line, which was either brave or insane, because a heaving line in forty-knot gusts was a prayer flag.

"Count," Cyrus shouted.

Tomas counted. Counted again. "Twelve on the reef. Can't tell about the water. Rafts are drifting east—they'll be outside the searchlight in four minutes."

"Devi! Can we hold position off the shelf?"

"If I can't, we join them!" But the engines changed note, and the *Perdita* swung her bow into the seas and began the terrible slow dance of holding station in a maelstrom—throttling up, backing down, angling, always moving, never quite losing the fight.

Tomas threw the first heaving line himself.

His grandmother's stories had never mentioned this part: the physics of mercy. How a line weighed down with a messenger weight becomes a whip in crosswind. How you have to lead the target by half a ship's length and pray the gust dies between your throw and the landing. How the first throw goes long, and the second goes short, and the third one lands across a man's chest in the surf and he grabs it with both hands like it's the last solid thing in the universe, which it is.

"One!" Tomas screamed, and hauled.

The man came through the surf like a landed fish, half-drowned, face bloodied, and Cyrus and Maren dragged him over the rail onto the deck, where he collapsed and immediately began vomiting seawater. Fleet-issue immersion suit. Fleet-issue boots. A young face under the hood—younger than Tomas, probably twenty-three, twenty-four, eyes wide with the specific terror of a man who'd been told he was the predator and discovered he was prey.

"Others," the man gasped. "Reef—there's more—officer Voss's people said—"

"We see them," Maren said, and her voice did something complicated. "Keep breathing. We've got you."

They got eleven more.

Some came by line. Two came by Stokes basket floated on a painter when the surf was too violent for swimming. One came by pure luck, swept against the tug's hull in a trough, and Tomas went down the pilot ladder himself—against every rule, against Cyrus's shout, because the man's hands were too frozen to grip and someone had to be there to catch him—and held him against the hull through two waves while Cyrus hauled them both up hand over hand.

The twelfth man was the lieutenant.

Tomas recognized him from the boarding party weeks ago—the polite one, the one who'd drunk Auntie Bee's tea and read her paperwork twice and signed the inspection log with a fountain pen, the one whose courtesy had been a weapon but who had, Tomas remembered now, made his men take their boots off before walking the deck. Lieutenant Anselm Reyes—no relation, though Auntie Bee had teased him about it for days. He came up the last line on his own power, refused the blanket, and stood dripping on the *Perdita*'s deck looking at the men who'd saved him with an expression Tomas couldn't read and didn't need to.

"You didn't have to come back for us," the lieutenant said. The wind tried to eat the words.

"No," Tomas agreed. "We didn't."

* * *

By dawn the storm had shifted into its ugliest phase and the *Halcyon Dawn* was gone—not sunk, not yet, but broken, her stern section awash, her superstructure leaning at an angle that meant the end was a formality. The two escaped life rafts were never found. Tomas tried not to think about that. There had been no one aboard them; the muster had confirmed it; he made Devi confirm it twice, and then a third time, because he needed to hear it.

Thirteen survivors crowded the *Perdita*'s mess and deck, wrapped in blankets, drinking Auntie Bee's tea with shaking hands. Some of them wept without noise. One of them, a bosun with grey in his beard, kept repeating "she folded, she just folded" like a man describing a car crash he'd caused.

And here was the thing Tomas couldn't stop watching: his crew among them. Devi, soaked through, treating a dislocated shoulder with the fleet medic like they were colleagues. Auntie Bee moving down the line of survivors pressing mugs into hands, saying "drink, drink, you'll feel human in a minute" in the tone she used on all drowning creatures. Cyrus at the wheelhouse window, counting heads again, because counting heads was how he prayed.

These were the men sent to destroy them. Warrants in their pockets. Boarding gear in their lockers. Offers of wealth in exchange for betrayal, drafted and ready. And the *Perdita*'s crew had spent the night hauling them out of the sea one body at a time, burning fuel they'd need to escape the storm, risking the boat, risking everything, because that was the job and there had never been a vote.

The lieutenant found Tomas on the aft deck near sunrise, in the strange bruised light between squall bands.

"My orders," Anselm said, "were to board this vessel, secure any documents, and detain the crew for transfer to fleet custody. In exchange for cooperation, certain considerations were offered." He looked out at the wreck of his own ship grinding itself to pieces on the coral. "I want you to understand that I'm telling you this because last night you pulled my youngest man out of the water by his collar, and he's nineteen, and he thought he was going to die, and so did I."

"Considerations," Tomas said. "Meaning money."

"Meaning money, and meaning the charges would be structured so that your captain took the fall alone." The lieutenant's jaw tightened. "I signed off on that plan. I read the file. I believed the summary—that you were pirates, that the documents were stolen property." He turned. "Then I watched you spend your own survival margin on thirteen men who came to arrest you. So my question isn't professional anymore. My question is: what's actually in that case you're protecting?"

Tomas held his gaze. Behind them, through the mess window, Auntie Bee was refilling cups, and Cyrus was talking quietly with Maren, and somewhere below in a sealed ballast tank rode a partial manifest and a letter naming a dead man a murderer—or a mourner; they still didn't know which, and maybe the truth was both.

"It's not treasure," Tomas said finally. "It's the opposite of treasure. It's the bill."

The lieutenant studied him for a long moment. Then he reached into his jacket—Tomas's hand moved before his brain did—and produced not a weapon but a waterproof notebook, and tore out a page, and wrote something on it against the bulkhead.

"When we're picked up—and someone will pick us up, eventually, the fleet will mount a recovery operation—I am going to report that this vessel conducted a rescue under extreme conditions, that no contraband was observed, and that the crew's conduct reflected credit on the maritime tradition." He handed Tomas the page. Coordinates. A cove, marked in his precise navy hand. "That buys you perhaps two days of confusion. After that, my report gets read by people who don't drink tea."

"And why?" Tomas asked. "Why write it that way?"

Anselm looked at the horizon, where the monsoon's heart was still rolling toward land, and at the broken ship, and at the small stubborn tug riding at anchor in the lee of a reef that had swallowed better vessels than both of them.

"Because I've spent six years enforcing the version of events I was given," he said. "And last night was the first time I saw the unedited footage." He tucked the notebook away. "There's a cove eight miles south-southwest. Your cook will know it—it's on the old charts. Storm shelter, deep water, no fleet traffic. If you're wise, you won't be here when the recovery helo arrives."

He walked forward to check on his men, boots quiet on the wet deck.

Tomas stood alone in the grey light, holding nothing, feeling the weight of everything. Below, the engine ticked as it cooled. Ahead lay the cove, and the vote they'd have to take, and Priya Nair waiting somewhere beyond the storm with a satellite uplink and a history of buried stories.

He went to find Cyrus. There were tides left—two, maybe three—and for the first time since this began, Tomas Reyes knew exactly which way they were running.
