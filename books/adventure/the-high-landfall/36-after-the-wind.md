## 36. After the Wind

Maren Voss had spent her whole life learning to read weather the way other people read faces, and what she read in the sudden hush after landfall was not mercy. It was a held breath.

The eye of the monsoon had come ashore early and ugly, and now it sat over the archipelago like a patient animal, a circle of impossible calm maybe forty miles wide. Through it, the sky showed a bruised, luminous grey, and the sea—still running high from hours of violence—had settled into long, exhausted swells that lifted the Perdita and set her down again like something being rocked to sleep.

They had run for the cove on Elias's old annotations, Devi at the helm with her hands white-knuckled on the wheel, and they'd made it in with minutes to spare, the reef breaking white behind them like a wall coming down. Behind them, further out, the Halcyon Dawn had not been so lucky—or rather, her captain had not been so wise. She was aground on the outer shelf, hard enough that even from two miles inside the cove Maren could see her list through the binoculars, could see the orange smudge of her life rafts scattered downwind like spilled paint.

Her rafts. Her people. In the water an hour ago, before Cyrus turned the Perdita around and went back for them, against every argument Maren had made and some she hadn't said aloud.

Now those people were on her deck.

She stood at the Perdita's rail with a mug of coffee she wasn't drinking and watched the survivors arrange themselves along the far side of the deck with the careful, deliberate spacing of men who were not prisoners but knew exactly how close to prisoners they were. Nine of them. Sailors mostly, salvage techs, two of the boarding team who'd come aboard under a warrant with Maren's name on it. One of them—the big one with the split lip—kept looking at Tomas Reyes, who had pulled him out of the water by the collar of his survival suit, and looking away again, and looking back.

The lieutenant was there too. Vasquez, his name was—the one Auntie Bee had charmed with tea and paperwork while the manifest sat in their ballast tank. He stood apart from his own crew, arms folded, watching the cove walls with the expression of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like.

"You should eat something."

Auntie Bee appeared beside her with a bowl of rice porridge, steam curling off it into the cool air. She didn't offer it so much as present it, the way she presented everything, as though refusal would be both rude and foolish.

"I'm not hungry."

"You haven't eaten since yesterday." Auntie Bee's voice was mild, which meant nothing. "Your hands are shaking, girl. That's not fear anymore, that's just empty. Eat."

Maren took the bowl because arguing with Auntie Bee was like arguing with tide tables. The porridge was ginger-hot and too salty and the best thing she'd ever tasted, and she hated herself a little for how fast she ate it.

"Cyrus is below," Auntie Bee said. "With the ledger. He's been sitting with it for an hour."

"I know."

"He hasn't opened it since the second dive."

"I know that too."

Auntie Bee looked at her then, really looked, the way she did perhaps twice a year, and Maren felt it like a searchlight. "You could have taken the payout," the old woman said. "In the wheelhouse, when they offered. You could have walked away rich and clean and let them have us."

"The payout was never mine to take." Maren heard her own voice come out flat. "My backers sold me out. The contract died when they did. What's left is just—" She stopped. Started again. "What's left is the truth and whoever's still standing near it."

"Mm." Auntie Bee collected the empty bowl. "Cyrus is going to show that ledger to the fleet captain. I've watched him decide for the last hour. You might want to be standing next to him when he does it. Not because he needs you there." A pause, precise as a knife tip. "Because you do."

* * *

The fleet captain's name was Okonkwo, and he came up to the wheelhouse soaked to the waist and refusing a towel, which told Maren most of what she needed to know about him before anyone said a word.

He was younger than she'd expected. That was the first thing. The Halcyon Dawn's master had been a voice on the radio for three days—clipped, procedural, faintly bored—and Maren had built a man out of that voice: fifty-some, corporate-polished, a careerist in a pressed uniform. The man climbing aboard her rival's tug was thirty-eight at most, with salt crusted in a short beard and a healing sunburn across his nose, and eyes that moved over the Perdita's patched hull and borrowed gear with something that wasn't contempt. It looked more like recognition.

Devi had insisted on the wheelhouse. Neutral ground, she'd said, and small, and with the door open where everyone could see everyone. So they gathered there in the strange golden-grey light of the eye—Cyrus and Maren and Devi, Auntie Bee pouring coffee nobody had asked for, Tomas leaning in the doorway with his arms crossed, and Lieutenant Vasquez standing just outside it, technically not listening, obviously listening.

Okonkwo took the coffee. Held it in both hands like it was something rare.

"Nine of my people are alive because you turned around," he said to Cyrus. No preamble. His voice was roughened from shouting into wind. "I want that understood before anything else happens in this room. Whatever else is true, that's true."

"Your people would've done the same," Cyrus said.

"No." Okonkwo shook his head once. "That's the thing. They wouldn't have been allowed to. And half of them wouldn't have tried." He drank. Set the mug down on Devi's chart table with exaggerated care, as if deciding where this conversation would live. "So. You have something you want me to see."

It wasn't a question, and it wasn't quite an accusation either. Maren watched Cyrus reach into his jacket and felt her heart do something complicated and unwelcome.

For three days she had managed this operation like a chess game—charters, cover stories, the liaison officer photographing equipment lists, the convenient customs window, every move calculated three tides deep. For three days she had lied to these people by increments, telling them only as much truth as each moment required, and she had told herself it was necessary, and it had been necessary, and it had also been the easiest thing in the world, and that ease was the part she couldn't stop thinking about now. Lying to Cyrus Kavanagh had cost her almost nothing. That fact sat in her chest like swallowed ballast.

And now he was going to hand the whole board to the enemy because nine sailors had needed pulling out of the water, and she found—to her genuine surprise—that she didn't want to stop him.

She wanted to stand next to him while he did it. Auntie Bee had called that one exactly.

The ledger was a slim waterproof case, and inside it the documents were wrapped in plastic and tied with dive slate cord, and Cyrus laid them on the chart table the way you'd lay out a hand of cards you already knew you'd lost. The complete passenger manifest of the Corvane. Two hundred and eleven names. And beneath it, the consortium's payment schedule—dates, amounts, account routing, initials—running backward from the sailing date like a countdown.

"Sela Amari Voss," Cyrus said quietly. "Page four. Third column."

Okonkwo went very still.

"My sister," Maren said. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "She was twenty-six. She was going to take a new job in Port Callum. The consortium scuttled that ship for the insurance money with passengers aboard, and they paid a diver to confirm the charges were set, and then they paid a navy liaison to lose the survey report, and then they paid a salvage firm—" she gestured at the bulkhead, at the direction of the grounded ship, at the whole apparatus of it, "—to make sure no one ever got to the wreck first. Your firm. Your charter. You've been the last pair of hands on a ten-year cover-up, Captain, and I don't think you knew. But I think you're smart enough to know now."

The wheelhouse was silent except for the creak of the hull and, distantly, the sound of the storm rebuilding itself beyond the eye wall—a low rolling mutter, like something turning over in its sleep.

Okonkwo read. He read the way Devi read sonar returns, methodically, top to bottom, twice. When he reached the page with the diver's name on it—Elias Kavanagh, payments received, dates matching—he looked up at Cyrus with an expression Maren couldn't parse until she realized what it was: it was the face of a man watching someone else's worst day arrive.

"He was your captain," Okonkwo said.

"He was my captain."

"And this says he helped sink it."

"This says he took money to help sink it." Cyrus's jaw worked. "There's a difference. It took me ten years and a flooded cargo deck to learn what it is, and I'm still not sure I can explain it. But the full story comes out either way. A journalist has the copies. Priya Nair. She publishes from a jurisdiction your employers can't touch, and she publishes within the week. The only variable left is what side of it you're standing on when it breaks."

Okonkwo straightened. For a long moment he looked at the ledger, then at the porthole, where the Halcyon Dawn lay aground and dying on the outer shelf, her crane arm bent like a broken finger.

"You understand what you're asking," he said. "If I stand down, I don't get to un-stand-down. My company will disown me before the story even runs. There are people above me whose names might be in that book, or adjacent to names in that book, and they will not forgive this."

"I know," Cyrus said.

"I'm asking if *he* knows," Okonkwo said, and turned to Maren, and she understood suddenly that the whole conversation had been angled toward her from the start, that the radio voice and the warrant and the boarding party had all been one thing and this was another, and that he had known who she was—Voss, the sister, the name on page four—for longer than any of them had guessed.

"I know," Maren said. "I built half of this machine, Captain. I hired these people to do a job I didn't explain, using money from backers who sold me out the moment the risk got real. If anyone here understands what standing down costs, it's me." She put her hand flat on the ledger, over the manifest, over Sela's name. "Two hundred and eleven people drowned believing the company that booked their passage would spend anything to save them. The company spent nothing. Somebody has to be the first one who spends something instead. It might as well be us. We're the ones who know better."

Okonkwo looked at her for a long time. Then at Cyrus. Then, briefly, at Lieutenant Vasquez in the doorway—who gave a single nod, small and formal and final, the nod of a man surrendering a private argument he'd apparently been having with himself since the ballast tank.

"All right," Okonkwo said.

Just that. All right. He picked up his coffee and finished it like a man closing a file.

"My ship is aground and my charter is void and my career is over as of this conversation," he said, "so I'd appreciate it if your cook kept the coffee coming, because I intend to sit in this cove until the eye passes, and then I intend to spend the rest of my life testifying."

* * *

Afterward, the strangest hour of Maren Voss's life happened all at once and very quietly.

It happened in the galley, where Auntie Bee fed nine enemy sailors rice porridge and interrogated them about their families with the cheerful ruthlessness of a customs officer, and the big one with the split lip—his name was Ferro, and Tomas had saved him, and neither of them could look at each other without something passing between them that had no name yet—asked for seconds and got thirds.

It happened on deck, where Devi and the Halcyon Dawn's chief engineer discovered, within ten minutes, that they had both cursed the same manufacturer of decompression computers, and became friends about it, the way engineers do, instantly and permanently.

It happened in the wheelhouse, where Cyrus slept sitting upright in the corner chair with the logbook open on his knee, and Okonkwo stood watch at the window without being asked, and neither man acknowledged the arrangement because acknowledging it would have required words neither of them had.

And it happened at the rail, where Maren stood alone with the last of the coffee, watching the light change inside the eye.

The storm wall was visible now all around them, a colossal grey cylinder turning slowly somewhere past the headlands, and the light inside it had gone the color of old pearl. Birds had come into the cove ahead of the wind—terns, dozens of them, riding the calm the way they always did, resting in the middle of the disaster because the middle was the only place to rest. She counted them without meaning to. Eleven. Twelve.

"You know what I keep thinking," Cyrus said, materializing beside her the way he did, soundless, a habit from a decade of moving around a small boat. He didn't look at her. He looked at the birds. "Elias mapped this channel years before the Corvane ever sailed it. He knew the water here better than the hydrographic office. And he used all that knowledge to help sink a ship in it." He turned the mug in his hands. "And then he spent ten years diving wrecks the navy preferred forgotten, teaching me everything he knew, keeping us alive, keeping us honest-ish. Like he was trying to pour it all back."

"Do you forgive him?" Maren asked. She hadn't planned to ask. It came out anyway, the way true things did now, in this strange pressure-less light.

"I'm working on it." A pause. "You?"

She thought about Sela at twenty-six, laughing on a ferry dock, waving with both arms. She thought about the escape hatch, and what they'd found wedged in it, and the knife, and the unbearable arithmetic of that last night—which of them had stayed, which of them had cut, whether love and betrayal could wear the same hands. She thought about how she'd spent ten years sharpening herself into an instrument for finding this wreck, and how finding it had not brought anyone back, and how the only thing it had actually done was put her on this deck, in this light, next to this man, holding warm coffee in a storm's dead center surrounded by former enemies eating porridge.

"I think forgiveness is like the eye of a storm," she said finally. "It comes to you. You don't steer for it. You just try to be somewhere it can find you."

Cyrus huffed—not quite a laugh, but related to one. "Auntie Bee say that?"

"Auntie Bee said eat the porridge. The rest is mine."

Beyond the headlands, the western wall of the eye was beginning to lean. Maren felt it before she saw it—the pressure shifting, the terns lifting off the water all at once, the sea's long swells shortening as the far side of the monster started reaching for them. Devi appeared in the wheelhouse doorway and didn't say anything, just tapped the barometer twice with one knuckle, and Auntie Bee began stacking bowls, and Okonkwo raised his voice and his crew began moving to lines, and the truce, such as it was, packed itself up to survive the second half of the storm.

But before the wind came, Cyrus caught her arm. Just briefly.

"When we clear the reef," he said, "there's a memorial. Rook and Sela. We read the names into the water. It's not much. It's what we've got." His eyes went to the west, to the rising wall of grey. "After that—Priya's boat makes its rendezvous tomorrow night, storm or no storm. And after *that*, Voss—"

"After that," Maren said, "we find out what the world does with the truth."

The first gust hit the cove like a door slamming, and the terns scattered, and the Perdita leaned into the building sea with nine strangers and one ledger aboard, and ran.
