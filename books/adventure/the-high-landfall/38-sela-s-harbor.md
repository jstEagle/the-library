## 38. Sela's Harbor

The monsoon left the way it came—without apology and without warning. One morning the swell simply lay down, the barometer climbed, and the sky over Tanah Merah went that washed, rinsed blue that only follows a week of violence. Devi stood on the Perdita's aft deck with her coffee and said, "Window's open. Three days, maybe four."

Cyrus had been waiting for exactly that sentence since the night they'd tied up in the cove with the Halcyon Dawn's survivors sleeping on their deck plates.

Nobody had said the word *memorial* out loud. It didn't need saying. Ten years ago Elias had forbidden one—had forbidden the names entirely—and for a decade the crew had honored a dead man's silence like ballast they didn't dare shift. Now the manifest was on every front page from here to Geneva, the warrants were ash, and the silence was the only thing left of Elias's orders worth keeping or breaking. Cyrus broke it at breakfast.

"Tomorrow," he said. "The reef. Both of them."

Auntie Bee put down her ladle. Tomas looked at his boots. Devi nodded once, slow, the way she nodded when a weld held under pressure.

"Both," she agreed. "Rook never got anything else right either."

That was as close to a eulogy as any of them could manage before noon.

* * *

They spent the day preparing, because preparation was the only liturgy this crew had ever practiced. Auntie Bee cooked—not provisions, but offerings: rice packed tight into banana leaves, sweet bread wrapped in oilcloth, a jar of the plum wine Rook used to smuggle aboard in the bilge where nobody officially looked. She arranged them in the galley like instruments before surgery.

Tomas built two markers. He was no carpenter—the man could splice wire but couldn't cut a straight board—but he worked all afternoon in the shade of the wheelhouse with a handsaw and a piece of teak salvaged from the Corvane's rail itself, wood that had waited sixty meters down for ten years to become something other than wreckage. By dusk he'd shaped two simple tablets, sanded them smooth, and burned letters into them with a hot nail: **ROOK**. **SELA**. No dates. Dates were navy thinking; the sea didn't keep calendars.

Maren arrived at slack water in her own small boat, uninvited and unsurprising. She came up the ladder carrying a canvas bag and stopped at the rail as if asking permission to cross.

"I wasn't sure you'd want me," she said.

Cyrus considered it. A month ago he'd have thrown her bag back over the side. But Maren Voss had shared his regulator at thirty meters, had given him the last honest hour of his captain's life in a flooded cargo deck, and had then stood on the Perdita's deck while Priya Nair photographed the ledger and said, "My sister's name is on page three. Make sure they spell it right." You didn't throw a woman like that overboard. You let her aboard.

"There's room," he said.

She opened the canvas bag. Inside was a folded paper, soft with age, and a child's hair ribbon gone grey with salt. "Nadia's," she said. "From her last letter. I've been carrying it since I was nineteen." She paused. "I thought maybe the water should have some of it."

Cyrus looked at the ribbon a long moment. Then he took the paper, read the few lines—a girl writing home about the sea, excited, ordinary, doomed by a date on a shipping schedule—and handed it back.

"Bring it down yourself," he said. "If you can."

* * *

They dove at first light, all six of them, which was its own kind of miracle. The Perdita anchored inside the reef shelf, over the trench where the Corvane lay at sixty-two meters, and the morning was so clear that from the surface you could see the pale ghost-shape of the hull below, like a ship remembered rather than seen.

Devi ran the dive plan herself, and she ran it conservative—deeper margins than the job required, gas to spare, a schedule written on a slate and agreed to by every diver before anyone touched water. No heroics. The whole point of the day was to come back up.

"Ten minutes on the wreck," she briefed, tapping the slate. "Twenty if someone's struggling. We are not salvage crews today. We're just people saying goodbye." Her voice caught on the last word, and Tomas put a hand briefly on her shoulder without looking at her, and that was the whole ceremony of it.

Cyrus went down first.

The descent through clear water was nothing like the working dives—no narcotic rush of urgency, no clock screaming in his ears, no fleet broadcast crackling on the radio. Just the blue deepening around him, the light going from gold to green to that violet dark where the Corvane waited, and his own breathing loud and steady in his ears. He'd made this drop four times now, twice for evidence and once for Sela, and each time the wreck had been a problem to solve. Today it was only what it was: a grave.

He settled onto the cargo deck near the breach—the hole punched inward, the wound that had told the truth no official report ever would. The coral had taken another season's purchase. What had been bone-white in the floodlights was softening toward cream and rose, the reef doing what reefs do, folding ruin into itself until ruin became architecture. Fish moved through the superstructure the way passengers once had, indifferent, at home.

Tomas descended beside him carrying the markers. They fixed them to the broken rail above the breach with stainless clamps Devi had machined from scrap—teak tablets standing upright against the sky-filtered light, facing the open sea. Rook. Sela. The bubbles rising off them silvered the letters for a moment, then cleared.

Auntie Bee came down slower than everyone, her age and her dignity both insisting on it, and when she reached the deck she knelt in the silt—which no trained diver ever does—and placed the banana leaves and the bread and the jar of plum wine in a careful row against the rail. Silt bloomed around her knees and hung in the water like incense. Through her regulator her face was composed, ceremonial, and when she finished arranging the offerings she pressed her palm flat against the teak tablet marked SELA and held it there for the length of three full breaths.

Maren came down last. She hovered at the edge of Cyrus's vision, uncertain, until he gestured her in. She swam to the rail, unwrapped the oilcloth, and let the paper go. Nadia's letter turned once in the current, caught the light, and drifted through the breach into the dark of the hold—going where Nadia had gone, finally, delivered ten years late. The ribbon followed, unspooling behind it like a thin grey wake.

Then Devi did the thing they'd all known was coming and none of them had rehearsed. She pulled her regulator, there at sixty-two meters, insane and deliberate, and spoke into the water.

"Rook Andaya." Her voice came out in a burst of bubbles, half-lost, and she replaced the reg, breathed, pulled it again. "Sela Marchetti."

Tomas took the next name even though it was the same two names. "Rook Andaya. Sela Marchetti."

Auntie Bee. Maren, her eyes streaming in the salt water, whether from pressure or grief impossible to tell. Cyrus last. He pulled his own regulator and gave the water the names, felt his voice dissolve into bubbles almost before it left him, and understood that this was how it had always needed to be done—not spoken to people, who forget, but spoken to the sea, which keeps everything.

Sixty-two meters down, six voices reading two names into the water, over and over, until the dive tables said stop.

They ascended slow as smoke.

* * *

On the surface, in the interval before the second dive, nobody talked much. Auntie Bee brewed tea in the galley and brought cups up to the deck, and they drank them watching the flat sea hold the wreck's shadow beneath it. Tomas sat close to Devi without touching her, which after everything was its own kind of touching. Maren stood at the bow rail alone, and Cyrus let her.

"You could have told us about the letter years ago," he said eventually, coming to stand beside her.

"And you'd have done what? Punched me?" She smiled without humor. "You'd have been right to. I spent twenty years building a case I couldn't file, and then I found the one crew crazy enough and charted enough to get there, and the only way in was to buy you." She turned the empty cup in her hands. "I told myself the money was clean leverage. It wasn't. It was cowardice with a contract number."

"It worked," Cyrus said.

"That's not the same as being right."

"No," he agreed. "But it happened. And she's named now. On the record and on the rail." He nodded down at the water. "Whatever else is true, that part's done."

Maren looked at him for a long moment, and whatever she saw made her nod and turn back to the sea, and neither of them said anything else, because some reconciliations are completed better in silence than speech.

* * *

The second dive Cyrus made alone.

Devi argued with him on the ladder—of course she did—but the plan was already filed: shallow profile, forty meters max, twenty minutes, line and stage bottle, Tomas on standby at the trapeze. A maintenance dive, she called it, glaring. He called it the same thing, and she let him go, because they both knew what he was actually going down to do and because some errands can't be delegated.

He carried the knife.

It had lived in the strongbox locker beside Elias's logbook for a month now, and Cyrus had handled it exactly once—enough to clean the corrosion from the blade and see that the handle wrappings were still sound. A diver's knife, heavy-bladed, plain. The knife that had been found wedged in the escape hatch beside Sela's remains, and whose meaning had nearly killed him: *Elias cut her line*, or *Elias stayed*. Two readings, one object, and for a month the ambiguity had sat in his chest like a swallowed anchor.

But the final entry in the logbook had settled it—or as much as anything could be settled. The sentence completed in different ink: *If you're reading this, finish it.* Different ink meant later. Later meant Elias had lived past that night, past the payments, past the lie, and had spent ten years trying to buy back a debt that couldn't be bought back. And a man who stays in a flooded hatch with a dying diver doesn't cut her line. He cuts her harness free so she won't be trapped. Or he cuts his own air line to share hers. Or he simply refuses to leave, and the knife is the last tool of a man trying everything before accepting the one thing left.

Cyrus would never know which. The sea kept that answer along with everything else. But he'd stopped needing to know. That was what the manifest had bought—not certainty, but permission. Permission to believe the best version without feeling like a fool for believing it.

He dropped down the shot line through the green water, past the thermocline's cold shoulder, to the wreck. The tablets stood on the rail above him, small and bright against the dark. He swam the length of the cargo deck slowly, fins barely moving, past the breach, past the strongroom door hanging open like a mouth done speaking, to the escape hatch where the worst of it had lived.

The hatchway was quiet now. Coral had begun its patient work along the edges. He hooked himself to the rail beside it with a short tether, took out the knife, and held it in both hands.

"Elias Kavanagh," he said into his regulator, voice lost to bubbles, which was fitting, because the conversation had always been one-sided anyway. "I don't know what you did in this hatch. I know what you did after—for ten years, in the dark, alone, too ashamed to tell us and too decent to run far enough that we'd never find out. You mapped the channel so somebody could get here someday. You finished your sentence." His throat tightened around the mouthpiece. "We finished it for you."

He wedged the knife into the rail of the escape hatch, hilt outward, blade seated deep in the old corrosion—the way a man leaves a tool he intends to be found, or a flag planted, or a debt paid. Then he rested his gloved hand on it for a long moment, breathing, the wreck holding still around him as if it were listening.

When he let go, the knife stayed.

He ran the ascent exactly per the tables, every stop honored, and surfaced into late gold light with Tomas hauling him over the gunwale and Devi already checking his gauge numbers with maternal suspicion. Auntie Bee handed him tea. Maren watched from the wheelhouse steps and said nothing, which was the correct amount.

"Done?" Devi asked.

"Done," Cyrus said.

He looked back at the anchorage, at the pale shadow of the Corvane below its two small markers, at the reef holding all of it—wreck, names, offerings, knife—as gently as it had held its secrets. Grief didn't end, he understood now. It just finally got a place to live, instead of riding everywhere with you in your chest. They'd given it the best harbor they knew: sixty-two meters of quiet, and the truth moored alongside it.

Auntie Bee came up beside him at the rail with the last of the pot.

"Weigh anchor in the morning?" she asked.

Cyrus breathed the salt air, tasting the end of something ten years long. Behind him, Devi was already unrolling fresh charts on the deck table, and Tomas was coiling lines that didn't need coiling, and the horizon north of the reef lay open and ordinary and waiting.

"Yeah," he said. "Morning."
