## 39. The High Landfall

The contract came in on a Tuesday, which felt right. Honest work, Cyrus had learned, arrived without drama. It was the other kind that showed up at midnight with a wire transfer and a name nobody said aloud.

*Pipeline survey, sector seven, Tanah Merah shelf,* the email read. *Multibeam mapping, video transects, sediment cores where marked. Six weeks, weather permitting. Rate: standard commercial.*

Devi read it twice, then read it aloud to the wheelhouse like she was announcing a birth. "Standard commercial," she repeated. "Do you know how long it's been since someone offered us standard commercial?"

"Since never," Tomas said from the doorway, a wrench in one hand and suspicion all over his face. "What's wrong with them?"

"Nothing's wrong with them. They're a gas company. They're boring."

"Boring people don't hire us."

"We're cheap," Devi said. "And we're available. And apparently we have references now." She turned her screen toward him. There it was, three lines down: *Referred by M. Voss, Voss Maritime Consulting.*

Cyrus took the tablet and looked at the referral for a long moment. Six weeks ago he'd have read that name and felt his jaw tighten. Now it did something else — something quieter, harder to file.

"She's consulting now," he said.

"She's been consulting since the story broke. She's got an office. With a door." Devi's grin was small and sharp. "Auntie Bee saw it. Says there's a plant in it. A real one, not plastic."

"A plant."

"That's what I said."

He handed the tablet back. Outside the wheelhouse glass, the Perdita sat at her berth looking better than she had any right to — new paint below the waterline, the patched hull section welded clean, the sonar dome replaced with equipment they'd actually paid for, with receipts. The survey contract money hadn't made them rich. It had made them legitimate, which was its own strange wealth, and one Cyrus was still learning to stand upright under.

"Tomas," he said. "How's the port engine?"

"Honest question?"

"Always."

"It runs like a man with something to prove. Which, fair." He spun the wrench once, caught it. "I'd give us six weeks of pipeline before she complains. Eight if I baby her."

"Baby her."

"I always do. That's my whole personality."

* * *

They took the contract out of Tanah Merah on a morning so clear the sea looked manufactured, all glass and glitter, the kind of water that made diving feel like a courtesy rather than a negotiation. Auntie Bee had the galley stacked with provisions and the chart table cleared of everything except the working charts — the new ones, printed, unannotated. Elias's old set lived in the drawer beneath, wrapped in oilcloth. Cyrus hadn't opened the drawer in weeks. He found he didn't need to. The channel was in his hands now, in his sleep, in the way he read water. That was the inheritance that mattered, and it wasn't going anywhere.

The first week was exactly as advertised: mind-numbing. They ran lines back and forth over the shelf while the multibeam painted the seafloor in colors, and Devi narrated the sediment like a sports commentator, and Tomas invented a game called Pipeline Bingo that involved spotting things on the sonar that were definitely not pipelines. Auntie Bee won every round by claiming she could see the pipeline's feelings.

"You can't see feelings on side-scan," Devi told her.

"I see yours just fine from up here."

It was, Cyrus thought, the most boring work he'd ever been paid fairly to do, and he caught himself smiling about it at odd hours — at 0400 when he took the watch and the horizon was a clean line, at noon when the only thing anyone was racing was lunch. Ten years of measuring life in fathoms, in bottom time, in how deep you could go before the sea started making decisions for you. Now he measured in miles again. Miles of line run, miles logged, miles between the Perdita and everyone who wanted anything from them.

The miles led somewhere. That was the part that kept catching him off guard. For a decade the miles had led away — away from the Corvane, away from the night Rook and Sela didn't come up, away from whatever Elias had carried in the sealed locker and the last decade of silence. Every heading was a heading chosen to avoid something. Now the headings were just headings. North-northwest along the shelf, four knots, nothing down there but sand and pipe and the slow honest business of earning a living.

On the ninth day, Maren's boat came over the horizon.

* * *

She ran a thirty-foot center console now, which Cyrus found both funny and fitting — the woman who'd once moved money like weather, reduced to a fast little boat and a cooler full of drinks. She came alongside with the easy seamanship he remembered from certification days, when they'd been two hungry kids trying to out-dive each other in a quarry in Wales, and she stepped aboard the Perdita carrying a document case and no entourage at all.

"Liaison officer?" Cyrus asked. "Should I be photographing your equipment list?"

"My what?" Then she got it, and laughed — a short, real laugh, one of the ones that had gotten rarer and then more common again over the past month, like a tide coming back in. "God. No. The case is contracts. Actual ones. You want to hear something insane?"

"The suspense is killing me."

"There are three more offers on my desk. Legitimate ones. Survey, environmental monitoring, one wreck-preservation job for a museum — a *museum*, Cyrus, they want to *preserve* a wreck instead of strip it." She set the case on the chart table and looked around the wheelhouse like she was checking whether any of this was contagious. "Your name came up in two of them. Not mine. Yours. 'The Perdita crew,' they said. Like it's a brand."

"We're a brand."

"You're a brand. I'm a consultant." But she said it lightly, and there was no flinch behind it, and Cyrus understood that the difference between them had finally closed to something survivable.

Auntie Bee appeared with tea before anyone asked, because Auntie Bee always knew who was aboard before the gangway touched. She set two cups down, looked at Maren for a long moment — the same look she'd given her across the wheelhouse during the confrontation, the look that had weighed her sister's grief against their captain's lie and found both true — and said, "You've been eating."

"I've been working."

"Eating," Auntie Bee repeated, in the tone of a woman whose diagnosis was final, and went back to her galley.

Maren watched her go. When she turned back, some of the polish had dropped away, the way it did lately in small doses, like she was practicing being a person in front of them. "I'm not here about the offers. Those can wait." She opened the case and took out a single sheet. "I'm here about this."

It was a partnership agreement. Not a charter contract, not a hiring paper — a partnership. Two names at the bottom, equal lines, equal shares. Her name was already typed above one signature block. The other block was blank.

Cyrus read it twice. "Voss Maritime and Perdita Salvage."

"Joint venture. Shared risk, shared profit, shared blame." She tapped the page. "I spent ten years buying people, Cyrus. Brokers, divers, informants, one very expensive naval liaison. It worked right up until the day my backers sold me to a boarding party." Her voice stayed level, but her hand didn't quite, and she flattened it against the table. "I'm done owning things. I'd rather own half of something with people who turned back in a storm to pull sailors out of the water. Including sailors sent to arrest them."

"They weren't sent to arrest us. They were sent to take the manifest."

"And your crew pulled two of them out of a capsized launch anyway. I was there. I did the math on that for about three weeks straight." She slid the paper across the chart table. "I'm not asking you to trust me. I'm asking you to structure things so you don't have to."

That, Cyrus thought, was the most honest sentence anyone had ever put in front of him on a boat.

He didn't sign it that day. He made her stay for dinner — Auntie Bee's fish curry, the good batch, the one she only made when something needed celebrating or forgiving — and he listened to Maren argue engine theory with Tomas until Tomas declared her "annoyingly correct," and he watched Devi catch herself liking her and pretend she hadn't. And after dinner he sat alone in the wheelhouse with the unsigned page and the drawer with the oilcloth, and he thought about belonging.

He'd inherited his place on this crew. Fifteen years old, orphaned into Elias Kavanagh's shadow, given a bunk and a role and a family the way you're given a lifeboat — gratefully, and without ever quite believing you'd earned the seat. Everything since had been payment on a debt he couldn't name, to a dead man he couldn't question, for sins he'd spent a decade not looking at directly. The loyalty had been real. So had the cage around it.

But this — the blank line, the equal share, the boring legal pipeline waiting on the shelf — this wasn't inheritance. Nobody was handing it to him because of his surname. They'd earned it. All of them, together, in a storm, with a waterproof case and a heaving line and a choice that no contract had required. Belonging you earned held differently than belonging you were given. It fit better. It didn't chafe.

In the morning he signed it at the chart table with the whole crew watching, and Auntie Bee brought out a second pot of tea like it was champagne, and Maren Voss — partner, not employer — shook every hand aboard including the cook's, which took nerve, and got hers nearly crushed by Tomas for sport.

* * *

The monsoon season settled into its long rhythm, and the Perdita learned to work inside it. They surveyed between squall bands, dove the shallow transects in the morning windows when the water lay flat, and spent the afternoons in the wheelhouse reviewing data while rain hammered the deck plates like applause. Cyrus dove more in those six weeks than he had in years, and for the first time since he could remember, the dives were just dives. Forty feet of green water, a pipeline gleaming in his torch beam, a crab giving him attitude. No sealed manifests. No holes punched inward through steel. No names waiting in escape hatches.

He still thought about Sela. He expected to for the rest of his life. But the thoughts had changed shape — less like wounds reopening, more like scars getting interviewed. He'd left the knife on the wreck's rail, and in his mind's eye he could still see it resting there in the coral dust, and he'd found that the image didn't hurt the way he feared it would. It hurt the way a grave should. Which is to say: it held.

Elias, though. Elias was taking longer, and Cyrus had decided that was allowed. Some mornings he woke up angry at the old man all over again — the payments, the coded logbook, the decade of silence that had let a lie grow roots through all their lives. Other mornings he woke up grateful for the annotations on a chart that had threaded them through a reef field in a cyclone, for the finished sentence in different ink: *If you're reading this, finish it.* Both men existed. Cyrus was learning to hold both without dropping either, and he suspected that was the actual work of loving anybody, alive or dead.

On the last day of the contract, with the final data package transmitted and the client's payment confirmation sitting in Devi's inbox like a small miracle, the crew gathered on the aft deck and did nothing at all. That was the celebration. Nothing. Tomas strummed a guitar badly. Devi narrated the sunset like sediment layers. Auntie Bee fried something she refused to name. Maren sat on the rail with her boots hooked in the lifeline, partner's privileges, watching the water with an expression Cyrus recognized because he wore it too — the look of a person who has stopped scanning the horizon for threats and discovered what else was out there.

"So," Devi said eventually. "We're legit now. We have a brand. We have references. What's next, Captain?"

Cyrus looked at the horizon. The monsoon clouds were stacked along it, purple and enormous, the leading edge of the season doing its annual landfall somewhere north of them. Behind the clouds, invisible, the sea went on for thousands of miles, holding everything it held — wrecks, names, knives, debts, the whole drowned archive. Let it keep them. The Perdita had a new contract offer already: a cable route survey, eight hundred miles of it, starting in a harbor none of them had ever worked.

Eight hundred miles. He liked the sound of that. Miles you could count on.

"North," he said. "There's a harbor up north that hasn't had the pleasure of us yet."

Tomas grinned. Auntie Bee raised her teacup in benediction. And Maren leaned over, shoulder almost touching his, and nodded at the darkening line where the storm met the sea.

"Landfall tonight," she said. "You think it'll hit hard?"

Cyrus watched the first wind arrive, bending the palms on the far shore, smelling of rain and salt and the clean cold start of things. The old him would have checked the barometer twice and started planning evasions. This him just breathed it in.

"They always do," he said. "But we're built for weather."

The Perdita rode the first bands out at anchor, steady as a promise, and the high landfall broke over the coast in sheets of silver, and nobody aboard was running from anything at all.
