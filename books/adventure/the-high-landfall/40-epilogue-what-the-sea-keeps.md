## 40. Epilogue: What the Sea Keeps

The post office in Tanah Merah still smelled of mildew and old glue, the way it had when Auntie Bee was a girl sending letters to a brother who never wrote back. She liked that about it. Some things held their shape.

She stood at the counter with three parcels wrapped in brown paper and twine, each one square and heavy as a brick, and she took her time addressing them, because Auntie Bee believed that anything worth doing slowly was worth doing slower than that.

The book had come six weeks ago from a printer in Singapore that Priya Nair swore didn't ask questions. Five hundred copies, softcover, plain cover stock. The title ran across the front in type so spare it looked almost apologetic:

*What the Sea Keeps: The Corvane, the Consortium, and the Divers Who Told the Truth.*

Priya's name went on the spine. The crew's names were inside, on the acknowledgments page, spelled correctly because Bee had checked them twice. Her own name was there too — Beatrice Osei-Lin, *cook and navigator* — and she'd read it four times before she could look at it without her eyes going hot. Ten years of being "Auntie Bee" to everyone from customs officers to gunrunners, and here was a stranger's book telling the world she had a whole name, a whole self, before the sea got hold of her.

She'd kept one copy for herself. One for the shelf above the galley stove, between Elias's tide tables and the photograph of Rook grinning like an idiot in his drysuit. The rest were for giving away.

The first parcel she addressed to a law office in Zurich. Inside it, along with the book, she tucked a short note on Perdita stationery — Tomas had bought real stationery after the story broke, god help him, cream paper with a little anchor watermark, as if they were a shipping concern instead of what they were. The note said:

*Messrs. Aldringen, Vogel & Roth —*

*You represented men who signed papers that put two hundred and eleven people at the bottom of a trench. Two of your clients are in prison now and one is dead by his own hand, which I do not celebrate. I only want you to know that the families are reading this. All of them. And that we know who drew up the insurance rider.*

*Some debts are settled by courts. Some by telling the story.*

*B. Osei-Lin*

It wasn't kind. Bee had sat with the note for three evenings, turning it over, deciding how much mercy it deserved, and decided: none. Mercy was for people who asked for it. These were men who had known the Corvane was going down and had gone to dinner anyway.

The second parcel went to Lieutenant Aris Thorne, Royal Navy, currently serving out a suspension in a coastal town whose name Bee had to look up twice. She'd liked the boy during the boarding — liked his terrible poker face and the way he'd drunk his tea without commenting on the fact that she served it in a chipped mug painted with hibiscus, and the way he'd looked at Cyrus across the wheelhouse with something that wasn't quite suspicion anymore. He'd filed his report honest, in the end. It had cost him his career trajectory and probably would cost him more before it was done.

Her note to him was shorter:

*Lieutenant — You did the right thing slowly, which is the hardest way to do it. The chapter about the boarding is accurate except that I have described your tea-drinking as graceful, which it was not. Consider this my apology and my thanks in one envelope.*

*Come by the boat if you're ever in Tanah Merah. We eat at seven.*

*Bee*

Tomas had laughed himself sick over that one. "You're inviting the navy to dinner," he'd said. "Ten years running grey cargo and you're inviting the navy to dinner."

"Boy," Bee had told him, stirring the pot without looking up, "the navy is just people in a uniform. People eat."

The third parcel was the hard one.

Bee had been carrying the address around in her head for a year — longer, if she was honest, since long before any of this started, back when Sela Marchetti used to sit in the Perdita's galley with her boots off and her feet up on the cooler, eating Bee's jollof straight from the pot with a wooden spoon she was forbidden to use and used anyway, talking about the house she was going to buy.

"Inland," Sela always said. "Somewhere with hills. Somewhere where you can't hear water unless you walk to it." She'd picked the town off a map the way other people pick lottery numbers — pointed at a green smudge in the high country three hundred miles from any coast, a place called Marrow Bend that had a river, a library, and according to Sela's cousin, houses cheap enough for a diver's savings to actually buy one. "I'll get a garden," she said. "Tomatoes. Something stupid and easy. No more holding my breath for money."

Rook used to tease her about it. "You'll last four months inland, Sel. You'll hear rain on a roof and start twitching."

"I'll last forever," Sela said, "because I'll be *there*, and you'll be *here*, and that's the whole difference."

That had been eleven years ago. Sela had died ten years ago, wedged in an escape hatch sixty-two meters down, in the dark, in the cold, with Elias Kavanagh's knife beside her and no one left alive who knew exactly what those last minutes had held. The book said what the evidence said and nothing more. Priya had been ruthless about that — *we don't speculate, we don't dramatize, we give them the documents and let the silence be loud.* There was a paragraph in chapter nine that Bee had read maybe forty times, the one about the hatch, and every time she read it she heard Sela's voice saying *tomatoes,* and every time she had to put the book down and go do something with her hands.

But the address. The address had stayed.

Because here was the thing Bee had figured out, somewhere in the storm, somewhere between the reef and the landfall, watching Devi steer a dying tug through a channel no chart recommended: grief doesn't want a grave. Grief wants a forwarding address. You spend years not knowing where to send all the things you never said, and they pile up in you like undelivered mail, and eventually either you find the box or the mail crushes you.

Sela had a PO box in Marrow Bend. Had opened it herself, Bee learned later from the cousin — box 114, rented for five years in advance, paid through the very month the Corvane went down. Whether she'd ever collected whatever came to it, whether anyone had kept paying after, whether the little post office in that green hill town still had a box 114 with her name stenciled under the number — none of that Bee knew. It didn't matter. Mail finds its way or it doesn't. That was the post office's business, and God knew they'd had a hundred years of practice at delivering things to the dead.

So she addressed the third parcel carefully, in her best schoolteacher hand:

*S. Marchetti*
*PO Box 114*
*Marrow Bend Post Office*
*Marrow Bend*

And inside, wrapped around the book, she put the letter she'd been writing in her head for a decade.

---

*Dear Sela,*

*If this reaches you, then someone kept your box open, and I hope they know what a service they've done. If it doesn't reach you — if the box is closed and this comes back marked undeliverable — then I will keep it on the shelf above my stove until I die, and that will be all right too, because some things need sending more than they need arriving.*

*We found the Corvane. You probably know that already; the whole world knows it now, which I suspect would make you laugh until you cried. All those years of diving quiet wrecks for quiet money, and it turns out the loudest thing any of us ever did was tell the truth in public.*

*I want you to know what happened down there is in the book, as much of it as anyone can know. The charges were set before she sailed. Elias confirmed them. I know what that does to your memory of him, because I know what it did to mine, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise or soften it. But I also want you to know the last thing he wrote, in different ink, years after — "If you're reading this, finish it." He spent his whole last decade trying to climb back toward the man he'd been before that night. Whether he made it isn't ours to judge. Whether he tried — that part's true, and I'm telling you because you deserve both halves of a man, not just the one that hurts.*

*Cyrus carries him easier now. Not lightly — I don't think he'll ever carry him lightly — but easier. He leaves things on the wreck's rail when we pass. Last time it was the knife. I don't know if that was a burial or a return. Maybe down there it's the same thing.*

*Rook's name is in the book too. Page forty-one, with yours. Priya read the list aloud at the launch event in Singapore, all two hundred and eleven passengers and crew, plus the two divers the sea took early. She read your name last, Sela. I think she knew.*

*The tomatoes thing — I put it in. Chapter three. "She talked about a garden the way other people talk about heaven." Your cousin verified the details and cried on the phone, and then laughed, and then told me about the PO box, which is how this letter exists.*

*I hope Marrow Bend has hills. I hope wherever you are has hills. I hope there's water nearby but not too near, and that nobody down there — or up there, or wherever — is making you hold your breath for money ever again.*

*Eat well. That was always your problem and mine, and I fixed mine, so consider this me fixing yours at long distance.*

*With love that outlasts everything, including stubbornness,*

*Bee*

---

She sealed the envelope, and her hands were steady, and she was proud of them for it.

* * *

The clerk at the counter was a young man named Faisal who had weighed Bee's packages since he was sixteen and had learned, in that time, never to ask what was in them. He weighed the three parcels now, frowned mildly at the Zurich one, and stamped them all without comment.

"Going far?" he asked, which was his version of never asking.

"One's going nowhere," Bee said. "One's going to a navy boy. And one's going to the hills."

Faisal nodded as though this explained everything, which, in Tanah Merah, it did.

Outside, the morning was doing that thing the harbor mornings did in the season after monsoon — washed clean, gold-edged, the water lying flat as poured metal. The Perdita sat at her berth looking better than she had any right to: new paint, patched hull, the survey gear lashed down neat as surgery instruments. Pipeline contract, starting Monday. Boring work. Legal work. Tomas complained about it daily and grinned while he complained, because boring and legal turned out to taste better than exciting and damned once you'd had a full year of both.

Devi was already aboard, of course. Devi was always already aboard; Bee suspected the girl slept in the engine room some nights, curled against the warm generator like a cat. Maren Voss's car was parked at the head of the pier — Maren kept strange hours and stranger habits, but she showed up when the work started, and these days she signed the manifests as partner, not employer, and nobody aboard had quite gotten used to seeing her handwriting next to Cyrus's and finding that it fit.

Cyrus was on deck reading. That was new too — the reading. A year ago he'd treated books the way he treated feelings, as things you acknowledged existed and kept locked below the waterline. Now there was a paperback bent open in his hands most mornings, and today, when Bee climbed the gangway, she saw it was the book. Their book. His own face was on the back cover photo, younger-looking than he had any right to be, and he was reading the acknowledgments page again, the way he did sometimes, like a man checking a lock he'd already thrown.

"You know it doesn't change," Bee said, setting down the market bags. "You've read that page fifty times. It still says the same thing."

"I know." He didn't look up. "I'm checking."

"Checking what?"

He was quiet a moment. Out past the breakwater, a fishing fleet was heading out in loose formation, engines muttering, gulls wheeling over their wakes.

"That it's all still true," he said finally. "That we really did it. Some mornings I wake up and I'm sure I dreamed the whole year. The wreck, the storm, the lieutenant drinking tea in my wheelhouse. Then I read the page and it says our names and I think — well. Somebody wrote us down. Must have happened."

"Somebody wrote you down," Bee agreed, and unpacked the fish, and let him have his ritual.

Maren arrived at eight sharp with coffee and pastries in a grease-spotted box, and Tomas came up from below complaining about a fuel line, and Devi surfaced from the engine room with oil on her cheekbone and the survey schedule memorized, and for a few minutes the deck of the Perdita was just noise and breakfast and argument about whether the pipeline job paid enough to justify the boredom, which it did, which they all knew, which they argued about anyway because arguing was half of what family was for.

Bee watched them from the galley doorway with her coffee going cold in her hand, and she thought about the three parcels sitting in the back of Faisal's post office, waiting for the noon truck. One to men in glass towers who would open it and feel the floor tilt. One to a suspended officer who might, if the tea invitation held, actually come. And one to a green hill town three hundred miles from salt water, addressed to a woman who had wanted tomatoes and gotten a trench instead.

The truth, she'd learned, was like the tide. You couldn't stop it. You could only decide whether you'd be standing on the shore when it came back around, or hiding from it, or — best of all — being the thing that carried it in.

"Auntie Bee." Cyrus, from the deck, holding up the book. "Page ninety-three. The bit about the jamming. Tomas says he spoofed the ROV in under four minutes and Devi says six. Who's lying?"

"Both of them," Bee called back. "It was nine. I timed it. Now sit down and eat before I feed you like children."

They laughed, and the harbor light came up full and gold, and the Perdita rocked gently on water that had given up its dead man's secrets and, having given them, seemed — for the first time in ten years — willing to simply carry the living.

Bee went inside and started breakfast, and above the stove, between the tide tables and Rook's grin, the book sat with its title facing out, keeping watch over the galley the way she did, the way they all did now.

What the sea keeps, it keeps. But what the living tell — that travels farther.
