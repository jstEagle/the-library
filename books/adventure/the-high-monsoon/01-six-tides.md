## 1. Six Tides

The rain came in sideways off the estuary, the way it always did in the weeks before monsoon, and Freya Okafor stood on the deck of the *Pelican's Mercy* pretending to check the barometer while actually watching the Syndicate collector's launch cut a white seam across the brown water toward them.

"He's early," said Sefa, without looking up from her charts.

"He's punctual," Freya corrected. "There's a difference. Early implies eagerness. Punctual implies he wants us to know he could be early whenever he liked, and chose this moment to remind us."

"That's the most depressing sentence I've ever heard."

"You should hear my other ones."

The launch bumped against the hull. Freya tucked the barometer under her arm like a clipboard she had every intention of using, and went to meet the man who owned, in a manner of speaking, the next six days of her life.

His name was Corvin Ashe, and he had the particular politeness of a man who had never once needed to raise his voice, because everyone around him did the arithmetic for themselves. He climbed aboard with a canvas satchel, shook the rain off his coat with two efficient shrugs, and smiled at her the way a bank manager smiles.

"Captain Okafor."

"Mr. Ashe. You're getting wet."

"I'm getting paid, eventually. The wet is incidental." He glanced at the sky, the river, the boat — the boat got the longest look, a slow inventory that made Freya's jaw tighten. "Shall we?"

They sat in the wheelhouse, because Freya refused to conduct business below decks. Below decks was where the crew lived, and she wasn't going to have the smell of the Syndicate soaking into the cushions. Milo made tea without being asked, which was either hospitality or a stalling tactic, and Freya loved him for not specifying which.

Ashe laid a ledger on the table. The paper was good paper. That was the thing about the Vane Syndicate — everything about them was good paper, good ink, good manners. The rot was always somewhere in the middle of the page.

"Your storm data," Ashe said. "The Kessel Bank readings you sold to the coastal survey office last month."

"What about them?"

"They were wrong."

"They were *not* wrong." Freya kept her voice level and her hands flat on the table, because the alternative was wrapping them around his excellent collar. "The Kessel Bank readings were the finest predictive work done on that stretch of water in a decade. The survey office said so themselves."

"The survey office said so," Ashe agreed pleasantly, "right up until the squall line came through forty minutes ahead of your forecast and put two of their launches on the rocks. Their insurers are asking questions. Their insurers ask the Syndicate questions, because the Syndicate underwrote the survey. And the Syndicate, Captain, is now asking *you* questions."

The rain drummed on the wheelhouse roof. Milo set the tea down with the exaggerated care of a man trying not to be present for any of this.

"Forty minutes," Freya said. "That's within tolerance."

"Forty minutes is a drowned deckhand, on somebody else's boat."

"Nobody drowned."

"This time." Ashe opened the ledger and turned it toward her. Numbers marched down the page in that beautiful ink. "Equipment replacement, insurance penalties, contractual penalties, and the Syndicate's standard fee for reputational inconvenience. The total comes to four thousand, two hundred crowns."

Freya looked at the number for a long moment. Four thousand crowns was roughly everything the *Pelican's Mercy* was worth, plus the *Pelican's Mercy*. Two hundred crowns was roughly everything Freya personally owned, if you counted the barometer, which apparently someone had.

"I can offer you a counterproposal," she said.

"I'd be disappointed if you didn't."

"You take the data from this season — the monsoon readings, the full run, everything we gather between now and landfall. Good data, live data, worth triple what the survey paid. You sell it to the insurance consortiums before the storms hit and you'll make back your four thousand in a week and look like a genius doing it."

Ashe considered this with the expression of a man tasting a wine he had already decided about. "And your payment terms?"

"Forgiveness of the debt. Full and final."

"Mm." He closed the ledger. "Here is my counterproposal, Captain, and I'll be plain because I respect you and plainness is the courtesy of equals. The Syndicate will take the season's data *and* the debt, unless the debt is paid in full by the time the monsoon makes landfall."

"That's—"

"Six tides," Ashe said. "The long-range buoys put landfall six tides out. After the sixth, the ports close, the rivers flood, and the Syndicate's collectors go home to dry socks. Whatever you owe when the sixth tide turns, Captain, gets collected in whatever form it's found. The boat is acceptable collateral. The boat is, frankly, *preferred* collateral — she's worth more than you are."

The wheelhouse was very quiet. Milo had stopped pretending to wipe the counter.

"Get off my boat," Freya said, without heat, the way you'd tell the rain to stop.

Ashe stood, buttoned his coat, and paused at the door with his hand on the frame. "For what it's worth — and it isn't worth much — I hope you make it. You're the best chaser on this river, and it would be a waste." He smiled his bank-manager smile. "Five and a half tides, really, by the time I'm ashore."

The launch receded into the rain. Freya stood at the wheelhouse window until the white seam of its wake disappeared, then turned around.

"Milo."

"Aye, Captain."

"Was that tea for me or for him?"

"Both. I made his weak. Petty, but it's what I had."

"Good man."

* * *

The crew gathered in the cabin that evening, which meant all four of them wedged around a table built for two, which meant knees and elbows and the ship's cat, Barnacle, occupying the only genuinely comfortable spot as was his right.

Freya laid it out plainly, because they deserved plain. The debt. The number. The six tides.

"Six tides," repeated Sefa. She was the navigator, small and sharp and older than she looked, with a habit of repeating bad news as if hearing it twice might improve the flavor. "That's three days."

"Two and a half, if the buoys are optimistic, and the buoys are never optimistic."

"Four thousand two hundred." Milo was doing the arithmetic aloud, which was a kindness, because it meant nobody else had to. "Even if we sold the season's data the day we gather it, the consortiums don't pay that fast. Nobody pays that fast. That's the whole point of consortiums."

"Thank you, Milo."

"I'm just saying the math has a shape, and the shape is bad."

Tam, who had been silent at the far end of the table — Tam was always at the far end of the table, a large quiet ex-merchant sailor who communicated mostly in shrugs and the occasional devastating single sentence — raised one finger.

"Run," he said.

The word landed in the cabin like a dropped wrench.

"There it is," Sefa said. "Somebody said it. I was going to say it, but Tam beat me to it, and Tam saying it is somehow worse."

"Run where?" Freya asked. Not angrily. Genuinely. It was a fair question and they all knew it.

Tam shrugged, elaborately. "Upcoast. The Karst ports. Syndicate's got no paper there."

"The Karst ports have no *money* there either. And no survey work, no insurance contracts, no nothing. We'd be fishing. We'd be *fishing*, Tam. Do you know what my father would say if I turned the *Pelican's Mercy* into a fishing boat?"

"He'd say the fish don't care about the weather," Milo offered. "That's kind of the appeal of fish."

Freya looked around the table. Milo, twenty-three, deckhand and cook and the closest thing she had to a younger brother, watching her with those worried river-grey eyes. Sefa, arms crossed, already mentally plotting escape routes because that was what Sefa did with everything, including breakfast. Tam, immovable, reasonable, wrong. Barnacle, asleep, unbothered, the only one among them with no debts.

"I'm not running," Freya said.

"Captain—"

"Listen. Running means the debt follows us, and it will — the Syndicate's paper travels better than we do. Running means starting over with nothing, in a place with less than nothing. Running means we spend the rest of our lives looking over our shoulders at every polite man with good ink." She leaned forward. "Six tides is not a death sentence. Six tides is a *season*. It's the monsoon run, and the monsoon run is the richest data on the coast, and there are buyers who pay cash on the barrel if the data's good enough. We've done harder things with less."

"Have we?" Sefa asked.

"...We've done things of comparable hardness with marginally less."

"That's not the same."

"It's adjacent."

Milo laughed despite himself, and Freya counted it a small victory. That was the trick of it, she'd learned — you didn't beat fear in one blow. You chipped at it. You made it laugh once, and then twice, and by the third time it had forgotten what it was doing there.

"Here's what we do," she said. "We run the river ahead of the storm front, same as every year, but we run it *hard*. Every reading, every pressure drop, every wind shift, logged clean and sold fast. The upriver stations pay cash for monsoon data — old man Kess does, anyway, if he's still alive. We work the whole river in six tides."

"The whole river," Sefa said flatly, "in two and a half days. In monsoon conditions."

"Yes."

"That's suicide with extra steps."

"It's *ambition* with extra steps."

"Those are frequently the same thing."

"Then we'll be in good company." Freya stood, which in a cabin that small meant she was suddenly participating in the conversation with her entire body. "We leave on the morning tide. Milo, I want the engine checked twice. Sefa, chart us the narrows — the real narrows, not the ones on the map, the ones that are actually there. Tam, fuel and stores, and if you have to haggle with the chandler, remember he's scared of you and use it."

Tam nodded, gratified.

"And tonight," Freya added, softer, "somebody make something with the good salt. We're not eating like a drowning crew on the eve of a run. We're eating like a crew that's about to make history and get paid for it."

They dispersed to their tasks with the particular energy of people who had decided to be hopeful out of spite, which Freya had long ago learned was the most durable kind. Milo paused at the cabin door.

"Captain. For what it's worth — I'm glad we're not running."

"You're glad because running would mean cooking in a smaller kitchen."

"I contain multitudes," Milo said, and ducked out before she could throw anything.

Freya stood alone in the cabin a moment. Through the porthole she could see the river running high and brown with the first flush of the monsoon's approach, carrying sticks and silt and whatever else the flooded banks upstream were giving up. Six tides. She touched the porthole frame, the way her father had taught her to touch a boat — firmly, like a handshake, like a promise.

*We'll make it, old girl,* she told the *Pelican's Mercy*. *We always do.*

The river, for its part, kept moving, and kept its secrets — and the next morning, when Milo's sounding line came up out of the flood with something heavy and wrong on the end of it, Freya would learn exactly how many secrets a river could keep, and what one of them was going to cost her.
