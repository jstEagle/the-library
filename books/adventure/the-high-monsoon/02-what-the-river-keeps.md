## 2. What the River Keeps

The river had swallowed the road a week ago, and now it was pretending it had always been water.

Milo knew that trick. Rivers lied the way his mother used to say politicians lied—smoothly, confidently, and with a current underneath that would drown you if you believed the surface. He stood at the bow of the *Salt Lark* with the sounding pole in his hands, reading the depth the way other men read weather, and tried not to think about how much the flooded country below him looked like home.

"Talk to me, Milo," Freya called from the wheel. She had her sleeves rolled and her hair tied back with what appeared to be a length of signal flag, because Freya Okafor did not own hair ties so much as she confiscated them from the emergency kit.

"Four fathoms," Milo said, driving the pole down. "Clear. Sand and silt, nothing that'll take our keel off."

"Nothing that'll take our *keel* off," Sefa repeated from the chart table, not looking up. "He says that like we have a keel worth taking off."

"The *Lark* has a perfectly serviceable keel."

"The *Lark* has a keel that's held on by sentiment and three coats of paint."

"That's still a keel," Milo said. "Sentiment is load-bearing."

It was the kind of exchange that happened forty times a day on the *Salt Lark*, and Milo loved it the way you love a heartbeat—without noticing, until you thought about it, and then all at once. Six of them on a forty-year riverboat with a hull that groaned in its sleep, running data runs up the Vess before the monsoon closed the whole watershed like a fist. Storm pressure readings, wind shear profiles, salinity logs. Boring numbers to anyone with sense. To the right buyers, worth enough to keep the Vane Syndicate's collectors from knocking politely on their hull.

Six tides. That was what the collector had given them. Milo was trying very hard not to count.

"Mark that bend," Freya said. "The current's going to want to shove us into the east bank when we come around."

"The east bank is a mango grove right now," Milo said. "Or it was a mango grove. Now it's a mango *archipelago*."

"Then we'll be the first boat in history to be wrecked by fruit." Freya's mouth twitched. She'd been doing that more lately—almost smiling, then catching herself, like the debt had taught her that joy cost interest. "Drop the dredge line at the next pool. If we're sounding anyway, we may as well see what else the river's offering."

"The river's offering silt," Milo said. "The river is always offering silt."

"The river," said Sefa, without looking up from her charts, "is offering whatever it took from somebody else. That's what rivers do."

Milo drove the pole down again and said nothing, because Sefa had a way of saying things that landed in him like a fishhook.

* * *

The pool below the bend was deep—seven fathoms, then eight, the pole vanishing into brown water that gave back nothing. Milo called the depths while Tam cranked the little dredge winch they'd rigged from a salvage winch and, if you believed Tam, a bicycle.

"Eight fathoms," Milo said. "Eight and a half. It shelves off fast here."

"Old channel," Sefa said. "The river moved. This used to be the main stem."

"Used to be," Milo murmured, and the words sat strange on his tongue. He was used to places that *used to be*. He'd been born in one.

The winch stopped. Tam leaned over the rail, squinting. "We've got weight."

"Silt," Milo said. "It's always silt."

"It's not silt. Silt comes up smooth. This is—" Tam cranked harder, and the cable went taut at an angle, groaning. "This is *heavy*, and it's *shaped*."

The whole boat drifted a degree off the current as the winch fought. Freya cut the throttle and came aft, and by the time the shape broke the surface, all six of them were leaning over the rail like children at an aquarium glass.

It was a chest. An actual chest—iron-banded, oak-planked, crusted with forty years of river in a skin of gray clay, trailing weeds like a drowned man's beard. Water sheeted off it as Tam swung it inboard, and it hit the deck with a boom that Milo felt through his boots and, he suspected, through his teeth.

"Please be full of coins," said Tam. "Please, please be full of coins."

"Please be full of something we can *sell*," Freya corrected, already kneeling at it, knife out, working at the clay. "Coins we'd just lose to the Syndicate. I want something with *buyers*."

Milo hadn't moved.

He was looking at the lid.

The clay had cracked away from one corner, and beneath it, in a circle the size of his palm, there was a seal pressed into the metal band. Milo knew the seal. He knew it the way you know your own name—not learned, just *known*, the way you know the smell of your mother's kitchen, the sound of rain on a particular roof.

A wave, curled like a sleeping cat. A star above it, eight-pointed. And beneath, in the old script that hadn't been written anywhere in the world for forty years: *Meridia*.

Meridia, which was not on any map. Meridia, which was not in any atlas, any registry, any schoolbook. Meridia, whose harbors now belonged to three different nations whose names had been invented by men in conference rooms, whose people had been issued new papers and told their birthdays were still valid but little else was. Meridia, which had been erased so thoroughly that when Milo had told Tam where he was born, Tam had checked twice and then said, gently, like breaking bad news, "I don't think that's a real place, mate."

Milo had smiled and said, "No. I suppose it isn't."

His hands were shaking. He put them behind his back, which was the oldest trick he knew, from the year the new government's men had come through the villages asking questions, and anyone whose hands shook was asked more questions.

"Milo." Freya glanced up at him, knife still working. "You're quiet. You're never quiet. Quiet from you is a *weather event*."

"Just thinking about the keel," Milo said.

"Sentiment is load-bearing," Sefa said, and there was something odd in her voice too, something Milo couldn't place because he was working so hard to keep his own face smooth. Sefa was staring at the seal with an expression he recognized from mirrors—the look of a person seeing a ghost that everyone else insists is a stain on the wall.

The lock was fused with corrosion, but Freya's knife found the hasp's old weak point, and Tam brought the pry bar, and between the two of them the chest gave up its secret with a crack that echoed off the flooded banks.

Inside: not coins.

Paper. Dozens of oilskin-wrapped bundles of paper, and beneath them, gleaming dully, a row of ingots stamped with the same curled wave and eight-pointed star. And on top of everything, a logbook bound in green leather, swollen with river water but whole, its cover stamped with the seal.

"Ingots," Tam breathed. "Actual—Freya. Freya, that's—"

"I can count," Freya said. Her voice had gone flat and fast, the voice she used when the numbers were bad. She lifted the logbook, turned it over, frowning. "Meridia. That's not a shipping line. Sefa, is that a shipping line?"

Sefa didn't answer.

"Sefa."

"No," Sefa said quietly. "It isn't a shipping line."

"Then what is it?"

"It's a country," Milo said.

The word was out before he could stop it, and it cost him something to say it, the way it cost something to say the name of a dead person in a house that has decided not to grieve. Everyone looked at him. He shrugged, aiming for casual and hearing, in his own ears, how far short he fell.

"Was a country," he amended. "Before the Reclamation. They scrubbed it off everything. Maps, treaties, the lot. Officially it never existed." He kept his voice level, bored, a man reciting trivia. "Old story. Nobody talks about it."

"Nobody talks about it," Freya repeated, "and yet here's a chest full of its money sitting in a river pool, forty years heavy." She was looking at the ingots the way she looked at storm cells—calculating, hungry, afraid. "Sefa, how much does erased-nation gold go for?"

"Depends who you sell it to," Sefa said. "And whether the people you sell it to decide you found too much."

"That's cheerful."

"That's accurate."

Freya stood, wiping her hands on her trousers, and Milo watched her run the arithmetic—he could *see* her running it, behind her eyes, the debt against the ingots against the six tides against the six of them. And then he watched the arithmetic stop, and something colder move in behind it.

"Wait," Freya said. "Wait, wait, wait. Rivers don't keep things in pools. Rivers *move* things. This didn't wash down from upstream." She turned, scanning the brown water, the drowned banks, the mango archipelago. "This was *buried*. Something sank here. Something big enough to be carrying a nation's treasury, and it sank *here*, and the river covered it, and we just—" she gestured at the winch, the cable, the hole they'd dragged through forty years of silence— "we just rang the dinner bell."

The *Salt Lark* rocked gently on the flood current. Somewhere across the water, a bird made a sound like a rusty hinge.

"Freya," Tam said, "nobody knows. We dredged it up, it's ours, no one saw—"

"The river saw," Milo said quietly. "The river tells everyone everything. Fishermen. Salvagers. Every scavenger between here and the delta is going to know a boat dragged something heavy out of the deep pool below the bend, because that's how it works. That's how it *always* works." He heard his own voice and was surprised by how steady it was. "You don't find a thing like this. A thing like this finds you, and then it finds everyone else too."

Freya looked at the chest. At the ingots. At the green logbook in her hands, which she had not put down, which Milo suspected she was not going to put down.

"Six tides," she said, mostly to herself. "And now every boat on this river is going to come see what we're carrying."

* * *

That night, anchored in the lee of a drowned church spire, Milo took the first watch and stood at the rail with his hands around a mug of tea he wasn't drinking.

The seal was still in his mind. The wave, curled like a sleeping cat. He'd been four years old when they came with the new papers. He remembered his grandmother pressing the old coin into his hand—the only one she'd managed to keep—and telling him, *They can take the name off the maps, little fish. They cannot take it off the water. The water remembers.*

He'd thought that was just the kind of thing grandmothers said.

Below him, in the flooded dark, the river moved over whatever else was down there—whatever the chest had come from, whatever Freya had already started calling *the wreck* in the low, fast voice she used for plans she hadn't told anyone yet. The water remembered. And tomorrow, Milo thought, watching the moon come up over the drowned mango trees, so would everyone else.

Because far downriver, where the delta met the sea, there were boats that made their living finding what other people had lost—and one of them, he'd heard, flew a banner with a vane on it, and its captain shared a name with the people who owned Freya's debt.

The tea had gone cold. Milo drank it anyway, and kept the watch.
