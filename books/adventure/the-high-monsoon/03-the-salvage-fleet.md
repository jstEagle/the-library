## 3. The Salvage Fleet

The smoke came up over the eastern bend at midmorning, thin and gray and patient, the way smoke does when it has all the time in the world.

Freya saw it first, because Freya always saw weather first. She was on the foredeck of the *Kestrel's Debt* with a mug of Milo's terrible coffee, watching the sky do its slow arithmetic, when she noticed the column rising off the water like a question nobody wanted to answer.

"Woodsmoke," Sefa said behind her, before Freya could say anything. Her navigator had a gift for ruining mornings efficiently. "Cook fires. More than one. And diesel under it—generators, maybe pumps."

"Pumps," Freya repeated.

"Salvage pumps." Sefa's jaw did the thing it did when he was doing math he hated. "Somebody's working this river."

Freya set down the coffee. It was terrible anyway. "Milo! Kill the engine noise. I want us quiet."

They drifted then, engine idling low behind a screen of reeds that had grown tall and drowned-looking since the river began its annual climb, and Freya took out the glass. Three hulls beyond the bend, moored bow-and-stern in the working fashion, cranes folded like sleeping birds. Painted funnels. A pennant snapping gray-green against the haze.

She knew that pennant. Everyone on this stretch of water knew that pennant. The Cormorants didn't hide; they didn't have to. They were the Vane Syndicate's hands, and the Syndicate's hands were everywhere the money was, which lately meant everywhere.

"They're dredging," Milo said, appearing at her elbow with the particular silence of a man who had spent his life not being noticed until he chose to be. He was squinting downriver too, and there was something tight around his mouth that Freya filed away without understanding yet. "See the spoil barges? They're pulling silt by the ton. That's not prospecting. That's a search pattern."

"A search for what?"

"I couldn't say." Milo said it smoothly, easily, and Freya noticed that too—the smoothness—and let it go, because there were larger problems swimming toward them wearing smaller problems as hats.

The largest problem had a name, and the name was Ilsa Vane.

* * *

Ilsa Vane came out to meet them herself, which was either courtesy or a message, and with Ilsa it was always both.

Her launch cut across their anchorage at noon, white wake folding open like a fan, and she stood at the wheel with her coat collar turned up and her hair whipping, looking for all the world like a woman out enjoying the weather rather than a Syndicate captain deciding whether a rival boat was an obstacle or an expense. She brought two crewmen who stood very still in the way hired muscle stands still, and she brought a bottle of plum brandy, which was worse.

"Captain Okafor." She stepped aboard without asking, which was also a message. "You're a long way upriver for storm data. Season's late. Water's high. Dangerous work."

"We like dangerous work. It pays our rates."

"It pays *somebody's* rates." Ilsa smiled. She had a good smile, warm at the edges, cold at the center, the kind you could toast marshmallows over if you didn't mind what the marshmallows were made of. "Word is your last sale went poorly. Word travels fast when a buyer feels cheated."

"The buyer wasn't cheated. The buyer was wrong about what he'd purchased, which is a different crime entirely, and one I don't commit."

"No. You sell exactly what you promise. That's your reputation, isn't it? Honest Freya. Honest to a fault." Ilsa said it kindly, which somehow made it land like a slap. "So here's my thought. This river's big enough for two fleets if we stay honest with each other. You take your soundings north of the bend, we work south. Nobody's lines tangle. Nobody's divers get nervous. And when the monsoon comes—and it will come, Freya, look at that sky, even you can't argue with that sky—we both run for shelter and everybody lives to be poor another season."

"That's very generous."

"I'm a generous woman. Ask anyone I've ruined." She laughed at her own joke, and her crewmen didn't, and Freya found herself laughing too despite everything, because Ilsa Vane had always been funny the way a knife is funny when it misses you by an inch.

"And in exchange for this generosity," Freya said, "you want?"

"Nothing. Truly. A truce between professionals." Ilsa's eyes went past Freya's shoulder, casual as a passing cloud, and rested for half a heartbeat on the tarp amidships where the chest lay hidden under fishing net and spare line. Then they came back, warm again. "Though if you ever did happen to pull something interesting out of this river—something with provenance, something with *history*—well. The Syndicate pays fair for history. Fairer than any buyer you'll find in the delta towns. Fairer than the ones who cheat you."

"I'll keep that in mind."

"Do." Ilsa raised the brandy bottle, toasted the air, and left it on their deck as a parting gift, which Freya understood perfectly: a small debt, freely given, to be called in later. Everything with the Vanes was a ledger. "Six days, Captain. Then the sky closes. Let's both be rich or alive by then. Ideally both."

She went back down her ladder, and the launch pulled away, and Freya stood watching the white wake close over itself until there was nothing but river.

"She knows," Sefa said quietly.

"She suspects. There's a difference, and the difference is how long we live." Freya turned. "Get below. Both of you. We're going to count what we actually have before anyone else counts it for us."

* * *

They opened the chest in the hold with the hatch battened and a single lamp burning, the way you'd open something stolen, which technically it wasn't, but felt like.

The salvage was modest, mostly: rotted bolts of cloth gone to sludge, a case of candle wax fused into one pale block, coins so corroded they might have been buttons. Enough value to feed them for a month, nowhere near enough to feed the Syndicate's appetite. Freya catalogued it aloud in a flat voice while Milo wrote, and Sefa held the lamp, and none of them said the word *Meridia*, though the seal stamped into the chest's lead lining sat there among them like a fourth crew member nobody had invited.

Then Freya lifted out the logbook.

It was oilcloth-wrapped, tarred at the seams, the kind of wrapping a man uses when he means a thing to last longer than he does. She peeled it back and the smell came up—brine, mildew, and underneath it something faintly sweet, old paper dreaming of better shelves.

The handwriting hit her before the words did.

She knew that handwriting. She had known it across school primers and grocery lists and the margins of tide tables, a slanted, impatient script that crossed its sevens and dotted its i's slightly left of center, as if the letters themselves were always leaning toward the next thing. She had known it on notes tucked under her pillow before dawn runs. *Back by the second bell. Mind the lamp.*

Elias Okafor. Keeper of the light at Kess. Vanished forty years ago along with a nation, and here was his hand, leaning toward the next thing, on a page dated three days before the wreck.

"Freya?" Milo's voice, far away.

She read. The early pages were ship's business—cargo manifests, depth notes, complaints about the cook—but the entries grew stranger as the dates advanced. Coded marginalia. References to *the cargo that isn't cargo*. A page near the end written in a hand pressed hard enough to tear the paper:

*If they find this, know the chest was never meant for markets. It was meant for memory. What Meridia was cannot be sold, only told. I am sorry for the weight of it. I am sorry most of all to F., who deserved a father more ordinary than this.*

F.

Freya closed the book. Her hands were steady, which surprised her; she'd expected them to shake. Steadiness, she was learning, was just shaking that happened somewhere deeper down where no one could audit it.

"That's—" Sefa began.

"Don't." She said it quietly. "Not yet. Please."

Sefa, to his credit, said nothing. Milo looked at the book, then at her, and there was that tightness around his mouth again, that thing he knew and wasn't saying, and Freya was too full of her own crack to press him on it.

Because here was the shape of it, settling onto her shoulders like ballast: her father had been aboard this ship. Not a passenger lost to chance. A man with a plan, with a chest wrapped to outlast empires, with a letter addressed—to her, someday, somehow—with an apology already drafted forty years before she'd need it. Every story she'd ever been told about Elias Okafor, every comfortable hole where a man should have been, suddenly had edges. He hadn't simply vanished. He had *chosen* something, and the something had eaten him, and now it was sitting in her hold wearing a Meridian seal.

And above them, three hulls away, Ilsa Vane was drinking her plum brandy and waiting for the word *history* to come back across the water.

"Catalogue's done," Freya heard herself say, in her flat voice, her routine voice, desperation dressed in its work clothes. "Seal it back up. Milo—double the anchor watch tonight. If anything moves on that river that isn't rain, I want to know before it knows we're awake."

She climbed out into the gray afternoon and stood at the rail. Downriver, the Cormorant pumps throbbed through the water like a second heartbeat, patient as tide. Upstream, the sky had begun assembling the monsoon in earnest, stacking blue-black thunderheads with the unhurried confidence of something that had never once been late.

Six tides, the collector had said. Five now, maybe.

Freya looked at the logbook's oilcloth wrapping still caught on her sleeve, and thought: *I am sorry most of all to F.*

"Well, Papa," she said to the water, quietly enough that no one could hear, "what exactly did you leave me?"

The river, being a river, declined to answer. But far off past the bend, a Cormorant crane swung its arm out over the current and began, deliberately, to dredge.
