## 4. Dead Nation, Living Debt

Sefa had learned to read in three alphabets before she was ten, and the fourth—the Meridian river-script—she'd learned from her grandmother's kitchen wall, where a painted blessing curved around the window like water deciding to stay. *May the current remember you.* That was the whole of it. Fourteen characters, and Sefa could still write every one of them with her eyes shut, though she hadn't spoken a full sentence of Meridian aloud since she was eleven years old, since the maps changed, since her aunt's house became a coordinates nobody was allowed to name.

So when Milo set the salvaged logbook on the chart table and slid it across to her without a word—just that look, the one he thought he was hiding behind his usual cheerful blankness—Sefa felt something old and cold turn over in her chest.

"You've been staring at it for an hour," Freya said from the helm. The *Tern* was anchored in a backwater eddy, willows screening them from the main channel, because a chest stamped with a dead nation's seal made a boat very popular very fast.

"I'm being careful," Sefa said.

"You're being slow."

"I'm being careful," Sefa repeated, "which is what you pay me for, except you don't pay me, which is why we're in this mess." She turned the logbook under the lamp. Water-stained leather, swollen at the spine, but the pages inside were dry as prayer. Someone had sealed this book against the river with real intention. Meridian wax in the seams. Her grandmother would have called that work *loving*—the kind of care you gave a thing you expected someone to need long after you were gone.

The handwriting hit her first. Not the content—the handwriting. A tall, slanted script, numbers written like they were hurrying somewhere.

"That's Okafor penmanship," she said quietly. "I've seen your charts. You hold your sevens the same way."

Freya came off the helm so fast the boat rocked. "What?"

"Your father wrote like you do. Look." She turned the page toward Freya and immediately regretted it, because Freya's face did something complicated and then went carefully flat, which was worse.

"Read it," Freya said. "All of it. Then tell me what we have."

Sefa read. She read while Milo pretended to coil rope that was already coiled, while the rain started up soft on the deck above, while the lamp flickered and she fed it oil rather than stop turning pages.

It took her two hours. By the end of it her hands were not entirely steady, and she was glad for the low light, because she did not want anyone seeing what her face was doing either.

* * *

"It's the *Meridian Rose*," she said finally. "The treasury ship. The one that 'sank in open water' the week before the borders closed." She laid out the pages in order along the table, weighting the corners with brass instruments because there was nothing else heavy enough. "Except it didn't sink. It was scuttled. Here—" her finger found a page dense with soundings and tide tables, "—your father took her into the shallows himself. Deliberate grounding. He was the master's pilot, or he was posing as one. And then they moved everything ashore before the hull went down."

"Moved what?" Milo asked, though Sefa suspected he already knew, because Milo always knew things and then arranged to be told them.

"The treasury. Or what the Syndicate said was the treasury." Sefa tapped the ledger pages. "Forty years ago, when Meridia was dissolved, the Vane interests bought the nation's debts for pennies and sold the story that the treasury ship went down with everything aboard. Insurance paid twice. Debts collected three times. An entire country's wealth walked off the books and into six families' pockets—and the proof that it happened is sitting in our hold in a box with a wax seal on it."

She looked up. "This isn't salvage, Freya. This is a confession. Signed, dated, witnessed. There are transfer orders in here with names I know. Names that are still on buildings. There are letters between the Vanes and the survey office arranging the *erasure itself*—the map change, the record burnings. They didn't just steal from Meridia. They made Meridia never have existed, so no one would go looking."

The cabin was quiet except for the rain.

"And your father," Sefa said, gentler now, "didn't scuttle her and run. He stayed with the truth. Hid it. Sealed it. Wrote this logbook knowing someone might find it someday, if the river ever decided to give it back." She swallowed. "*May the current remember you.* That's the blessing over the seal page. He knew the words."

Freya sat down slowly, like her knees had filed a complaint.

"So," Milo said into the silence, with the delicacy of a man defusing something, "do we celebrate, or panic? I can do either. I've got snacks for both."

"We decide," Freya said.

"Ah," said Milo. "The hard one."

* * *

They argued until the lamp burned low.

Milo wanted to sell—to anyone, quietly, fast, before the Cormorant fleet finished pretending to fish the far channel. "A confession this good has buyers," he said. "Historians. Exiles. One very nervous old family or two who'd pay enormous sums for it to disappear. We don't even have to choose a side. We're a shop. We sell to whoever knocks."

Sefa heard herself say, "No."

Everyone looked at her. Sefa did not often say *no*. Sefa said *bearing two-seven-zero* and *reef to port* and *we should probably not eat that*. This was different. This was the fourth alphabet rising up through her after thirty years underwater.

"No," she said again, and found her feet under her. "You weren't there. None of you were there. When they closed the border, they didn't just move lines on paper—they moved *people*. My aunt's village got renamed twice and then deleted. My mother spent nine years writing petitions to offices that officially had no jurisdiction over a place that officially had never been. My grandmother died waiting for a reply from a government that had agreed, on paper, that her whole life was a clerical error." Her voice cracked and she let it. "And these pages say it wasn't an accident of history. It was a purchase. Somebody signed for my childhood the way you sign for a delivery."

"Sefa—" Freya started.

"I'm not finished." She wasn't sure she'd ever said that to Freya Okafor before. It felt terrible and correct. "We broadcast it. Every coastal station, every archive, every exile family still keeping the language alive in their kitchens. We give it away. Free. Because the moment it's everywhere, it can't be bought back, buried, or used to buy *us*, and the Vanes lose the thing they've been guarding for forty years: the story that nothing happened."

"And then what?" Freya's voice was quiet, which meant it was dangerous. "We own a boat with no fuel and a debt with a deadline. Six tides, Sefa. The collector counted them on his fingers like a man telling a child a bedtime story. If we give this away, we go into landfall owing the Syndicate more than our lives are worth, and they *know* we have something worth stealing. Generosity doesn't clear a ledger."

"Neither does dying rich in someone else's story."

"It's not about rich. It's about *alive*." Freya leaned forward, hands flat on the table, around the spread pages like she was afraid they'd blow away. "You think I don't want to burn them down? My father is in this book. My father chose this wreck over coming home, and I have spent twenty years thinking he simply—left. Abandoned us. Chose the river over his daughter." Her jaw worked. "He didn't leave. He was *keeping watch*. And the people who made him do it are the same people counting down my six tides. Don't tell me I don't want this. I want it more than you do. That's exactly why I can't trust myself with it."

"So we sell it quietly," Milo said, "to the right buyer—"

"There is no right buyer," Sefa said. "That's the trap. The moment it's a transaction, the Vanes can outbid, out-threaten, or out-drown us. The only price they can't beat is free."

Freya laughed, short and bitter. "Free. You want us to be martyrs with a boat."

"I want us to be *people* with a boat. There's a difference and it's the whole difference."

The rain thickened overhead. Somewhere upriver, faint as a headache, a fleet engine turned over.

Milo cleared his throat. "For the record, I'd like to note that I originally suggested selling, but watching you two argue has made me realize I mostly suggested selling because I'm scared, and I'd rather be scared and right than comfortable and wrong. Also I am still open to snacks."

"Helpful," said Freya.

"I contain multitudes."

But Sefa saw him glance at the porthole, toward the far channel, and she knew he was counting engines too. Everyone on this boat was doing arithmetic all the time—fuel against distance, courage against interest rates. That was what debt did. It made mathematicians of everyone.

"Here's what I know," Freya said at last, standing, gathering the pages with hands that were almost steady. "Whatever we choose, we choose before the fleet finds this anchorage. And whatever we choose—we choose it together, or not at all. Nobody gets sacrificed for anybody's conscience. Including mine." She met Sefa's eyes. "Including yours."

"That's not how ledgers work," Sefa said.

"No," Freya agreed. "It's how crews work. Ledgers I'll deal with my own way."

She went up on deck with the logbook wrapped in oilcloth, and Sefa watched through the porthole as she stood in the rain holding it like something between a weapon and an infant.

Milo sank onto the bench beside her. "You know what happens next," he said softly.

"I know what happens next."

"He's going to offer to buy it. Tomorrow, maybe sooner. And Freya's going to listen, because she has to, and you're going to hate her for listening, and none of us are going to sleep."

Sefa looked down at her hands, at the ghost of fourteen characters she could still write blind. *May the current remember you.*

"The current remembers," she said. "The question is whether anyone else does."

Out in the dark, upriver, the first light of the Cormorant fleet's lamps swung across the water—and began, unhurried, to come their way.
