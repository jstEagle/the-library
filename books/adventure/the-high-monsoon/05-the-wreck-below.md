## 5. The Wreck Below

The river gave them ninety minutes of dark, and Freya intended to use every one of them.

She stood at the stern rail of the *Six Tides* while Sefa killed the running lights, one by one, until the world was nothing but the smell of coming rain and the slow brown muscle of the water below. Upstream, the fleet's lamps burned like a string of carnival bulbs someone had dropped and left to drown. Ilsa Vane kept her divers on day shifts. That was her mistake, or her arrogance—Freya hadn't decided which, and wouldn't, not tonight.

"Current's up another hand's width," Milo reported, hauling in the sounding line hand over hand. His voice was steady. His face, in what little light leaked from the cabin, was not. "And it'll keep rising till the front comes through. Whatever you're going to find down there, you'd best find it quick."

"I love it when he talks romantic," said Tam from the engine hatch, where she was pretending to fix something that didn't need fixing. She'd been doing that all evening. Pretending things were fine had become a kind of shipboard choreography; everyone knew their part.

Freya pulled on the dive suit slowly, letting each buckle be its own small argument against turning back. The chest sat under the deck boards now, wrapped in oilcloth, and beneath the deck boards with it was the logbook—and the name she'd found in it three times before she could make herself look a fourth time.

*E. Okafor.*

Not a mention. Not a passing reference in some clerk's inventory. Her father's initials in the margin of the final page, in his cramped, impatient hand, beside a line that read only: *It stays down unless it must come up.*

He'd written that eleven days before the *Meridian Star* went missing. Eleven days before the storm that officially swallowed her father, forty years of maps erased Meridia, and the Vane Syndicate rose out of nowhere like something surfacing.

"Freya." Sefa stood close enough that Freya could feel the warmth of her through the damp air. She pressed something into Freya's glove: a small knife, wrapped at the handle so it wouldn't flash. "In case. And—" she hesitated, which Sefa never did, "—if you find him down there, you say goodbye properly. You hear me? You don't rush it for our sake."

Freya's throat did something complicated. "I'm going to find a wreck, not a funeral."

"Yes," Sefa said gently. "That's exactly why you should promise."

Freya promised. It cost more than the debt did.

* * *

The water took her the way it always did—cold first, then indifferent, then almost kind. She let the anchor line feed through her hands and listened to the river rearrange itself around her body. Her lamp made a small yellow room in the blackness, and everything outside that room was the flood's business, not hers.

Forty feet down, the bottom came up to meet her: silt, snags, the ribs of drowned trees. And then, off to starboard where Milo's sounding line had kissed something solid—the hull.

Even furred over with four decades of river, the shape of her was unmistakable. A treasury ship didn't look like other ships. Too much beam amidships, too few gunports, a keel laid for cargo worth guarding rather than cargo worth selling. The *Meridian Star*. Not a ghost story. Not a rumor Sefa's grandmother had carried out of a burning country. A ship, lying on her side in the mud like a woman asleep who would not wake.

Freya swam the length of her slowly, lamp held high, and grief ambushed her the way it always does—not at the obvious places but at a small stupid one: a coil of rope still lashed to a cleat, tied off in a bowline. Her father had taught her that knot when she was seven, standing on a dock no bigger than this memory, saying *a bowline never lies to you, Frey, whatever else does.* She hung in the cold water with her lamp shaking slightly and let herself have ten seconds. She counted them. Then she got back to work.

The cargo hold gape had been forced open recently—scorch marks from someone's cutting torch, weeks old, abandoned. The Cormorant fleet had been here. They'd opened the hold, found the chests stripped of anything bearing value, and moved on. Salvage logic: no gold, no salvage. They'd never understood what they were looking at.

But Freya was starting to.

Because the strongroom door—inner, iron, the kind of thing you put behind an already-locked hold—was closed. Sealed. Welded shut from the *inside*, if her lamp wasn't lying to her, and it wasn't. Someone had gone into that room with cutting gear and sealed themselves—or something—in, deliberately, permanently. The welds were amateur but thorough. Desperate-man work. Or determined-father work.

Her regulator hissed. Her heart hammered loud enough that she could hear it competing with it. She checked her gauge: fine. Time was fine. Everything was fine except the fact that her father had apparently walked into a steel box at the bottom of a river and shut the door behind him.

There was a gap in the welds, low, near the hinge side—a seam left open just wide enough for a flat case. Deliberate. An invitation, or a will's instruction. She worked the case free with fingers gone clumsy inside the gloves. It was lead-lined, document-weight, and stenciled along one edge in fading paint:

*FOR F. OKAFOR — ONLY.*

The letters swam. She blinked hard, twice, and they resolved into the same cramped handwriting she knew better than her own name, because both names had come from the same hand.

Inside the case: a bundle wrapped in waxed linen, dry after forty years, which meant whoever packed it had known exactly what they were doing. And on top of the bundle, a letter. Envelope, seal intact, her name across the front in block capitals.

*FREYA.*

Just that. No title, no explanation, no *in the event of my death*, none of the legal language that would have let her hide behind formality. Just her name, written by a man who had been dead longer than she'd been alive, addressed to a girl he'd last seen crying on a dock.

She held it in both hands and felt the river press in from all sides, patient as a creditor.

Every instinct she owned said *open it*. Open it here, in the dark, in the quiet, where nobody could see her face do whatever it was going to do. But her gauge said twenty minutes of air and her training said the current was rising and Sefa's voice in her head said *you don't rush it for our sake* — and underneath all of it, colder and clearer than any of them, sat the arithmetic she couldn't argue with: a letter read by lamplight in a flooded wreck was worth nothing. A letter read by a crew that trusted each other, with the fleet's divers already in the water—

The lamp swung as she turned, and caught movement.

Light. Downstream. Two of them, maybe three, moving slow, sweeping the bottom in a search pattern she recognized because she'd invented half of it. Ilsa Vane's night divers. Working the same stretch of river she was standing in.

*So much for day shifts,* Freya thought, with the strange calm that arrives precisely when panic would be most useful.

She killed her lamp.

Blackness took her completely, absolute and total, and for one long moment she was twelve years old again, lost in a storm cellar, certain the dark was going to eat her. She breathed. In. Out. The regulators hissed. Somewhere to the south, lights swept past like slow searchlights through fog, thirty yards off, then twenty-five.

They hadn't seen her go down. They were working the hull blind—which meant they'd find the hold, and eventually the strongroom, and eventually the welds, and ask exactly the right questions about who'd cut them and when.

Freya tucked the case inside her suit, against her chest, where her heartbeat could reach it, and began to swim.

* * *

Surfacing was like being born, if birth involved a deckhand named Milo grabbing your harness and hauling you over the rail while whispering *shut up shut up shut up* with tremendous affection.

She lay on the wet deck planks and breathed real air and let Tam peel the mask off her face, and for a moment nobody spoke, because upstream the fleet's searchlights were combing the water and everyone understood the mathematics of sound carrying over a river at night.

Then Sefa knelt beside her. Looked at her face. Looked at the bulge inside the dive suit where the case rode against her ribs.

"You found it," she whispered.

Freya nodded. Her voice, when it finally worked, came out rougher than the river had left it. "I found a letter."

"From him?"

"From him." She worked the case free and set it between them on the deck, oilcloth gleaming faintly. "Sealed. Addressed to me."

Sefa looked at it the way you'd look at a live grenade, or a miracle—hard to tell the difference at this range. "Open it."

"Not yet." Freya heard herself say it and was surprised by how sure the words were. "The fleet's in the water. If Ilsa takes this boat in the next hour, everything in that case goes into a Syndicate vault and disappears for another forty years, along with everyone who ever saw it." She looked up at her navigator, at her friend, at the last person alive who remembered Meridia as a place instead of a wound. "It has to be somewhere safe first. Then I'll read it. I swear it."

Sefa held her gaze for a long moment. Whatever she saw there seemed to satisfy her, or at least to be accepted as payment on account. She nodded once and stood.

Tam appeared with tea nobody had asked for, which was Tam's way of saying *I noticed you nearly died and I refuse to discuss it.* Milo crouched at the rail, watching the searchlights, and said quietly, "We can't stay anchored here. They're quartering the river. By morning they'll grid us."

"Then we move at slack tide," Freya said. She picked up the case. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed more than the boat. "Somewhere with walls."

"The lighthouse," Sefa said immediately. "Kess Point. Nobody's kept that light since your father—"

"Kess Point," Freya agreed, before the sentence could finish becoming a memorial. She tucked the case under her arm and stood, legs unsteady, debt unpaid, father's letter unopened against the growing weight of everything she suddenly needed to know.

Above them, far upstream, a flare burst from the fleet's lead ship—white, then red, then white again. Recalling their divers. Calling them home.

Or calling them onward.

Milo watched it fall and said, very softly, "Freya. They stopped searching."

"I know," she said.

"They stopped searching *because they found something.*"

The rain arrived then, all at once, the way monsoons do—no warning, no courtesy, just the sky closing like a fist. And Freya Okafor stood on the deck of the boat her debts owned, holding a dead man's letter she didn't dare open, and watched the fleet's lights turn downstream.

Toward Kess Point.
