## 6. Bargaining with Optimists

Milo had learned three things about Ilsa Vane in the years since she'd bought his sister's fishing debt and turned it into a leash: she never raised her voice, she never repeated herself, and she always poured tea for her guests as though the outcome of the meeting were already a pleasant memory they shared.

The tea was terrible. That was the one mercy.

"You'll forgive the setting," Ilsa said, gesturing at the Cormorant's wardroom, which was less a room than a suggestion—one folding table, two stools, and a chart of the river pinned up with four different knives. "We don't entertain often."

"We noticed," Milo said. "Your smoke's been visible from three miles off. Very festive."

Something flickered behind Ilsa's eyes—not annoyance, exactly. Interest, maybe. She liked people who didn't flinch. It made them more fun to ruin. "Freya Okafor sent you to talk."

"Freya Okafor sent me to listen," Milo corrected. "There's a difference. One of them is free."

Ilsa poured. The tea was the color of river water and smelled faintly of tar. Milo accepted his cup anyway, because refusing hospitality on the water was bad luck in every tradition he'd been raised in, including the ones that had drowned.

"The chest," Ilsa said. "Meridian seal. You dredged it up out of the flooded bank near the old customs weir—don't bother looking surprised, we have eyes on every mudflat between here and the delta. Freya owes the Syndicate eleven thousand crowns. I'm offering to clear it. All of it. Today."

"And all you want is—"

"A location." Ilsa smiled. It was a good smile, professionally maintained, like the paint on a ship that was rotting underneath. "You found something the whole coast has spent forty years dreaming about. We're not monsters, Milo. We're optimists. We believe everyone has a price, and we're happy to pay yours."

*Optimists.* Milo turned the word over. In Meridia—the Meridia that existed only in his mother's stories now—they'd had a saying: *the optimist drowns in the same water as the pessimist; he just apologizes on the way down.* His mother used to say it while mending nets, laughing, before the maps changed and the laughter went out of her.

He kept his face pleasant. He'd gotten very good at pleasant. It was the face you wore when you recognized a seal on a salvaged chest and said nothing, even though your hands shook so badly you dropped the sounding line twice.

"Eleven thousand crowns," he said. "And Freya walks away clean. No interest, no collection riders, no 'administrative fees.'"

"No fees. A clean slate." Ilsa sipped her tea without wincing, which Milo considered the most frightening thing he'd seen all season. "You'd be fools to refuse."

"Oh, absolutely," Milo agreed warmly. "Fools. The question my captain asked me to ask—and she wants me to be precise about this—is *what happens after*. Because in her experience, and mine, debts cleared by the Syndicate have a way of... regrowing. Like moss. Like mold."

"Like family," Ilsa said, and for just a moment the professional smile slipped and something older showed underneath—something tired. Then it was gone, smoothed away, and she set down her cup with a small precise click. "After, there is nothing. That's what *cleared* means. No ledger, no rider, no claim. The Syndicate will consider your crew finished."

Milo heard the words correctly. He was sure of it later—he played them back a dozen times in the dark, on the deck, listening to the rain start. *Finished.* But the word arrived in his ears wrapped in cotton, delayed by half a heartbeat, the way sounds come to you underwater, and by the time his mind caught up Ilsa had moved on to logistics, tide tables, a rendezvous point marked on the chart with a knife-tip.

"There's one condition," she added, almost as an afterthought, which was how the worst things always arrived. "The handover happens aboard the Cormorant. Your whole crew attends the signing. Standard practice—it keeps everyone honest."

"Keeps everyone *in one place*, you mean."

"I said what I said."

* * *

He walked back along the fleet's pontoon walkway in a drizzle that couldn't decide whether to be weather or just an apology for weather, and his mind was doing the thing it did when the sounding line came back shorter than the chart promised—that cold recalculation, depth where there should have been channel, rock where there should have been mud.

Aboard the Cormorant, waiting for the meeting, he'd been given tea in the galley first. He hadn't wanted it. He'd drunk it anyway, because bad luck. And while he'd sat there pretending to admire a rack of drying oilskins, two of Vane's collectors had come in behind him, talking low, thinking the Meridian boy scrubbing rust off a porthole was furniture.

"—after the signing, then. Both boats."

"The tall one too? The navigator?"

"Everyone on the manifest. Captain's orders. You don't clear a debt and leave witnesses who know why it got cleared. Forty years the Syndicate's stayed buried because nobody living could point at the wreck." A pause, the sound of a match striking. "Okafor's girl especially. You know whose boat that was, don't you? Before."

"I thought the father was lost at sea."

"He was lost at sea the way a loose thread is lost in a sweater. Pull it and everything comes undone."

Milo had kept scrubbing. Rust flakes in the bilgewater. Counting his breaths the way his mother taught him, four in, six out, until his hands stopped shaking enough to hold the brush.

Now, walking back to the *Kestrel* through the gray afternoon, he ran the numbers again and they came out the same. Eleven thousand crowns against five lives. A clean slate against no slate at all, no ledger, no anything, five names struck off a manifest that would end up weighted and dropped somewhere the river kept its secrets.

Stopping had never been an option. He understood that now with the strange calm of a man who has stopped arguing with the flood and started measuring it.

* * *

The Kestrel's cabin smelled of wet wool and Sefa's burnt coffee, which Sefa insisted was tea. Everyone was there—Sefa hunched over the logbook with her magnifying loupe, Freya standing at the chart table with her arms crossed like a woman holding a door shut against weather, and young Pip in the corner pretending to splice line and listening to everything, because that was Pip's entire job description as far as anyone could tell.

"Well?" Freya said.

"She offered to clear the debt. Eleven thousand. Full pardon, no riders." Milo hung his dripping coat on the hook. "In exchange for the chest's location, signed transfer, everything above the waterline handed over proper."

Sefa looked up sharply. "That's cheap. That's insultingly cheap. The Syndicate doesn't pay market price for miracles."

"That's not the worst part." Milo sat down on the bench, and the bench creaked, and he took a moment because his voice wanted to do something embarrassing. "The handover happens on the Cormorant. Whole crew attends. And while I was waiting in their galley, two of their collectors were discussing the manifest." He met Freya's eyes. "Everyone on it. After the signing. They used the word *finished*, Freya. About us."

The cabin went very quiet. Rain ticked against the deck above them, soft as fingers.

Pip stopped splicing. "Finished like—"

"Like a job," Milo said gently. "Not like a debt."

Freya hadn't moved. Milo watched her take it in—the way she took in storm fronts, all at once, no wasted motion—and watched her arrive somewhere cold and stay there. "My father," she said. "They knew whose boat the wreck was. They've known all along."

"They called him a loose thread," Milo said. "In a sweater."

Sefa closed the logbook with great care, the way you close a door on a sleeping child. "Then there's no deal to make. There never was. They were always going to drown us in the same hole as the proof."

"Unless we're not there when the hole gets dug," Freya said. Her voice had gone level and bright, the voice she used when the barometer fell. "Milo. When do you tell her yes?"

Milo blinked. "I'm sorry—*when* do I—"

"You're going back tomorrow to accept. On her terms, all smiles, no suspicion. You're going to tell Ilsa Vane that Freya Okafor is desperate and broken and ready to sign, but—" and here Freya smiled, and it was not a nice smile, it was the smile of a woman who had read six tides of weather in a cup of tea, "—but she wants to choose the rendezvous herself. Somewhere *she* feels safe. She'll pick the deep pool below the narrows, because that's where she'd pick, because it's defensible and she's careful. Which means the whole fleet anchors at the narrows."

Understanding moved through the cabin like current through water. Sefa's mouth twitched. Even Pip grinned, briefly, before remembering to look solemn.

"You want them all in one place," Sefa said, "at the only pinch on the river where one boat ahead of them can—"

"Read the water," Freya said simply. "It's what I'm for."

Milo looked at her a long moment. "You understand I'll have to lie to her face. She can smell fear and she can smell lies, and I'll be serving her both with terrible tea."

"You can do it," Freya said. "You're the best liar on this boat."

"That's slander."

"It's a compliment. Take it."

He laughed despite himself—a short, cracked sound—and outside the rain thickened, drumming now, the river rising in the dark the way it always rose before the monsoon made its mind up. Three tides, maybe four, before landfall. Less before the narrows became a deathtrap for anyone who didn't love them properly.

That night Milo stood his watch alone at the bow, and he practiced. Just quietly, to the rain. *Captain Okafor accepts your terms. She'll come to the pool below the narrows. She's frightened, she's tired, she wants it over.*

And under his breath, in the language the maps had erased, the one his mother had sewn into him along with his name, he added the true part, the part for the river only:

*But we will not be there. None of us will be there. Count your optimists, Ilsa Vane. See if any of them surface.*

Upriver, three miles off, the Cormorant's lights burned steady through the rain, patient as a trap.

Two tides, he thought. Two tides, and then the narrows.
