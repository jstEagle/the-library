## 7. Three Tides

Sefa counted tides the way other people counted money, because tides were the only currency the river respected. Three left. She'd marked them on the chart table in pencil, small careful strokes beneath the running tally of their debt, and this morning she'd drawn a line through one and felt her stomach drop like a stone off a wharf.

Two after that. Then the monsoon made landfall somewhere south of them with all the patience of a debt collector.

"Course says we take the narrows," Freya said, standing over the chart with her sleeves shoved to her elbows. Rain tapped at the wheelhouse glass in a rhythm Sefa had learned to distrust—too steady, too polite. Weather that knocked before it came in was weather planning to bring friends.

"The narrows," Milo repeated from the doorway, holding two cups of tea he'd clearly abandoned halfway through making because the kettle had started making noises like a dying gull. "The narrows where the river goes from half a mile wide to a hundred yards between two cliffs that hate each other."

"That's the one."

"And you're proposing we take *her*—" he tipped his head toward the deck, toward the *Kestrel*, their battered survey boat, "—through there ahead of a fleet with three times our engine and none of our sentimental attachment to staying alive."

"I'm proposing we take her through there *because* of the fleet." Freya turned the chart so they could all see. "Ilsa Vane's divers are heavy boats. Deep draft. The narrows won't fit them at mid-tide, and they know it. They'll have to run wide around Kess Point and lose six hours. We go through now, on this tide, and we're a full day clear."

"And if we're wrong?"

Freya smiled, and it was the smile Sefa had catalogued over four years of working with her: the one that meant she'd already done the arithmetic and didn't love the answer but had decided not to share it.

"Then we learn something about the narrows."

Sefa looked down at her own hands on the chart edge. She had an exit planned. Everyone did; that was the unspoken covenant of a found family assembled out of desperation—you loved each other, and you each kept a bag packed. Hers was a ferry ticket out of Port Amman folded inside her grandmother's atlas, good any time, no questions asked. Milo had his cousin's fishing shack upcoast. Even Freya, Sefa suspected, had some fantasy of disappearing into a storm system where no collector could follow.

But nobody mentioned the bags. Not today.

* * *

The narrows announced themselves an hour later as a change in the water's voice.

Sefa stood at the bow rail with the sounder while Milo took lines and Freya conned from the wheelhouse, and she listened. That was the part people never understood about Freya's storm-craft—it wasn't instruments, though she had plenty, most of them held together with solder and prayer. It was listening. The river had a grammar, and Freya read it aloud for anyone willing to hear.

"Hear that?" Freya called down. "The pitch just dropped. Water's deepening off your port bow—that's the old channel, follow the quiet."

"It doesn't sound quiet," Sefa shouted back. "It sounds hungry."

"Hungry is fine. Hungry means moving. What kills you in the narrows is the water that stops arguing—the eddies behind those rock teeth. If you hear silence, *that's* when you worry."

So Sefa worried, in the professional manner, every time the roar thinned. The cliffs rose on either side, black stone streaked white with old flood-marks, close enough that she could have thrown a biscuit onto either bank. The river compressed and accelerated, piling into standing waves that marched upstream against the tide, and Freya threaded the *Kestrel* between them like a woman dancing with a partner twice her size who might change her mind at any moment.

"Milo! Fender off the starboard side—we're going to kiss that ledge whether I like it or not!"

"We're going to *what*?"

"Politely!"

They kissed the ledge politely. There was a shriek of paint against stone that Sefa would hear in her dreams for a month, a lurch, a curse from Milo in three languages, and then they were through, sliding into the wider water below the narrows where the river exhaled.

Sefa allowed herself four seconds of relief. She was midway through the fifth when Freya's voice came down again, stripped of all its showmanship.

"Sefa. Get below. Check the fuel line."

"Why?"

"Because I can smell diesel, and it isn't ours."

It was theirs. Of course it was theirs. The near-capsize hadn't happened yet, but the river had apparently decided to get its retaliation in early—a fitting had cracked when they'd kissed the ledge, and they were bleeding fuel in a thin rainbow ribbon down the current behind them.

Milo got the leak stopped with a clamp and what Sefa privately considered an excessive amount of profanity. But the damage was done in the way that mattered: they'd lost perhaps a third of their stores. Enough to run. Not enough to run far, and definitely not enough to run far *while* a fleet with three times their engine came around Kess Point behind them.

Sefa sat on the engine-room steps doing the arithmetic she hated. With the fuel they had, they couldn't reach the deep-water ports. They couldn't even reach Port Amman by the safe route. They could make the ruined lighthouse at Kess—and nothing beyond it.

Her hand went, without her deciding it would, to the pocket where the ferry ticket lived.

* * *

She found Milo on the foredeck that evening, mending the fender he'd sacrificed to the ledge. He worked with the patient concentration of a man who found it easier to fix things than to talk about them, which was why she liked him. Meridians and Milo-people, her grandmother used to say, mend first and mourn after.

"You felt it too," Milo said without looking up. "The ticket."

Sefa blinked. "How did you—"

"You touched your pocket during the leak. Everyone does it. Touches the thing they'd leave with." He pulled a stitch tight. "I think about my cousin's shack maybe nine times a day lately."

"Why don't you go?"

"Because if I leave, I'll spend the rest of my life knowing I left *you two* on a boat with Ilsa Vane coming up behind you." He said it simply, the way he said true things, which was the only way he said anything. "And also because the shack smells like fish. Both reasons are load-bearing."

Sefa laughed despite herself, and then the laugh caught on something. "My grandmother's atlas has a map of Meridia in it. A real one. Printed before the erasure. Every time I look at it, I think: this country existed. Millions of people. Coastlines with names. And someone decided it was tidier without us." She watched the water slide past. "That chest isn't salvage to me, Milo. It's a receipt."

"So we give the truth away and starve?"

"I don't know. That's the terrible thing—I don't know what survival is worth anymore." She hugged her knees. "But I know I stopped reaching for the ticket somewhere back in the narrows. Somewhere between the ledge and the leak, I realized if we split up now, the Syndicate picks us off one at a time, and worse—" she hesitated, embarrassed by the softness of it, "—worse, we'd deserve each other's absence."

Milo set down the needle and thread. He reached into his jacket and produced his own exit: a crumpled letter from his cousin, offering the shack, dated two years ago, worn soft from handling.

He dropped it over the side.

"Milo! That was—"

"Load-bearing," he said. "Was. Now it's ballast." He grinned. "Your turn."

Sefa thought about it. She genuinely thought about it, the ferry ticket warm against her hip like a small cowardly animal.

"Not yet," she said. "When we're clear of the fleet. When Freya decides what she owes her father. Then—" she met his eyes, "—then I'll burn mine, if she burns hers. Together. Deal?"

"Deal." He spat ceremonially over the rail to seal it, missed entirely in the wind, and pretended he hadn't.

* * *

Freya found them at dusk on the aft deck, where they were sharing tea that tasted faintly of diesel and pretending it was fine. She leaned on the rail beside them, and for a long moment none of them spoke. Behind them, far downstream, a smudge of smoke sat on the horizon like a held breath. The Cormorant fleet, rounding Kess Point, six hours behind and closing.

"One tide gone," Freya said finally. "Two left."

"Fuel says we make Kess and nowhere else," Sefa said. "You should know that."

"I know." Freya was watching the smoke, and her face did something complicated—grief and calculation wrestling for the same chair. "There's something I haven't told you both. Something I surfaced with last night. A letter. Addressed to me. From him."

Sefa felt the air change. Milo set down his cup very carefully, as though the boat had become fragile.

"You haven't opened it."

"I can't. Not while they're behind us and the choice is still—" Freya pressed her palm flat against the rail. "Whatever's in that letter, whatever he wanted—he left the decision to me, and I keep thinking, what if what he wants and what keeps us alive aren't the same thing?" She laughed, short and unamused. "Four years I've been selling weather to pay a debt, and it turns out my father was the collateral all along."

"Then we go to Kess," Milo said. "You read the letter behind walls, with the fleet anchored offshore and two tides on the clock, exactly the conditions under which everyone makes their best decisions."

Freya looked at him. Then at Sefa. And something in her shoulders came down half an inch—not surrender, Sefa thought, but setting down weight she'd been carrying alone.

"You're both staying," Freya said. It wasn't quite a question.

"My cousin's shack smells like fish," said Milo.

"My ticket's expired emotionally," said Sefa.

Freya nodded once, and turned back to the helm, and Sefa saw her hand move briefly to her coat pocket—to the letter, or maybe to her own packed bag, Sefa couldn't tell—before settling on the wheel.

Ahead of them, past the darkening water, the ruined lighthouse at Kess rose against the last grey light, its lantern room broken-crowned like an old soldier who'd stopped saluting. Two tides left. One night to decide what the truth was worth.

And behind them, steady as a heartbeat, the smoke on the water kept coming.
