## 8. The Lighthouse at Kess

The lighthouse rose out of the rain like a tooth that had outlived its jaw.

Freya had known it was there before Sefa called it out—had felt it in the way the current bent around the point at Kess, the water remembering a structure her charts had forgotten. That was the thing about rivers. They kept better records than governments did.

"Starboard," she said quietly, and Milo brought them in under the broken gallery, where forty years of storms had peeled the lantern room back like the lid of a tin. Below it, a keeper's cottage clung to the rock, half-swallowed by salt-rot and strangler fig, but with walls still standing and a roof that was mostly roof.

"Cozy," Sefa said, in the tone of someone describing a grave.

"It's shelter."

"It's a lighthouse with no light."

"Then nobody's looking for us here." Freya killed the engine as Milo threw the line around a stanchion that had been waiting, patient as everything else in this place, for hands to hold it again.

Her hands knew the knots before she did. Her father's knots—the ones he'd taught her on a dock smaller than this one, when she was seven and all thumbs. She looked at her fingers wrapped in the rope and thought: *of course*. Of course the river would carry them here. The river had been carrying them here since the chest came up out of the silt wearing Meridia's seal like an accusation.

"You've been here before," Milo said. Not a question.

"A long time ago." She took the second line from him. "I was small."

"What was it like?"

"Warm." It surprised her, saying it. "I remember it being warm. Which is ridiculous, because this coast is nothing but wet and wind."

* * *

They made camp in the keeper's cottage while the sky went the color of a bruise deciding whether to bloom. There was a stove that hadn't rusted through, and Sefa coaxed it alive with driftwood and profanity until it smoked and then, grudgingly, burned. Rain came down on the roof in sheets, and inside, improbably, they were dry.

Milo found the kettle. Of course Milo found the kettle; the man could find a kettle in a shipwreck, and frequently did. He set it on the stove with the ceremony of a man lighting a votive.

"There's tea," he announced. "It might be older than I am. Possibly than all of us combined."

"If it kills me," Sefa said, "tell my sister it was quick and I complained the whole time."

"You'd want her to know that specifically?"

"She'd be suspicious otherwise."

Freya sat apart, near the window that faced the sea, with the letter in her jacket pocket and her hand over it like a bandage. She had been sitting like that, off and on, for two days. The letter was waterproofed in oilcloth and tied with waxed cord, and it bore her name in her father's handwriting—*FREYA*, and beneath it, smaller, in the way he always wrote when he was trying not to make a thing too heavy: *when you're ready, not before.*

She had been ready for eleven years. She had been ready every day since the day the Syndicate's men came to their door with polite faces and a ledger, asking questions about a storm-chaser named Emeric Okafor who had gone out ahead of a squall and never come home. She had been eight years old and she had lied beautifully, because he'd taught her how to do that too, and afterward she had understood, in the wordless way children understand catastrophes, that she was the only person left who remembered him warm.

And now here was his handwriting, and she could not make her fingers work the cord.

"Freya." Sefa set a tin cup beside her. The tea smelled like dust and eucalyptus and something faintly criminal. "You're doing the thing."

"What thing?"

"The thing where you sit very still and pretend you're making decisions but actually you're just weathering yourself." Sefa lowered herself onto the bench across the table, joints cracking like kindling. "Drink the tea. It's terrible. It'll help."

Freya drank the tea. It was terrible. It helped.

"Milo thinks you should open it," Sefa said. "He didn't say so. But he's been walking past your chair approximately nine times an hour, radiating concern like a damp stove."

"I know."

"And I think you shouldn't. Not yet." Sefa turned her cup slowly, watching the leaves settle. "Not because you're wrong to want to. Because whatever's in there, once you read it, you can't unread it. And tomorrow there'll be a fleet in that anchorage, and you'll need to be whoever you decide to be, and I'd rather you decide *before* you know what he wanted. If he wanted something."

"He hid proof of a nation's murder in a river," Freya said. "He wanted something."

"Maybe. Or maybe he just wanted it kept safe, and the wanting was ours." Sefa's voice gentled. "My family died because people decided what Meridia deserved without asking anyone from Meridia. I don't intend to let a dead man decide for you. Even him. Especially him."

Outside, the rain thickened. Somewhere beyond it, out in the black anchorage, lamps were coming on one by one—the Cormorant fleet, arriving with the patience of people who owned the debt and the water and, if they got their way, the ending of this story. Freya counted five lights, then six. Ilsa Vane anchoring offshore like a cat outside a mousehole, courteous enough to wait for morning.

"They're not attacking tonight," Sefa said, following her gaze. "Too rough even for Vane money."

"They're not in a hurry. That's worse."

"Yes." Sefa stood, groaning, and squeezed Freya's shoulder once on her way to her bunk. "Whatever you choose, choose it as yours. Not his. Not theirs. Yours."

* * *

The others slept. Freya did not.

Instead, somewhere past midnight, with the storm testing the windows and the sea growling against the point, she found herself climbing the lighthouse stair. Her boots knew the way. That was the horror of it—that after twenty years her feet still rose and fell in the rhythm of steps she'd taken at four years old, riding her father's shoulders up toward the lamp room, counting the turns aloud while he corrected her numbers with warm, false patience.

The lamp room was open to the sky now, glass long gone, the great lens shattered into teeth of thick old crystal that caught the lightning and threw it in pieces across the floor. Freya stood among the glitter and looked out at the dark water, and the dark water looked back.

"You used to stand right here," she said to no one. "You told me the light wasn't for the ships. You said—" her throat closed. She made herself finish it. "You said it was so the water knew somebody was awake. So it didn't get any ideas."

The wind came through the empty panes and moved her hair like a hand.

*All right,* she thought. *I'm awake. Say what you have to say.*

She went back down to the cottage, took the letter from her pocket, and untied the cord before her courage could file another objection.

The paper inside was soft with age, folded twice, written in ink gone brown. His hand filled two pages. She read it standing up, because sitting down felt like something she couldn't survive.

*Freya. If you are reading this, then the river has done what I asked it to, which is more than most rivers ever do for me.*

*I am going to assume you found the chest, which means you also found the logbook, which means you already know some of it. So let me tell you the part the logbook can't. The men who came to our door after I vanished—they did not lose me in a storm. I lost myself in one, deliberately, because it was the only place left to hide the treasury's proof where the Vanes would never dredge it. I scuttled the Meridian Star myself, with the evidence sealed below, and I swam away from it, and I have been ashamed of swimming away every day since.*

*Here is what you must understand, and forgive me for it: I hid the truth not only from them. I hid it from you. Because the truth about Meridia is worth more than money—it is worth everything, to everyone—and I could not ask an eight-year-old to carry it. So I carried it alone, badly, and I am sorry.*

*Now it is yours, and you are grown, and I will not tell you what to do with it. That is the whole point of leaving it to you. Sell it and be free of them—I would understand, and love you anyway, and never once blame you. Spend it and be hunted forever. Burn it and keep your crew safe and let the dead stay dead. Any of these is a life. Only you can say which life is true.*

*But hear me on one point, and then I'll stop talking, which you'll find hard to believe I'm capable of: whatever you choose, do not choose it out of fear. Fear is the tide. It comes in, it goes out. What you owe the living and what you owe the dead—those are the fixed stars. Navigate by those.*

*The lighthouse at Kess has a dry bunk in the north room and I have hidden nothing in it, because I promised your mother no more hiding. Give Milo my regards—he sounds exactly like the sort of man you'd collect. Mind Sefa's knees. Come home someday, if home still exists.*

*—Da*

Freya read it twice. Then she sat down on the floor of the cottage, among her sleeping crew, and laughed until it turned into the other thing, quietly, into her sleeve, while the storm hammered the roof like applause or like fists, and she honestly couldn't have said which.

* * *

Dawn came gray and mean, and with it, the fleet.

Freya stood in the ruined lamp room with a spyglass and watched six hulls lying to their anchors in the roadstead, black against a blacker sea. Boats were already lowering. Not assault boats—courtesy boats, brass fittings glinting, Ilsa Vane's personal launch cutting a neat white seam toward the point. A parley, then. A last polite chance to sell.

Sefa climbed the stair behind her, moving like her knees were filing formal complaints. "Well?"

"Well." Freya lowered the glass. In her pocket, folded flat, the letter pressed against her ribs like a second heartbeat. She thought of her father swimming away from his own truth, ashamed. She thought of Milo finding kettles in ruins, of Sefa's dead and Meridia's erased name, of six tides burning down to one. She thought of the ransom that would clear her debts and the broadcast that would clear her conscience, and understood, finally, with the calm of a woman reading weather, that she could not afford both—and that one of them had never really been for sale.

"They're coming ashore," she said. "Wake the others. And Sefa—"

"Yes?"

"Get the transmitter ready. Whatever happens next, we're going to need everyone on the coast to hear us before the storm takes the signal down."

Below, the launch touched the rocks at Kess, and Ilsa Vane stepped out into the rising wind.
