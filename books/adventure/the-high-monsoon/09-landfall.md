## 9. Landfall

The sky went the color of a bruise at dawn, and by mid-morning it had decided to finish the job.

Freya stood in the lighthouse lamp room with her father's letter folded against her heart, watching the sea arrange itself into something with intent. The swells came in long and low, spaced too far apart, the way water breathes before it stops breathing politely altogether. She'd seen this posture of weather her whole life. It was the stance a storm took when it had stopped considering options.

Behind her, the stairs clanged. Milo came up two at a time, breathless, salt-spray already in his hair though the rain hadn't started.

"They're moving," he said. "The Cormorant boats. Three of them, running straight for the channel. Ilsa's flagship holding back at the anchorage."

"Of course she is." Freya lifted the binoculars. The fleet cut white wakes through green-gray water, small as toys, determined as toys. "She sends her divers and lets the ocean take the risk. That's always been the Vane way. Other people's bodies for your own profit."

"Four hours," Milo said. "Maybe three, if the front comes in early."

"And if it does?"

Milo grinned, which was how Freya knew he was frightened. "Then we're the only fools on this coast who'll be exactly where we planned to be."

* * *

They'd spent two days making the lighthouse ready, and Freya suspected that what they'd really been doing was pretending. Sefa had patched the radio array with scavenged wire and optimism, rigging an antenna from the old lightning rod because, as Sefa put it, "if anything on this rock is going to get struck by God, it might as well be useful about it." Milo had hauled their one remaining crate of fuel up ninety-one steps — he'd counted them aloud the whole way, which nobody had asked him to do — and stacked it beside the generator like an offering. Freya herself had done the arithmetic eleven times: broadcast range, signal strength, how many coastal stations still ran receivers through monsoon season, how long they'd have before the storm tore the atmosphere into static.

Eleven times the answer had come back the same. One window. Maybe six minutes of clear transmission between landfall and blackout, if the antenna held, if the generator held, if the sky cooperated even slightly.

Six minutes to say forty years of silence out loud.

Down in the lamp room's lower gallery, Sefa sat cross-legged before the logbook, the Meridian proof spread around her like a card player's winning hand. She'd transcribed everything — the treasury manifests, the scuttling orders signed with initials that matched the Vane Syndicate's founding charter, her father's careful accounting of where he'd hidden each document inside the wreck. Her handwriting had gotten shakier toward the end of the third day, and she hadn't let anyone else touch the pages since.

"There are copies," Sefa said now, without looking up. "You know there have to be copies. I made them while you slept."

"I know."

"So when you burn yours—"

"When I burn mine," Freya said, "it's so that no one can ever buy it back. Not the Syndicate. Not us." She crouched beside the older woman. "Sefa. If I thought burning the ransom papers meant the truth died with them, I wouldn't light the match. But you've got every page memorized twice over, and the originals go out over the air today. What burns is only the price tag."

Sefa looked at her then, eyes red-rimmed and steady. "Your father left you a choice. This is what you chose with it?"

"This is what I chose with it."

Sefa nodded slowly, and then, unexpectedly, laughed — a wet, cracked sound. "He would be insufferable about this. He'd find some way to make it a lesson about tides."

"He usually did."

* * *

The rain arrived not as rain but as a wall being pushed across the sea, a gray curtain walking toward them at the speed of bad news. Freya felt it before she saw it — pressure dropping, ears popping, the lighthouse groaning in its ancient bones like an old man settling into a chair he intends to never leave.

And through it, cutting across the channel, came the Cormorant boats.

"They're mad," Milo said, pressed to the seaward glass. "No one runs a landing in a pre-landfall squall. They'll lose a hull."

"They'll lose more than that," Freya said, "but they don't know it yet. Ilsa told them the chest was here. Told them it was worth more than any of their lives. When you tell men that, they stop doing arithmetic." She turned from the window. "Get below. Everyone to the gallery. It's time."

The first thunder rolled in as she said it — not the sharp crack of nearby lightning but the long, rolling complaint of the whole horizon clearing its throat.

* * *

They came ashore badly, exactly as predicted. Two of the three boats broached in the surf; men spilled into the shallows and dragged themselves up the shingle coughing, gear scattered, dignity abandoned somewhere behind them in the foam. Freya watched from the lamp room as perhaps a dozen figures regrouped on the rocks below and began the climb — not the stairs, which were barred and chained, but the old maintenance scramble up the windward face, handholds slick with forty years of neglect and now with horizontal rain.

Sefa threw the generator switch. It caught, sputtered, caught again. The transmitter hummed to life with a sound like a hive waking.

"Stations standing by," Sefa reported, headphones clamped tight, fingers hovering over the key. "Kess Harbor's answering. Port Amelie. Even the weather bureau's listening — they think we're a distress call."

"We are," Freya said. "Just not ours."

Below, the first of the fleet's men reached the gallery door and began hammering. Then something heavier — a pry bar, maybe an axe — and the old oak complained.

"How long?" Milo asked, bracing a shoulder against the door frame as though his hundred and sixty pounds would matter to an axe.

"However long it takes," Freya said. "Sefa. Send it."

Sefa sent it.

What went out over the air in the next five minutes was read slowly, deliberately, in a navigator's clear voice while a storm tried to drown it: the name of a ship that officially never sailed. A nation erased from every chart. Treasury manifests. Scuttling orders. Initials. Dates. The founding arithmetic of the Vane Syndicate, spoken plainly into the teeth of the monsoon, repeated station to station along four hundred miles of coast as operators woke each other up and passed it along like people passing buckets in a fire line.

Freya stood at the iron stove with the ransom documents in her arms — the appraisal sheets, the buyer's letters, the Syndicate's own valuation of the Meridian proof, everything that translated truth into money. She fed them in one page at a time. The fire took them greedily, orange light jumping in the lamp room's gloom, and with each sheet her future grew simpler and poorer and lighter.

Milo watched her over his shoulder at the door. "That's the debt," he said quietly. "That's the six tides. You're burning the six tides."

"I'm burning the leash," Freya said. "The rest is just paper."

The gallery door split down the middle just as Sefa signed off with the old Meridian sign-call — three short, three long, three short — and somewhere out in the roaring gray, Freya could have sworn she heard it answered.

* * *

Ilsa Vane came up the stairs last, alone, having ordered her soaked and muttering crew back down the rocks with a single gesture. She stood in the ruined doorway with rain streaming off her oilskins, and she looked — not at Freya, not at the transmitter, not at the ashes — but out the seaward glass at the wall of the storm coming ashore behind her.

"It's done, then," she said. "It's going out everywhere."

"Everywhere," Freya agreed.

"You understand what you've cost yourself. There's no buyer now. No ransom. The debt stands, and the Syndicate—" Ilsa stopped. Something moved across her face that Freya had never seen there before, and it took her a moment to name it: relief. "The Syndicate is finished," Ilsa finished, very quietly, as if saying it softly might keep it true. "Forty years, and my aunt built it on a scuttled ship, and some navigator's daughter just read its bones over the radio to every fishing boat on the coast." She laughed, once, without humor. "Do you know what happens to a fleet when its backers burn? The crews scatter by morning. Half my divers have family in the drowned districts. They've been waiting their whole lives for someone to say that name out loud."

"Milo told me you'd erase us after the deal closed," Freya said.

"I know what Milo told you. He was right." Ilsa met her eyes at last. "Stopping was never on my list of options either. My aunt doesn't permit it." She glanced at the broken door, the dead stove, the transmitter still warm. "But there's no deal anymore, Okafor. There's no Syndicate to serve by morning. Which means—" and here her voice did something complicated, "—there's nothing keeping me on this rock except the weather. And the weather says go."

She turned to leave, then paused, one hand on the splintered frame.

"The lighthouse," she said. "It was his, wasn't it? The keeper who vanished. Everyone always wondered."

"He kept it well," Freya said.

"Yes," Ilsa said. "I can see that."

And she went down into the storm, and her boats went out into it, and Freya stood at the glass and watched the fleet dissolve into the gray like a sentence someone had finally stopped writing.

* * *

The monsoon made full landfall at dusk.

The lighthouse took it the way it had taken forty years of storms — stubbornly, complainingly, with its old stones leaning into the wind like a man walking uphill into weather he refuses to admit is beating him. They rode it out in the lamp room with the shutters battened, the generator chugging, tea brewed on the stove where the ransom had burned. Milo told a story about the fuel-crate count that got funnier with each telling. Sefa dozed sitting up with the logbook under her hands like a sleeping child guarding a drum.

Near midnight, between gusts, the receiver crackled.

Freya leaned in, cup halfway to her lips. Through the static, faint and wavering, a voice — Kess Harbor station, then another, then another — reading back fragments. Manifests. Initials. And threaded through all of it, spoken by strangers in accents from every port on the coast, one word repeating like a bell:

*Meridia. Meridia. Meridia.*

Sefa woke. She didn't say anything. She just put her hand flat on the receiver as though it were a living thing, and listened to her drowned nation being born again out of noise, and Freya saw that she was smiling.

Outside, the storm raged on. Inside, the light — the actual lamp, restored, turning slow above them through the lashed dark — swept its beam out over a sea that owed them nothing, and gave it anyway.

Milo raised his tin cup. "To the six tides," he said.

"To the ones we kept," Freya said.

And far to the south, through a break in the clouds that lasted only seconds but long enough, she saw it: a small boat riding the swell toward Kess, running dark, running home — and flying, impossibly, the old nine-starred flag of Meridia.

The storm wasn't finished with them yet. But neither, it seemed, was the morning.
