## 10. After the Water

The storm left the way a rude guest leaves: loudly, without apology, taking things that weren't strictly its to take.

Milo woke on the lighthouse floor with Sefa's boot against his ribs and salt water in his hair and no memory of having fallen asleep. The windows had held. That was the first miracle. The second was Freya, asleep sitting up in her father's old chair, the burned scrap of paper still curled in one fist like she'd been afraid it would come back if she let go.

He stood carefully. Everything hurt in the specific way that meant nothing was broken, just thoroughly used.

Outside, the world had been rinsed. The sky was the pale washed blue of a sheet hung out too long, and the sea lay flat and glittering as if it had never done anything wrong in its life. The *Kestrel* sat where they'd beached her above the tideline, listing gently, looking like a dog that had swum a river and was pretending to be fine about it.

She was not fine. Milo walked her length twice and made a list he didn't want to make: a cracked rail, a torn jib, the portside hull seam weeping in a slow, embarrassed way, and the fuel stores—what remained of them after the narrows—soaked into uselessness. The radio antenna was bent at an angle that suggested prayer.

But she floated. He put his palm flat against her hull and felt her breathe with the tide, and something in his chest unclenched for the first time in six tides.

Behind him, the lighthouse door banged open.

"Right," said Freya, hair wild, eyes red-rimmed but level. "Inventory. Then breakfast. In that order, because if I think about food before I know how bad it is, I'll lie to myself."

"You already lied to yourself," Sefa said, following her out with the kettle. "You're calling this breakfast." She held up a tin of condensed milk and half a packet of ship's biscuit gone soft as sponge.

"Feast of kings," Milo said.

"Kings," Sefa agreed, "in exile."

They ate on the rocks with their feet in the shallows, which was absurd, because the water was still cold enough to ache, but nobody moved. The sun came up properly. Somewhere down the coast a bird was making an argument nobody had asked it to make.

It was Sefa who heard it first—the radio, patched through the lighthouse's ancient receiver, running on battery power Freya had rationed like blood. A voice, thin and crackling, reading shipping notices. And then, between notices, the voice said a word it wasn't supposed to say.

*"—Meridian coastal waters, advisory—"*

Milo stopped chewing.

Sefa set down her biscuit very slowly, the way you'd set down something alive. "Say it again," she whispered, to nobody, to the radio, to forty years of silence.

The voice did not repeat itself. It moved on to tide tables. But it had said it. Meridian. On the air. Out loud. As if the word were simply a place, the way it used to be, before the maps got hungry.

Freya looked at Milo. Milo looked at Sefa. Sefa was crying without any fuss about it, tears just going about their business while her face stayed perfectly calm, and Milo reached over and took her hand and she let him.

"The broadcast went through," Freya said quietly. "Before the storm killed the signal. Somebody heard. Somebody's repeating it."

"Every station on the coast will have it by now," Sefa said. Her voice wobbled and steadied. "You can't un-say a thing like that. You can't un-hear it."

"No," Freya agreed. "That was rather the point."

* * *

The repairs took three days, and Milo learned things about his crew that six tides of terror had somehow never taught him.

He learned that Sefa sang while she worked—not well, not on purpose, just a low Meridian counting-song she claimed she didn't remember learning, keeping rhythm as she stitched the jib with sail thread salvaged from three different boats. He learned that Freya could hold eleven hull rivets in her mouth at once and still deliver a coherent insult, and that she laughed now in a way she hadn't before—a loose, startled laugh, like someone remembering a language.

He learned, on the second day, what Ilsa Vane had left behind.

The Cormorant fleet had limped north ahead of the storm's tail, all but one vessel: a tender, run aground on the far spit, abandoned. Milo rowed out to it with Freya and found it stripped but seaworthy, and in its locker, wrapped in oilcloth, a crate of Syndicate-marked salvage tools—and beneath them, a ledger page in Ilsa's precise hand. Debts. Names. Cancellations.

One line read: *Okafor, F. — balance voided. Asset lost. No further claim.*

"She wrote us off," Freya said. She didn't sound angry. She sounded like someone setting down a heavy bag after carrying it so long she'd forgotten it was there.

"The Syndicate's finished," Milo said. "Half those names are salvage captains who'll never collect either. When the Meridian proof spreads, every contract they signed gets dragged into the light. Nobody pays a debt to a drowning man."

Freya turned the page over, then tucked it into her jacket anyway. Milo understood. You kept proof of your own freedom the way you kept a scar—evidence, in case anyone asked.

On the walk back along the spit, she was quiet for a long time, and then she said, "My father chose the truth over the money. Forty years ago. And it cost him everything, and it bought him nothing anyone could see." She kicked a shell into the surf. "I used to think that was a bad trade."

"And now?"

"Now I think he was paying forward. To people he'd never meet." She glanced sideways at Milo. "To a deckhand from a country that isn't supposed to exist."

Milo's throat did something complicated. "Meridia exists," he said. "It's just been holding its breath."

* * *

The third evening, they hauled the *Kestrel* back to the waterline, and it was time for the conversation none of them wanted to have, so Freya had it over dinner, casually, the way she did everything brave.

"Port of Kess is two days south," she said, studying her biscuit. "There'll be work there. Honest work, slow pay. Or—" she turned the biscuit over, "—Sefa's cousin runs a survey boat out of the northern channels. Storm data, charting, the kind of contracts that don't ask where you slept last winter. Or anyone can walk. Kess has a rail line. No hard feelings. We're not—" she gestured vaguely at the ruined boat, the empty fuel tin, the whole wrecked economy of their lives, "—we're not exactly a going concern."

Silence. The fire popped. Somewhere the bird was still arguing.

"I'll walk," Milo said.

Everyone looked at him. He let it hang for exactly as long as he could bear, which was four seconds.

"To the rail line," he added. "Which is in the opposite direction from the northern channels, so actually I can't, logistically. So I suppose I'm staying."

"Milo," Sefa said, pained.

"What? It's true. Look at a map. Oh wait—" he grinned, "—you can't. Not for Meridia."

Sefa threw a biscuit at him. He caught it and ate it, because waste was waste.

"I'm staying," she said then, simply, wiping her eyes with the back of her hand and pretending she hadn't. "The survey work. With my cousin. If we're all—"

"We're all," Freya said.

Tomas, who had been silent by the fire sharpening a tool that did not need sharpening, cleared his throat. "I ran the numbers," he announced. "Staying together costs less than splitting up. Shared mooring fees, shared stores, shared watch rotations. It's simple arithmetic."

"Tomas," Freya said gently, "you cried when I said we were staying."

"I had salt in my eye."

"It was an indoor fire."

"Sea spray," said Tomas, with dignity, and went back to his knife.

Milo leaned back against the *Kestrel*'s hull and looked up. The stars were out, thick and indifferent and beautiful, and the lighthouse above them stood dark and repaired enough, and the radio inside murmured its late-night frequencies, and somewhere in that murmur, once, faintly, a voice said *Meridian waters* again, and this time nobody froze, because it was becoming a thing words could do.

His father had named him for a river in a country that no longer appeared on any map. For forty years that name had been a private grief he carried like a stone in his pocket, touching it in quiet moments, wondering if anyone else remembered the shape of it.

Now the whole coast was saying it back to him.

* * *

They sailed on the morning tide, north, toward the channels and the survey work and whatever came after. The *Kestrel* rode the swell with her new-stitched jib and her scarred rail, leaking very slightly in a manner Milo chose to interpret as personality.

Freya stood at the wheel. Sefa called the depths from the bow, singing her counting-song under her breath. Tomas napped with the dignity of a man who had earned it through arithmetic. And Milo sat on the stern with his legs over the water, watching the wake unspool behind them like a thread being let out of a great spool.

The radio crackled. Shipping notices, weather advisories, the ordinary music of a coast waking up. And then a new voice—younger, uncertain, reading from something—and it said: *"…the historical territory of Meridia, whose records, recently recovered, indicate…"*

Milo closed his eyes and let the sun land on his face.

Debt-free by ruin rather than payment. Poorer than they'd ever been. Richer than he could explain to anyone who hadn't been there.

"Deckhand!" Freya called from the wheel, and there was that loose, startled laugh in her voice. "Stop brooding at the ocean and bring me the chart. Apparently there's a country on it now."

Milo grinned, hauled himself up, and went to look.
