## 1. Miles, Not Years

The morning fog came off the river like a held breath, and Oscar Fairweather stood at the mouth of the Kettle Spur tunnel with her coffee going cold in one hand and her notebook open against her forearm, counting ties.

Forty-one good. Sixty-two rotten past saving. Nine missing entirely, stolen years ago for firewood by people who had probably never known the line carried anything but freight and rumor. She wrote the numbers down in her small, square hand — *Kettle Spur, mile 118.4 to 121.0, tie condition: terminal* — and felt the small, square satisfaction that always came of it. Numbers didn't lie. Numbers didn't die on a curve in the rain with her ring still warm in his pocket.

She drank the cold coffee anyway. Waste was a sin out here.

Three years now since the accident, and she had learned to measure time the only way that didn't hurt: in miles. Not years — years were full of anniversaries, and anniversaries were full of Thomas — but miles. She had walked or ridden every closed mile of the mountain divisions the company paid her to condemn, eleven hundred and forty of them by last count, and she could tell you the condition of every culvert between Copper Fork and the divide the way other women could tell you the ages of their nieces and nephews. The work was slow, solitary, and officially pointless — surveying lines already scheduled for abandonment, producing reports filed in drawers nobody opened — and she had fought for the assignment with everything she had, because it was exactly that. Pointless. A life measured in dead track could not be blamed for failing to live.

"Morning, Miss Fairweather." Pell, her one hired hand, came up the ballast slope with the transit case balanced on his shoulder and his hat pushed back. He was nineteen, gap-toothed, incurably cheerful, and the closest thing she had to a conversation in any given week. "Fog's thick as porridge downriver. You want the instrument set at the north portal first?"

"North portal," she said. "Then we work back through the cut. I want grades shot every hundred feet through the whole spur before Friday."

"You got it." He hesitated, the way he did when he was about to say something human. "You sleep any?"

"I slept."

"That's what you said yesterday."

"Pell."

"Yes'm." He grinned and went to set up the tripod, whistling something tuneless, and Oscar let the silence close back over her like water.

* * *

They worked the spur all morning. It was good work, honest work, the kind that filled the hands and left the mind nothing to do but the arithmetic of decline. The Kettle Spur had been built in '09 to serve a silver camp that had played out within the decade, and the line had been dying ever since — rails rusting to lace, ties rotting to mulch, the rock cuts slowly shrugging their shoulders and letting the mountain back in. Oscar shot her grades and recorded her elevations and noted, with professional detachment, that the second trestle would not survive another winter. In her report it would read *structure condemned, removal recommended.* What she actually thought, standing under it, looking up through its bones at the white sky, was that it had stood thirty years past any reason to stand, holding nothing, going nowhere, and there were worse fates than that.

At noon they ate hard bread and canned beef in the lee of the cut, and Pell talked about a girl in Copper Fork who worked the hotel desk, and Oscar listened the way she listened to weather reports — attentively, without expectation. He was a good boy. He'd quit her by summer; no one stayed long on the dead lines. She'd write him a reference that would get him onto a live crew, and he'd forget her name inside a year, and that was correct. That was how it should go.

It was mid-afternoon when the light changed.

She noticed it before she understood it — the fog on the river thinning, the air taking on a strange amber weight, the kind of stillness that isn't peace but attention, the whole valley listening for something. Pell felt it too. He stopped cranking the tape and looked downstream, toward the gorge below the old loading ramp where the river ran fast and gray-green over drowned pilings.

"Hear that?" he said.

She hadn't heard anything. That was the thing. The river made its noise always, constant as breath, and beneath it now there was a wrongness — a rhythm broken, a sound arriving out of order. Then, distinct: a cry. Thin, torn by distance, more animal than human.

Oscar was moving before she decided to move, down the ballast slope, along the rotted walkway beside the loading ramp, Pell's boots hammering behind her. At the ramp's edge the river ran wide and shallow over gravel bars, and caught on the bar at the far channel — snagged like driftwood, half in the water — was a man.

He was old, bearded to the chest, dressed in canvas gone gray with years of weather, and he was not moving except that one arm kept reaching, weakly, toward the bank, the way a drowning man reaches even after he has stopped believing in banks.

"He's hung up on the bar," Pell shouted. "Current'll peel him off any—"

"I see it." Oscar was already kicking off her boots. "Rope. The coil in the transit case. Now."

"The current through that channel—"

"*Now*, Pell."

He went. She waded in to her knees and the cold hit her like a fist, the snowmelt cold that stops the heart's argument in one sentence. The man was forty feet out, maybe fifty, wedged against a deadfall lodged in the gravel, and the current broke white around the deadfall and pulled hard beyond it. She could swim — her father had seen to that on the lakes before the lakes, before anything — but fifty feet of that water would take her and give back nothing.

Pell came pounding back with the rope. She tied a bowline around her own waist with fingers that wouldn't obey, checked it twice, checked it again, and paid out line into Pell's hands.

"If I go under, don't be a hero," she said. "Walk the bank. Keep slack off me."

"Yes'm."

The water took her at the thigh and then at the waist and then everywhere at once, and the world became noise and cold and the single bright fact of the old man's arm. She swam on an angle, letting the current help, and the rope dragged at her like a debt, and twice her feet found gravel and lost it. Then her hand closed on canvas — his sleeve, then his wrist, then, God willing, a grip that answered — and she got her arm across his chest and her back to the current and shouted, though she couldn't hear her own voice, for Pell to haul.

They came ashore ten minutes later, if time meant anything, coughing and crawling, the old man between them, and lay on the gravel while the river went on saying what it always said, indifferent, eternal.

* * *

They carried him up to camp on a litter of cross-ties and canvas, and laid him by the fire in the lean-to shelter they'd rigged against the weather, and Oscar did what could be done, which was not much. He was sixty if he was a day, maybe older — mountain age was a guesswork — and he was hurt in ways that didn't show cleanly. Blood at his temple, dried black. Bruising across the ribs. And when she cut away the shirt from his left side she found the wound that explained the rest: a bullet hole, high under the arm, ugly and angry, days old at least, badly bound with a strip of filthy cloth.

"Somebody shot him," Pell said quietly.

"Somebody shot him," Oscar agreed. Her voice came out flat. Flat was useful. Flat was a tool. "Boil water. Get my kit. And saddle nothing, touch nothing else until I say."

The old man surfaced once while she cleaned the wound, eyes opening — pale gray, sharp as chipped flint even now — fixing on her face with an intensity that had nothing feverish in it.

"Fairweather," he rasped.

She went still. "How do you know my name?"

His mouth moved. It might have been a laugh. "Asked... after you. Copper Fork. Telegraph fella owed me." His hand came up — trembling, black-nailed, strong underneath — and closed on her wrist with a strength that startled her. "You're the one walks the dead lines. Measures 'em."

"I survey them. Who are you? Who shot you?"

"Names later." His eyes were losing focus and dragging themselves back by main force. "Listen. Listen to me, girl, because I haven't got the time to say it pretty, and you've got the look of someone who counts things careful." The grip tightened. "I need a promise. Before I let go of you, I need your promise."

"You need a doctor. There's one in Copper Fork, we can have you there by—"

"No doctors." It came out with sudden force, and then a fit of coughing bent him nearly double, and she held him through it and felt the ruin of his lungs under her hands. When he could speak again it was barely above the fire's crackle. "No doctors. They'll ask questions. Questions travel. You understand me? Everything travels. Everything gets bought."

"Who's *they*?"

He looked at her for a long moment. Whatever he saw in her face seemed to settle something.

"Harkness," he said. "Ezra Harkness. Prospector. And before you decide I'm fever talking — check my pack. It's downstream yet, or sunk, and either way—" Another cough. "Either way the map's sewn in the bedroll lining. Brass tag with it. You'll know the town name. Everyone knew the town name. Nobody says it anymore."

"Bellhaven," Oscar said, before she could stop herself.

Something moved behind his eyes — grief, or vindication, or both wearing the same coat. "So you do remember." He sagged back against the canvas. "Fifteen years ago the papers called it a flood. Act of God. Dam up the High Passage gave way natural, they said, buried the valley town, and the company paid out pennies and the government signed off, and everyone who might've said different got quiet or got gone." His hand found hers again. "I was up in the passage country when it happened. I saw what came down that river. Floodwater doesn't carry *that*. And I've spent fifteen years walking back up to prove it, and they've spent fifteen years making sure I wouldn't arrive."

Outside, the fog was coming down off the ridges again, folding the camp into itself. Pell stood at the edge of the firelight with the kettle in his hands, not pretending he wasn't listening.

"Ezra Harkness," Oscar said slowly. "There was a notice posted in Copper Fork this spring. Company circular. Reward for information regarding a trespasser in the upper watershed. They described a man with a white beard."

"They described me," Harkness agreed, almost cheerfully. "Good likeness, too. Cost them money, that poster. Everything costs them money, and they've got so much of it they've forgotten what it's for." He turned his head and looked at her straight on, and the fever was in his face now, riding him, but the will behind it burned steady as a signal lamp. "Here's the bargain, Miss Fairweather. I'm dying. I've known it since the bullet went bad, and I'd have made the source canyon anyway if the river hadn't spat me out here. But the river's dropping — dry season, every day lower — and there's one window left to run the Passage before it goes too shallow to float anything out. Nine days. Maybe ten."

"Float what out?"

"What I buried there. Proof. Core samples. Photographs. Papers with signatures on them that would hang the men who built that dam knowing — *knowing* — it would fail." His breath rattled. "I can't carry it. You can. You're the only soul on this river who knows the dead lines, and the dead lines are the only way up that isn't watched. That's why I asked after you. That's why I've been asking after you for two years."

The fire popped. Somewhere downriver the water said its one word, over and over.

"You're asking me to break the law," Oscar said.

"I'm asking you to keep a promise to a dying man," Harkness said. "The law can sort out whose side it was on afterward. It usually does, eventually, if enough people make it embarrassed." He smiled then, briefly, terribly, blood on his teeth. "You measure your life in miles, girl. I've watched you do it — the way you count everything, ties and grades and days, so you don't have to count anything that matters. Well. Here's a mile worth measuring. Here's the one that goes somewhere."

Her hand had gone cold, and it wasn't the river's doing. *Nobody says it anymore.* But she remembered Bellhaven — she'd been twenty, and the newspapers had been full of it for a month, and Thomas had read her the casualty lists aloud in his careful voice until she'd asked him to stop. Forty-one souls, final count. A town erased from the timetables the same year the spur lines started closing, one more entry moving from *active* to *abandoned* in the great ledger of things the company no longer maintained.

She had been measuring dead track for three years because living track had killed the man she was going to marry. And here was a dying stranger telling her the dead track was the only road left worth walking.

"Promise me," Harkness whispered. His grip was failing now; she could feel it going, strength draining out through the wound like heat. "Carry it to the source. Before the dry season ends. Promise me, and I can let go."

"Don't talk like that. Drink this—"

"Promise me."

And Oscar Fairweather, who had spent three years promising herself nothing, who had structured her entire existence around the principle that promises were how the world got its hooks into you — looked down into the pale flint eyes of a man the river had given her, and heard herself say:

"I promise."

Harkness exhaled, long and slow, like a man setting down a load carried a very long way. "Good," he murmured. "Bedroll. Sewn lining. Don't let anyone see you take it out." His eyes drifted shut. "Dawn... I'll tell you the rest. Where I cached it. The marks on the map. You'll want to..."

He didn't finish the sentence. He slept, or sank, breathing in shallow pulls, and Oscar sat by him through the falling evening with her wet clothes steaming at the fire and her promise sitting in her chest like swallowed stone.

Pell brought her dry blankets without being asked and crouched across the fire from her, face orange in the flames.

"Miss Fairweather," he said carefully, "those company men that come through Copper Fork last month asking about trespassers. Clean-shaven fellows. New boots."

"I remember."

"You think they're still looking?"

Oscar looked at the old man's ruined side, at the filthy bandage, at the brass tag she had not yet gone to find, and felt something in herself shift — some long-frozen thing cracking along an old seam, not pain exactly, not yet, but the place where pain would be, waking up.

"Yes," she said. "And tomorrow, so are we."

* * *

Ezra Harkness died at dawn.

It was quick, at the end — a rattle, a stillness, a gray light coming up over the water — and he went with his right hand clenched around something he had held all night without waking. When Oscar pried the fingers open, gently, the way she did everything now, she found a brass survey tag stamped with a lot number and two words:

**BELLHAVEN — 41**

Forty-one. The final count. He had carried the number of the dead against his skin for fifteen years.

Oscar stood at the river's edge as the sun cleared the ridge and lit the water gold, the tag heavy in her palm, the map waiting in the bedroll behind her, nine days ticking somewhere in the sound of the current. Three years of miles, and every one of them had led here.

She closed her hand around the tag and turned upstream.
