## 2. The Prospector's Bargain

The fever broke once in the night and Oscar allowed herself to believe it might break for good.

She sat by the fire with the stranger's pack across her knees, going through it the way she went through everything — methodically, without sentiment — because a man bleeding out on her bedroll had a right to have his affairs in order, and because if he died she would need to know who to send word to. The pack held little: a tin cup dented nearly flat, a sack of dried beans gone to dust at the bottom, a folding knife worn to a sliver of its original blade. Forty years of a life, and it fit inside two hands.

And then there was the oilcloth packet, tied with waxed cord, that he had kept beneath his shirt against his skin until the blood and sweat had stained the cloth the color of old tea.

She hadn't opened it. She told herself this was principle. It was also fear. Some part of her — the part that still woke at night hearing the shriek of couplers and the long groan of steel giving way — understood that packets wrapped in oilcloth and carried against the heart were not carried for their health. They were carried because they were dangerous, or precious, or both, and she had buried enough of both kinds of thing already to last her the rest of her days.

The fire ticked and settled. Up the slope, the dead spur ran off into the dark like a sentence no one had finished. Somewhere below, the river worked at its stones, patient as arithmetic.

Harkness stirred. His eyes came open — not all at once but in stages, like a house being lit room by room — and found her.

"You went through my things," he said. His voice was gravel dragged over gravel.

"I did. I'd apologize, but you'd have done the same."

A sound that might have been a laugh, if laughter could be bled. "Fairweather." He said her name slowly, tasting it. "You're the one who closed the Kestrel Spur. Three years back. There was a piece in the *Gazette*. Woman engineer. First of her kind to sign a decommission order." He coughed, wet and deep. "They made you famous for burying a railroad."

"They made me useful," Oscar said. "Famous is a different animal."

"And which are you tonight?"

She fed a stick to the fire rather than answer. The flame took it greedily, and in the sudden light she saw how bad he looked — the gray under the brown of his skin, the way his chest hitched between breaths like an engine losing compression. She had run locomotives long enough to know the sound of something running on its last reserve.

"The bullet's still in there," she said. "I can see the wound's angry. I can boil water and I can bind it clean, but I can't cut, Harkness. I don't have the instruments and I don't have the nerve. You need a doctor in Copper Bend, and that's two days downriver even if we started now, and you can't be moved."

"I know what I need." He said it without self-pity, the way a man reports weather. "I've known since yesterday morning when I couldn't get up the bank anymore. I'm not asking you to save me, Miss Fairweather. I'm asking you to finish something."

He held out his hand — palm up, fingers curled slightly inward, a hand shaped by sixty years of work into something like a root. "The packet. Give it here."

Oscar hesitated one heartbeat longer than she meant to. Then she untied the cord and set the oilcloth bundle in his palm, and watched him hold it the way other men held sleeping children.

"Open it," he said. "No. Wait." He closed his eyes, gathering himself the way a swimmer gathers before a cold river. "First you'll hear why. Because if you open it without knowing why, you'll do the sensible thing, and the sensible thing is exactly what they're counting on."

"Who is *they*?"

"The Meridian Consolidated Mining Company," Harkness said, "and every man whose boots they've shined and whose mouth they've bought from the source of the Serrano clear down to the sea." He opened his eyes again, and there was something in them now that hadn't been there before — not fever, or not only fever. A banked fire, forty years banked, finally given air. "You asked me last night who shot me. I lied. It wasn't claim jumpers. It was two men in company canvas, and they shot me because I wouldn't hand this over, and they think I'm dead, and the only reason they think so is that I fell into the river and let it carry me four miles before I could crawl out."

Oscar felt the hair rise along her forearms. She kept her voice level. "Then they may come looking."

"They'll come looking where I was going, not where I ended up. That buys us time. Not much." He shifted against the bedroll and winced, and she saw him master the pain by sheer habit, the way she herself mastered grief — by refusing it a chair at the table. "Now open it."

She untied the knot. Inside the oilcloth was a second wrapping of waxed linen, and inside that, folded into eighths, a map.

It was drawn on survey linen, the good stuff, the kind that cost more than a prospector earned in a season — and it was old. The ink had gone brown at the folds. But the hand that drew it was sure, and trained: elevation lines laid in with a draftsman's discipline, benchmarks marked with the official triangular symbol, and running up the left margin, in letters small and fierce as thorns, a title:

**THE HIGH PASSAGE — SURVEY OF THE UPPER SERRANO AND THE SOURCE CANYON — 1889**

Oscar went very still.

Every surveyor in the territory knew the name the way sailors knew the name of a drowned ship. The High Passage was the route that wasn't. Thirty miles of canyon above the last gauge station, where the Serrano climbed into country so broken that three separate expeditions had gone in during the eighties and come back short-handed and silent. The railroad had surveyed it once, at the height of the boom, and then the surveys had stopped, and the maps had been recalled, and within five years the whole upper valley had been quietly struck from the public record — folded into a mining district, posted against trespass, and forgotten by everyone except the men who owned it.

Oscar had spent three years surveying dead lines. She knew better than most what it meant when a living line was made dead on purpose.

"This is a government survey," she said. Her finger hovered above the benchmark triangles without touching them. "This isn't a prospector's sketch. This came out of the Territorial Survey Office."

"It did." Harkness's voice had dropped, as though the map itself might overhear. "I was on the crew. Junior rodman, nineteen years old, wet behind the ears and grateful for the wage. Eleven of us went up the Passage in June of '89. Seven came down in August." He paused. "And the seven of us came down knowing less than we'd carried up, because Captain Aldous collected our field books at the trailhead and told us the survey was voided. Told us we'd seen nothing worth recording. Told us that if we spoke of it, the Company had long arms and the Territory had short memories."

"What did you see?"

For a long moment she thought he wouldn't answer. The fire cracked. The river murmured its one word over and over.

"We saw the dam," Harkness said.

Oscar's hand stopped moving over the map entirely.

"There's a gorge up there — the Narrows, the old charts call it — where the whole upper valley pinches down to eighty yards of granite. Any engineer with half an eye looks at that gorge and thinks what we thought: *there's a dam site God himself drew the plans for.* And someone thought it before us. When we came through, it was built. Finished. Fresh masonry, new timber cradle, a reservoir backed up six miles behind it — and not one word of it on any public record, because the Company built it in secret to feed their mills, and a secret dam doesn't get inspected, and an uninspected dam gets built cheap." His breath caught. He pushed through it. "We sounded the reservoir. We measured the masonry. My job was the rod, and I'll tell you what the rod said if you tell me you understand figures, Miss Fairweather, because I've carried those numbers forty years and I'd like to put them down before I go."

"I'm an engineer," Oscar said quietly. "Give me the numbers."

He gave them to her. Crest height. Masonry thickness at base and crown. Spillway capacity. The composition of the fill they'd scraped samples of when the crew chief wasn't watching — gravel and poor lime, when the figures demanded cut granite and hydraulic cement. She did the arithmetic in her head as he spoke, the way she did everything, and the answer arrived in her chest like a coupling snapping under load.

"That dam fails," she said. "Under spring flood, it fails. It isn't a question of whether. It's a question of when."

"Spring of '91," Harkness said. "April the ninth."

The date landed in the firelight between them and lay there glowing.

"Bellhaven," Oscar said. The word came out of her before she could stop it, and once it was out she heard everything packed inside it — the newspaper accounts she'd read as a girl, the official finding reprinted in every paper in the territory: *cloudburst, natural flood, tragic act of God*, the town of Bellhaven washed from the map with two hundred and eleven souls, the valley sealed afterward for "reclamation," the file closed, the story finished.

"There was no cloudburst," Harkness said. "There was a wall of water ninety feet high with a broken dam behind it, and the Company knew for two years that the dam would fail, because we told them. We sent the figures down with Captain Aldous. And instead of draining the reservoir or rebuilding the crest, they moved their equipment out of the flood path and moved nothing else, and they waited, and when it came down they called it weather." He was shaking now, whether from fever or fury she couldn't tell. "Two hundred and eleven people, Miss Fairweather. Farmers. A schoolteacher. Children. And the men who killed them wrote the report on the killing."

Oscar discovered that she had risen to her feet without deciding to. She stood over the fire with the map in her hands and felt something move in her that had been fixed and rusted shut for three years — some great gate in her chest grinding open an inch, letting through a draft of air that hurt.

She thought of the Kestrel Spur. She thought of the accident that had not been her fault and the engagement that had been, in every way that mattered, its casualty — Daniel's face in the last letter, polite as a receipt. She thought of how she had measured her life since in track miles, in decommissioned bridges, in the tidy arithmetic of things ending, because endings were safe and beginnings were where the trains collided.

And she thought: *two hundred and eleven.* Someone had counted them. Someone always counted.

"Sit down," Harkness said gently. "Before you fall down. And listen to the rest, because the rest is the bargain."

She sat.

"The Company has spent thirty years making sure this stays buried. Every man from that survey crew who talked was ruined or worse. Old Aldous drank himself into the river in '94. The others scattered and quieted. I kept my head down and my mouth shut and I prospected the edges of the Passage for thirty years, and I told myself I was waiting for the right moment, and the truth is I was a coward with a good excuse." He looked at his own hands. "Two months ago I found proof they can't burn. Not testimony — testimony dies with the witness. *Evidence.* Core samples from the dam's own masonry, pulled in '89 by our crew and cached in a survey vault at the source, with photographs and the Company's own internal memos — correspondence between their chief engineer and the board discussing the cost of doing it properly versus the cost of letting it fail. Their own words, in their own ink, sealed in iron. I've spent two months getting to it and two weeks getting back, and I got as far as the lower canyon before they caught up with me."

"The vault," Oscar said. "At the source."

"At the source. Where the river's born and nobody's supposed to go." He turned his head and looked at her straight on, and the fever burned behind his eyes but the will behind the fever was granite. "Here's the bargain, and I won't dress it up. I'm dying. You know it, I know it, and the only mercy left in it is speed. I can't carry this. But the dry season ends in five weeks, and when it ends, the Serrano drops too low to float anything heavier than a leaf out of the upper canyon. Whatever comes out of that valley has to come out by water, and it has to come out soon, or it doesn't come out at all — and the Company knows the clock as well as we do. They're sealing the trails now. By month's end the Passage will be shut tighter than a drum, and whatever's in that vault will sit in the dark until the world ends."

He lifted the map toward her. His arm trembled with the weight of it, which was no weight at all, which was everything.

"So I'm asking. Take the Passage upstream. Find the vault. Bring the proof down the river before the dry season ends, and put it in front of eyes that can't be bought." A ghost of a smile crossed the ruin of his face. "I'd ask a man to do it, but every man I'd trust is dead, bought, or scared, and I haven't got time to sort out which is which. Besides—" the smile widened a fraction— "they gave you a medal for closing railroads. Let's see what they give you for opening one."

Oscar didn't take the map. Not yet.

"Why me?" she said. "You fell into my camp by accident. You don't know me."

"I know your file," Harkness said. "I've read it twice. I know you signed the Kestrel order with a protest attached — a formal protest, on the record, against the inspection shortcuts that caused the washout. Nobody makes a protest like that unless the numbers keep them up at night." His voice dropped to almost nothing. "And I know about the trestle at Mile Forty-One. What happened there. Who was lost."

The fire popped. Oscar's jaw tightened until her teeth ached.

"Don't," she said.

"I'm not saying it to wound you. I'm saying it because I need you to understand that I know what I'm asking. I'm asking a woman who buried her whole future at Mile Forty-One to walk back into a canyon full of the same kind of dying, for strangers she never met, on the promise of a dead prospector." He drew a breath that whistled. "I'm asking because grief that's got nowhere to go turns to poison, Miss Fairweather, and I've drunk mine for thirty years, and I would spare you the taste if I could. This—" he touched the map— "is the only cure I ever found. Use mine. Don't waste yours."

Above the camp, wind moved down the dead spur, sighing through the ties of a line that no train would ever ride again. Oscar looked at the map in her lap — at the fine brown lines climbing toward the source, at the small annotation in Harkness's later hand beside the Narrows: *the numbers do not lie, whatever the men do.*

She thought about lying. About telling him she'd try, and then riding down to Copper Bend and handing the packet to a magistrate and letting the law do what the law did, which was nothing, slowly. It would be sensible. It would be safe. It would be exactly what they were counting on.

Three years ago she had stood in the wreckage at Mile Forty-One and promised herself she would never again be responsible for another human life, because responsibility was the thing that broke. Now a dying man was putting two hundred and eleven lives and one living woman's remaining years into her hands and calling it a bargain.

"What do I get?" she asked, and heard how hard her own voice came out. "Out of this bargain. What's in it for me?"

Harkness considered her for a long moment, and something almost tender moved through the fever-glaze of his eyes.

"A reason," he said. "That's all it ever is. That's all it ever was."

* * *

He lasted through the night, and she gave him the dawn.

Toward the end the fever took him somewhere far away, and he talked — fragments, names, a litany of the dead and the silenced: *Aldous, who drank; Prentiss, who took the money; the telegraph woman at Deadfall Junction, Quist, she's true, find Quist; the tag, don't forget the tag.* Oscar boiled water and changed the dressing and held his shoulders through the worst of the shaking, and at some point in the black hours she realized she had stopped counting the minutes and started simply being present, and that this, too, was a kind of arithmetic she had forgotten how to do.

Near first light he surfaced one last time, clear-eyed, urgent.

"The tag," he whispered. "In my coat. Left pocket."

She found it: a brass survey tag on a steel ring, stamped with the Territorial Survey mark and a benchmark number, and beneath the number, punched deep into the metal by a hand that had wanted the marking to survive fire and water and time:

**BELLHAVEN STATION — EST. 1874**

Bellhaven Station. A benchmark set in the town itself, at the depot, by surveyors who had measured that ground as solid and permanent and forever. A town that officially never flooded, stamped in brass by the government that said so.

"Hold it where I can see it," Harkness breathed.

She held it. The brass caught the first gray light coming up over the ridge, and for a moment it glowed like a small stubborn star.

"That's the whole argument," he said. "Everything else is paper. Paper burns. That—" his fingers brushed the tag, and slipped— "that's the ground itself, testifying."

His hand fell. His breathing slowed, spaced itself out, became the sound of a tide going out. Oscar sat with his hand in hers and watched the light strengthen over the dead spur, and when the sun finally cleared the ridge and laid a bar of gold across the camp, she knew — the way she knew a rail was cracked before she ever saw the fracture — that he was gone.

She sat for a while longer. Then she closed his eyes, folded his hands over the oilcloth packet's absence, and went to the river to wash her face in water that had come down from the forbidden source, past the secret dam, over the drowned town, carrying — as it had carried for thirty years — everything nobody would say aloud.

When she came back she packed her kit with the economy of long practice. Theodolite case. Rations. Rope. The map, wrapped again in its oilcloth, rode against her ribs where his heartbeat had been.

Last of all she picked up the brass tag and stood turning it in the morning light, reading the benchmark number, thinking of a depot platform two hundred souls strong that had been there one April evening and gone by midnight.

Five weeks until the dry season ended. Thirty miles of sealed canyon. Two hundred and eleven reasons.

Oscar Fairweather shouldered her pack, turned her back on the grave she had dug at first light, and started up the river — and did not yet see, far downstream around the first bend, the glint of oars and the flash of clean white canvas where two boats had entered the current, following her wake.
