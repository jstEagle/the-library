## 3. Men in Clean Boots

They came up the dead spur at mid-morning, three days after she'd put Harkness in the ground, and Oscar Fairweather knew them for trouble before she could see their faces — because of their boots.

She was re-staking a curve on the old grade when she heard them, boots on rotted ties carrying farther than voices in that thin mountain air. She'd spent three years listening to mountains, and she had learned to read sound the way other people read weather. These footsteps were wrong. Too even. Too deliberate. Men who walked like that were men who expected the ground to hold them, and no man who worked this country expected anything of the kind.

She straightened, set her stake mallet down with care, and turned.

Three of them, coming along the abandoned right-of-way in single file, and every one of them dressed as if he'd stepped off a city street an hour ago. Good wool coats. New canvas packs. And the boots — polished leather, city boots, the kind of boots that had never known mud deeper than a hotel lobby. In a country where Harkness had worn through two pairs of hobnails in a season, they were as conspicuous as a lit window at midnight.

The one in front lifted a hand in greeting. He was forty or so, square-built, with a face like a closed ledger and a smile that arrived a half-second after it should have, as though it had been sent by wire from somewhere else.

"Morning, ma'am." His voice was pleasant, unhurried, the voice of a man who had never once been interrupted. "Fair day for surveying."

"It's a fair day," Oscar said, "for most things."

"Name's Corliss." He didn't offer to shake. Neither did his companions, who flanked him with the easy spacing of men who had done this before — not violence, exactly, but the geometry of it. "We're looking for a prospector. Goes by Harkness. Old fellow, gray beard, half-deaf in the left ear. Understands he's been working this stretch of river."

"You understand correctly that you're a long way from anywhere," Oscar said. "Who's asking?"

Corliss produced a paper from inside his coat with a flourish that wanted very much to be official. "Warrant of inquiry. We have reason to believe the man is holding property belonging to the Meridian Consolidated Mining and Development Company."

Oscar took the paper and read it slowly, giving herself time to think, which was the first useful thing fear had done for her all morning. The document was typeset neatly enough. It named Harkness. It cited some statute or other in language that sounded legal the way a recording of rain sounds like rain. But there was no seal at the bottom. No signature she recognized. No county, no court, no commission — nothing but a printed assertion wearing the costume of authority.

"This has no seal," she said.

"The seal is being renewed," Corliss said smoothly. "Administrative matter. The warrant's validity isn't in question."

"Everything's validity is in question until it's sealed," Oscar said. "That's what a seal is *for*. I'm a licensed engineer, Mr. Corliss. I deal in documents that carry weight. This carries none."

Something moved behind the ledger-face — not anger, quite. Recalculation. "You're Fairweather," he said. "The engineer. We heard there was a woman running levels on the dead lines."

"You heard correctly."

"They say you surveyed every closed spur between Silver Bend and the divide. Three years of it. That's a strange occupation for a woman of your reputation."

"I find it restful."

He smiled again, that delayed smile, and glanced past her shoulder at her camp — the tent, the instrument cases lined up with military precision against a fallen log, the cold fire ring. One of the flanking men drifted a step sideways, casually, the way a man drifts when he wants a better angle on something. Oscar felt the movement register along her spine like a change in grade.

"Harkness," Corliss said. "Have you seen him?"

* * *

Here is what Oscar Fairweather had never done in thirty-one years of careful living: lied.

Not well, not badly, not once. Her father had raised her on railroads and rectitude — *a level tells the truth or it's broken, and so do you* — and she had built her whole life on that foundation, plank by measured plank. She had reported her own errors on surveys at cost to her standing. She had returned overpayments to companies that would never have missed them. When Daniel died — when the bridge failed, when the inquiry asked its questions — she had answered every one of them plainly, though plainness cost her everything she had left of him, including the right to grieve without an audience.

She did not lie. It was not virtue, exactly. It was architecture. Truth was the material she had built herself from, and she had never trusted any other load-bearing substance.

And now here stood a man in clean boots asking her a question whose answer was folded into a theodolite case six feet behind her, and she understood — with the strange clarity of a woman reading a grade she'd have sworn was level — that the truth would get someone killed.

Because Harkness was dead. Because she had buried him at dawn two mornings ago under a cairn of river stone above the high-water mark, and because in his last hours he had pressed a rolled oilcloth map into her hands and made her promise — *you carry it up, girl, you carry it to the source before the water drops, swear it on whatever you've got left to swear on* — and because she had looked into his failing eyes and sworn.

If these men learned she had seen Harkness, they would want to know what passed between them. If they learned what passed between them, they would take the map. If they took the map, then Harkness had died for nothing, and Bellhaven — drowned Bellhaven, unmarked Bellhaven, the town that officially never flooded — would stay drowned forever, and the men who flooded it would go on buying silence by the pound.

So: lie, or bury another thing beside Harkness.

"Haven't seen him," Oscar said.

Her voice came out level. That surprised her. Inside, something enormous shifted — a settling, like a bridge taking a load it wasn't rated for — but the voice held.

"When?" Corliss said.

"I don't follow."

"When did you last see him? If ever."

"I told you. Never." She let her eyes go flat and professional, the way she looked at a set of figures that didn't merit concern. "Prospectors don't come to my camps. I measure dead track, Mr. Corliss. I don't entertain."

The flanking man had drifted again. He was maybe fifteen feet from her instrument line now, hands loose at his sides, gaze moving across the theodolite cases with the idle thoroughness of a customs inspector. The third man hadn't spoken at all. He stood slightly back, watching her face rather than the camp, and it was him she liked least, because he was doing what she did — reading fine measurements.

"Mind if we look around?" Corliss said. "Standard procedure. Man goes missing with company property, we check the vicinity."

"He's not missing. He's holding company property, according to your paper. Those are different conditions." She said it pedantically, deliberately, because pedantry was armor and she knew how to wear it. "And no, you may not. This is my survey camp, established under a commission from the Department of Railways. You have an unsealed paper and no jurisdiction I recognize. Come back with a magistrate and a seal, and I'll make you coffee while you search every case I own."

The wordless one spoke for the first time. "Could just look," he said quietly. "Save everybody the paperwork."

"You could," Oscar agreed. "And I could file a complaint naming three unidentified men who accosted a government surveyor in the field, which would be investigated by the same department that pays my wages, and which would put your names and descriptions in a record that doesn't wash out. Gentlemen, I am paid to be precise. Be certain you want precision."

Silence, except the river below, talking to itself over its stones.

It was Corliss who broke it, and he broke it lightly, which frightened her more than a threat would have. He spread his hands. "No offense meant, Miss Fairweather. Field folk are touchy about their camps. Perfectly understandable." He began turning away, then paused, and the pause was engineered, she realized later — every second of it. "One more thing. The old man. If he comes through — sick, maybe. Dying men wander toward rivers." He reached into his coat and brought out a small card, holding it out. "Wire that address. There's a reward. Substantial. Enough that a woman measuring dead track might stop measuring dead track."

Oscar looked at the card and did not take it.

"I'll keep it in mind," she said, which was true, and therefore cost her nothing, and was also the third lie of her life by construction, because keeping it in mind was precisely what she intended — to remember the address so she would know, always, where the enemy kept his door.

Corliss set the card on top of her instrument case instead, patted it once, and smiled his delayed smile. "Pleasure, ma'am. Watch the river this season. She runs low early."

Then they went, back down the dead spur in single file, their clean boots loud on the rotten ties, and Oscar stood in her camp with her heart going like a driving rod and did not move until the sound of them was gone entirely, and then for a long time after.

* * *

She gave them an hour. Then she walked the spur a quarter mile south and climbed the talus slope above it, to a granite shelf where she could see the line both ways, and confirmed with her field glasses that they had not doubled back. They had not. They had gone downriver, unhurried, men with nowhere to be because everywhere was theirs.

*Watch the river this season. She runs low early.*

They knew about the dry season. Which meant they knew about the High Passage. Which meant Harkness had been right that they would come, and wrong only about how much time he'd have.

Oscar sat down on the cold granite and made herself think like an engineer, because feeling like a human being was not currently survivable.

Assessment: they believed her. Provisionally. A woman alone, flat-voiced, technically correct — she fit their picture of harmless. But the wordless one had watched her too closely, and Corliss had left a card on her instrument case like a man marking a claim. They would ask downstream. Deadfall had a telegraph office; Deadfall had Mara Quist; Deadfall had eyes. Sooner or later someone would mention a dying prospector heading upriver with a map, and then the provisional belief would expire, and they would come back, and next time there would be a seal on the paper or there wouldn't be a paper at all.

Time remaining: unknown. Days, probably. Not weeks.

Conclusion: she could not wait for the supply train, could not resupply properly, could not run the passage with the leisurely thoroughness of three years' habit. She would have to go now, light, fast, upstream, ahead of them and ahead of the falling water.

Which left the map.

She descended, packed her camp with the economy of long practice, and then stood over the instrument line, considering. The theodolite case was the obvious hiding place, which made it the worst. The obvious place was where searching eyes went first. But there was a second consideration: the map needed to be on her body or nowhere, because camps could be burned, tents overturned, cases opened at gunpoint. What couldn't be searched easily was a woman's person, and what wouldn't be searched at all was the one thing no fixer would think to open.

She took the transit case — the big one, mahogany and brass, her father's, older than her grief — and emptied it. Into the fitted velvet slots where the scope should sit, she coiled the oilcloth map, tight, snug as a cartridge. Over it she laid the spare lens tissue and the cleaning kit. Then she closed the lid, threw the latches, and slung it across her back where a transit case belonged, in plain sight, carried openly by a surveyor doing what surveyors do.

Let them look. Let them lift it, weigh it, hear the rattle of lens tissue. Nobody on earth opens a transit case to find it empty of its transit — except that hers would be, and the emptiness would tell them nothing, because they'd assume she'd stowed the instrument separately, because everyone assumes an engineer travels with her instrument.

Harkness would have laughed. *Hide it in the truth, girl.* He hadn't said that. But he would have.

She kicked dirt over her fire ring, took one last bearing on the cairn above the high-water mark — a compass point she intended to keep for the rest of her life — and started upriver.

She had lied three times in one morning, and the sky had not fallen, and the mountains had not shrugged her off their shoulders. Instead something else had happened, something she examined on the climb with the detached interest of a woman testing new steel: the fear was still there, cold and constant, but it had changed shape overnight. It had stopped being the fear of a cornered animal and become something with edges and direction. Fear with a bearing. Fear she could stake a line with.

They thought she was a woman measuring dead track. Fine. Let them measure her that way.

She adjusted the transit case on her shoulder, checked her watch against the sun, and set her pace for the miles ahead — and if the pace was faster than any survey required, and harder than any honest work demanded, that was nobody's business but hers, and the river's, and the dead man's.

Behind her, far down the valley, a train whistle sounded — thin, distant, wrong for this line, which had no schedule and no reason to run. Someone was coming up the mountain by rail.

Oscar didn't slow down. But she listened, all the way around the bend, and memorized the sound.
