## 4. The Inspector on the 4:10

The 4:10 out of Meridian Junction ran eleven minutes late, which Elias Vane noted without judgment because lateness was the honest condition of every train in this country and he had long ago stopped expecting the world to keep its appointments. He stood on the platform with his carpetbag in one hand and his commission folder in the other, watching steam crawl along the shed roof like something living that couldn't decide where to die, and he thought about his father's hands.

He thought about them often lately. More since the wire had come from the Department — *proceed to Kestrel Division, investigate reported sabotage of supply traffic, file interim assessment within fourteen days* — because sabotage was a word with a particular smell to it, and Elias had learned young that when a word smelled wrong it was usually the paper underneath that was rotten.

The conductor took his ticket without looking at him. Inspectors were not popular men. Elias found an empty compartment near the rear of the second coach, settled the carpetbag at his feet, and opened the folder for perhaps the hundredth time.

Three pages. That was all the Department had given him. Three pages on the death of one Silas Harkness, prospector, age sixty-one, cause listed as exposure and heart failure, found by a survey party working the closed spur above Cold Run. And stapled behind those three pages, thin as a confession nobody wanted to read aloud, a single sheet listing the names of persons who had been *in contact* with the deceased prior to death.

One name on that sheet was underlined in someone else's ink.

**O. Fairweather — railroad surveyor (contract), currently engaged: Kestrel Division closed-line inventory.**

Elias knew the name before he'd ever seen it on this page. He had known it for six years, in the way a man knows a scar on his own body — not by looking at it but by the way weather made it ache.

He closed the folder and looked out the window instead, at the platform sliding away, at the water tower with its faded company crest, at the mountains assembling themselves in the west like a jury being seated.

---

There was a version of the story the Department believed, and Elias had been hired — insofar as a government man is ever hired rather than used — to make the evidence agree with it. Silas Harkness had died of natural causes. The supply trains on the Kestrel line had suffered damaged couplings, cut brake lines, and one derailed ore car, all attributable to vandals or disgruntled former employees. The Consolidated Mining & Water Company, whose freight contracts fed half the territory's revenue, had expressed concern and offered full cooperation.

Full cooperation. Elias turned the phrase over in his mind like a coin he suspected of being shaved. In his nine years as an inspector he had learned that the words most freely offered were the ones least meant. Companies cooperated the way rivers cooperated with bridges.

What troubled him was not the sabotage. Sabotage was almost always smaller than it looked — two men and a wrench, a grudge, a payday. What troubled him was the geography. The incidents clustered along exactly forty miles of track, and those forty miles were the only stretch of the Kestrel Division that ran within wagon distance of the upper river. The same river, if a man traced it far enough upstream past the reservoir works and the sealed survey camps and the fences with the new wire, that fed the valley where Bellhaven had drowned.

Bellhaven. Population four hundred and twelve. Destroyed in the flood of '09, officially attributed to cloudburst and natural dam failure upstream. Officially. There had been no inquiry, because there had been nothing left to inquire about — the town was under thirty feet of silt and the company had paid out settlements with a speed that Elias, even at twenty-six, had recognized as less like grief than like housekeeping.

His father had worked the Bellhaven district. Had ridden the inspection circuit through those hills for eleven years and come home each autumn thinner and quieter, until the last year he hadn't come home at all — killed, they said, when his stage went off the Bellhaven road in high water. Six weeks after he'd filed a report questioning timber practices in the upper watershed. The report had never been published. Elias had spent two years of his twenties trying to find a copy and had found instead a filing system so clean, so orderly, so perfectly devoid of the documents a man would expect to exist, that he understood finally that cleanliness itself was the message.

He had stopped asking after that. He told himself it had been wisdom. Some nights he told himself other things.

The train climbed. Pines came down to meet the right-of-way, then fell away into cut slopes, and the light went from gold to grey-green, and somewhere past the third tunnel the conductor came through and said, without preamble: "You'll be wanting to know there's a survey crew working ahead of us. Closed spur, Cold Run section. Company's got a contract woman doing inventory." He said *contract woman* the way other men said a swear word they'd grown fond of.

"Fairweather?" Elias asked.

The conductor's eyebrows rose a fraction. "You know her?"

"I've read her file."

"Hm." The conductor lingered, which conductors do when they have something to sell and are deciding whether the buyer can afford it. "She's a strange one. Three years now, walking dead lines. Measures everything twice. Doesn't drink, doesn't gamble, doesn't talk. The boys call her the Widow Mileage." He shrugged. "Anyway. She flagged our run yesterday. Says she needs transport down-valley for resupply. She'll be at the Cold Run flag stop at seven tomorrow morning."

"Is that a fact," Elias said.

"It's a fact I'm telling you," the conductor said, and moved on.

Elias sat with that. A surveyor who didn't talk, requesting passage down-valley eight days after a dying prospector put her name on a government page. It could mean nothing. Most things meant nothing; he had built a career on the assumption that they did. But the coincidence sat in his chest with a specific weight, the weight of a door he'd once opened in a records annex and immediately wished he hadn't.

Because here was the thing he had never written down, never spoken, barely permitted himself to think in full sentences: six years ago, before the Department, before the commissions and the seals, Elias Vane had spent one season as a junior rodman on a survey crew in the eastern ranges. And for one week of that season — seven days he could still number — there had been a chainwoman on the adjacent crew named Oscar Fairweather, who read survey tables the way other people read scripture, who laughed once at something he'd said about a mule, and whose fiancé had died on the Kestrel trestle job in a rockslide that the coroner ruled accidental and that Elias's own crew chief, drunk one night in a line camp, had sworn up and down was nothing of the kind — that the slope above the trestle had been logged against regulation, that the company knew, that everyone knew, and that no one would ever say so where it counted.

Elias had said nothing. He had been twenty-three and temporary and afraid, and the crew chief had recanted by morning anyway, and the season ended, and Elias had gone east to take his examinations, and he had told himself for six years that a rodman's silence weighed nothing on any scale that mattered.

He had never told anyone that he knew her name. Not the Department, not the file clerks, not the mirror. When the assignment wire came and he saw *Fairweather* underlined in another man's ink, he had felt the floor of his professional life tilt very slightly, the way a bridge feels when it takes a load it wasn't rated for.

Now the train was carrying him toward her at forty miles an hour, and the honest question — the one he kept turning face-down like a card — was whether he was going to Kestrel to investigate sabotage, or to find out at last what his own silence had cost.

---

* * *

Cold Run at dawn was all silver smoke and cold iron. The flag stop was a plank platform and a leaning signal post, and the forest came right up to the rail line as if it had only paused and might resume at any moment. Elias stepped down onto the gravel with his carpetbag and watched the 4:10 diminish down the grade until its sound became the sound of wind, and then there was nothing but the mountains and himself.

She came out of the treeline on foot, leading a pack mule, and Elias's breath did something unprofessional.

Six years had carved her. The woman he remembered had been all quick movement and ink-stained fingers; this woman walked like terrain — deliberate, load-bearing, finished. Her hair was bound back severely. Her jacket was canvas gone soft with wear, and across her back she carried a theodolite case with a strap worn shiny. She saw him at forty yards and did not change pace, did not touch a weapon, did not do anything at all except look at him with the flat patient attention of someone counting something.

"Miss Fairweather." He touched his hat brim. "Elias Vane. Government inspector, Rail and Public Works Commission." He produced the commission folder and let her see the seal, such as it was. "I'm investigating the damage to supply traffic on this division. I understand you requested transport down-valley."

"I requested a boxcar," she said. Her voice was low and even, a voice used to being the only voice for miles. "The conductor said space on the passenger run. I don't need conversation with my freight."

"No," Elias agreed. "I imagine you don't."

Something moved behind her eyes at that — not anger; recalibration. She looked at him the way she presumably looked at a span rated below its load. "Then we understand each other, Inspector."

"We understand each other partially," he said. "It's my experience that partial understanding is where accidents live."

"Your experience," she repeated, without inflection, and turned to signal the approaching train.

They rode down-valley together because the schedule gave them no choice, and the compartment was small, and Elias spent the first hour learning the grammar of her silences. There were many kinds. There was the working silence, during which she annotated a ledger of mileposts with a pencil sharpened to a needle. There was the defensive silence, which descended whenever the window showed a side-canyon opening toward the upper river — he counted three of those, and noted the exact bends where they occurred. And there was a third kind, rarer, that came over her when the train passed the ruins of a trestle on the old Kestrel high line, blackened pilings standing in the creek like burnt ribs, and her pencil stopped moving entirely for nearly a minute, and her face did nothing at all, which was how Elias knew the face was doing a great deal.

He knew whose trestle that was. He knew whose crew had died on it. He knew, with a certainty that had no evidentiary basis whatsoever and therefore no place in any report he would ever file, that the man she had been engaged to marry was buried under that hillside, and that the hillside had been logged against every regulation in the book.

He said none of it. He looked at his own reflection ghosted in the glass, laid over her profile like a bad double-exposure, and understood that he was doing it again — the silence, the tidy professional withholding, the cowardice dressed as discretion. Six years older and wearing a better coat, and still saying nothing.

"You're staring, Inspector," she said, without looking up from her ledger.

"I'm thinking."

"Dangerous habit in your line of work."

"It's the only part of the work I trust."

That earned him a real look — brief, assessing, almost interested. Then it was gone, banked like a fire in wet wood. "What does the Commission want with dead lines?" she asked. "Sabotage is a live-line problem. Couplings and brake crews. You're a long way from any of that."

"The damage reports cluster oddly," Elias said, and watched her. "Forty miles of track. All of it within reach of the upper watershed. I'm told that's coincidence."

"Who told you?"

"The Company."

"Ah." She returned to her ledger. "Well. They'd know their own country."

"That they would," Elias said mildly. "Though in my experience a man knows his own crimes better than his own country."

Her pencil stopped again. This time she looked up fully, and for a moment the guarded thing fell away and he saw what lived underneath it, and it frightened him slightly, because it wasn't hardness. It was grief with the patience of geology. "Is that why you're here, Inspector? To learn the Company's crimes?"

"I'm here to learn the truth of a dead prospector and some broken couplings," Elias said. "Whether those turn out to be the same inquiry remains to be seen."

"And if the truth has a price attached? If the Department would rather you found a different truth?" She said it flatly, testing him, the way you test ice. "Will you pay it?"

Elias held her gaze and felt the whole architecture of his career standing behind him like scenery on a cheap stage — one good push and it would all go over. "I've paid worse prices for less honest goods," he said. "Ask me again at the end of the line."

She considered him for a long moment. Whatever verdict she reached, she kept. She bent back to her ledger and said, "Flag stop at Miller's Bend is twenty minutes. My supplies are heavy. You can carry the flour."

It was not gratitude. It was something more useful than gratitude: it was admission. He had passed some inspection of hers, provisional and unrecorded, and been allowed aboard.

---

At Miller's Bend they stepped down into air that smelled of resin and coming frost, and while she supervised the transfer of crates, Elias wandered the length of the platform and did the thing he had boarded the train intending not to do: he asked questions.

The station agent was an old man with a beard like winter moss and the loose tongue of the thoroughly ignored. Yes, he'd seen the prospector Harkness, come through twice this summer heading up-country, buying cartridges and salt pork, paying in dust. Strange old bird. Talked to his mule more than to people. But the second time through — the agent lowered his voice with the pleasure of a man lowering a boom — the second time through, there'd been gentlemen waiting for him at the Junction. Clean-dressed fellows. City boots. Asked Harkness polite questions in the station hotel parlor, doors closed, and next morning Harkness bought double cartridges and wouldn't meet anybody's eye.

"Clean boots," Elias said.

"Shined like church furniture," the agent said happily. "Nobody wears boots like that up here unless they came up to step on somebody."

Elias thanked him, bought a newspaper eleven days old, and walked back down the platform. Fairweather was cinching the last crate to the mule's pack frame, her movements economical, her back to him, and Elias stopped ten feet away and looked at her and felt the two halves of his knowledge grind against each other like mismatched gears.

Half of him held the file: *O. Fairweather, contact of deceased, request surveillance, assess complicity.* Half of him held a September morning six years gone: a chainwoman laughing at a mule joke, a crew chief's drunken confession, a trestle, a rockslide, a silence he had helped maintain by simply continuing to exist politely inside it. The file said she might be bought. His memory said she was the only person he'd ever met who treated a measurement as a moral act.

And underneath both halves, quiet as groundwater, lay the thing he had already known since reading the underlined name and would not say aloud even to himself in full words: that the Company had killed a town, that his father had probably died asking about it, and that the government had sent him — of all the inspectors in all the districts — the one man whose private debts ran to both Fairweather and the flood, because somewhere in the Department there was a clerk who understood that compromised instruments give compliant readings.

"Inspector." She was looking at him over the mule's back. "You've got the face of a man who just learned something he wishes he hadn't."

"Do I."

"You do. I've seen it in mirrors." She swung up her pack. "Whatever it was — boots, or timetables, or the price of flour — I'd thank you to keep it to yourself until we're off this platform. Platforms have ears. Mountains just have echoes, and echoes are honest."

"How do you know the mountains are honest?"

She looked up at the ridgelines, north and west, where the river came from, where the fences were, where whatever Harkness had died carrying was still traveling toward the sea at the speed of melting snow. For a moment she seemed younger and much older at once.

"Because I've spent three years listening to them," she said, "and they've never once lied to me about where the bodies are buried."

She turned and led the mule toward the waiting caboose, and Elias Vane stood alone on the platform of Miller's Bend holding an eleven-day-old newspaper, and made — quietly, without ceremony, the way avalanches start with one grain — a decision that would end his career, save his life, and cost him nearly everything else:

He would not file the surveillance recommendation.

He would follow her instead.
