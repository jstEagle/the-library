## 5. What the River Keeps

The river had a voice, and by the third day Oscar Fairweather had stopped trying to ignore it.

It spoke in the grammar of water over stone — a low continuous syllable beneath the canoe's hull, punctuated by the sharper consonants of riffles ahead. She had spent three years listening to rivers the way other people listened to weather reports: professionally, impersonally, as data. This one was different. This one ran out of the High Passage, out of country no surveyor had bothered to name since the railroads gave up on it, and it carried with it the particular silence of a valley that had been emptied on purpose.

She dug the paddle in and let the current argue with her for a moment before winning, as currents always did. The canoe — a battered cedar strip she'd bought off a widow in the last town with a name — rode the bend and slid into morning light that came slantwise through the pines, gilding the water where the canyon walls fell away enough to allow it.

Two days behind her, somewhere downriver, were men in clean boots. She hadn't seen them since the spur line, but absence of evidence was not evidence of absence; she'd learned that much from Harkness's map, which showed what wasn't there as carefully as what was.

The map lay in its oilcloth sleeve under the thwart, wrapped around the brass tag she still hadn't decided what to do with. At night, camped on gravel bars with the fire small and downwind, she unrolled it by lantern light and worked through the prospector's annotations like a woman reading letters from someone who could no longer explain himself.

Harkness had written in pencil, in a hand that started confident and deteriorated toward the margins as the years went on. Elevation figures. Dates. Distances paced off in his own units — *paces, honest ones* — and a scattering of symbols she didn't recognize until this morning, when the river itself provided the key.

---

It happened at the first narrows above the old flume crossing. The canyon pinched down to perhaps forty yards, the water stacking up green and smooth over a shelf of bedrock, and Oscar ran it clean but fast, faster than she intended, the canoe's bow throwing spray that froze her hands. Below the drop she eddied out against a slab of granite and sat breathing hard, letting the adrenaline drain out through her arms.

That was when she saw the marks.

They were cut into the rock face at the waterline — not recent, not crude. Chiseled numerals, each about the height of her palm, arranged in a vertical column climbing the granite like a ladder. Survey benchmarks. She'd cut a thousand of them herself in other ranges, though never with a cold chisel and never below any reasonable high-water mark, because no engineer in her right mind would place a benchmark where the river could reach it.

Unless the point of the benchmark was the river reaching it.

Her heart began doing something she resented. She shipped the paddle and drifted closer, close enough to lay a glove against the stone. Lichen had gotten into the chisel cuts — years of it, decades. The lowest numeral sat barely two feet above the present waterline.

She hauled the canoe onto the slab, knelt, and got the map out with fingers that wanted to shake and weren't permitted to.

There it was, in the margin near the narrows, the symbol she hadn't understood: a short vertical stroke crossed by three horizontal ticks, repeated down the page in a descending column of dates. Harkness had been recording these marks. Coming back year after year — or season after season — and writing down what the river had done to them.

And beside each date, in that deteriorating hand, a number. A depth. A stage height.

Oscar spread the oilcloth flat on the granite and weighted its corners with stones, and for the next hour she did the thing she was best at in all the world, the thing that had kept her sane and insensate for three years: she compared figures.

Harkness's stages against the flood records. She knew the official record for this drainage better than she knew her own birthday — every gauge station, every historical crest, the 1911 ice-jam flood, the spring freshets, all of it catalogued in the regional hydrology surveys she'd studied until the numbers wore grooves in her memory. Three years of burying herself in other people's disasters had given her an encyclopedic memory for water.

The numbers didn't match. Not approximately. Not within any error she could attribute to a drunk prospector's pacing.

The marks said the river had stood thirty-one feet above its recorded maximum. Twice. On dates that corresponded to nothing in any gauge log — except one date that made her go cold all over, because she knew it without looking it up. Everyone in the territory knew it. The fourth of October, eleven years ago. The night the Bellhaven valley flooded, and the company's newspapers printed *cloudburst* in headlines an inch tall, and four hundred people went into the water along with their church, their sawmill, and their dead.

A cloudburst. In October. In a rain-shadow valley that averaged nine inches of precipitation a year, most of it snow.

She had read those headlines as a girl and filed them under *acts of God*, the way everyone had, the way the inquiry report had instructed everyone to. She'd even surveyed through the Bellhaven country two summers ago — walked the drowned townsite's periphery on a decommissioned spur, measuring track mileage over ground where houses had stood, and felt nothing at all, because feeling nothing was the whole discipline of her life. Miles, not years. Miles, not grief.

But Harkness's numbers were not a headline. They were chisel marks and pencil figures, and they said the water had come down this canyon thirty-one feet deep and moving like freight.

Water doesn't do that. Not from a cloudburst. A cloudburst makes a flash rise of three feet, six feet, ugly and local, gone in hours. Thirty-one feet of standing stage meant volume — an enormous, sustained volume released all at once from upstream. From above the narrows. From the source.

From something holding the river back.

Oscar sat back on her heels on the sun-warmed granite, and the river talked on below her, indifferent, and she understood that it had been talking the whole time. That rivers keep records more honestly than men do. That the marks on this rock were testimony, sworn in chisel-cut stone, and someone had come back season after season — an old man with bad lungs and a pencil stub — to take down the deposition.

*They'll call me a crazy prospector,* Harkness had rasped, the night he pressed the map into her hands. *Crazy's cheap insurance. Crazy men live longer than witnesses.*

Except he hadn't lived. And neither, she now suspected, had the other man whose handwriting appeared in the map's upper corner — a different pencil, a different pressure, annotations in a neat draftsman's script that simply stopped mid-column eleven years ago, between one date and the next. Between one date and the fourth of October.

She turned back to the first pages and looked at that script properly for the first time. *J.M.C.* — initials, and a surveyor's license number, and elevations reduced with a precision Harkness could never have managed. A second surveyor had been on this river before him. A good one. One who had also found the benchmarks, and measured the impossible stages, and written them down in a fair hand.

One who had stopped.

Oscar looked upstream, where the canyon narrowed again into shadow, and felt the numbness she had cultivated so carefully for three years develop its first hairline crack. Because this was how it always began — she knew that now, knew it the way you know the sound of a rail going soft before it spreads. First the numbers stop matching. Then the numbers have names.

She got out her notebook and drew a profile of the valley from memory — she had every contour of this drainage in her head from three seasons of closed-line work — and marked the Bellhaven townsite at its elevation, and drew the flood stage Harkness had recorded, and looked at the two lines intersecting.

Thirty-one feet over the town. Moving water. At night.

Four hundred people.

Her pencil lead snapped. She stared at the broken point and realized her hand had closed around it, and that her eyes were burning, and that none of it belonged to her — the grief wasn't hers, couldn't be hers, she had buried hers three years ago under forty miles of decommissioned track — and yet it rose in her anyway, huge and unwelcome, grief for strangers, for a draftsman named J.M.C., for a whole valley of people who had been told by men in good suits that God did it.

*The past becoming personal* — she would have laughed at the phrase if she'd had breath to spare. It was worse than personal. It was professional. Every hour she'd spent measuring dead lines to avoid measuring her own life, she'd been training for exactly this: to be the one person on this river who could read what the water had written and refuse to look away.

She repacked the map with exaggerated care, rolled the oilcloth tight, and stowed it. Then she stood on the granite slab in the middle of the river with the canyon walls rising on either side, and spoke aloud to no one, because there was no one to hear and she needed to say it anyway.

"You didn't die of a cloudburst," she said to Bellhaven, to J.M.C., to four hundred names on a drowned church roll. "I know."

The river carried the words downstream, toward the world, which was the wrong direction. Everything that mattered was upstream.

She pushed off into the current.

* * *

She made twelve miles that afternoon, poling and paddling through water that dropped steeply and cleared to the color of bottle glass. The canyon changed character above the narrows — wider benches, terraces of old flood gravel stacked like masonry, cottonwoods gone gold early at this altitude. Signs of the high water were everywhere once she knew to look for them: debris lines of bleached driftwood lodged sixty feet up the talus slopes, scarred tree trunks facing downstream, silt bands in the cut banks at heights that made her stomach tighten. The whole valley was an archive, and it agreed with Harkness to the foot.

Near dusk she passed the ruin of a logging dam — just cribs of rotted timber in a wide pool, moss-eaten, abandoned long before the flood — and it gave her an idea about where to camp: the old log pond would be flat water, sheltered, defensible. She ran the last riffle and slid into stillness among the drowned pilings.

She ate cold beans and hardtack because a fire's glow carried too far on still water, and after full dark she took out the map again, because sleep was not going to come and pretending otherwise was wasting lantern oil.

That was when she found the second cipher.

It was in the lower margin, so faint she'd taken it for water staining: a column of tiny figures in Harkness's later hand, each row keyed to a date. Pacing distances, yes — but paired with compass bearings, and the bearings all converged on a single point upstream, in the gorge country below the source. And beside the last entry, written larger, harder, the pencil having torn the paper slightly:

*V-notch, west wall, 2 mi below the falls. Stone work. Not natural. Brought tag. Will need help. Am being watched.*

*Am being watched.*

Oscar sat very still in the canoe, the lantern hooded, listening to the night sounds of the valley — the river, an owl, wind in the cottonwoods — and hearing underneath all of it the silence of a man who had known, for years, that the men in clean boots were coming for him eventually. He had kept surveying anyway. He had kept writing anyway. He had traded his map to a stranger on a dead rail line because a stranger was the only kind of person left he could trust.

*Will need help.*

He'd needed help, and he'd gotten a dying bargain with a woman too numb to feel anything, and somehow that was going to have to be enough.

Below the falls. Two miles below the falls, a V-notch in the west wall, stone work that wasn't natural. Something built into the canyon wall — a vault, a tunnel, a cache. Harkness had brought a tag. The brass tag in her pack was stamped with a town name that officially never flooded. If there was a second tag—

If there was a second tag, there had been a second carrier.

She thought of the neat draftsman's hand that stopped in October, eleven years ago, and did not finish the thought, because finishing it would make it real, and she needed to be functional tonight.

She snuffed the lantern and lay back against the pack, watching stars wheel slowly past the canyon rim, and made herself do the arithmetic she'd been avoiding since the narrows. Nine days, maybe ten, before the dry season dropped the river below the gorge rapids and sealed the High Passage for another year. Maybe less, if the fixers moved faster than she did — and they would, they had horses and helicopters of money behind them, while she had a cedar-strip canoe and a dead man's arithmetic.

The owl called once, far off, and the river answered in its endless low voice, keeping its records, keeping its dead.

Oscar closed her eyes and found, to her non-surprise, that she was not afraid. What she felt was older and more useful than fear — the flat, bright clarity that used to arrive before a difficult bridge inspection, when the structure was suspect and the only way through was across.

Tomorrow she'd run the flats hard and make the falls by evening. Tomorrow the numbers would start having names.

Somewhere upstream, in the dark above the falls, something waited in a notch of stone that no cloudburst had carved — and she was no longer the woman who measured miles to avoid counting years. She was the one carrying the count.

Sleep took her like a current takes a canoe: gently, and in exactly the wrong direction.
