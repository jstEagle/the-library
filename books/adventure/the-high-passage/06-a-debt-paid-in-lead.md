## 6. A Debt Paid in Lead

The river spoke to her all evening, the way it always did at this bend — a low, continuous argument between water and stone that most people would call silence. Oscar Fairweather had learned to hear it as a language with three words: *normal*, *rising*, and *wrong*. All day it had said *normal*. She had cooked her supper of beans and hardtack on a flat rock above the gravel bar, checked her canoe's lashings twice, spread Harkness's map inside the tent to study it by lantern light, and written nothing in her journal because there was nothing in her head worth the ink.

That was how she liked it now. Empty was safe. Three years of empty, measured out in track miles and river miles, in soundings and sight lines, in the careful arithmetic of a woman who had subtracted everything from herself that could be taken away.

She banked the fire low, the way she'd been taught — coals raked together under ash, no flame to carry on the wind off the water — and crawled into the tent with the map folded against her chest like a second heartbeat. The canvas smelled of spruce pitch and old rain. Somewhere upstream an owl asked its one question and got no answer, which struck her, drifting toward sleep, as about right for this country.

The first wrong thing was the birds.

Not the owl — the smaller ones, the dark shapes that roosted in the alders along the bar. They went up all at once, a soft explosion of wings, not startled but *evacuating*, the way birds leave a place when something two-legged moves through it without care for what it steps on. Oscar's eyes opened in the black. She did not move. Her body made the decision before her mind caught up: breath shallow, hand closing around the cold iron of the pry bar she kept at the tent's mouth for driving stakes.

Then the fire pit hissed.

Someone had poured water on her coals. Not to put them out — to kill the light, so that whatever came next would happen in the dark they preferred. She heard boots on gravel, more than one pair, unhurried, the walk of men who believed they had all night and no opposition. A voice murmured, too low to catch words, and another answered.

Three, she thought. Maybe four.

Her heart was doing something strange — not pounding but *knocking*, slow and heavy, like a fist on a door from the inside. She had read about fear in novels; she had never understood until this moment that it could be quiet, that terror could sit down beside you politely and wait its turn.

They would check the canoe first. Anyone finding a camp would check the canoe — see what tonnage she carried, whether she'd be going downstream or back. That bought her perhaps a minute. The map was against her chest. Her knife was in her boot. The pry bar was in her hand. Everything else — the tent, the food, the survey instruments, three weeks of careful provisioning — was already lost, and some cold, clear part of her brain began itemizing the loss even as her other parts worked out how to stay alive.

She cut the back wall of the tent with two strokes of the knife, slow, sawing rather than slicing, because canvas tears loud. The night air came in cold and green-smelling. She slid through into the alder scrub on her belly, the map case strapped tight under her jacket now, and began to move downstream along the bar, bent double, putting each foot down like a woman walking on eggs.

Behind her, a flashlight beam bloomed inside her tent and a man's voice said, conversationally, "Empty."

Then the night tore open.

The shot came from the trees above the bar — high, left, aimed not at where she'd been but at where whoever fired guessed she'd go. The bullet took the gravel six feet ahead of her and spanged away into the dark, and the sound of it, flat and enormous, broke something loose in her chest. She ran.

Gravel is the worst ground in the world to run on and she ran it anyway, arms pumping, boots punching through the loose stone, the river roaring somewhere to her left and invisible. A second shot cracked past close enough that she felt the air move, and then a third, and then voices shouting behind her — not words anymore, just the animal noise of men who had expected a lone woman asleep in a tent and were getting something else.

"Down to the water!" someone bellowed. "Cut her off at the bar!"

They knew the ground better than she did. That was the thought that saved her, perversely: they knew it, which meant they'd go for the obvious route, the gravel shallows where a canoe could be dragged out, the place any sensible person would take to the water. So Oscar did the senseless thing. She turned her back on the gentle shallows and ran straight at the roar.

The bank ended the way banks do in canyon country — all at once, a lip of clay and root, and beyond it black water moving fast over something unseen. She had maybe half a second to understand what she was looking at: the head of the riffle she'd scouted that morning, a quarter mile of standing waves funneling into a chute between two granite teeth. In daylight, with her gear, with a boat under her, she'd have run it without a second thought. Without a boat it was a death.

A fourth shot hit the clay bank beside her head and clay stung her cheek like thrown sand, and the choice stopped being a choice.

She jumped.

* * *

The river took her the way a crowd takes a falling man — completely, without malice, every part of it grabbing at once. The cold closed over her head and the current rolled her end over end, and she learned in those first seconds that the water here was not one thing but many things layered on each other: a surface current dragging her down-channel, an undertow pulling at her heels, an eddy line snapping at her like a whip wherever the flows met. She had read drowning manuals. She had taught river rescue to track crews once, in another life, in the life before the life where she'd stopped counting years. *Don't fight the main current. Float feet-first, toes up. Back-paddle with your hands for the eddies.*

Her body remembered what her grief had tried to bury. Feet up. Hands out. Breathe when the water let you.

The first wave hit her like a wall and she went under, spinning, and came up gasping with the taste of silt and pine in her throat. The second one threw her sideways and her hip struck something submerged — a log, a rock, she'd never know — hard enough that white pain lanced from her hipbone to her ribs. She rode it. She rode all of it, the way you ride a horse that means to throw you: not resisting the big movements, only correcting the small ones, keeping her feet downstream so the rocks would take her boots instead of her skull.

The chute came. She heard it before she saw it — the whole river compressing into a single furious note — and then the granite teeth rose on either side, black and dripping, and the water accelerated until it wasn't swimming anymore, it was flight, it was being fired through the mountain's throat. Spray filled the air. The stars wheeled overhead, upside down, indifferent. For one long moment Oscar Fairweather was not a surveyor, not a mourner, not a woman carrying a dead man's map against her ribs. She was weight and motion and nothing else, and some traitorous corner of her mind noted, even now, that it was the closest thing to peace she'd felt in three years.

Then the chute spat her out into the pool below, and the pool was almost still, and stillness after violence has its own kind of violence. She swam for the eddy behind a house-sized boulder and hauled herself onto the stones at its base, coughing, shaking, alive.

It was only when she tried to stand that she found the blood.

Her left arm, above the elbow — she'd have sworn nothing had touched her there, but the sleeve was torn and wet and the wet was warm. A graze. A bullet or a rock; in the dark it hardly mattered. It bled freely but not fatally, the kind of wound that looks worse than it is, and she sat on the boulder in the starlight and bound it with a strip torn from her shirttail, knotting it one-handed with her teeth holding the slack, and the whole time her hands shook and her breath came in ragged pulls and she let them, because fighting the shaking cost more than it saved.

They had shot at her. Not over her head — the first shot, maybe, the warning — but the last one had taken clay from the bank an inch from her ear. Men who warn don't aim at your ear. These men had graduated from warning to work, and the work was her.

She looked back upriver. Far above, faint on the black water, a flashlight beam swept the gravel bar where her camp had been. They were searching her things. By now they'd have the tent, the food pack, the theodolite case with her instruments and her field notes — three weeks of soundings, gone — and they'd burn the rest or sink it, and come morning there would be no evidence she'd ever camped there at all except a strip of torn canvas and a dead fire.

But not the map. The map was under her jacket, dry in its oilskin case, pressed against skin that still held a pulse. Harkness's map. The old man's dying gift and dying burden both.

"They didn't get it," she said aloud, to the boulder, to the river, to nobody. Her voice sounded strange — scraped raw, younger than she was. "You didn't get it."

* * *

She walked the rest of the night.

Not far — a mile, maybe two, putting the boulder and the eddy and the pool between herself and the men upstream — but far enough to find a fold in the canyon wall where a seep had grown a curtain of moss ferns, and behind the ferns a hollow deep enough to sit in with her back to stone. She did not dare a fire. She did not dare sleep, though sleep pulled at her like the undertow had, patient and total. She sat with the pry bar across her knees — she'd somehow kept hold of it through all of it, her hands welded to it without her permission — and watched the stars wheel the right way round again, east to west, and inventoried.

What she had: the map. The brass tag on its cord around her neck. One knife. One pry bar. The clothes she stood in, soaked. A graze wound that would stiffen by morning. What she had lost: the canoe, the tent, the food, the stove, the instruments, the notes, the spare cartridges for a rifle she'd also lost, the letters from her sister she'd never answered anyway. Half her supplies — no. More than half. Nearly all.

By any rational accounting, she should turn back. Deadfall Junction lay two days downstream on foot, or she could strike east over the divide to the railhead and be in telegraph range of the world in three. She could report the ambush. She could wash her hands of Harkness and his map and his promise and go back to measuring dead lines, back to the numb arithmetic, back to the long gray safety of a life with nothing in it to lose.

She turned the idea over and examined it honestly, the way she examined all her data, and found it wanting — not because turning back was cowardly, but because it wouldn't work. That was the thing she understood sitting in the moss-dark with her own blood drying tacky under her bandage: they hadn't ambushed her camp because she was trespassing. They'd ambushed her because of what she carried. Turning back wouldn't end it. They would follow her to the railhead, to the telegraph office, to her sister's porch if it came to that, until the map and the woman carrying it were both at the bottom of some hole no one surveyed. There was no exit from this river except through.

Harkness had known. Dying in her camp with his hand clamped on her wrist, he'd known exactly what he was handing her, and he'd done it anyway, because a promise from Oscar Fairweather was the one currency in these mountains that didn't devalue. She had given her word to a dead man to run the High Passage before the dry season ended, and she had meant it, and meaning it had seemed — three days ago, a lifetime ago — like a matter of endurance. Paddle far enough, decode carefully, keep your head down.

Keep your head down. She almost laughed, and the laugh came out as a sob, one, quickly strangled. They had shot at her. The company that owned the dams and the newspapers and the lawmen had sent men with clean rifles to put lead in a railroad engineer on a public river, and that changed the shape of everything. This was not investigation anymore. Investigation assumed you'd live to file your findings. This was survival, plain and mean, and survival had different rules: move at night, sleep in daylight, trust the water and the stone and nothing on two legs. Get to the source. Get the proof. And then —

And then what? She was one woman with a knife and a pry bar. The proof would be paper and glass plates, and paper burns, and glass breaks, and witnesses drown so easily in fast water. Unless the proof was bigger than one campfire, bigger than one canoe, unless it went somewhere they couldn't follow — a newspaper, a federal office, a hundred sets of eyes at once. She didn't know yet how. But she knew the direction, and direction was the one thing a surveyor could always find.

Oscar Fairweather looked at her hands in the starlight. They had stopped shaking. Somewhere in the last hour, quietly, without ceremony, the terror had finished transmuting — the way base metal turns in a crucible, ugly heat and then, abruptly, something harder. She was afraid still; she expected she'd be afraid until this ended one way or the other. But the fear had changed jobs. It was no longer telling her to hide. It was telling her to hurry.

"Nine days," she said to the river — for the dry season clock was running, the water dropping a finger's width every day, and the upper passage would soon be too shallow for anything that floated. "Maybe less."

She bound the map case tighter to her body, tested the bandage on her arm, and stood. Downstream, the canyon narrowed to a black slot where the water sang over hidden gravel, and beyond it, hours south, lay Deadfall Junction and whatever waited there — supplies, maybe, if Mara Quist's name on Harkness's letter meant what Oscar hoped it meant. An old telegraph operator who'd refused to sell her silence. In a valley full of bought men, one unbought woman was worth more than a canoe full of beans.

Oscar stepped out from behind the ferns and started walking, favoring her left leg, guarding her left arm, keeping the water's voice on her right side like a handrail.

She did not see, because the bend hid it and the dark hid the rest, the thin gray smoke rising from a camp two miles below hers — a solitary figure sitting up late over a cold stove, a government inspector who had told himself he was only following procedure, and who would find, come morning, a trampled gravel bar, a torn tent, and four spent cartridge cases that told him exactly how badly his superiors wanted this river kept quiet.

But the river knew. The river carries everything, sooner or later, to everyone.
