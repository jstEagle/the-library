## 7. The Man Who Wouldn't Lie Well

The report was due at nine o'clock in the morning, and by eight Elias Vane had written and destroyed four versions of its first sentence.

*Preliminary findings indicate the collapse of the Bellhaven diversion dam to be consistent with natural causes.*

He read it back and heard his father's voice reading it aloud — not accusing him, which would have been bearable, but *approving*, in that warm gravel tone he'd used for everything from a well-tied knot to a well-told lie: *That's the way of it, lad. Say it plain and sign it clean.* His father had been a bridge man. Forty years of iron and timber across half the territory's rivers, and he had died on one of them — the Kestrel Span, a winter night, eleven men in the water when the ice took out a pier everyone in the district privately knew was rotten. The inquiry had called it an act of God. The company that owned the span had sent flowers. Elias's mother had kept the flowers' card in her sewing box for twenty years, as if she might one day find the sentence hidden inside it that explained anything.

He fed the fourth draft into the stove and watched the ink curl brown before the paper caught.

His office in the Territorial Engineering Bureau was three rooms that had once been four, the partition removed to make space for a filing system nobody used. Rain worked at the window in long grey fingers. On the desk, under a brass paperweight shaped like a surveyor's arrow — a gift from his father, another thing he could not throw away — lay the file that had brought him here: *BELLHAVEN DIVERSION WORKS — FAILURE OF RECORD — INSPECTOR E. VANE, ASSIGNED.*

Assigned. That was the word they'd used, as though the file were a parcel and he a delivery boy. He had been assigned it by Deputy Commissioner Harrow himself, in a meeting that lasted eleven minutes and smelled of cigar smoke and certainty.

"Natural causes," Harrow had said, tapping the folder with one blunt finger. "Spring melt, unusual ice loading, foundation scour. It's all in the geologist's addendum. You'll confirm it, Vane, and you'll do it quickly, because there are insurance matters pending and the company's counsel is already making noise about liability. We don't want this dragging through the winter."

"Has anyone inspected the site?" Elias had asked.

Harrow had looked at him the way a man looks at a dog that has asked a question instead of performing a trick. "The site is under water, Inspector. Has been since April. What exactly would you inspect?"

"The abutments. The spillway training walls. The core samples from the original construction — if any exist."

"They exist." A pause, thin as wire. "They're sealed. Company property."

*Sealed.* Elias had turned the word over all the way home, and all the way here, and now, standing at his window watching the rain, he turned it over again and found the same sharp edge every time. Survey data was not sealed in this territory unless someone feared it. He had spent fourteen years in government service learning what things cost and who paid, and the arithmetic of a sealed vault was simple: somebody had built something cheaper than the drawings said, and somebody else had died because of it, and the difference between those two somebodies was being measured out right now in lawyers' fees and flowers.

And Oscar Fairweather was somewhere upriver in the middle of it.

He caught himself at the thought and made himself set it down, the way he set down any tool he wasn't ready to use. But it came back, the way thoughts do when you've been carrying them longer than you admit. *Fairweather, O.* Her name had been in the old files — he'd found it the first night, hunting for the dam's original survey crew, and stopped breathing for a moment like a man stepping into cold water. Three years of closed-line surveys, meticulous, obsessive, a woman walking dead track through the mountains measuring things no one had asked her to measure. He knew why people did that. He had done a version of it himself since Kestrel: taken assignments nobody wanted, filed reports nobody read, kept his hands busy so his mind couldn't count the dead.

And then she'd surfaced again — a supply manifest, a canoe permit signed in a hand that pressed hard enough to tear cheap paper, heading up the very river the Bureau wanted left alone. Coincidence was a word Elias had stopped trusting around the same time he'd stopped trusting flowers.

* * *

At half past eight, Miss Aldous knocked and put her head in. She had been the Bureau's senior clerk since before Elias was born, or looked it, and she possessed the rare administrative talent of seeing directly through furniture.

"You've burned something," she said.

"A draft."

"Mm." She came in without invitation, which was her privilege, and set a cup of coffee on his desk — real coffee, hoarded, which meant she thought he needed steadying. "Deputy Commissioner telephoned twice. He'd like your report by nine."

"He'll have it by nine."

"Will he?" She looked at the stove, where the ash of four drafts still held the ghost of lines. "Mr. Vane, I have filed reports in this office for thirty-one years. I can tell a true report from a comfortable one at ten paces. Yours is going to be whichever one lets you sleep, and I only mention it because I liked your father, and he slept badly for the last ten years of his life."

Elias looked up at her. She met it without flinching, hands folded, a woman built out of patience and ink.

"What do you know about my father?"

"That he argued with the Bureau for six years over the Kestrel inspection standards and was told, each year, that the matter was under review." She said it flatly, without malice, the way one reads a train timetable. "I typed the replies. Some of them were mine to type." Something moved behind her eyes, quickly banked. "I'm not telling you to be brave, Mr. Vane. I'm telling you that whatever you write at nine o'clock, I will be the one who files it, and I will know what it is. That's all. Good morning."

She left him the coffee and the silence, and both were heavier than they should have been.

* * *

He tried a fifth draft sitting down, pen moving because motion sometimes shook truth loose.

*Natural causes cannot be confirmed or excluded on present evidence. Recommend full physical inspection of the Bellhaven site upon seasonal low water, including recovery of original construction records currently held under corporate seal.*

It was honest. It was also, he understood perfectly, a declaration of war delivered in the politest possible language. Harrow would read *recommend* and hear *defy*. And behind Harrow stood the Consolidated Mining & Power Company, whose counsel was indeed already making noise, and behind them stood the quiet arrangement between capital and bureau that kept the territory running like a well-greased machine — greased, Elias had come to understand, partly with the names of dead men.

He wrote instead:

*Interim report follows. Final determination withheld pending site access.*

Shorter. Colder. A stalling action dressed as procedure. Every officer learned to write these; they were the bureaucratic equivalent of a fighting retreat, buying ground with time. It would hold Harrow off for perhaps two weeks. Then the pressure would come down somewhere else — not on Elias directly, at first, but sideways. His caseload would thicken. His travel requests would develop delays. Someone would begin asking, in corridors, whether Inspector Vane was quite reliable. He had watched it happen to a hydraulic engineer named Sorel, five years ago, who had questioned the load ratings on a company flume; within a season Sorel had been transferred to a coastal drainage project so remote his letters arrived damp. Within two seasons he'd resigned. Elias had thought, at the time, that Sorel had been foolish to fight. He thought differently now, and the difference sat in his chest like a swallowed stone.

Because here was the thing he had never said aloud, not to Miss Aldous, not to anyone: his father had not simply died on the Kestrel Span. His father had *signed* the Kestrel Span. Signed the final inspection certificate eighteen months before the ice took it — signed it after arguing for six years, signed it in the end because a man has a family and a pension and a bureau that does not forgive, and because he had convinced himself, in the way tired good men convince themselves, that the margin was small but sufficient. Eleven men went into the water. One of them was the man who had signed. The river had not cared about the distinction between the guilty and the compromised, and neither, in the end, had his father, who in his last year had begun saying strange things at supper — *a signature is a debt, lad, remember that, a signature is a debt* — until the night the span went down and the debt was collected.

Elias had been nineteen. He had spent fourteen years paying down a debt that was not his, in the only currency the Bureau accepted: diligence, silence, and reports confirming what he was told to confirm. He had told himself it was duty. Standing at the window with the rain writing its own grey report on the glass, he admitted at last what it actually was. It was fear, wearing duty's coat. Fear of becoming his father. Fear, worse, of having become him already without noticing — a man who signs.

The clock said five minutes to nine.

He took the interim report — the short one, the retreat — and carried it down the corridor to Miss Aldous's desk. She read it in ten seconds, all of it, the way she read everything.

"And the rest?" she asked quietly.

"Withheld," Elias said. "Pending site access."

"Whose site access?"

"Mine," he said. "I'm requesting the High Passage assignment myself."

Her eyebrows rose a fraction — the Aldous equivalent of open astonishment. "The upper river? That's outside your jurisdiction entirely. That's Consolidated's private works corridor. There's nothing up there but abandoned track and—"

"An inspector's authority extends to any failure of record affecting public waters," Elias said. "The Bellhaven failure is upstream of four public crossings. If the cause is natural, as the Deputy Commissioner is confident, then a site inspection can only confirm it. If he objects to an inspection—" he let the sentence stand unfinished, a door left open onto a room Harrow would not want seen.

Miss Aldous almost smiled. Almost. "You're asking me to file a request that will make the Deputy Commissioner very angry, in the same hour as the report meant to placate him."

"I'm asking you to file both. In that order. Let him read the report first, breathe easy, and then find the request underneath it like a stone in his boot."

For a moment she studied him with those thirty-one years of eyes. "Your father would have approved of this one," she said. "The other kind, I mean. The kind he argued for." She pulled a fresh form from the drawer. "Requesting the High Passage. Reason for assignment?"

Elias looked out the corridor window, north, where the mountains stood behind their weather like a jury behind a screen. Somewhere up in that grey country a woman was paddling alone toward a sealed vault, pursued by men whose boots were too clean, carrying a dead prospector's map and a promise. He did not know how he knew she was still alive. He knew it the way he knew the load rating of a bridge — by feel, by the hum in the structure, by everything he could not put in a report.

"Reason for assignment," Miss Aldous repeated, pen waiting.

"Site inspection," Elias said. "Failure of record. Public waters."

She wrote it. The pen scratched like small rain.

* * *

Harrow's summons came at noon, and it came as Elias had predicted: not as fury but as hospitality. Lunch, in the Deputy Commissioner's private rooms, with a view of the harbor and a bottle of the good claret that appeared whenever the Bureau needed something from a man or needed a man to stop wanting something.

"Elias." Harrow never used surnames over lunch; it was part of the architecture of the favor. "Sit. Drink. Your interim report — concise, professional, exactly what I'd expect. Withheld pending site access, though. That's the one wrinkle." He poured two glasses without asking. "So imagine my surprise when Aldous brings me a second form an hour later. Requesting the High Passage corridor. *Yourself,* Elias. Personally."

"The crossings below Bellhaven carry the main line," Elias said. "If the dam failed from scour, the same foundations serve the trestles downstream. I want to walk them before the melt season argument gets settled by lawyers instead of engineers."

Harrow smiled the smile of a man who has already checked the boot for stones. "You want to walk them. Upstream. Through forty miles of company land, in the last weeks of the dry season, alone." He turned his glass by the stem. "There's a woman up there, you know. Fairweather. Ex-railroad surveyor. Been poking along that river since spring. The company believes she may be connected to the sabotage incidents on the spur lines — you've seen the file. They've asked us, informally, to have the matter looked into." He raised his eyes. "Informally."

"I've seen the file," Elias said, which was true, and let the rest lie.

"If you go up there," Harrow said pleasantly, "you could look into it. Two birds. Confirm the dam report, assess the Fairweather situation, come down before the water drops and file it all together. Clean work, Elias. The company would remember it." He slid a folded paper across the table — not orders, just a memorandum, unsigned, the kind that existed nowhere and everywhere. "And if the Fairweather matter resolved itself in a way that spared everyone further correspondence, there's a commissionership opening in the southern district in the spring. A man could grow old comfortably in the southern district."

Elias looked at the memorandum. He thought of his father's signature, and of the weight of a pen, and of eleven men in the water, and of a woman pressing hard enough to tear paper. He thought of the word *resolved* and what it meant when companies used it.

"I'll need a warrant of access for the company corridor," he said. "Full authority. Written."

Harrow's smile thinned by a degree. "Of course. Procedure." He refilled the glasses, and the claret moved like something dark and patient. "You're your father's son, Elias. He was the best field man this Bureau ever had." A pause, delicate as a blade laid flat. "And the most reasonable."

Elias drank, and agreed that he was, and took the warrant when it came, and walked back through the rain to his office feeling the chafe of it all along his shoulders like a pack strap cut wrong — duty, his whole life worn as duty, suddenly sitting against the bone.

On his desk, Miss Aldous had left a slip of paper, filed-form pink, no message required. It was the dispatch log entry for the northern relay stations, and one line was circled in her neat hand:

*DEADFALL JUNCTION RELAY — OPERATOR M. QUIST REPORTS UNIDENTIFIED TRAVELER PASSING UPRIVER, SOLO CANOE, 14TH INST. FURTHER TRAFFIC INTERCEPTED PER COMPANY DIRECTIVE 9.*

Further traffic intercepted. Which meant the wires above Deadfall were already company ears, which meant whatever happened upriver now would happen unwitnessed — unless a witness got there first.

Elias took a blank requisition form, and beneath *destination* he wrote, in a hand that pressed hard enough to emboss the page: *High Passage — via Deadfall Junction.*
