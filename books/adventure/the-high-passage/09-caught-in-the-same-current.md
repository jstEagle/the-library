## 9. Caught in the Same Current

*Oscar*

She heard him before she saw him — a footfall on the gravel bar where no one had any business walking at dusk. Not the soft, deliberate tread of a hunter. Something heavier. A man who wanted to be heard.

Oscar's hand went to the rifle across her knees before her mind caught up with her body. Three years of sleeping alone in dead camps had built that reflex into her bones: sound first, thought second, fear third and last. She did not turn around. She let the firelight do its work on her face and kept her eyes on the river sliding past Deadfall Junction, black water carrying the last copper light of evening down toward the lowlands.

"You walk like a surveyor," she said. "Not like a company man."

"Maybe I'm both."

Elias Vane came around the edge of the collapsed freight shed into the ring of her fire, and up close he looked worse than he had on the 4:10 — three days of stubble, dust in the seams of his coat, a cut over his eyebrow gone brown with age. He carried no rifle. His hands hung open at his sides, empty, and somehow that was more alarming than a gun would have been.

"How did you find me?"

"The same way you found the river." He nodded upstream. "You follow the only road there is. I've been two days behind you since Deadman's Narrows. You camp high, burn small fires, and you cross your bridges. That last habit nearly got you at the trestle — I watched you from the far bank Tuesday morning."

"I crossed because it was standing."

"It was standing because the company hasn't gotten around to burning it yet. They will." He crouched at the edge of the firelight, close enough for warmth, far enough to be struck if she moved. "Rennick has six men on the upper trail and a reward notice wired to every station between here and the coast. Two hundred dollars for information leading to your arrest. Five hundred if you're stopped before you reach the source."

"And what's the government offering?"

"For you?" A ghost of something crossed his face — not quite a smile. "Nothing official."

* * *

*Elias*

He had rehearsed this a dozen times on the trail, and every version of the speech had died somewhere between his throat and the firelight. The truth was simpler and worse than any script: he had been sent to stop her, and he had ridden two days hard through country he didn't know to do it, and now that he sat within arm's reach of her and her map, he could not make himself say the words.

*You're under administrative detention pending investigation.* That was the order. It sat in his breast pocket on departmental letterhead, signed by a deputy commissioner who had never seen the river, and it weighed more than his revolver.

She hadn't asked about the order yet. She was watching him the way she'd watched him on the train — sideways, patient, a woman accustomed to reading terrain rather than faces but applying the same skill to both. He had the sudden, unwelcome sense of being surveyed. Contoured. Marked for grading.

"You knew Harkness," she said. It wasn't a question.

"I read his file. Prospector, sixty-one, no next of kin, deceased of exposure and blood loss near Mile 114 of the closed Cascade spur." He paused. "The file also says he was delusional. Talked for years about a flood that never happened, in a town that never flooded."

"Bellhaven."

"Bellhaven," Elias agreed quietly.

The fire popped. Somewhere down the ruined main street of Deadfall, a sheet of tin peeled off a roof and clattered, and they both turned toward the sound and back again, and in that small shared flinch Elias felt something shift — the first plank of a bridge neither of them had agreed to build.

"My father worked for the Water Commission," he said, before he could stop himself. "Thirty-one years ago. There was an inspection report on the Alder Creek dam that he wrote and then withdrew. Two weeks later he was dead. Train derailment. The commission called it coincidence and gave my mother a pension and a sealed envelope she was instructed never to open." He looked at the fire. "I opened it when I was nineteen. It was his original draft. The one that said the dam wouldn't hold."

Oscar said nothing. But she reached over and fed another branch to the flames, and the gesture — ordinary, domestic, offered without comment — undid him more thoroughly than any argument could have.

* * *

*Oscar*

She should have run. That was the arithmetic her careful life demanded: a government inspector alone at her fire, an order surely in his pocket, six hired men between her and the source, nine days of river dropping by the hour. Every prudent calculation said take the canoe and the dark water and don't look back.

Instead she heard herself say, "There's coffee. If you're going to sit there, you might as well drink it."

He took the cup without letting their fingers touch, which told her he'd thought about whether to let them touch. She poured her own and settled back against her pack, and for a long while neither of them spoke, and the silence was not comfortable but it was not hostile either. It was the silence of two people measuring the same span from opposite ends.

Finally she reached behind her and brought out the theodolite case. She set it on her knees, opened the latches, and lifted out the oilskin packet inside. Harkness's map. She held it a moment, feeling the weight of the promise folded inside it — *carry it to the source, girl, before the dry season takes the water* — and then, deliberately, watching his face the whole time, she unrolled it across the flat rock between them.

Vane went very still.

The firelight moved over the paper: the High Passage in Harkness's cramped surveyor's hand, forty miles of abandoned rail grade and dead river from Deadfall to the source canyon, annotated in three colors of ink across thirty years of visits. Depth soundings along the lower gorge. Elevations. Dates. And along the Bellhaven reach, a column of figures Oscar had spent four days verifying against everything she knew about water, and which matched no flood on any record anywhere.

Vane read it the way she imagined he read everything — completely, silently, twice. His jaw tightened at the soundings. At the dates, something worse happened to his face; it went not pale but *old*, as though the numbers had aged him ten years in ten seconds.

"You've seen this before," she said.

"No." He said it fast, and it was true, and it wasn't the whole truth, and she watched him decide, visibly, painfully, to add to it. "But I've seen the shape of it. The commission keeps a vault of withdrawn reports. My father's is in it. So is a 1909 survey of the Bellhaven dam — unsigned, unfiled, marked *substandard construction, do not certify*. Somebody buried it. Somebody buried my father for digging too deep, and somebody has been burying this—" his hand hovered over the map without touching it, "—for twenty years. I was ordered up this river to make sure it stayed buried."

"Then do your job." Her voice came out flat and cold and she was glad of it. "Arrest me. Take the map back to your deputy commissioner and collect whatever they pay men like you for men like me."

He didn't move. The fire cracked. Down the dead street of Deadfall the wind walked through broken windows, playing the town like a rusted instrument.

"If I take that map," Vane said, "it goes into a vault. Next to my father's report. Next to the 1909 survey. And in thirty-one years some other inspector's son or daughter opens a sealed envelope and learns that we knew, and did nothing, and that the water rose anyway." He finally looked up at her, and his eyes in the firelight were steady and miserable and certain. "I'm not going to be the envelope, Miss Fairweather."

* * *

*Elias*

Later — much later, when the fire had burned to a red heart and the river had gone from black to invisible — she asked him the question he had been dreading.

"Why didn't you take it? Back on the train. You knew my name before I ever gave it. You could have had me and the map in Meridian and been home by now."

Because I read your file, he almost said. Because there was a photograph in it, taken eight years ago at a station platform, a woman in a surveyor's coat laughing at something outside the frame, and he had known that laugh the way you know a voice heard once through a wall — but that was not a thing a man said aloud on the first night, or perhaps ever.

"Because I checked your references instead," he said. "Every engineer who ever worked with you. Do you know what they all said? The same sentence, word for word, like they'd rehearsed it: *She doesn't lie.* Twenty years of railroad men, and not one of them could name a single lie you'd told." He turned his cup slowly in his hands. "I've spent my career among people whose whole profession is the managed untrue. Reports that say less than they know. Findings shaped to fit the budget. I thought I'd forgotten what the other thing looked like."

"And now?"

"Now I'm looking at it." He meant the map. He was not entirely sure he meant the map.

She studied him across the coals, and he endured it, and after a long moment she nodded once — the nod of an engineer accepting a load rating, provisional, subject to inspection.

"Here's how it will be," she said. "I don't trust you. Trust is for people who haven't been followed for two hundred miles. But I can't run the Passage and watch my back at the same time, and Rennick's men are better armed than either of us alone. So: you come with me. Officially, you're escorting your prisoner upriver to recover stolen documents. Unofficially—" she tapped the map, rolled it, and held it out to him across the fire, handle first, like a man offering a tool, "—you're going to help me carry the truth out of this valley, and then you're going to stand up in front of God and the commission and tell them what's in that vault, with your name on it."

Elias took the map. It weighed nothing and everything.

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you'll have my rifle, my supplies, and a two-day head start, and I'll go up the river alone, because I made a promise to a dying man and I keep those." She banked the fire with her boot heel, unhurried, already turning toward her bedroll. "Sleep, Inspector. We launch at first light. The water won't wait for either of us to be sure."

* * *

*Oscar*

She did not sleep. She lay listening to his breathing even out across the ashes, to the river working at its endless arithmetic below the bank, and she understood that she had just done the most reckless thing of her careful life: she had handed a stranger the only proof that a town full of people had been murdered by a ledger entry, and she had done it because of the way he'd said *my father*, and the way he hadn't touched her fingers taking the cup, and the way he had looked at Harkness's soundings like a man reading his own death warrant and signing it anyway.

Trust kindling against judgment. That was what it felt like — a small flame in wet wood, smoking, uncertain, refusing to go out.

At first light the river lay gray and low under a sky the color of tin, and Elias Vane stood on the gravel bar holding the bow line of her canoe like a man who had decided something overnight and was not going to announce it. She stepped past him into the stern. He pushed off and swung aboard, and the current took them both, and neither of them looked back at Deadfall.

They were three miles up the narrows, the walls closing in gold and shadow, when he said quietly, "There's something you should know. Rennick offered me a deal yesterday. Retirement money, or my name on the sabotage charge. I told him I'd think about it."

Oscar's paddle did not break stroke. "And are you thinking about it?"

"I'm thinking," Elias said, "that I've been lying to everyone I work for since the day I took this assignment, and it's the first honest work I've ever done." He glanced back at her, and for the first time since the 4:10 he smiled — brief, crooked, real. "Besides. Prisoners don't get a vote. Where the boat goes, I go."

Around the next bend the river narrowed to a throat of white water, and from somewhere ahead — faint, rhythmic, wrong — came a sound Oscar knew from her worst surveys: the knock of steel on stone. Someone was working the rapids upstream.

Someone was building a barrier across the High Passage.
