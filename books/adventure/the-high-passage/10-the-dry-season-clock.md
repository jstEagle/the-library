## 10. The Dry Season Clock

The river told her first.

Oscar had been awake since the gray hour before dawn — a habit three years of solo surveying had carved into her body deeper than any clock could — and she was standing at the water's edge with her coffee going cold in one hand when she saw it: a pale seam of stone exposed along the near bank, running like a scar where yesterday there had been only current. She crouched, touched it. Dry. The granite still held the night's chill, but no water had moved over it since before midnight.

She walked the bank with her notebook and measured. Twelve inches overnight. She checked the gauge she'd nailed to the leaning alder at their last camp — a board marked in carpenter's pencil, Harkness's own trick, she suspected, because the old man had thought of everything — and confirmed what her eyes already knew. The Kettle River was falling faster than any dry season she'd charted in six years of work on these watersheds.

By full light she had the figures spread on a flat rock beside the cold fire ring, and Elias stood over them with his hands jammed in his coat pockets, reading upside down the way he did when he didn't want to touch her work without permission. He'd learned that about her somewhere between Deadfall and here — that her instruments were not to be handled by other hands — and she found herself cataloguing these small courtesies the way she once catalogued bridge spans, storing them against some future need she refused to examine too closely.

"Twelve inches," he said. "Is that bad?"

"It's catastrophic." She said it flatly, because flatness was how she kept her voice from shaking. "Dry season drawdown on this stretch should run two, maybe three inches a day once the high country snowmelt quits feeding it. This is four times that. The river isn't just dropping, Elias. It's being dropped."

He looked up sharply. "You're saying someone's holding water back."

"I'm saying there's exactly one structure upstream capable of holding twelve inches of the Kettle River in its teeth, and we both know who built it and why they'd rather see this canyon dry as a kiln than let a canoe float out of it." She tapped the map case at her hip. "The dam. They've cut the spillway gates. Every day they hold the water back is a day less we have."

Elias was quiet for a long moment. He had gotten better at silence since Deadfall — or maybe she had gotten better at hearing what lived inside his silences. When he finally spoke, his voice had the careful evenness of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like.

"How long do we have?"

Oscar turned to the map, weighted at the corners with stones, and ran her finger up the blue thread of the river to the gorge Harkness had marked with three exclamation points and a single word in his cramped hand: *LAST*. Beyond it, the water narrowed into the slot canyons of the source country, unnavigable even at flood. Everything — the vault, the proof, the truth rotting in its sealed chamber — lay above that gorge. And everything they carried would have to come back down through it.

"The final passage needs eighteen inches over the sill at the gorge mouth for a loaded canoe. We're at twenty-six now." Her finger traced down the penciled columns. "At a normal drawdown, we'd have three weeks. At this rate—" She did the division twice because she wanted to be wrong. "Nine days. Maybe eight if the company opens the gates wider tonight."

"Nine days." Elias said it the way a man reads a sentence at his own trial.

"Nine days," Oscar agreed, "and the gorge itself is a full day's hard water each way, and we haven't even reached the source yet. Which means we stop camping like travelers and start moving like people being chased. Because we are."

* * *

They broke camp in twenty minutes. Three years of living out of a pack had taught Oscar to strike a site fast, but Elias matched her now, move for move, and she caught herself watching him coil the rope in the flat overlapping loops she used and thinking: *when did he learn my knots?* Somewhere between the truce at Deadfall and the kiss neither of them had mentioned since — mentioned, but never discussed, the way you don't discuss a lit fuse — he had stopped being an inspector shadowing her route and become something else, something whose hands knew her gear, whose silences she could read, whose life she had begun, without deciding to, counting alongside her own.

The river fell all morning. By noon they passed a beaver lodge standing high and dry, its mud walls cracked in the sun like old pottery, and Oscar felt the sight land in her chest with a weight out of proportion to its size. Even the animals' calendar was broken. The whole valley was being unwritten.

It was mid-afternoon, with the sun slanting gold through the smoke haze from some distant fire, when Elias, poling ahead through the shallows, suddenly backed water and raised his fist — the signal they'd agreed on. Oscar drove her paddle in and slewed her canoe alongside his against the bank.

Ahead, where the river bent around a gravel bar, something white fluttered on a post.

They approached on foot, low, Elias ahead with his hand resting on the revolver he still wore with the uncomfortable familiarity of a man who'd been issued one and had never liked it. The post was new lumber, bright-cut, driven into the bar within the last week — the sawdust at its base hadn't even weathered. Nailed to it was a printed notice, and beneath the notice, hanging from a second nail, swung a tin star stamped with the logo of the Meridian Consolidated Mining and Power Company.

Elias read the notice twice. Then he read it aloud, though Oscar had already taken it in, because saying things aloud seemed to be how he processed horror.

*"NOTICE TO ALL PERSONS. The upper Kettle drainage is closed to entry by order of Meridian Consolidated under authority of territorial lease provisions. Reward of five hundred dollars will be paid for information leading to the apprehension of a woman and a man traveling by canoe, believed to be in possession of stolen company property. The woman may use the name Fairweather. Approach with caution. Report to any company agent."*

"Stolen company property," Oscar repeated. Her voice came out strange to her own ears, very calm, very far away. "That's what we are now. Stolen property carrying itself upstream."

"They put your name on it." Elias's jaw was tight. "Not mine. They know who you are, and they're keeping me off the notice — because if I'm named, the government asks why its own inspector is posted as a fugitive, and questions start getting asked they can't afford." He tore the notice off the post, folded it, and put it inside his coat. "Evidence," he said, before she could ask. "I'm still technically an inspector. Let me collect things. It's the only jurisdiction I have left."

Five hundred dollars. Oscar turned the figure over in her mind as they pushed back onto the water. Five hundred dollars was more than a section hand earned in a year. There were men in every river town, every logging camp, every rail junction between here and the coast, who would sell their grandmothers for half that. The company wasn't hunting them anymore. The company had deputized the entire territory.

And below the reward notice, in smaller print, the last line: *The upper trail is sealed. All bridges and passes above Mile 40 are closed to travel.*

Sealed. She understood what that meant by evening, when they rounded a bend and saw it: the old footbridge across the river at the mouth of Sawtooth Creek, the crossing Harkness's map showed as the only practical escape route east out of the canyon, sagging into the water with its cables cut. Fresh cuts, bright metal gleaming in the frayed strands. And on the far bank, small in the distance but unmistakable, a line of men with pack mules moving north along the rim trail — sealing the upper country the way a farmer seals a well.

"They're not just chasing us anymore," Elias said quietly. "They're building a box."

* * *

They made nine miles that afternoon, which should have been fifteen, because the falling water turned the easy stretches into obstacle courses of exposed bars and strainers — trees toppled by the shifting banks, their branches combing the current like fingers waiting to catch a hull. Twice Oscar had to line her canoe down rapids that Harkness's annotations rated as simple riffles, and both times she thought about the numbers in her notebook, ticking downward like sand through a cracked glass.

They camped on a high bench above the river, back from the bank, no fire. Oscar sat with the map open across her knees while Elias took first watch, and she studied the gorge — *LAST*, Harkness had written, and beneath it, in letters pressed so hard the paper had nearly torn: *RUN IT EARLY OR NOT AT ALL. THE RIVER DOES NOT WAIT AND NEITHER SHOULD YOU.*

"He knew," she said softly, mostly to herself. "Harkness knew the water would fail. That's why he marked the dates on every sounding. He was racing this same clock years ago, and he lost."

Elias came and sat beside her, close enough that she could feel the warmth of him through her jacket, and did not say anything, which was its own kind of answer. Below them the river murmured over its shrinking bed, and the sound had changed in two days — thinner, more broken, a voice going hoarse.

"Nine days," he said at last. "What does the arithmetic look like? Honestly."

"Honestly?" She smoothed the map flat. "Day one and two, we run the middle canyon — good water, make distance. Day three, the narrows, hard but passable. Day four, we reach the source fork and cache the canoes; from there it's climbing, and the vault is another day's hike if Harkness's bearings are true. So call it five days up. That leaves us four to get back down through the gorge with whatever we find — and the gorge will be lower every hour we're gone." She looked up at him. "If anything goes wrong up there — weather, injury, Rennick — we don't come home. Not by the river. And the trails are sealed, and the bridges are cut, and every man on the rim has a poster with my name on it."

"So we don't let anything go wrong."

"That's not a plan, Elias. That's a wish."

"It's the only kind I've got left." He said it lightly, but she heard the thing underneath it, the same thing she'd heard the night he told her about his father — the sound of a man who had spent his whole career believing the system would bend if you pushed it correctly, and had finally, at terrible cost, stopped believing it. "Besides," he added, "you forgot one variable."

"Which is?"

"You're the best river engineer I've ever seen, and I've spent two weeks pretending I didn't notice. If anyone can beat a dying river downstream, it's the woman who measures rivers for a living." He paused. "Also, I've decided to be useful. I can read water now. Badly. But I can."

Despite everything — the poster with her name, the cut cables, the numbers draining out of her notebook like blood — Oscar laughed. It surprised her, the laugh, coming up out of her like a bird startled off a wire, and for a moment the canyon held nothing in it but the sound and the thin murmur of the failing river and the two of them sitting shoulder to shoulder in the dark above it.

Then Elias went rigid beside her.

His hand closed on her wrist — not tenderly, urgently — and tilted his head toward the south rim, where the last light was dying. At first she saw nothing. Then she saw it: a pinpoint of flame, high on the ridge, bobbing. Another beside it. A third. Torchlight, or lanterns, moving in a deliberate line along the rim trail, parallel to the river. A search line. And far behind them, fainter, a sound she felt in her ribs before she heard it with her ears: the stuttering cough of a gas engine, the kind that powered a winch or a compressor — the kind that built roads.

"They're not waiting for the reward posters to work," she breathed. "They're coming down after us."

"Or cutting us off ahead." Elias was already moving, rolling the map, dousing even the memory of their fire. "Either way, we've lost the luxury of sleeping."

Oscar stood, slung her pack, and looked one last time at the river sliding dark and diminished past the foot of their bench. Nine days, she had promised herself. But standing there with lanterns crawling along the rim above her and the water falling faster than grief, she understood that the number was already a lie. The clock didn't tick in days. It ticked in hours now, and every hour belonged to Rennick until she took it back.

"We run the narrows tonight," she said. "By moonlight. While there's still water to float us."

Elias looked at her — at the map case strapped to her hip, at the set of her jaw — and whatever he saw there, he didn't argue. He picked up his paddle.

"Together, then," he said. "Badly."

Together, she thought, pushing the canoe out into the black and silver water. Badly, if necessary. But upstream, and on time — because the river would not wait, and neither, anymore, would she.
