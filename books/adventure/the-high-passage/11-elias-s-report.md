## 11. Elias's Report

The telegraph office at Deadfall had a desk that wobbled on three legs and a fourth made of a folded survey stake, and Elias Vane sat down to it the way a man sits down to his own execution — carefully, with dignity, taking note of the small discomforts because they were the last honest things available to him.

The report form was standard issue: Department of Railways and Interior Waters, Interim Field Assessment, Form 7-C. He had filled out perhaps four hundred of them in eleven years of service. They were the architecture of his career, each one a brick in a wall of institutional trust he had spent his whole adult life building, because his father had taught him that a man's word in an official document was the only kind of word that could not be taken back.

*Conditions along the upper corridor,* he wrote, and stopped.

Through the office's cracked window he could see Oscar Fairweather down by the water, kneeling on the shingle with Harkness's map spread across her knees, her hair tied back with what appeared to be a piece of signal cord, her hands moving over the contour lines with the tenderness other men reserved for the faces of their children. She was not reading the map. He understood that now. She was listening to it.

Behind him, in the corner where the stove ticked as it cooled, the evidence lay wrapped in oilcloth: photographs of core samples, company memos bearing signatures of men who dined with deputy ministers, and the second brass tag from the Portage of Bones, stamped *BELLHAVEN SURVEY — 1909*, the year the dam was poured thin and everyone who knew it was paid or buried.

He dipped the pen again.

*Conditions along the upper corridor remain consistent with seasonal variation. Water levels at the gorge have dropped approximately fourteen inches since last measurement, attributable to drought. No evidence of structural failure at the Bellhaven impoundment has been observed at this stage of inquiry. Further assessment requires extended survey of the source canyon, estimated seven days.*

Every sentence was true except the one that mattered, which was worse than lying, because it was lying with the truth's own materials. He read it back twice. His hand did not shake. That surprised him. He had expected his body to rebel against this the way it would rebel against poison, some vomiting of the conscience, but instead there was only a great stillness, like the air before snow.

His father had died in a tunnel collapse outside Corven Hollow when Elias was nineteen. The official report — Form 7-C's grim ancestor — had called it *a sudden and unforeseeable release of ground water*. Elias had believed it for six years, until he found his father's own field notes in a trunk and read, between the lines of careful measurements, a man documenting corners being cut, documenting them the way you document anything you intend to survive: thoroughly, dated, signed. Three weeks after the last entry, the tunnel came down. Eleven men. The company paid out pensions and the government filed its paper and the river of ordinary life closed over the whole affair like water over a stone.

Elias had joined the inspectorate because he believed the paper could be made honest. He had spent eleven years believing it. And now he sat in a dead town's telegraph office with a pen full of the department's own ink, composing the first lie of his career, and the remarkable thing — the thing he would carry to whatever reckoning waited for him — was how light it felt. Not weightless. Light the way a door is light once you understand it was never locked.

He blotted the page, folded it, and addressed it to the Regional Office at Cairnmoor, care of the evening wire.

Mara Quist materialized beside him the way old women do in empty rooms, without sound, as though she had always been standing there. She looked at the envelope, then at him, over the rims of spectacles she wore more as ornament than necessity.

"You want that sent tonight," she said. It was not a question.

"I do."

"And you know what's in it."

"You watched me write it."

"Mm." She took the envelope and turned it over in her fingers. "Forty-one years I've tapped keys in towns like this. I can tell you something about messages, Inspector. Every one of them is a little door. Once it's open, it don't close just because you're sorry." She held his eyes. "You sure about the direction you're opening this one?"

"No," Elias said honestly. "But I'm sure about the other direction."

Something moved behind the old woman's face — approval, or grief, or the place where those two things share a border. She tucked the envelope into her apron. "Evening wire goes at eight. If you change your mind, you've got till then." She paused at the door. "Though in my experience, men like you don't change your minds. You just spend the rest of your lives describing the moment you didn't."

She left him alone with the ticking stove and the wobbling desk and the sound, faint under everything, of the river running lower.

* * *

He found Oscar at dusk on the flat above the shingle, where she'd built the fire small and screened, the way she did everything — efficiently, invisibly, a woman who had learned to take up as little room in the world as possible and then resented herself for it. She was cleaning the graze on her forearm with whisky they couldn't afford to waste, jaw set against the sting, and when she heard his boots on the gravel she didn't look up.

"Report's filed?" she asked.

"It goes out at eight. Mara has it."

Oscar wound the bandage with her teeth pulling the end tight. "And what does it say?"

"That the gorge is dropping, that I need seven more days, and that nothing at Bellhaven suggests anything but nature having a bad season."

She looked up then. Firelight caught the planes of her face — a face built for weather, he thought, for wind and long distances, beautiful the way a rail cut through granite is beautiful, which is to say honestly and at cost. "That's perjury by paperwork," she said quietly. "They'll hang you with it if they find out."

"They'll hang me with it when they find out. There's no *if*. Rennick will request my field notes within the month regardless of outcome. A false interim report is discoverable, provable, and fatal." He sat down across the fire from her, elbows on knees. "I've done the arithmetic three times. It doesn't improve."

"Then why—"

"Because the alternative report was also fatal. Just to other people." He fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. "I've been thinking about what to tell you. I've started six times since Deadfall. I keep choosing the wrong words, so I'm going to choose none of them and simply say it."

"Elias—"

"My father was a mining engineer. Corven Hollow, Number Three tunnel. You may have heard of it — eleven men, 1898, 'unforeseeable release of ground water.'" He said the phrase the way a man spits out a bad tooth. "I believed that language for six years. It's very good language. It's designed to be believed. Then I found his notes."

Oscar had gone very still. The fire popped; neither of them moved.

"He knew the timbering was underspec. He knew the water table readings had been altered — he'd compared them against his own barometric logs. He documented all of it, dated and signed, because he thought documentation would protect him. He thought the paper was a shield." Elias heard his own voice go flat, which was what it did instead of breaking. "It was a target. Three weeks after his final entry, the tunnel came down. I was nineteen. My mother never recovered, and I — I did something worse. I recovered. I recovered into the institution that killed him. I put on its uniform and I carried its forms and I told myself that if I was scrupulous enough, honest enough, if my own reports were beyond reproach, then somewhere in the machine there would be one clean gear turning, and it would matter."

"And now?"

"Now I'm sitting in a burned-out telegraph town filing the first dishonest report of my life, and it is the only honest act I have ever committed inside that uniform." He looked at her across the fire. "You asked me once, on the train, why a man becomes an inspector. I gave you the answer I give everyone. Here's the real one: I became an inspector to find out whether the paper could be made to tell the truth. Eleven years. The answer is no. The paper tells whatever story the men who own the paper require. So the truth has to travel some other way — in brass tags and oilcloth bundles and a dead prospector's map carried upriver by a woman too stubborn to drown."

Oscar's eyes had not left his face. In the firelight they were the color of the river in shadow, dark and moving. "Harkness made me promise," she said. "Carry it to the source before dry season ends. I thought it was a strange bargain to make with a dying man. Now I think he wasn't buying a courier. He was buying a witness. Two, if he could get them."

"He got two."

"Yes," she said softly. "He got two."

For a while neither spoke. Down below, the river muttered over its stones, lower every hour, the dry season closing like a fist. Somewhere east, Rennick's men were reading maps of their own. Elias was aware of all of it — the clock, the danger, the seven days he had purchased with his career — and aware, simultaneously, of something much closer: the small distance across the fire, four feet of gravel and smoke, which had somehow become the longest survey of his life.

"When I pulled you out of the gorge," he said, "you weren't breathing. Did you know that? Thirty seconds, maybe forty. I counted them against my pulse because counting was the only thing keeping me sane. And in those thirty seconds I discovered something I had managed not to discover for two hundred miles." He rose, crossed the fire, and crouched in front of her so that they were level, eye to eye, the heat of the coals pressing against his shoulder. "I am going to tell you this once, plainly, because I have spent my whole career writing things down and this is the first thing I want spoken. I love you, Oscar Fairweather. Not comfortably. Not conveniently. Against every regulation I swore to and every plan I had, which were, I'll admit, never very good plans. I love you the way water loves the low place. It isn't a decision. It's a topography."

Her breath went out of her slowly. For a moment she was the woman on the closed spur again, the one who measured her life in track miles, and he watched her reach for a deflection — he could see it forming, some dry remark about regulations or topography — and then he watched her refuse it. She reached out and took his hand, the one that had held her head above the black water, and turned it over and studied the palm as though reading soundings in it.

"I measured my life in miles for three years," she said. "Because miles don't end. You can always add another one, and as long as you're adding them, you never have to arrive anywhere, and as long as you never arrive, nobody can be taken from you at the destination." Her thumb traced the line of his lifeline, deliberate, an engineer's touch. "Then a dying man handed me a map, and a man who lies badly handed me his honesty, and somewhere on this river I started measuring in days again. Nine days of water left. Seven bought by your signature on a lie." She looked up. "Do you know what I did when you pulled me out of the gorge? Before I could breathe? I was angry. Because I had gotten used to the idea of drowning, and you interrupted it. That's what grief does — it makes death feel like a schedule kept." Her grip tightened on his hand. "I don't want the schedule anymore, Elias. I want the interruption."

He kissed her.

It was not gentle and it was not planned; it was the kiss of two people who had been circling a fire for two hundred miles and had finally run out of circumference. Her hand came up to his jaw, coal-warm, and the fire spat, and far off a night bird called across the water, and for one whole minute there was no report, no Rennick, no clock — only the astonishing, unprofessional fact of her mouth against his, of her pulse beating under his palm where it rested at her throat, quick as telegraph code, saying something urgent in a language he found he could read.

Then she drew back an inch, forehead against his, and laughed — a low, wrecked, wonderful sound.

"Well," she said. "That was a mistake."

"Entirely," he agreed.

"We should regret it."

"Immediately and at length."

Neither of them moved. Her thumb was still at his jaw. The fire settled into embers with a sigh, and the stars over Deadfall came out hard and cold and indifferent, and Elias Vane understood that he had crossed a line from which the department, the career, the whole paper architecture of his life could never recall him — and that he would build the rest of his existence on this side of it gladly, on gravel, by a small fire, next to a woman who lied for the first time in her careful life to protect a dead man's promise.

"Regretted," he murmured.

"Not regretted," she said.

* * *

They were still sitting close when Mara Quist's key began to chatter in the office below — a sound like rain on tin, carrying up through the broken window into the dark. Then it stopped. Started again. Stopped.

Oscar lifted her head. "That's not traffic pattern. That's someone sending short bursts and waiting."

Elias was already on his feet. They came down the slope together and through the door into the office, where lamplight swung and Mara sat rigid at the key, receiver clamped to her ear, her free hand raised for silence. On the desk before her lay his report, unopened, unsent — and beside it a flimsy sheet of message blank covered in her jagged transcription. She wrote as she listened, letter by letter, and Elias read upside down as the words assembled:

*RENNICK TO ALL STATIONS STOP SUBJECT FAIRWEATHER AND INSPECTOR VANE BELIEVED TRAVELING TOGETHER STOP REWARD DOUBLED STOP VANE TO BE TREATED AS TURNCOAT STOP AUTHORIZATION RECEIVED STOP DISPOSITION AT DISCRETION OF FIELD AGENTS STOP*

The key chattered off. Mara removed her spectacles with terrible calm.

"Well," she said. "Your eight o'clock wire's moot, Inspector. Somebody upstream already reported your position. Which means—" she nodded at the transcription, at the words *DISCRETION OF FIELD AGENTS*, meaning *shoot on sight*— "they're not waiting for the dry season either."

From the darkness outside, down toward the shingle where the canoe lay, came the unmistakable sound of a boot on stone.

Oscar's hand found Elias's in the dark, and squeezed once: *run.*
