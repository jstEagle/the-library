## 12. Quist's Last Transmission

The telegraph office at Deadfall smelled of dust and hot bakelite and forty years of Mara Quist's pipe smoke, soaked so deep into the clapboard that no amount of burning would ever fully get it out — a thought that would come back to Oscar Fairweather later, at the worst hour of her life, with terrible precision.

For now it was evening, and the lamp was lit, and the old woman sat before the brass key with her sleeves rolled to the elbow like a surgeon about to begin.

"Say it again," Mara said. "Slow this time. I'm seventy-one years old, girl, and I've been dead twice already, so I can't afford to mishear anything important."

Oscar stood in the doorway with Elias Vane's coat over her arm — he was out checking the handcar shed, because he still pretended to be her jailer even though neither of them had used the word *prisoner* in three days — and she made herself say it again, plainly, the way you'd read out an indictment.

"You tap a message to the Junction relay. You say you've seen us. Two travelers on the east fork trail, moving toward Silver Bend, asking after pack horses. You name Elias by his commission number, because that's what they're tracking him by. You sign off as yourself. Then you cut your line and go."

"And they'll believe it because I'm the last operator on this stretch who's still on the wire, and because for thirty years I have reported every stray dog that wandered through this town like it was a federal matter." Mara flexed her fingers over the key. "Boring people are believed. That's the whole trick of it. Nobody suspects the woman who reads the shipping manifests aloud for pleasure."

"It'll bring them east," Oscar said. "Rennick's men, all of them, or near enough. They'll ride hard for Silver Bend and it's empty country — two days' ride before they figure out there's nothing there but coyotes and a burned-out stamp mill." She heard how it sounded. She'd heard how it sounded the first time, too. "It buys us the window we need to reach the source canyon before the river drops."

Mara didn't look up from the key. "And if it doesn't buy the window? If Rennick sends only half his men east and keeps the other half on the west trail?"

"Then we're caught in the canyon, and none of this matters anyway."

"Mm." The old woman reached under the operating table and came up with a bottle of rye without a label, poured two fingers into a chipped cup, and pushed it across the desk toward Oscar. "You look like you need that more than I do. Drink it or don't, but stop standing in my doorway like a debt collector."

Oscar took the cup because refusing seemed crueler than drinking. The whiskey was terrible and warm and it settled in her chest like a coal.

"I keep thinking there has to be another way," she said.

"There isn't, and worse, you know there isn't, which is why you're standing there hating yourself instead of arguing with me properly. I raised four sons, Miss Fairweather, and buried three of them, and the fourth thinks I died in '09. I know every shape a lie of necessity takes, and I know the difference between a sacrifice and a waste, and this" — she tapped the brass key once with one yellowed fingernail — "is neither. It's a transaction. Harkness owed me eleven dollars and a grudge, and I'm settling his account the way he'd have wanted: expensively, and against people who deserve it."

"You knew him a long time."

"Forty years. He'd come down off the mountain every spring with ore samples in his pockets and opinions nobody asked for, and he'd sit in that chair you're leaning against and read me his letters home before he mailed them, because he said I had better handwriting than God and could tell him where he'd misspelled things." Her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "Last time he came through, three years ago now, he wasn't reading me letters anymore. He was reading me depth soundings. And he asked me, casual as you please, what I knew about Bellhaven."

Oscar set down the cup. "What did you tell him?"

"I told him Bellhaven flooded in April of '11 and the company paid out settlements and everyone moved on, same as the papers said." Mara's eyes came up, and they were not an old woman's eyes just then; they were sharp as a telegraph signal, all edge and interval. "And then I told him the part the papers didn't print. My sister lived in Bellhaven. She ran the boarding house on Cord Street. When the water came through, it came through *sideways*, Oscar — from the north, from the canyon side, in the middle of a dry week with no rain anywhere in the range. A flood doesn't come sideways out of a clear sky unless something upstream opened that shouldn't have been open. I wrote that in a letter to the commission. My letter never got an answer. But a man from the company got on the train at Junction two days later, and after that, my wire started being inspected. Every message I sent went through a man's hands before it went onto the line. So I learned to send two kinds of truth: the kind they let through, and the kind I kept."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere outside, the wind moved through the dead junction town, rattling the shutters of houses that had been empty for a decade, and Oscar thought: this is what it costs, this is what it has always cost, the company doesn't kill you first, it makes the whole country into your cell and then waits for you to die politely inside it.

"Harkness kept his kind," she said quietly.

"He kept everything. That man filed his whole life like survey notes." Mara pulled open the drawer beneath the key and lifted out a flat oilcloth packet, tied with wire. "He left this with me in the spring, when he passed through heading up-country for the last time. Said if a woman came looking — a surveyor, dark-haired, careful, who measured everything twice — I was to give it to her and tell her one thing." The old woman's voice roughened, just slightly, like a wheel bearing going dry. "He said: *tell her the map is true but the river is faster.*"

Oscar took the packet. Inside, wrapped around something small and hard, she found a spare stylus, a coil of copper ground wire — and a second brass survey tag, stamped BELLHAVEN SURVEY, 1911, its edges worn smooth as if it had spent years turning in a man's pocket like a coin. Not the tag Harkness had died holding. Another one. There had been more than one of them. More than one man who had known, and carried it, and —

She looked up. Mara was watching her with the expression of someone reading a long transmission come in slow, character by character.

"How many?" Oscar asked.

"That's the wrong question. The right question is whether you're going to stand there cataloguing the dead, or whether you're going to help me burn my office down tonight." Mara stood, joints cracking like green timber, and began pulling ledgers off the shelves with brisk, unceremonious efficiency. "Because if we're doing it, we do it right. I've watched men half-burn evidence before and it's embarrassing for everyone involved."

* * *

They worked through the evening, and the work had a rhythm to it that steadied Oscar more than the whiskey had.

Mara sorted. Everything in that little office was sorted — that was the thing Oscar hadn't understood until she saw the drawers opened, the whole architecture of the woman's long vigil laid bare. Forty years of messages, copied by hand into ledgers before they were sent, because Mara Quist had never trusted a line she couldn't also hold in ink. Shipping manifests and weather reports and birthday congratulations, yes — but underneath those, in the back drawers, in pencil, a second record. Dates of company shipments that didn't match their manifests. Names of men hired at Junction who never came back down. Wire transfer numbers routed through the mining company's Denver office in the weeks after the flood, payments that were far too large for dam repairs and far too regular for grief.

"They bought witnesses," Oscar said, turning pages with hands that wanted to shake. "This is the payroll."

"This is *part* of the payroll. The rest is in Denver, locked up tighter than a widow's purse." Mara fed a fistful of telegrams into the stove, watching them curl. "Harkness's letter named the ones hereabouts. Storekeeper at Junction. The coroner at Bellhaven — well, what was Bellhaven. Two section foremen. Me, they never offered money to. I should be insulted." She snorted. "Instead they gave me an inspector. Man used to come through every quarter, read my logbook, drink my coffee, and ask me questions in a voice so friendly it made my teeth ache. I gave him nothing but boredom for thirty years, and boredom, Miss Fairweather, is armor."

"And now you're giving it all away."

"Now I'm spending it. There's a difference." Mara closed the drawer. "A record in a drawer protects nobody. A record in fifty newspapers might. Harkness understood that at the end, and I understand it now, and the both of us are too old to pretend otherwise."

Outside, boots on the porch boards. Oscar's hand went to the revolver at her hip — three days ago she wouldn't have known where it rode, and now her body found it without instruction, which was itself a kind of grief — but then Elias's knock came, the particular double-tap he'd established as theirs, and he came in with cold air and woodsmoke clinging to him.

"The handcar's sound," he said. "Axle bearings need grease but she'll run. Westbound track's clear to the trestle, and after that—" He stopped. He'd read the room the way he read most rooms, fast and completely. The open stove. The ledgers stacked for burning. The oilcloth packet in Oscar's lap. "It's tonight, then."

"Tonight," Mara confirmed.

Elias looked at the old woman for a long moment. Whatever he was weighing behind those gray eyes, he weighed it thoroughly — Oscar had learned that much about him; he did nothing at half weight — and then he crossed the room and took the ledgers from the stack nearest the fire and began feeding them in himself, methodically, page by page, so the flames wouldn't choke.

"You don't have to help," Mara said.

"No," Elias agreed, and kept feeding the fire.

* * *

At half past nine by Mara's cracked regulator clock, the old woman sat down at the key one final time.

Oscar stood behind her left shoulder. Elias took the window, the way he always did, silhouette against the starlit street of the dead town, and Oscar caught herself registering that — catching herself *relying* on it — and put the noticing away somewhere she'd deal with later, or wouldn't.

Mara's hand hovered over the key. Then, without preamble, she began to send.

Even Oscar, who knew nothing of the art, could hear the difference between Mara's sending and the chatter that came down the wire at random hours: this was a master's hand, unhurried, clean, each letter struck with the confidence of a woman who had been putting words into lightning for longer than most of her listeners had been alive. The message went east through the Junction relay, letter by letter:

SEEN TWO TRAVELERS WEST FORK TRAIL HEADING SILVER BEND STOP ONE ANSWERS TO COMMISSION NUMBER 4471 STOP ASKING AFTER PACK HORSES AT THE MILLER PLACE STOP WILL WATCH AND REPORT STOP QUIST.

Then silence. Then, deliberately, Mara keyed a single character — a fault, a stutter — and pulled the main line fuse from its clips with a snap, plunging the office's sending circuit dead.

"There," she said, almost gently. "Now it looks like I was interrupted mid-watch. They'll spend an hour wondering if my line was cut and two hours deciding it wasn't worth riding out to check, because why would anyone bother cutting the wire of a boring old woman in a town with six standing buildings?" She screwed the fuse back in loosely, enough to make the circuit look intact but not carry. "By the time anyone reaches me, I intend to be a rumor."

The stove had burned low. The ledgers were ash. The room smelled of scorched paper and ending.

Mara rose, took down the framed certificate above the key — THIRTY YEARS OF FAITHFUL SERVICE, some railway telegraph division, the ink faded to the color of weak tea — looked at it for a moment, and set it face-down on the desk.

"Take the lamp," she said. "We'll light the rest with the stove."

* * *

They fired the building at the north wall, where the paint was oldest and driest, and by the time the flames found the tar roof the three of them were standing in the middle of Deadfall's empty street, and the night had turned orange.

It went fast, the way old wooden buildings do — faster than felt reasonable, as if the office had simply been waiting decades for permission. Heat rolled across the street and Oscar felt it press against her face and thought of the depth soundings, of water coming through sideways, of a town called Bellhaven that existed now only as a brass tag and a sister who'd written a letter and gotten silence for an answer.

Mara watched it burn with her pipe unlit in her hand.

"My husband built that desk," she said, conversational, over the crackle. "Worst joiner in three counties. It wobbled for twenty-six years and I loved it for all of them." She struck a match, finally, and got the pipe going, and the little flame lit her face from below. "You're doing it again, Miss Fairweather. That thing where you carry other people's losses on top of your own like you're a freight engine and grief is cargo. I can hear the wheels straining from here."

"I used you as bait," Oscar said. The words came out flatter than she meant them to. "I stood in your office and asked an old woman to draw killers onto herself so I could save my own skin, and I called it strategy, and you let me, and I don't—"

"Stop." Mara turned. In the firelight her face was all planes and shadow, and her voice, when it came, was quiet in the way that rails are quiet before a train. "You did not use me. I have been sitting at that key for forty years waiting for exactly one message worth sending with my whole heart, and tonight you handed it to me. Do you understand what I am telling you? Tonight was the best night of my working life. I lied beautifully. I lied like an artist. And somewhere east of here there is a man named Rennick who is going to wake up tomorrow and ride his men into empty country because of me, an old woman with a pipe and a bad hip, and I will laugh about it until I die." She drew on the pipe. "The guilt is mine to refuse, and I refuse it. You may borrow that refusal if you like. It travels well."

Oscar found she couldn't speak. She nodded instead, and it must have been enough, because Mara turned back to the fire.

Elias came and stood beside Oscar, close, not touching, which was its own language now, one they'd been writing carefully since the night at the fire when everything had almost been said and then had been said, actually, in the end — and she leaned a degree into his shoulder and let it count.

"We should move," he said softly. "Fire draws eyes. We want the handcar gone before anyone comes down the west trail to gawk."

"In a minute." Mara held up one finger. "One more thing, and then I'll vanish into the hills like a proper legend."

She walked — unhurried, hip complaining audibly — to the corner of the burning office, pried loose a loose board she clearly knew intimately, and brought back a small canvas satchel, which she pressed into Oscar's hands.

"Spare batteries, a field glass, dried apples, and Harkness's spare notebook — his real one, the soundings he never showed the company, cross-checked against mine." She patted the satchel like a horse's neck. "You'll need all of it. And you'll need this." From her coat pocket she produced a folded paper, sealed with plain wax. "For the hearing, if there is one. My sworn statement. Dates, names, transfer numbers, and the text of the letter I wrote in '11 that disappeared. I've signed it and had it witnessed by the only honest man in Junction, who happens to be blind and therefore immune to intimidation by paperwork."

"Mara—"

"If you say *thank you* I'll box your ears. Go measure your river." The old woman stepped back, and the firelight made her tall, somehow, or made Oscar see her as she had always been: a woman who had held a line open through forty years of darkness so that somebody, someday, could send the truth across it. "Silver Bend road forks at the second cairn past the mill ruins. Take the left. And Miss Fairweather — the map is true, but the river is faster. Don't let it beat you."

They loaded the handcar by firelight, the burning office roaring behind them like a forge, and pushed out onto the westbound rail as the roof timbers began to fall. At the curve past the water tower Oscar looked back one last time. Mara Quist stood in the middle of the street, small and straight-backed against the blaze, and raised one hand — not waving, exactly. Signaling. The way an operator signals a train coming through her station: *line clear, proceed.*

Then Oscar bent to the bar, and Elias matched her stroke for stroke, and Deadfall fell away behind them into the dark, orange at its heart, while ahead the rails ran pale under the stars toward the high country, and somewhere behind them, east, men were already saddling for a wild-goose chase — and somewhere ahead, the river was dropping, drop by drop by drop, like a clock with water for hands.

The second cairn came sooner than expected. The left fork. And on the gravel beside it, fresh in the starlight, boot prints — many, heavy, recent, headed west along the very route they'd meant to take.

Elias crouched over them, and when he looked up at Oscar his face was grim in the dying glow of the fire miles back.

"They didn't all go east," he said. "Somebody stayed behind."
