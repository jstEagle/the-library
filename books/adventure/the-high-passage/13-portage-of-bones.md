## 13. Portage of Bones

The river gave out on them at noon.

Not all at once — it thinned first, the way a voice does when a man is dying, going shallow and apologetic over the stones until it was barely a hand's width deep and moving no faster than spilled tea. Elias stood hip-deep in the middle of it with his trousers rolled and his boots slung around his neck, looking upstream at the white glare of exposed gravel bars, and said what they had both been thinking since dawn.

"That's it, then."

"That's it." Oscar dragged the canoe the last few yards onto the bank and let it settle into the willows. Her arms were shaking — not from fear now, just from the plain arithmetic of three days of paddling against current that had been dropping under them like a floor giving way. "Harkness's notes say there's water again past the canyon. Snowmelt comes back in off the western faces. But between here and there—" she looked up at the walls rising sheer and rust-colored on either side of the valley, closing in like a book being shut, "—we walk."

The canyon mouth was maybe forty feet across. The map showed the old survey trail hugging the left wall, cut into the rock by the original crews twenty years ago, before the company decided the High Passage had never existed and did its best to make the geography agree.

Elias waded out and stood wringing his shirt hem, watching her face. He'd gotten good at reading her, these last weeks. She hated it and was grateful for it in equal measure.

"You've gone quiet," he said.

"I'm always quiet."

"You've gone *that* quiet. The kind you get when you're doing sums you don't like."

Oscar looked at the canyon again. Somewhere behind those walls, according to a dead man's pencil marks, lay the proof that Bellhaven hadn't drowned by accident. And somewhere in this country, Rennick's men were moving faster than they were, because Rennick's men didn't have to portage a canoe or ration their powder or stop to sleep.

"Load your pack," she said. "We'll cache the boat here. Marked. If we come back down this way, I'd rather not swim."

They sank the canoe in a side pool under a ledge, weighted with stones, tied to a root, and covered with cut willow boughs. Oscar drove one of Harkness's brass tags into the bark of an alder at the waterline — the last of the three she carried, stamped with a survey number that officially belonged to nothing. Then she shouldered her pack, checked the map case one more time with the flat of her hand against the oilskin, the way she'd check a pulse, and walked into the canyon.

The light changed immediately. It went from open sky to a narrow ribbon overhead, and the air went cool and mineral-smelling, and their footsteps came back to them doubled off the rock. For the first half mile the trail was exactly what the map promised: a neat shelf of cut stone, two men wide, graded with a surveyor's care. Oscar found herself reading the cuts without meaning to — the drill marks, the drainage notches, the little stone cairns set at intervals as witness points. Whoever built this had been good. Good enough that she caught herself thinking, with an old professional ache, *I could have worked with these men.*

Then the trail turned a shoulder of rock, and she stopped thinking about engineering at all.

The first piece of equipment was a winch. It sat against the wall where someone had abandoned it, its cable paid out thirty feet and frayed to wire, its housing split open like a dropped melon. Beyond it were crates — dozens of them, some broken, some intact, stenciled with a company name she recognized from freight manifests she'd signed off on years ago in another life. A tripod theodolite lay on its side in the scree, its telescope bent at an angle no instrument survives. Canvas tents, rotted to lace. A cook stove with its firebox sprung. Rope, coiled and then simply left, stiff with age and bleached the color of bone.

It went on for a quarter mile. Maybe more. The whole crew's world, dumped along the trail as if the men had walked out of it mid-meal and never come back.

Elias crouched beside the theodolite and turned it over slowly. "This is transit-grade equipment," he said quietly. "Government issue. This is *survey* gear, Oscar. Not mining gear."

"I know what it is."

"A full party. Instruments, camp, instruments—" He stopped. He was holding something small that he'd lifted from inside the theodolite's case, and whatever it was, it had made his voice go strange.

She came and looked over his arm.

A notebook. Water-swollen, its cover furred green with mold, but bound in the same government-issue board covers Elias carried his own reports in. The name on the flyleaf had run, but the ink ghost of it was still legible enough, and Elias read it aloud anyway, though she'd already seen it:

*"T. Aldous Merrick, Asst. Surveyor, Dept. of the Interior."*

"Merrick," Elias said. "There's a Merrick in the departmental rolls. There *was*. He resigned in — God, nineteen years ago. Went west 'for reasons of health.' That's all the file says. Reasons of health." He looked up at her, and the ribbon of sky above the canyon threw a thin light across his face, and she watched him arrive at the same place she'd arrived. "He didn't resign. He was sent. They sent a federal surveyor up here to certify the dam site, and then they buried him so far even the department forgot he existed."

"Or they didn't forget," Oscar said. "Maybe forgetting was the assignment."

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a fallen slab, because the trail narrowed after the equipment field and neither of them wanted to feel their way along it in the dark. Elias built a fire small enough to hide in a coffee can, which was a trick he'd learned somewhere he'd never explained, and they ate cold beans and hard bread and drank creek water boiled in the dented pot, and neither of them talked much.

But Oscar couldn't sleep. She lay on her bedroll watching the firelight move on the canyon wall, and the wall kept showing her things.

Merrick's notebook sat in her pack now, wrapped in oilskin next to Harkness's map. She'd read what she could of it by firelight before the light failed — pages of soundings, of core descriptions, of elevations measured and re-measured, and then, beginning abruptly two-thirds through, a different hand. A hurried hand. Sentences that stopped and started like a man writing while listening for footsteps.

*They have my instruments. Told me the figures are clerical errors. The figures are not errors. The dam as specified will not hold the spring rise. I have written this in duplicate. If anything happens to me the second copy goes—*

And there it broke off. The rest of the page was blank. The rest of the book was blank. Nineteen years of blank.

Oscar got up quietly, took the notebook, and walked a little way down the trail to where the moonlight reached the canyon floor. She sat on a boulder with the book in her lap and tried to finish the sentence for him. *The second copy goes where?* To the department? The department had filed him under *reasons of health.* To a newspaper? Which newspaper prints the word of a man who officially doesn't exist? To another surveyor, another inspector, anyone with a seal and a conscience?

To a prospector, maybe. To a hard-bitten, half-mad old man who trapped the high country and asked no man's permission and kept his own counsel — a man who might find a body, someday, and find the copy on it, and understand enough to keep it, and spend the rest of his life learning how to use it.

Her breath went out of her slowly.

That was how Harkness had gotten his tag. That was how Harkness had known the depth soundings matched no flood on record — because he'd held the original soundings in his hands, taken by a government man who died carrying them. Harkness hadn't stumbled onto the truth. He'd *inherited* it. And he'd spent nineteen years too old and too alone to do anything with it but wait for someone who could.

"Someone else died carrying this," she said aloud, to the canyon, to Merrick, to nobody. The words came back off the rock, flattened and strange.

Behind her, boots on stone. Elias, wrapped in his coat, stopping a few feet away. He'd heard. Of course he'd heard; the canyon repeated everything.

"Yes," he said. "And the department wrote *reasons of health* on it and closed the file." He came and sat on the boulder's edge, not touching her, close enough that she could feel the warmth of him. "I keep trying to work out whether that makes me a fool for serving it or just late."

"It makes you a man deciding," Oscar said. "Late is better than never. I'd know. I spent three years measuring track miles instead of living, and I told myself that was duty too. Grief dressed up as diligence. It isn't. Duty is what you do *for* something. What I was doing was hiding *from* something." She turned the notebook over in her hands. "Merrick hid too, in the end. Or they hid him. Either way, the truth stayed in this canyon because everyone who could carry it out was afraid, or bought, or dead."

"And us?"

"We're none of those yet."

He almost smiled. In the moonlight it looked like something breaking open. "Yet is doing a lot of work in that sentence."

"Elias." She waited until he looked at her. "When we find the vault — if we find it — there won't be a clean way out. You know that. Whatever's in there, Rennick would burn this whole canyon down to keep it buried. We're not carrying evidence anymore. We're carrying a live thing, and it bites."

"I've been bitten before," he said. "By worse than paper."

"No," she said. "You haven't. Your father's death nearly ate you alive and you never even opened your mouth about it. This time you'll have to open it. In front of tribunals, in front of newspapers, in front of men who will call you a liar and a traitor and worse. Merrick couldn't do it. Harkness couldn't do it alone. Quist couldn't do it — she sold her silence for a telegraph office and twenty years of pretending she didn't remember." She heard her own voice go low and steady, the way it did when she read bridge loads. "Somebody has to be the one who carries it all the way out. All the way. Not most of the way. Not until it gets heavy."

Elias was quiet a long moment. Down the canyon, the shrunken river murmured over its stones, patient as a clock.

"When I was a boy," he said finally, "my father used to say that a man's word is the only instrument he owns that can't be recalibrated. I thought it was just something fathers say." He put his hand over hers, over the notebook, over Merrick's unfinished sentence. "I'm done recalibrating. Whatever's at the source, it goes out. Both copies. Every tag. Everything. Even if it costs—" He stopped himself, and she felt him choose not to soften it. "Even if it costs everything. That's the promise. Witness me."

"Witnessed," Oscar said.

* * *

They broke camp before first light and pushed up the trail while the canyon was still blue with shadow, and within the hour the equipment field fell behind them and the character of the country changed. The walls drew closer. The trail began to climb in switchbacks cut into the living rock, and the air grew colder, and somewhere above them they could hear water again — a real sound, a rushing sound, the snowmelt Harkness had promised falling into the gorge from some hanging valley they couldn't see.

It was on the third switchback that Oscar found the cairn.

It was bigger than the survey markers below — a deliberate pile, waist-high, wedged into a niche where the trail widened. And it wasn't survey work. Survey cairns were tidy. This one had been thrown together by desperate hands, and it had been built to be found, because wedged into its top, facing outward, glinting dully in the early light, was a brass tag.

Oscar's heart went once, hard, and then settled into a cold even rhythm. She climbed to it and worked the tag free of the stones with fingers that felt like they belonged to someone else.

It was Harkness's tag. Exactly. Same stamp, same number series, same maker's mark on the reverse — the little anchor-and-bar that meant it came from the same foundry batch, ordered the same year, issued to the same project. She'd known there were others; Harkness had told her the tags came in sets, that the survey parties carried them by the dozen. But knowing and holding were different things. Holding it meant the man who'd carried this one had stood on this exact switchback, in this exact niche, long enough to build a cairn around it in a country where building anything took hours.

"He was here," she said. "Merrick. Or whoever came after him. They marked the route."

"For who?" Elias had come up beside her, breathing hard from the climb. "Nobody was supposed to follow."

"Somebody always follows." She turned the tag over. On the reverse, scratched fine with a knife point, almost worn away, was a single line of characters. She tilted it to the light and read them aloud, and her voice sounded far away to her own ears:

"*VAULT — BELOW THE FALLS — UNDER THE THIRD PILLAR.*"

Below them, faint but unmistakable, carried up through the cold morning air, came a sound that did not belong to the canyon at all.

Voices. More than one. And beneath the voices, rhythmic and patient, the ring of steel on stone — men climbing the switchbacks behind them, gaining, unhurried, certain.

Elias met her eyes. Neither of them said Rennick's name. They didn't need to.

Oscar pocketed the tag, tightened her pack straps, and started up the trail toward the falls, climbing now not away from the proof but straight into its arms — with the sound of pursuit ringing off the canyon walls below like a bell tolling the only hour that mattered: *too late, too late, too late.*
