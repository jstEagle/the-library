## 14. The Turncoat's Offer

The rain came up the valley sideways, the way it always did in the high country, and Elias Vane stood under the eave of the Deadfall depot watching it turn the world to pewter. Three hundred yards of rotting boardwalk. A church with no steeple. A town that had been dying for thirty years and had finally gotten around to it.

Behind him, through the depot's grimy glass, he could see Oscar Fairweather bent over Harkness's map with a lantern pulled close, her hair pinned up carelessly, a pencil behind her ear, her lips moving as she read the dead man's annotations aloud to herself in numbers. She had been at it for two hours. She would be at it until the candle guttered, because that was who she was — a woman who measured things, who trusted measurements, who had built a whole life out of the belief that if you surveyed honestly enough the truth would hold still long enough to be found.

He was going to have to tell her what Rennick had just offered him.

Not yet. First he needed to know exactly how much of himself he was willing to spend, so that when he told her, there would be nothing left to negotiate.

* * *

Rennick had come for him an hour after sundown, alone, which was itself a message. The chief fixer traveled with men the way a general traveled with staff; showing up without them meant the conversation was not going on any record, not even his own.

Elias had been checking the hand-car's axle bearings in the old freight shed when the lamp at the door swung up and Rennick stepped out of the dark like a man stepping off a train that ran on money instead of rails.

"Inspector." Rennick was a compact man, silver at the temples, dressed for the mountains the way a banker dresses for the mountains — expensively and briefly. "You're a hard man to find alone."

"I find being alone improves my judgment."

"It does the opposite for most men." Rennick set his lamp on a nail keg, unhurried, proprietary, as though the shed were his and the town too and possibly the rain. "Mind if I sit? My knees are fifty-four years old and they've got opinions about standing."

"There are crates."

Rennick chose one, dusted it with his glove, sat with his ankles crossed and his hands loose between his knees. A man at ease. A man who had rehearsed.

"You've been up here eleven days," Rennick said. "Eleven days chasing a surveyor who isn't sabotaging anything, on a line that hasn't carried a paying customer since before the war, to investigate a flood your own bureau already closed the file on. I've read your interim report. It's elegant work. Very little said, very persuasively."

"You read my mail."

"I read everything." He smiled, showing good teeth. "That's my job. Your job, officially, is to confirm natural causes and go home. Unofficially — and this is why we're talking — your job has become something else. Something you invented. Something involving Miss Fairweather and a dead prospector's map and a great deal of my employers' anxiety."

Elias kept his hands still. In the lamplight the shed's shadows lay across the stacked crates like felled timber. Somewhere outside, water dripped from the eaves in a slow metronome.

"Say what you came to say, Rennick."

"All right." Rennick leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for a moment the ease dropped away and what was underneath was simply a tired, capable, dangerous man doing arithmetic. "There's a pension fund. Not a government one. Private. It pays two thousand a year for life, indexed, to retired inspectors whose discretion proved valuable during their careers. There are four men on it now. Good men. Men you'd know by name. One of them testified against the Northern Pacific once, and now he fishes in Montana and doesn't testify against anybody."

"And what does discretion cost?"

"In your case?" Rennick spread his hands. "Nothing. That's the beauty of it. You file your final report — natural causes, spring melt, an old dam that failed the way old dams do. You note regretfully that Miss Fairweather's evidence could not be authenticated. You go back to the city. And in the autumn, a letter arrives, and after that, every year, so does the money."

The drip from the eaves counted out the silence. Elias found he was thinking of his father's watch, the one with the cracked crystal that still kept perfect time, sitting in his trunk at the bottom of the supply crate. *A man is what he measures*, his father used to say, winding it. Elias had measured his whole career in files closed cleanly, reports that matched, the small honest machinery of government turning as it was designed to turn. He had told himself for eleven days that he was stalling. That he was gathering evidence. That he would file the true report when he had enough to make it stick.

And some of that had even been true.

But he knew, standing in the dripping shed with the company's chief fixer offering him two thousand a year to forget Bellhaven, that part of him had been waiting for exactly this — waiting to be tempted properly, so that whatever he chose next, he could never again pretend the choice had been made for him.

"There's another version," Rennick said, when the silence had done its work. "I'd be negligent not to mention it. It's less pleasant, so let's get through it quickly. If you don't take the pension — if you keep riding upriver with that woman, carrying that map — then somewhere in the next week a report reaches your commissioner naming Elias Vane as the saboteur on the mountain lines. Cut telegraph wire. Tampered switches. The incident at Cascade Summit last March, where a brake van went into the gorge." He paused. "You were within forty miles of Cascade Summit last March, Inspector. So was I, but I can prove where I was. Can you?"

"I was investigating," Elias said quietly. "On assignment. There will be records."

"There will be records," Rennick agreed pleasantly. "Records can be edited. That's rather the theme of our evening." He stood, brushing off his coat, retrieving his lamp. "I don't want the second version. Genuinely. You're the first inspector in six years who's been worth the trouble of this conversation, and I mean that as a compliment, though I know how it sounds. Take the week. Think about it. Miss Fairweather will run out of river soon anyway — the dry season doesn't care whose side she's on. By the time she's stopped, the question will have answered itself."

He lifted the lamp. Its light swung across the crates, the rotted ties stacked against the wall, Elias's face.

"For what it's worth," Rennick said at the door, "the men who took the pension don't sleep badly. I've asked them. They sleep fine. It turns out conscience is mostly a matter of practice, and most men have practiced looking away their whole lives. They're just doing more of what they're good at." He tipped his hat. "Good night, Inspector. Whatever you decide — decide it alone. That's advice, not a threat."

Then the dark took him, and the rain filled in the space where he'd been, and Elias Vane stood alone in a freight shed full of ghosts, weighing two futures on a scale only he could see.

* * *

He did not sleep. He did not expect to.

Instead he built a fire in the depot's potbellied stove — the depot was empty at this hour, Mara Quist having gone up into the hills to check a relay box she claimed needed checking, though Elias suspected the old woman simply preferred her own company and the hills — and he laid out the pieces of the thing the way Oscar would have laid them out. Measured. Honest. Refusing to round down.

Piece one: the map. Harkness's map, with its impossible depth soundings, its annotations in a hand that had grown shakier over years of adding to it. He had studied it by firelight while Oscar slept, pretending he hadn't, and he had understood it the way she understood it — not as accusation but as measurement. The soundings didn't lie. Rivers rose and fell, but they didn't rise ninety feet above the highest recorded flood without something upstream failing. And nothing upstream failed naturally in a season with no rain. He had known it since Deadfall. Before Deadfall, if he was honest. Since the moment in chapter and verse of his father's old files he had recognized the shape of the thing: a disaster with paperwork.

Piece two: the memos he hadn't seen yet but knew existed. Companies like Rennick's employers did not survive on luck. Somewhere in a vault or a lawyer's safe there was a correspondence trail, because there was always a correspondence trail; men who bought silence wrote receipts. Harkness had found it. Harkness had died holding a brass tag from a town that officially never flooded, and there would be a second tag, and a third, because that was how cover-ups worked — they left tags, they left bones, they left portages littered with equipment from crews that had been sworn to secrecy and then sworn at differently.

Piece three: himself. His career. Twenty-two years, unblemished, and worth precisely as much as Rennick's pension fund thought it was worth — which was two thousand a year, indexed, forever, payable in exchange for the one thing he had left that couldn't be bought twice.

He put his hands out toward the stove and watched the firelight move on his knuckles.

His father had drowned in a rockslide on the Kettle River line when Elias was nineteen. Official cause: natural. Spring thaw, unstable cut, tragic. He had believed it for twenty years because believing it was easier than asking why a road crew had been sent to blast that same cut three weeks earlier, and why the blasting permits bore signatures that didn't match the handwriting of any engineer who'd ever worked the Kettle. He had been nineteen and grieving and the government had handed him a closed file and he had taken it the way you take a coat someone offers in cold weather — gratefully, without examining it.

Oscar had lost her whole future in one afternoon on a bridge. Elias had lost only a father, and had spent twenty years not looking at how.

*We are what we measure.* Well. Here was a measurement.

The stove ticked. Rain walked across the depot roof. And Elias Vane, who had lied well in reports and badly in person all his life, admitted the plain thing at last: he had requested this assignment. Chapter seven of his own private ledger — the request memo, drafted in his own hand, citing the sabotage investigation as pretext. He had known what the High Passage held before he ever boarded the 4:10. He had known, and he had gone anyway, and he had let Oscar believe he was a man circling her with a warrant when in fact he had been circling her with the same wound.

She deserved better than a man who decided things alone in freight sheds. But she also deserved a man who had decided.

* * *

Morning came up gray and clean, the rain finished, the valley washed. Oscar was on the platform when he found her, folding the map with the particular reverence she gave documents — creasing along existing folds, aligning edges — and when she looked up at him, her eyes had that quality he was learning to read the way he read gauges: the needle steady, but the mechanism working hard underneath.

"You didn't sleep," she said.

"You did?"

"An hour. Maybe two." She tucked the map into its oilcloth sleeve. "Quist says there's smoke on the east ridge. Signal fire or a camp. She's deciding which." Her gaze sharpened. "Elias. What happened last night? Quist saw a light in the freight shed past midnight and a man leaving before dawn, and she knows every man in this valley by his walk. It wasn't one of ours."

So much for deciding it alone. He almost smiled. Rennick's advice, undone by a telegraph operator with sharp eyes and no loyalty to anyone's privacy.

"He came to offer me a pension," Elias said. "Two thousand a year for life. All I have to do is write the report they wrote for me before I ever arrived — natural causes, case closed, and a paragraph regretting that your evidence couldn't be authenticated." He held her stare. "And if I decline, they'll name me the saboteur. Cascade Summit. The cut wires. All of it. They can make it stick, Oscar. They've done it before. There may be men fishing in Montana right now on the strength of exactly this conversation."

The wind moved along the platform, stirring grit against the boards. Down the track, the hand-car sat gleaming wet, pointed upriver, absurd and faithful.

"What did you tell him?" she asked.

"I told him I'd think about it. Alone." He took a breath. "Which was a lie, because I'd already decided, and I wanted the night to try to talk myself out of it, and I couldn't. So here it is, out loud, where you can hear it and hold me to it: I'm filing the true report. Not the safe one. When we reach the source, when we have Harkness's proof in hand, I'm sending it through every channel I can find, and when they come for me — and they will come for me — I'll stand there and let them."

Oscar was quiet for a long moment. He watched her do what she always did: measure. Weigh the statement against the man, test the load-bearing members.

"You understand what it costs," she said. Not a question — a sounding. Depth finding.

"Twenty-two years. My commission. Probably my freedom, for a while." He shrugged, and found the shrug was almost true. "My father spent his life keeping trains honest on a line nobody thanked him for. I spent mine closing files. I'd like to open one, before the end. Just one."

Something moved through her face then that he had no instrument to read — grief and relief and a fierce kind of hope, all at once, like weather changing over a ridge.

"Then we'd better get upriver," she said. "The dry season won't wait for your conscience to arrive."

"No," Elias agreed. "It never does."

They turned toward the hand-car together — and stopped.

At the far end of the platform, where the boardwalk gave onto the east trail, a figure stood in the new morning light: Mara Quist, come down from the hills faster than an old woman should climb, her shawl still damp, her face the color of the ash in a cold stove. She was carrying something in both hands, carefully, the way you carry a bird with a broken wing.

In her fist, catching the sun, hung a brass survey tag.

Stamped into it, worn but legible, were two words: *BELLHAVEN — 2.*

"Not mine," Mara said, and her voice was steady the way a wire is steady under load. "Found it this morning, half a mile up the east trail, hung on a branch at eye level. Somebody wanted us to know they've been up there." She looked at Elias, then at Oscar, and her jaw set like a woman closing a switch on a line everyone said was dead. "And Inspector — whoever hung it also cut the telegraph wire north while I was in the hills. Every direction except east."

East. Toward the company's men. Toward Rennick's waiting report.

"They want us running," Oscar said quietly.

"Then let's not disappoint them," said Elias, and reached for the hand-car's handle.
