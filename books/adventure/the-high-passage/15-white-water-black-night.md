## 15. White Water, Black Night

The river told them first.

Oscar heard it before she saw it — a change in the water's voice, from the steady mutter of a working river to something lower, harder, like stone grinding under a door. She stood at the lip of the gorge with the last light gone and the moon not yet risen, and she listened the way she had once listened to rails: for the note that meant something was wrong.

"The channel's dropped," Elias said behind her. He had his coat off already, sleeves rolled, the map case strapped across his chest where she'd made him wear it. "Listen to the banks. You can hear it pulling back."

She could. The waterline on the granite walls stood a hand's width above the current, wet stone gleaming in the starlight like a tide mark left by a sea that was leaving. Nine days, Mara's arithmetic had given them. The river had decided to be less generous than an old woman's guess.

"Then we go tonight," Oscar said. "By moon."

Elias came up beside her and looked down into the gorge, where the water ran black between walls that would shear a canoe in half without slowing. "You've run this stretch?"

"I've run worse." That was true, though the worse had been daylight, and spring water, and a version of herself three years younger who hadn't yet learned what the river could take away. "But not at night, and not this low. Low water is meaner. Everything shows its teeth."

He didn't argue. That was one of the things about him she'd stopped being surprised by and started depending on: he weighed a thing once, quickly, and then he stood by the weight of it. He began lashing their packs into a single bundle, waterproofed in oilcloth, tied to the stern thwart. The map case went inside his coat again, against his skin, because Oscar had argued herself hoarse about it and lost. *If we separate,* he'd said, *the evidence stays with me. If I drown, it drowns with me, and Rennick never gets it.* It was a terrible argument and a correct one, and she hated him a little every time she thought of it.

They carried the canoe down the goat track to the shingle at the gorge mouth, and the moon came up while they worked — not the fat yellow moon of storybooks but a hard white coin, high and thin, throwing shadows sharp enough to cut. By its light the river looked like poured iron.

Oscar sat in the bow. Elias took the stern, which was backwards from every convention of canoeing and exactly right for them; she read water better, he read her better, and somewhere over the past hundred miles they had stopped pretending otherwise.

"Talk to me," he said, pushing off. "All the way down. If you stop talking, I'll know something's wrong."

"I'll be too busy screaming to talk."

"That counts. Screaming carries."

The current took them.

* * *

For the first mile it was almost kind. The gorge ran straight, the channel deep in its low-water bed, and the moon laid a silver road down the center of the current that Oscar steered by the way she'd once steered by lamplight along a dead spur — reading the shine, trusting the dark on either side to stay where it belonged.

"V-vee ahead," she called back, using the old survey shorthand, pitch and bearing compressed into letters. "Easy water. Left of the island rock."

"Left of the island rock." His paddle matched hers, stroke for stroke, and the canoe slid through the vee like a needle through cloth.

It was in the second mile that the river stopped being a road and became a staircase. She heard it before the drop — a long, hollow boom echoing up the canyon walls, the sound of a great deal of water falling a short way and doing it angrily. She backed water, swung the bow toward a granite shoulder, and read the drop by moonlight as best she could: two steps, maybe three, a chute on the right side running clean, a fang of rock dead center with the foam combing off it like white hair.

"Right chute," she said. "Hard. Keep us square or we broach."

"Square," Elias repeated, and she could hear him smiling, and she loved him for the smile, and there was no time to tell him so.

They went down. The first step hit like a train taking a switch too fast — the bow buried, cold water over her wrists, the world reduced to noise and silver spray and the feel of the hull answering her hands. The second step threw them sideways and Elias caught it, a single powerful draw that swung the stern around, and then the chute had them and they were shooting through a slot barely wider than the canoe, walls flashing past close enough to touch, spray sheeting up through the moonlight like thrown glass.

And then they were through, floating in a pool below, both of them breathing like bellows, and Elias laughed — actually laughed, a short helpless bark of a sound — and Oscar found she was laughing too, giddy and appalled, alive all the way down to the marrow.

"You're insane," he said.

"You're following me."

"Somebody has to keep the evidence dry."

The third mile was where the river lied.

She heard it as a wrongness in the echo — the boom of the drops coming back off the walls slightly doubled, smeared, as if the canyon were repeating itself. She knew what that meant even before her eyes confirmed it: a bend below, tight enough that the sound was folding back on itself, and no way to see what waited around it until they were committed.

"Elias. There's a turn I can't read."

"How bad?"

"I don't know. That's the problem."

They eddied out against the left wall, holding position in the slack water behind a boulder the size of a chapel, and Oscar stood as high as she dared in the rocking hull and looked downstream. The bend was fifty yards on. Beyond it the moonlight showed nothing but a wall of spray hanging in the air like a curtain, lit from within, glowing faintly silver — and under the spray, half-heard beneath the general roar, a sound she knew from nightmares: the flat, continuous thunder of water going over something wide and shallow and unforgiving.

A ledge. A long one. The kind of drop that didn't kill you with violence but with repetition — a quarter mile of broken water and exposed rock, the river spread thin over its own bones, exactly what low water made worst.

"We can't run that blind," Elias said. He'd read her silence correctly, as always. "And we can't portage it tonight. Not on these walls."

"No," Oscar agreed. Her mouth had gone dry. "We line it. Down the left side, ropes on the bow and stern, walk her through the shallow channel as far as it goes. Then we run whatever's left."

"And if what's left is worse?"

"Then we'll have had a good evening up to that point."

He hauled the canoe against the current toward the bank, and that was when the river took its lie back.

The eddy behind the boulder was not an eddy. It was a seam — a tongue of main current curling back on itself, invisible in the dark, and as the bow crossed it the whole river grabbed the boat like a hand closing. The canoe slewed. Elias dug for the far side. Oscar threw her weight to counter, and for one suspended instant the hull balanced on the seam's knife edge, moonlight on the water, spray hanging, everything perfectly still —

— and then they were over.

The cold was total. It closed on her like a fist, drove the air out of her chest, turned the world to black roaring chaos. She surfaced somehow, gasping, and the first thing she saw was the map case — torn loose from Elias's coat in the capsize, oilcloth streaming, riding the current ahead of them toward the ledge and the quarter mile of rock teeth beyond.

The second thing she saw was Elias, twenty feet downstream, going under.

There was no decision in what she did next. Three years of measuring her life in miles, in safe increments, in distances that couldn't hurt her — all of it ended at the moment her hand closed on the map case strap as it swept past. She should have let it go. Every rational part of her mind, the surveyor's part, the part that calculated risk in decimals, screamed at her to let it go: it was a case, it was paper and photographs, it was not worth a life.

She held on anyway.

The river took them both. The case dragged her like an anchor, and the current dragged the case, and together they went down the throat of the ledge run in a tumbling silver-and-black confusion of water. She took a rock across the shoulder — felt the crack of it distantly, through layers of cold, as if it had happened to someone else. She rolled twice, three times, lost all sense of up, breathed water, coughed it, breathed more. Somewhere in the whitewater arithmetic of it she understood, with a clarity that had nothing frantic in it at all, that she was drowning. Not might drown. Was drowning. The river was closing its account with her the way it had closed it with the other thing she'd loved, three years ago, on a night much like this one, and the fairness or unfairness of it was beside the point entirely. Water did not negotiate. She had known that since she was a child. She had spent three years pretending otherwise, measuring miles instead of years, and now the miles had run out and here was the river, patient as arithmetic, collecting.

Her lungs burned. Her grip on the case was failing finger by finger. And in the last clear second before the black came up to meet her, she thought — not of Harkness, not of Bellhaven, not of justice or evidence or the promise she'd made a dying man — but of Elias saying *screaming carries*, and she thought: *I never told him.*

That was the thought she drowned on. That was the debt the river collected.

* * *

Hands. Hard hands, on her collar, hauling.

She came up out of the black into moonlight and agony — air like knives in her chest, coughing so hard she felt something tear, Elias's voice cutting through it all, rough and terrified and absolutely steady, the way a man's voice goes when terror has been given a job to do.

"Breathe. Oscar. Breathe, damn you — there you are. Again. Breathe again."

She was on the shingle below the ledge, on her side, and he had her by the shoulders and was beating the water out of her with the heel of his hand between her shoulder blades, methodical, brutal, exactly right. She vomited river water and kept coughing, and he kept her folded forward and kept talking, a low stream of words that she only later realized were survey shorthand — *bearing mark, hold, hold, coming about* — the language of her trade repurposed into comfort, because it was the only language he trusted himself not to break in.

When she could finally breathe in more than sips, she became aware of several things in order. First: the map case, lying in the gravel six inches from her hand, strap snapped, oilcloth split, but sealed. Second: her shoulder, which announced itself properly now that the drowning was postponed, and which was either badly bruised or cracked, and which she elected not to investigate. Third: Elias's hands, shaking. Not his voice. Only his hands.

"You held onto it," he said. His voice did break then, just at the edge, one hairline fracture. "You stupid, magnificent — you held onto the case instead of swimming."

"It was going to get away."

"It nearly took you with it. I watched you go under twice. Twice, Oscar." He turned her gently to face him, and in the moonlight his face was stripped down to something she hadn't seen on him before — not the inspector, not the man circling her with careful words, but simply Elias, thirty-one years old and frightened. "I thought—" He stopped. Started again. "I pulled four bodies out of water when I was a boy, after the Willow Creek washout. My father was the fifth. I know what they look like when it's too late. You looked like that."

Oscar reached up and put her hand against his jaw. Her arm shook. She let it shake.

"I'm here," she said. It came out as a rasp. She said it again, louder, because it deserved volume. "I'm here, Elias. I'm not your father. I'm not anyone's ghost. I'm here."

He bowed his head until his forehead touched hers, and they stayed like that for a long time, two people dripping on a gravel bar in the bottom of a canyon, the river running loud and indifferent past them, the moon climbing higher and whiter over the gorge wall. Somewhere upstream the ledge thundered on, having failed to keep them.

"The case," she managed finally. "Check the case."

He checked it with the care of a man defusing something. The tin box inside the oilcloth had taken a dent; the seals held; when he opened it, the photographs and the memos and Harkness's letter lay inside, damp at the edges, legible, intact. Harkness's brass tag had knocked loose and lay in the corner like a coin at the bottom of a well.

"All of it," Elias said. "Every page."

"Then the river can bill me for the rest."

He laughed — a wrecked, relieved sound — and then he did the thing she had thought about in the water, the thing she'd feared she'd die without doing or hearing. He took her face in his unsteady hands and kissed her, there on the gravel, tasting of river and fear and something underneath both that had been building for nine days and a hundred miles and perhaps, if she was honest, since a supply train platform and a man who wouldn't lie well.

When it broke, neither of them moved away.

"Mortality reviewed," she rasped. "Love undeniable. We're very on schedule."

"Shut up and let me look at your shoulder."

* * *

They made camp above the flood line on a shelf of rock, and Elias built a fire small enough to hide and large enough to matter, and Oscar sat wrapped in the oilcloth with her shoulder bound and her clothes steaming, and watched him move around the fire with the economy of a man who had finally stopped rationing himself.

Below them, the river ran low and getting lower. Above them, the stars came out in the cold hard numbers she had always preferred to people.

"Nine days was generous," Elias said, feeding the fire. "Call it five. Maybe four. After that, the source canyon's a walk-in and a carry-out, and Rennick will have men on every trail."

"Four days." She turned Harkness's tag over in her fingers, moonlight catching the stamped letters: *BELLHAVEN SURVEY — 14*. Fourteen. Somewhere in a canyon above them, someone else had carried a tag with a different number and died holding it. "Then tomorrow we climb."

He looked at her across the fire — this man who had boarded her train to bury the truth and was now burning his career log by log to dig it up — and whatever he saw in her face made him nod slowly, once, the way he did when he weighed a thing and stood by the weight of it.

"Tomorrow we climb," he said. Then, quieter, almost to himself, looking up at the black walls of the gorge: "God help whoever's waiting for us up there."

Oscar followed his gaze upward, to where the canyon bent north toward the river's forbidden source, and felt — beneath the ache of her shoulder, beneath the exhaustion, beneath the terrifying new lightness of a life measured in years again — the cold certainty that whoever was waiting had been waiting a long time.

And that come morning, they would no longer be alone.
