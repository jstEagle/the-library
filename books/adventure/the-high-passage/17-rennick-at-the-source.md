## 17. Rennick at the Source

The sound came up the canyon before the machine did — a flat, rhythmic hammering against the stone walls, doubling and redoubling until it seemed to come from everywhere at once.

Oscar froze with the vault lid half-lifted, her hands full of paper that had waited eleven years in the dark. Elias was already moving, scrambling up the shelf of rock to the canyon rim, and when she followed him her breath went out of her in a single sharp exhale.

It came around the shoulder of the mountain like a dragonfly made of iron — a helicopter, olive-drab, company markings painted over but not well enough to hide them from a woman who had spent three years reading other people's stencils on dead rolling stock. It hung above the river's source for a long moment, rotor wash flattening the water into white rings, then settled onto the gravel bar downstream with the delicacy of something that knew exactly how much it weighed.

"Four men," Elias said. He had field glasses up before Oscar had finished climbing. "Five. The big one in front is Rennick."

"You know him."

"I've had lunch with him." Elias lowered the glasses. His face had gone very still, the way it went still when he was doing arithmetic he didn't like. "Oscar. Listen to me. Whatever happens down there, they cannot see you. If they take you, they take everything you're carrying, and if they take what you're carrying, Harkness died for a map and Bellhaven stays a footnote. Do you understand me?"

"I understand you're about to stand between five armed men and the thing they came eleven hundred miles to bury."

"That's the general shape of it, yes." He turned to look at her then, and there was no fear in his face, which frightened her more than the helicopter did. There was only that careful inspector's calm, the one she'd first seen on the 4:10, and underneath it something warmer that he'd stopped pretending wasn't there somewhere around the portage of bones. "Three places," he said. "You told me three places. Tell me again that you can find them blind."

"The cairn above the falls. The hollow spruce at Deadfall, behind the third telegraph pole — Quist's pole, she knows it. And the dry culvert on the old Bellhaven spur, under the east abutment." She recited it the way she recited grades and clearances, because that was how she kept her hands steady: by treating terror as survey data. "I built none of them this morning, but I could find every one of them drunk, in the dark, in winter."

"Good." He took off his coat, folded it once, and set it on the rock. Underneath he wore his commission jacket, brass buttons bright in the high sun, government insignia at the collar — a costume he'd been carrying folded in his pack since Deadfall like a man carrying a knife he hoped not to use. "Then all you have to do is be a ghost for twenty minutes while I remind those gentlemen that this canyon is a federal investigation site and that shooting an inspector is a different order of crime than shooting a trespasser."

"Elias—"

"Twenty minutes." He touched her jaw once, briefly, as if sealing a document. "I've stalled better men than Rennick with less paperwork. Go."

She went.

* * *

ELIAS

Rennick crossed the gravel bar without hurrying, which was its own kind of message. A man who hurried could be read; a man who strolled toward you with four shooters fanned out behind him wanted you to understand that time belonged to him and always had.

He was broader than Elias remembered from the corridor outside the commissioner's office, heavier through the shoulders, with the weathered, competent face of a man who had started on crews and ended owning them. He stopped six feet away and looked Elias up and down with the frank appraisal of a buyer at a stock auction.

"Inspector Vane." He said the name like he was checking a manifest. "You're a long way from your jurisdiction."

"This canyon is inside my jurisdiction as of nine days ago, when I requested it." Elias kept his hands loose at his sides and his voice at the level he used for men arguing over demurrage charges. "Federal survey site, pending inquiry. Sealed by order of the Commission."

"Sealed by your own order." Rennick smiled. It was a good smile, warm at the edges, and it reached nothing above his cheekbones. "That's convenient. You filed an interim report last week saying natural causes. Now you're sealing the source. Which is it, Inspector? Are you finishing your investigation or closing mine?"

"A report is interim by definition. The inquiry isn't concluded until the evidence is concluded." Elias let his gaze drift past Rennick's shoulder to the helicopter, to the men standing by it with the loose readiness of professionals. "And whose evidence is yours, Mr. Rennick? Because from where I stand, you flew five hours over sealed wilderness to reach a site the Commission closed, carrying weapons into a federal investigation, and I'd very much like to hear your explanation before I write it down."

Something flickered behind the smile. Good. Doubt was a lever, and Elias intended to work it until his arms gave out.

"My explanation," Rennick said, "is that a thief has been looting these mountains for two months. A woman. Fairweather, her name is — you may have met her, I hear you shared a train. She stole proprietary documents belonging to my employer, and my employer has retained me to recover them. That's not a crime, Inspector. That's a recovery operation."

"It's a crime if the documents are government property. And if the documents are your company's own surveys of the Bellhaven dam—" Elias watched Rennick's eyes, and saw the fraction of a second where the man stopped breathing, "—then they're also evidence in a mass-casualty investigation, and everyone who touches them is an accessory. So which are they, Mr. Rennick? Proprietary papers worth a recovery team? Or proof your engineers signed off on a spillway half the required capacity?"

The gravel bar was very quiet. One of the men by the helicopter shifted his weight, and leather creaked.

"You've got a smooth mouth," Rennick said at last. "I offered you a way out through channels. You didn't take it. So here's the version that doesn't go through channels." He stepped closer, and his voice dropped to something almost gentle. "My men are going to walk this canyon. Every ledge, every cave, every pile of rock. If we find the woman, we recover the property, and you can write whatever report keeps your pension intact. If we find you interfering—" he spread his hands, apologetic, "—accidents happen in country like this. Ask anyone. This valley has a history of them."

Elias held his ground and thought: *twenty minutes*. Fourteen left, maybe less. He thought about his father coming home from a smelter town in a box with a coroner's note reading *misadventure*, and about how many boxes Rennick had shipped.

"Search it, then," he said. "With my compliments. But my notes go out with the evening wire tonight regardless, and my report names each of you by description already. Kill me and the descriptions become the whole story. Let me live and you might still talk your way out of the inquiry." He paused. "Your move, Mr. Rennick. I'm told you're a man who appreciates odds."

Rennick studied him for a long moment, and Elias understood that the man was doing arithmetic too — and that Elias's arithmetic only had to hold a little longer.

"Sweep the canyon," Rennick said quietly, not taking his eyes off Elias. "Gentle-like."

* * *

OSCAR

Above the falls, the world narrowed to stone and breath.

Oscar moved the way she'd learned to move along dead lines — weight low, boots finding the quiet seams in the rock, every step chosen twice. Below her, voices carried up off the water, distorted by the canyon walls: Rennick's men spreading out along both banks, kicking through scree, their confidence loud enough to track by.

She had fifteen minutes' head start on being found and nowhere near enough altitude. The vault was behind her now, its seal broken, its contents divided into three oilcloth packets wrapped tight as bandages. The original map stayed against her body, in the waterproof case she'd carried through white water and nearly drowned for. Everything else was copies — Harkness's letter, the memos in the company's own hand, the core-sample logs with the numbers that proved the dam had been built to fail and the signatures proving they'd known. Three packets. Three hiding places. Only one of her.

The cairn first. It sat above the falls on the north rim, a stack of survey stones some dead lineman had raised decades ago, and she rebuilt it around the first packet with a mason's patience, resetting each stone in its original bed so the profile wouldn't change. Her hands were steady. That surprised her. Somewhere below, men with rifles were combing the rocks for her, and her hands were steady as a level bubble because for three years she had trained herself to turn feeling into measurement, and now, finally, the skill was saving her life instead of hollowing it out.

A stone clattered down the south bank, close. She flattened into shadow between two boulders and counted her breathing the way she counted rail joints. Two men worked the shoreline below, sweeping their eyes across the cliffs without looking up — looking for a camp, a canoe, tracks in the mud. Nobody looked up. Nobody ever looked up. It was the one mercy of a landscape that punished anyone who did.

She climbed.

The traverse above the falls was the worst forty yards of her life — a shelf of wet granite no wider than her boot, the river roaring white thirty feet below, her pack straps snagging every projection like the mountain itself trying to keep her. Halfway across, a voice rose directly beneath her, close enough that she could hear the man breathing.

"Nothing on the bar. Camp's cold — cold for days. She's moved on."

"She hasn't moved on." That was Rennick, farther off, carrying perfectly in the thin air. "Vane's stalling. That means she's here. Sweep wider."

*Stalling.* So Elias was still talking. She put the number of his remaining minutes out of her mind where she couldn't count them and kept moving.

The spruce at the second cache was four hours' hard travel downriver — too far. She'd known that when she chose the sites; that was the point of them, scattered wide enough that no single capture could end it. But the third place was closer than the spruce and better than the cairn, and she'd been keeping it in reserve the way she kept a spare fuse in her kit: the old Bellhaven spur began just upstream of the source, a dead branch line running up out of the canyon toward a mine that had died before the flood. There was a culvert under the east abutment, dry as bone, and she'd slept beside it two nights ago without yet knowing why she'd noted it.

She reached the spur line as the light began to go copper. Rotted ties, weeds waist-high in the ballast, and the abutment ahead — a mass of mortared stone with the culvert's dark mouth at its base, half-choked with slide rock. She knelt, wedged the second packet deep into the throat of the pipe behind a slab of stone, and stacked the slide rock back over it in the same jumbled pattern the mountain had chosen. Then she sat back on her heels, and for one breath allowed herself to feel it: two caches set, the truth of Bellhaven now living in three places at once, unkillable unless someone killed the whole mountain.

Which, she reflected, rising, was more or less what the company had done to the town.

Below her, back at the source, a rifle shot cracked off the canyon walls.

Then another.

* * *

ELIAS

Elias heard the first shot and understood, with the clarity of a man watching a bridge start to fail, that his arithmetic was done.

Rennick heard it too. Both of them looked toward the sound — upriver, north rim, where a figure in canvas was sprinting the skyline above the falls with a rifleman angling to cut him off. Too far away to be Oscar. One of hers? No — she had no one. Then who—

The second shot answered the question differently: it came from the south ridge, and it wasn't aimed at the runner. It hit the gravel ten feet from Rennick's men, throwing up a fan of grit, and a voice rolled down off the heights — an old woman's voice, cracked and carrying, amplified by the canyon into something biblical.

*"That's warning! Next ones ain't!"*

Mara Quist stood on the south ridge like a monument, telegraph key slung across her back on a strap, a deer rifle in her hands that looked older than she was. And behind her, rising out of the trees along the ridgeline, came others — a dozen, two dozen, men in wool and leather with hunting rifles and a couple of ancient shotguns, mountain people, river people, faces Elias recognized from Deadfall's empty street. They spread out along the rim with the unhurried competence of people who had been shooting at things in these hills since childhood, and every rifle on that ridge found a target on the gravel bar below.

Rennick's men stopped moving. All five of them, simultaneously, the way animals stop.

"How," Rennick said, very softly, "did a bunch of hill farmers know to be here?"

Elias felt the answer arrive in his chest before his mind caught it, and it arrived as warmth. *"Quist's pole, behind the third telegraph pole."* He'd thought it was just a hiding place. He hadn't understood it was also a signal line — that Mara had strung a wire out of Deadfall years ago, or never taken one down, and that Oscar's cache instructions were a code the old operator could read like weather.

"They knew," Elias said, "because somebody tapped it out. That's generally how information moves in this country, Mr. Rennick. You should try it sometime. It's remarkable what people do with the truth — they act on it."

Rennick's hand drifted toward his hip, and twenty rifles on the ridge shifted by a degree, a whisper of oiled steel, and the hand stopped.

"You're bluffing," Rennick said, but his voice had lost the gravel bar.

"Count them," Elias said.

Rennick counted. Elias watched him do it, watched the arithmetic run behind the weathered face — five guns on the sand against a ridgeline full of them, a canyon with one exit and his own helicopter sitting in it like a fat, slow beetle, and an inspector standing six feet away who would testify to every second of what happened next.

"We're not finished," Rennick said.

"No," Elias agreed. "We're just getting to the part with witnesses."

For a moment Elias genuinely believed Rennick might shoot him anyway — spend himself out of spite, die badly, take the inspector with him. He watched the man weigh it. Then one of Rennick's men said, low, "Boss — fuel's short as it is," and the moment passed, the way such moments do, collapsing not with a bang but with a man turning his back because turning his back was cheaper than dying.

They boarded. The helicopter lifted in a storm of dust and river spray, swung once over the canyon — Rennick's face visible at the window, memorizing everything — and beat away south over the mountains, growing small, growing silent, gone.

The echo faded. The river ran on. On the ridge, the mountain people began to laugh, easy and rough, and Mara Quist started down the scree toward the gravel bar with her rifle over her shoulder like a woman walking to church.

Elias stood where he was and let his knees inform him, privately, that they had been shaking for some time.

* * *

OSCAR

She found them all together at the source when she came down off the spur — Elias sitting on a boulder looking like a man who had aged a year in an afternoon, Mara Quist planting herself before him with her fists on her hips, and the ridge men drifting down in twos and threes to fill the gravel bar, curious and armed and grinning.

"You," Mara was saying, poking a finger at Elias's commission jacket, "are either the bravest fool or the foolingest brave man I've seen in sixty years, and I wired dispatches through two wars."

"Both, most likely," Elias said, and saw Oscar, and stood up so fast he nearly went over.

Their eyes met across the gravel and neither of them spoke for a moment, because there was a whole language in the silence — *you lived, you stalled them, you climbed that ridge alone, I heard the shots* — and neither of them trusted their voices with it yet.

"Two caches set," Oscar said at last, because reports were easier than feelings. "The cairn above the falls and the Bellhaven culvert. Third goes out tomorrow, downriver. After that it doesn't matter what they do to us. The truth exists in triplicate."

"Triplicate," Mara snorted. "Girl, it exists in quadruplicate. While you two were playing cat and mouse with that flying machine, I was tapping." She patted the telegraph key slung on her back almost fondly. "Every station operator between here and the coast who owes me a favor — and honey, I have been collecting favors for forty years — got a summary this morning. Names, dates, the dam numbers. Not the full proof, mind. Just enough that when your evidence surfaces, nobody in authority can say they never heard a whisper. The story's already loose in the world. Can't stuff it back in the vault now."

Elias stared at her. "That's — Madam, that's obstruction, dissemination of unverified claims, possibly sedition—"

"That's a telegram," said Mara Quist.

One of the mountain men — a gray-bearded giant with a boy's grin — laughed so hard he had to sit down on the gravel.

But Oscar wasn't laughing. She was looking south, down the canyon, where the sky had gone the color of hammered tin. The wind had come around, and it smelled wrong — dust and heat and the dry-season ache of a river shrinking toward its bones.

"Mara," she said slowly. "How long did it take you to get here from Deadfall?"

"Six days, coming fast, and my knees will tell you about it till I die."

"Then Rennick won't be gone six days. He'll fly to the division office, get real warrants, real men, maybe fifty of them, and be back inside three." Oscar turned to Elias, and the steadiness in her voice now cost her more than the fear had. "And the river drops another foot by tomorrow night. The lower gorge will be unnavigable in two days, maybe less."

"So we float the evidence out now," Elias said.

"In what?" She gestured at the gravel, the canyon, the single battered canoe hidden upstream that had barely held two of them through the last rapids. "There's no boat here that survives the gorge. There's no road. There's—" She stopped. Her gaze had gone, without her willing it, to the rusted rails of the old Bellhaven spur, climbing away up out of the canyon into the gathering dusk. Rotted ties. Weeds in the ballast. A dead line she knew tie by tie, grade by grade, culvert by culvert, because she had walked it for three years measuring her grief against its mileage.

Elias followed her eyes. So did Mara. Understanding moved through the group like current through a wire.

"There's a hand-car," the gray-bearded giant said. "Or what's left of one. Section house at mile twelve still stands. My boys could get it rolling."

Oscar looked at the rails going up into the dark, and felt — for the first time since a stranger had stumbled bleeding into her camp on a closed spur, a lifetime ago — not dread.

Purpose.

"We roll at first light," she said.
