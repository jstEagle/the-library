## 18. The Long Way Down

The river gave them three more miles and then took back its promise.

Oscar stood on the gravel bar where the water had shrunk to a brown ribbon between stones the size of sleeping cattle, and she watched the last of their passage die in front of her. The current that had carried them through the gorge at midnight — that had nearly taken her under, that Elias had hauled her from with both fists locked in her collar — was gone now, pooled and slack, barely deep enough to float a canoe, let alone a canoe loaded with a survey vault's worth of proof.

"Nine days," she said. "I measured it wrong."

"You measured it right." Elias crouched at the water's edge, turned a stone over with one finger. His hands were still raw from the rope burn, wrapped now in strips of her spare shirt. "The gorge dropped faster than any table I've seen. That's not season, Oscar. That's somebody upstream with a gate."

She hadn't thought of that, and the thought landed like a stone in her chest. Of course. The company owned the headwaters. They could not follow her into the source canyon by trail, so they had done what rail men do — they had moved water instead of men. Every hour they'd spent drying out their gear and repacking the evidence cases, Rennick's crew had been closing the distance behind them with a lever and a pen.

"They're not chasing us anymore," she said slowly. "They're herding us."

Elias looked up at her. Two days of white water had stripped him down to something leaner than the inspector who'd boarded her supply train — jaw stubbled, coat gone, shirtsleeves rolled and stained to the elbow. But his eyes were the same: patient, watchful, holding back more than he said.

"So we don't go where they're pushing us," he said.

* * *

The High Passage rail line crossed the river four times between the source canyon and Deadfall Junction. Oscar knew this the way she knew her own pulse, because she had walked every mile of it three years ago with a level rod and a notebook and a heart full of nothing, measuring track that would never carry a train again. It had been decommissioned the same autumn as the flood — officially for cost, unofficially because nobody wanted a public line running through a valley where a town used to be. The company had pulled the rails' good sections for scrap, left the rest to rust, and let the mountains begin taking it back.

She led Elias up the bank through lodgepole pine to where the grade cut across the slope, and there it was: the old right-of-way, a shelf of gravel and blackened ties threading north along the canyon wall like a scar that hadn't decided whether to heal.

"I surveyed this," she said. Her voice came out strange. "After. Three years ago. Forty-one miles of dead track, and I logged every rotted tie and washed-out culvert in a ledger nobody ever read. I told myself it was work." She touched the nearest tie. It crumbled at the edge under her thumb, soft as old bread. "It wasn't work. It was me walking away from Bellhaven one tie at a time, counting miles because I couldn't count anything else."

Elias stood beside her and didn't say anything, which was the thing about him she had come to trust most. He waited.

"There's a section house at Mile 29," she went on. "And a maintenance shed at Mile 17 with a pump trolley — if it's still there, if the wheels aren't seized. The grade is two percent or better the whole way down to Deadfall. A hand car can make thirty miles in a day if the ties hold."

"And if the ties don't?"

"Then we walk, and Rennick's men ride our dust all the way to the junction." She turned to face him. "But they'll be watching the river trails. Nobody watches a dead line. Nobody has for three years except me."

Something moved behind his eyes — not quite a smile. "You memorized your own grief well enough to run it as an escape route."

"I measured everything," she said. "That was the whole point."

* * *

The shed at Mile 17 stood with its door half off its hinges, and inside, under a drift of pine needles and porcupine leavings, the pump trolley waited like a promise kept by no one in particular. Its wooden deck had grayed, its handle bars were furred with moss, but when Oscar threw her weight against the lever the wheels turned with a groan and then a grind and then, blessedly, a rhythm — iron on iron, the first sound those rails had made in years.

They loaded the evidence into the waterproof case lashed center-deck, along with the last of their food, the map, Harkness's brass tag, and the second tag from the portage canyon. Elias insisted on carrying the case himself, strapped across his chest like something born rather than packed.

"You realize what this is," he said as they pushed off down the grade, the trolley gathering speed, wind rising in their faces. "This line runs straight through the valley. Through where Bellhaven stood."

"I know."

"The hearing's at Deadfall. Mara's wire reaches every federal office on the continent. But if we come down this line—" He glanced back at her, and she saw he understood before he finished saying it. "Then the evidence arrives the same way the lie did. By rail. Through the town they drowned."

"That's why we take this route," Oscar said. "Not around. Through."

The trolley hummed beneath them. Ties clattered in a rhythm she felt in her teeth, in the old surveyor's arithmetic of her bones. She had counted these miles once in numbness; now each one counted itself back into her like blood returning to a sleeping limb, painful and warm.

* * *

Mile 22 was a trestle across a side creek, eighty feet of creosoted timber standing over a green pool, and the trolley crossed it at a walk while both of them stared down between the ties at black water thirty feet below. Halfway across, one tie cracked under Elias's boot like a pistol shot, and neither of them breathed again until the far abutment took the wheels.

Mile 26 was a rockslide, forty yards of fallen slope spilled across the grade, and they lifted the trolley over it piece by piece — deck, then running gear, then wheels — working in silence broken only by Elias's counting under his breath, and once, when she skinned her palm on a flange, by his hand closing over hers without a word asked or given.

Mile 28 was where she heard the helicopter.

It came up the canyon behind them, a dry insect whine at first, then a chop that echoed off the walls until she couldn't tell direction from noise. They shoved the trolley into the shadow of a cut bank and lay flat in the ballast while it passed overhead — close enough that rotor wash flattened the grass along the grade — and hung for a moment above the river bend ahead, searching the water, searching the trails, finding nothing on the route they were supposed to be taking.

"It's reporting our position as unknown," Elias murmured against the gravel. "Rennick just spent money learning nothing."

"He'll learn something soon." Oscar watched the machine dwindle northward. "He knows the river's dead below us. There are only so many ways down this canyon, and one of them is under our wheels. If I can figure that out, so can he."

"Then we don't stop."

"We don't stop."

They ran the next six miles flat out, taking turns on the pump handles, four minutes on and four off, the canyon walls strobing past, shadow and sun and shadow. Oscar's arms burned. Her graze wound from the rapids had stiffened into a hot wire along her ribs, and every stroke pulled it tight, and she pumped anyway, because the alternative to pain was stopping and the alternative to stopping was Rennick.

At Mile 34 the line entered the long curve above the valley floor, and the trees opened, and she saw Bellhaven.

Or where Bellhaven had been. The reservoir had been drawn down since the failure — another thing the company couldn't hide from anyone who looked — and the drowned valley floor lay exposed in a wide crescent of gray silt, cracked into polygon plates like the skin of some enormous sleeping animal. Here and there the tops of things broke the mud: a chimney, a fence post crowned with dried weed, the ridgepole of a barn. The church steeple stood at the crescent's center, tilted fifteen degrees, its shingles scabbed with silt, pointing at nothing.

The rail line ran directly above it along the bench. Of course it did. She had surveyed this stretch with the water high, knowing only that the lake below her rod was deep and that she did not want to know what it covered.

Now she knew. Now the water had given it back.

Elias brought the trolley to a stop without being asked. The wheels ticked and went quiet.

"My engagement ring is down there somewhere," Oscar said. She heard her own voice as if someone else were speaking. "Thomas's family had a farm on the east bench. We were going to be married in June. The dam went in April, and they told us it was rain, an act of God, a hundred-year flood, and everyone who said otherwise got bought or buried." She looked at the tilted steeple. "Harkness was one of the ones who wouldn't get bought. Took him twenty years to find the proof, and he traded it to a stranger with clean hands because she promised to carry it."

"You kept the promise."

"Not yet." She put her hands back on the pump bar. "Come on. Miles, not years. That's how I count now."

They took the curve fast, and as they swung above the valley floor she did not look away from the steeple. She made herself look. Made herself log it, the way she'd once logged culverts and grades: *Bellhaven, elevation 2,140 feet, population 312, cause of death not rain.* Somewhere below her wheels lay the reason for every mile she'd ever measured, and for the first time the miles were pointed somewhere instead of merely away.

* * *

Rennick caught them at the Mile 38 washout.

She heard it first as a vibration in the rails — a tremor too regular to be water, too fast to be anything but steel — and threw herself off the trolley onto the uphill ballast, dragging the evidence case with her, shouting Elias's name. They rolled into the ditch as the first of the company's motor speeders came around the bend below, a low gasoline railcar with a canvas top and four men aboard, and behind it a second, and riding the second one's deck, hatless in the wind, the broad unhurried shape of Silas Rennick himself.

"Fairweather!" The voice carried up the canyon wall, amplified by rock, almost friendly. "You're on private right-of-way! Company property! Step away from the case and this stays civil!"

Oscar pressed her cheek into the gravel. Thirty feet below, the speeders braked with a squeal of flanged steel, and boots hit ballast. She counted them by sound: six men spreading out along the grade, climbing.

"Elias Vane!" Rennick again, closer now. "Inspector. You're a long way from your jurisdiction, son. Last chance to remember whose signature is on your commission. Come down, bring the girl, and you're a hero who recovered stolen corporate records. Stay up there, and you're an accomplice. Those are the only two versions of tomorrow, and I write them both."

Oscar looked at Elias. He lay beside her in the ditch, raw-handed, mud to the chin, and she watched him weigh it — really weigh it, she could see the scales moving — and then he grinned at her, a wild un-Inspector grin she'd never seen on him, and said quietly:

"He forgot the third version."

"Which is?"

"We come down the mountain anyway, and the newspapers write it." He nodded upslope. "There's a tunnel portal at Mile 39. You surveyed it. Tell me I'm right."

"Three hundred yards through the spur," she breathed. "And on the far side the grade drops seven percent to the Deadfall flats. No motor speeder will risk those ties at speed. We'd have the whole downhill to ourselves."

"Then we're going to outrun them through a rotten tunnel on a dead railway." He shouldered the evidence case. "On foot, because we're not getting that trolley up this bank in ten seconds."

Below them, boots crunched closer. A flashlight beam swept the ditch and slid past.

"Fairweather," Elias whispered. "How fast can you climb?"

* * *

They went up the cut bank on hands and knees through slide alder, the case passed hand to hand between them like a newborn, while behind and below Rennick's voice rose into something less friendly. A shot cracked off the rocks — a warning, high and wide, meant to herd — and then a second that was not a warning, snapping through the alder a foot from Oscar's ear, and after that she stopped thinking in words altogether.

Her body remembered. That was the terrible gift of the last three years: her body had learned to move through country alone, efficiently, without hope, and now it spent that whole cold education at once. She found the sheep trail that switchbacked above the grade, the one she'd noted in her ledger as *game path, marginal utility,* and took it at a run. Elias pounded behind her, breathing in ragged fours. The shots fell behind and below as the angle steeped and the trees thickened, and the last thing she heard from the rail line was Rennick shouting orders — *the tunnel, cut them off at the tunnel* — and the receding buzz of the speeders grinding north.

"They'll drive through to the far portal," Elias gasped as they climbed. "We can't use it."

"No," Oscar said, and her lungs burned and something in her chest was laughing, actually laughing, and she couldn't have stopped it with both hands. "But they think we have to. And while they sit on that portal waiting for us—" she pointed up-slope, where the grade bent around the shoulder of the mountain toward a timbered saddle, "—we go over the top. Old survey access trail, Mile 40 to Mile 41, drops straight into the Deadfall flats behind the junction yard. I logged it three years ago. *Overgrown, passable, estimated descent ninety minutes.*"

"You put an escape route in a government ledger."

"I put *everything* in the ledger." She pulled him up the last pitch to the saddle, where the whole world opened: below them the Deadfall flats spread gold in late light, the junction town's rooftops small and dark among the trees, and rising from the telegraph office, thin and unmistakable, a thread of smoke.

Smoke.

Oscar stopped. The laugh died in her throat.

"Mara," she said.

They went down the north face at a pace that should have killed them, sliding the last fifty feet on their heels through pine duff, and came out on the flats at dusk with the smell of woodsmoke sharp in the air. The office stood blackened, roof collapsed inward, embers still ticking in the ruin. On the platform, chalked large across the boards where no fire could miss it, in a hand steady as a telegraph key:

**WIRE SENT EAST BEFORE THE FIRE. ALL OF IT. GO TO DEADFALL HOUSE. I AM NOT BAIT ANYMORE. I AM A WITNESS. — M.Q.**

"She sent it already." Elias stood reading the chalk, ash drifting around him like gray snow. "Before Rennick burned the office. Which means—"

"Which means Rennick isn't chasing evidence anymore." Oscar turned toward the lights of the town, where windows were lighting one by one, where figures were gathering in the street — more figures than a dying junction should hold, and among them, unmistakably, a cluster of men with rifles and a tall old woman standing in front of them like a switch stand set against a runaway train. "He's chasing witnesses. And every witness in this valley just found out they're allowed to talk."

Down in the street, Mara Quist raised one arm and pointed — not at them, but past them, up the darkening line of the High Passage, where the headlamps of Rennick's speeders were just coming down the final grade toward a town that had finally stopped being afraid.

"Come on," Oscar said, and started running for the lights.
