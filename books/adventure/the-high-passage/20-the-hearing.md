## 20. The Hearing

They gave the inquiry the old assize hall in Deadfall Junction, which was a joke of geography and everyone knew it. The company had built the town; the company had emptied it; now the province had chosen it as neutral ground because no other building between here and the capital could hold two hundred people and a wood stove. Oscar Fairweather stood in the witness room behind the chamber with her hands flat on a scarred table, listening to the crowd settle into the benches beyond the wall like gravel finding its level in a sluice box.

Three years she had measured her life in track miles. This morning she had measured it in steps: forty-one from the hotel door to the hall, each one counted the way she used to count rail joints, because counting kept the shaking out of her hands.

Elias stood at the window with his back to her, in his inspector's coat, though he had no commission left to pin to it. They had taken that from him eleven days ago, formally, with a letter on heavy paper. He had read it once, folded it, and used it to light the stove.

"They'll try to make you the story," he said without turning. "The woman, the map, the romance of it. Every question will be aimed at you when it should be aimed at the memos."

"Then I'll aim them back."

He turned. In the grey light through the frosted glass his face looked older than it had on the 4:10, a lifetime ago, when he had boarded her supply train with a warrant and a lie of his own. "Oscar." He crossed the room and took her hands, and her hands went quiet inside his. "Whatever happens in there — Harkness's promise is already kept. Whatever they decide."

"That's not what he bargained for."

"No," Elias said. "It's more."

The bailiff — one of Mara Quist's mountain men wearing a borrowed badge and wearing it badly — put his head around the door. "They're ready for you, miss."

* * *

The hall had been a courtroom once, before the company bought the county, and it remembered its purpose. Judge Aldous Crane presided from a bench that had been hauled out of storage and still smelled of creosote and thirty forgotten years. Behind him hung the provincial seal, slightly crooked. Before him sat counsel for the Meridian Consolidated Mining and Water Company: three lawyers in city wool, led by a silver-haired man named Corliss whose voice Oscar had heard once before, over a telegraph wire, dictating the word *deny*.

And before all of them, packed onto benches built for a town that no longer existed, sat two hundred people who had come by hand-car, by mule, by walking the dead line itself. She recognized faces from Bellhaven's satellite camps. A woman in a black shawl who had been pointed out to her as the widow of the dam's night watchman. Two men from the dredge crews, hats in their laps. And in the third row, upright as a fence post despite everything, Mara Quist, her burned hands wrapped in clean linen, her eyes sharp enough to tap out a message across the length of the room.

Corliss rose first. "Madam Fairweather. You understand this inquiry concerns the flood event of October fourteenth, classified at the time as an act of nature."

"I understand it concerned ninety-one people," Oscar said, "classified as weather."

A sound moved through the benches like wind through standing grain. Crane's gavel came down once, gently, more warning to himself than to her.

"You are not an engineer of dams."

"I am an engineer of lines. I survey what is built, where it stands, and how it failed. For three years I have surveyed closed mountain railways, Mr. Corliss, which means I have spent three years reading the wreckage of other people's decisions. I know what failure looks like. It has a signature."

"And you claim the Bellhaven flood bore this signature."

"I claim more than that." She nodded to the clerk, and the evidence came forward on a trestle table under guard: the oilskin map case, water-stained but whole, that she had held above the white water while the river tried to drown her for it. "I claim the company knew. Not suspected. Knew, in writing, fourteen months before the dam went."

She opened the case herself. Her hands did not shake. She had counted forty-one steps and saved her trembling for later, or perhaps forever.

"The core samples were taken by Meridian's own survey crew in August of the year prior. The borings show gravel and river silt at eighteen feet where the plans called for bedrock. The engineer's marginal note reads — and I quote it exactly, because I have read it every night since I found it — *"Foundation inadequate. Recommend redesign or abandonment. Present spec will not hold a hundred-year head."* Below that, in different ink, initials R.C.R., and the word **proceed**."

She laid the memo flat, turned it to face the bench, and stepped back from it as if stepping back from a cliff edge.

"R.C.R.," Corliss said smoothly, "is not established as any officer of this company."

"Rennick Charles Rennick," Oscar said. "Vice-president of operations. Your chief of security travels under the same initials because he signs nothing else. I have seen him sign an order for my death with those letters. So have federal agents, though the order itself is in the custody of the capital prosecutor now, so you'll want to be careful how hard you press the point."

The wind-sound again, louder. Corliss's composure held, but something behind it shifted its weight, like ice deciding whether to calve.

"These documents could have been fabricated."

"By whom? Harkness died before I ever met him. The vault at the source canyon was sealed with a Meridian padlock, stamped with your own serial convention — I brought the lock. And here—" she lifted the second brass tag, twin to the one Harkness had clutched at dawn, both of them green with river years, "—here is the tag of a man named Aldous Wren, assistant surveyor, who carried copies of these same samples down the High Passage nine years ago and never arrived. His remains and his satchel were recovered at the portage of bones. The company reported him a deserter. He was twenty-six years old and he was carrying your arithmetic."

For the first time, Corliss looked at the tags instead of at her. She watched him do the arithmetic himself — the cost of two brass discs against the cost of ninety-one lives against the cost, now, of the room full of people watching him do it.

Judge Crane leaned forward. "Madam Fairweather. You state that the failure was foreseen. Can you state, on your professional oath, that it was *caused* by the decision to proceed?"

"Yes." She said it plainly, the way she'd say *the bridge is out*. "A dam built on eighteen feet of gravel, to a specification its own engineer condemned, holding back a reservoir it was never rated to hold, in a season of ordinary rain. That is not weather, Your Honor. That is a signature. I've been reading signatures in wreckage for three years, and this one was signed."

* * *

They broke at noon, and the hall emptied into the street like a river through a breached sill, and Oscar stood on the assize steps in the cold sun and let people look at her. Some of them only looked. One old dredge worker took off his hat. A woman pressed a tin of biscuits into her hands and said, "You eat something, girl, before they finish you off," and was gone before Oscar could answer.

Elias found her at the rail. He looked like a man who had slept in a chair, which he had.

"Corliss will move in the afternoon session," he said. "He'll attack the chain of custody. He'll suggest you and I conspired — that I fed you government material to save my own skin after my report was discovered false. He may produce witnesses to say the vault was accessible, the memos planted."

"Can they?"

"No. But truth isn't the weapon he trusts. Volume is. He'll bury the afternoon in procedure and hope the province loses its nerve before spring." Elias looked out over the street, at the hand-cars parked along the dead spur like horses at a hitching rail. "There's something else. A wire came this morning. The capital papers have the memos. All of them. Someone leaked the prosecutor's file."

"Mara?"

"Mara swears it wasn't her, and for once I believe her modesty. Which means someone else in the company decided the ship was sinking and wanted to be seen in a lifeboat." He almost smiled. "Empires don't crack all at once, Oscar. They crack the way dams do. Quietly, internally, and then all in one afternoon."

* * *

The afternoon began exactly as Elias had predicted, with volume. Corliss produced four hours of procedural objection, two surveyors of his own who testified the core logs were "inconclusive," and a long, silky examination of the chain of custody designed to make a brass tag seem less trustworthy than a rumor. Through all of it Oscar sat in the witness chair and answered in the flat, precise cadence of a woman reading elevations, and she watched the room stop listening to Corliss somewhere around the third hour. You could hear it happen. The benches went quiet in a way that was not respectful. It was the quiet of people waiting.

Then Judge Crane, who had said little all day, set down his pen.

"Mr. Corliss. The inquiry will hear the night watchman's widow."

Corliss rose. "Your Honor, the widow Tuller is not on the schedule of—"

"The schedule," Crane said, "was prepared before this inquiry received certain documents. The schedule is amended." He looked down the length of the hall, at the woman in the black shawl rising from the third row with her hands folded like a woman going to communion. "This inquiry was convened to determine causes. It appears the causes include persons. We will hear them."

Widow Tuller testified for eleven minutes and did not raise her voice once. She spoke of her husband's last telegram, sent the evening before the flood, reporting seepage at the downstream face "of a color and quantity outside his experience." She spoke of the company man who had come to her door afterward with a settlement and a paper waiving inquiry, and of how she had signed because her children were hungry, and of how she had kept her husband's copy of the telegram sewn into her shawl for nine years because she could not bring herself to burn it and had nowhere lawful to take it. She unfolded it at the rail with fingers that trembled until they touched the paper, and then did not.

The hall made no sound at all. Ninety-one names had been a number for nine years. Eleven minutes made them people again.

After her came the dredge men, one after another, each with a piece: the night the water ran muddy above the dam; the crew ordered to log the seepage as "seasonal"; the paymaster who distributed silence money and kept a ledger of his own, which his daughter now produced from a flour sack. After them a former Meridian bookkeeper, gray-faced with fear, who identified the initials R.C.R. on the proceed order and then, asked directly by Crane whether he had seen Rennick sign such orders, said, "I entered the disbursements, Your Honor. Nine years of disbursements. To widows, to surveyors' families, to a telegraph operator in Deadfall Junction for her continued good health." His eyes flicked to Mara Quist, and Mara met them without flinching. "I can account for every payment. I brought the accounts."

Corliss objected. Corliss objected again. Somewhere past five o'clock, Corliss requested a recess to consult his clients, and Judge Crane granted it with the thin smile of a man watching a river drop below a boat's draft.

When the hall cleared, Oscar stood at the rail and looked at the trestle table: the map, the tags, the memo with its condemned foundation and its single cold word, *proceed*, and around it now the widow's telegram, the paymaster's ledger, the bookkeeper's accounts, growing all afternoon like alluvium settling out of a flooded river. Evidence, she thought. It didn't arrive like a verdict. It arrived like sediment. Layer on layer, year on year, until one day the channel was a different shape entirely and no power on earth could put the water back where it had been.

Elias came to stand beside her. He said nothing for a long time.

"He's finished," Oscar said. "Isn't he."

"Rennick is finished. The company will cut him loose by Friday and call it the act of a rogue officer. Corliss will discover a conscience and settle. The province will build a memorial and name a road." Elias's voice was dry, but underneath it ran something else, tired and fierce. "That's how it works. The law arrives late, takes credit for the weather, and goes home. You should know that going in, so the shape of it doesn't wound you later."

"It won't." She touched the brass tag at the table's edge, Harkness's tag, the one he had traded his last morning for a promise. "He didn't ask me for the law. He asked me for the source. Everything else is weather."

Crane's clerk appeared at the door. "Madam Fairweather. His Honor asks you back. Alone." A pause, and the clerk's young face went carefully blank. "And there's a gentleman arrived on the evening train. From the capital. He says he's from the prosecutor's office, and he says—" the clerk glanced at his card, as if the words might rearrange themselves, "—he says he has a warrant for the arrest of Rennick Charles Rennick, and a list of names he needs read to him. Names of witnesses still living. He says the only person who knows where all of them are hiding is you."

Oscar looked at Elias. Elias looked at the window, where the evening train was pulling out of Deadfall Junction with its lamps lit, running the dead line south toward the capital, carrying whatever came next.

"Well," she said, and picked up Harkness's tag, and weighed it in her palm like a coin about to be spent. "Then we'd best not keep the law waiting. It's been late for nine years."
