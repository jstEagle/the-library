## 22. Measuring Years

The morning of the first train came in clean and gold, the kind of autumn light that made even rust look like a deliberate choice.

Oscar Fairweather stood at the end of the rebuilt Bellhaven memorial spur with her hands in her coat pockets and her surveyor's instinct running unasked beneath everything else — noting the grade of the fresh ballast, the true line of the rails, the way the new ties had been laid with a care that said someone had loved this work. The spur ran a quarter mile from the town's edge down to a granite platform overlooking the river, and it ended nowhere. That was the point. It was not a line to anywhere. It was a line to *then*.

At its terminus stood the memorial: a low wall of local stone, and set into it, row upon row, brass tags. Not the company's stamped inventory tags — those had been melted down by court order, every last one, in a foundry that Rennick's lawyers had tried three times to keep out of the record — but hand-cast replicas of the kind Harkness had carried. One for each name. Oscar had counted them twice, then stopped counting, because some numbers were not meant to be held casually.

Bellhaven had been gone eleven years before anyone said its name out loud again.

Now there were bunting flags on the telegraph poles, and children on the platform fence, and an old woman in a wheelchair near the front with a wool blanket over her knees and a telegraph key mounted on the armrest, because Mara Quist had refused to attend any ceremony where she could not send a message if the mood took her. The mood, so far, had taken her twice: once to complain about the length of the mayor's speech, and once to inform the entire wire network between Deadfall and the coast that the coffee was terrible.

Oscar watched the track curve away eastward, toward the pass, toward the High Passage itself — reopened now, not for freight but as a protected historical route, its bridges restored, its tunnels gated against weather, its story posted on bronze plaques that told the truth in letters a child could read. She had surveyed half of that restoration herself. She had walked every mile of it with a level and a chain and a notebook, and somewhere around mile forty she had realized that she was no longer measuring distance to avoid feeling anything. She was measuring it because it felt like something worth measuring.

That was the difference. It had taken her a year to find the words for it, and Elias had been patient while she did, which was perhaps the most remarkable engineering feat either of them had ever witnessed.

"Fairweather."

She didn't turn. She'd have known his voice through a crowd of thousands; she'd have known it underwater, in the dark, in a gorge full of white water and black night. "Vane."

He came to stand beside her, hands clasped behind his back in the way he still did when he was trying to look like a government man, though he hadn't been one for eight months now. He wore civilian clothes badly and well at the same time — a good coat that he kept forgetting to button, boots that would never be described as clean by anyone who remembered what clean boots meant coming up a mountain trail.

"They're asking for you," he said. "The engineer's asking, specifically. Says the woman who ran Deadfall Gorge on a moonless night has no business letting anyone else drive the first train."

"The woman who ran Deadfall Gorge nearly drowned doing it."

"And yet here you are." He glanced sideways at her. "Nervous?"

Oscar considered the question honestly, because she had promised herself honesty the way other people promised themselves religion — imperfectly, daily, with lapses. "No," she said. "I keep waiting for it to come and it doesn't. I keep waiting for the part where my chest goes tight and I count something instead of feeling it." She looked at the rails, shining their two bright lines into the mountains. "It doesn't come."

"That's called being well," Elias said. "I've read about it."

"You've read about it."

"In a pamphlet. Quist sent it to me. There's a whole section on men who bury their fathers' deaths under federal paperwork."

"She didn't."

"She annotated it."

Oscar laughed, and the sound went out over the platform and no one looked at her strangely, and that too was part of being well — laughter in public, unmetered.

Down the line, a whistle blew. Long, clear, unhurried.

They turned together to watch it come.

* * *

The engine was an old Consolidation the railway had pulled out of retirement and reboilered herself, black paint over scars, brass fittings polished by volunteers. It came down the spur at walking pace, as befitted the occasion, steam rising off it in banners that the wind took and tore and scattered over the river. The cars behind it were open platforms, filled with people — Bellhaven survivors and their children, railmen in their oldest coats, surveyors, a scattering of newspaper people whom Mara had personally vetted and personally threatened, and one Federal judge who attended in defiance of three separate requests that he stay away, because, he said, he liked trains and owed the dead better than a letter read into a cold record.

Oscar drove.

Elias rode the fireman's side and handed her water when she wanted it and said nothing when she didn't, which was its own kind of fluency they had built across a year of working together — first on the evidence recovery, then on the hearing preparation, then on the restoration surveys, learning each other's silences the way she had once learned the soundings of a river.

At the end of the spur she brought the train to rest with a brake application so gentle the passengers barely swayed, and the crowd on the platform cheered anyway, because it wasn't about braking, it wasn't really about the train at all.

The ceremony was short. The judge spoke four sentences. A choir of schoolchildren sang, slightly out of tune, a hymn that had been sung at Bellhaven's founding and never at its destruction, because its destruction had officially never happened. Then the names were read.

All of them. Every name on the wall. Read by volunteers in relay, each taking a dozen, until they reached the last section, and Elias Vane stepped forward without being asked and read the final twelve himself, including Harkness, including the second tag's unknown carrier whose name they had finally recovered from a widow in the eastern valleys — a young company surveyor named Aldous Pell, twenty-six years old, who had died in the canyon carrying proof he never got to deliver.

Oscar stood beside the engine through all of it with her hat off. When the reading finished, the crowd observed a silence, and in that silence the river spoke instead — loud and free below the bluff, running at full seasonal volume through a channel that no longer answered to any dam, because the dam was gone. They had blown it in June, with the whole valley invited to watch from the ridgelines, and the sound of its going had carried for miles, and afterward the river had run brown for three days and then cleared, and fish had returned to the upper reaches by August, which the fisheries men said was fast and Oscar privately suspected the river had simply been waiting.

Then, because ceremonies that end only in silence leave people stranded, the band struck up, and the tables came out, and Bellhaven's living descendants began the business of feeding everyone, which is how communities have always marked resurrection.

* * *

Late afternoon found the two of them apart from the crowd, up on the old watch cut above the spur, sitting on the same flat rock where Oscar had eaten lunch alone during the restoration surveys and pretended she preferred it that way.

Below, the last of the visitors were boarding the train for the return run. Mara Quist had been loaded aboard over her objections, which she voiced by keying a message to the conductor's portable set that read, in full, AM BEING KIDNAPPED, SEND HELP AND BETTER COFFEE. The conductor, wise to her ways, waved cheerfully.

"She'll outlive us all," Elias said.

"She'll outlive the republic." Oscar unwrapped the bread one of the Bellhaven women had pressed on them and broke it in half. "She asked me yesterday what we're doing next winter. I said I didn't know. She looked at me like I'd confessed to murder."

"What are we doing next winter?"

And there it was — the question underneath the question, the one they had been circling since spring with the careful footwork of two people who had each lost things to circumstance and did not intend to lose more. Oscar chewed, and watched the river, and decided that a year ago she would have counted something right now. Track mileage. Days remaining. Anything to keep from standing in the open.

"I got a letter from the Historical Routes Commission," she said. "Official. They want a complete survey of the High Passage. Not the restoration work — the original alignment. Every grade, every tunnel, every bridge site, documented before the weather takes any more of it. Five years of fieldwork, minimum." She paused. "They asked if I'd take it."

"Did you tell them yes?"

"I told them I'd consider it." She turned the bread crust over in her fingers. "The route runs from the junction at Deadfall to the source canyon. Four hundred and eleven miles. I know, because I measured it twice — once running for my life, and once last summer, slowly, with you complaining about the biscuits." She smiled at the memory of his complaints, which had been frequent, elaborate, and entirely insincere. "It's hard country. Winters close most of the high passes. You'd want a partner who could handle the cold, read water, talk his way past suspicious locals—"

"—and file reports nobody reads," Elias said. "Don't forget my core competency."

"—and who owes the Commission a favor or three," Oscar finished, "from certain testimony given under difficult circumstances."

Elias was quiet a moment. Below them, the train pulled out, whistle singing down the valley, and the sound rose up the bluff and washed over the rock where they sat, and neither of them moved until the last echo came back off the far wall of the gorge.

"You're asking me," he said carefully, "to spend five years in the mountains with you."

"I'm asking you to spend five years *measuring* the mountains with me. The living-in-them part is incidental."

"Is it."

"Entirely incidental," Oscar said, and heard her own voice go soft on the word, and let it.

He looked at her then, fully, the way he had looked at her across a fire at Deadfall Junction over a stolen map, deciding whether trust was a thing he could afford. He had learned to afford it. She had watched him learn, the way she had watched the river learn its freed channel — tentatively at first, then all at once, then like it had never known any other way to run.

"Five years," he said. "Unhurried."

"Unhurried. We stop where the country's worth stopping. We name nothing after ourselves. We write it all down so the next fool who walks that line knows what happened there — the floods, the cover-up, Harkness, Pell, the dam, all of it. The High Passage becomes what it should have been from the start: a road with a history attached."

"And after five years?"

Oscar looked out at the valley. The light was going long and amber, and the river caught it and threw it back, and the memorial wall below glowed brass-gold, a hundred small suns for a hundred extinguished ones. Somewhere downstream, water was moving over ground that had drowned once and would not drown again. Somewhere upstream, in the source canyon, a sealed vault stood empty now, its contents distributed to courts and archives and newspapers, its location marked only by a cairn she and Elias had built and a plaque that read, in Mara Quist's suggested wording: THE RIVER KEPT IT. WE ONLY CARRIED IT OUT.

"After five years," she said, "we'll measure something else."

He reached over and took her hand, and his thumb found the thin white scar across her palm from the gorge rocks, the one she used to trace at night like a rosary of survival, and held it gently, the way you hold anything that reminds you what things cost.

"Mrs.—" He stopped. Smiled. Started again. "Miss Fairweather. Are you proposing a professional engagement or a personal one?"

"Both," Oscar said. "I'm an engineer. We believe in combined loads."

His laugh came out of him unguarded, and it echoed off the cut, and above them a hawk turned once in the gold air and dropped down the canyon on the wind.

* * *

They walked down as the shadows went blue, past the wall of names, and Oscar stopped at it — as she always stopped — and put her fingers briefly on the lowest tag. Harkness's. The brass was already going soft-edged from the touch of many hands, which was right; memorials were not meant to be sharp.

"Three years," she said quietly. "That's how long I spent counting track miles so I wouldn't have to feel anything. Eleven thousand miles of dead line, give or take. I thought I was mapping the railways." She shook her head. "I was mapping a way around myself."

Elias said nothing, because there was nothing to say, and he had learned that too.

"But the strange thing," she went on, "is that every one of those miles turned out to lead here. The numbness, the surveying, Harkness stumbling into camp, the map, the river, you—" She looked up at him. "I don't believe in fate. I'm an engineer. But I believe in load paths. Everything that comes down on a structure finds a way through, if the structure holds. Mine held. Barely. Because of the people who propped it."

She straightened, and the last of the day's sun caught her face, and for a moment she looked exactly like the woman who had once stood on a dead spur measuring grief in miles — and completely unlike her.

"Eleven thousand miles," she said, "and then four hundred and eleven more, and now whatever comes next. That's how long it took me to get from the accident to here." She took his arm. "From now on, I'm measuring in years. And I intend to collect a great many of them."

They came down off the bluff together as the dusk came up, two figures on the rebuilt spur, and behind them the river ran on in the dark, free and loud and going somewhere, carrying the light of the first stars down out of the mountains toward the sea.

Above the memorial wall, on a pole Mara Quist had insisted be strung with wire before she'd agree to attend anything, the telegraph key waited in its box, silent for now.

But just before the two of them passed out of earshot, it clicked — twice, quick, unmistakable — and if either of them had been close enough to read the tape, they would have seen the message Mara was sending east on the night wire, to every office and friend and former rival she possessed:

THEY SAID YES. TOLD YOU SO. — Q.
