## 1. Miles, Not Years

The Coriander ran the lower Thessaly like a woman who knew every wrinkle in an old friend's face.

Wendell Lockridge stood at the helm with one hand on the wheel and the other holding a tin cup of coffee gone lukewarm an hour ago, and she did not need to look at the water to know what it was doing. She could feel it through the deck plates — that low, muscular shove of current against the hull, the river leaning on them the way a tired man leans on a fence. The morning fog was burning off in rags. Somewhere upstream, a kingfisher was working the shallows, and she'd marked its position without thinking about it, the way she marked everything: gravel bar at the bend, deadfall half-submerged off the east bank, the smell of the water saying *snowmelt, still cold up high, spring running late this year.*

Three years since Tom died, and she had learned to measure time the way she measured distance. Not in anniversaries or birthdays or the slow gray creep of her hair at the temples, but in miles. In runs completed. In tons of freight delivered dry and on schedule. The river kept its own calendar and so did she, and neither of them had a page for grief.

"Captain." Bo's voice came up through the engine-room voice tube, roughened by diesel and years. "Number two's running hot again. Nothing to worry about, but I want her cooled down before we hit the Narrows stretch."

"How hot?"

"Warm enough to make conversation."

"Then cool her down and stop making conversation."

There was a grunt from below that might have been laughter. Bo Okonkwo had been her engineer for six years now, a broad, deliberate man who moved through the engine room the way a priest moves through a chapel, and who had never once in six years told Wendell something was fine when it wasn't. If he said nothing to worry about, there was nothing to worry about. That was the whole architecture of the life she'd built: small certainties, stacked tight, load-bearing.

She drank the cold coffee anyway. Waste not. Her father had said that; Tom had said it too, usually with his mouth full, and she pushed the thought away before it could put down roots. That was the trick of it. You didn't drown the memories. You just didn't let them anchor.

* * *

Priya found her at midmorning on the foredeck, coiling line with the quick economy of someone who'd grown up handling paper instead of rope and had decided, somewhere along the way, that rope suited her better.

"We'll make Callow's Landing by fourteen hundred," Priya said. "Sefa wants to know if you're eating lunch at the wheel again or if you're going to be a person today."

"Tell Sefa I'm a person every day."

"Tell her yourself. She doesn't believe me anymore." Priya leaned on the rail beside her, squinting downstream. She was twenty-six and had hands that were always slightly ink-stained no matter how much she scrubbed them, a leftover from three years as a junior archivist before she'd walked out of that life and onto Wendell's gangplank with a duffel bag and no explanation Wendell had ever asked for. That was another rule of the Coriander: nobody's past got audited. You showed up, you worked, you belonged. The boat didn't care where you'd been. The river didn't either.

"Fog's lifting early," Priya observed.

"Wind's backing around to the southwest. We'll have it on our stern by the landing."

"You can tell that from the birds?"

"I can tell that from the birds."

Priya watched a V of geese cross high overhead, angling downriver, and said nothing for a while. Then: "It's good, having you back on the water. You were strange all winter, hauled out."

"I'm always strange."

"Stranger." Priya pushed off the rail and went forward to her lines, and Wendell let the silence close behind her like water over a stone.

Strange all winter. Hauled out. Well, yes. Winter was the hard season, not because of ice — the lower Thessaly barely froze — but because winter was when the miles ran out. A boat tied to a dock was just a box with opinions. There was nothing to measure. She'd spent January repainting the pilothouse trim because standing still made her feel like she was being buried alive, and Bo had let her repaint it twice rather than say what they were both thinking, which was that the third anniversary was coming and none of them knew what to do with that except stand near her and pretend not to count the days.

The crew counted anyway. She could tell. They were bad at pretending, her people. Good at everything else.

* * *

Callow's Landing came up at ten minutes to two, which would have annoyed Wendell if it had come up late and bored her if it had come up early, so ten minutes to two was exactly right. The landing was a shelf of rotted pilings and a gravel ramp and a general store whose owner, Mrs. Aldous, bought freight from the Coriander twice a month and sold Wendell coffee beans at a markup she considered a personal insult and paid without complaint, because Mrs. Aldous had known Tom, and kindness between people who'd known Tom was a currency that didn't get haggled over.

They took on flour, salt pork, nails, a crate of lamp oil, and two barrels of Mrs. Aldous's terrible homemade wine that Sefa swore he could cook with. Sefa stood at the top of the gangplank directing the unloading with a wooden spoon he used for everything except cooking, and Wendell watched him work and felt the day settle into its groove.

Sefa Ilic had come aboard four years ago answering a notice for a cook, and had turned out to be less a cook than a kind of weather system. He fed people the way other men prayed. He was sixty-one and widowed and carried a photograph in his galley of a young woman in a graduation gown whom nobody ever asked about, because the rule held even for the living. He called the crew *my children* when he was pleased and *you animals* when he wasn't, and both titles were bestowed with equal love.

"Captain." He appeared at her elbow on the dock, spoon in hand, wine barrel under the other arm. "You will eat. Sitting down. At a table. Like a human woman."

"I ate breakfast."

"In March. I remember." He steered her toward the store's porch with the gentle tyranny of a man who had raised three daughters and lost all patience for excuses. "Sit. Mrs. Aldous has stew. I have inspected it. It is acceptable, which is the highest praise I give another cook, and I give it rarely, so be honored."

Wendell sat. The stew was acceptable. The sun was warm on the porch boards, and the river slid past below, brown-green and patient, carrying itself toward the sea the way it had since before there were names for any of this. Down at the boat, Priya and Bo were arguing cheerfully about the correct way to lash a barrel, which meant Bo was right and Priya was enjoying herself.

Three years ago, there had been four of them plus Tom. Five plates in the galley. Now there were four plates, and the fifth hung on a nail by the stove where Sefa had put it the week after the funeral and where it stayed, washed and waiting, and nobody ever said why it stayed, because the rule held even for the dead.

Tom had been the better swimmer. That was the thing that lived in her chest like a fishhook, the detail she couldn't work loose. Her little brother, two years younger, who'd swum the mill pond at eight years old on a dare, who'd fished the Thessaly every season of his life, who'd died in cold water forty miles upriver on a night Wendell could not remember — not wouldn't, *could not*, the doctor had explained it carefully, the mind protects itself, Captain, think of it as a mercy — and the last three weeks of that final voyage existed for her only as a white space shaped like a wound.

Everyone else had a story about that night. The inquiry had taken statements. Odile Marchetti had given hers in that low, level voice she used for contracts, and Wendell had sat through it feeling like a stranger reading her own obituary. Weather, they'd concluded. Ice, an overloaded hold, a night gone wrong. An accident. The word had been repeated so many times it stopped meaning anything, the way a word does, and then the inquiry closed, and Odile had left town, and Wendell had taken the Coriander back onto the water because the only alternative was to look at the white space until it swallowed her too.

Miles, not years. It worked. Most days it worked.

* * *

They cast off from Callow's Landing at 1630, on schedule, and the river took them back the way it always did, folding them into its current like a hand closing around a coin.

Evening came down gold. Sefa produced a dinner — river trout Priya had snagged at the noon stop, flatbread, the terrible wine cooked into a sauce that was somehow magnificent — and the four of them ate in the galley while the pilot light swung gently at its mooring and the water talked to itself outside the hull.

Bo told a long, involved story about a valve he'd once fixed with a spoon, and Sefa demanded the return of his spoon, and Priya laughed until she had to put her cup down, and Wendell sat among them and felt the particular ache that comes from being happy and knowing it's built on a foundation with a room sealed shut inside it.

"You're quiet," Sefa said, not looking at her, refilling her plate without asking.

"I'm eating. You wanted me eating."

"Eating and brooding are different activities. I run a kitchen. I know the difference."

"I'm eating," Wendell said, and Sefa looked at her for a moment with those dark, unhurried eyes that missed nothing, and let it go. For now. Sefa always let things go *for now*. It was his most dangerous quality.

Later, Wendell took the evening watch alone, because she liked the helm at dusk and because the others had learned — without ever discussing it, the way they learned everything — to leave her the helm at dusk. The river went violet and then black. Bats stitched the air above the water. On the east bank a fox screamed once, thin and wild, and fell silent.

She thought about the white space. She thought about it the way you touch a bruise to check if it still hurts. It still hurt.

Three years, and she had never gone back up past Kestrel Narrows. The upper river was closed to her — not by law, not by ice, but by the simple fact that the water above the Narrows was the water Tom had died in, and she steered the Coriander around that fact the way she steered around a snag, wide and careful, every season, without ever naming it. Her charter map had a blank stretch on it, forty miles of river drawn in faint dotted lines, and she had told clients for three years that the upper route was unreliable, seasonal, not worth the risk, and they had believed her because she was Wendell Lockridge and she did not lie about rivers.

It was the only lie she told.

* * *

Two days later, running the long straight reach above Miller's Bend with the morning sun flat on the water, Wendell saw the figure on the dock at Harrow's Ferry before the boat's bow wave reached the pilings, and her stomach dropped through the deck plates as if the river had opened under her.

She knew the silhouette. You could know a silhouette across twenty years and a hundred miles of bad water. The stillness of it. The way the coat hung. The particular angle of the chin, lifted, patient, a woman who had never in her life waited for anything she hadn't decided to wait for.

Odile Marchetti was standing at the end of the Harrow's Ferry dock, and she was watching the Coriander come, and she was smiling.

Wendell brought the boat in anyway. Because the river was the river, and a dock was a dock, and whatever else Odile Marchetti was, she was standing on Wendell's water, and there were rules older than grief.

But her hands were cold on the wheel, and the coffee in the tin cup at her elbow had gone untouched since dawn, and somewhere below, Bo's engine changed pitch — just slightly, just for a second — as if the boat itself had felt the weather turn.
