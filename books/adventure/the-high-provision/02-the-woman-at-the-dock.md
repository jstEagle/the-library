## 2. The Woman at the Dock

The herring boats came in at dawn, and by nine the dock at Calloway's Landing smelled like money and fish guts in equal measure. Wendell Lockridge liked it that way — honest smells, honest work, a harbor busy enough that nobody had time to wonder why the captain of the *Coriander* never took her boat above Mile 300.

She was coiling line on the foredeck when she saw the car.

It was wrong for the Landing the way a heron is wrong for a trout stream: too still, too deliberate, waiting for something to make a mistake. Black, low-slung, city plates, parked at the head of the ramp where even the harbormaster's truck didn't dare idle. The driver stayed behind the wheel. The woman who got out did not.

Wendell's hands kept moving on the rope. Her hands were good like that; they'd learned, over three years, to keep working while the rest of her went somewhere else entirely.

Odile Marchetti came down the ramp the way she did everything — like she'd already paid for the right of way. Charcoal coat, no hat despite the wind coming off the water with teeth in it, gray threading her dark hair now at the temples, and a leather document case under one arm that she carried the way other people carried verdicts. She stopped at the foot of the gangplank and looked up at the *Coriander* for a long moment, and Wendell watched her do it, watched her read the boat the way you'd read a letter from someone you'd wronged.

The old girl had been repainted since Odile last saw her. Green hull gone to a working blue, new rub strake, the name re-lettered in Priya's careful hand. But the lines were the same lines. Boats don't lie about their bones.

"Captain Lockridge." Odile's voice carried across the water flat and clear, pitched to reach the deck without carrying to the fish buyers ten feet away. She'd always been good at that. Making a public thing private with nothing but volume control.

"Marchetti." Wendell didn't stop coiling. "Dock office handles charters. I don't."

"I'm not here for a charter." Odile set one hand on the gangplank rail. Not climbing it. Asking permission with her body in a way her voice never would. "I'm here for you."

"There's a difference?"

"There's all the difference in the world." A pause. The wind pushed at her coat and she let it. "May I come aboard?"

"No."

The word landed between them and neither of them flinched. Down the dock, a fish buyer laughed at something, loud and stupid and ordinary, and Wendell hated him a little for it.

Odile nodded slowly, as if Wendell had answered a different question correctly. "Then I'll say my piece from here, and you can throw me off your dock when I'm done. That seems fair. You've earned the right to throw me off things."

*Three years,* Wendell thought. *Three years, two months, eleven days.* She didn't count them. She made herself not count them, the same way she made herself not look at the empty berth where Tom's gear used to hang, the same way she ran the same forty miles of river week after week because the sandbars there were known sandbars, mapped sandbars, sandbars that had never once shifted in the night and taken something from her.

"What do you want, Odile?" She made herself use the name. It tasted like river water after a storm — technically clean, full of silt.

"One run. Upriver." Odile lifted the document case slightly, an offering. "Triple your going rate, plus hazard. Sealed cargo, sealed manifest. My people load, my people offload. No questions from me, none from you. Six weeks, start to finish, and then we're done, and you never have to see me again as long as either of us draws breath."

Wendell finally put the coil down. She came to the rail because it was better than shouting across water, and she told herself that was the only reason.

"You know what you're asking," she said quietly.

"I know exactly what I'm asking."

"You know what happened the last time somebody said *upriver* on this deck?" Wendell's voice stayed level. She'd had three years of practice keeping it level. It was the single greatest engineering feat of her life, this voice, this flat calm instrument, and she maintained it daily the way Bo maintained the engine. "Because I'll tell you what happened. A boat went upriver. A crew went upriver. My brother went upriver. And what came back was a hull with a hole in it and a list of names, and half that list is crossed off."

"I read the incident report."

"You wrote half of it."

Something moved through Odile's face then — quick, deep, gone — like a fish turning under ice. "Yes," she said. "I did."

The dock rocked gently under them both. Somewhere aft, Sefa was banging around in the galley getting lunch started, and the smell of onions came out the vent, warm and domestic and absurd against the cold coming off the water. Wendell gripped the rail. The wood was sun-warmed even in October; the *Coriander* held heat like a living thing.

"The answer's no," Wendell said. "It was no three years ago when they offered, and it's no now, and it'll be no when you triple it again, which you will, because that's how you negotiate — you treat everything like it has a price and wait for the person to show you theirs."

"And what's yours, Wendell?"

"Don't."

"Everyone has one. I've watched you long enough to—"

"You've *watched* me?" Wendell heard her own voice crack open half a tone and slammed it shut again. She looked down at Odile Marchetti standing on the public dock in her city coat, and twenty years of history stood up between them like a wall of water. The academy. The first boat they'd crewed together, back when they were two hungry kids arguing about current lines. The letters. The loan Odile had quietly arranged when the *Coriander*'s hull needed replating and Wendell's pride wouldn't let her ask. The night at the boathouse, after, when Odile had sat with her until dawn saying nothing, because there was nothing to say, and then left before morning and never come back.

Nobody on the crew knew any of it. Nobody knew why Wendell went quiet every time a certain shipping company's barges passed, or why there was a photograph in the wheelhouse drawer turned face-down, or why the captain could name every sandbar on four hundred miles of river except the ones above Mile 300, which she spoke of the way sailors speak of wrecks.

"We're done," Wendell said. "Take your contract back to whoever sent you."

"Nobody sent me."

"Nobody does anything for free. Least of all you."

That landed. Wendell saw it land, saw the hit go home somewhere under the coat and the composure, and felt no satisfaction at all, only the old sick churn, grief and anger fused so long they'd become a single alloy she couldn't separate anymore.

Odile looked at the boat again. At the wheelhouse windows, where a shape — Priya, probably, pretending to inventory charts — ducked out of sight. At the galley vent breathing onion-smell into the cold. When she spoke again, her voice had changed register. Lower. Stripped of the negotiation.

"They're closing the high route, Wendell."

The words meant nothing and everything. Wendell kept her face still.

"Glacier survey came through last month. The calving season's starting earlier every year, and this year the ice field above the Kestrel confluence is going to let go big — the whole face, they think. After that, the upper Thessaly silted with brash for a decade, maybe forever, the way the climate's trending. There's a window. Six weeks, maybe less, before the first calving shuts it down." Odile paused. "After this season, there is no more upriver. Not for you. Not for anyone who isn't flying."

"So take a plane."

"It's not the kind of cargo that flies."

"Then it's not the kind of cargo I carry."

"Wendell." Odile said her name carefully, the way you'd step onto ice you suspected was thin. "I need to tell you something, and you're going to want to throw me off the dock, and I'm asking you to hear it first."

"Nothing you say—"

"He's still up there, Wendell."

The dock went very quiet. Or the world did. The fish buyers, the gulls, Sefa's radio murmuring through the vent — all of it receded like water pulling back from a shore, and Wendell stood at the rail of her own boat and could not move.

"That's a lie," she said. Her voice came out strange. Distant. Like someone else's on a radio channel.

"The recovery teams got everyone they could reach. Everyone but—" Odile stopped. Started again, and Wendell realized with a distant, ringing horror that Odile Marchetti's hands were not steady. "The section of bank below the old cache site. The slide took it the day after. They marked it unrecoverable. He's still up there, under the moraine, near the High Provision. And in six weeks, when the glacier lets go, that ground goes into the lake, and there won't be anything left to bring home. Ever."

Wind. Gulls. The ordinary screaming of the harbor rushing back in.

"You have some nerve," Wendell said at last, barely above a whisper. "You come down here, onto my dock, after three years, and you use him—"

"I'm not using him. He's the reason. He's the whole reason." Odile's composure was cracking now, visibly, publicly, and some part of Wendell that hated her registered the fact that Odile wasn't even trying to hide it. "I have spent three years building a case I can't finish and paying people I can't trust, and the window is closing, and you are the only captain alive who can run that river in the time we have left. So yes. I came here and I told you the one true thing I knew would hurt enough to make you listen. I'm not proud of it. I'm not asking you to be proud of me."

"Six weeks," Wendell said. The number sat in her mouth like a stone.

"Maybe five. Maybe less. The survey team's estimates keep getting worse."

Above Mile 300. Above the narrows, above the portage, above the cold green country where the river came down out of the ice and nothing was ever the same twice. Three years of running the lower water like a monk runs a cloister, and here was the woman who'd helped build the wall of Wendell's silence standing at its foot with a hammer.

And Tom. Tom under the moraine, in the dark, in the cold, while she'd spent three years measuring her life in safe miles.

"If I say yes," Wendell said slowly, "it's not for you. Whatever you think this is — whatever story you've built about us, about history, about debts — burn it now. If I run this, I run it for him. And when it's done, you and I are finished. Completely. Do you understand me?"

"I understand."

"And if I find out — *when* I find out — that you knew anything about that night that you didn't put in your polished little report, there won't be a harbor on this river where you can show your face."

For a moment — just a moment, a single frame of film — Odile Marchetti looked at her with an expression Wendell had never seen on her before. It looked almost like relief. Like a woman who'd been carrying something heavy up a long hill and had finally been given permission to set it down, even if setting it down meant it might roll away from her forever.

"Yes," Odile said. "I imagine you will."

She held up the document case. Wendell stared at it. Then she turned and went down the gangplank herself, boots ringing on the boards, took the case out of Odile's hands without touching her fingers, and walked back up without a word.

At the top she stopped. Below, Odile was already walking back toward the black car, coat snapping in the wind, not looking back, because Odile Marchetti never looked back — that was the whole trouble with her, that had always been the whole trouble with her.

"Six weeks," Wendell said to the empty air, and the wind took it upriver, toward the ice.

Inside the wheelhouse, Priya was doing a magnificent impression of having been reading the same chart for twenty minutes. Sefa's head appeared in the galley doorway with a wooden spoon held like a scepter. From below, the steady heartbeat of Bo's engine room, pausing — he'd heard. Of course he'd heard. Sound carried over water; everyone on the *Coriander* had heard every word, and everyone was now very carefully pretending otherwise, which was its own kind of family.

Wendell set the document case on the chart table. It was heavier than paper had any right to be.

"Lunch in ten," Sefa said gently, from the doorway. "Captain. You look like you've seen a ghost."

"Not yet," Wendell said, and reached for the clasps.
