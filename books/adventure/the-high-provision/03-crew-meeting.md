## 3. Crew Meeting

Priya noticed the way Wendell's hands moved before she said a word. That was the thing about living on a boat with four other people for three years — you learned to read each other the way you read weather. And Wendell Lockridge's hands were doing something wrong.

She was setting the table. Not the usual slap-and-go of a captain who ate standing up half the time, but the careful arrangement: plates centered, mugs to the left, the good lamp — the brass one Sefa had won in a card game off a dredge operator two seasons back — lit and hung over the galley table instead of its usual hook by the chart desk. Priya had seen this once before. The night they'd voted on taking the winter contract hauling fuel drums to the logging camps, when Wendell had known the money was good and the work was ugly and hadn't wanted either of those facts to tip them.

So it was a vote. Priya dried her hands on her trousers and took her seat without comment.

The Coriander's galley was built for four and held five the way a boot holds a foot that's still growing. Bo came down the ladder last, smelling of diesel and river damp, his forearms still streaked black from an afternoon spent coaxing the port engine's fuel pump back from the dead. He dropped onto the bench across from Priya with a groan that came all the way up from his boots.

"Pump's held together with hope and baling wire," he announced. "Hope's the structural component."

"How long?" Wendell asked. Not looking at him. Arranging a napkin.

"Ask me again in a week." Bo reached for the bread. "Maybe ten days if I baby her."

Sefa set the pot down between them — rice and beans and the smoked fish she'd traded for at Miller's Landing, cooked into something that tasted like someone had thought about it, which was Sefa's whole religion in one dish. She sat, folded her hands, and looked at Wendell the way she looked at a sauce that wasn't reducing right.

"You've been quiet since the dock," Sefa said.

"I'm always quiet."

"You're *quiet-quiet*. Different animal." Sefa served herself, unhurried. "Eat first or talk first?"

Wendell looked around the table — at Bo already chewing, at Priya, at Sefa's level gray gaze — and Priya watched her make the decision to stop stalling, the way you'd watch someone step off a dock into water they couldn't see the bottom of.

"Eat while I talk," Wendell said. "Because some of what I'm going to say is going to be hard to sit still for."

Priya picked up her fork. Bo didn't stop chewing, but he stopped moving otherwise, which for Bo was the equivalent of standing at attention.

"Odile Marchetti came to the dock today."

The name landed on the table like something dropped from a height. Priya knew it the way she knew names out of history books — Odile Marchetti, who'd run the *Vespera* upriver faster than anyone, who'd raced Wendell and Tom Lockridge season after season until the racing became legend and the legend became the kind of story deckhands told new hires who didn't know better. She also knew, from three years of careful not-asking, that the name meant more than legend. She'd never once heard Wendell say it aloud. Until now.

"She wants to hire us," Wendell went on. Her voice was flat and even, a pilot reading depths aloud. "One run. Upriver."

Bo's chewing slowed.

"Triple rates, paid half in advance. Enough to clear what we owe the yard — all of it, plus interest — and refit the boat properly. New planking below the waterline. Both engines rebuilt instead of resurrected. A real winch. Maybe even that ice-rated hull coating Bo keeps drawing pictures of."

"That's—" Bo started.

"It's a lot of money," Wendell said. "It's more money than we've seen in one place since I bought this boat, and it's being offered by someone I have every reason to distrust, for a route I have every reason never to run again."

The lamp hissed softly above them. Outside, the harbor water lapped at the hull in its patient rhythm, and somewhere down the dock a gull complained about nothing.

"The cargo is sealed," Wendell said. "Crates, manifest sealed too. No questions clause — standard for high-value freight, but it means we don't know what we're carrying. Two of her people come aboard as surveyors. Passengers, effectively, though she's calling them crew support. We go up the Thessaly as far as—" Wendell paused. Priya saw her jaw set. "—as far as she directs. Then we come home. Six weeks, maybe eight. The high route closes after that for the season, and this year, per her charts, possibly for good. Glacier's retreating wrong. Calving season's starting early and running long."

"Why now?" Sefa asked. "Why her? Why us?"

"Because I'm the best pilot on this river and she knows it," Wendell said, without a flicker of false modesty, "and because the route is closing and whatever she wants hauled up there has to go before it does."

"And why do you distrust her?" Sefa asked. Gently. Which was how Sefa asked the questions nobody else at the table would dare.

Wendell's hands went still on either side of her plate.

"We were rivals," she said. "A long time ago. Before Tom. Before—" She stopped, restarted. "We ran against each other for years. It ended badly. That's all anyone needs to know tonight."

*That's not all,* Priya thought. But she kept it behind her teeth, because she'd learned — from archives, from restoration work, from a former life spent handling other people's fragile papers — that you don't pry open a binding to see what's glued beneath. You wait for the pages to loosen on their own.

"What's the cargo weigh?" Bo asked. Practical, always practical, bless him. "Sealed crates, you said. How many?"

"Forty-odd. Heavy ones. She says survey equipment."

"Survey equipment doesn't come in forty heavy crates."

"No," Wendell agreed. "It doesn't."

Bo leaned back, arms crossed over his chest, and Priya could see the arithmetic running behind his eyes — the same arithmetic he did every time the pump failed, every time the yard invoice came, every time he stayed up past midnight sanding a plank by lamplight rather than let the rot spread another week. The Coriander was old. Older than any of them except Wendell, and Wendell treated her age like a personal insult she intended to argue with daily. They owed the boatyard eleven months of payments. The starboard engine had more patches than original metal. The winch drum was cracked and they'd been nursing it since spring.

Triple rates. Half in advance.

"I'm in," Bo said.

Sefa turned her head slowly, like a turret. "You haven't heard the rest."

"Heard enough. Money clears the debt, debt cleared means the boat's ours outright, boat ours outright means nobody can take her away from us. I've been waiting three years to hear somebody say a number that big out loud." He shrugged. "Also I want the ice-rated coating."

"You want the coating," Sefa repeated.

"I've wanted the coating for two years, Sefa. You've seen the drawings."

"I've seen the drawings." Sefa looked at Wendell. "And you. What do you want?"

The question hung there. Priya set down her fork. This was the part she'd been waiting for — not the terms, not the money, but the thing underneath, the thing Wendell would try to carry past them without letting them see the weight of it.

"I want the boat sound," Wendell said. "I want the crew paid. I want—" and here her voice did something Priya had almost never heard it do, which was hesitate, "—I want it done. Whatever it is. Finished. Over. Some things follow you until you turn and face them, and maybe this is one of mine."

"Upriver," Sefa said quietly.

"Yes."

"To the high route."

"Yes."

Sefa nodded slowly, and Priya watched her — really watched, the way she watched everything, cataloguing, cross-referencing, the archivist's habit that had survived the end of her archive career. Because Wendell had said *upriver*, and when she'd said it, something had happened to her face. Small. A contraction around the eyes, a fractional tightening at the corners of her mouth, gone in half a second. Priya had spent years learning to read damaged documents — water-stained ledgers, fire-scorched letters, ink faded to ghosts. She knew what damage looked like when it surfaced. Wendell's face had just shown her a watermark.

There was a history here that predated the boat, predated the crew, predated Priya herself. Something with Tom in it. Something with Odile Marchetti in it. And Wendell had just volunteered to sail straight back into it with all of them aboard.

"I smell trouble," Sefa said.

"Sefa—"

"No, let me finish, because I'm going to say this once and then I'll cook and keep my opinions in the kitchen where they belong." Sefa folded her napkin with terrible precision. "Trouble pays triple. That's the first rule of this river and everybody on it knows it. Nobody offers triple rates for easy work. Easy work pays single and you fight for that. So whatever's in those crates, whoever those surveyors are, whatever 'as far as she directs' means — it's trouble wearing a nice coat. Second rule: when a woman comes to your dock after three years of silence and offers you more money than sense, she isn't buying freight. She's buying something else. Freight is just how it gets delivered."

"Then why are you smiling?" Bo asked.

Sefa looked down and discovered, apparently to her own surprise, that she was. "Because I'm a fool," she said. "Same as you. Because I've eaten at this table for three years and I'm not ready to see it sold out from under us, and triple rates might be the only number that saves it. Because trouble wearing a nice coat is still trouble, but so is a leaking hull in November, and I can survive trouble better than I can survive watching this family lose its house." She unfolded the napkin. Refolded it. "But I want it on the record that I smelled it first."

"Noted," Wendell said. "For the record?"

"For the record: I don't trust her, I don't like sealed cargo, and I think this ends with somebody crying in my galley. But I'll vote yes if the others do, because a crew that splits over money stops being a crew, and I'd rather face trouble together than watch us come apart safe." She picked up her spoon. "Now somebody pass the fish before I change my mind."

Priya passed it. Her own thoughts were moving slower than the conversation, turning the evening over like a page under raking light, looking for the impressions beneath the ink.

Three things, she decided. Three things worth saying.

"First," she said, and both Bo and Sefa turned to her, because Priya spoke rarely enough at these tables that when she did, people listened — a habit she'd never quite shaken from the reading rooms, where a junior archivist learned that silence made your words carry further. "First: I want to see the contract before we vote. All of it. Every clause. If there's a no-questions clause, I want to read exactly what it forbids us to ask, because the shape of a secret tells you a lot about the secret. People write their fears into contracts. It's the most honest document most companies ever produce."

"Fair," Wendell said.

"Second: the surveyors eat at this table or they don't eat. I don't care what Marchetti calls them. Anyone living on this boat is crew, and crew eats together, and I want to watch them while they do. You learn more about people from how they take their coffee than from anything they'll ever tell you."

Bo snorted. "That's grim, Priya."

"It's efficient."

"Third." She paused. This was the hard one, and she weighed whether to say it, and decided that a crew about to bet its house deserved the truth even when the truth was small and strange. "When you said *upriver* — earlier, and again just now — you flinched."

The word dropped into the quiet like a stone into still water.

Wendell didn't deny it. Priya watched her not deny it — watched her sit with it for a long moment, her thumb moving along the grain of the table, following the scar where a chain had gouged the wood years before any of them arrived.

"Yeah," Wendell said finally. "I expect I did."

"You don't have to tell us why."

"No. But I will tell you this much, because you're about to sign your names next to mine and you deserve it straight." Wendell looked up, and her eyes in the lamplight were the color of deep water over gravel — beautiful and unreadable and cold if you didn't know how to look. "The last time I ran the high route, my brother died on it. Tom. Most of you know the name; Bo, you've sanded his initials off enough planks to know it well. There was an accident. Ice, water, night. I was there. I don't remember the end of it — the doctors called it traumatic memory loss, the river calls it what it calls it — and I have spent three years not going back, because part of me believes that if I ever see that stretch of water again, the remembering will start, and I don't know what it'll bring up."

Nobody breathed. Even the lamp seemed to burn quieter.

"So understand what you're voting on," Wendell said. "Not just sealed crates and triple pay. You're voting to sail with a captain who may come apart on that water, and I'm telling you I'll do everything in my power not to, but I won't lie to you and promise it. That's the honest offer. Vote accordingly."

The silence stretched. Somewhere above them, footsteps crossed the deck — a dockhand, late rounds — and faded.

Bo broke it, because Bo always broke silences eventually; it was physically painful to him. "Well," he said. "Hell, Wendell." He rubbed his jaw. "Okay. Here's my thinking. You told us true when you didn't have to. That counts for — I don't know, a lot. Everything, maybe. And the way I figure it, if the remembering starts, better it starts with us aboard than with you alone in some bunkhouse dreaming at it. We're not passengers. We're ballast. Good ballast. Ballast that cooks."

"That's the nicest thing anyone's ever called me," Sefa murmured.

"I mean it structurally."

"I know you do. That's what makes it nice." Sefa looked at Wendell, and her voice lost its edge and found something underneath it, warm and worn smooth like a handle used daily for years. "I voted yes before you told us. I vote yes harder now. Somebody on this boat should know what they're afraid of, and now we all do, and that's worth more than the money. Barely." She pointed her spoon at Wendell. "But if you come apart, you come apart *in my galley*, where I can put food in you. Not alone on deck. Agreed?"

"Agreed," Wendell said, and her voice caught slightly on it, and Priya pretended not to notice, because that was the currency of this family — noticing everything and acknowledging only what could bear acknowledgment.

They all turned to Priya.

She took her time. She looked at the contract summary Wendell had laid beside her plate — she'd read it twice during dinner, the way she read everything twice — and she thought about debts and engines and the particular gravity of a woman who measured her life in miles because miles didn't ask questions. She thought about the flinch. She thought about Odile Marchetti's name sitting on the table like a card played face-down.

"Yes," Priya said. "With conditions. I read the full contract line by line before signing. The surveyors eat with us. And—" she met Wendell's eyes, "—if the remembering starts, you tell me. Not because I'm the softest shoulder on this boat, because I'm probably the boniest. But because I spent six years recovering damaged text for a living, and if there are fragments coming back to you, I know how to handle fragments. How to lay them out, order them, look for what connects them. You shouldn't have to do that alone, and you shouldn't have to do it badly."

Wendell held her gaze for a moment. Something moved behind her eyes — relief, maybe, or fear of the relief.

"All right," Wendell said. "All conditions accepted. Then it's settled — four yes votes, honestly given, eyes open." She exhaled, long and slow, like a woman setting down something heavy and immediately picking up something heavier. "We sign tomorrow morning. She loads day after. We run at first light the following dawn, tide and weather willing."

"Upriver," Bo said, grinning now, the grin of a man who'd already spent the money three different ways. "High route. One last run. We're going to need a bigger spare-parts inventory."

"We're going to need a miracle with a torque wrench," Sefa said, rising to collect plates, and the ordinary clatter of dishes came back into the room like birdsong after a storm, everyone gratefully returning to the small mechanics of living.

Priya helped stack bowls, and carried them to the basin, and stood a moment at the little porthole above the sink while Sefa ran water behind her. Out in the harbor, the lights of the town wavered on the black water, and farther out, past the breakwater, the Thessaly ran north toward country none of them had seen in three years — toward gravel bars and cold rapids and, somewhere high up where the river went white and violent, whatever it was that Odile Marchetti wanted hauled, and whatever it was that Wendell Lockridge had lost.

*The shape of a secret tells you a lot about the secret,* she'd said. And Wendell's secret had a shape Priya was beginning to trace: a dead brother, a missing memory, a rival returned, and a route that closed forever at the end of the season. Four fragments. There would be more. Fragments always came in floods once the first one loosened — she'd seen it a hundred times, a document surrendering all at once after years of holding.

Behind her, Sefa said something about breakfast, and Priya answered, and the boat rocked gently on its moorings, already leaning, in some way Priya couldn't name, toward the current.

Up on deck, through the galley's overhead hatch, she heard Wendell's boots cross to the rail and stop there. Heard her stand a long time in the dark, facing north.

Priya dried her hands, and went up to stand watch beside her without being asked, and neither of them said a word about it, and that, too, was the currency of the family.
