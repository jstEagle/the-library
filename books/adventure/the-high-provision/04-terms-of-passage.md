## 4. Terms of Passage

The contract sat on the table between us like something dead that hadn't finished dying.

Odile had brought it to my cabin, of all places — not the dock office, not some neutral ground where I could have witnesses and a door that opened onto a street. She'd come aboard the *Coriander* at seven in the morning, when the mist was still peeling off the water and the only soul awake besides me was Bo, hammering somewhere down in the engine room with the rhythm of a man who thought better in percussion. She stood dripping on my threshold, held up a leather folder, and said, "You'll want to read it before you say no again."

Now she sat across from me while I read it, hands folded, patient as a cat outside a mousehole. Three years since I'd shared air with her longer than it took to cross a street, and she still sat the same way — spine straight, weight even, like a woman who had learned early that boats punish anyone who leans.

I made myself read every line. It was good work, the kind of contract a lawyer drafts for someone who expects to be sued: triple rates, hazard premiums, a salvage clause generous enough to make my teeth ache. The cargo was described as "survey equipment, sealed, contents not the carrier's concern." The destination was given as a waypoint code I recognized from charts I'd sworn never to open again — high river, above the Kestrel Narrows, above everything.

"You left out the part where this route closes," I said. "Calving season starts in six weeks. After that, the high water comes down and anything above the Narrows is a coffin with a current."

"The window is sufficient."

"That's not what I asked."

Odile's mouth did the thing it always did — the small, controlled almost-smile that meant she'd decided an answer wasn't worth spending. "The window is sufficient," she repeated, "for a captain of your reputation. Which is why I'm offering it to you and no one else."

I set the contract down and looked out the porthole. The mist was burning off. Down the dock, Priya was coiling line with the particular violence she reserved for mornings she thought nobody was watching her think. Sefa had the galley vent open; the smell of frying bread drifted across the water like a promise that today could still be ordinary.

"I have conditions," I said.

"Of course."

"They're not negotiable, so don't waste breath pretending to consider them."

"Wendell." My name in her mouth, after three years. It landed wrong, like a coin dropped into deep water — you hear it go down but you never hear it hit bottom. "I ran freight with you for nine years. I know how you negotiate. You put the terms on the table and then you watch my face while I read them, because you learned that from your brother."

I hadn't expected that. I'd expected charm, or flattery, or the smooth professional warmth she wore like a coat. What I got was Tom's name, spoken plainly, and it went through me like a splinter finding its old channel.

I kept my voice level. "Condition one. I choose the route. Every mile of it. Your charts are suggestions, nothing more. If I say we hold at a bank, we hold. If I say we turn around, we turn around, and you pay full rate for the distance covered."

"Agreed."

Too fast. I noted it and moved on. "Condition two. No passengers beyond your two surveyors. No one boards at any stop who isn't on this manifest. I'm not running a taxi service for whatever this actually is."

"Voss and Ansel. That's all. Agreed."

Also too fast. Odile Marchetti had negotiated river contracts for a decade; she once argued me down four percent on a grain run over a dispute about ballast weight. Now she was agreeing to everything inside ten minutes, and every easy yes rang a little bell somewhere under my sternum, small and tinny, like ice shifting in a glass.

"Condition three." This was the one I'd built the night around, turning it over in the dark before she ever knocked. "Anyone aboard can walk away. At any resupply stop, any crew member — including mine — can step off, take their pay pro-rated, and I will run them back downstream myself if it comes to that. No argument, no penalty. And that includes me. If I decide this run stinks worse than the money, I beach the boat and we're done, and there's nothing in this contract that says otherwise."

For the first time, Odile hesitated.

It was brief — a half-second, maybe less, the space between one breath and the next — but I'd spent thirty years reading faces on water, and water teaches you that the danger isn't the wave you see coming. It's the flat spot behind it. Her eyes flicked toward the folder, then back to me, and something moved behind them that I couldn't name. Calculation, maybe. Or relief. With Odile those two had always worn the same coat.

"Anyone can leave," she said slowly, "at any scheduled stop. Yes. Agreed." Then, softer, almost gentle, which was worse: "Even you."

"Especially me."

She signed first. Her hand didn't waver. I signed beneath her name and watched the ink dry and felt the moment settle over the boat like weather — that specific pressure drop you feel in your back teeth before a storm you can't yet see.

"There's one more thing," Odile said, standing, gathering the folder. "The surveyors board tomorrow at first light. Voss handles their equipment personally. He'll ask to supervise loading, and I'd take it kindly if you let him."

"Nobody touches my deck without my say-so."

"I know." At the door she paused, half-turned, and the morning light caught her face at an angle that showed me lines I didn't remember from three years ago. "Wendell. Whatever you think this is—"

"Don't." I held up a hand. "Whatever it is, we'll both find out on the water. That's where truth floats, eventually. Everything sinks sooner or later, but truth floats."

She looked at me for a long moment. "Tom used to say that."

"No," I said. "He used to say it about me. There's a difference."

And she was gone, footsteps fading down the deck planking, leaving the smell of cold river air and some expensive perfume that had no business within a hundred miles of working water.

* * *

I didn't tell the crew about the signing until evening.

Not out of secrecy — or not only. I told myself it was professionalism. A captain doesn't announce terms until they're final; a captain doesn't parade uncertainty in front of people whose sleep depends on her certainty. That was the story I sold myself while I walked the boat alone all afternoon, checking lashings that didn't need checking, testing hatches that had closed clean for years, running my thumb along the rail where Tom's paint job still showed under three coats of newer color, because neither Bo nor I had ever had the heart to sand past it.

Professionalism. Dread wearing a borrowed uniform.

At dusk I gathered them in the galley. Sefa fed them first — she always fed people before hard news, a habit I'd stopped questioning years ago — rice and smoked fish and the hot sauce Priya imported at criminal expense. Then I laid it out flat: one run, upriver, triple pay, sealed cargo, six weeks of window before the calving season shut the high route like a door. I gave them every term. I told them anyone could walk away at Reprieve or anywhere else we tied up, no hard feelings, no debt.

What I didn't tell them — what sat in my chest like a swallowed hook — was how easily Odile had agreed. How every condition I'd built as a wall, she'd walked through like it was tissue paper. A woman like Odile didn't concede route authority, passenger limits, and walk-away rights in ten minutes unless she'd already decided none of them would matter.

Bo heard it out, wiped his hands on a rag, and said, "Triple pay clears the yard debt and re-plates the hull. I vote yes before you finish asking."

Sefa said nothing for a long time, which from Sefa was a speech. Then: "Sealed cargo going upriver during calving season." She looked at me over the lamp. "In my country we had a saying. When a stranger offers to carry your heaviest load uphill, you check what he's put inside yours first."

"We'll check," I said. "We'll check everything."

Priya just watched me. Twenty-six years old, sharp as a filleting knife, missed nothing and pretended to miss less. "Upriver," she said quietly. Not a question. Just the word, set down between us like a stone.

"Yes."

"Past the Narrows."

"Past the Narrows."

She nodded slowly and didn't push, and I loved her for it, and I hated that I noticed myself loving her for it, because noticing meant I knew exactly how close she'd come to the thing I couldn't say aloud: that upriver was where Tom died, and I was about to pilot a boatload of everyone I loved straight back into the water that took him.

* * *

The lockbox was under my bunk, bolted to the frame, and I opened it that night the way I'd opened it a thousand times — out of ritual, not need. Inside: my grandfather's barometer. Tom's first knife, the one with the antler handle. A photograph of the two of us aged nineteen and twenty-three, squinting into sun on the deck of our first boat. Papers. Deeds. And my river journal — thirty years of soundings, notes, sketches of gravel bars and current seams, every secret the Thessaly had ever taught me, written in my own hand. The single most valuable object I owned, worth more than the boat, because the boat could be replaced and the knowledge in those pages could not.

The lockbox hung open and empty where the journal should have been.

I knelt there a long time. Long enough for my knees to ache against the deck plates. Long enough to check twice, three times, moving the knife, the barometer, the photograph aside, as though the book might have slid somewhere, might have hidden itself, might have done anything except the impossible thing it had done, which was cease to exist.

The lock hadn't been forced. The hinges weren't scratched. Whoever took it had either my key or the patience to pick a lock without leaving a mark, and had known exactly which item to lift out of a box full of valuables and leave behind. They'd taken paper. They'd left gold.

Only one kind of thief does that. The kind who wants knowledge, not money. The kind who knows what's in the pages before they open the box — or who wrote some of the entries themselves.

I sat back on my heels and let the cold come up through the deck into my bones, and I thought about a woman who had signed every one of my conditions without blinking, and I understood, finally, the shape of what I'd agreed to.

She hadn't agreed too easily because the terms didn't matter.

She'd agreed because the journal was already gone, and with it every secret route, every hidden channel, every private sounding I'd ever recorded on that stretch of river. She didn't need my permission to choose the route.

She'd already chosen it. Using my own hand.

Somewhere above me, through the deck, I heard the river moving in the dark — steady, patient, carrying its cold meltwater down from places I'd sworn I'd never see again. Tomorrow the surveyors would board. Tomorrow the crates would come aboard stamped with logos I didn't recognize. Tomorrow I would cast off lines I'd spent three years tying myself to this dock with.

I closed the empty lockbox. I turned the key out of habit, and the mechanism clicked shut on nothing, and the sound was very small and very loud.

Upstream, faint as artillery, came the first distant crack of ice letting go.

Six weeks, she'd said.

Listening to that sound, I'd have bet the boat it was five.
