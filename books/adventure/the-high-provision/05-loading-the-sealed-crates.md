## 5. Loading the Sealed Crates

Bo Hargrove had a rule about engines and a rule about people, and both rules said the same thing: listen to them before you trust what they tell you. An engine that ran rough was telling you something its owner might not know or might not want you to know. A person who talked too smooth was doing the same thing with their mouth.

The two surveyors Odile Marchetti had brought aboard talked very differently from each other, and Bo listened to both of them all morning while the Coriander took on cargo at Pier Four.

The man — Voss — said maybe forty words before noon, and half of those were *careful* and *mind the corner* and *that one goes amidships*. He was tall in a folded-up way, like a man built for doorways narrower than the world provided, and he wore his coat buttoned to the throat on a morning warm enough that Sefa had already stripped to her shirtsleeves on the galley deck. His hands were soft. Bo noticed hands. Soft hands meant a man who hadn't hauled line since he was a boy, if ever, but Voss handled the crates like he'd been drilled in it — knees bent, back straight, no wasted motion. Trained careful, not worked tough. There was a difference, and Bo filed it away the way he filed everything: in the same dusty cabinet where he kept torque specs and old grievances.

The woman — Ansel — was another animal entirely. She arrived at seven sharp with two paper cups of coffee and gave one to Priya at the gangway like they'd known each other for years instead of eleven minutes. She laughed easily, asked questions easier, and by eight o'clock she knew Priya's home port, Sefa's spice supplier, and the name of the cat that sometimes slept on the fuel drums down at the yard. Bo watched her work the crew the way a current works a mooring line — gentle, constant, testing how much give there was.

He didn't hold it against her. He'd spent enough years around engineers to know that charm was just a tool some people were issued instead of a wrench. But he noted it. Everything got noted this morning. Bo couldn't have said why, exactly, except that his rule about engines applied here too: when something hums wrong, you don't turn up the radio. You open the panel.

* * *

The crates came off a flatbed truck at first light, twelve of them, strapped down under tarps like they were ashamed to be seen. The truck driver was a contract hauler Bo half-recognized from the grain runs, a man named Duley who tipped his cap and asked no questions, which told Bo that questions had been discouraged somewhere upstream of him.

Each crate was maybe a meter long, half again as wide, built of plywood over a frame that looked heavier than it needed to be. They were stenciled in gray on three sides with the same mark: a hexagon split by a vertical line, and beneath it a string of letters and numbers — **MERIDIAN EXTRACTION GROUP / MXG-4410 / HANDLE LEVEL** — and along the bottom edge of each one, smaller, almost an afterthought: **PROPERTY OF MERIDIAN. DO NOT OPEN IN TRANSIT.**

Bo crouched by the first crate as the dock crane swung it over and read the stencil twice. Meridian Extraction Group. He turned the name over. He knew the mining outfits on this stretch of the Thessaly the way he knew his own scars — Calloway Deep Rock, the Ferris consortium, the little placer operations that scraped the gravel bars and paid their fines late. He'd serviced pumps for half of them and cursed the other half. Meridian wasn't any of them. He'd never seen the hexagon mark, never heard the name spoken at the chandlers or the union hall or the grumbling tables at the Saltmarsh Tavern where every outfit's sins got recited like scripture.

A company big enough to ship twelve sealed crates upriver through Odile Marchetti should have left fingerprints somewhere in Bo's memory. It hadn't.

"Careful with that one," Voss said, appearing at his shoulder without sound. The man moved quiet for someone his size. "Level, please. All of them level."

"They'll be level," Bo said. "Boat's going to roll anyway, once we hit water."

"Level when they're placed," Voss said, without inflection, and moved on to supervise the next swing of the crane.

Bo looked at the crate again. Level when placed. Freight didn't care about level; freight cared about being lashed down so it didn't shift and punch a hole in the hull when the river got opinions. You stacked freight by weight and shape, heavy low, long side fore-and-aft, and you never — not once in thirty years of engine rooms and cargo decks — arranged it by *level*. Unless what was inside needed to stay oriented. Instruments did. Gyroscopes did. Certain kinds of batteries did. Core samples in sealed tubes, maybe, if you were fussy about them.

Or something that would break.

He helped guide the first crate down onto the forward hold hatch cover, took the weight against his shoulder as it settled, and felt — faintly, unmistakably, the way you feel a bad bearing through a handrail before you ever hear it — a hum.

Not vibration from the crane. The crane had swung free and the winch motor was thirty feet away. Not the dock. The dock didn't hum. This came from inside the crate, a thin steady tone right at the bottom edge of hearing, the kind of sound you felt in your teeth more than your ears. Bo held still, palm flat against the plywood, and counted five seconds. The hum didn't waver. Didn't pulse. Just sat there, patient as a pilot light.

He took his hand away. The hum kept on, or stopped — he genuinely couldn't tell which, and that bothered him more than the sound itself.

"You feel that?" he asked Duley, who was unhooking the sling.

Duley glanced at the crate, then at Bo, then at Voss across the deck. "Feel what?"

"Nothing," Bo said. "Bad knee."

* * *

By nine the deck of the Coriander looked like a chessboard nobody had finished setting up. Twelve crates, lashed in two ranks of six, running from just aft of the pilothouse to the forward hatch. Wendell had come down from the wheelhouse around eight-thirty, stood with her arms crossed watching the placement, and said nothing for ten full minutes, which anyone who knew her understood to be a speech. Then she'd checked the lashings herself, tugged each one hard enough to make the deck timbers complain, and gone back up without a word. Bo had caught her eye on the way. Something passed between them that neither of them named — the captain and her engineer agreeing, wordlessly, that the morning tasted strange.

Priya worked the cargo plan on a clipboard, calling positions, and Ansel hovered at her elbow supplying numbers from a sheet of her own. The two of them got along like a house fire. That was the thing about Ansel — she made liking her feel like your own idea.

"So what's in them?" Sefa asked around ten, arriving on deck with a tray of coffee that was less hospitality than reconnaissance. She poured for everyone, surveyors included, and planted herself next to the second rank of crates with the tray balanced on her hip. "Twelve boxes of secrets. My grandmother always said a gift you can't unwrap is just a debt wearing a bow."

"Survey equipment," Voss said.

"Geological instruments," Ansel said, at the same moment.

There was a pause the length of one river wave slapping the hull.

"Both," Ansel added brightly. "It's both. Surveying is geology with better boots." She laughed, and Sefa laughed, and Priya smiled, and the moment papered itself over so smoothly that anyone not watching for the seam would have missed it entirely.

Bo missed nothing. He'd spent his life listening for the knock in an otherwise clean rhythm.

* * *

They broke for lunch at noon, and Bo didn't. He told Sefa he'd eat at the bench, waved off her look — the one that said she could count on one hand the number of lunches he'd skipped in six years and remembered what each of them had cost him — and stayed on deck while the others went below.

The deck was his for twenty minutes. Voss had gone ashore to make a call from the harbormaster's office, walking stiff and upright like a man carrying a full glass of something. Ansel had gone to find the chandlery, announcing she needed sun gloves and that anyone who laughed at sun gloves could buy their own. The dock traffic thinned. Gulls argued over something behind the fish sheds.

Bo knelt beside the third crate in the second rank and put his ear against the plywood.

Nothing. Or — he closed his eyes, slowed his breathing, did the trick he did with engines, shutting out the harbor noise layer by layer until only the immediate remained — there. Faint. Steady. A hum with a frequency he'd have sworn sat somewhere near sixty cycles, like mains power, except there was no power running to these crates, no cable, no battery terminal, nothing. He moved down the rank, ear to wood, crate to crate. One, two, four, five, six: silent as furniture. Three hummed.

He went to the first rank. Fourth crate hummed. So did the seventh overall, and the eleventh. Four humming crates out of twelve, scattered through the load like raisins in a loaf.

He sat back on his heels and looked at them, and the looking raised questions faster than he could bank them. Why seal geological instruments? Instruments wanted calibration, wanted access panels, wanted to be checked before you trusted a reading they gave you two hundred miles upriver where no replacement existed. Why ship them under a company name nobody local had heard of? Why did the cheerful one and the quiet one disagree about what the cargo was, and recover so fast? And why — this was the question he kept circling back to, the splinter under the skin — why did four of twelve crates need power, or contain something powered, when the manifest Odile had shown Wendell said, in Priya's paraphrase, *survey equipment, non-hazardous, no declarations required*?

Non-hazardous things didn't hum.

Bo was not a suspicious man by nature. Suspicion took energy, and he preferred to spend his on gaskets. But he had also, once upon a time, been the second engineer aboard a boat called the *Larkspur*, and he had signed off on a fuel line inspection that another man had done badly, and he had learned what happened when you let the official story do your thinking for you. Two men had burned. One had died. Bo had carried the lesson out of that inquest stitched into him like wire: *the paperwork tells you what should be true. Your hands tell you what is.*

His hands were telling him something now.

He got up, went to the tool locker, and came back with a carpenter's pencil and the flat cedar shingle he used for shimming engine mounts. On the shingle, in his small square handwriting, he wrote:

*Oddity 1 — co. name unknown (Meridian Ext.). No record.*
*Oddity 2 — crates placed by LEVEL, not by weight/shape. Who does that?*
*Oddity 3 — V. & A. differ on cargo ("survey eq." vs "geological instr."). Recovered quick.*
*Oddity 4 — 4 of 12 crates hum. #3, #4(1st rank), #7, #11. Constant tone, ~60 Hz feel. No power source visible. Non-hazardous my ass.*

He looked at the list. It looked small and enormous at the same time, the way a hairline crack looks on a crankcase — nothing today, everything later, depending on what the metal decided to do.

He thought about taking it to Wendell. He actually stood up and took two steps toward the pilothouse ladder before stopping. What would he say? *Captain, your rival's cargo makes a noise I can't prove, and the nice lady and the quiet man used slightly different words.* Wendell was carrying enough this morning — he'd seen it in her jaw, in the way she'd checked the lashings twice, in the three seconds too long she'd stood looking north up the river before turning back. She'd hired on for triple pay because the boat needed it and because some part of her needed the miles, and Bo was not going to bring her ghosts a rattle he couldn't yet name.

Besides. He wanted to know more first. A good engineer didn't report a knock until he knew whether it was a loose bolt or a thrown rod. The difference determined whether you mentioned it at dinner or started writing letters to the families.

He slid the shingle into the toolbox, under the feeler gauges where no one else ever reached, and went to find something useful to do with his hands.

* * *

Useful turned out to be the intake strainer, which was fouled with cottonwood fluff, and then the port fuel line's second filter, which was due, and then — because the afternoon was long and the surveyors were ashore — the bilge.

Bo liked the bilge the way other men liked churches. It was the lowest, truest place on a boat. Every vessel told the truth down there, in the smell of its own water and the state of its own frame. He went down with a trouble light and a rag, mostly to check the limber holes ahead of departure, and worked his way forward through the dim, rocking dark, hand over hand along the frames.

He was under the forward hold, directly beneath the rank of crates, when he stopped.

The hum was louder here. Not much. But down in the bare bones of the boat, with the river muttering against the planking a foot from his ear, the sound came through the deck above him clear as a struck tuning fork laid on a table. He put his palm flat against the underside of the hold sole and felt it: a fine tremor, steady, indifferent, like something asleep up there that could wake up.

Four crates. Sixty-cycle-ish. No power run to them. Sitting over his head in the belly of the only family he had left.

"Okay," Bo said quietly, to the bilge, to himself, to whatever was listening. "Okay."

He stayed down there longer than the job needed. That was the thing he'd admit to no one: the dark under the deck plates was where he did his best thinking, and his thinking, this afternoon, kept snagging on the same knot. Odile Marchetti had walked onto their dock with money and manners and a story smooth as varnish, and Wendell — who trusted nobody, who trusted Odile least of all, and had signed anyway — was sailing them all into country Bo had only ever heard about in the kind of stories that ended with widows. Triple pay bought a lot of refit. It did not buy back a hull holed by whatever a humming crate became when it stopped humming.

He climbed out into the late light, wiped his hands, and found the deck empty except for Priya, standing at the rail with her clipboard, checking lashings by lamplight the way Wendell had taught all of them — tug everything twice, trust nothing once.

"Deck secure?" she asked.

"Deck's secure," Bo said. Then, because Priya was the one person aboard who collected information the way he collected oddities, and because saying it aloud would keep it honest: "You happen to lean on any of those crates today?"

Priya frowned. "I leaned on all of them. Why?"

"No reason." He picked up his toolbox. At the companionway he paused, half-turned, the question working at him like water at a seam. "The fourth one in the front rank. You notice anything? When you leaned?"

Priya considered. "Cold," she said slowly. "Colder than the others, I thought. I figured it was shade from the pilothouse." Her eyes came up to his, and he watched the archivist in her wake up behind them, the woman who had spent years handling documents and learning that what mattered was never what was written on top. "Bo. Why are you asking?"

He looked back at the crates, gray and silent in the dusk, twelve identical boxes with four heartbeats among them.

"Because three of them aren't cold," he said, "and I'm going to find out what's different about the ones that are."

Above them, in the pilothouse, the lamp clicked on, and Wendell's shadow moved across the wheelhouse windows as she began, alone, her evening ritual of studying the charts — studying, though neither of them knew it yet, the route north that Odile's maps swore no boat could take.
