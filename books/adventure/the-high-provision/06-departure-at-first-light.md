## 6. Departure at First Light

Sefa woke before the alarm, the way she always did on departure days, and lay in her bunk listening to the Coriander breathe.

Every boat had a voice if you cooked aboard it long enough. The old girl spoke in creaks and ticks — the hull settling against the fenders, the water heater ticking as it warmed, Bo already down in the engine room running through his pre-departure litany of taps and gauges. Sefa had spent eleven years on other people's boats learning to read those sounds the way other people read faces. A boat told you when it was nervous. This morning the Coriander sounded like a woman getting dressed for a funeral she'd agreed to attend but didn't believe in.

She swung her feet onto the cold deck plates and reached for her boots. Through the porthole, the harbor was still dark, but the dock lamps were on, and under them she could see the shapes of crates waiting to go aboard. Sealed crates. She'd watched Wendell sign for them yesterday evening without opening so much as one, and she'd said nothing then, and she would say nothing now, because that was the arrangement this family ran on: you carried your own weight, and you let other people carry theirs, and you fed everyone regardless.

But she noticed things. That was her job too, though it wasn't written anywhere.

* * *

The galley was four steps long and three wide, and Sefa could work it blind, which was good, because she did most mornings. She got the stove going, put water on for coffee, and started the dough for flatbread because flatbread was what you made when you wanted people to feel like someone was looking after them. It didn't keep well, which meant it had to be eaten fresh, which meant the crew had to come to the table, which meant — for twenty minutes, anyway — they would all be in one room together, chewing, before the river took them.

She'd learned that trick from her mother, back in a kitchen with a different sea outside the window. Food wasn't fuel. Food was an argument for staying at the table. You could get people to say true things over flatbread that they'd never say standing up.

She thought about that as she worked, because today there were true things sitting around this boat like unexploded ordnance, and somebody was going to have to start defusing them.

The money, for instance. Triple pay, Wendell had said, laid out honest at the crew meeting, and Priya had done the arithmetic out loud — debts cleared, refit paid, winter covered, and something left over besides. Bo had voted yes before Wendell finished talking. Sefa herself had smelled trouble the way she smelled scorched milk: instantly, and from another room. But she'd voted yes too, hadn't she. Because the money was real, and because Wendell had stood there asking with her jaw set like a woman handing over her own hide, and because you didn't leave family alone in a room with a thing like Odile Marchetti.

And the sealed cargo. And the two surveyors sleeping aboard tonight, Voss and Ansel, who'd come up the gangway with their gear and their polite smiles and their eyes doing quiet inventory of everything. Sefa had fed them dinner — rice and fish, nothing fancy — and watched how they ate. The man ate like someone who'd been trained to eat efficiently and resented it. The woman ate like she was grateful, actually grateful, which was either good manners or hunger or both. Neither of them had asked for seconds, which told Sefa more than anything else about the evening: people comfortable in a house ask for seconds.

Up on deck, footsteps. Wendell, doing her walk. Sefa knew the captain's rounds by sound — the particular rhythm of those boots, quick on the foredeck, pause at the rail, quick again. Wendell walked the boat the way some men paced a hospital corridor. Sefa set the first flatbread on the griddle and listened to the captain stop moving.

Through the galley's little aft window, she could see the dock. The lamps. The crates. And one figure standing at the far end of the pier, hands in pockets, not coming closer.

Marta Elling.

Sefa's spatula paused above the griddle for half a second, then kept working.

* * *

By full light the whole crew was up, and the farewell crowd had begun to gather, and Sefa understood immediately that it was going to be a strange crowd, small and wrong-shaped, like a family photograph with half the faces cut out.

There was old Tuomas from the chandlery, who brought a sack of oranges "for the vitamin," he said, pressing it into Priya's arms and pretending he hadn't also pressed a folded bill into her hand. There were the two sisters who ran the net loft, waving from their doorway. There was a boy — maybe ten, the Kestrel boy whose skiff Bo had patched last summer — sitting on a bollard with his fishing rod across his knees, not fishing, just watching the boat with the naked adoration of a child watching something he intended to become.

And there was Marta Elling, still at the far end of the dock, still not coming closer.

Sefa carried a tray of coffee up onto the deck and stood beside Wendell at the rail while the captain pretended to study the sky.

"You see her?" Sefa asked quietly.

"I see her."

"She's been there since before dawn. I saw her when I got up."

"I know." Wendell's voice was level, which with Wendell meant nothing at all. "Tom's partner. Was. Before."

"Before the accident."

"Before I came back from the accident being me instead of being useful." Wendell took the coffee Sefa offered. Their fingers brushed; the cup trembled, very slightly, and Wendell steadied it with both hands like a woman catching something falling. "She said what she said at the memorial. In front of everyone."

Sefa remembered. She hadn't been there — she'd joined the Coriander eight months after, hired by a captain who interviewed her for forty minutes and never once asked why a woman of fifty-two wanted deck-galley work on a freight run — but the story had come to her the way stories do on boats, worn smooth by retelling: Marta Elling, big voice, bigger grief, pointing at Wendell Lockridge across the memorial hall and saying, *You came back and he didn't, and you can't even tell us why.* And then swearing, loud enough for the whole town, that she would never speak to Wendell Lockridge again as long as the river ran.

Three years, the river had run. And here was Marta at the end of the dock at dawn, keeping her oath and breaking it at the same time, because you couldn't swear never to speak to someone and then stay away when they were leaving — maybe leaving forever, maybe leaving toward the exact stretch of water where her brother died.

"Why is she here, Captain?"

Wendell drank her coffee. Down on the dock, Tuomas was shaking Bo's hand with both of his own, and the Kestrel boy had stood up off his bollard as if the boat might take questions.

"Because hate is just love that lost its road home," Wendell said. "My grandmother used to say that. I used to think it was nonsense." She set the empty cup on the rail. "I don't anymore."

Then she went forward to check the lines, and Sefa went below to feed the surveyors, and neither of them said anything else about it, because that was the arrangement.

* * *

Breakfast was flatbread, smoked fish flaked with lemon, eggs scrambled soft the way Bo liked, and a pot of coffee strong enough to strip paint. Sefa called it a departure breakfast, which on the Coriander was a tradition exactly three departures old, but traditions had to start somewhere and she intended this one to survive.

Voss came down first, sat at the end of the bench, thanked her for the meal in a voice like a filing cabinet closing, and ate precisely. Ansel came next, and Sefa watched her take in the galley — the copper pots hanging from hooks, the jar of dried chilies Sefa had strung like a garland, the little tin on the shelf labeled SUGAR (DO NOT TOUCH, BO) — with an expression Sefa recognized from mirrors. Homesickness. Or its cousin, whatever you call the ache of seeing a household that works when yours doesn't.

"This is a real boat," Ansel said. "I mean — people live on it. Actually live."

"People live everywhere," Sefa said. "Some of them just forget to cook."

Ansel laughed, and it was a good laugh, unguarded, and Sefa felt the familiar turn of her own attention — the cook's attention, the mother's attention, the attention of a woman who had once missed every warning sign that mattered and had sworn, since, to miss none. Something in this woman was braced. Something in her was braced the way you brace before a wave hits.

"Eat," Sefa said, putting the extra flatbread on her plate without asking. "Growing girls. Even grown ones."

Bo arrived smelling of diesel and took his seat with the groan of a man twice his age, which was his morning ritual and nobody's fooling anybody. Priya came down with the oranges already peeled, distributing segments like communion. Wendell came last, wet to the elbows from checking the bilge, and stood in the galley doorway a moment looking at her crew gathered around her table, and Sefa saw her do the thing she always did — count them. One, two, three, four, five. Not six. Never six. The counting was quick and the pain of it was quicker, gone behind the captain's face before it finished arriving, but Sefa had been watching for it since her first week aboard and she always caught it.

"Sit, Captain," Sefa said. "You're a passenger until we clear the breakwater."

"I'm never a passenger."

"Sit anyway."

Wendell sat. For ten minutes the Coriander's galley held the best version of itself: steam and talk, Bo complaining about the new intake gaskets, Priya telling the Kestrel boy story from yesterday, Ansel asking genuine questions about the river, even Voss almost smiling once, briefly, like a man testing whether his face still remembered how.

Nobody finished their breakfast.

Sefa noticed it the way she noticed everything, and she didn't comment, because commenting was not the same as understanding. But plate after plate came back to her sink with food still on it — Bo, who never left eggs; Priya, who treated waste as a moral failing; Wendell, who ate like fueling a machine and always cleaned her plate. Today the bread went stale-warm and untouched. Today the coffee went cold in cups. Appetite was the body voting, Sefa's mother used to say, and this morning every body on the boat was voting no.

They were excited, some of them. Bo was excited — triple pay, new parts, the refit he'd been sketching on napkins for two years. Priya was curious; curiosity was her engine. But underneath the excitement, in every one of them, was the other thing, the thing nobody had named at the crew meeting: they were going upriver. Upriver was not a direction on this boat. Upriver was a wound with a wake.

Sefa scraped the plates and said nothing, and hated saying nothing, and did it anyway.

* * *

At seven-forty, Wendell gave the order, and the Coriander came alive.

Sefa stayed at her galley station for the casting-off — her post was below, but the aft window framed the dock perfectly, and she had long ago decided that watching departures was part of feeding people. You needed to know what a leaving cost. Otherwise the meals you cooked afterward were just calories.

Bo took the stern line in, coiling it with the tenderness he showed only rope and engines. Priya handled the bow, quick and sure. Wendell stood at the helm, and even from below Sefa could read her posture: spine straight, hands loose, the studied ease of a pilot showing the river she was not afraid of it.

The crowd waved. Tuomas waved both arms. The net-loft sisters waved. The Kestrel boy raised his fishing rod overhead like a sword, and Bo saluted him back with a wrench, and the boy's mouth opened in a shout they couldn't hear and didn't need to.

And at the far end of the dock, Marta Elling stepped forward.

Not all the way. Just one step, out of the shadow of the lamp standard, into the early sun, so that for the first time Sefa could see her face clearly — a broad, weathered face, handsome the way driftwood is handsome, and wrecked with the effort of standing there. Her hands were out of her pockets now. They hung at her sides, opening and closing, opening and closing, like she was holding something invisible that kept trying to escape.

Wendell saw her. Everyone saw Wendell see her; the whole wheelhouse went still the way a room goes still when a glass is about to fall.

For three heartbeats, the two women looked at each other across fifty feet of planking and three years of silence and one dead man between them. Sefa found she was holding her breath, and that Priya beside her was holding hers, and that somewhere below, even the engine seemed to hold its.

Marta Elling raised one hand. Not a wave. Just the hand, palm out, fingers spread — the gesture of a person stopping traffic, or surrendering, or reaching for something across a gap too wide to reach across. Then she turned and walked away up the dock, fast, shoulders high, not looking back, and the Coriander slid out past the breakwater into open water, and the door closed behind them with a sound only Sefa seemed to hear.

* * *

They cleared the harbor at eight sharp. The river took them the way it takes everything — without opinion.

Sefa washed the breakfast dishes and stacked the untouched bread under a cloth and made herself useful in the ways that were hers. By nine she had the day's stock counted, the galley squared, and a pot of soup bones simmering for supper, because supper would need to be good tonight. First night out was always hard. People slept badly in new motion, and hungry sleepers dreamed worse.

It was mid-morning when she climbed topside with a thermos, on the pretext of offering coffee and the actual purpose of reading her crew, and found the boat arranged like a diagram of itself. Wendell at the helm, eyes on the water, reading it the way illiterate men read letters — fluently, totally, with her whole history. Bo below, singing to his engine, off-key, happy. Priya amidships with charts spread on the hatch cover, cross-checking, always cross-checking. Ansel leaning on the rail genuinely delighted by the country sliding past. Voss standing apart, watching everything, enjoying nothing.

And Odile Marchetti at the stern rail, elegant as a knife in a drawer, watching the shoreline with an expression Sefa couldn't file anywhere.

Sefa poured her coffee anyway. "Ma'am."

"Thank you." Odile accepted the cup. Up close she smelled of some expensive nothing, soap and ambition. "You've been with Captain Lockridge long?"

"Long enough."

"She speaks highly of your cooking."

"The captain doesn't speak much at all," Sefa said pleasantly. "So when she does, I listen close."

Something moved behind Odile's eyes — reassessment, quick and professional, the look of a woman updating a ledger. "Of course." She sipped. "It must be difficult, this route. After the last time."

And there it was. Polite as a handshake, and Sefa felt it land exactly where it was aimed.

"We're a freight crew, ma'am," Sefa said. "We go where the cargo goes." She took back the thermos, nodded, and walked away unhurried, and only when she reached the companionway did she let her hands shake, just once, and then stop.

Below, in the galley, she stood over her soup pot and let herself think the thought she'd been refusing since the dock: *that woman knows exactly what she bought when she bought this boat.* Not the hull. Not the miles. She bought the wound. She bought Tom Lockridge's ghost, riding along in the hold with the sealed crates, and she bought the one person alive who might be able to make that ghost speak.

Sefa stirred the pot. Salted it. Tasted it. Too much salt — grief did that to her cooking, made her hand heavy — and she corrected it, because correcting things was what she was for.

That night she'd get each of them alone, one at a time, over dishes or cards or the small chores that take two hands. She'd find out what each of them was carrying, and how heavy, and whether any of it was going to blow. Not because she was a spy. Because she was a cook, and a mother once, and a woman who had learned the hardest way there is that secrets rot faster in close quarters than fish does, and when they go bad they poison everybody at the table.

She looked up through the galley window at the river unspooling north, brown and patient, carrying them toward the High Provision and whatever waited there, and she said a small fierce prayer to no one in particular: *let them be ready.*

Behind them, far downstream, the door was already closed. Ahead, the water darkened toward the hills, and the hills stood white-shouldered, and if you listened — Sefa listened, because she listened to everything — you could almost hear, under the engine's song, a long low boom roll down from the ice fields like the first word of a sentence the mountains had been waiting three years to finish.
