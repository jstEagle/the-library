## 7. The Mood of the Water

The river woke before anyone else aboard the *Coriander*, and so did Wendell.

She stood at the helm in the blue hour, coffee going cold in her fist, and listened. That was the first thing they never taught you in pilot school — that a river was mostly sound. The Thessaly spoke in registers: the low conversational mutter of water over gravel, the sharper argument of current against stone, and beneath it all, when you knew how to listen, a kind of hum, like a tuning fork struck somewhere under the world. This morning the hum was a half-tone flat.

"Bar's moved," she said to no one, because no one was there yet, and that was how she liked it.

Three days out from port and the river had already changed its mind about itself twice. Spring melt came down from the high country in pulses — warm afternoons swelling the tributaries, cold nights letting everything settle — and the Thessaly rearranged its furniture accordingly. A channel that had held six feet of water in April might hold four by June. The charts were a year old. The charts were always a year old. Charts were history; the river was now.

Wendell drank her coffee and watched a heron work the shallows on the east bank. It stood too far out for comfort, hip-deep where it should have been knee-deep, stabbing at water that should have been running past its legs instead of pooling around them. Birds didn't read charts either. They just knew.

*East channel's silting in,* she thought. *West side's carrying the flow.*

Behind her, the deck boards creaked — Bo's tread, heavy and deliberate, a man who announced himself to stairs.

"Mist on the water," he said, coming up beside her with his own mug. "Means cold night, warm day coming."

"Means the melt's starting early." She nodded at the heron. "And that bird's standing where it shouldn't be. East bar's grown a foot since we locked through yesterday."

Bo squinted at it. "Charts say six feet through there."

"Charts say a lot of things." She drained her cup. "Wake Sefa. I want us through the east run before the light gets high enough to flatten the water. After that, we'll see about the Narrows approach."

"The east run." Bo said it carefully, the way a man tests ice with one boot. "That's the pretty way."

"That's the *true* way," Wendell said. "This morning, anyway."

He went below without arguing, which was one of the thousand small reasons she'd have taken a bullet for him.

* * *

By seven the mist had burned off into a hard, clean morning, and the *Coriander* slid into the east channel with forty feet to spare on either side.

It was, Wendell would admit privately, showing off. The west channel was wider, deeper on paper, the sensible choice, the choice Odile Marchetti's surveyors would have expected. But she'd watched the heron, and she'd heard the flat note in the river's voice, and thirty years of miles had taught her that the paper was lying.

Priya worked the bow line, coiling and uncoiling with an archivist's neatness even out here where neatness bought nothing. Sefa had come up to watch, arms folded against the morning chill, and Voss stood at the rail with his hands clasped behind him like a man at a funeral, saying nothing, which was fine. Ansel leaned on the starboard rail with her chin in her palm, watching the water go by with an expression Wendell couldn't file anywhere — not tourist wonder, not boredom. Something hungrier than either.

Odile came up last.

Wendell felt her before she saw her — that particular pressure at the back of the neck, three years dormant and apparently still functional. Odile settled at the corner of the pilothouse, out of the way, out of conversation range, wearing expedition khaki that fit her too well to be new and a pair of binoculars she hadn't raised once.

"You're taking the east channel," Odile said.

"I'm taking the east channel."

"My charts show a bar across the middle of it. Six years of surveys."

"Your charts show what the river was doing six years ago." Wendell eased the wheel two degrees starboard, feeling for the current's shoulder the way you'd feel for a horse's mood. "Rivers don't keep records, Odile. They keep grudges. Different filing system entirely."

Something moved at the corner of Odile's mouth. Not quite a smile. An acknowledgment, maybe, filed under the same heading as respect: *things that cost something to give.*

Ahead, the channel bent left around a gravel point, and Wendell saw it — or rather, she saw the water telling on itself. A long seam of ruffled surface downstream, subtle as a scar, where the current ran over a bar shallow enough to comb the boat's keel if she let the river put her there. To an untrained eye it looked like nothing. Wind, maybe. Texture.

To Wendell it was a sentence written in a language she'd been reading since she was nine years old, standing on her father's knees with her small hands inside his big ones on the wheel.

"Hold fast back there," she called. "We're going to thread it."

"Thread what?" Ansel asked, straightening.

"That," Wendell said, and pointed with her chin at the innocent-looking ripple. "Is a gravel bar with ideas above its station. And we're going to pass over it with six inches to spare, because the deep channel on the far side is running two knots faster and I'd rather be slow and dry than fast and sorry."

She worked the throttle down, then up, then down again — feathering the *Coriander* along the seam like a woman easing a splinter out of skin. The boat's hull whispered over the bar. Priya, at the bow, held up one hand: five fingers, then four, then three, counting down depth fathoms by eye against the color of the water, olive to amber to the pale tea-color that meant sand close beneath. At two fingers Wendell gave the engine a breath more power, lifting the bow just enough, and the *Coriander* crossed the bar with a soft shudder, like a dog shaking off rain.

Then the channel opened, deep and green and generous, and Sefa laughed out loud — a short, startled bark of delight — and covered her mouth like she'd sworn an oath against it.

"Every time," Bo said behind Wendell, grinning. "Every single time. I've watched her do this for eleven years and my stomach drops every single time."

"It's supposed to drop," Wendell said. "If your stomach's calm, you're not paying attention."

She glanced at Odile. Odile was looking at the water ahead, but Wendell had caught the look before it got put away — the quick, involuntary assessment, pilot to pilot, the thing that happened between people who shared a craft before either remembered they were supposed to be rivals. Three years ago that look had been easy between them. Now it cost Odile something, and she paid it anyway, and Wendell turned back to the wheel and told herself the warmth in her chest was just the sun coming up.

* * *

They made the mouth of the Kestrel approach by mid-afternoon, and that was where the shortcut presented itself.

Officially, there was no shortcut. Officially, the Kestrel Narrows could only be entered from the lower staging pool — a wide, slow anchorage where boats waited their turn, took on pilots if they needed them, and entered the Narrows through the marked channel, a long S-curve that added half a day and ate rudders for sport. Every chart, every guidebook, every grizzled old-timer at every dock between here and the headwaters said the same thing: *you go around the Horn, or you don't go.*

But Wendell had gone up this river the year Tom died, and the year before that, and the year before that, and she knew something the charts didn't. There was a side slough that cut off the whole lower bend — a braided maze of channels through a drowned willow flat that the guidebooks dismissed as impassable, because in most years it was. In low-water years it went dry. In flood years it became a torrent. But in a year exactly like this one — early melt, steady pulse, water holding at the top of normal — it ran deep enough, and it cut twelve miles and four hours off the approach.

Four hours mattered. Four hours meant entering the Narrows in afternoon light instead of dusk, with the sun high enough to read the water instead of fighting glare off it. On this river, four hours wasn't convenience. It was survival margin.

She laid the charts out on the chart table and weighted the corners with mugs, and Odile came to stand across from her, and for a moment they were just two women looking at the same lie.

"The Horn," Odile said. "My surveyors' report is specific. The Willow Flat route hasn't been run commercially in nine years. The last attempt wrapped a barge around a cottonwood."

"The last attempt was skippered by a man who navigated by confidence." Wendell tapped the slough's entrance on the chart. "There's a window. Melt's peaking midday, dropping overnight. If we enter the flat at first light tomorrow, we get the tail end of the high water — enough draft, not enough speed. By ten o'clock it starts falling and the window closes till next season."

"And if your window's wrong?"

"Then we spend a very quiet afternoon aground in a willow flat, waiting for the evening rise, and you get to tell me you told me so for the rest of the trip." Wendell met her eyes. "Which, given the rest of the trip, might take a while."

Odile studied her. Wendell let herself be studied. This was the part she'd missed, she realized, and hated realizing — the chess of it, two minds pushing against each other until one of them found the true shape of the problem. With anyone else it would have been a fight. With Odile it had always been something closer to a duet, and that was precisely the danger of her.

"The crew follows you," Odile said finally. Not a question. An observation, made carefully, the way you'd note the position of a piece on a board.

"The crew follows whoever's right," Wendell said. "So far that's been me. Ask me again at the Narrows."

* * *

She called the crew meeting at dinner, because decisions went down easier with Sefa's food underneath them.

"Tomorrow we don't take the Horn," she said, while Sefa ladled out stew and everyone pretended not to watch the two surveyors for their reactions. "We cut through Willow Flat. Saves half a day, puts us at the Narrows entrance with good light."

Silence, of the working kind. Bo chewed. Priya set down her fork.

"The flat that's on the charts as 'impassable,'" Priya said.

"The very one."

"The flat where the Danner barge—"

"Different year, different water, different skipper." Wendell kept her voice level. "I'm not asking for a vote. I'm telling you what we're doing, and I'm telling you why: light and margin. But I'll tell you the risk too, because you're not passengers. If I misjudge the window, we're hard aground for six hours minimum, possibly longer, and we burn a day of Odile's schedule doing it."

"Schedule," Sefa said mildly, from the galley doorway. "Whose schedule is it, exactly? Because I notice the woman who wrote the contract doesn't cook, doesn't stand watches, and has opinions about rivers she reads off paper."

"Sefa." Wendell said it gently.

"No, it's a fair question." Sefa wiped her hands on her apron, unhurried. "I've been on this boat eleven years. In that time I've learned one rule: the river tells you what it's doing, and the person who listens best gets to decide. So. Has anyone else been listening?" She looked around the table. "Because I have. And this morning our captain stood on deck for twenty minutes watching a bird stand in the wrong place, and then she took us through a channel your charts call dead, and we're all still dry. So as far as I'm concerned, the listening's been done."

Nobody said anything. Ansel was smiling faintly into her stew. Voss ate the way he did everything — completely, silently, thoroughly.

"We'll need everyone on lines tomorrow," Wendell said. "Willow Flat is a poling job in the narrow sections. Long poles, push bars, everybody works. Questions?"

"One," Priya said. "Why do I feel like you're proving something?"

Wendell held her gaze. "Because I am," she said. "Just not to you."

* * *

First light came up gold and smoke-colored over the willow flats, and the *Coriander* turned off the main channel into water no commercial boat had touched in nine years.

It was beautiful, which Wendell distrusted immediately. The slough braided through drowned forest — silver willows standing hip-deep in glass-still water, every tree doubled perfectly in the reflection, so the world seemed to extend forever in both directions. Mist hung in the branches. Somewhere a loon called, that ancient, unhinged laughter, and got no answer.

Then the beauty ended and the work began.

The channels narrowed to boat-widths. Current came at them from angles that made no sense, sliding off unseen bars, doubling back on itself. Twice Wendell killed the engines entirely and had them pole through silence, the crew leaning into push-bars, the *Coriander* ghosting between willows so close that leaves brushed the rail. She navigated by reading the surface the way other people read print: that dark seam meant depth, that pale fan meant sand, that line of bubbles meant the current had just come off a ledge and was diving deep. Once she stopped the boat altogether, backed water fifteen feet, and took a different fork on instinct alone — and thirty seconds later Priya, at the bow, reported a strainer dead ahead on the fork they'd abandoned, a fallen cottonwood combing the entire channel, sharp as a trap.

Nobody said anything about it. But Wendell felt the boat change behind her, the way a crew changes when fear becomes faith. Hands moved faster. Lines came cleated without being asked for. Bo started anticipating throttle calls before she made them.

Ansel worked the hardest of anyone. Whatever else she was — whatever she and Voss were actually doing on this boat, whatever the sealed crates held — the woman hauled line like someone paying off a debt, sweat-soaked and silent, and when they cleared the worst of the flat and Wendell called a rest, Ansel sat down on the deck plating and laughed, breathless and real.

"I thought it would be like the pictures," she said, to no one in particular. "It's not like the pictures at all."

"No," Wendell said. "Pictures are taken by people who arrived after the work."

At 9:40 in the morning, four hours saved, the *Coriander* slid out of the willows into the lower staging pool, and the marked channel to the Kestrel Narrows lay open ahead — broad, official, and empty, because every other boat on the river was still rounding the Horn behind them.

Odile stood at the rail. She'd watched the whole passage without speaking, and now she lowered her binoculars — finally, actually using them, though there was nothing left to look at — and turned to Wendell.

"My surveyors are updating their report," she said.

"They should. It's wrong in at least two places."

"I meant about you." Odile said it evenly, but her eyes stayed on Wendell a beat too long, and there was something in them Wendell refused to name — something older than this contract, warmer than this river, and much more dangerous than either. "The report said you were careful. Careful is a small word. Whoever wrote it was being polite."

"Careful keeps crews alive."

"So does whatever that was." Odile gestured back at the willows, at the impossible morning. Then, quieter, almost to herself: "Tom used to say you could hear the river thinking."

The name landed like a hook set deep. Wendell kept her hands on the wheel and her face toward the water, and gave the current ahead of them her full attention, and said nothing at all.

Because that was the trouble with Odile Marchetti. She never lied about the things that didn't matter. Which made it very hard to know what to believe about the things that did.

Ahead, the walls of the Kestrel Narrows rose up to meet them, and the water between them went suddenly, ominously quiet.
