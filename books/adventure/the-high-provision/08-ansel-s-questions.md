## 8. Ansel's Questions

Priya had a system for the night watch, and the system was this: tea at the top of the hour, a slow circuit of the deck, and then forty minutes in the wheelhouse with her back to the river, because watching black water slide past in the dark for too long made her feel like she was standing still while the world moved — an illusion she'd never learned to tolerate.

The Coriander ran quiet at night. Bo throttled down after dark to spare the old engine, so the boat moved at the speed of gossip, which meant there was time for everything: time to hear the water combing against the hull, time to count the stars that survived the haze off the lowlands, time to notice when the cabin door opened behind her and Ansel came out wrapped in a blanket like a woman escaping her own bed.

"Couldn't sleep," Ansel said, by way of explanation or apology. She was holding two mugs. "I made too much tea. Occupational hazard of being raised in a big family."

Priya accepted the mug because refusing it would have required a conversation about why, and she didn't have one. The tea was good — cardamom, honey, too sweet, the way Sefa disapproved of and Ansel apparently hadn't learned better. "There's a chair," Priya said, nodding at the second stool in the wheelhouse, the one Tom used to sit in, though she didn't say that part.

Ansel sat. She was younger than Priya had first guessed — mid-thirties maybe, with laugh lines already cut deep and a way of occupying space that suggested she'd grown up in a house where you had to be loud to be heard. Voss, by contrast, occupied space like a man apologizing for it. Priya had catalogued them both in the first two days, the way she'd once catalogued accessions at the provincial archive: provenance unknown, condition fair, handle with caution.

"This is the best watch," Ansel said, looking out at the river. "Sefa told me. She said if you can't sleep, come find Priya, because Priya doesn't talk your ear off."

"Sefa talks enough for both of us."

"She does." Ansel laughed, and the laugh was easy, unguarded, and Priya found herself disliking how much she liked it. "She fed us leftover rice pudding tonight and told us about the time Bo flooded the galley trying to fix a coffee maker."

"He did flood the galley."

"How long have you all been together?"

"Four years. Me and Sefa. Bo came aboard three years ago, spring."

Something moved through Ansel's face then — quick, controlled, gone — and Priya, who had spent six years reading marginalia for a living, caught it the way she'd catch a smudged date in a ledger. Not surprise. Recognition. Like Ansel had known the answer before she asked.

"And before that?" Ansel said. "Was there a crew before you?"

"Why do you ask?"

"No reason. I just — boats like this feel lived-in. Like a house with old photographs on the wall. You wonder who took them." Ansel turned the mug in her hands. "Odile said the Coriander used to run this route years ago. Before my time with her company. She said it was famous up here."

"Famous is generous."

"What was it famous *for*?"

Priya looked out at the water. A log turned over in the current off the starboard bow, pale belly flashing, and slid under again. Behind them, downriver, the country they'd left was flat farmland and drowned willows; ahead, invisible in the dark, the valley walls were already beginning to gather themselves up toward the high country. Three more days to the narrows. Nine, if the weather held kind, to the calving fields.

"We ran freight," Priya said. "Whatever paid. Seed grain, fuel drums, mail when the post boats wouldn't go. Captain Lockridge had a reputation for taking loads nobody else would take and getting them there anyway."

"Captain Lockridge," Ansel repeated. "That's Wendell?"

"Wendell Lockridge, yes."

"And there was another one? A brother?" Ansel's voice stayed light, conversational, but Priya heard the careful placement of it now, the way you'd set a foot on ice you suspected was thin. "I thought I heard Sefa say something. Or maybe I imagined it."

"You didn't imagine it."

"Tom," Ansel said softly. "Tom Lockridge."

The name sat between them. Priya drank her tea and let it sit. This was a skill she'd learned in the archive too, though she'd never thought of it as a skill until later: the patience to let silence do work. People filled silences. People told you things in silences they'd never tell you in questions.

Ansel lasted eleven seconds. "I'm sorry. That was clumsy of me. Odile mentioned — when she hired us, she gave us background on the boat, the route, the crew. Standard briefing. And she said the Coriander hadn't run the upper river since—" She stopped herself, visibly, like someone catching a door before it swung wide. "Since some accident. She didn't give details. She said it wasn't hers to tell."

*Since some accident.* Priya turned the phrase over. It was true, technically. It was also shaped wrong. Odile Marchetti struck Priya as a woman who chose every word the way Wendell chose a channel — testing depth before committing weight. If Odile had wanted to say *since the accident*, she'd have said it. If she'd wanted to say nothing, she'd have said nothing. *Some accident* was a door left open exactly the width of a person.

"That was considerate of her," Priya said.

"Wasn't it." Ansel smiled, and for a moment the smile looked tired around the edges, like a lamp burning low. Then it rekindled. "So what happened? If it's not rude to ask. I know it's probably rude to ask."

"It's a little rude."

"I grew up with five brothers. Rude is how we say hello."

Priya laughed despite herself — a short, surprised sound — and hated that she'd laughed, and understood in the same instant that this was precisely what Ansel was for. Voss was a locked room. Ansel was a warm kitchen. You told kitchens things.

"The Coriander went upriver three years ago," Priya said. "Spring run. High water. There was an incident above the calving fields. The boat came back damaged. Tom Lockridge didn't come back at all." She kept her voice level, archival, the tone she used for reading casualty lists aloud. "Wendell was hurt too. Not in the body. In the body she was fine."

"In the head," Ansel said quietly.

"She lost the last day. All of it. From morning to whenever it ended. She woke up in a hospital in Reprieve with a bandage on her scalp and no memory of why her brother was dead. The doctors called it traumatic amnesia. Wendell calls it Tuesday." Priya heard the bitterness arrive in her own voice and didn't chase it out. "We don't talk about it much. We talk around it. Sefa cooks around it. Bo repairs around it. I archive around it."

"Around it," Ansel repeated. "That's a good word for it."

"What did Odile tell you about the route?"

"Just that it opens late and closes early, and that this year might be the last year at all. Glacier's retreating weird, she said. Calving season's coming sooner. After this season the high route closes for good." Ansel shrugged inside her blanket. "Hence the rush. Hence the triple pay. Hence two surveyors nobody asked for."

"Hence," Priya agreed.

They sat a while. The river murmured its one long sentence. Somewhere aft, Bo's radio murmured a station from downcountry, too faint to make out, a man's voice dissolving into static like sugar into tea.

"Do you ever think about what happened?" Ansel asked. "The missing day. Whether it'll come back."

"Every day," Priya said, before she could decide not to be honest. "I think about whether it should. Wendell says she doesn't want it back. She says the river already took its toll and memory would just be paying interest." She paused. "I don't believe her. I've watched her not-want-it for three years. Nobody works that hard at not wanting something unless they're afraid of wanting it."

Ansel looked at her for a long moment, and something in her expression shifted — softened, or saddened, or both. "You're loyal to her," she said. It wasn't a question either.

"I'm loyal to the boat," Priya said. "The boat happens to contain her."

* * *

Morning came up gray and gold at once, mist standing in the sloughs like an audience waiting to be seated. Priya went off watch at six, slept badly for four hours, and woke with Ansel's voice still running in her head like a stuck groove: *she gave us background on the boat, the route, the crew.*

Background on the crew. Standard briefing.

She stood at the galley counter while Sefa fried something involving onions and yesterday's potatoes, and turned the phrase over and over like a stone with something written on it. Because here was the thing Priya kept circling: a briefing on the route made sense. Water levels, hazards, timing. A briefing on the *boat* made sense — draft, engine quirks, where the leaks were. But background on the *crew*? What did two contract surveyors need to know about a cook and an engineer and a deckhand?

Unless the surveyors weren't surveyors.

Unless the cargo wasn't cargo.

Or unless — and this was the version Priya liked least — Odile had briefed them on the crew because the crew was relevant. Because whatever had happened three years ago involved these people, this boat, this family, and Odile had wanted her hires to understand exactly whose grief they were sailing through.

"Priya." Sefa slid a plate in front of her. "You've got the face."

"What face?"

"The face you get when you're doing arithmetic with people instead of numbers. Eat."

Priya ate. The potatoes were excellent, as they always were, and she resented them slightly for it. Through the galley's aft window she could see the foredeck, where Wendell stood at the rail with a chart case under her arm, talking distances with the morning. Beyond her, Ansel was coiling line with genuine competence, laughing at something Bo said, fitting herself into the boat's rhythms like she'd been born to them.

That was the other thing. Priya had worked enough reference desks to know the difference between someone learning a place and someone remembering it. Ansel learned fast — but she learned fast in the specific way of a person being reminded. Her hands knew hitches before her eyes studied them. She'd called the head rope the *spring line* on her first morning, unprompted, and Priya had filed it and forgotten it, and only now, with the whole shape of the woman assembled, did the file open itself.

"Where's Wendell going?" Sefa asked, following her gaze.

"To find out what we're carrying," Priya said, "or to keep not finding out. One of those." She drained her tea. "Actually — send her to the wheelhouse when she's done. I need to report something."

Sefa's eyebrows went up. "Report? We taking turns playing captain now?"

"It's watch business."

"It's never watch business with you." But Sefa waved her off with the spatula, and Priya climbed to the wheelhouse and waited, and the waiting had a texture she recognized from her archiving days: the texture of knowing a document was misfiled somewhere and not yet knowing which drawer.

Wendell came up twenty minutes later, chart case under her arm, hair tied back wet from washing her face. She had the particular economy of motion she always had in the wheelhouse — home waters, even when the waters were strange. "Sefa said you wanted me. Report."

"Ansel couldn't sleep last night. She brought tea. We talked."

"That's not a crime yet."

"She asked about the boat's history. I deflected. She asked about Tom." Priya watched Wendell's hands on the chart case — the knuckles, the grip, the small recalibration of pressure. "I told her the public version. Damaged boat, lost brother, lost day. She took it well. Apologized for prying. Said Odile had briefed them before hiring — background on the boat, the route, the crew."

Wendell went very still.

It was a specific stillness, and Priya had seen it exactly twice before: once when a gravel bar appeared where the charts swore there was channel, and once when a letter had come addressed to T. Lockridge care of the Coriander, two years after he died. Stillness like a held breath underwater. Stillness like a woman listening for the pitch of water over stone.

"Briefed them on the crew," Wendell said.

"On all of us. By name, apparently. Ansel knew yours. She knew Tom's." Priya paused. "She knew there *was* a Tom."

The wheelhouse clock ticked. Down on the foredeck, Ansel's laughter came up through the deck boards, bright and ordinary, and Priya watched Wendell listen to it with an expression that had nothing ordinary anywhere in it.

"Surveyors," Wendell said at last. The word came out flat, weighed, found wanting. "He hired surveyors who needed a briefing on my dead brother."

"Odile hired them."

"Odile." Wendell set the chart case down on the shelf with exaggerated care, the way she did anything when her hands wanted to do otherwise. "Three years ago somebody knew this boat well enough to hurt it. Now somebody's hired help knows this boat well enough to brief on it. I keep telling myself those are different somebodies." She turned to the window. The river ran green-gray and confident below, all its secrets moving too fast to see. "Tell me exactly what she said. Word for word. You're the only one on this boat who keeps word-for-word."

So Priya gave it to her verbatim — the briefing, the *some accident*, the door left open exactly the width of a person, the hands that knew knots before they were taught. Wendell listened without interrupting, which was itself remarkable; Wendell interrupted weather. When Priya finished, the captain was quiet for a long moment, and then she said, in a voice stripped of everything except decision:

"Nobody touches her. Nobody ices her out, nobody gives her the cold shoulder, nobody changes how we treat her. If she's fishing, I want her to keep casting." She picked the chart case back up. "And from tonight, somebody's awake on the surveyors' cabin passage every watch. Quietly. Bo can rig the bilge fan belt to squeal past their door — he'll enjoy that."

"You think they're here for the cargo."

"I think," Wendell said, "that I signed a contract with a sealed hold, a false manifest, and two passengers who studied my family before they ever saw my boat. I think the past just knocked politely at the door, and polite isn't how the past usually knocks." She rolled the chart open across the wheel, pinning the corners with mugs, and her finger came down on the river ahead — on the narrowing valley, on the first inked hatchings of the Kestrel Narrows, two days out. "But whoever they are, they're on my boat, in my channel, in my season. So we keep running. We keep watching. And when they slip — because people always slip —"

She didn't finish it. She didn't have to. Priya nodded, gathered her mug, and was halfway down the wheelhouse stairs when Wendell's voice stopped her, quieter now, almost to herself:

"Priya. The briefing Odile gave them. Did Ansel say when Odile wrote it?"

"...No. Why?"

"Because there are only three people alive who could brief anyone on this crew." Wendell was looking at the chart, but Priya would have sworn she was looking through it, down through paper and water and three years of silt, at the bottom of the missing day. "Me. Sefa. And the woman who was there the night my brother died — and remembers every minute of it."

The fan belt began, faintly, to squeal.
