## 9. First Rapids

The Kestrel Narrows announced themselves an hour before they arrived.

Wendell felt it in the deck first — a new tremor under her boots, the Coriander's hull picking up a conversation with water that had turned from talk to argument. Then the sound came downriver to meet them: not the broad white roar of open rapids but something tighter, angrier, a noise like a crowd pressing through a doorway. She had heard it once before, three years and a lifetime ago, and her body remembered it before her mind did. Her hands went cold on the wheel.

"Bo," she called down the engine hatch. "Full pressure on the cooling. We're going to be running hot."

"Running hot's my middle name," Bo said, though his voice had gone careful in the way it did when he was worried and didn't want anyone counting.

The Narrows were a geological grudge match: two walls of granite leaning toward each other over half a mile of river that should have been three miles wide. The Thessaly came off the high country carrying everything the glaciers gave it, and where the canyon pinched, the river quit being a river and became a decision made at speed. There were three channels through. The postcards showed the left one, wide and photogenic. The charts Odile had provided marked all three with the same weary annotation: *impassable at seasonal flow.*

The charts were wrong about the right channel. They'd been wrong for forty years, ever since some surveyor in a canvas jacket had looked at the standing wave at its mouth and gone home to write *impassable* instead of *difficult.* Wendell knew because Tom had known, because Tom had run it twice with their father before Wendell was old enough to hold a tiller, and because Tom had drawn it for her once on a napkin in a dockside bar, laughing, saying *you don't fight the wave, Wren, you ask it nicely.*

She hadn't thought of that napkin in years. It surfaced now, whole and bright, and she pushed it back down where she kept everything else that belonged to him.

Odile stood at the rail amidships, binoculars up, watching the canyon wall slide closer. Voss and Ansel flanked her. Ansel looked genuinely excited; Voss looked like a man doing arithmetic he didn't like the answer to.

"You're taking the right side," Odile said. Not a question. Her voice carried easily over the building water.

"I'm taking the right side."

"The chart says—"

"The chart says a lot of things." Wendell kept her eyes forward. "Left channel's got a strainer across it — deadfall jammed in the throat, probably since spring flood. Center's a washing machine this time of year. Right channel wants respect, but it pays out."

"And if you're wrong?"

"Then we'll all have a very short conversation about it." She raised her voice. "Priya! Lines off the rails, everything lashed. Sefa, secure the galley — I mean *everything*, including yourself."

Sefa appeared at the cabin door wiping her hands on her apron, took one look at the water ahead, and said something in her grandmother's language that Wendell was fairly sure was a blessing and possibly also a curse. She disappeared inside. A minute later there came the sounds of pots being nested and latched, the small domestic percussion of a cook preparing to be thrown around her own kitchen.

Priya worked the deck with quick hands, coiling and cleating, checking every line twice. When she passed the helm she paused. "You've done this before," she said. It wasn't quite a question either.

"I've done it before."

"With him?"

The name went unspoken between them, the way it always did. Wendell nodded once. Priya squeezed her shoulder — brief, hard, no words wasted — and went forward to lash the last of the crates.

The canyon swallowed them.

It happened fast, the way canyons do. One moment there was sky; the next, granite rose on both sides close enough to touch, wet and streaked with mineral stain, and the light went the color of pewter. The river narrowed from a hundred yards to sixty to forty, and the current picked the Coriander up like a hand closing around her stern. Wendell felt the boat accelerate and let it. This was the first law of fast water, older than engines: you do not brake where the river is deciding things. You steer. You commit. You become part of the argument instead of an obstacle to it.

The right channel opened at the canyon's elbow, exactly as Tom's napkin had promised — a slot maybe thirty feet wide between a submerged shelf on the left and a rooster-tail eddy fence on the right. At its mouth stood the wave. It was bigger than she remembered, or she was smaller than she used to be. Fifteen feet of green water standing still while the river poured through it, cresting into foam, folding and unfolding like something alive considering whether to eat them.

"Ask it nicely," she murmured, and put the nose square into the gap.

For eleven seconds — she would count them later, lying awake, the way she counted everything — the Coriander belonged to the river.

The bow climbed. The world tilted until the canyon wall filled the windows and the sky was a gray stripe overhead. Somewhere below her feet Bo was feeding the engine everything it had, and the boat dug in and *climbed*, spray sheeting over the pilothouse glass, and for one suspended heartbeat they were weightless, hanging on the wave's shoulder —

— and then the crest broke beneath them and the Coriander plunged down the far side into the chute, and the canyon became a blur of speed and noise, and Wendell flew.

This was the part nobody understood who hadn't done it. This was the part she could never explain to people who measured a good life in solid ground and steady pay. For eleven seconds there was no grief, no debt, no sealed cargo, no woman named Odile Marchetti standing amidships with her secrets. There was only water and wheel and the animal joy of being exactly, precisely, perfectly matched to the moment. Tom had called it *flying the boat*. He had laughed when he said it. She could hear him now, under the roar, laughing.

The chute spat them out into a long pool above the second drop, and the roar fell away behind them, and Wendell realized she was grinning like an idiot and let herself do it for three more seconds before she put the face back on.

"Second drop's technical," she called aft. "Tight S-turn, then we're through. Stay braced."

That was when Voss lost his nerve.

Later, everyone would describe it differently. Priya would say he moved before anyone understood what he was doing. Bo would say he'd been watching Wendell's hands on the wheel for the whole first drop and something in his face had been coming apart by degrees. Ansel would say nothing at all, which told Wendell more than anything else. But in the moment, all Wendell saw was movement in the mirror — a tall shape coming up the starboard side at a dead run, past Priya's startled grab, past the rail, into the pilothouse.

"He's going to hit the—" Priya shouted.

Voss grabbed the wheel.

His hands closed over hers — not gently, not asking, *taking*, hauling the wheel hard to port while the boat sat in the pool above the second drop, and the Coriander's nose swung away from the channel line toward the submerged shelf, and Wendell saw the whole future in a quarter second: the hull striking the shelf edge-on, the boat broaching, rolling, six people in fifteen-degree water moving at twenty knots through a granite canyon.

There was no time to think and no need to. Her body had been trained by thirty years of rivers, and her body did the arithmetic.

She slammed her hip against the console to anchor, wrenched the wheel back to starboard with her left hand, and drove her right forearm up and outward into Voss's grip — not a punch, never a punch, a lever, using his own panic as the fulcrum, bending his wrist back over the rail's steel lip with the full force of the boat's roll behind it.

Something cracked. It sounded, absurdly, like a green branch in winter.

Voss screamed and let go and folded against the pilothouse door, cradling his hand, and Wendell had the wheel alone again with the second drop rising in the glass, and there was no time for any of it — no time for anger or apology or the shaking that wanted to start in her shoulders. The S-turn was *now*.

She flew it. Hard starboard around the first bend with the rocks combing foam off the bank close enough to spit on, then a full-lock port through the seam where two currents braided together, the Coriander heeling, Sefa's voice from the galley below saying something rhythmic and calm that might have been a prayer or a recipe, and then the canyon opened its hands and let them go, and the Thessaly spread out below into wide, glittering, ordinary water, and it was over.

Wendell set the boat on autopilot lock, checked the heading twice, and only then turned around.

Voss sat on the pilothouse deck with his back against the door, holding his right wrist against his chest. His face was the color of tallow. The wrist was already swelling, thickening under the skin like dough rising. Broken clean, she judged. Maybe both bones.

Priya stood over him with a look Wendell had never seen her wear — fury held very carefully, the way you'd hold a lit fuse. Ansel had come up the steps and stopped halfway, one hand on the rail, looking between Voss and Wendell with an expression that wasn't shock. That was the thing Wendell kept snagging on, later. Ansel didn't look shocked. She looked like someone watching a bill come due.

"You could have killed us," Priya said to Voss. Quiet. Worse than shouting. "All of us. You put your hands on her wheel in whitewater."

"I thought—" Voss's voice came out in pieces. "I thought she was going to hit the wave wrong. I thought I could—"

"You thought." Wendell crouched to his level. Up close she could see he was younger than she'd taken him for, mid-thirties maybe, and that under the fear there was something worse than fear: shame, arriving early and planning to stay. "Mr. Voss. On this boat, in this water, there is one wheel and one pair of hands on it. Those hands have run this river longer than you've been alive. The day I need help steering, I'll ask for it. Until then, you touch that wheel again and I will put you ashore wherever we happen to be, contract or no contract, and Odile can swim upriver after her cargo. Do you understand me?"

He nodded. His throat worked. "I'm sorry," he said, and here was the surprise — he meant it. She could hear it. Whatever had sent him lunging for that wheel hadn't been malice. It had been terror, plain and stupid and human, the terror of a man who had signed onto something without understanding what the water would demand of him.

"Tape it, splint it, Priya knows how," Wendell said, standing. "And Mr. Voss — next time you're scared, you tell me *before* the rapids. Fear's allowed on my boat. Grabbing isn't."

She went back to the wheel.

* * *

They made camp that night on a gravel bar below the Narrows, a long pale crescent of stone and driftwood with the canyon glowing rust-colored in the evening light behind them. The Coriander sat anchored in the slack water off the bar, her running lights doubled, and Sefa built a fire in the driftwood windbreak she'd spent two hours constructing because, as she put it, *a crew that eats badly thinks badly, and a crew that thinks badly dies.*

Voss sat apart with his splinted wrist propped on a crate, dosed on Sefa's willow-bark tea because nobody had thought to bring proper painkillers on a survey expedition, which told Wendell yet another small lie about what these two actually were. Surveyors carried medical kits. Smugglers carried manifests. Whatever Voss and Ansel carried, it wasn't field equipment.

Odile came to the fire late, after the plates were cleared, when the others had drifted to their bedrolls and only embers and Wendell remained. Wendell had known she would. Odile Marchetti did not leave debts sitting overnight, even small ones. Especially small ones. Small ones were cheap to settle and bought goodwill at a discount.

"That was well handled," Odile said, settling onto the log across the fire. "Both the water and the man."

"Which man?"

"Any of them." Odile smiled. It was a good smile. It had been disarming for thirty years and it was disarming now, and Wendell hated how much of her still responded to it, some old muscle memory of friendship turning toward warmth like a plant toward a window. "But I meant Voss. You broke his wrist and kept your boat straight through a class four S-turn, and you managed to make it look like the same motion. I've watched pilots freeze when a passenger touches the wheel. I've watched them freeze when a *wave* touches the wheel."

"I don't freeze."

"No," Odile said softly. "You never did. It's the most infuriating thing about you."

Wendell fed a stick of driftwood to the embers and waited. With Odile, waiting was always rewarded. Silence made her generous.

"Hazard pay doubles tomorrow," Odile said. "Effective from the Narrows. I'll amend the contract in the morning."

"We haven't hit the hard part yet."

"I know what's ahead, Wendell." A pause, and then, deliberately lighter: "High Water Country. The meltwater stretch. I've read the reports."

"You've read reports." Wendell looked at her across the fire. "I ran that stretch twice before the accident. Once with Tom. The river up there doesn't read like any water south of the calving fields. It changes hour to hour with the melt. You can't navigate it by sight — the water's too opaque with rock flour, too fast, the channels move overnight. You navigate it by ear. By the pitch of the water over stone." She poked the fire. "My father taught Tom. Tom taught me. Nobody else on this river does it anymore, because everybody else who tried it is at the bottom of it."

"Then it's fortunate," Odile said, "that I hired the one pilot who can."

"It's fortunate you hired the one pilot whose brother died learning it." Wendell said it flatly, without heat, and watched it land. Odile's face did something very small and very controlled — a tightening around the eyes, a fractional stillness — and then smoothed over, and Wendell thought: *there. Again. Every time I say his name, that flicker. Grief? Guilt? Or just an old wound of her own?*

Or calculation. She couldn't rule it out. That was the trouble with Odile Marchetti; she had taught Wendell everything she knew about reading people, and had never once shown her own pages.

"Doubled hazard pay," Odile repeated, rising, brushing gravel from her coat. "And Wendell — what you did today, with Voss. I want you to know I understand why you couldn't tolerate it. Why you reacted the way you did."

"Do you."

"I do." Odile's voice went quiet, almost gentle, and that gentleness was the most dangerous thing she'd produced all day. "You've lost enough to other people's hands on the wheel."

She walked back toward the surveyors' tents, her lantern bobbing away across the dark bar, and Wendell sat alone with the fire and turned the sentence over like a stone found in a fish's belly.

It was, on its face, a kindness. An acknowledgment. *I know what the accident cost you; I know why a stranger grabbing your wheel would feel like the end of the world.*

And underneath it, threaded through it so finely you'd miss it unless you were listening for thread: *I know what happened that night. I know things about that night that you don't remember.*

Wendell stared into the embers until they went from orange to gray, and the river talked to itself in the dark, patient and enormous, carrying everything downstream toward the sea — the meltwater, the silt, the broken green branches, and every secret that anybody had ever trusted to the current, certain the water would keep it.

Behind her, from the direction of the surveyors' tents, she heard low voices. Voss's, strained. And Ansel's — clear, urgent, and sharp enough that one word crossed the gravel bar to her distinctly before the wind changed:

"—before the season turns."

Wendell went very still.

Then she banked the fire, checked the anchor line twice, and went below to write the day into the ship's log, in her own hand, in ink, the way her father had taught her: *Ran the Kestrel right channel. All hands safe. One wrist broken, mine to answer for.*

She underlined *mine to answer for* and sat looking at it a long time, wondering which of them it was true for.
