## 10. What Bo Found Below

The intake fouled on the third morning above Reprieve, which was the kind of luck Bo had stopped believing in and started merely cataloguing. He felt it before he heard it — a flutter in the deck plates under his boots, a stutter in the port engine's note like a singer clearing her throat — and by the time Wendell rang down from the pilothouse with that two-bell signal she used instead of words, he already had his tool roll open on the workbench.

"Port's running rough," she said through the voice tube, though she didn't need to say it. She never needed to say it. That was the thing about the Coriander: the boat talked to both of them, and they'd learned to listen in different rooms.

"I know," Bo said. "Give me an hour."

"River gives you forty minutes. There's a slack eddy past the bend."

"Then I'll be fast."

He wasn't fast. He was thorough, which on the Coriander amounted to the same thing over time.

Bo Okonkwo had been turning wrenches for thirty-one years, first on his uncle's dredge out of Kettle Falls, then on tugs, then on anything that floated and complained. He knew engines the way priests knew scripture — not just the words but the silences between them, the places where meaning lived. The Coriander's port engine was a Detroit Diesel older than Priya, temperamental as a cat in rain, and it had been telling him for two days that something was wrong with its breathing. An engine that can't breathe is an engine that's about to die, and Bo did not let things die if he could help it. Not anymore.

The intake grate sat below the waterline amidships, fed by a sea chest that hadn't been properly cleaned since — well, since before Tom Lockridge died, if Bo was honest, because after that nobody had wanted to go into certain parts of this boat. The river up here carried glacial flour, fine gray silt that got everywhere, and woven through it came the usual river debris: leaves, twigs, a plastic bag from some town downstream, the occasional dead fish. Something had packed itself against the grate and starved the pump.

He killed the port engine, closed the seacock, and dropped down into the bilge space beneath the hold floor with a headlamp and a pry bar, folding his long body into a gap built for a thinner man. The bilge of an old riverboat is a country unto itself — low ceilings of greased steel, pipes running overhead like exposed nerves, everything slicked with a film of oil and river water that smelled of iron and something older, something mineral and cold. Up here, north of Reprieve, the bilge smelled of glacier. It was a smell Bo associated with bad news.

The grate came off easy. The clog was worse than expected — not debris but a mat of fibrous material, pale and stringy, that he hauled out in fistfuls and piled on the plate beside him. Some kind of root fiber, maybe, torn loose from the banks upstream. He'd burn it later and see how it smelled. Riverboats taught you to read their garbage like tea leaves.

With the grate off, he ran his hand along the inside of the sea chest, feeling for scoring or cracks, and that was when his knuckles hit something that shouldn't have been there.

Steel. Flat steel, welded smooth, where the hull plating should have shown rivets.

Bo went still. In the dark under the hold, with only his headlamp making a small yellow island around him, he put his palm flat against the surface and held it there like a man taking a pulse.

Every boat has secrets. Bo had worked on enough of them to know that a welded-over compartment was usually one of three things: smugglers' space, ballast correction, or a repair so ugly the owner had decided to bury it rather than explain it. The Coriander had been owned outright by Wendell for eleven years, and before that by a salvage consortium out of Grayling that had gone bust. Before *that* — Bo didn't know, and had never asked, because boats are like people that way; you take them as they come to you.

But he'd been aboard six years now. Six years of crawling every inch of this hull, and he had never once found this seam.

He lay there in the muck and thought about it. Thought about the sealed crates stacked three high in the hold directly above his head, stamped with a mining logo he couldn't find in any registry, one of which hummed when you leaned against it. Thought about Odile Marchetti standing at the rail with her expensive coat and her expensive calm, watching the river like she was reading a letter she'd written herself. Thought about the surveyors, Voss with his quiet hands and Ansel with her laugh that switched off too clean, like a radio finding silence between stations.

And he thought about Tom Lockridge, whom Bo had liked enormously and known barely at all, because Tom had died three years before Bo signed on, and the Coriander's crew did not talk about him. They didn't not-talk about him either. He simply occupied a room in the boat's memory that everyone walked past without looking in. His tools were still racked in the engine room — Bo used them daily, kept them sharp, never moved them. His mug was on the hook by the galley door, chipped blue enamel, and Sefa washed it every week and hung it back up, and no one said why.

Bo fetched the angle grinder.

Not to cut — not yet. He fitted a wire wheel instead and went at the weld seam slow, cleaning it back to bare metal, reading it the way Wendell read water. Welds tell stories. This one was good work, professional, done with care — but it was done in haste too, the beads overlapping unevenly at the corners where a man had been working against something. Time, probably. Or weather. Or fear.

There was a seam line, faint, where a plate had been fitted over an opening. And at the lower corner, almost invisible under grime, a small square of brighter metal: the ghost of a removed label, or a handle mount.

Bo sat back on his heels in the bilge sludge and looked at it for a long time.

Above him, muffled through three layers of steel and wood, he could hear the boat living. Sefa's radio playing low in the galley, some old crooner half-swallowed by static. Priya's footsteps crossing the deck on watch. The river itself, pressing patiently against the hull from outside, the way it always did, the way it would long after all of them were gone.

He should go get Wendell. That was the correct move, the loyal move, the move the man he tried to be would make without hesitation. Find the captain, show her the seam, let her decide. It was her boat. Her grief. Her brother.

His hand stayed on the grinder.

Here was the thing about loyalty that Bo had learned late and paid for early: loyalty is not obedience. Loyalty is wanting what's best for someone even when it costs you their good opinion. And Bo had watched Wendell Lockridge for six years now, watched her run grief like she ran the river — at speed, eyes forward, never looking at the thing directly lest it pull her under. He had watched her sign Odile Marchetti's contract with a face like a woman signing her own death warrant and calling it paperwork. He had watched her flinch — actually flinch, a full-body thing she buried in half a second — when Priya asked how far upriver they were going.

If there was a compartment under his feet with Tom Lockridge's name on it — and some cold, sure part of Bo already knew there was — then Wendell either knew about it or she didn't. And he genuinely could not say which possibility frightened him more.

He started grinding.

It took forty minutes. The wire wheel walked the seam, throwing orange sparks that died in the bilge water, and slowly the weld gave up its secrets: a rectangular patch, maybe a meter by seventy centimeters, set into the void space between the hold floor frames. Bo worked the corners last, carefully, and when the final bead parted, the plate shifted with a sound like a coffin lid settling, and cold air breathed out of the gap and touched his face.

Glacier air. Dry, mineral, impossibly cold, as if the space inside had been keeping winter all these years.

He got his fingers into the gap and pulled.

The plate came free with a shriek of rusted contact, and Bo set it aside and aimed his headlamp into the dark.

The compartment was shallow — built into the void between frames, maybe eighteen inches deep, lined with cedar planking gone silver-gray with age. Someone had built it to protect whatever went inside from the damp. Someone had cared.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, lay three objects.

Bo lifted them out one at a time and laid them on the deck plate beside him, and his hands, which did not shake — thirty-one years of rotating machinery will cure a man of shaking — were shaking anyway.

The first bundle held rope. Climbing rope, kernmantle, coiled in a careful figure-eight, the kind mountaineers use, the kind that has no business on a riverboat except that rivers up here end in ice and ice ends in walls. The rope was cut. One end frayed and fuzzy, the other sheared clean, as if it had parted under load. Bo held the clean-cut end under the light and saw the glaze of heat-melt on the fibers and understood, in the wordless way he understood engines, that this rope had broken while holding something heavy. While holding someone.

The second bundle was smaller. A carabiner, aluminum, snapped at the gate — not worn through, not fatigued, but snapped, the metal deformed in the way metal deforms when it takes a shock far beyond its rating. Beside it, a belay device with one side gouged bright. Bo turned these over in his palms and felt the weight of them like stones.

The third bundle was flat. A rectangle of brass, tarnished nearly black, screwed at one time to a backing board — the screw holes still showed. Bo rubbed it with his thumb, then with the corner of the oilcloth, working at the tarnish the way you'd work at a name you were afraid to read.

Letters came up out of the dark one at a time.

T. LOCKRIDGE

Bo set the nameplate down very gently on the deck plate, the way you set down something sleeping.

Then he turned off his headlamp.

He didn't decide to do it. The dark just came, the way it comes in the bilge when your thumb finds the switch, and Bo let it. He sat in the black under the hold of the Coriander with a broken rope and a snapped carabiner and a dead man's name, and the river pressed in from outside, patient and enormous, and somewhere above him Sefa's radio had gone quiet and the only sounds were the tick of cooling metal and his own breath.

Three years ago, Tom Lockridge had drowned in the Thessaly above the calving fields. Everyone knew the shape of it — the spring trip, the ice, the accident, the boat coming home short-handed. Wendell had brought the Coriander back alone. Alone, Bo understood now, meant without her brother and without her memory of the last night, because that was the other thing everyone knew and no one said: Wendell Lockridge had come back from the high river missing a piece of herself, and she had rebuilt her life around the hole the way you patch a hull — weld it over, paint it over, keep moving, don't press on the seam.

Someone had pressed on the seam. Someone had welded this compartment shut — after? before? — and Bo's mind kept snagging on that question like a fouled prop. If the compartment predated the accident, then Tom had hidden these things himself, stowed them away against some need, and died without coming back for them. If it postdated the accident — if someone had sealed a dead man's climbing gear into the belly of this boat and welded it shut —

Then who. And why. And what else was down here that Bo had never found, crawling every inch of this hull for six years?

He thought about the humming crate in the hold above his head. He thought about Odile Marchetti's charts, which called the shortcut impossible that Wendell had run that morning like threading a needle, and the look on Odile's face afterward — not surprise. Recognition. Like watching someone do a thing she'd seen done before, by someone else, in another life.

He thought: *this job is not a freight run.*

He thought: *I have known that since the dock, and I voted yes anyway.*

That was the complicated part, the part he sat with in the dark. Because Bo had voted yes for reasons that were simple and good — the debts, the refit, the new planking the Coriander needed, Priya's mother's medical bills back in Grayling, Sefa's quiet determination to send money to a school in the town where her daughter had grown up. He'd voted yes because family votes yes. That's what family is: the people you vote yes for even when the cargo is sealed and the manifest is false and the woman paying triple rate smiles like she knows how the story ends.

But family also deserves better than a captain who won't tell them why her hands aren't steady. Family deserves the truth even when the truth is a welded box in the bilge with a dead man's name on it.

And Wendell — Wendell deserved to hear it from Bo, not from Odile, not from the surveyors, not from the river itself when it finally gave up whatever it was holding. If there was going to be a reckoning on this boat, and Bo's bones told him there was, then it should start with family. With the people who washed a dead man's mug every week and hung it back on its hook.

He clicked the headlamp back on.

The light found the nameplate first, brass glowing dully, and then the rope, and then the carabiner with its snapped gate gaping like a small surprised mouth. Bo photographed each item with the battered phone he kept in a ziplock in his breast pocket — Priya had taught him that trick, archivist to engineer, evidence preservation across disciplines. Then he wrapped everything back in the oilcloth, exactly as he'd found it, and set it back in the cedar-lined dark.

He didn't close the plate. He weighed that for a moment — putting it back would buy time, would let him think, would keep the boat's fragile peace intact another day. But the plate was heavy and the welds were cut now, and besides: some things, once opened, should stay open. He'd spent thirty-one years learning that an engine sealed up with a problem inside doesn't heal. It waits. And it gets worse.

He left the plate leaning against the frame, the compartment's dark mouth visible, and climbed out of the bilge on arms that ached like he'd been hauling line in a storm.

Up in the engine room, the air was warm and ordinary and smelled of diesel and coffee. Bo washed his hands at the utility sink — twice, three times, working the bilge smell out from under his nails — and looked at himself in the polished chrome of the fire extinguisher mount. A big man, sixty-one next month, gray coming in at the temples, a face that other people described as unreadable and that was actually just careful.

Through the engine room doorway he could see down the companionway to the main cabin, where Sefa was setting out mugs for the afternoon coffee ritual, and Priya stood at the chart table with her finger on the river map, and beyond the aft windows the Thessaly ran gray-green and glittering toward the white wall of mountains at the world's end.

Wendell was at the helm. He could see her through the pilothouse glass, one hand on the wheel, doing the thing she did — reading the water, flying the boat on instinct and miles. From here she looked invulnerable. From here they all did.

Bo dried his hands on a rag that had wiped Tom Lockridge's tools more times than anyone could count, and he made himself a promise, standing in the warm hum of the engine room: he would tell her tonight. Not because the law said evidence belonged to the captain, and not because loyalty meant obedience, but because she had a right to meet her brother's ghost before Odile Marchetti decided she'd met him already.

He was still holding the rag when Priya appeared in the doorway, chart in hand, and stopped. Her eyes went past him — to the open floor hatch, to the bilge access, to the deck plate sitting where a deck plate should not sit.

"Bo," she said quietly. "What's down there?"

Behind her, from the direction of the galley, Sefa had gone silent. And above them all, faint through the deck, the Coriander's bell rang once — Wendell, calling for eyes forward, because the river ahead was changing, and the ice was starting to talk.

* * *

They gathered without anyone calling a meeting, the way families do. Sefa brought the coffee pot down into the engine room itself rather than ask Bo to leave it, which was her way of saying she understood without asking questions yet. Priya stood with her arms folded, chart forgotten. Through the open hatch, the compartment gaped, and the oilcloth bundles sat where Bo had laid them out again, unwrapped, under the workbench light.

Sefa looked at the nameplate for a long time. When she finally spoke, her voice was steady in the way Bo knew cost her something. "Tom's?"

"Has to be," Bo said. "Rope's cut under load. Carabiner's blown." He tapped the brass letters once, lightly, like knocking on a door. "Somebody hid his gear in my bilge and welded it shut."

Priya crouched, examined the weld seam without touching it, and said the thing Bo had been circling for an hour: "This isn't hiding. Hiding is a locker, a lockbox. This is—" She searched for the word. "This is burial. Somebody wanted this *gone*, not stored."

"Or protected," Sefa said.

"From who?"

"From whoever came looking."

The engine room clock ticked. Somewhere above, Wendell's boots crossed the pilothouse deck, unhurried, four steps to the window and four steps back — her thinking walk, the walk she did when the river gave her room to think, which meant the water was easy and her mind was not.

"She doesn't know this is here," Bo said. "Or she does. And I've been staring at those two possibilities all afternoon and I can't split them."

"Then we don't guess," Priya said. "We ask. Tonight. Off-watch, just us—"

The bell rang again. Twice this time. Not Wendell's routine signal — the other one, the one she'd invented the day Bo signed on and never had to use until now. *Come to the helm. Now. Something's wrong.*

Bo was moving before the second ring finished, Priya behind him, Sefa killing the burner and following, and none of them, as they climbed the companionway stairs into the cold northern light, said the thing they were all suddenly thinking:

Whatever was coming down the river at them — it had started in the dark under the deck, same as everything else on this boat.
