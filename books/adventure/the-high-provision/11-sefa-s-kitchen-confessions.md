## 11. Sefa's Kitchen Confessions

Sefa had a theory about people and kitchens, and it was this: nobody could hold their shape over a cutting board. Give them a knife and something to chop, put the smell of garlic and browning onions in the air, and the armor came off piece by piece — not all at once, never all at once, but like frost leaving a window. She'd learned it in a hospital cafeteria twenty-two years ago, feeding her daughter's nurses, learning which ones were kind by what they did with their hands when they talked.

She was thinking about that theory now because the Coriander was carrying too many secrets for one hull, and secrets, in her experience, were just grief that hadn't been fed yet.

The galley was small enough that two people in it were a crowd and three were an argument. Copper pots hung from hooks Bo had polished until they looked like medals. The stove was marine-grade and temperamental, and Sefa loved it the way you love anything that fights back honestly. Outside, the river ran green-gray and fast, the banks climbing steeper every day, the air coming through the porthole smelling of pine and cold stone and, underneath it, faintly, of ice.

Dinner was braised lamb shoulder she'd been thawing since morning, flatbread, lentils cooked soft with cumin and preserved lemon, a cabbage slaw sharp enough to wake the dead. Comfort food. Deliberate food. You didn't feed people braised lamb if you wanted them to keep their mouths shut; you fed them beans and rice and let them sulk. Lamb said *sit down, stay awhile, tell me where it hurts.*

She'd planned the seating the way Wendell planned a run through rapids — every variable accounted for, every current anticipated. Priya first, alone, while the bread finished. Then Bo, on the pretext of tasting. Then Ansel, who was easiest and therefore most dangerous to leave for last. Voss she'd serve in the mess with everyone else; that man's shell wasn't cracking tonight, and she knew better than to waste good lamb on a locked door. Odile ate in her cabin. And Wendell —

Wendell would get the last plate, and the last hour, and whatever truth Sefa could pry out of her with a spoon.

* * *

Priya came down at five-thirty, as punctual as tide tables, drawn by the smell before Sefa even called. "You need hands?"

"I need company," Sefa said, and handed her a knife and half a cabbage. "Thin slices. Thinner than that. You're archiving, not demolishing."

Priya smiled and adjusted. Her knife work was careful, methodical, each slice measured against the last. They worked in silence for a while, the comfortable kind, the kind Sefa had spent years learning to distinguish from the other kind.

"You've been quiet," Sefa said finally. Not accusatory. Just setting the hook in the water.

"We've all been quiet."

"Quiet like a held breath." Sefa stirred the lentils. "I've been on boats thirty years, girl. I know the difference between a crew that's resting and a crew that's waiting."

Priya kept slicing. The cabbage fell into pale ribbons. "Something's wrong with the cargo," she said at last, so quietly Sefa almost lost it under the simmer. "Not dangerous-wrong. *Wrong*-wrong. Ansel asked me about the boat yesterday. About its history. And she knew things she shouldn't have known. About the last upriver trip."

Sefa's ladle went still in the pot. Tom Lockridge lived in this galley too — in the dent in the doorframe where he'd caught his shoulder carrying a crate, in the hook by the window where his rain jacket used to hang until Wendell quietly moved it to storage and then quietly moved it back. Everyone aboard knew the shape of that absence.

"Did you tell Wendell?"

"This morning. She went very still. Still like water before it drops." Priya set down the knife. "Sefa, I copied some coordinates off Voss in his sleep. I've been cross-checking charts. There's a point upstream of any legal claim, and we're heading straight for it, and nobody will say why."

"And you haven't told the others."

"Not yet. I want to be sure before I start a fire." Priya's jaw tightened. "In the archive, you learn that the worst damage isn't from the documents that burn. It's from the ones that get misfiled. Truth in the wrong place does more harm than truth hidden."

Sefa studied her — this thin, precise woman who'd come aboard three years ago with two suitcases and no explanation and had never once been late for a watch. "And where do you think your truth is filed right now?"

Priya opened her mouth. Closed it. "Somewhere between," she admitted.

"Then here." Sefa pressed a piece of warm flatbread into her hand, dragged through the lemon oil. "Eat. And when you're sure — be sure fast. Boats don't wait, and neither does whatever's upriver." She watched Priya's eyes go to the porthole, to the darkening water, and knew the girl understood exactly what she meant.

* * *

Bo arrived the way Bo always arrived — announced by the deck plates complaining under his boots. He filled the galley doorway like weather. "Smells like somebody's apologizing for something."

"Somebody's feeding her family. Same thing." Sefa handed him the tasting spoon. "Lentils. More salt or more lemon?"

He tasted, considered with the gravity he gave engine tolerances. "Both. Little more cumin." He took the spoon back for a second taste, and Sefa saw the opportunity open the way she'd seen it all afternoon.

"Bo," she said, casual as a cleat knot, "you've been down in the bilge twice this week. Intake trouble?"

His face did something complicated. Bo's face was built for honesty — big, open, slow to hide anything — but he tried anyway, and the trying was itself a tell. "Intake's fine."

"Mm." She turned back to the stove. "Funny. I heard you down there Tuesday night for two hours. Alone. In the dark. With the intake running fine."

The silence stretched long enough that the lamb fat began to sing in the oven. When Bo spoke again, his voice had dropped its working register. "There's a compartment under the hold. Welded shut. Somebody welded it from inside the frame — professional job, done a long time ago." He rubbed the back of his neck. "I got it open."

Sefa turned around slowly. "And?"

Bo looked at the floor, at the galley table, anywhere but at her. "Rope. Climbing rope, river-grade. A carabiner, snapped clean. And a nameplate off a station door. Brass." His throat worked. "*T. Lockridge.*"

The pot lid rattled under the boil. Sefa reached over and killed the flame without looking away from him.

"Tom's things," she said. "Sealed in our own hull."

"Three years they've been down there. Maybe longer. Somebody hid them, Sefa. Hid them *in the boat.*" Bo's voice cracked on the last word, and she realized with a lurch that this big gentle man had been sitting alone in the bilge holding a dead man's nameplate, grieving secondhand, carrying it solo because he hadn't known whose trust it would break to share it.

"Why didn't you bring it up?" she asked gently. "To Wendell?"

"Because either she knows and it'll gut her, or she doesn't and it'll gut her worse." He finally met Sefa's eyes. "Which do I pick?"

Sefa crossed the little galley and put both hands on his forearms, feeling the tremor in them that his face wouldn't show. "You pick neither alone. That's the whole trick of a family, Bo. Nobody carries the heavy thing by themselves, not even to protect the others. Especially not to protect the others." She squeezed. "Tonight, after dinner. All of us. Or I'll do it for you, and you know how I am about dramatic timing."

A wet laugh escaped him. "You're terrible."

"I'm thorough. Sit. Taste my lamb and tell me it needs nothing, because I need one easy customer tonight."

* * *

Ansel came down next, all bright eyes and helpful energy, offering to carry plates before being asked. Sefa let her carry plates. Let her settle onto the bench. Let her admire the copper pots and ask, with genuine delight, whether the preserved lemons were homemade.

"My mother's recipe," Sefa said. "Salt and time. Two ingredients most people won't give anything anymore."

"That's beautiful." Ansel said it like she meant it, and that was the thing about her — Sefa couldn't find the seam anywhere. Either the cheer was real or it was the best-made thing on the boat, and Sefa had inspected hulls long enough to distrust flawless surfaces.

So she went at it sideways. "Your sister," she said, folding dough for the last flatbreads. "Bo mentioned her. During the portage. Said you carried more than your share because of her."

Something flickered behind Ansel's brightness — quick, deep, gone. "She'd have liked this boat. She liked anything that moved." A pause. "She can't move much now. The valley we grew up in — the water's not safe. She's sick from it. Has been for six years." Ansel smoothed the napkin on her knee, once, twice. "The company that owns the mine upstream sent lawyers instead of doctors. So when they offered me this contract, I thought — leverage. Get close to whatever they're afraid of."

"And are they afraid?"

"Yes." Ansel said it simply, and it landed heavier than any shout. "That's what scares me. I signed on to spy for them, and somewhere on this river I started wanting them to lose." She looked up, and for one unguarded moment the cheer dropped entirely, and Sefa saw a young woman standing in the wreckage of her own loyalties. "Don't tell Voss. Please. He's not — he's not free the way I'm free. He has reasons he can't walk away."

"Everyone on this boat has reasons they can't walk away," Sefa said. "That's what makes it a family instead of a payroll." She slid the fresh flatbread across to Ansel. "Eat. And Ansel — when you decide which side you're actually on, decide out loud. Half-loyalty sinks faster than mutiny."

* * *

Voss got his plate in the mess with the rest, ate efficiently, thanked her politely, and offered to wash dishes, which told Sefa he'd been raised right and sealed tight. She declined with a smile and let him go. Some doors you marked for later. Or never.

* * *

Wendell came down at nine, after the evening watch change, when the galley was clean except for one covered plate and one poured glass of the plum brandy Sefa rationed like medicine. The captain stood in the doorway a moment, reading the room the way she read water — checking for hazards, finding none, entering anyway.

"You kept something back from the crew table," Wendell said.

"I keep something back from every table. Tonight yours is lamb." Sefa uncovered the plate, pushed it across. "Sit, Captain."

Wendell sat. She ate the way she did everything — steadily, completely, without wasted motion — and Sefa watched her shoulders come down a quarter inch as the food and the brandy did their slow work. Outside the porthole, the last light was leaving the water. Somewhere far upstream, so distant it might have been imagination, came a sound like a door closing in a very large house. Ice, moving. Both of them heard it. Neither mentioned it yet.

"Sefa." Wendell set down her fork. "Whatever this is, say it."

"I lost a daughter."

It came out plainly because Sefa had decided, somewhere over the lentils, that tonight she would trade true for true. Wendell went still — that particular stillness, the water-before-the-drop stillness Priya had described.

"Marisol. She was nineteen. Leukemia." Sefa turned her glass but didn't drink. "Fourteen months in a ward where I learned every nurse's name and made every doctor earn mine. I cooked for that whole floor. It was the only thing I could do that felt like doing *something*. And when she died, I discovered that grief doesn't shrink. It just gets quieter neighbors." She looked up. "I chose this boat, Wendell. Out of forty offers, I chose a riverboat with a sad captain and a loud engineer, because I wanted a family I could feed. Floating families don't drift apart. There's nowhere to go."

Wendell's eyes had gone bright. "Sefa—"

"Hear me out, because I only have the courage for this once a decade." Sefa leaned forward. "This boat is full of secrets right now. Bo found a compartment under the hold with Tom's nameplate in it. Priya has coordinates she copied out of Voss's sleeping mouth. Ansel is halfway to defecting and doesn't know which direction. And you—" she pointed, gentle and immovable, "—you flinch every time someone says *upriver*, and you've been sleeping four hours a night and dreaming loud enough for the whole aft cabin to hear."

Color rose in Wendell's face. "My dreams are my own."

"They are. But here's my professional opinion, thirty years of kitchens and hospital corridors talking:" Sefa tapped the table once. "Secrets rot faster in close quarters. On land, a secret can breathe — spread out, dilute. On a boat, ninety feet of steel, six souls, weeks of river? Every secret ferments. And fermented truth doesn't stay corked, Captain. It finds a seam and blows."

"You think I should tell them everything."

"I think you don't *have* everything, and that's the problem. You're guarding a vault you can't open yourself." Sefa softened her voice. "I watched you after Tom died. You didn't grieve, you *scheduled*. Miles instead of mourning. And I let you, because we all need armor, and yours happened to be a timetable. But we're past armor country now. Whatever's up that river — and something is, don't tell me otherwise, I count the looks at dinner — you cannot steer through it alone."

Wendell sat with that. The lamp hissed softly. Down the corridor, the boat ticked and settled around them like a living thing.

"If I open it," Wendell said finally, very quietly, "whatever's in there comes out on all of them. Not just me."

"Yes," Sefa agreed. "That's what family means. It's the one contract where everybody signs for damages they didn't cause." She refilled the brandy glass, a small sacrament. "But you should also know what I know, so you're deciding with the full board. Bo will tell you himself tonight — he's ready, or near enough. Priya's building toward something. And Ansel—" Sefa hesitated, weighing a confidence against a crew. "Ansel I'll leave to Ansel. But hear this much: not everyone on this boat is loyal to the same thing anymore. And that might turn out to be the best news we've had since we left the dock."

Wendell absorbed it all, and Sefa watched her do it — the captain's mind moving like her hands on the wheel, testing each current, refusing to fight the water, looking for the line through.

"The sound outside," Wendell said at last. "You heard it too."

"Hard to miss."

"That was calving. Weeks early, if the almanac holds. Which means the season's turning faster than Odile's charts say, which means either she doesn't know—" Wendell's jaw tightened, "—or she does, and she didn't tell us."

"There's your first seam, Captain," Sefa said. "Right there. Follow the crack."

Wendell stood, steadied herself with one hand on the table — the brandy, or the truth, or both — and looked down at Sefa with something raw and grateful fighting through the command posture. "Why do you do this? Feed us all and take us apart, course by course?"

Sefa smiled and began stacking the dishes. "Because Marisol taught me that love isn't a feeling. It's maintenance. Somebody has to check the seams." She nodded toward the corridor. "Go on. Bo's waiting on deck, working up his nerve. Be kind to him — he's been carrying your brother's name in the dark for two days."

Wendell stopped in the doorway. For a moment she was just a woman in a galley light, holding more than she could carry.

"Sefa. Thank you."

"Eat breakfast tomorrow," Sefa said. "That's thanks enough. And Captain—" she waited until those gray eyes came back around, "—whatever you decide to tell them, tell them soon. Because I smelled that ice on the wind today, and rivers don't wait for anybody to finish their confession."

Above them, muffled through the hull, the glacier boomed again — closer this time — and the Coriander shivered from stem to stern, like a dog shaking off rain, like a body remembering what was coming.
