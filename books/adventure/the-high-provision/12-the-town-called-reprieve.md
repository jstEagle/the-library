## 12. The Town Called Reprieve

### Wendell

The town announced itself by smell before sight — woodsmoke and diesel and the green rot of a sawmill pond — and then around the last bend of the river it came into being all at once: a scatter of tin roofs on stilts above the flood line, a single long dock reaching out like a hand extended in greeting or warning. REPRIEVE, said the sign on the harbor board, though half the letters had been repainted so many times they'd drifted from their original shapes. The R leaned. The P had been a B once.

Wendell brought the *Coriander* in slow, against habit, because for once she wasn't racing anything. The current here was lazy, braided around gravel shoals the color of old bone, and she threaded them without thinking while her mind ran elsewhere — on the manifest that wasn't real, on the crates humming in her hold, on Odile Marchetti standing at the rail beside her as if she owned the view.

"You've been quiet since the Narrows," Odile said.

"I'm always quiet."

"You're never this quiet." Odile turned the collar of her coat up against the river wind. "This is the stop. Your terms. Anyone who wants off can walk to the road from here."

"I know what my terms are. I wrote them."

"Then say it to them, not to me."

Wendell cut the engine and let the boat drift the last fifty yards, silent except for the water clucking along the hull, and she felt the whole crew feel it — that particular stillness of people listening to a captain decide something. She rang for Priya to take the lines and slid alongside the dock smooth as a coin into a pocket.

Regrave, the harbormaster, was waiting at the head of the gangway, a wide man in a wool coat gone gray at the shoulders, with a smile he kept holstered like most men keep pistols. "Captain Lockridge," he said. "Heard you were dead."

"Heard wrong."

"Happens up here." His eyes went past her to Odile, and something passed between them — quick, weightless, a stone skipped across deep water — and then his smile came back out of its holster. "Fuel, provisions, whatever you need. Town's small but we're honest about our prices."

*That's* the one lie you'll tell today, Wendell thought, and shook his hand anyway, because that was the river's oldest rule: you shake hands with everyone until you know which ones to stop shaking.

She gathered the crew on deck before anyone touched the gangway. Bo stood with his arms crossed, grease still under his nails from the morning's intake work. Sefa had come up from the galley wiping her hands on a towel, as if the meeting might be settled over food if it came to that. Priya leaned against the wheelhouse door, watchful. The two surveyors hung back — Voss with his splinted wrist cradled against his chest, Ansel with that open, eager face that made Wendell distrust her more than Voss's silence did.

"We're taking on supplies," Wendell said. "We'll be here through tomorrow night. And I want it understood — by everyone — that this is a door, not a formality. Anybody steps off this boat to stay, they get their back pay, no hard feelings, and a ride to the rail line when the weekly truck comes through. That was the deal. It stands."

Silence, of the good kind. Sefa squeezed her towel. Bo looked at his boots. Priya watched Wendell with those archivist's eyes that catalogued everything and gave back nothing for free.

"That's it?" Ansel said finally. "No speech?"

"No speech. A door's not a door if I stand in front of it describing what's behind it."

Odile, at the rail, said nothing at all. But Wendell caught the look she gave the crew — patient, almost fond, the look of a woman watching weather she'd already read — and it put an itch between Wendell's shoulder blades that wouldn't scratch.

They went ashore.

### Priya

Priya had worked six years in the provincial archive at Corvane before the archive burned, and one thing that job taught her was that towns tell you everything in their records — not the official ones, the accidental ones. The depth of ruts in a road. Which storefronts had been painted recently and which hadn't. Where the money lived.

Reprieve's money lived in three places: the fuel depot, the outfitter, and the harbormaster's office, which was the largest building in town and better built than any of the houses. Everything else was plywood and rust and patch jobs held together with hope. She noted it the way she noted everything, without deciding yet what it meant.

Ansel fell into step beside her on the boardwalk, hands jammed in her pockets, radiating the cheerfulness of someone working very hard at it.

"So do you have a home port?" Ansel asked. "Or is the boat it?"

"The boat's it mostly. We berth at Grayling Bend in the winter."

"And before the boat?"

Priya glanced at her. "Before the boat I filed things."

"Files." Ansel laughed. "You don't seem like a files person."

"Nobody seems like what they were. That's the point of leaving." She nodded at the general store ahead, where a hand-painted sign advertised rope, batteries, and *fresh-ish eggs*. "What about you? Before the survey?"

For just a moment — a half-second, a frame dropped — Ansel's face went somewhere else entirely. Somewhere with smoke in it. Then the cheerfulness snapped back on like a generator catching. "Oh, you know. This and that. Mostly I wanted to see the high country before they close the route. Everyone says the glacier's different every year. You have to see it while it's still making itself."

That was a strange sentence, Priya thought. Most people described glaciers as disappearing. Only someone from mining country talked about land making itself.

They bought batteries, rope, lamp oil, and a case of the fresh-ish eggs, and Ansel chattered the whole time, asking questions with the artlessness of a child and the persistence of a bill collector — how long Wendell had run the river, whether the *Coriander* was the same boat that used to run freight to the upper camps, whether there'd been trouble on the high route once, years back, some kind of accident.

"There was an accident," Priya said, counting change into the storeman's palm very carefully. "Years back."

"People died?"

"One person."

"Was it —" Ansel began, and then stopped herself, visibly, physically, like someone stepping back from a ledge. "Never mind. Old news. Sorry."

Old news. Priya carried the egg case back down the boardwalk and turned the phrase over. Ansel knew there'd been a death on the high route. Had asked about it with a specificity she couldn't disguise. Which meant someone had briefed her — briefed her badly, or briefed her and left out the part that mattered.

At the harbor board, she stopped. A new notice had been pinned up since morning, crisp white paper among the faded bills for lost dogs and snowmobile auctions, in handwriting Priya recognized now — Odile Marchetti's, precise and unhurried:

**EXPEDITION NOTICE. The vessel *Coriander* proceeds upriver to the supply cache designated HIGH PROVISION, above the calving fields, for contracted salvage operations. Crew positions available for return leg. Enquire aboard. All persons wishing to disembark must do so by 1800 tomorrow.**

Above the calving fields. Priya stood in the wind reading it three times, doing what archivists do — comparing versions. Because Wendell's terms had said nothing about a destination beyond "upriver, as far as safe." And Odile had just published the true destination on a public board in a town full of gossips, which was either arrogance, honesty, or bait.

Bait, Priya decided. Bait for the crew. Post the destination where everyone can see it, and watch who flinches.

She went to find Wendell.

### Sefa

Sefa liked towns the way other people liked churches — briefly, respectfully, and mostly for what they could give you. She took her market basket and went hunting.

Reprieve's butcher had moose and dubious pork; its grocer had flour gone soft in the bag but honest coffee, real coffee, which Sefa bought four pounds of without blinking because a crew running scared needed real coffee even more than a crew running comfortable. She traded gossip with the grocer's wife the way other women traded recipes, giving a little and keeping much, and learned three useful things: that Marchetti money had rebuilt the dock two years ago; that Regrave the harbormaster had gotten a new truck last spring nobody could account for; and that the last vessel to run above the calving fields had been seven years ago, captained by a Lockridge, and hadn't come back the same.

"We knew Tom," the grocer's wife said, quieter now, weighing coffee beans Sefa hadn't ordered. "Fished off our dock whenever he came through. Sweet man. Funny." She weighed a little longer than the beans required. "His sister come through here after? Didn't talk. Bought diesel and left before dawn. Some folks said she shouldn't have been piloting again so soon. Some folks said the opposite." She slid the bag across. "Which are you?"

"I'm the cook," Sefa said. "I feed whoever's on the boat tomorrow. That's my whole politics."

But walking back with the basket heavy on her arm, she thought about what she'd actually been asked, and about the notice on the harbor board, and about the dinner she was going to cook tonight — because Wendell would never ask her crew point-blank why they stayed, and somebody had to.

She cooked like she interrogated: generously, and with intent. Moose stew, dumplings, the good coffee, and bread baked hot enough to make the whole boat smell like forgiveness. She fed them in shifts, the way she always did, and got each of them alone in turn, because that was the trick — nobody tells the truth at a table. They tell it over a sink, or a stove, or a cigarette at the rail.

Bo she got first, drying dishes beside her, which he did without being asked and always had.

"You could walk," she said. "Three months' hazard pay, cash in hand, right now. Start fresh somewhere warm."

"Nah." He scrubbed a pot. "Where would I go?"

"That's not an answer, that's a symptom."

He laughed, short. Then quieter: "There's stuff under this deck, Sefa. Stuff welded in. I found—" He stopped. Looked at the porthole, at the town lights coming on across the water. "I'll tell you when I know what it means."

She let him go. That was the other half of the trick — knowing when not to push.

Voss she got by accident, bringing stew down to the surveyors' cabin, and he ate it in silence with his splinted wrist in his lap like a bird with a broken wing, and when she turned to leave he said, without looking up, "Your captain broke my wrist."

"She did."

"She was right to." He said it flatly, factually, like reporting a measurement. "I panicked. On the water, panic kills boats. I knew that and I panicked anyway." He finally looked up, and his eyes were the tired eyes of a much older man. "I'm not quitting because I don't deserve to quit. That's stupid, probably. But there it is."

"It's not stupid," Sefa said. "It's penance. Different currency. Spend it carefully."

Ansel she caught at the rail afterward, wrapped in a blanket, watching the town. Sefa handed her coffee and didn't ask anything, and after ten minutes Ansel said, unprompted, staring at the water, "My sister would've loved this. Boats. Rivers. She never saw either." Her voice did the thing it had done on the boardwalk — dropped through the floor of its own cheerfulness. "I keep thinking about how she never got to choose how she died, and I keep getting to choose everything, and it doesn't balance out. Nothing balances out. But this feels closer to balancing than anywhere else I've stood."

Sefa put her hand briefly on the girl's shoulder and said nothing, because some accounts aren't hers to audit.

Last, Wendell, who came to the galley at midnight expecting leftovers and got tea instead, because Sefa had been waiting up.

"You posted a door," Sefa said, setting the cup down. "Nobody walked through it. You want to know why, and you're too proud to ask, so I asked for you."

"Sefa—"

"Bo's carrying something heavy. Priya's carrying something sharp. The surveyors are both running from funerals. And you—" Sefa looked at her captain, this woman she'd chosen four years ago at another dock in another town, this woman who measured grief in nautical miles, "—you're carrying something you won't even name, and you hired a boatload of broken people to help you carry it without telling them what it is."

Wendell held the tea and didn't drink it. "Would it change your answer? If I told you?"

"My answer's already given. I'm telling you why theirs is." Sefa tapped the counter once, softly. "Secrets rot faster in close quarters, Wendell. Whatever's in those crates, whatever Marchetti's really paying for — this family's going to find it out on this boat, in these close quarters, and you'd better be the one who tells them first. That's all. Go to bed."

Wendell drank the tea. It had gone lukewarm. She thanked her anyway, which from Wendell was practically a eulogy, and went.

* * *

### Bo

Bo couldn't sleep in a town. Too quiet — no engine hum, no water talking to the hull. So he sat in the engine room at two in the morning with a lantern, next to the welded compartment, drinking the good coffee and having an argument with himself.

The argument went like this: *Tell her.* And like this: *Tell her what? You found a box with a dead man's nameplate and some climbing gear in it. Maybe the captain knows. Maybe the captain welded it shut herself. Maybe that's exactly why nobody should be saying anything.*

He'd sat in this same spot in the dark bilge a hundred miles downstream and felt the nameplate with his thumb — **T. LOCKRIDGE** — until the letters wore smooth under his skin. He'd known about Tom Lockridge the way everyone on the river knew about Tom Lockridge: the accident, the lost boat, the sister who stopped talking for a season. He'd never connected the name to a compartment under his own feet until he'd been lying in the bilge oil with his fingers on it.

And now they were here, in the dead man's town — because it was his town, Bo could feel it, the way the grocer's wife had weighed those coffee beans — headed for whatever the High Provision was, and the compartment sat under his feet holding its silence.

At dawn he climbed out, joints cracking, and found Wendell already at the rail, watching the ice fog burn off the far bank.

"You slept aboard," she said.

"Couldn't sleep ashore." He came and stood beside her. Down the dock, Odile was talking to Regrave the harbormaster, low and easy, two people discussing the price of fuel and possibly nothing else. "Captain. Tomorrow, when we go — anybody asks me why I stayed, I'm going to say it's the money. Triple pay. That true?"

"It's true enough."

"Yeah." Bo turned his coffee mug in his hands. "It's also a lie. I stayed because of what's under the deck plates. And because whatever happened to your brother, I think it's welded into my engine room, and I think you know it, and I think you're going upstream to find out." He looked at her sideways. "I didn't walk because I want to know too. That's all I came to say."

Wendell was very still for a long moment. The fog lifted off the water in ropes. Somewhere upriver, faint as artillery ten miles off, the first distant boom of the season rolled down the valley — the glacier, clearing its throat.

"You heard that," Wendell said.

"Whole town heard it."

"Season's starting early." She drained her own coffee and set the mug down with a click. "Then hear this too, Bo. When we're above the calving fields, there's no walking away. The window closes and everybody's in it together. Last chance to choose is tomorrow at eighteen hundred."

"I chose," Bo said. "Two nights ago, in the bilge."

* * *

### Wendell

At noon, Odile posted the notice, and by evening the whole town had read it, and Wendell spent the day watching her crew read it.

She saw Priya stand at the harbor board a long time, arms folded, and then walk straight toward her with the particular stride of a woman delivering a report. She saw Sefa come back from marketing and glance at the board and then at Odile, and file something away behind those warm, terrible eyes. She saw Bo read it twice. She saw Ansel read it and go pale, and Voss read it and go still.

Eighteen hundred hours. Wendell stood on the dock with her back to the board, and called it plainly, once: "Door's open till six. After that it closes for the season."

Nobody moved. The town moved around them — kids on the pilings, a dog investigating the gangway, Regrave watching from his oversized office with his holstered smile. The glacier boomed again, nearer, or maybe just clearer.

Priya spoke first. "I archived things for six years. Dead files, sealed files, files nobody was supposed to reopen. Somebody always comes back for them eventually. I'd rather be on the boat when they do." She shrugged. "Also, triple pay."

Sefa snorted. "I stayed because nobody on this boat eats right without me. Also, triple pay."

"The money's real," Odile said mildly.

"The money's a detail," Sefa said, not looking at her.

Bo just said, "I'm in," and crossed his arms, and that was that.

Ansel looked at Wendell with wet eyes and a fierce, borrowed steadiness. "I'm in."

Voss, last, cradling his splint, said only: "I'm in," and Wendell believed him, which surprised her.

And then it was six o'clock, and the door closed, and Wendell stood on her own dock in the town named for second chances and understood, with a clarity that felt almost like vertigo, that the choice had never been hers to offer. She'd framed it as mercy — *anyone may leave* — but standing there watching her crew, her strange assembled family, she saw it plain: they hadn't stayed despite the danger or the secrets or the humming crates. They'd stayed *for* something. For each other, maybe. For the truth under the deck plates. For reasons she hadn't given them and couldn't take credit for.

They had chosen this. All of them, eyes open, on a public dock, with the destination nailed to a board behind her head.

"Well," said Odile beside her, quietly enough that only Wendell heard. "Now you know what you're carrying."

"It's not cargo," Wendell said. "Cargo doesn't get a vote."

"No," Odile agreed. "It gets a captain."

Across the water, the light was dying gold, and up the valley the glacier boomed a third time, and Wendell turned to her crew and gave the order to cast off at first light — and beneath her feet, in the welded dark, Tom Lockridge rode north with the rest of them.
