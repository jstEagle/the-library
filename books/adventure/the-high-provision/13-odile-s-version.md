## 13. Odile's Version

The harbormaster's office at Reprieve had a porch the way a coffin had handles — ornamental, never used for what it was made for. Odile Marchetti sat on its edge anyway, because it was the only seat in town with a view of the river, and she had always believed that a person should face the water when telling a story about it. Lies went down easier with current behind them.

She had chosen the hour carefully. Dusk, when the light came low and amber off the western bluffs and turned everything it touched into something gentler than it was. The harbor board behind her still bore her chalked announcement: *Destination: High Provision cache, above calving fields. Salvage operation. Inquiries to O. Marchetti.* She had written it that morning knowing exactly who would read it first, and how Wendell Lockridge would stand there reading it, jaw tight, one thumb hooked in her belt like an anchor against drift.

Now Wendell came up the plank walk from the boat, boots ringing on the boards, and Odile let her arrive without turning around. Let her choose where to stand. That was part of it too — you didn't corner a woman like Wendell Lockridge; you gave her open ground and let her think it was hers.

"You posted it," Wendell said.

"I said I would."

"Most people post destinations before they hire the crew. Not three hundred miles in."

Odile turned then, unhurried, and looked up at her. Three years had put new lines in Wendell's face, but they were honest lines, earned ones, and something about that hurt worse than if she'd gone soft. "Sit down, Captain."

"I'd rather stand."

"I know." A ghost of a smile. "You'd rather do everything standing. It's why your knees are shot."

Wendell didn't smile back, but she didn't leave either. She leaned against the porch rail instead, arms crossed, and Odile counted that as a victory small enough to build on.

Below them, Reprieve was settling into evening the way river towns did — lamps coming on one by one along the shore road, the smell of woodsmoke and fried onions drifting up from the boarding house, Sefa's voice carrying faintly across the water from the Coriander's galley, scolding somebody about rice. The crew was ashore except for Priya, whose silhouette moved now and then behind the wheelhouse glass. Nobody was leaving. Odile had watched them all day, watching the harbor board, watching each other, and nobody had packed a bag. She had planned for many contingencies on this expedition. Their loyalty had not been one of them, and it unsettled her more than any sandbar or storm season could have.

"They deserve to know where they're going," Wendell said.

"They know now."

"They knew after we were committed."

Odile spread her hands. "Would you have signed otherwise?"

The silence stretched long enough to have a shape. Down on the dock, a mooring line creaked against a bollard, patient as a clock.

"No," Wendell admitted finally, and Odile saw what it cost her to say it. "But I'd have told them the truth about why I said yes."

"And what's the truth, Wendell? Say it out loud. I'm curious whether you can."

Wendell's jaw worked. The dusk was doing its work on her, softening the edges, and Odile leaned into that, keeping her voice low and even, pitched just above the sound of the water. This was the craft of it. You didn't push. You opened doors and let people walk through them believing they'd found the rooms themselves.

"The money," Wendell said. "And the route. And—" She stopped.

"And me," Odile finished quietly. "Yes. All three are true. Only one of them is comfortable to admit, so let's set that one aside and talk about the other two, because those are the ones your crew is betting their lives on."

She patted the porch boards beside her again, and this time Wendell took the invitation — not sitting so much as lowering herself onto the edge, forearms on knees, staring out at the darkening water. Close enough that Odile could see the fatigue under her eyes, the salt dried white on her collar. Good. Tired people wanted simple stories. Tired people wanted the past to have been nobody's fault.

*Salvage,* Odile thought. *Give her salvage.*

"Ask me anything," she said. "One question first. Whatever you've been chewing on since the dock at Callow's Bend."

Wendell didn't hesitate. She'd been carrying it the whole way upriver; Odile could see it in the way her hands had gone quiet, no longer moving when she talked. "The cargo. What's actually in the crates?"

"Equipment. Mine." Odile said it plainly, meeting her eyes, because the first rule of the polished lie was that you told most of it true. "I financed the original survey three years ago. Seismic gear, core drills, sampling rigs, cold-rated instruments. When the expedition ended the way it ended, the company sealed the site and wrote off the equipment rather than fund a recovery. I bought the write-off for pennies. On paper, it's salvage — my own property, stranded above the calving fields, recoverable only in the six weeks the high route opens. That's the whole architecture of it, Wendell. Everything else you've been imagining is weather you're making yourself."

"Weather," Wendell repeated.

"Weather," Odile agreed, and let the word sit there between them, doing its quiet damage.

Because that was the second rule: never deny the grief. Denying it made people dig in, defend their dead, sharpen into suspicion. You honored the grief instead. You built the lie *on top of* the grief, the way a bridge is built on pilings driven into bedrock. Tom Lockridge was bedrock. She could feel him under every word she said, holding her up.

"I know what you think happened up there," she went on, softer now. "Three years of not remembering has left you a hole, and holes fill with the worst thing available. That's not weakness. That's just what minds do. So let me give you what actually happened, as far as anyone living knows it."

Wendell had gone very still. Odile recognized the stillness — it wasn't calm. It was a person holding a door shut against a draft.

"It was April. Late start to the melt, thick ice on the lower fields, and the forecast called for stable cold through the week — that's why we pushed for the cache when we did. Tom was running point on the recovery team. He was good at it, Wendell. Better than I was, better than anyone I've hired since. He'd charted the approach over two days, flagged the seracs, timed the crossings by temperature drop." Her voice did what she needed it to do here — caught, slightly, on nothing. She let it catch. "We were hauling the last sledges across the flats below the moraine when the field let go. No warning. The old-timers will tell you there's always a warning, a groan, a crack, some sign. There wasn't. The whole shelf went at once, and the channel behind us filled with brash before we'd finished turning around. Tom was on the last sledge. He was thirty meters ahead of me." Thirty meters. She had chosen the number years ago and rehearsed it until it lived in her mouth like a scar. "He never shouted. I want you to know that. Whatever your memory has been showing you — shouting, my voice, someone calling — there was no time for shouting. The ice doesn't negotiate."

Wendell's breathing had changed. Shallow. Controlled. Odile watched her hands, white-knuckled on the porch edge, and knew the fragments were moving behind her eyes right now — cracking ice, cold water, a lantern sinking. Fragments were dangerous. Fragments were the one thing she couldn't fully control. But fragments weren't memory yet, and she had three years of practice telling herself which version fit the pieces.

"The inquiry cleared it as natural calving," Odile continued. "Unseasonable stress loading, early meltwater percolation. Nobody's fault. That finding isn't a cover-up, no matter what you've spent three years fearing it was. Sometimes the truth is just the kindest thing available, and we refuse it because kindness feels like being cheated."

"That's a hell of a sentence," Wendell said hoarsely.

"It's a true one." Odile paused, then took the risk she'd been weighing all day. She reached over and put her hand briefly over Wendell's knuckles — warm, brief, gone again before it could be refused. Contact opened people. It also closed them, if they flinched, and Wendell hadn't flinched. That frightened Odile more than she showed. "I loved your brother too. In whatever way I love anything, which is badly and at a distance, but I did. And I have spent three years wanting to finish his work because unfinished things are the only ghosts I believe in."

"What work?" Wendell asked. "You said salvage."

"The survey he died recovering." Odile gestured vaguely north, upriver, into the dark. "There's data on that glacier worth more than this boat. Groundwater maps. Melt-rate baselines. The kind of science that takes ten years and costs a fortune, and we got eight months of it before the mountain ate our funding. The crates aren't treasure, Wendell. They're a second chance at a record the world needs, and I am asking you to help me climb up there and carry it home. That's the expedition. That's all of it."

It was beautiful. She knew it was beautiful; she'd built it the way Sefa built a stock, layer by layer, simmering it down until every element supported every other. The salvage papers existed. The write-off purchase was real, documented, notarized — she'd paid extra to make the paperwork boring, and boring documents were the strongest kind. The seismic gear really was up there. Every verifiable fact in the story checked out, and the unverifiable parts were wrapped inside verifiable ones like poison in fat.

And she watched it land. Watched Wendell's shoulders come down half an inch. Watched her look out at the water instead of at Odile, which meant she was looking inward, testing the story against the fragments, and the fragments — cracked ice, sinking light, cold — fit. Of course they fit. They'd happened. The sabotage had been built to look exactly like the story Odile was telling now. That was the elegance of it, the thing she'd hated and admired in the same breath when it was done: the man who'd done it had given her this lie ready-made, gift-wrapped, and all she'd ever had to do was keep telling it.

"Tom charted the approach," Wendell said slowly. "Timed the crossings."

"He did. His notes are in the site records. I'll show you when we reach the cache — I kept everything."

That was true too, and it was the cruelest thing she'd said all night, and she said it steadily.

For a while neither of them spoke. The lamps of Reprieve burned along the shore. Somewhere a dog was barking at the river, and somewhere farther off, so far it might have been imagination, came a sound Odile felt in her back teeth before she heard it — a deep, tectonic report from upstream, like a door closing in the earth. The glacier, talking in its sleep. Early this year. Earlier than any season she'd known.

Wendell heard it too. Odile saw her head lift, saw the captain in her surface over the grieving sister — the woman who measured life in miles going still, listening, calculating. When she spoke again, her voice had changed registers.

"If it's salvage, why the sealed manifest? Why the surveyors? Why no questions?"

"Because companies are paranoid and lawyers are expensive, and because I've learned the hard way that questions on a dock become headlines in a month." Odile shrugged. "Voss verifies condition and value for the insurance recovery. Ansel does environmental compliance — the upper watershed has protections now that didn't exist three years ago, and recovering equipment from a protected site requires a compliance officer. Ask her; she'll talk your ear off about riparian statutes." All true, or true enough. Ansel would talk about anything rather than about what she actually knew. "I seal manifests because openness costs triple and buys nothing. You of all people know that. How many times have you run freight with a false declared value because honesty was a luxury?"

Wendell almost smiled. Almost. "More than once."

"There it is." Odile stood, brushing off her trousers, giving the conversation a shape — an ending, arrived at naturally, nobody cornered. Never trap a woman like Wendell Lockridge on a porch at night. Let her walk away believing she'd stayed by choice. "The window's shrinking. You heard it. We load fresh water tomorrow and run for the narrows at first light, and in nine days we're at the calving fields, and in ten you can stand where your brother stood and see for yourself that it's exactly what I've told you. Ice, and weather, and a box of instruments worth saving." She held out her hand. "No more lies between us, Captain. Just the river."

Wendell looked at the hand for a long moment.

Then she took it. Her grip was dry and strong and brief, and when she pulled away she didn't quite meet Odile's eyes, and Odile understood that she had won the evening without winning the war — that somewhere in Wendell a room had just been locked and labeled *settled*, and locked rooms in that particular mind had a history of bursting open at four in the morning.

"First light," Wendell said. "Don't be late to your own salvage."

She walked down the plank walk toward the dock, boots ringing, and Odile sat back down on the coffin-handle porch and listened until the footsteps faded into the creak of the gangplank and Priya's low greeting carried across the water.

*Almost*, she thought. *She almost believes it.*

Almost was enough. Almost was all it had ever taken — from the inquiry, from the investors, from herself, on the bad nights, in the good coat, telling the same story to the mirror until the mirror stopped flinching.

The sound came again from upstream, louder now, unmistakable. A boom rolling down the valley like artillery, followed by a long grumbling silence. Below in the galley, Sefa's voice had stopped mid-scold.

Odile stayed on the porch a while longer, alone with the lamps and the river, and told herself the story one more time, quietly, the way you check a knot.

Up in the wheelhouse, unseen, a lamp burned late. Priya's silhouette bent over something on the chart table — charts, Odile assumed, though she couldn't say why the woman would need lamplight to study waters she'd already helped pilot.

Odile watched the lit window for a long moment, and for the first time in three years, the knot wouldn't hold.

* * *

She slept badly, which was unusual, and woke before dawn, which was not. The Coriander's deck lights were already on. Someone was moving below decks — Bo, probably, chasing some engine grievance in the dark the way he did. And at the rail, wrapped in a blanket against the cold, stood Ansel, looking north up the black ribbon of the river, and she did not look like a compliance officer admiring a view. She looked like a woman counting exits.

Odile poured coffee in the harbormaster's kitchen and drank it standing at the window, watching her expedition assemble itself in the grey light, piece by piece, and found she could no longer name which pieces she trusted.

The knot had slipped. She retold the story to herself — salvage, weather, thirty meters — and for the first time the numbers felt like someone else's rehearsal.

Down at the boat, Wendell emerged from the wheelhouse and stood at the rail beside Ansel without a word, two women looking north at the same dark water, and Odile set down her cup, because whatever was coming next would not be announced.

It would arrive, as these things always did on the Thessaly, with the current.
