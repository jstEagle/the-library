## 14. Fragments

The dream did not announce itself. It never did. It arrived the way fog arrives on a river — no front, no warning, just a slow thickening until the world she'd been standing in was gone and she was somewhere else entirely.

Somewhere cold.

Wendell stood on ice that stretched white to a horizon the color of old tin. She knew it was a dream because the ice made no sound under her boots, and real ice always made sound — a creak, a tick, a complaint. This ice was silent as glass in an empty house. Above her hung a sky with no stars in it, only a pale smear where the moon might be behind cloud, and far off, at the edge of hearing, something boomed. Once. Then again. A deep percussion she felt in her back teeth.

*Calving,* some part of her said, calm and professional even inside the dream. *The glacier is calving.*

She began to walk. She didn't choose to; her legs simply went, carrying her across the featureless plain toward the sound, and the silence of the ice underfoot grew more wrong with every step until it was the loudest thing in the world. Then, ahead of her, a lantern appeared — a small yellow bloom of light sitting on the ice like a dropped coin — and beside the lantern, a shape. A man's shape, kneeling, working at something low to the ground with both hands.

"Tom," she said, and heard her own voice come out flat and strange, stripped of everything but the name.

He didn't turn around. He kept working. And now she could see what he was working at: a crate, one of the sealed ones, its stenciled logo pale under the lantern light, and Tom was prying at its lid with a bar, and he was talking — his lips moving, words coming out in a rush she couldn't parse, the way voices go underwater —

The ice cracked.

Not under him. Under *her.* A sound like a rifle shot, then a long tearing groan that rolled away across the plain in both directions, and the lantern light swung wildly as Tom looked up for the first time, and his face —

His face was wrong. Not injured, not afraid — just *wrong*, blurred, as if the dream had been built from a photograph left too long in the sun. She knew the set of his jaw. She knew the way he pushed his sleeve up over one forearm when he worked. But the face itself slid away from her every time she tried to hold it, and the grief that hit her then was worse than any specific grief: it was grief for the face itself, for the fact that three years had done to her brother's features what water does to a riverbank.

"Wendell." His voice came out clear this time, impossibly clear, cutting through the boom of the distant ice. "Wendell, listen —"

A shout answered him.

It came from everywhere and nowhere — from behind her, from under the ice, from inside her own chest — and it was a woman's voice, sharp and commanding, pitched high with something between command and fear, and every hair on Wendell's arms rose because she *knew* that voice. She had known it across card tables and contract negotiations and the deck of a boat at first light. She had heard it say *triple pay* and *no questions* and *the route opens for six weeks only.*

Odile.

"No!" the voice shouted, and the word split into echoes — *no, no, no* — and the ice beneath Wendell's feet went from solid to moving, tilting, a whole field of it sliding with a grinding roar, and Tom's lantern tipped, and the light spilled out of it like something alive and ran across the ice in a thin bright stream toward a seam of black water that hadn't been there a moment before.

The lantern went into the water.

That was the thing about dreams — they skipped. There was no falling, no splash, no reaching. One moment the lantern sat on the ice and the next it was below the surface, sinking, and Wendell was on her knees at the edge of the black water watching it descend, the flame still burning somehow, still burning *underwater*, throwing its small stubborn gold light against the dark as it dropped — a foot, two feet, five — illuminating nothing but its own descent, and Wendell understood with a certainty that lived in her body rather than her mind that if the lantern went out before she could reach it, she would lose whatever it was showing her forever.

She reached into the water.

The cold took her arm to the shoulder. It wasn't cold like winter; it was cold like *meaning*, cold with intent, and her fingers closed on the lantern's wire handle just as the flame guttered — and by its last light she saw, down in the dark below it, shapes. Crates. Stacked crates on the riverbed, dozens of them, stamped with the same logo, and among them something else — a shape like a door, like a vault door, round-handled, set into the stone —

The flame died.

* * *

She woke with her own hand clamped over her mouth.

The Coriander's cabin was dark except for the green wash of the running lights through the porthole, and she could hear the river going by outside — that soft continuous conversation of water against hull that had been the last sound she heard every night for twenty years. Her heart was going hard enough that she could feel it in her ears. Her right arm ached from the shoulder down, though there was nothing wrong with it; the cold of the dreamwater had followed her up into the waking world and settled in the muscle like weather.

Slowly, deliberately, she unclamped her hand from her mouth.

And found the word there.

It was just sitting on her tongue, the way a word sits when you've been saying it in your sleep — *provision* — and she lay very still in the dark and tasted it and felt the floor of her stomach drop away, because she had not put that word anywhere. She hadn't read it today, hadn't spoken it, hadn't thought it. Odile had never used it. The manifest said *survey equipment, mining concern, upper Thessaly route.* Nothing about provision.

But her sleeping mouth knew it. Her sleeping mouth had been saying it, apparently, for who knew how long — long enough for the word to wear a groove.

Wendell sat up, lit the lamp, and drank half a cup of the cold coffee Sefa always left covered on the warmer for her night wakings, which were frequent enough that the crew had stopped pretending not to know about them. The coffee was terrible and perfect. Through the bulkhead she could hear Bo's snore, steady as a pump, and above it the faint tick of the engine cooling, and somewhere forward, the creak of Priya turning over in her bunk.

*Provision.*

She said it aloud, once, quietly, testing it against the room. It meant supply. It meant stores laid in against a need. It meant, in older usage, something provided — something *given ahead of time* to someone who would need it later. A cache. A cache was a provision. And the High Provision —

She stopped.

She hadn't thought those words before either. They had simply arrived, fully formed, the way the word had arrived on her tongue: *the High Provision.* As if they were a place name she'd always known. As if somewhere upstream there was a thing called that, and part of her had been to it, or toward it, or had heard Tom say it across a galley table while steam rose off his coffee, and the knowing had gone down into the drowned country of her memory where the doctors at the clinic in Graywater had told her things might live that would never come back up.

"The mind protects itself," the clinic doctor had said, in his office that smelled of paper and lilies. "What you can't remember, Captain, your mind has decided you're better off without."

She had wanted to hit him. She hadn't. She had signed herself out the next morning and gone back to the river, because the river didn't ask her to remember anything except where the sandbars moved, and sandbars she could do.

Now the river itself seemed to be asking.

* * *

She didn't go back to sleep. She dressed, took the lamp, and climbed to the pilothouse, because that was the oldest rule she had: when the past got loud, steer something. Even a moored boat could be steered a little — checked, trimmed, listened to.

The Coriander rode at anchor in a wide bend above the second gorge, tied to a deadfall pine Bo swore would hold until the heat death of the universe. The night was clear and enormous. Up here the Thessaly ran silver under a sky crowded with stars, and the mountains on either side were black cutouts, and far to the north — so far it might have been imagination — the horizon held a faint pulsing glow, like heat lightning that never resolved into a storm.

Glacier light. Or the aurora. At this distance she couldn't tell, and she stood at the wheel with her hands resting on the spokes and watched it and let the boat talk to her instead of letting herself think.

The dream came back anyway, in pieces, the way it always did — and she noticed, for the first time, that the pieces were changing.

Three years of these nights, and the dreams had always been the same four shards, worn smooth by repetition like stones in a current: cracking ice. Cold water. Tom's lantern sinking. And a shout — always just a shout, voiceless, sourceless, a sound with the meaning torn off it. That was all her sleeping mind had ever given her. Four stones, turned over and over.

Tonight there had been a fifth. Tonight the shout had a voice attached, and the voice was Odile's, and the word the voice was shouting was *no* — and Wendell turned that over slowly, carefully, the way you'd turn over a stone you suspected had a scorpion under it.

Because there were two ways to shout *no* on breaking ice. There was the *no* of someone causing a thing and being surprised by how big it got. And there was the *no* of someone trying to stop it.

She couldn't tell which one she'd heard. That was the cruelty of it — the dream gave her the voice and withheld the meaning, handed her the shell and kept the kernel. She gripped the wheel and stared north at the pulsing glow and hated, with a clean hot hatred, the blankness in her own head. Three years ago she had woken in a Graywater hospital with a broken collarbone, frostbitten fingers, and a hole where eleven days should have been, and everyone had told her the same story in the same gentle voices: storm came early, ice failed, the boat was lost, Tom was lost, you were lucky, Captain, you were lucky. Odile had come to the hospital herself. Odile had held her good hand and cried, actually cried, and said *I'm so sorry, Wendell, I'm so sorry I asked you to run that route,* and Wendell had believed her because grief makes everything believable and because she had loved her once, in the years before the rivalry curdled, and love leaves doors open that should have been welded shut.

*Like a compartment under the hold,* she thought, and flinched at her own mind.

Bo hadn't told her yet. She'd seen it in his face two days ago when he came up from the bilge with grease on his hands and something else on them, something heavier than grease, and he'd looked at her and decided — she'd watched him decide — to carry it a while longer. She let him. Whatever he'd found down there, he'd bring it to her when he could, and meanwhile it sat between them like ballast, and the boat rode a little deeper for it.

The lamp hissed softly. The river talked. North, the glow pulsed.

*Tell W.*

The thought arrived the way the word had arrived — from below, unbidden, wearing no explanation. Two words in Tom's voice, or the ghost of it, or just the shape of it: *tell W.* Tell Wendell. Tell her what? That the crates weren't survey equipment? That the route was a lie? That the woman shouting *no* on the ice was shouting it at whom?

Wendell pressed her forehead against the cold brass rail of the wheel and breathed, and for a moment — just a moment — the dream came back with one more piece, a sliver so thin she almost missed it: the smell of diesel and snow, and her own hands, gloved, hauling on a rope that burned through her palms, and someone else on the other end of the rope, someone heavy, someone she was trying to pull out of black water, and her own voice screaming a name that the memory tore away from her even as it offered it —

Gone. Like a fish sounding. Like a lantern going out.

She straightened. Her eyes were wet and she let them be, briefly, alone in the pilothouse where nobody could spend their loyalty on her grief, and then she wiped them on her sleeve the way she'd done since she was nine years old, and looked north.

Six weeks, Odile had said. The window was already narrowing — she could feel it in the water, in the higher colder mornings, in the way the melt-fed current had sharpened overnight like a knife finding its edge. Whatever was up there — provision, cache, evidence, lie — the river was going to close over it soon, the way the ice had closed over the lantern, and this time there would be no six-week window ever again. The company was buying the route. Odile had said that much with her chin up, daring Wendell to be impressed.

Which meant the dream was right to be urgent. Which meant the word on her tongue was right.

*Provision.*

She said it once more, out loud, giving it to the dark pilothouse like an offering, and then she killed the lamp and stood a while longer in the starlight with her hands on the wheel, steering nothing, listening to everything.

Below decks, faint through the sole, she heard footsteps — someone up, someone moving quiet the way crew move when they don't want to wake anybody. She tracked the sound absently, professionally, the way she tracked anything unusual on her boat. It paused. It started again. It stopped altogether.

Probably Priya, unable to sleep. Probably nothing.

But Wendell stayed at the wheel a long time after the footsteps faded, watching the northern glow pulse like a slow heartbeat, holding the word in her mouth like a stone she couldn't spit out — and she did not yet know that in the surveyors' cabin below, a man who never spoke above a murmur was talking in his sleep, and that the numbers he was murmuring would matter more than any numbers she'd ever steered by.
