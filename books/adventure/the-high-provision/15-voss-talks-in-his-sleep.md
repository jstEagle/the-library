## 15. Voss Talks in His Sleep

The night watch belonged to Priya from midnight until four, and she had come to love it the way you love a thing that asks nothing of you except attention.

She stood at the wheel under the running lights, the Coriander sliding upriver through a channel so narrow the banks breathed on either side of her — spruce and shadow and the occasional startled splash of something diving away from the wake. The river here was glassy where it pooled between riffles, black as poured ink, holding stars. She had learned to steer by them when the channel markers ran out, which they had done two days ago, somewhere below the last named rapid on Odile Marchetti's charts.

Above her, the pilothouse roof ticked and settled as the night cooled. Below, the engine turned over at its steady half-throttle murmur, Bo's careful economy, saving wear for whatever was coming. In the galley, Sefa slept with the door cracked because he claimed he could hear trouble better that way. In the forward cabin, Ansel and Voss shared the space the way strangers share a train compartment: politely, silently, with all their belongings squared away like evidence.

Priya had been an archivist for eleven years before the river took her. She knew what it looked like when people organized themselves against discovery. She had spent a decade watching institutions do exactly that — file the inconvenient thing, seal the record, stamp the box CLOSED PENDING REVIEW until review became a word that meant never.

So she watched.

Not obviously. She'd learned that too, in the archive: the best observers were the ones who seemed to be doing something else. She did her rounds on schedule, checked the bilge, logged the depth soundings in her neat hand, topped off the tea thermos Sefa left filled every night without being asked. And she listened. To the boat, mostly — to the small vocabulary of its complaints, the knock in the port shaft bearing that Bo kept promising to fix, the way the hull spoke differently in shallow water than deep. But also to the people in it.

Voss talked in his sleep.

She'd noticed it the third night out, passing the forward cabin during her rounds. A low muttering through the bulkhead, rhythmic, almost conversational — the cadence of a man reading aloud to someone who wasn't there. She'd paused, felt immediately like a trespasser, and moved on. It wasn't her business what the man dreamed.

But the fourth night, the muttering had numbers in it.

* * *

By the ninth night, Priya had a system, and she hated herself a little for how good she'd gotten at it.

It wasn't hard, technically. The forward cabin's vent opened into the passageway three feet from the helm station, and with the engine at half-throttle and the water flat, the boat was quiet enough that words carried if you stood in the right place. She didn't stand in the right place. She stood at the chart table, ostensibly reviewing tomorrow's run, and let the vent do the work. If anyone asked — and no one ever asked; night watch was sacred territory aboard the Coriander — she was studying charts. Which she was. That was the elegant part. The cover story and the crime were the same activity.

Voss's voice came through thin and flattened by the ductwork, but the numbers survived transit better than anything else. Numbers were hard-edged. They didn't blur.

Tonight he started around one-forty.

She heard him turn over first — the bunk creaking, the particular sigh of a man dropping from shallow sleep into something deeper where the guards came down. Then the murmuring began, and she had her pencil moving before she'd consciously decided to move it.

"...forty-one... no. Forty-one point..." A pause, long enough that she thought he'd sunk back under. Then, clear as a radio transmission: "Six-two-seven. Forty-one point six-two-seven."

Her pencil stopped.

She knew those digits. Not this exact pair, but their shape, their grammar. Latitude. Sixty-one degrees north put you in the middle of the Thessaly's high reach; sixty-two would put you above it, in the country the maps shaded white and labeled, in the small type mapmakers used for places they hadn't bothered to flatter, *glacial — un surveyed*. Nobody gave latitude to four decimal places unless the number mattered more than the saying of it.

Voss muttered on. "...minus one-oh-nine... minus one-oh-nine point..." Another creak. "Four-four-oh."

Longitude. West. She wrote it beneath the first line, her handwriting suddenly less neat than usual, and sat back, and looked at the two lines of figures on the margin of the chart she wasn't really reading, and felt the specific cold that archivists know — the cold of touching a document that has been waiting for you.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing she had been circling for nine nights without letting herself land on: Voss was not a surveyor. She'd told herself he might be — quiet men made good surveyors, patient men, men who liked instruments more than conversation — but surveyors talked about their work the way cooks talked about food and engineers talked about engines, helplessly, compulsively, out of love. Voss never talked about his work at all. He cleaned and maintained equipment she'd never seen him use. He read the same paperback with the cover torn off, over and over, or appeared to. He watched Wendell the way you watch weather — constantly, professionally, without expression.

And now he was reciting coordinates in his sleep, to some audience in his head, with the urgency of a man delivering instructions he was afraid of forgetting.

"Before the season turns," Voss said then, distinctly, and the phrase went through Priya like a fishhook.

His voice had changed. The flat recitation had lifted into something almost pleading — the tone of a man arguing with someone, or reporting to someone, or begging. "Before the season turns. It has to be before the season turns, or—" The bunk creaked again, violently, and then there was a sound she recognized from her years among paper records: the sound of a person waking themselves up out of a bad dream, the sharp inhale, the held silence while they establish where they are.

She marked her place in the chart book, closed it, and stood very still at the wheel, looking out at the black water and the doubled stars, listening to the boat carry her north toward a point on the earth that a sleeping man had just given her the keys to.

* * *

At two-thirty, with the channel straight and wide for a mile and nothing to hit, she allowed herself to open the chart book again.

This was the part she was good at. This was, if she was honest, the only part she'd ever been great at — the cross-check, the collation, the moment when two records that had never met are laid side by side and one of them changes meaning forever. She'd found a falsified land grant that way once, buried in a county archive, by noticing that a surveyor's signature dated 1911 used ink that wasn't manufactured until 1923. It had cost the firm a client and cost her the job, eventually, though not in those words. In those words it had been *not a team player*. What it had actually been was: she couldn't stop once she saw the seam. Most people, shown a forgery, feel a thrill and then let it go, because letting go is how families and offices and countries keep functioning. Priya had never acquired the talent.

She plotted the coordinates with a ruler and a fine pencil, working from the chart's graticule, estimating the fractions by eye and then re-estimating, because if she was going to do this she was going to do it properly.

Forty-one point six-two-seven north. Minus one-oh-nine point four-four-oh west.

The pencil tip came to rest in the upper left quadrant of the sheet, in the region the cartographers had left nearly blank — contour lines fading out mid-slope, drainage drawn speculatively, a single note in eight-point italic: *icefield, poorly known.* Upstream of everything. Upstream, she realized as she traced the river's thread northward with her finger, of the last mining claim on record, upstream of the last grazing lease, upstream even of the old park boundary that existed mostly as a historical curiosity. There was nothing legal out there. There was nothing *claimable* out there. The whole point of that country, legally speaking, was that no one had ever wanted it enough to fight over it.

So why was a man paid to be a surveyor dreaming about a fixed point in the middle of it, at four-decimal precision, with a deadline attached?

*Before the season turns.*

She sat with that. Around her the boat ran on, obedient, the compass light glowing amber, the river making its small continuous remarks against the hull. She thought about what *the season* meant up here. Not summer — summer was already dying; she'd watched the alders go yellow along the banks for three days now. The season that mattered, the season everyone on this boat measured time against whether they said so or not, was the calving season. The weeks when the glacier shed its edges into the headwaters and the upper river choked with ice and closed. Odile had bought six weeks of open water. Everyone knew the window was shrinking; Wendell had said so at dinner with the flat calm she used for bad news, and Bo had nodded like a man confirming a diagnosis.

*Before the season turns* meant: before the route closes. Before the ice comes down and makes the point unreachable — or makes whatever is at the point unreachable, or unrecoverable, or unverifiable.

Or it meant: before the season turns and someone else can get there.

Priya closed the chart book and pressed her palm flat on its cover, as if the figures underneath might rearrange themselves if given privacy. They wouldn't. Figures never did. That was the whole religion of archives: the numbers stayed put; it was only their meanings that migrated.

She ran the inventory of oddities she'd been keeping in her head since departure, the way Bo kept a tally of engine hours. The sealed crates nobody would explain. The false manifest — she'd seen the real freight weights in the draft marks when they loaded, and the boat was sitting three inches deeper than any manifest she'd ever reconciled. Ansel's friendliness, which was genuine, and Ansel's questions, which were aimed. The way both surveyors had gone silent at Reprieve when Odile posted the true destination on the harbor board — not surprised, Priya had thought at the time. *Confirmed.* And now this: a sleeping man broadcasting a location above the edge of the mapped world, on a clock.

None of it proved anything. That was the maddening, familiar shape of it. Nine separate facts, each explicable alone — surveyors have equipment, manifests have errors, friendly people ask questions, men have dreams. It was only the pattern that accused, and patterns weren't evidence, and she knew exactly how institutions responded to patterns offered as evidence. She'd been the pattern once. She'd lost a career to the difference between *this looks wrong* and *this is provably wrong.*

But she wasn't an archivist anymore. She was a deckhand on a boat owned by a woman who navigated by the pitch of water over stone, and whose brother had died upriver, and who had signed a contract with sealed cargo and a locked jaw. And whatever was at forty-one point six-two-seven, minus one-oh-nine point four-four-oh, was close enough to their course that Voss hadn't needed to dream about it — he could have looked at the same chart she was holding. Unless the point wasn't on their course. Unless it was somewhere Wendell wasn't planning to go.

Unless the surveyors' destination and the captain's destination were not the same destination.

The thought arrived fully formed, the way the important ones always did, and Priya stood with it in the amber dark of the pilothouse and felt her curiosity finish its long slow hardening into something else. Suspicion was a colder mineral. It didn't glow. It held an edge.

* * *

At three-forty she made her final round, as she always did, checking the deck cleats and the anchor lashings and the bilge alarms, moving through the boat's sleeping body with her flashlight hooded to a coin of light. Past the galley, where Sefa's breathing was audible and easy. Past the engine room hatch, where Bo had left a rag folded over the shaft bearing access in a gesture of optimism she found oddly moving. Past the aft cabin where Wendell slept — or lay, anyway; Priya had heard her moving at odd hours every night since the Narrows, the restless shifting of a woman negotiating with her own skull.

Past the forward cabin, where Voss had gone quiet at last.

She stopped. Not for him. For the chart table in the corner of the common space, where Ansel had left her own materials spread out — she was casual about them in a way Voss never was, folders squared but not locked, a pencil cup, a folding rule. And on top of the topmost folder, weighted under the pencil cup, a single sheet that hadn't been there at midnight.

Ansel worked late sometimes. Priya had noted it before and filed it as innocuous — the cheerful ones often did, burning their sociability after dark in private labor. But the sheet under the pencil cup was a plotting sheet, the kind surveyors actually used, gridded and pre-printed, and even in the hooded coin of her flashlight Priya could see that a position had been marked on it in red pencil. A cross, and beside the cross, small block letters:

**H.P. — VERIFY / DESTROY**

High Provision. Verify. Destroy.

The boat's diesel hummed beneath her feet. Somewhere ahead, miles up the black channel, the river bent east toward the calving fields, and beyond them the icefield waited with whatever Tom Lockridge had found and died keeping. Priya stood in the dark common room with her heart going hard and even, and did not touch the paper, because touching it changed nothing and being seen touching it changed everything. She had learned that much, at least, from the wreckage of her first life: you don't seize the document. You document the seizure.

She memorized it instead — the grid square, the red cross, the two words and their terrible economy — and went back up to the wheel, and wrote the coordinates in her logbook in her neatest hand, the hand of a woman keeping ordinary records on an ordinary night, and beneath them, after a long moment, she added a line she had no professional justification for:

*He says it has to be before the season turns. So does she.*

At four o'clock she woke Wendell for the morning watch, and stood aside while the captain blinked awake in the lamplight, and heard herself say, in a voice she kept level by main force:

"Before you take the wheel — I need to show you something on the charts."
