## 16. The Portage

The river ended without warning.

One moment the Coriander was riding a channel of green meltwater between granite shoulders, and the next the water simply gave out — a gravel fan spreading wide and shallow, braided into threads too thin to float a canoe. Wendell brought her alongside the bank with the same unhurried precision she used for everything, backed her down onto a shelf of smooth stone, and killed the engine.

"End of the line," she said. "For the boat, anyway."

Bo stood in the engine room doorway with grease to his elbows and looked at what the charts called the Kettle Bypass — two miles of moraine, scree, and stunted pine between this dying channel and the living river beyond. He'd hauled gear over worse ground in his younger days, but he'd never tried to move forty tons of wooden boat across it.

"We walk her?" Priya asked.

"We walk her," Wendell said. "Rollers, sledges, winch lines off the trees. Bo and I have done it before."

She said it lightly, but Bo caught the flicker that crossed her face when she said *before* — the same flicker he'd been seeing more often lately, like a lamp guttering in a draft. The last time the Coriander had gone overland, Tom Lockridge had been on the capstan with him.

Odile Marchetti came down the gangway in boots that had never seen mud until this trip and looked at the moraine the way a woman looks at a bill she'd rather not read.

"There's no road?"

"There's a game trail," Wendell said. "Barely. We make our own road. That's what the pay's for."

Ansel was already at the rail, looking at the portage with an expression Bo couldn't place — not dread, exactly. Something closer to hunger. "How long?"

"Three days if we're lucky. Five if we're honest." Wendell swung down onto the stone. "Everybody off. We strip her light. Everything that isn't hull comes off and goes on the sledges first."

* * *

They spent the first day taking the boat apart without breaking her.

Bo had always loved this part, though he'd never have admitted it — the Coriander opened up like a patient on a table, her deck cargo craned off by the portable derrick, her rails padded, her keel protected with greased skids. Sefa cooked twice the usual portions because hauling began tomorrow and bodies would need fuel. Voss worked in silence beside him, unbolting the wheelhouse steps, and Bo noticed the man knew tools — knew them the way a man knows tools who has been poor and had to fix things himself rather than pay someone. It didn't fit with the pressed shirts and the clipboard. Very little about Voss fit.

By dusk the Coriander sat on six cradles of stacked timber like a beached whale wearing a saddle, and the crew sat around Sefa's fire eating beans and salt pork and not talking much, because tomorrow would take everything they had.

Wendell traced the route on the chart one last time with a burnt matchstick. "Two miles as the crow flies. Two and a half the way we'll go, around the bluff. First half is gravel bar and easy grade. Second half climbs sixty feet through the moraine and drops back down. There's a creek crossing at the low point — cold enough to kill a man in four minutes if he goes under, so nobody goes under."

"And the rollers?" Odile asked.

"Green timber. Bo's felling them tonight." Wendell glanced up. "You'll want gloves. Real ones."

Odile looked at her soft palms and said nothing, which was its own kind of answer.

Bo took the saw and his headlamp and went into the pines alone, because felling was work you did alone or you did it badly. The trees up here were thin and stubborn, wind-stunted, but straight enough for rollers. He worked by lamplight until midnight, dropping twelve of them, limbing them, dragging them down to the staging area where they lay pale and raw-smelling in the starlight like bones.

When he came back to the fire, only Ansel was still awake. She had a tin cup of Sefa's terrible coffee and she was sitting with her back against a sledge runner, looking north toward the glacier country, where the sky had a faint bruised glow above the peaks — ice reflecting starlight, or something else.

"You should sleep," Bo said.

"So should you."

"I've slept less before a harder day."

She smiled at that, tired and real. "I believe it. You've got that look. My father had that look — the look of a man who measures himself by how much weight he can carry."

Bo lowered himself onto a crate opposite her. His shoulders ached already, in anticipation. "And what does your father carry?"

"He carried us. Me and my sister, after my mother went." Ansel turned the cup in her hands. "He died three years ago. The sister —" She stopped. Started again from a different angle, the way people do when the direct road is washed out. "You've been up this river your whole life, haven't you? Mr. Bo."

"Bo. And yes. Since I was sixteen."

"Then you'd know about the Meridian Creek camps. Up past the calving fields. Company towns."

Bo kept his face still. He knew about them the way everyone on the river knew about them — the tailings ponds, the fish with lesions, the settlements downstream that stopped drinking their own water. He also knew better than to say so in front of the surveyors. "I know there are mines up there."

"There was a town called Solenne." She said the name carefully, like setting down something fragile. "My sister lived there. Married a mechanic who worked the processing plant. Good wages, company housing, the whole promise." She drank the cold coffee. "Four years ago the tailings pond failed. Not a disaster — nothing so clean. A slow failure. A seep. The company knew for two years. They had the reports. They paid for water testing and then they paid to bury the water testing." Her voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "The kids got sick first. Then everyone. By the time anyone outside heard about it, the company had bought the story — equipment failure, natural contamination, compensation fund, confidentiality agreements. My sister signed one. Eighteen months later she was dead, and the agreement meant I couldn't even say how."

The fire popped. Somewhere up the moraine, ice settled in the high country with a sound like a door closing in a distant house.

"I'm sorry," Bo said. It wasn't enough and they both knew it, but it was what there was.

"When Odile's people approached me — the survey contract, good money, three weeks' work — I thought: fine. I'll get inside. I'll see the reports with my own eyes. The real ones, the ones they buried. I'll find the numbers that prove what they did to Solenne, and I'll use them." She looked at him then, and her eyes were dry in the way of eyes that have finished crying long ago and don't intend to start again. "That's why I work so hard, Mr. Bo. Not loyalty. Leverage."

"Bo," he said again, automatically. Then, because the night was dark and the truth was loose between them: "You could have just asked us. Half this crew would've helped you burn their offices down."

Something moved through her face — surprise, then a grief that was almost funny to her, a short exhale through the nose. "Could I have? I didn't know that. When you sign on with people like this, you assume everyone's bought. That's how they keep you. You assume the whole world is transactional because the alternative is believing you were alone the whole time."

Bo thought about the welded compartment under the hold, and the nameplate wrapped in cloth in his footlocker, and said nothing.

"Get some sleep," he said instead. "Tomorrow we carry a boat up a mountain."

* * *

The first mile went almost well, which Bo would learn to distrust forever after.

They rolled the Coriander on the green logs, levering her forward with timbers and hand winches anchored to the biggest pines, and she moved — slowly, groaningly, a few feet at a time, but she moved. Wendell ran the operation like she ran the river: reading the ground ahead, calling the anchor points, knowing before anyone else when a log was about to kick or a cradle was going to bind. Priya managed the lines with an archivist's precision, every rope coiled and tagged. Even Odile hauled, gloves and all, red-faced and silent, refusing help getting over the gravel bar and accepting it ten minutes later without acknowledgment.

Voss worked like a machine and spoke like a locked door. Ansel worked like a woman possessed.

Bo saw it by mid-morning of the second day. Everyone else had found their pace — the sustainable grind, the long-haul rhythm that lets you go all day. Ansel had no such gear. She pulled at the capstan bars until her hands bled through her gloves, took the heaviest end of every timber, waded into the freezing creek crossing to set rollers when the water was up to her thighs and Wendell shouted at her to get out before the current took her. She laughed while she did it. That was the part that worried Bo. People who work that hard with joy in it aren't working for money.

At the noon halt, he sat down beside her on a sledge runner and handed her half of Sefa's flatbread. She took it like it was a sacrament.

"You're trying to kill yourself," he said.

"I'm trying to earn my spot on this crew."

"You earned it yesterday. Nobody here is keeping score of tons moved." He chewed. "Except maybe you."

She was quiet for a while. Below them, the Coriander lay on her rollers in the sun, patched and patient, waiting for men and women to carry her over a mountain. Above them the moraine rose in its long gray rampart, and beyond it, faint even in daylight, came the first far-off sounds of the ice — deep percussive thuds, spaced minutes apart, like artillery from a war nobody had declared yet.

"My sister's name was Maren," Ansel said.

Bo went still. He remembered what she'd said last night — the agreement, the silence she'd sold. She was breaking it. Right here, on a gravel bar in the middle of nowhere, for an audience of one engineer with flour on his shirt.

"She was older than me by two years. She was the brave one. I was the careful one — I checked the locks twice, I saved money, I read contracts. She jumped off bridges into rivers and married a man she'd known five weeks." Ansel smiled at the middle distance. "When the seep started, she was the one who went door to door. She organized the testing. She stood up at the company meeting with her baby on her hip and asked them to read the numbers out loud. On camera. That footage exists somewhere — some archive, some lawyer's drawer. Maren Okafor, asking a room full of suits to please just say the number."

"The baby?"

"Lives with my mother now. Healthy. Maren made sure of that before —" Ansel's jaw tightened. "Before. She fought the way she did everything. All at once, no safety rope." She brushed crumbs from her hands with great care. "I'm telling you this because you've been decent to me, and because I watched you today with that boat, and I think you understand carrying things that aren't yours to carry."

Bo looked at her. "What do you mean, things that aren't mine?"

"This crew grieves a man none of you will name. I've watched it for two hundred miles. The captain flinches at the word *upriver*. Your cook leaves an extra plate out at dinner and takes it away again like she doesn't know she's doing it. You sleep in the engine room, Mr. Bo, and there's a locker in there you touch every night before you turn in." She said it without accusation, gently, the way you'd note the weather. "Whatever happened to him, you're all still hauling it. Every day. Like this boat."

The words landed harder than any load he'd shifted all week. Bo looked at his hands — scarred, competent hands that could fix anything except the thing under the hold.

"His name was Tom," he said quietly. "Tom Lockridge. The captain's brother."

"I know."

"How do you know?"

Ansel held his gaze, and for the first time since he'd met her, she looked frightened — not of him, but of something behind him, upstream, waiting. "Because I've read the file," she said. "The real one. The one the company keeps on this expedition. And Tom Lockridge is in it, Mr. Bo. In a way that would break your captain's heart, if she ever sees it." She glanced upriver, toward the calving fields, toward the cache they were all being dragged toward. "Which may be exactly the point."

* * *

The second half of the portage nearly killed them all, and in the way of such things, it finished making them a crew.

The climb through the moraine took eighteen hours that should have taken eight. The ground was unstable — frost-heaved rock that shifted under the cradles, forcing them to rebuild the road beneath the boat a dozen times. At the worst pitch, a forty-degree wall of loose slate, the stern winch anchor tore out of its pine and the Coriander slid six feet downhill before Bo threw himself under the trailing skid with a blocking timber and held her, screaming for wedges, the whole hull's weight shuddering through his shoulders and spine while Priya and Voss drove chocks and Wendell rigged a new anchor with hands that never shook.

Afterward, Sefa force-fed him soup and Wendell sat beside him on a rock and didn't say thank you, because between the two of them thanks would have been an insult. She just handed him her own gloves, the good ones, and looked at his ruined palms, and nodded once. It was the most either of them had ever said.

They crested the bluff at dusk on the fourth day and saw the far channel below — broad, fast, glittering, alive. The sound of the ice was louder now, a regular muttering from the north, and once, distinctly, a crack like a rifle shot that made everyone stop and look at each other.

"Season's turning early," Wendell said, to no one in particular. Her voice was calm. Her knuckles, on the capstan bar, were white.

They brought the Coriander down the last slope in the dark, by lantern light, inch by inch, and floated her at dawn on the fifth day. She took the water like a tired animal taking a river — settling, sighing, whole. Bo went below immediately to check her seams, and found three weeping but holding, and sat on the bilge stringer for a moment in the familiar dark, listening to the water talk to the hull.

Through the planking overhead he could hear the others — Sefa already cooking, Priya laughing at something, Ansel's voice among them, easy now, belonging. A week ago Bo had catalogued her as the enemy: hired eyes, sealed crates, false manifest. Now he turned over what she'd said on the gravel bar, and the fear underneath it.

*I've read the file. The real one.*

A file on this expedition. A file with Tom in it. Kept by the company whose logo was stamped on thirty humming crates in the hold below his feet.

Ansel hadn't told him what was in the file. She'd stopped herself — he'd seen her do it, seen the sentence die on her lips. Which meant she was still afraid. Still choosing, every day, how much truth this boat could survive.

Bo climbed the ladder into the morning light and found Wendell at the helm, checking the channel with those miles-not-years eyes, and Odile Marchetti standing beside her, pointing at something on the chart, smiling her smooth smile.

And he understood, with the certainty of a man who has spent his life listening to engines — hearing the fault in the bearing before the bearing knows it itself — that whatever was coming at the top of this river, Ansel knew it, and it was aimed at all of them.
