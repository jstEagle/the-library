## 17. High Water Country

The river changed its mind at dawn.

Wendell felt it before she saw it — a shift in the Coriander's wake, the way the water quit arguing with the hull and started shoving. For three days below the portage the Thessaly had been a broad brown animal, lazy and deep, meandering through country so flat you could watch weather coming for an hour before it arrived. Now the banks drew in like a held breath. The water went from tea-colored to the pale green of bottle glass, and cold rolled off it in visible ribbons, fog burning off slow even under a hard blue sky.

Glacial melt. They'd crossed some invisible line in the night, and now they were drinking from the ice itself.

"Temperature's dropped four degrees since midnight," Bo reported, coming up from the engine room with grease to his elbows and a look on his face that meant he had more to say and was deciding whether to say it. "Intake's running cold enough I'm worried about condensation in the fuel lines."

"Strain it every hour," Wendell said.

"That's not what I came up here to say." He wiped his hands on a rag that was dirtier than his hands. "The water's different. You can hear it in the shaft. It's got a — a voice now. Like it's mad about something."

"It's fast," Wendell said. "Fast water always sounds angry. It's just in a hurry."

Bo grunted, unconvinced, and went back down. Wendell stood at the wheel and watched the river narrow ahead of them into a corridor of granite, and admitted to herself that Bo wasn't wrong. This water didn't sound angry. It sounded young. Meltwater was a child of the glacier — born yesterday, in a rush, knowing nothing yet about patience. It hadn't learned where it was going. It changed its mind every hundred yards, and it would drown you without malice, without even noticing, the way a child drowns an ant.

She throttled down and listened.

* * *

There were pilots who navigated by eye, by chart, by depth sounder, by the almanac of other men's wrecks. Wendell's father had taught her all of those and then taught her the one that mattered, up here where none of the others worked. In high water country the charts were guesses and the sounders lied — the signal bounced off silt suspended so fine it hung in the water like smoke, and returned readings that would put you confidently aground on water forty feet deep or confidently afloat over a hole that would swallow your whole boat. The eyes weren't much better. The green water read shallow when it was deep and deep when it was a shelf of stone waiting just under the surface like a liar's smile.

But water moving over stone sang, and it never lied about the song.

Every obstruction had a pitch. A gravel bar two feet down hummed low and round, a cello note, almost pleasant. A boulder shelf at eighteen inches sharpened it, raised the tension, a violin drawn toward its bridge. And the killer — the slab of granite lying flush beneath the surface, the one that could peel a hull open like an orange — that one sang high and thin and clean, a whistle, almost pretty, the kind of note a person might lean toward if they didn't know better. Tom had called it the angel's note, once, years ago, standing beside her on this same deck. He'd said it because their father had said it, and their father had learned it from a pilot whose name nobody remembered, and that was how knowledge survived out here: passed hand to hand, mouth to ear, from the dead to the living, until somebody forgot to pass it on.

Wendell killed the engine to idle and stood at the rail with her head cocked, one hand resting on the wheel the way you rest a hand on a horse's neck.

The crew had learned to read her listening. Sefa appeared at the galley door with coffee she didn't offer anyone, because offering meant talking and talking meant breaking whatever thread Wendell was following. Priya came up quietly and leaned on the rail, watching the banks instead of the water, calling out landmarks — that split rock, that dead spruce leaning east — matching them against Odile's charts, which lay clipped to a board beside her. Ansel hovered at the edge of things, hands folded, wearing the expression of someone watching a surgeon work. Voss stayed below. His wrist had come out of its splint two days ago and he still favored it, and he still didn't meet Wendell's eyes, but he'd stopped flinching when she spoke, which was its own kind of treaty.

Odile stood at the stern rail with her arms crossed, as she had stood for three days, saying nothing, watching everything.

"Chart says two hundred meters wide through here," Priya said. "Shoaling gradually to the north bank."

"The chart's describing a different river," Wendell said. "Or a different month. This is July melt running maybe double seasonal average. Look at the debris line." She nodded at the banks, where a ragged fringe of sticks, grass, and one unfortunate muskeg raft of peat sat well above the current level. "That's where the water was last week. We're riding a foot and a half of nobody-knows."

"So we trust the ears," Priya said.

"We trust the ears."

She eased the throttle forward and let the Coriander walk into the narrows.

* * *

For an hour it was the purest work there was, and Wendell forgot, in the way she only ever forgot on the water, that anything else existed. The granite walls rose on either side, streaked white with mineral stain, and the river ran between them in braided channels that separated and rejoined like argument and reconciliation. She chose channels by sound alone: idling down at each fork, head tilted, reading the water's throat. Low note left — gravel, safe but slow, and slow cost them against the current. High thin whistle right — no. Middle channel carried a mixed chord, gravel over deeper water, a bar you could cross with a hull that drew four feet, which the Coriander did, barely, these days.

"Port side green," she called, and Priya marked it, and Bo below felt the difference in the shaft note and adjusted without being asked, and Sefa brought coffee that was somehow always hot at exactly the moment hands needed warming, and the boat moved through the cold country like a thought moving through a mind — smooth, purposeful, unargued.

This was the thing Odile had never understood, back when they were young and rivals and both too proud to say so. You could buy a boat. You could hire a crew, buy charts, buy the best engine money floated upriver. But you couldn't buy this — the accumulated listening of a life, a father's voice in your ear saying *the angel sings before it kills*, thirty years of rivers teaching your body what your mind could never write down. Wendell was not a modest woman, but she was an honest one, and honesty forced her to admit that what she did at moments like this wasn't skill. It was closer to kinship. The river and she had grown up together, and you don't navigate past family; you negotiate.

At the third major fork the channels collapsed into one, and the river dropped.

She heard it before she saw it — a change in the whole register of the water, the sound going from speech to roar, and beneath the roar, threading through it, a note she knew. Not the angel's whistle. Something worse. A stuttering pulse in the roar, rhythmic, like a heart with a murmur.

"What is that?" Ansel asked. Her voice had gone careful.

"That," Wendell said, "is water hitting a ledge and folding back on itself. Which means somewhere ahead there's a drop with a reversal under it." A hydraulic. A keeper. The kind of feature that took boats and held them, turning them over and over at the base of a drop while the river poured through the wreckage like it was nothing, like it was Tuesday.

Priya had the chart board up. "Nothing marked here. Nothing at all — the survey stops twelve miles back."

"Of course it does." Wendell throttled down to bare steerage way. "Nobody surveys water they can't get a boat home from."

Ahead, the canyon pinched to perhaps sixty meters, and the river filled every meter of it, green water stacking up into standing waves, and the mist hanging above it all caught the midmorning sun and made a rainbow — a full, insolent, beautiful arch right over the worst water Wendell had seen since the accident, and she thought, with a strange detachment, *even the river is showing off today.*

"Options?" Odile asked from the stern. Her voice was level. Give her this: the woman never wasted breath on panic.

"Port wall has a shelf — rock apron, maybe six feet wide, if the debris line doesn't lie. We could line the boat down. Ropes, winch, two hours, maybe three." Wendell watched the water. "Or we run it."

"And if you run it?"

"Then I need everyone doing exactly what I say, exactly when I say it, and I need the engine to answer the first time, every time." She turned her head. "Bo?"

From below, slightly muffled, slightly offended: "It always answers."

* * *

They ran it.

Later, Wendell would try to reconstruct the decision and find it slippery — not courage, not calculation, exactly, but the particular arithmetic of a captain who knows her boat's temper better than her own. Lining down meant three hours on a wet rock shelf above killing water with a heavy boat and six people and ropes that could part. Running meant ninety seconds of trusting everything she'd ever learned. She chose the ninety seconds. She would choose them again. That was the whole truth of it and there was no cleaner version.

"Everyone sits," she ordered. "Sefa, strap the galley. Priya, you're my eyes aft — call anything I can't see behind us. Ansel, hold onto Voss whether he likes it or not. Odile—" she paused, and found she had no instruction for the woman, and settled on, "hold on."

"I always do," Odile said, and for once it didn't sound like a boast. It sounded like a confession.

Wendell took them in on the tongue of smoother water hugging the port side — the tongue, the river's one courtesy, the lane of calm water every rapid offers to those who can find it. The roar built around them like a wall of sound. She felt it through her boots, through the wheel, through her teeth. The standing waves came up ahead, five of them, then six, glassy green hills marching upstream against the current, impossible-looking, each one a hill that would break over the bow if she hit it wrong.

"First wave, hit it square," she said, mostly to herself. "Don't fight the trough. Ride the trough."

The Coriander climbed.

The bow went up and up and the world became sky and rainbow and roaring, and then the hull slammed down into the trough with a concussion that rattled fillings, and they were through the first one, and the second was bigger.

She heard the angel's note then — clear, unmistakable, rising out of the chaos on the starboard side, and she understood in one cold flash that the channel bent right below them, that the river they were running curved toward a submerged slab, and that the slab's whistle was singing directly under their starboard quarter.

"Hard port!" she shouted, and threw the wheel herself, and the Coriander heeled, and the third wave — the big one, the one she'd been saving her respect for — took them sideways.

The world went green.

Not over the bow — over the port side, a solid sheet of meltwater coming aboard like a wall falling, cold enough to stop the heart it touched. Wendell heard Sefa go down, heard a crate break loose below and become a live animal in the hold, heard Priya screaming coordinates that were useless now, and the boat was on her beam ends, deck at forty degrees, the starboard rail kissing the river, and Wendell's hands were full of wheel and the wheel was full of nothing because a rudder needs flow and they had stopped flowing. They were sliding. Sideways, down the face of the rapid, toward the angel's note, and the note was singing louder now, and she understood with perfect clarity that in about four seconds the Coriander was going to trip over that slab and roll, and everything after that would be archaeology.

There was one move. There is always one move, and it is always the one that feels like dying: give the river what it wants.

She slammed the throttle full ahead and put the wheel hard to starboard — into the capsize, into the slide, turning the boat's nose *down* the rapid instead of across it, surrendering the line, giving up the channel, choosing speed over control. The hull swung. The starboard rail lifted, groaning, an inch, two inches, and the propeller bit water that wanted to kill it and the Coriander shot down the face of the rapid like a stone thrown by God, straight at the fifth wave, the biggest one, the one shaped like a closed fist.

"Hold ON—"

They hit it bow-first at eleven knots.

The boat stood on her tail. Literally stood — Wendell felt the propeller leave the water and scream, felt the whole vessel go vertical for one long, ringing, impossible second, spray exploding off the bow in a white fan that caught the sun and made a second rainbow inside the first, and then the bow came down into the boil beyond and the Coriander punched through into the outwash and the roar fell away behind them like a door closing.

* * *

Silence. Or near enough — the mutter of the outwash, the tick of the cooling engine, water running off the deck in sheets, and six people breathing like they'd run a mile underwater.

The Coriander wallowed upright in the long green pool below the rapid, listing slightly to port, taking stock of herself. Wendell realized her hands were locked on the wheel and made them let go. Her forearms shook. She looked down at them with mild academic interest, as though they belonged to someone else.

"Head count," she managed.

"Aft, alive, furious," Priya called, voice cracking in the middle of *furious.*

"Galley's a disaster and I'm fine," Sefa said from the deck, where she was sitting in a spreading puddle, holding a dish towel like a weapon. "I'm fine. I'm sitting in the soup course, but I'm fine."

"Below's a zoo," Bo reported up the companionway, "but the engine's turning. Whatever you did up there, don't do it again, my spare mounts are original to the boat."

Ansel had Voss in a grip that had clearly gone from protective to mutual somewhere during the vertical second. Both of them were soaked, white-faced, and clutching each other with the unselfconscious desperation of people who had briefly stopped being strangers. Voss's splint arm was wrapped around Ansel's shoulders. He noticed Wendell looking and didn't let go.

And Odile Marchetti stood at the stern rail, drenched to the skin, hair plastered flat, and she was laughing.

Wendell stared. In thirty years of knowing Odile Marchetti — rival, employer, ghost, whatever she was now — Wendell could count on one hand the times she'd heard the woman laugh, and none of them had sounded like this. This was helpless, undignified, gasping laughter, the laughter of someone who had fully expected to die and been profoundly inconvenienced to survive.

"You should see your face," Odile wheezed. "You should — the rainbow came *through the window* —"

"The rainbow came through the pilothouse window," Priya confirmed, starting to laugh herself, the shaky post-terror laugh that isn't a choice. "I saw my whole life in it. It was very short."

"It was *vertical,*" Sefa said, and then they were all laughing, even Bo up the hatch, even Voss — a rusty, startled sound, like a gate opening after years — the whole crew of them laughing in the spray-soaked aftermath, giddy and stupid and alive, terror transmuting itself in real time into something else, something that would set in the bones like a weld. Wendell felt it happen in her own chest and let it. This was the oldest ritual there was, older than charts, older than rivers: you survive a thing together and the surviving becomes a rope between you, stronger than any contract Odile could print.

She laughed too. It hurt her ribs and she didn't care.

* * *

They anchored in the pool below the rapid and spent the afternoon putting the boat back together. Bo found the loose crate and lashed it down properly, and found, wedged behind the fuel tank where the flood had shoved it, a coil of old climbing rope and a snapped carabiner that he went quiet about in a way Wendell noticed and didn't ask about. Sefa rebuilt the galley and produced a dinner of rice and smoked fish that tasted, everyone agreed, like the best meal any of them had ever eaten, which is the specific magic of nearly dying. The evening came down golden and cold. Somewhere upstream, faint as a far-off door slamming, the glacier spoke — one soft boom, rolling down the canyon, and everyone paused, and no one said anything, and the pause lasted a beat longer than it would have a week ago.

Wendell took the middle watch. Midnight found her alone at the rail with tea going cold in her hands, listening to the pool lap the hull, replaying the rapid. The angel's note. She'd heard it in time. Thirty years ago her father had heard it a half-second late, on the Kettle, and lost a barge and nearly a life, and had made her swear she'd always keep one ear open for the pretty notes. She had. She'd kept it open tonight, and it had saved them, and she should have felt triumphant.

Instead she kept hearing something else underneath the memory — a fragment that had surfaced during the vertical second, when her whole life had flashed horizontal: cracking ice, yes, that was familiar, that lived in her nightmares permanently. But tonight, for the first time, layered under the crack of the ice, there had been a voice. Shouting. Two words, maybe three, torn away by wind and water, in a voice she almost —

Almost.

"Wendell."

Odile, at the pilothouse door, wrapped in a blanket, holding two cups of something steaming. She handed one over. For a while neither woman spoke. The glacier boomed again, farther away, gentler, like thunder remembering itself.

"You saved the boat," Odile said finally.

"I saved the boat."

"Three years ago, on the last run, there was a moment—" Odile stopped. Started again, slower, choosing each word like footing on scree. "There was a moment on the ice field where you made a call, and it was the right call, and nobody thanked you for it. I've thought about that moment every day since. I want you to know that."

Wendell looked at her. The moon was up, silvering the canyon walls, and Odile's face was half light and half dark, and Wendell could not read the dark half at all.

"Is that why you hired me?" she asked. "To relive your moment?"

"No," Odile said. "I hired you because you're the only pilot alive who could have run today's water. But I'll confess the other thing costs me nothing anymore." She turned toward the rail, toward the black water sliding north. "Ask me what happened that night, Wendell. Any night you want. I'll tell you true. I've decided you've earned true."

Wendell held her tea and watched the current and said nothing, because her heart was hammering and because some treacherous part of her — the part that had dreamed of cracked ice and a sinking lantern — whispered that she already knew what happened that night, that the knowledge was close now, close as the angel's note under the hull, rising.

In the morning, Sefa would go into the surveyors' cabin to clean, and find the ledger.

But that was tomorrow. Tonight the river ran cold and quick under the moon, carrying them north, and Wendell stood her watch alone, listening to the water sing.
