## 18. The Ledger

Sefa had a theory about cabins: you could read a person the way you read a river, if you knew where the silt settled.

The Coriander's passengers' cabin was six feet by eight, wedged between the engine room bulkhead and the head, and it smelled like every cabin did — diesel ghosting through the seams, old varnish, the mineral tang of river air that got into everything no matter how many times she scrubbed. But layered over that, in the three weeks since Reprieve, it had acquired the particular smell of two people who kept themselves very tidy and told each other very little. Wool. Boot leather. Something faintly chemical, like the inside of a camera case.

She knocked anyway, out of habit, though she'd been given the standing instruction to clean while they were on deck taking measurements. Voss and Ansel spent hours on deck now, hunched over instruments with tripods and brass fittings, sighting upriver at nothing Sefa could see. Surveyors. That was the word Odile used, and surveyors they remained.

"Housekeeping," Sefa announced to the empty room, and let herself in.

She'd been cleaning this boat for eleven years. She knew its rhythms the way Bo knew his engines — which bolts loosened first, which seams wept in cold water, which corners collected dust like confession. And she knew, without having decided to know it, that something in this cabin had changed since the portage. Before the land bypass, Voss's bunk had been made with parade-ground corners and Ansel's had been a cheerful disaster of maps and fruit peels. Now both bunks were made identically. Same fold at the blanket's edge. Same pillow squared to the frame.

People who expected their rooms to be searched made them identical. She'd seen it before, in another life, in a house in Kavala where her husband kept two sets of books and folded everything twice.

Sefa pulled on her gloves — she wore thin cotton ones below decks, a cook's vanity about her hands — and began at the door, as always, working counterclockwise. Floorboards swept. Under-bunk storage emptied onto a canvas drop cloth and inventoried without touching anything longer than necessary: spare film canisters, a coil of signal wire, a tin of hardtack Ansel had hoarded from Reprieve, a folding ruler, a Bible with the spine cracked open to Job that Sefa suspected belonged to neither of them and had come with the boat's previous owner twenty years ago. She put everything back exactly as it lay. The trick was never to search. The trick was to clean so thoroughly that searching became incidental, and then to remember everything.

The cabin's one table swung on gimbals against the outboard wall, and beneath it hung a chart drawer that stuck in damp weather. It had stuck since before the portage; she remembered cursing it gently while wiping salt off its face. Today it slid free on oiled runners, silent, easy.

Somebody had fixed it. Somebody had been in this drawer recently enough to want it working smoothly.

Sefa stood very still for a moment, listening to the boat. Above her, boots crossed the deck — Priya at the wheel watch, by the lightness of the step. Forward, the muffled argument of water through the intake grate. Nobody coming down.

Inside the drawer: charts of the upper Thessaly, the same ones Odile had distributed, dog-eared and annotated in pencil. A grease-pencil case. A folded oilskin map case, flat, tied with a rubber band gone brittle. And beneath those, wrapped in a chamois cloth, a book.

She should have left it. She knew that even as her gloved fingers closed on the chamois. There was a version of Sefa who cleaned around things like this, who kept her hands on linens and galley steel and let other people carry their own weight. That woman had existed once. Then there had been a hospital corridor in Kavala, and a doctor who wouldn't meet her eyes, and a daughter whose name Sefa now said only on Sundays, alone, facing the water — and after that the woman who cleaned around things had drowned quietly, and this one had taken her place. This one believed that what people hid under their floors would eventually rot the whole house, and that it was better to find it early, while the house still stood.

She carried the bundle to the doorway, into the better light from the companionway, and unwrapped it.

It was a ledger. Not a ship's log — she'd seen enough of those to know the breed — but an account book, bound in gray card stock, the kind sold in any harbor-town stationer for keeping track of fuel and flour. The cover had no title. The first page had no name. Only columns, ruled in green, filled in a small, disciplined hand.

*Date. Party. Consideration. Settled.*

Sefa turned pages with the tips of her gloves, reading the way she read recipes — fast, greedy, hunting for the thing that would ruin or save the dish.

Most of it meant nothing. Payments in figures she didn't recognize — not dollars, not crowns, some third currency rendered in shorthand — against initials and place names. *K.T. — 400 — settled.* *M.V. — 1200 — half, balance on delivery.* A long run of entries marked simply *field,* amounts varying, dates marching upstream week by week like the expedition itself. Someone had been paying their way north, entry by entry, and someone else had been keeping score.

Then, a third of the way in, a name she knew.

*H. Rell — 2,500 — retainer, ongoing.*

Harl Rell. Harbormaster of Reprieve. The man with the laugh like a hinge who'd shaken Wendell's hand on the dock and called her *Captain* with just enough warmth to make it a warning. The man who'd posted Odile's true destination on the harbor board with his own hand and watched, smiling, to see who flinched. Sefa had liked him the way you liked a dog that watched you too closely while you ate.

*Retainer, ongoing.* Paid to keep the dock quiet? To keep the resupply moving? To keep certain ships logged and certain ships forgotten? Sefa thought of the harbor board, of the manifest posted there in Rell's careful clerk's hand — the false one, the one that said *geological survey, equipment only* — and felt the floor of her certainty tilt a degree.

She kept turning pages. Near the back, the entries thinned, became larger, more deliberate. Final settlements. And then the last used page, and on it, two lines.

The first line named a sum so large it took Sefa a moment to parse the zeros. Against it, in the *Party* column: *R.M.*

Odile Marchetti. R for Renata — Odile's first name, which she never used, which appeared nowhere on the charter papers, which Sefa knew only because years ago, drunk on plum brandy after a funeral, Odile had told the whole galley a story about a girl called Renata who'd stolen a boat to spite her father. Sefa had filed the story away the way she filed everything, and here it surfaced now, cold and exact.

*R.M.* And in the final column, where *settled* ran down the rest of the book like a heartbeat: *pending.*

Pending. The largest figure in the book, attached to the woman who owned this expedition, and it was not yet paid. Which meant the expedition was not yet finished. Which meant somebody, somewhere, was waiting on a delivery.

Sefa's mouth had gone dry. She looked at the second line.

It was short. The sum was modest — almost insultingly so, next to Odile's pending fortune, a tenth of it, maybe less. The date was three years old. Three years and two months. Sefa didn't need to do the arithmetic against the calendar; her body did it for her, the way it always did when late September came, the way the river itself seemed to stiffen. The date sat square in the season Tom Lockridge died.

And in the *Party* column, written plainly, without flourish:

*L.*

Below it, in the settlement column, a single word in that same disciplined hand: *retained.*

Sefa stood in the companionway light with the ledger open on her palm and felt the boat rock under her, though the water outside was glass. *L.* One letter. One letter and a payment dated to the worst season of all their lives, entered in a book that lived in the surveyors' chart drawer, wrapped in chamois like something precious, like something being saved for later.

There were only two women on this boat whose names started with L besides — no. There was Priya, P. There was Ansel, A. Odile was O or M or R.M., and R.M. had her own line. Wendell Lockridge. W. Everyone knew it was W. Half the river knew it was W. The *Rivermouth Courier* had spelled it out above the fold three years ago: *CAPTAIN W. LOCKRIDGE SURVIVES THAW TRAGEDY.*

But initials were slippery things. *L* could be a surname. Lockridge. Or it could be a first name the world didn't use — and Sefa thought suddenly, with a lurch, of Odile saying *Renata* with her eyes shut, of how a woman who buried her first name might bury other letters too, might sign herself however the moment required. Or it could be nobody aboard at all. Some company man. Some clerk. Some stranger whose single initial meant nothing.

Except the book had been hidden in a drawer that had been freshly oiled, on a boat carrying sealed crates stamped with a logo nobody recognized, chartered by a woman whose payment was still *pending*, and the only other initial on the last page shared its date with the night Tom Lockridge went into the water.

Sefa closed the ledger. Her hands were steady, which surprised her; her hands shook when she burned her thumb on a skillet, but they went iron-still at moments like this, as if grief and fear boiled off into some residue that stiffened rather than weakened her. She rewrapped the chamois. She replaced the bundle in the drawer exactly as it lay, angle and depth preserved, the brittle rubber band resettled with a fingertip. She pushed the drawer shut until it glided home on its new-oiled runners, and she hated the smoothness of it.

Then she went back to work. That was the discipline, the whole of it: you finished the room. She swept the floorboards. She wiped the gimbal table and greased its pivot because a sticking table was the kind of thing a cook noticed and nobody else did, and she wanted to be nobody else today. She laundered nothing, moved nothing else, disturbed no dust pattern she couldn't restore. When she was done, the cabin smelled of wool and boot leather and faintly of camphor from her cleaning rag, and it was perfect, and she despised how perfect it looked.

On deck, the afternoon had gone gold. The river here ran wide and braided through gravel shoals, the water so full of glacier milk it looked like poured pewter, and the light lay on it in long bars. Voss and Ansel were aft with their tripod, silhouetted against the glare, and Odile stood at the rail near them, hands folded behind her, watching the current with the stillness of a heron. Watching, Sefa thought, the way a creditor watches.

Sefa climbed to the wheelhouse.

Wendell stood at the helm the way she always stood — weight low, forearms resting on the spokes without gripping them, eyes doing that thing Sefa had never gotten tired of watching, flicking from the water to the far bank to the birds and back, reading the river in sentences. Priya had surrendered the wheel and was bent over the chart table, making her neat pencil marks. The wheelhouse smelled of coffee and pencil shavings and, underneath, the cold breath of glacier air coming through the wing vents.

"Soup at six," Sefa said. "Barley and the last of the smoked fish."

"Mm," said Wendell, who was negotiating a channel split by a gravel bar that hadn't existed on any chart, and then, a moment later, as the keel kissed through the deeper lane by inches: "Thank you, Sefa."

Priya glanced up. "You look like you've seen the intake fouled again."

"I look like a woman with soup to make." Sefa said it easily, and it was almost true, and then it wasn't. She looked at Wendell's profile against the pewter water — the jaw set the way it set when the river demanded everything, the small scar through one eyebrow that Tom supposedly gave her with a fishing hook when they were children — and heard herself say, "Captain. When you've a minute. It's about the cleaning."

Wendell's eyes came off the water for the first time in an hour. "Trouble?"

"No trouble I can name yet." Sefa held her gaze. "A question I don't want to ask twice."

Something moved behind Wendell's eyes — she'd been waiting, Sefa realized, for exactly this kind of summons ever since the portage, ever since Bo came up from the bilge looking like a man who'd found a grave in his own engine room. The crew had been carrying separate weights in separate pockets for days, everyone polite, everyone watching. Secrets rot faster in close quarters. She'd told Wendell that herself, over onions, what felt like a lifetime ago.

"Take the wheel a moment," Wendell said to Priya, and stepped back from the spokes.

They stood in the corner of the wheelhouse by the chart table, out of the sightline of the aft deck, and Sefa unwrapped the words the way she'd unwrapped the ledger: plainly, in order, leaving nothing out. The drawer that slid too smoothly. The chamois cloth. The columns. *H. Rell, retainer, ongoing.* The great sum against *R.M.*, pending. And then she lowered her voice further, though the wheelhouse door was shut and the engine covered most things, and gave him the last of it: the date, and the letter, and the word *retained.*

"L," she said. "That's all it says. Just L."

Wendell had gone very still. Stillness on Wendell was not calm; Sefa had learned the difference years ago. Calm Wendell breathed slow and blinked normal. Still Wendell stopped breathing altogether, and her hands — her clever, restless hands that were always moving toward wheel or throttle or cup — hung at her sides like tools set down.

"The date," Wendell said. Her voice came out level. Too level, sanded flat. "Say it again."

Sefa said it again.

"That's four days before." Wendell's eyes had gone somewhere else entirely, somewhere downstream and three years deep. "Four days before we—" She stopped. The sentence stayed stopped. Four days before the ice took the *Provision*, before the night nobody aboard spoke about, before the blank in Wendell's memory that Sefa had watched her circle for three years the way a tongue circles a broken tooth.

"Initials lie," Sefa said gently. "Half the men on this river sign themselves X when the money's dirty. It could be anyone. A broker. A bank. Some company man we've never—"

"It could be me." Wendell said it fast, like pulling a splinter, and then stood there with the wound open, staring at Sefa as if daring her to argue.

"Wendell—"

"Three years, Sefa. I don't have that night. I have the morning after, and wet clothes that weren't mine, and nothing between sunset and dawn but a hole." Wendell's voice dropped to almost nothing. "Everyone tells me what happened. Odile tells it one way. The inquiry wrote it another. Tom's fishing partner won't look at me, which is its own kind of testimony. And now there's a book in my hold that says somebody signed L got paid *retained* four days before my brother died, and I am the only person on this boat who cannot swear she wasn't standing wherever that signature was signed." She laughed, one dry note with no humor in it. "Do you understand what that's like? To be the prime suspect in your own missing evening?"

Sefa reached out and gripped Wendell's forearm — hard, cook-strong, the grip she used to haul people back from stoves and rails. "I understand what it's like to fill a hole with whatever's nearest," she said. "Don't fill yours with guilt. Guilt is the easiest thing to pour. It sets fastest."

For a moment Wendell leaned into the grip, actually leaned, this woman who steered rapids with her elbows loose and took no one's arm on icy decks. Then she straightened, and Sefa watched the captain come back up through her like a diver surfacing — the shoulders squaring, the eyes refocusing on the present tense.

"Nobody else knows," Wendell said. "Not Bo, not Priya. Not yet."

"They'll need to know."

"When I know what it means. If I bring them an L and no answer, this boat turns into a courtroom, and I can't run white water with a jury in the wheelhouse." Wendell looked at the ledger-shaped bulge where the chamois bundle rode inside Sefa's apron pocket. "Can you copy it? Without them knowing it's gone?"

"Already planned to. Flour-dusted paper over the pages, pencil sideways, like tracing a stencil. Old trick. It'll take an hour, and I'll do it during the dinner service when they're both on deck." Sefa paused. "And the original?"

"Back in the drawer. Exactly where it lives." Wendell's jaw tightened. "If it disappears, whoever owns that book learns someone found it, and I'd rather they keep sleeping."

"And when you know what it means?"

Wendell looked out the wheelhouse windows at the river — at the braided channels splitting and joining, silver threads over gravel, each one promising a different way through and only one deep enough to float them home. Somewhere ahead, beyond the bend, the calving fields waited with their artillery weather, and behind them Reprieve waited with Harl Rell's hinge-door smile, and somewhere in the middle of all that geography sat a single letter with three years of silence stacked on top of it.

"Then," Wendell said quietly, "this crew gets every piece of it. Even the pieces that cut me." She put her hand back on the wheel, and the boat answered her before Priya had fully yielded it, as if the *Coriander* knew its captain's touch. "Go make your soup, Sefa. And Sefa — thank you for cleaning."

Sefa went. At the top of the companionway ladder she paused, one hand on the rail, and looked back. Wendell stood at the helm with the light failing gold around her, steering the pewter water, and her lips were moving — not prayer, Sefa thought, though she couldn't swear to it. Counting, maybe. Channels, miles, days left in the season.

Or one letter, said over and over, like a name she was trying to remember whether it was hers.

Down in the galley, Sefa tied on her apron, slid the ledger out, and laid her flour-dusted paper across the first page. Outside, the river ran on toward the ice, carrying all of them — the guilty, the grieving, and the letter L — at nine miles an hour, whether they were ready or not.
