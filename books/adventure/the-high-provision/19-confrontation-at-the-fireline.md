## 19. Confrontation at the Fireline

The fire burned low in its iron ring on the aft deck, the way Wendell liked it past midnight — more coals than flame, a red heart in a black country of water and stone. They had tied up below a bluff where the river bent north, and the cold came off the meltwater like something alive, pressing against her back while she sat facing the coals.

She heard Odile's boots on the deck plating before she saw her. She had always known that walk — unhurried, precise, the walk of a woman who had never once needed to hurry anywhere because she'd already arranged for the world to come to her.

"You should be sleeping," Odile said. "Tomorrow we run the shelf ice."

"Sit down."

Odile paused. In the coal-light her face was half ember, half shadow, and older than Wendell remembered it being three weeks ago, back on the dock in the salt-smell of home. She sat. She did it gracefully, folding onto the crate across the fire as if she'd been invited to exactly this conversation all along, which was one of the things Wendell had hated about her for twenty years and missed for three.

Wendell took the ledger from inside her coat. She'd been carrying it there for two days, against her ribs, like a splinter she couldn't reach. She laid it on the deck between them, on the iron plate, where the firelight could read it too.

"Found this in your surveyors' cabin," she said. "Sefa found it. Payments to the harbormaster at Reprieve. And a signature at the bottom that isn't mine."

Odile looked at the ledger the way a card player looks at a hand already shown: no surprise, only calculation, and beneath the calculation something tired.

"It's not what you think."

"I don't think anything yet," Wendell said. "That's the problem. I've spent three years not thinking about one night, and now every piece of paper on this boat is trying to think for me. So I'm going to ask you plainly, Odile, and you're going to answer me plainly, because we are four days from calving fields and I cannot steer a boat full of questions." She leaned forward. The coals popped. "What was Tom doing on the river that night? What was the deal?"

For a long moment Odile said nothing. Somewhere across the water an owl called once and got no answer. Then Odile reached out and turned the ledger toward herself, studying her own handwriting as if it belonged to a stranger.

"Do you remember," she said quietly, "the winter we ran freight together? Before either of us had a boat worth the name. You told me the river forgives everything except lies told to yourself."

"I remember telling you a lot of things you didn't listen to."

"This is me listening." She closed the ledger. Her hands stayed flat on its cover. "There was a deal. You're right. Three years ago, before the last run — a company wanted access to the upper Thessaly. Core samples, mineral survey, the usual language. They came to me because I knew the route and they thought I owned the route, which shows you what they understood about rivers. I refused them. Twice. The third time they didn't ask. They informed me the permits were already filed under my expedition name. My signature — real enough. Money had moved through my accounts. If I fought it, I would drown in court for a decade while they drilled anyway. If I went along, I kept control of who touched the high country."

"And Tom?"

"Tom found out." Something crossed Odile's face then, quick as a fish under ice — grief, or its well-practiced impersonation. "He'd been running his own survey ahead of yours. He found the cache site early. He found things in the ice that weren't supposed to be there. Old sample cores, test results, documents the company had buried rather than report. Proof the upper river was already poisoned by their first exploratory season and they'd hidden it. He contacted me. He said he was going to take it public."

"So you helped him."

"I tried to manage him," Odile said, and the words came out flat and honest and terrible. "I thought I could slow him down, route the evidence through lawyers, through people who could survive the company's counterattack. He wouldn't wait. He was going to move the cache contents downriver himself, on your boat, without telling you what he was carrying — because he knew you'd say no. Not because of danger. Because of principle. Your brother was the most principled man I ever met and it made him reckless as a drunk."

The fire shifted. A knot cracked and collapsed into orange dust.

"That's not an answer," Wendell said. "That's a eulogy. What happened on the water?"

"The ice happened on the water." Odile met her eyes and held them, and Wendell had to give her this much — she didn't flinch. "A warm front came down two days early. The calving fields started shedding a week ahead of every forecast. Tom was above the fields when the shelf let go. His boat was caught in the surge. That's the truth, Wendell. Weather. Ice. Bad luck with bad timing. I have carried it for three years and I will carry it to my grave if I have to."

"Then why," Wendell said slowly, "does the harbormaster at Reprieve have payments from you dated after Tom died?"

Silence. The river spoke against the hull, patient, indifferent.

Odile looked down at the ledger. When she answered, her voice had lost some of its polish, like paint worn away at the edges by a thumb rubbed over it too many times.

"Because after he died, the company came back to me with an offer. Silence, purchased. A settlement. I took it. I took their money and I signed their confidentiality and I walked away from the whole rotten business, and yes — part of that money went to making sure certain records at Reprieve stayed misfiled, stay unread. Call it bribery if you want the clean word. I call it buying time." She raised her eyes. "I'm not proud of it. I'm not asking you to be proud of me. But Tom's death was not murder, Wendell. It was weather. And the rest — the rest is shame, not crime."

Wendell sat very still. It was the story Odile had told everyone for three years, and hearing it again, here, in the firelight, with the ledger between them, she noticed what she had never let herself notice before: how smooth it was. How complete. Every question answered before it was asked. Real stories had holes in them. Real nights had gaps. This account was lacquered.

"There's one thing wrong with it," Wendell said.

"Tell me."

"Me." She stood. Her legs felt strange, too long, like the deck had shifted under her. She paced to the rail and back, the cold biting through her coat. "You said Tom was going to move the cache contents without telling me. But Tom wouldn't have done that. He'd have argued with me for a month first. He argued with me about whether to replace a galley window. He'd have—" She stopped. Pressed her palms flat against the rail. The iron was cold enough to burn. "And there's another thing. The night before he died, I was with him. Wasn't I. On the water. Above the fields."

Odile had gone very still in the way stones are still.

"You don't remember that night," Odile said carefully. "The doctors—"

"The doctors said trauma. The doctors said it might come back or it might not. Nobody said it wasn't there." Wendell turned to face her. "But you know what I do remember, Odile? Fragments. Since we left port they've been coming back. Cracking ice. Cold water. Someone shouting. And a lantern going down — Tom's lantern, sinking, still lit, green glass, the one our father gave him." Her voice did something she didn't authorize; she dragged it back under control. "I was there. Don't tell me I wasn't. You know I was there, because you keep building your story around the hole where I used to be."

"Wendell—"

"What plan was I part of?" The question came out of her like something breaking loose from deep gravel. "Because I've been chewing on it since the ledger surfaced, and there's a shape under all your smooth answers, Odile, and the shape has my initials on it. The ledger has a payment signed 'L.' You said tonight that Tom contacted you. Who did Tom contact *first*? Who did he trust first? Whose boat was he going to use?" She crossed back toward the fire, and stopped, and looked down at Odile sitting small and composed in the coal-light. "Say it. Whatever it is. Say it to my face."

Odile rose slowly. For the first time since the dock, she looked her age, and older — a woman standing at the edge of something she'd paid a great deal to avoid looking into. The wind came off the glacier country and stirred the coals so they flared briefly, throwing both their shadows huge and wavering against the cabin house.

"You agreed," Odile said.

The two words landed like ballast dropped from height.

"That night — the night before the accident — you were there. All three of us. We met on the water above the fields because Tom insisted on neutral ground, though God knows why he thought any ground near that ice was neutral." Odile's voice was steady but her hands were not; she clasped them behind her back like a cadet. "Tom laid it out. The cache, the evidence, the plan to bring it down and break the company open. And the risk — he was honest about the risk, the calving season coming early, the window closing. And you said yes, Wendell. You shook hands on it. You said, 'We run it together, first light.' Those were your words. I've had three years to forget them and I can't."

"No." Wendell heard herself say it, thin and far away. "No, I would have—"

"You want the truth or the comfortable version?" Odile's composure cracked at last, just a hairline, just enough to show heat underneath. "You asked for plain. Here is plain: your brother died running a plan you approved, on a river you chose, in weather you both underestimated. And when it went wrong — when the shelf came down and his boat went under and you went into that water after him and nearly died yourself — you lost the memory of agreeing, and the world kindly rearranged itself into a story where you were innocent. Where we were all innocent, except the weather." She stepped closer. Close enough that Wendell could smell woodsmoke and old perfume and, beneath it, fear. "I have watched you build a life on that missing night. I watched you swear off the river, sell your gear, hire a crew that never asks about the past. I let you do it. Maybe that's my sin — bigger than the bribes. I let you believe the lie because the lie was kinder than the ledger."

The fire had burned almost to nothing. Wendell became aware of it the way you become aware of your heartbeat in a silent room — the coals ticking as they cooled, the dark pressing in.

"If I agreed," she said, and her voice sounded like someone else's, scraped hollow, "then why does the memory feel like a warning? Why do the fragments feel like me saying *stop*?"

"Memory is a river too," Odile said softly. "It erodes whatever's easiest. Guilt erodes fastest of all. You've spent three years polishing your guilt into something shaped like innocence, and now the fragments are surfacing, and you can't tell anymore which way they cut. I understand that better than you know." She reached into her coat and drew out an envelope, worn soft at the corners, and held it between them without offering it. "There's more. There's always more. But I'll tell you this much for free, since you asked for plain: the company didn't just inform me of the deal three years ago. They showed me something. A document. With your signature on it. Dated before Tom ever found the cache. And when you're ready to hear what it says — really ready, not angry-ready, not grief-ready — I'll show you the copy I kept."

"Give it to me now."

"Not tonight." Odile pocketed the envelope. "Tonight you'd burn it, or I would, or we'd both stand here until the ice takes the boat, and none of that serves anyone. Sleep on it. Tomorrow we run the shelf. After the fields — after you've seen what's actually up there — ask me again."

She turned to go. Wendell caught her arm. The grip surprised them both; Odile didn't pull away.

"If you're lying," Wendell said, "if any part of this is built to protect yourself —"

"I'm lying somewhere," Odile said. "I've been lying somewhere for three years; even I've lost the map of it. That's why you need the cache, Wendell. Not my version. Not your fragments. The thing itself. Tom kept records. Tom kept *everything*." For one unguarded second her face opened like a door in wind, and what was behind it was not guilt exactly, and not grief exactly, but the place where the two had fused into something load-bearing. "He kept everything. Even about me."

She freed her arm, gently, and went below.

Wendell stood alone at the dead fire a long time. The river ran black and silver under the stars, going north without opinion, and she gripped the rail and made herself say it aloud, once, quietly, to the water and the dark and the ghost of a man with a green-glassed lantern:

*Did I agree?*

The water didn't answer. Water never did. But somewhere in the welded dark beneath her feet, in a compartment Bo hadn't known he'd found the edge of, the ship's log lay waiting with its torn pages and its ghost-text, holding the shape of every word she couldn't remember writing — and tomorrow, or the day after, when the fear got quiet enough, she was going to go down into the bilge with a lamp and rake the light across those bindings until the dead spoke.

* * *

She didn't sleep. At some hour past deepest dark she climbed to the pilothouse and sat at the wheel she wasn't steering and watched the northern sky, where a faint bruise-colored glow was starting along the horizon — light off the ice fields, miles upstream, the glacier shining in the night like something thinking.

Behind her, boots on the stairs. Priya, wrapped in a blanket, a mug in each hand. She set one on the chart table beside Wendell without a word and stood looking north at the glow.

"Couldn't sleep either?" Wendell asked.

Priya was quiet a moment. "I heard voices on deck. I wasn't listening. Mostly." She sipped. "Captain — the logbook from the compartment. The one with the pages torn out. I've been thinking about it. Bindings hold impressions. Pressure leaves ghosts in paper, even after the page is gone. With raking light, and patience, sometimes you can read what isn't there anymore."

Wendell turned slowly on the wheel seat. The glow off the ice lit half her face, and Priya, watching her, seemed to understand without being told that the question had already changed from *whether* to *when*.

"How soon can you start?" Wendell said.
