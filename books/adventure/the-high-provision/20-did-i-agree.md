## 20. Did I Agree?

Sleep didn't come. It circled the bunk like a gull over a fish that wouldn't surface, and around two in the morning Wendell gave up pretending and swung her feet to the cold deck planking.

The lamp in her cabin threw a small yellow room against all that black river. She sat at the chart table with Odile's words still sitting in her chest like swallowed ballast. *You agreed to the plan, Wendell. You were there when we shook on it.* The ledger lay between her hands where she'd left it, Sefa's photograph of it tucked inside like a pressed flower. *L.* Her own initial, signed neat as a signature on a freight manifest.

She turned her left hand over and looked at the palm. No scar there. But three years ago she'd woken in a hospital in Graywater with a split lip, a fractured wrist, and a hole in her memory the size of a night — and nobody had ever been able to tell her whether the hole was damage or mercy.

*Did I agree?*

It was a question you couldn't ask out loud on this boat. Not yet. Bo was carrying Tom's nameplate around like a coal in his pocket; Priya had the coordinates copied in her precise archivist's hand; Sefa was watching everyone eat for signs of rot. If Wendell walked into the galley tomorrow and said *maybe I helped kill my own brother*, the Coriander would come apart at the seams, and they still had four days of high water between here and the calving fields.

So she did what she always did with a problem she couldn't solve by feel: she went looking for paper.

* * *

The welded compartment was under the forward hold, behind the fresh-water tank, a space barely big enough for a man to kneel in. Bo had cut the seam open with a plasma torch two weeks ago and hadn't resealed it — he'd fitted a hatch instead, plain steel, no lock, because he said locks were how people lied to each other politely. Wendell had not gone down there since. She had stood at the top of the ladder while Bo lifted out the rope, the broken carabiner, the nameplate, and she had felt the whole boat tilt under her even though the water was flat.

Now she went down.

The bilge smelled of diesel and cold iron and old river. Her headlamp found the hatch latch on the second try. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth exactly the way she would have wrapped them, were the things Bo hadn't carried up that day: a canvas tool roll, a tin of spare fuses, and the ship's log from the last upriver voyage — the one that killed Tom.

She'd known it was down here. That was the ugly part. Some part of her had known since the compartment came open, and some other part had decided, without consulting her, that she wasn't ready. Three years of not-ready. She took the log out with both hands, the way you take something out of a fire.

It was a standard Thessaly Line logbook, cloth-bound, the spine cracked white from use. Her handwriting started on the first page — *Coriander, departure 0400, weather fair, cargo per manifest* — and ran forward through six weeks of soundings, sandbars, engine hours, and small dry notes in Tom's hand whenever he'd taken a watch. *Priya caught a grayling bigger than the frying pan. Sefa says we're out of cinnamon, which is apparently a crisis.* Reading him was like hearing his voice across a crowded dock; she had to stop twice, forehead against the cool steel of the hull, before she could keep going.

The final week was gone.

Not damaged. Gone. Seven leaves razored out close along the spine, so clean it had to have been done deliberately, with a straight edge, by someone who knew books. She turned the stubbled pages back and forth. Before the gap, everything ordinary. After the gap, an entry in her own writing dated the morning after — *returned to Graywater, all hands accounted, vessel sustained minor ice damage* — flat as a receipt, as if the week in between had never existed and the river had simply skipped a stitch.

She remembered tearing pages out of logs before. Bad runs, mistakes she didn't want held up to her forever. It was a habit she'd learned from her father and hated in herself. Had she done this? Could she have sat in this cabin with a razor and cut out the week her brother died, and then forgotten doing it?

Her hands were shaking. She set the log flat on the tool crate and made herself breathe the way she breathed in the Narrows — four counts in, hold, four counts out — until the shaking dropped to a tremor.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing she'd almost missed, the thing the lamplight had shown her and her panic had nearly buried: torn pages leave ghosts.

She'd known that once. Every old river pilot knew it, the way they knew bird behavior and gravel bars — you learned it from men who kept double books, or from harbormasters who audited them. Ink presses. Even the best paper holds the shape of the pressure that wrote on the page above it, especially in a book this well-used, where every leaf had been written on with a heavy hand braced against a moving deck. Tear the page away and the impression stayed behind, printed backward on the sheet beneath, faint as breath on glass.

You just needed light that came in low enough to catch it.

* * *

Raking light. That was the term Priya used, back when — no. Not Priya. Someone else. A surveyor on a government run, years ago, showing her how to read a faded chart: *Don't look at the ink, Lockridge. Look at what the ink did to the paper.* She couldn't remember his name. She could remember the angle of the light.

She climbed up to her cabin with the log hugged to her chest like a child, checked the passageway — empty, the boat breathing around her, Bo's engine ticking as it cooled, Sefa's galley dark — and shut her door.

Then she built the rig.

She killed the overhead lamp. She propped the log upright against the chart case, opened to the first torn stub, and took her reading lamp — the brass one, the one Tom had given her for her thirtieth birthday, *so you can read your charts and I can read my trash novels on watch* — and held it low and flat, almost level with the page, its beam skimming across the paper at a sliver of an angle.

At first: nothing. Just the tooth of the paper, the shadow of the binding cord, her own pulse tapping in her ears.

She lowered the lamp another inch. Moved it left. Held her breath.

Lines rose out of the paper like fish coming up to feed.

Backward, mirror-flipped, broken where the pen had skipped — but lines. Whole paragraphs of them, pressed deep by whoever had written hard that week, deeper than her usual hand. She stared until her eyes watered, then fetched the little steel rule and a pencil and began, letter by letter, transcribing the reversed impressions onto a blank chart margin, flipping each word in her head. It was slow as sounding a channel by hand, pole by pole, and it was the most important work she had ever done at this table.

The first legible fragments were hers.

*—wind backing north, running late, Odile wants—*

Her own name for Odile, uncapitalized, casual, the way you write about someone you see every day. So they had been together on the boat that week. Odile aboard, in the final days. That much matched the story Odile told on the porch at Reprieve. Wendell wrote it down and made herself keep going.

*—cache located ahead of schedule. T. furious. Says contents wrong, says—*

T. Tom. Her pencil stopped. *Says contents wrong.* Not *we found the cache and it was fine.* Wrong. He'd found something in it that shouldn't be there, or something missing that should be there, and he'd been furious about it, and she had written it down at the time and then cut it out of history and forgotten it.

The next impression was shallower, a different pen, a lighter hand. Tom's. She knew his script the way she knew his laugh, even mirrored, even half-gone.

*—do not move before inspection. W. agrees. O.—*

And there the line broke off against a tear in the paper, and Wendell sat very still in the dark with the lamp trembling in her grip.

*W. agrees.*

Agreed to what? To waiting? To inspection? To *not moving* whatever Tom had found — and if she'd agreed to that, then either she'd broken her word, or someone else had moved it, or—

Or Odile was telling the truth, and Wendell had agreed to something else entirely, something that lived in the pages she hadn't reached yet.

She worked until the porthole glass went from black to iron-gray. She filled two full margins of the chart. Most of it was navigation — soundings, bearings, the ordinary litany of a working river — and she logged those automatically, her pilot's mind filing them even now, noting that they'd taken a line through the upper bends that no sane captain would attempt in July. But five entries mattered. Five entries she copied out clean on a separate sheet, in order:

1. *Wind backing north. Running late. Odile wants speed over caution. Refused.*
2. *Cache located ahead of schedule. T. furious. Says contents wrong, says somebody got here first.*
3. *(Tom's hand)* *Do not move before inspection. W. agrees. O.—*
4. *(her own hand, pressed so hard the nib had nearly scored through)* *O. says the company man is coming up tomorrow. T. says nothing comes off that ice until I've seen it myself.*
5. The last one was shallowest, written fast, maybe standing up, maybe in the dark. Only three words survived the tear, and they were in a hand she couldn't place — not hers, not Tom's, not Odile's careful schoolroom script. Something smaller, cramped, urgent:

*—he knows. Tell—*

Tell who. Tell what. Who knows.

Wendell put down the pencil. Her cabin was cold; she realized she'd been sweating anyway. Outside, the river spoke its constant low sentence against the hull, and somewhere far upstream — too far to be real, surely, too far to hear — the ice boomed once, a sound like a door closing in a very large house.

She looked at the fifth fragment a long time. Then she looked at the fourth, at her own handwriting gouged into the page three years ago by a woman who cared enough about something to press hard enough to leave a ghost. *Nothing comes off that ice until I've seen it myself.* That didn't read like a woman agreeing to sabotage. That read like a woman standing guard.

But Odile had looked her in the eye on the harbormaster's porch and said *you agreed,* and Odile had no reason left to lie about the small things — she'd already confessed to the large ones.

Unless the lie was precisely there. Unless the whole confession had been built like a weir, designed to turn Wendell's suspicion away from the true channel and into the slack water of self-doubt, where it would circle forever and drown quietly.

There was one way to know, and it wasn't in this book. The impressions were fragments — a week of entries surviving as shadows, whole sentences lost wherever the pen had lifted or the paper had flexed. Somewhere in that torn-out week was the night itself. The cracking ice. The shout. The lantern going down. And somewhere in these ghost-lines might be the hour before it, the decision, the agreement or the refusal, spelled out in her own hand.

She gathered the pages, the log, the lamp, and stood in the gray light with her heart going hard.

She couldn't do this alone. Not because she lacked the patience — she could sit at this table until the ice came down — but because she no longer trusted her own eyes. A woman who could lose a week of her life could lose a line, misread a word, see what she feared instead of what was pressed into the paper. She needed someone whose whole trade was the recovery of lost text, someone methodical enough to work by rule and not by hope.

She needed Priya.

Which meant opening the compartment's secret wider, handing a stranger-to-that-night the first real words Tom had spoken in three years — *somebody got here first, do not move before inspection* — and trusting her found family with the possibility, still live, still unspeakable, that their captain had signed her brother's death warrant and forgotten it.

Wendell rolled the transcription sheet tight, tucked it inside her coat, and picked up the log.

Up on deck, dawn was coming up the color of tin. The river ran high and quick beneath a sky the size of everything. Two days to the calving fields. Behind her, faint under the engine noise, she thought she heard the glacier again — boom, and boom — patient as a debt.

She knocked on the archivist's door.

"Priya," she said, when the latch clicked open and a sleep-creased face peered out. "I need your eyes. And I need you to promise me something first."

Priya looked at the log in her captain's hands, and at her face, and whatever she saw there made her step back and hold the door wide.

"Come in," Priya said. "Tell me everything."
