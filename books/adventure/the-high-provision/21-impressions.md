## 21. Impressions

Priya had spent eleven years of her life coaxing dead words out of dead paper, and she had learned one thing above all others: documents did not want to die. Ink faded, pages burned, water dissolved — and still the words fought. A scribe pressing too hard left a groove that outlived the ink. A page pressed against wet writing carried its neighbor's ghost to the next leaf. Paper held memory the way riverbeds held the shape of floods long after the water moved on.

She had thought she'd left that work behind her when she signed on with Wendell Lockridge. She had told herself she wanted a life where things stayed found. Where nothing needed recovering because nothing got lost.

Now she sat in the galley at two in the morning with a dead man's logbook under a raking lamp, and every skill she'd buried came back like it had never gone anywhere.

The log was Bo's discovery, pulled from the welded compartment beneath the hold — Tom Lockridge's compartment, though none of them had known it existed until three days ago. The nameplate still sat on Bo's workbench, wrapped in a rag like something wounded. The binding impressions were visible to the naked eye if you knew what you were looking at: shallow troughs in the last surviving page where a pen had once pressed through the torn-out leaves above it. To Priya they looked like braille written by someone drowning.

She'd taken the log without telling anyone except Wendell. That had been the deal — Wendell had put the book in Priya's hands with an expression Priya had never seen on her before, something stripped and young, and said, "If there's anything of him in there, I want to know. Even if it's bad. Especially if it's bad." And then she'd gone up to stand her watch like a woman walking into weather.

Priya understood that kind of courage. It was not the courage of the climb or the rapids. It was quieter and worse.

* * *

The problem was light. Ordinary light fell flat across paper and showed you only what the surface wanted to show. But light laid low, skimming nearly parallel to the page, caught everything — the tooth of the paper, the ridges where fibers had been compressed, the faint silvered valleys of old pressure. Archivists called it raking light. Priya had spent whole months of her former life in darkened rooms with a single adjustable lamp, reading letters that had been scraped away four hundred years ago, palimpsests under palimpsests.

The Coriander's galley at night was as good as any conservation lab, if you ignored the smell of Sefa's stockpot and the gentle rock of the river underneath everything.

Priya worked methodically. First she photographed the final page under normal light, timestamped and logged, because procedure mattered even when no one would ever check it. Then she killed the overheads and angled the small lamp down to fifteen degrees, then ten, then five, moving it in slow arcs while she watched the paper through her loupe.

The first ghost appeared within minutes.

It was just a word at first — *samples* — surfacing out of the texture like a fish rising. Her breath went shallow. She adjusted the angle a fraction, and more came: a line, then half a paragraph, the strokes legible in negative relief, each letter a tiny trench in the page.

She wrote as she read, transcribing into her notebook with an archivist's discipline even though her hands were not entirely steady. This was the part of the old work she had loved — the moment of emergence, the dead voice clearing its throat. She had forgotten how much she loved it. She had made herself forget.

*Day 19 above the Kestrel. Found the cut in the east bank where the melt goes down. Cache is real.*

She read it twice. Found the cut. The cache is real.

Tom Lockridge had known about the High Provision before the expedition ever reached it. Weeks early, if the day-marks meant what they seemed to mean. Which meant he hadn't stumbled onto it. He'd been looking.

Priya sat back and let the boat rock her for a moment, listening to the river through the hull, the tick of the cooling stove. Above her, footsteps crossed the deck — Voss's watch, his gait still favoring the wrist Wendell had broken in the Narrows. Everyone aboard was carrying something now. The boat felt like a sealed jar with too many live things in it.

She bent back to the page.

*Day 20. Opened the outer hatch. Contents logged:*

The list that followed was long, and Priya transcribed every item, her pen hurrying: core sample cases, numbered. Water assay results in sealed tubes. A steel document case, locked, contents unknown. Company correspondence — she could make out fragments of letterhead, a name she didn't recognize yet, figures. And then, near the bottom of the inventory, a line that stopped her pen mid-stroke:

*One crate marked DO NOT MOVE BEFORE INSPECTION. Left it sealed. Something in there isn't samples.*

Something in there isn't samples.

She looked at the words for a long time. Outside, far off to the north, the glacier spoke — a deep boom rolling down the valley like a door slammed in another room. The calving had been getting closer every day, the intervals shorter. They were running out of season, everyone knew it, nobody said it. But this was a different kind of countdown. This was a dead man counting down toward them from three years away.

* * *

Toward dawn she hit the wall — the place where the impressions simply stopped, where whoever had torn the pages out had torn carefully enough to leave nothing readable in the final quarter-inch. She tried every angle, every intensity, even wetting the page slightly the way she'd been taught for certain papers, and got nothing but blank texture and her own frustration staring back at her.

But the binding impressions weren't the only trace. There were also the offset ghosts — the mirror-image transfers where the final written page had pressed against the leaf below it before the ink dried. Those ran backward, reversed, faint as breath on glass. She photographed them all and spent an hour flipping the images, enhancing contrast on the boat's battered laptop until her eyes burned.

That was where she found the entries Tom had written on his actual last page — the page whose ink had bled downward before it vanished.

*O. was on the dock at Reprieve when I came through. Said she was sending a survey team up behind us. Asked me to wait for them.*

*I didn't answer.*

Then, lower on the page, the handwriting deteriorating, hurried:

*Someone moved my cache markers. I set them high on the east moraine. They've been reset west, toward the calving fields. Someone wants anyone following me to end up on the ice when it goes.*

And then the last line. The very last line, the deepest impression in the whole book, pressed so hard the pen had nearly gone through:

*O. knows. Tell W.*

Priya sat motionless for so long that the lamp's bulb began to buzz in the silence.

Tell W.

Wendell. He had been writing to his sister. He had known — somehow, in those final days — that he might not make it back, and he had aimed his last words past his own death directly at her. *Tell W.* Not tell the company, not tell the authorities, not even tell Odile Marchetti, who was supposedly the one who'd financed all of it.

Tell Wendell.

Which meant Tom hadn't trusted Odile either. Or — worse, and Priya felt the cold of this move up her arms slowly — which meant Tom had trusted Odile completely, right up until the moment he wrote that line, and something between the dock at Reprieve and this final entry had broken that trust. Either way, the polished story Odile had told on the harbormaster's porch — salvage, equipment, bad weather — had just developed a crack wide enough to drive a riverboat through.

Because if Tom had discovered the cache weeks early, then the expedition's official purpose was already a lie. And if someone had moved his cache markers to route followers onto the calving fields — and the accident, the one that killed Tom and hollowed Wendell out and took her memory of the final night, had happened on the ice —

Then the weather hadn't killed anyone. Someone had drawn a map to a grave and waited to see who walked it.

Priya closed the notebook. Her hands were shaking now and she let them. In her old life she had handled terrible documents before — confessions, denunciations, the bureaucratic paperwork of atrocities — and she had prided herself on professional detachment. But this wasn't parchment under glass in a climate-controlled room. This was the brother of the woman who slept twelve feet above her, the brother of the crew that had become, without anyone ever saying the word aloud, her family.

The dead brother speaking. That was what this was. And what he was saying was: *I knew. I tried to warn you. Nobody listened in time.*

* * *

She didn't sleep. At some point she heard Sefa rise and start the morning bread, and rather than explain herself she gathered the log, the notebook, the photographs, and climbed to the wheelhouse, where Wendell stood her dawn watch alone, hands loose on the wheel, eyes reading the water the way other people read scripture.

Priya stood beside her without speaking for a while. The river here ran green-white with glacial flour, fast and muscular, banks closing in. Ahead, somewhere beyond the next bend, the calving fields waited with their artillery booms.

"I finished," Priya said finally. "There's a lot. I'll give you all of it. But there are three things you need to hear now."

Wendell didn't look away from the channel. "Go."

"Your brother found the cache before the expedition reached it. Day nineteen. He logged everything inside — samples, assays, a locked document case. One crate he marked do-not-move-before-inspection." Priya paused. "He wrote that whatever was in it wasn't samples."

Something moved through Wendell's face — small, controlled, expensive to control. "What else."

"Someone moved his cache markers off the east moraine and reset them over the calving fields. He logged it. He knew someone was steering people onto the ice." Priya made herself say the rest plainly, because softening it would be worse. "And the last thing he wrote — the deepest impression in the whole book, like he was bearing down hard enough to push it through time — was 'O. knows. Tell W.'"

The Coriander held her line through the current. Wendell's hands never moved. But Priya, watching, saw the exact moment the words landed — saw it in the stillness that came over her, total and ringing, like the pause after ice cracks.

"How long have you known about Odile?" Wendell asked quietly.

"Since five minutes ago. The ledger pointed at her initial. This—" Priya touched the notebook. "This points at her knowledge. Neither proves what she did. But together they prove your brother died knowing something, and trying to reach you, and that the story we've all been sailing on doesn't hold water anymore."

Wendell finally turned. In the grey dawn light her eyes were the same color as the river, and just as unreadable, and just as full of things moving underneath.

"She told me the cache was hers. Financed it, equipped it, lost it to the flood year." Wendell's voice was flat. "If Tom found it on day nineteen — before the main party ever got there — then either she lied to me on that porch, or she sent him looking and never expected him to find anything worth killing for."

"Or both," Priya said. "Archivist's rule: when two stories can't both be true, don't pick the one you like. Pull everything apart until the evidence picks for you."

A sound that was almost a laugh escaped Wendell, brief and dry. "You sound like him. Tom used to say the river keeps honest books."

"He sounds like someone worth keeping honest books for."

Wendell looked north again, toward the booms coming down the valley at shorter intervals now, regular as a heartbeat accelerating. When she spoke, her voice had changed — lower, settled, decided in the way Priya had learned to recognize as the most dangerous thing aboard.

"Wake Bo and Sefa. Get Ansel too — and watch her face when she hears this. Then meet me in the galley at eight bells." A beat. "We're going to talk about whether we turn around."

"And if we don't?"

Wendell's jaw tightened around something old and newly sharpened.

"Then somebody on this river owes my brother a delivery," she said, "and I intend to hand-carry it."
