## 22. Mutiny Weather

Bo had held the nameplate in his hands for two days before he could make himself carry it up the ladder.

Two days of running the engines, checking the seals, eating Sefa's food, laughing at Priya's terrible card shuffling — all of it performed with a dead man's name burning a hole in his breast pocket like a coal that wouldn't cool. He'd told himself he was waiting for the right moment, but that was a lie, and Bo was too old a river man to lie to himself for long. He was waiting because he was afraid. Afraid of what it would do to Wendell. Afraid of what it would do to all of them.

T. LOCKRIDGE. Brass, tarnished green at the edges, the screws still in it where someone had pried it off a door. He'd turned it over a hundred times in the dark of the engine room. He'd held it up to the work lamp and read it until the letters stopped looking like letters. He'd thought about Tom Lockridge exactly once — the man had died before Bo signed on, but his ghost was everywhere on this boat, in the way Wendell stood at the wheel, in the extra coil of line stowed just so, in the name Sefa still said sometimes when she set a third plate out of habit and caught herself.

The river that morning was running wide and gray-green, the banks stepping up into foothills that had lost their trees somewhere in the last hundred miles. Cold came off the water in sheets. The Coriander pushed north against a current that wanted her gone, and Bo climbed the ladder to the main deck with the nameplate in his fist and his heart going like a fouled pump.

Sefa was at the rail with coffee. Priya was mending a net. Wendell was at the wheel, of course — she was always at the wheel now, more than she needed to be, as if the boat would drift off course the moment she let go of it.

"Everybody," Bo said. His voice came out wrong, too loud. He tried again. "Everybody. Come here a minute."

They came. That was the thing about this crew, the thing he'd never gotten used to in nine years: when one of them said *come here*, they came. No questions first. Questions after, always, but never before.

Wendell didn't leave the wheel, but she turned her head, and her eyes went straight to his fist before he'd said a word. She'd always been like that. She read people the way she read water — the little disturbances, the boil under the surface that meant a rock you couldn't see.

Bo opened his hand.

The brass caught the flat gray light. For a moment nobody moved. The engine throbbed beneath them, patient as a heartbeat.

"Where," Wendell said. Just that. Her voice was level in a way Bo recognized, the way you recognize the stillness before a shear line — the air going flat and heavy before the weather turns.

"Under the hold. Welded compartment, port side, behind the intake plating. I went in after the fouling." He made himself keep talking, because if he stopped he might stop forever. "There was rope in there. Climbing rope, glacier gear, half of it rotted. A carabiner, snapped. And this." He held it out. "It was screwed to something, Captain. A door. A hatch. There's a whole space down there somebody welded shut from the inside of the hull, and they put *his name on it*."

Wendell didn't take it. That was the first crack — Bo had expected her to snatch it, and instead her hands stayed on the wheel, knuckles whitening, and she looked at the nameplate the way you look at a snake in the bottom of a skiff.

"Nobody touch the wheel," she said quietly, and came down off the helm stand, and took it from Bo's hand.

She weighed it. That's what she did — weighed it, turned it over, ran her thumb across the T. as if she could read something in the tarnish. Her face did something Bo had never seen it do, which was nothing at all. Total blankness, like a screen of ice over moving water.

"The compartment," she said. "Show me."

* * *

They went down together, all of them, which was too many people for the space, and nobody cared. Bo pulled back the plating he'd loosened and swung his lamp into the gap. The weld was old, done well, done by someone who knew their trade — he'd already decided it wasn't Wendell's work, and he watched her realize the same thing, watch her count the beads of the seam with her eyes and arrive somewhere terrible.

"I didn't weld this," she said. To no one. To herself.

Inside: the rope, coiled and slumped into rot. The carabiner, its gate hanging. A lantern with a cracked globe. A tin cup gone black with age. And a logbook, water-swollen, its cover furred with mold, lying where someone had dropped it or left it in a hurry.

Wendell reached in and lifted the logbook out with both hands, careful, the way you'd lift a wounded bird.

"Tom's log," Priya said softly. She was an archivist; Bo had forgotten that until just now, until he saw her face change, the way it did when she looked at old paper. "Captain — that's a ship's log. That's the old voyage. That's—"

"I know what it is." Wendell's voice had gone thin. She opened the cover with a gentleness that hurt to watch, and the first pages came apart at the spine, soft as wet bread, ink bleeding into gray ghosts.

"Careful," Priya breathed, and then caught herself, because *careful* was not what anyone was feeling.

Sefa had her arms crossed tight, and Bo saw her jaw working. She was the one who broke the silence, and she broke it loud, because that was Sefa — she cooked her feelings and served them hot.

"Three years," she said. "Three years that man's things have been sitting in a hole in the bottom of this boat, welded shut, and nobody told us. Not you—" she pointed at Wendell, then stopped, because Wendell was shaking her head.

"I didn't know."

"You didn't *know*." Sefa's laugh had no humor in it at all. "The brother you watched die, and his kit welded into your own hull, and you didn't know."

"I lost that night." Wendell said it flat, the way she'd said it once before, the night Odile appeared at the dock — the only time Bo had ever heard her admit it. "I lost the last night and everything in it. I told you that. I don't remember the accident. I don't remember the week before it. I don't remember what happened to his gear or who sealed this or why." She looked down at the logbook in her hands, and Bo watched the blankness crack, just at the eyes. "I don't remember, Sefa. That's not the same as lying."

"It's not the same as *knowing*, either," Sefa said, but some of the heat went out of her, the way it always did when Wendell went quiet instead of loud. That was the trouble with Wendell — you couldn't fight a woman who absorbed the blow like water.

Bo heard himself speak before he'd decided to. "Then we turn around."

Everyone looked at him. He hadn't planned to say it. It had come up out of him like water through a crack, two days of pressure finding its vent.

"Bo—" Priya started.

"No. Listen to me." He was surprised at how steady his voice was. "I've been tallying this whole trip. You want the tally? Crates that hum. Surveyors who won't say what they're surveying. Charts Odile won't let us see the back half of. A manifest that doesn't match the weight distribution — I did the math on the trim, we're riding forty centimeters heavy in the bow and nobody will tell me why. And now this." He pointed at the compartment, at the rope, at the dead man's name. "Every single thing on that list points the same direction, and the direction is *bad*. We are carrying something up this river for a woman who welded a dead man's belongings into a boat and let his sister pilot it for three years without a word. I want to turn around. I want to turn around *now*, while the window's still open, while we can still get home without running the calving fields."

"The window doesn't work that way," Wendell said quietly. "You know it doesn't. The high route closes from both ends. We're past the turn-back water. Another day and we're past the portage too."

"Then we portage south instead of north."

"Bo." She said his name the way she said *sandbar* — a thing to be read, not argued with. "I want to turn around too. More than you do, maybe. But turning around doesn't get us answers. It gets us home with a hole where the answers are, and I have lived three years inside that hole, and I am not doing it anymore."

"Then we're doing it *here*," Bo said. "We turn this boat, we go home, and we dig the truth out on our own ground, not on Odile Marchetti's."

"And if the truth is upstream?" Priya said.

They all turned. She was still holding the mended net, absently, like a rosary, and her voice was calm in the way that made Bo's teeth itch, because Priya's calm was always the calm of someone who had already read to the end of the chapter.

"The cache," Priya said. "The High Provision. Odile posted the destination at Reprieve — she *wanted* us to see it. Everything on Bo's tally points upstream, he's right. So the answers are upstream. Turning around doesn't get us away from the truth. It just gets us away from it slower."

"Or it gets us into it deeper," Sefa said. "Priya, honey, you're talking like this is a filing problem. It's not. That's a dead man's rope and a dead man's cup, and the woman who hired us welded the hole he came out of shut. You don't walk *toward* that. You run."

"Running is how people drown on this river," Wendell said.

The engine throbbed. Somewhere above them, water slapped the hull, patient, indifferent. Bo looked around the little space — the four of them and the dead man's belongings, packed tight as cargo — and felt the boat feel different under his feet. Smaller. He'd lived aboard nine years and it had never once felt small until now.

"Then we put it to the crew," he said. "All of us. Not just the three of you. Where's Ansel? Where's Voss?"

"Ansel's on watch. Voss is in his bunk." Wendell's mouth did something that was almost a smile and wasn't. "And this isn't a vote. Not yet. This is my boat."

"It's our boat," Bo said, and the words came out of him with a force that surprised them both. "That's what you told me my first season, when I asked why you bothered explaining your route decisions to a grease monkey. You said, *because it's your boat too, Bo, and a crew that isn't told things is a crew that leaves.*" He held her eyes. "You want to keep us? Tell us things."

Something moved behind Wendell's face — hurt, Bo thought, actual hurt, and he hated himself a little for putting it there and hated the world more for making it necessary.

"Tonight," she said. "After supper. I'll put everything on the table — the contract, the cargo, all of it. Everything I know, which is less than any of you think. And then we vote. All hands. That's the deal."

"That's the deal," Sefa said.

"That's the deal," Priya said.

Bo looked at the compartment one more time — the rope, the cup, the snapped carabiner that had held a man's weight over ice until it didn't — and nodded, and went back up the ladder to his engines, because engines were the one thing on this boat that told him the truth when he asked.

* * *

The weather turned in the afternoon, which felt less like coincidence than like commentary.

It came down off the glaciers to the west, a shear line of black cloud dragging its skirt through the valleys, and by four o'clock the river had gone the color of gunmetal and the wind was pushing whitecaps upstream against the current, which was a thing Bo had only seen on this stretch and hated every time. Wendell took them into the lee of the eastern bank and held there, engine at half, letting the worst of it walk past them, and the boat sat in the eddy and rolled, and nobody talked much.

Supper was Sefa's river stew, heavy on the pepper, and nobody finished it. She'd made it that way on purpose, Bo suspected — food you had to work at, food that kept your hands and mouth busy while the hard words got said. She'd done it before, the night they'd voted on Odile's contract. Sefa fed the crew the way other captains drilled them.

Wendell stood at the head of the table with everything laid out in front of her: the contract, the false manifest, the logbook, the nameplate. The lamp swung with the roll of the boat and the brass nameplate caught the light and threw it around the cabin like something alive.

"Here's what I know," she said. "And I want you to understand that the list is short, and that frightens me more than anything else I'm going to say tonight."

She told them. All of it. The job, the sealed cargo, the six-week window, the destination on the harbor board at Reprieve. Her history with Odile — not the details, but the shape of it: two pilots who'd come up together, who'd raced each other's boats and patched each other's hulls, who'd stopped speaking the day Tom died. And then the part that made the lamp seem to dim: the lost night. The missing week. The memory that ended like a chart with a section burned out.

"I woke up in the water," she said, "three days after the ice took the boat. They told me Tom went down with her. They told me I was lucky. And the part of my head that should have held that night — it's not damaged, exactly. It's *absent*. Like a page torn out clean. And every year I've told myself that was the ice, that was the injury, that was just what drowning does." She put her hand flat on the logbook. "But somebody welded his things into my hull. And people don't weld things into hulls over accidents. People weld things into hulls over *evidence*."

Silence. The wind had eased but the river still talked to the hull, a low continuous mutter, like the water knew something.

"Confront her," Sefa said. It wasn't a suggestion; it came out like a hammer coming down. "Tonight. Go up to that fancy cabin she's been hiding in and put the nameplate on her table and say, *explain this or we turn this boat around and you can swim home.* I've watched her for three weeks, Wendell. She's not a woman with a plan. She's a woman with a wound, and wounds make people stupid. We go at her now, before she goes at us."

"And if she's armed?" Priya said. "And if Voss is her man and not just her hire? And if confronting her tells her exactly how much we know and how much we don't?" Priya set down her spoon and folded her hands, and Bo recognized the posture — the archivist facing a document that might be forged. "We have fragments. A nameplate. A ledger with an initial in it. A destination. That's not a picture, that's a handful of puzzle pieces, and some of them might be from a different box entirely. If we move now, we move on half the evidence, and Odile is smarter than all of us put together when she's cornered. I say we wait. We keep working. We get into that logbook properly — I can recover text from water damage if the conditions are right, and I need time and dry air and raking light. We let Ansel and Voss keep thinking we're blind. And when we finally turn on her, we turn on her with the whole picture, not half of one."

"The whole picture might be at the bottom of a calving field," Bo said. "You want to wait for perfect information on a river that's closing behind us. That's not patience, Priya. That's a bet with the ice."

"And yours is a bet with Odile," she shot back, calm cracking just enough to show the steel under it. "You want to turn around. Fine. Say it plain. Say you want to abandon the answers and go home and spend the rest of your life wondering, because that's what turning around is. It's not safety. It's just a slower way of not knowing."

"It's my life," Bo said. "And yours. And Sefa's. And hers." He jerked his chin at Wendell. "I've been on one boat that went up this river chasing the truth already. I've welded over the hole it left. I'm not eager to make another one."

The words landed harder than he'd meant them to. He saw it hit Wendell — saw her flinch, actually flinch, the first time he'd ever seen it — and the cabin went so quiet he could hear the lamp swinging on its gimbals.

"I'm sorry," he said. "Captain. I'm sorry. But I meant it."

"I know you did." Wendell's voice was very quiet. "That's why it landed."

She looked down at the table. At the contract. At the nameplate. At the swollen logbook with its drowned pages. Bo watched her do what she always did at the wheel when the water got confused — stop fighting the current and read it. Find the deep channel in the middle of the argument.

"Here's where we are," she said finally. "Sefa wants to go at her now. Bo wants to go home. Priya wants to wait and dig. And I—" She stopped. Started again. "I have spent three years not knowing, and I will tell you honestly that there was a part of me that chose not to know. That kept the schedules and the sandbars and never once went looking, because I was afraid of what the missing night had in it. Afraid it had *me* in it. Odile said something at the fireline that I haven't been able to shake. She said I agreed to something, that night. And I don't remember whether it's a lie. Do you understand what that's like? To not know whether the worst thing anyone's said about you is true?"

Nobody answered. There wasn't an answer.

"So here's what I'm going to do," Wendell said. "I'm not turning around. I'm not confronting her tonight. And I'm not waiting for perfect information, because Priya's right that it's upstream and Bo's right that the river's closing. I'm going to hold course north, and we're going to work the problem from every side at once — Priya gets the logbook, Sefa keeps her ears on Odile, Bo keeps his tally, and I keep us off the rocks. And when we have enough to move, we move together. All of us. Because—" and here her voice did something Bo hadn't heard in it since before the accident, something rough and young, "—because I lost one family to that river already, and I am not losing this one to a *lie*."

She looked around the table. "But it has to be a real choice. Anybody who wants off at the next stop, I'll put them ashore myself and pay their way home and never think less of them. That was the deal at the dock and it's the deal now. So. One at a time. Stay or go."

Sefa spoke first, which surprised no one. "Stay. Somebody has to keep you idiots fed, and I want to look at Odile Marchetti's face when this breaks."

"Stay," Priya said. "The archive isn't finished."

They all looked at Bo.

He looked at his hands. At the calluses, at the grease in the creases that never fully came out, at the hands that had welded shut nothing and yet felt complicit in every weld on this boat. He thought about turning around. He thought about home water, the wide easy lower river, the freight runs that asked nothing of a man but his competence. He thought about growing old on safe water with a hole in his chest the shape of a question.

"Stay," he said. "But I want it on the record that I said turn around. And if this goes bad — if it goes *Tom* bad — I want us to remember that one of us said it."

"It's on the record," Wendell said. "Bo's dissent, entered into the ship's log, this date." And there — the ghost of the old Wendell, the dry one, the one who could put a joke on a grave and make it hold weight. "Thank you. All of you. Now get some sleep. Tomorrow we run the Narrows above the moraine, and I need every hand fresh."

They broke up slowly, nobody quite willing to be the first to leave the warmth of the table. Bo was last. He stood in the companionway with the engine's heartbeat under his feet and watched Wendell at the wheel through the open door, her silhouette steady against the black water, holding course north into weather that hadn't finished with them.

Behind them, far downstream, the ice was coming down.

Ahead, in the dark, a light blinked twice from the surveyors' cabin — then went dark, quick, like something that hadn't meant to be seen.

Bo stood a long moment, tallying.

Then he went and told Wendell.
