## 23. Ansel Breaks

Ansel had rehearsed the confession eleven times.

She knew the number because she counted things when she couldn't sleep — the rivets in the ceiling above her bunk, the strokes of the paddle wheel per minute, the hours since she'd last heard from anyone in the valley where her sister used to live. Counting was a way of holding still. And for nine days aboard the *Coriander*, holding still had been her entire job description: smile, ask friendly questions, eat what the cook gave you, and never, ever let your eyes linger too long on the crates.

But the confession kept refusing to stay rehearsed. In her head it came out clean, a single paragraph, almost bureaucratic: *My name is Ansel Reyes, I was hired by the Corvane Mining Consortium to verify and destroy the contents of the cache at the High Provision, and I am telling you now because I can't do it.* In her mouth, at three in the morning, staring at the rivets, it sounded exactly like what it was — the words of a woman who had spent nine days deciding whether a riverboat full of strangers was worth burning her one piece of leverage on.

The problem was that they had stopped being strangers somewhere around the portage.

* * *

It had happened, she thought later, at the exact moment Bo Halloran handed her the second rope.

The portage had been two miles of hell — the *Coriander* hauled over land on rollers and skids, every soul aboard pulling, the surveyors' cover story of "light equipment inspection" dissolving in the first hour under the honest weight of the boat. Ansel had worked harder than anyone. That wasn't heroism; it was arithmetic. Voss had watched her take the heaviest end of the skid and hadn't said a word, because Voss understood that guilt was a fuel like any other, and hers was high-octane.

Bo had fallen into step beside her on the third mile, both of them bleeding from rope burns, and said, "You pull like somebody's chasing you."

"Like somebody's *ahead* of me," Ansel had said, and Bo had laughed — a real laugh, surprised out of him — and something in Ansel's chest had cracked along a seam she'd welded shut in a Corvane conference room eleven months ago.

Because Bo had told her about his sister. Not that night — a few nights later, in the galley, over Sefa's terrible coffee, in the roundabout way men like Bo told things, as if the telling were accidental. A sister who'd gone into the water at a company town upstream of a Corvane tailings pond. A sister the company had paid out on with a settlement and a confidentiality clause. Bo had signed the clause. He said it the way a man says *I took the money*, and then he'd looked at Ansel with those steady engineer's eyes and said, "You ever sign something you'd take back?"

Ansel had said, "Once," and drunk her coffee, and gone to her bunk, and not slept.

That was four days ago. The glacier had started talking that morning — long, distant booms rolling down the valley like the mountain clearing its throat — and Ansel had understood that her window was closing from both ends. The ice was coming down behind them. And ahead, in a day, maybe two, they would reach the High Provision, and she and Voss would do what they were paid to do, and the last proof of what Corvane did to the upper Thessaly would go into the meltwater along with everything else.

She couldn't. That was the whole of it, stripped of the eleven rehearsals. She could not stand in Tom Lockridge's cache with a thermite charge in her hand and think of her sister's name.

* * *

She found Wendell Lockridge at the bow, alone, in the hour before dusk.

The captain stood at the wheel with her hands resting on the spokes the way other people rested their hands on the shoulders of old friends, reading the water with her whole body — Ansel had watched her do it for nine days and still didn't fully understand it. Wendell didn't look at the river so much as listen to it with her eyes. The glacial water here ran the color of skim milk, fast and cold and full of ground stone, and the channel braided and shifted by the hour, and Wendell read it the way Priya read documents: fluently, suspiciously, missing nothing.

"Captain." Ansel's voice came out wrong — too formal. She'd planned *Wendell*. The rehearsals had all said *Wendell*. But the word stuck, because the formality was armor, and she was about to need every scrap of it.

"Miss Ansel." Wendell didn't turn. "You're off your watch."

"I need to talk to you. Not here."

Wendell's hands stilled on the wheel. Just for a second — a pause so small that anyone who hadn't spent nine days studying her would have missed it — and then the wheel turned a quarter-spoke, correcting for a seam of faster water along the starboard bank, and she said, "Sefa's galley is full. Bo's in the engine room. There's the foredeck."

"The foredeck's fine."

They walked aft past the wheelhouse, past the stacked crates with their false mining-company stamps, and Ansel felt Voss's gaze on her back from the surveyors' cabin like a hand between her shoulder blades. Voss was playing cards with Priya. He'd looked up when she passed. He always looked up. He was a careful man, and careful men noticed when the rhythm of a boat changed, and Ansel knew — had known since breakfast, since she'd pushed her eggs around her plate and failed to make her usual jokes — that Voss had already read the change in her and filed it.

She was burning the last hours of her cover by the minute. Fine. Let them burn.

On the foredeck the wind came off the glacier fields ahead of them, cold and mineral, smelling of stone ground to flour. The light was going amber and long. Somewhere upstream, the ice boomed again — a sound like a door closing in a very large house — and Wendell's head turned toward it with the reflexive attention of a woman counting the same countdown Ansel was counting.

"Three booms today," Wendell said. "Yesterday, one. The season's turning early."

"I know."

"You wouldn't know. You're a surveyor." Wendell turned then, and her eyes were the flat gray of the river in shade, and Ansel felt the full weight of nine days of being seen through. "Surveyors don't know what calving sounds like. Surveyors don't pull like they're being chased on a portage. Surveyors don't flinch when I say the word *cache*." She folded her arms. "I've run this boat with stowaways, smugglers, a fugitive judge, and one very polite horse thief, Miss Ansel. I know what a person looks like when they're carrying something heavier than their luggage. So let's not spend the whole dusk getting there. What are you?"

The rehearsals were all gone. Ansel stood on the foredeck of the *Coriander* with the wind pulling her hair sideways and discovered she had nothing prepared, and that this — the unpreparedness, the rawness — was probably the only version Wendell Lockridge would believe.

"My name is Ansel Reyes," she said. "I'm not a surveyor. Neither is Voss. We were hired by the Corvane Mining Consortium."

She watched the name land. It didn't land like a surprise; it landed like a confirmation, which was worse, and which told her that Wendell had been assembling this picture for days and had simply been waiting for someone to hand her the last piece.

"Corvane," Wendell repeated. "The logo on the crates is fake. Bo checked. So whose logo is real?"

"Ours. Corvane's. The stamp on the crates is a shell company Corvane uses for exactly this — moving things up rivers nobody watches. The crates hold core samples and documentation from the upper Thessaly. Water assays. Internal memos. Records of what went into the river and when, and who signed off on it." Ansel's hands had closed into fists without her noticing; she made herself open them. "Voss and I were hired to reach the cache at the High Provision, verify its contents against a manifest, and destroy anything that could tie Corvane to the poisoning of the upper river. Thermite. Clean burn. The company's phrase was *site remediation.*"

The wind moved. Downriver, the paddle wheel kept its patient count. Ansel realized she was shaking and let it show, because hiding it now would cost more than showing it.

"And my sister," she said, "lived in Kettle Vale."

She hadn't planned to say the name of the village. It came out anyway, the way names do when they've been locked up too long.

"Kettle Vale is forty miles below the Corvane tailings outfall on the Sable Fork," she went on, and her voice went flat and fast, the way it went when she talked about this, the only way it could go. "Eleven years ago the containment berm failed in spring melt. Corvane called it a seep. The water table in Kettle Vale has been poisoned since. My sister — her name was Marta — she stayed. Everyone with money left and Marta stayed, because it was her house and her orchard and her husband's grave, and she spent eleven years filing claims and collecting water samples and getting letters back from lawyers who billed Corvane's retainer by the hour. Two years ago she sent me a box of her water assays. She'd tested them herself, at the school where she taught, with equipment she bought with her own salary. Her numbers matched the numbers in those crates. She'd proven it, alone, with a school lab." Ansel's breath caught. "She died last winter. Badly. Of the thing the water does. The company sent flowers. The flowers were white lilies, which is what you send when you want to say *funeral* without saying *sorry*."

Wendell hadn't moved. hadn't blinked. The river spoke for both of them, hissing along the hull.

"So when Corvane offered me this job," Ansel said, "I took it. Not for the money. For the manifest. For the paper trail — who hired me, what they ordered, what they were willing to burn. Because a woman with nothing but a dead sister and a box of water samples can't touch a company like Corvane. But a woman standing inside their own sabotage operation, holding their own orders?" Her jaw ached; she was clenching it. "That woman has leverage. That was the plan. Get inside, get the proof, get out, and spend the rest of my life making them pay for Kettle Vale."

"And now?"

"Now I'm standing on your foredeck," Ansel said, "telling you instead. Because somewhere between the portage and Bo's sister and Sefa feeding me like I was worth feeding, I stopped being able to tell the difference between my leverage and your brother's grave." She pulled in a breath that hurt. "That's the cost, Captain. That's what it costs to pick a side. You give up the thing you came for. And I need you to understand I'm not doing this because I'm good. I'm doing it because I looked at the math and the math changed."

Wendell was quiet for a long moment. Then she said, "Tom."

It wasn't a question, but Ansel answered it anyway.

"Your brother found the cache early. Before the season closed, three years ago. He logged what was in it — the samples, the documents, the proof of what Corvane was putting in the water. Corvane found out he'd found it. They sent people. And your brother died on the ice, and the company spent three years making sure the cache stayed buried until they could send someone to make it *stay* buried." She held Wendell's gaze. "That someone was supposed to be me."

"Odile hired you."

"Odile brokered us. Whether she knows what we're for—" Ansel stopped. Made herself finish. "I don't know what Odile knows, Captain. That's the honest answer. She took Corvane's money to assemble this expedition. What she was told about the crates, what she was told about the cache, whether she's a partner or a tool — I genuinely don't know. I know she watches you like she's keeping a ledger. And I know Voss reports to a man named Grell, and Grell is the one who gave us the thermite, and Grell is the one whose name is on the orders I'm about to give you."

She reached into her jacket.

She had carried the real manifest against her skin for nine days — folded into a oilcloth packet, taped flat beneath the waistband of her trousers, through inspections and river crossings and one mortifying moment when Sefa had nearly walked in on her changing. It was warm from her body. It felt like handing over an organ.

"Three copies existed," she said. "Voss has one. Grell has one. This is mine." She held it out. "Names, dates, payment schedules, the destruction order with Grell's signature, the reference codes for the cache contents — which will match your brother's log, if you ever find it. And a memo, dated fourteen months ago, approving the *acquisition* of the High Provision site. That's the word they used. Acquisition. For a cache your brother built."

Wendell took the packet. She didn't open it. She held it in both hands, looking down at it, and Ansel watched something move through her face — not softening, nothing so simple — more like a load shifting on a deck, the whole structure of the woman redistributing weight around a new fact.

"Voss," Wendell said at last. "He'll know you've talked."

"He already knows something. He's careful. He'll have noticed I stopped sleeping." Ansel almost smiled; it came out crooked. "He's not a monster, Captain. Whatever you're deciding about what to do with him — he's a man who does terrible work cleanly because it's the only work anyone will pay him for, and he's good to horses and bad to himself. But he'll follow his orders. That's the thing about Voss. He follows orders the way water follows grade. If Corvane tells him to burn the cache, he burns it, and he'll be sorry afterward, quietly, for years."

"And you?"

"I'm done following orders." Ansel lifted her chin. "I'm choosing. That's the difference, and it's the only difference, and I know it doesn't buy me anything. I came onto this boat to destroy the last proof of what Corvane did. I lied to every one of you for nine days. Sefa fed me. Bo bled next to me. Priya showed me her archivist's kit like I was a person worth showing things to. And I'd understand — completely — if you put me off the boat at the next shallow, or worse. I'm not asking for forgiveness. I'm asking you to take the manifest and use it, and to know that whatever you decide about me, the information is real."

Wendell finally opened the packet.

She read standing in the amber light with the wind snapping the pages, and Ansel watched her read, and watched the reading change her — watched the stillness in her go from the stillness of control to the stillness of a woman standing in a room where the floor has been removed. When Wendell reached the destruction order, her thumb stopped on Grell's signature and pressed there, hard, as if she could bruise ink.

"Grell," Wendell said. The name came out of her like a stone out of a sling. "The harbormaster at Reprieve."

Ansel went cold. "You know him?"

"He signed our resupply papers. He shook my hand." Wendell's voice was very quiet and very level, which Ansel had learned, in nine days of studying her, was the most dangerous register the captain owned. "He stood on his porch and wished us safe passage." She looked up. "He was on the river three years ago, too. The night my brother died."

"Captain—"

"Thank you for this." Wendell folded the manifest closed, and her hands were steady, and her eyes were not. "You should know what you've done. You've put yourself between Corvane and the thing they want burned, and Corvane does not forgive that, and neither does Grell, and you are on a boat in the middle of the ice with no way off until we run back down. There's no neutral ground left for you, Ansel Reyes. You understand that."

"I've understood it since I took the packet out of the oilcloth this morning," Ansel said. "I told you. The math changed."

Another boom rolled down the valley — closer now, or maybe just the light making it seem so — and both of them turned toward the sound, and for a moment the two women stood in the shared silence of people listening to a countdown.

"Go get some food," Wendell said. "Sefa will be watching the galley door and pretending she isn't. And Ansel—" Wendell's eyes came back to her, gray and cold and, underneath the cold, lit with something that might have been the beginning of a terrible gratitude. "Voss. Leave him to me. Don't tip him. Not yet. I need one more day, and I need him calm."

"And if he's not calm?"

"Then we'll find out what he does when the water changes grade," Wendell said, "won't we."

Ansel turned to go. She'd made it two steps when Wendell spoke again, quieter.

"Your sister's name. Marta." A pause. "I'll remember it. That's not nothing, on this river. Names get remembered here."

Ansel stood with her back turned for a moment, throat working, then nodded once and walked aft, and did not trust herself to speak again.

* * *

She didn't make it to the galley.

Voss was waiting at the surveyors' cabin door, cards still in his hand — he'd brought the deck with him, which was its own kind of message: *I know exactly where I was, and so do you.* He looked at her with those patient gray eyes, and he didn't ask what she'd told Wendell Lockridge, because Voss never asked questions he'd already answered for himself.

"You were gone forty minutes," he said.

"I went for a walk."

"On a boat." He turned a card over between his fingers — the queen of spades — and studied it rather than her. "Ansel. I'm going to say something once. Whatever you did or didn't do on that foredeck, there's a version of the next two days where we both walk away from this river. There's also a version where Grell's next payment goes to one of us and the other one goes in the water. I'd prefer the first version. I suspect you would too." He finally looked up. "So. Are we still partners?"

Behind him, through the cabin's small window, the last light was going off the glacier fields, and somewhere upstream the ice boomed a fourth time, and Ansel thought about Marta's white lilies and Bo's rope-burned hands and the weight of the packet no longer against her skin.

She smiled at him — the surveyor's smile, the cover-story smile, warm and easy and nine days practiced — and said, "Of course, Voss. We're partners."

And she walked past him into the cabin, already planning how to keep him calm for one more day, while behind them the mountain counted down in the dark.
