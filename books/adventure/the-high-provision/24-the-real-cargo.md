## 24. The Real Cargo

The manifest was written on the back of a survey form, in pencil, in a hand too neat for someone who expected to live long enough to be proud of it.

Wendell read it twice in the wheelhouse with the door shut and the lamp turned low, because what it said seemed to change the light. The paper trembled — her hand, not the river. The river was behaving. The river didn't know yet.

She had expected contraband. Weapons, uncut ore, a payroll for men who broke things. She had spent three years building an architecture of guesses around that sealed cargo, and every guess had been a kind of self-defense: if the job were dirty, then Odile Marchetti was simply Odile Marchetti, and Tom Lockridge had simply been unlucky weather, and the world kept its familiar crooked shape.

The manifest refused to let her keep it.

*Core samples, 41 units, sealed borings, Upper Thessaly headwaters, stations 1 through 41.* Beneath each station number, a reading. Wendell didn't need to be a chemist. The numbers marched down the page like a line of small graves — arsenic, mercury, leachate compounds with names she half remembered from a newspaper nobody in the delta could afford to subscribe to. Station 12 through 41 exceeded every threshold on the facing column by factors she read as multiples of ten, twenty, forty.

And beneath the table, in the same neat pencil, a paragraph:

*Company internal memorandum, ref. TH-77, confirms contamination known as of four seasons prior to first reported downstream illness. Memorandum recommends continued extraction with "monitoring" rather than remediation, citing cost differential of approx. 11 million against projected litigation exposure. Attached: signed authorization, board minutes, correspondence re: suppression of independent survey commissioned Marchetti Expeditions Ltd., including payment schedule to M. Corvus (Harbormaster, Reprieve) and termination clause re: original expedition principal.*

Termination clause re: original expedition principal.

Tom's name wasn't on the manifest anywhere. It didn't have to be. Wendell sat very still and let the words do their work, the way you let a hook set instead of fighting it, because fighting it only tears something.

The mission inverted. That was the phrase that came to her, absurdly clinical, the way her mind reached for terminology when the alternative was screaming. Three weeks ago she'd believed she was hauling a secret upriver. Then she'd believed she was hauling evidence of a bribe. Now the crates stacked under tarps below decks held the proof that a company had looked at a poisoned river — her river, the one that raised her, fed her brother, taught her every mile of its moods — and had done the arithmetic, and had chosen the arithmetic.

And Tom had died keeping this from them.

* * *

She went below without deciding to. Her body knew where it wanted to go before her mind caught up: down the companionway, past the galley where Sefa was kneading bread dough with her forearms and her whole grievous patience, past Bo's engine room humming its steady diesel hymn, to the hold.

Ansel was there. Of course she was. She hadn't left the hold since she'd handed over the folded pages, sitting on an overturned crate with her elbows on her knees, watching the sealed cargo like a woman standing vigil over a coffin she wasn't sure contained the right body.

"You read it," she said. Not a question.

"I read it." Wendell lowered herself onto a crate opposite. The hold smelled of rope and bilgewater and cold steel, and beneath all of it, faintly, that hum she'd stopped noticing weeks ago — the hum Bo had once leaned against and never mentioned again. "Forty-one stations."

"Forty-two." Ansel's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "They never drilled station 42. It would've come back clean — it's above the last outfall. They didn't want a clean number in the set. It would have made the curve look deliberate." She said it flatly, reciting, and Wendell understood that Ansel had recited these facts to herself many times, in many rooms, trying to make one of them feel bearable.

"How do you know that?"

"Because I wrote the drilling plan." Ansel looked up. In the lamplight her cheerfulness — that relentless, armor-plated cheerfulness she'd worn since Reprieve — was gone entirely, and underneath it she looked young, and tired, and from a country whose name Wendell had only ever seen on shipping manifests. "I'm a hydrologist, Captain. Did you know that? Everyone assumes the woman who smiles must be the assistant. Voss is the fixer. I'm the one they send when they need someone who can read a core sample and be trusted to lie about it." Her hands opened in her lap, empty. "My sister's valley is two hundred kilometers from the upper Thessaly. Different river, same company, same arithmetic. When they offered me this contract — verify the cache, destroy anything incriminating — I said yes because I thought I'd find leverage. Something to hold over them. I told myself that made it clean." A breath. "Then I met your crew, and your cook fed me, and your engineer taught me to pack a bearing, and I realized I wasn't going to destroy anything. I was just going to carry it the rest of my life."

Wendell heard herself say, "Why tell me now?"

"Because we're two days from the calving fields, and after that there's no telling anyone anything." Ansel stood. She was shorter than she seemed on deck; everything about her shrank when the performance came off. "And because you deserve to hear it from me before you hear it from the crates. Your brother found this cache first. He found it weeks early, off-route, because he was the kind of man who reads water other people don't. And he opened it. And he understood what it was. And he spent the rest of his life — however much of it was left — making sure it stayed closed until someone honest came for it."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere above, Priya's boots crossed the deck on watch, unhurried, faithful.

"The company bought Odile," Wendell said. It wasn't a question either, but she needed to hear it in air.

"They bought her silence. There's a difference, and the difference matters." Ansel gathered the survey form, the manifest, the photocopied ledger pages Sefa had passed along, and squared them into a folder with careful hands, as if orderliness itself were a moral act. "Odile financed the original survey to expose them. That part's true — ask anyone in the old guild, it was famous. Then the letters started. Threats, then offers, then threats wrapped inside offers. By the time your brother died, she'd already signed the termination clause. Whether she knew what 'termination' meant that night—" Ansel stopped. Started again, more quietly. "Whether she knew, or whether she just made sure she didn't know, is the question I can't answer for you. But the money moved. The money always moves."

* * *

Wendell took the folder topside and stood at the rail a long time while the Thessaly carried them north through high water country, the banks narrowing to gray stone shoulders, the water gone pale with glacier milk. She didn't open the folder again. She didn't need to. The numbers had taken up residence behind her eyes and were rearranging everything they found there.

Every memory of Tom re-sorted itself around the new gravity.

The summer he'd come home from the high route with frost scars on his knuckles and wouldn't say what he'd seen. The winter he'd asked her, over cards in the galley, whether she thought a person could own a river or only borrow it, and she'd laughed at him for getting philosophical over a bad hand. The last morning — the morning her memory still refused, the white gap at the center of everything — he'd stood on this same deck and touched her shoulder and said something she couldn't retrieve, and she had spent three years believing that the missing thing was a goodbye.

It hadn't been a goodbye. It had been a warning. She felt that now with total certainty, the way you feel a sandbar under the hull before you see the water change color. He had known. He had tried to tell her. And whatever had happened in the white gap had made sure she never heard it.

Bo appeared beside her with two enamel mugs and handed her one without asking. That was Bo. Three years of wordless coffee.

"You look," Bo said, "like a woman reading charts of a river she thought she knew."

"That's about the size of it." Wendell drank. The coffee was terrible; Bo made it by conviction rather than method. "Bo. If I told you the cargo we've been hauling north is proof that somebody poisoned the upper river on purpose — knew, and chose it, line by line, station by station — what would you say?"

Bo considered the water with the same unhurried attention given to a fouled intake. "I'd say I'm surprised they let it sit in a cache for three years instead of burning it."

"There was a man guarding it with his life."

Bo nodded slowly. "That'd do it." A pause. The engine room ticked and breathed below them. "And Odile?"

"That's the part I don't know yet."

"It usually is." Bo drained the mug and set it upside down on the rail, boatman's habit, nothing spills nothing rolls. "For what it's worth, Captain — I leaned on one of those crates our first day out. Never told anybody. Felt like leaning on a bank vault. Whatever's down there, it was built to survive somebody trying real hard to destroy it." He looked at her sideways. "Sounds like it survived."

* * *

Sefa called dinner early, which meant trouble, which meant care. Wendell had learned to read the galley the way she read the river, and a meal served ahead of schedule with extra of everything was Sefa's way of saying *something is coming and we will face it fed.*

The six of them crowded the bench — Wendell, Bo, Priya, Sefa, Voss, Ansel. Voss sat with his splinted wrist cradled against his chest, eating steadily and saying nothing, which for Voss constituted sociability. The lantern swung gently with the river's long roll. Outside, the light was turning to that thin gold that meant latitude and season both advancing.

Wendell put the folder in the middle of the table.

She told them all of it. The stations, the numbers, the internal memorandum, the payment schedule with the harbormaster's name on it, the termination clause. She watched their faces take it the way a hull takes a wave — Priya sharp and still, already indexing; Sefa's hands going quiet in her lap; Bo nodding once, unsurprised, keeper of the tally; Ansel staring at her plate, paying in full for her confession a second time in front of witnesses; Voss, at the end, setting down his fork with a click that was the loudest sound in the wheelhouse.

"And Tom," Sefa said softly. Not a question. Sefa never asked questions she already knew the shape of.

"He found it first," Wendell said. "He protected it. And the company put a price on doing exactly that, and someone collected." She heard her voice stay level and marveled at it distantly, the way you marvel at a bridge holding. "I spent three years thinking my brother drowned in bad weather. He didn't drown in bad weather. He drowned in arithmetic."

Nobody spoke. The lantern swung. Downstream — upstream, rather, ahead of them — the evening boomed faintly, far off, like a door closing in another wing of a very large house. The glacier, announcing itself. The window shrinking.

Priya broke the silence, because Priya could always find the load-bearing sentence. "The evidence isn't just in the cache. It's in those crates." She tapped the folder. "Core samples are physical testimony. You can burn paper. You can't un-drill forty-one holes in a riverbed. If we bring these home—"

"If," Bo said.

"—if," Priya acknowledged, "then it's not a story anymore. It's geology. It's court-admissible, it's headline-proof, it's the thing companies build entire careers to prevent. Which is why they hired Voss and Ansel to destroy it."

All eyes went to the surveyors. Voss met them without flinching, which Wendell noted, and filed. Ansel lifted her chin.

"I'm done destroying things," Ansel said.

Voss said, "I was hired to verify and dispose. I have verified." He picked his fork back up. "I am still considering disposal."

Sefa snorted despite herself — a single wet laugh that broke the tension like a seam of sun on gray water. Even Wendell felt her mouth twitch. Trust Voss to threaten the cargo with paperwork.

But under the small warmth, Wendell felt the true weight settle, the way ballast settles, low and permanent. The mission had inverted and it kept inverting. She had signed on to run freight for a woman she hated, up a river she'd sworn off, toward a cache she feared. Every step of it had been framed as a job — miles, cargo, pay, done. And now the freight was her brother's unfinished work, and the pay was irrelevant, and the woman she hated might be the least guilty person aboard, and the river she'd sworn off was dying quietly, station by station, number by numbered number, and had been for years while she ran her comfortable loops below the contamination line, measuring her life in miles because miles didn't ask her to remember.

"Sefa," Wendell said. "How much food have we got?"

Sefa didn't even glance at her stores; she kept her inventory in her spine. "Full rations, nine days. Stretch it to twelve if we eat like pilgrims."

"Fuel?"

"Enough to run home fat," Bo said. "Not enough to run home fat and fight ice."

Wendell looked down the table at her family — the family that had assembled itself out of grief and proximity and ten thousand shared meals, none of whom owed her anything, all of whom were here. Beyond them, through the glass, the Thessaly ran north into the gathering dark, and beyond that, invisible but audible now on calm nights, the calving fields waited with their artillery booms, and somewhere in that white chaos a vault held her brother's last three years of work, packed and provisioned, waiting for whoever came next.

Two days. Maybe less, if the season turned early. The window was closing at the speed of warming air.

"Tomorrow," Wendell said, "we reach the moraine, and we start hauling. And I want to say one thing before anyone sleeps." She waited until every face was on her. "Three weeks ago this was Odile's job and I resented every mile of it. Today it isn't. This cargo belongs to a dead man who gave his life keeping it safe, and to every soul downstream who's been drinking that arithmetic without knowing it. We're not running freight anymore. We're running testimony." Her voice did finally move — just barely — and she let it. "And I intend to deliver it."

The lantern swung. The glacier boomed again, closer, patient, indifferent — and for the first time since leaving port, Wendell heard it not as a threat but as a clock.

Two days. She only hoped the ice would give them the time that honesty had finally earned.
