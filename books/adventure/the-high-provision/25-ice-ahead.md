## 25. Ice Ahead

The first boom came at dawn, and Wendell knew before she opened her eyes what it was.

She'd grown up on a river fed by ice. Every spring of her childhood, her father had taken her out to listen to the calving fields the way other fathers took their children to hear church bells — standing in the bow of a leaky skiff, cupping his hand behind his ear, saying, *There. You hear that? That's the mountain deciding what it keeps.* The sound carried for miles across open water: a deep concussion followed by a long rolling growl, like thunder that had learned patience.

This one came from the north, from the direction they were headed, and it was wrong in every way that mattered.

It was too early. The season had weeks left in it — Odile's own charts said so, and whatever else Odile Marchetti was, she was not careless with ice. The calving fields held their breath through late summer, shedding modest bergs into the channel, saving the great collapses for the turn. But that boom had been no modest shed. It had the weight of a wall coming down.

Wendell lay in her bunk listening, counting the seconds until the echo died against the valley walls. Eleven seconds. When she was a girl, eleven seconds meant ice falling ten miles away. Ten miles upstream. Between them and home.

She got up, dressed in the dark, and climbed to the pilothouse without waking anyone.

* * *

The river had changed overnight. She saw it before she understood it — the water running higher, colder, a new gray-green tint where yesterday it had been the pale milk-blue of fresh melt. Somewhere above, a glacier was discharging everything it had been holding back all season, and the Thessaly was drinking it fast.

She stood at the wheel with her hands resting light on the spokes and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding since Reprieve. Distance to the cache: two days' run if the channel held. Time at the cache: however long it took to dig Tom's evidence out of whatever vault he'd built into the moraine. Distance home: four days downriver, five if the ice ran ahead of them and choked the narrows. Total: seven days, maybe eight, with margin for nothing.

The window, by every chart and almanac she trusted, should have been three weeks wide.

The window was closing like a door.

Another boom rolled down the valley, longer this time, and beneath the pilothouse floorboards she felt the Coriander shiver as the current picked up half a knot. The boat had opinions about that. Bo would be awake soon, listening to his engine strain against water that didn't want them going this direction. Even the river was voting to turn around.

"Wendell."

Odile's voice. Wendell didn't turn. She watched the northern horizon, where the peaks stood white and clean against a sky just beginning to bruise pink, and somewhere below those peaks was the High Provision, and somewhere inside it was the truth about her brother, and she was done pretending she couldn't feel both things pulling on her at once.

"You heard it," Odile said, coming to stand at the rail beside her. She was dressed already, coat buttoned to the throat, hair tied back like a woman reporting for work. In the gray light she looked older than she had on the dock in Corvane — or maybe Wendell was finally seeing her without the varnish.

"I heard it."

"That was the Serrat Tongue. The main calving face." Odile said it flatly, the way you'd read a diagnosis aloud. "It shouldn't go for another nineteen days. I've run this route eleven times. I've never heard it this early."

"Then say what you're actually thinking."

Odile was quiet a moment. Below them, the deck was still; even Priya, who took dawn watch like a religious duty, hadn't come up yet. The river slid past, faster than it should have been, carrying their boat north toward the thing that was trying to close the road behind them.

"I'm thinking we have four days," Odile said. "Five if we're lucky and the wind holds out of the south. After that, the lower channel fills with brash and nothing bigger than a canoe gets through until next June." She turned to look at Wendell directly. "I'm thinking I owe you an answer you asked for two nights ago, and I'd rather give it while there's still time for it to matter."

Wendell kept her eyes on the water. "You've had three years to give it."

"And I spent them hoping I'd never have to." Odile exhaled, and something in the sound was older than the expedition, older than the rivalry, older than the dock in Corvane. "All right. No more polish. You want it plain, I'll give it plain."

* * *

"The company found me first," Odile said. "Not after the accident. Before. Eight months before."

They were still in the pilothouse, but Wendell had put the kettle on because some conversations needed something to do with your hands, and now they sat across the little chart table with tin mugs between them, steam rising, the wheel lashed and the river steering itself straight for a few merciful miles.

"Halewater Mining," Odile said. "You've seen the stamp on the crates. They'd gotten word that someone was surveying the upper Thessaly — core samples, water readings, photographs. They assumed it was a rival. It took them a month to figure out it was one man in a converted fish boat with a hand-cranked drill and a camera older than he was."

Tom. Wendell's chest did the thing it always did — the tightness, the flinch — and she let it, because Odile was finally talking and she would not interrupt for anything less than the boat catching fire.

"They offered him money. He laughed at them. So they offered me money." Odile turned her mug slowly on the table, not drinking. "I'd financed his expedition. Equipment, permits, fuel — my name was on every document, because his name was too well known to Halewater's land agents and mine wasn't. Which meant when the offer came, it came to me. Quietly. Through a lawyer in Corvane who represented a shell company that represented another shell company. A sum I won't repeat out loud, because it was obscene, and because saying it makes it sound like a reason instead of what it was."

"Which was?"

"A door." Odile looked up. "They didn't ask me to hurt anyone. That's the part I need you to hear, Wendell, because I've told myself the same lie for three years and I'm done with it. They asked me to do nothing. To withdraw financing. To let the expedition starve on the vine, quietly, legally, so the samples never reached anyone who could use them. And I said yes. I sent the letter pulling funding effective end of season. Tom got it six days before—" Her voice caught, just once, and she mastered it. "Six days before the accident."

Wendell set her mug down very carefully, because her hands wanted to shake and she wouldn't let them. "That's not plain talk, Odile. That's a confession with the middle torn out. Tom didn't starve on the vine. Tom went through the ice with a rope around his gear and a lantern in his hand, and I was there, and I can't remember why. Somebody cut that crossing short. Somebody rigged it. Ansel told us what Voss was hired to do — verify and destroy. So either you're lying again, or there's a piece between your letter and my brother's death that you're still hiding."

"There is a piece," Odile said. "And here it is, and God help me, I've never said it to another living soul." She drew a breath. "When Tom got my letter, he didn't rage and he didn't quit. He wrote back. One page. He said he'd already found what he was looking for — the cache, the samples, the proof that Halewater had been dumping tailings leachate into the headwaters for six years and falsifying every downstream reading. He said the evidence was secured where no one could burn it, and that he intended to bring it out himself, funded or not, and that he forgave me for the letter because he thought I'd been threatened." Her eyes came up, steady and terrible. "He was right. I had been. Two days after I signed, a man from Halewater's security division paid me a visit and explained, very politely, what happens to expeditions that don't take hints. And then he said something worse. He said they'd handle Lockridge themselves if I didn't call him off."

"So you called him off."

"I tried." For the first time since Corvane, Odile Marchetti looked her age, and then some. "I sent a second message warning him to stop, to come home, to leave the cache where it was. And I know how that sounds. I know exactly how that sounds, sitting here telling you I warned him off the very thing that killed him. But I swear to you on whatever you'll let me swear on — I was trying to save him. I told them I'd called him off. I bought him a week with a lie. And in that week, somebody from Halewater went up that river anyway, because a man named Corrick doesn't leave loose ends, and Tom was the loosest end on the map."

Corrick. The harbormaster of Reprieve. The name sat in Wendell's stomach like a swallowed stone. Sefa's photograph of the ledger, the payments, the initial *L* that might mean a dozen things or might mean her — it all rotated slowly into a new arrangement, and none of the arrangements were comfortable.

"You hired me," Wendell said slowly, "to finish what he started."

"I hired you because I couldn't live with it anymore." Odile's voice dropped, roughened. "Three years, Wendell. Three years of knowing the proof was sitting in the ice above the calving fields, and knowing that every season it stayed there was a season Halewater kept poisoning that river, and knowing that the only person alive stubborn enough and skilled enough to go get it was the sister of the man I got killed. I told myself I was hiring the best pilot on the Thessaly. That was true. But mostly I was buying penance the only way I could afford it — by putting it in the hands of someone better than me."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Out on the water, a loon cried once, far off, and the sound made the silence afterward feel enormous.

"You understand the problem," Wendell said at last. "Every word you've said is unfalsifiable. The letter pulling funds, the threat, the second message — paper that conveniently doesn't exist, testimony only you can give. You've had three years to build this version. It's got a villain I already hate and a motive I almost want to believe. That's not evidence, Odile. That's craft."

"Yes," Odile said simply. "It is. I'm a liar, Wendell. I've been one so long I can't fully vouch for my own memory anymore — I've retold this story to myself so many times it's worn smooth. That's the honest truth of it. Which is why I stopped relying on my word." She reached into her coat and laid something on the chart table between them. "Tom's reply to my first letter. He sent it to my Corvane office. I've kept it in a fireproof box ever since, and I've never shown it to anyone, because it damns me and absolves me in the same paragraph and I couldn't bear to read it twice."

Wendell stared at it. A single envelope, soft with age, addressed in a hand she would know anywhere — the strong slanted script that had labeled her birthday packages and corrected her river charts and signed itself, always, *your brother, T.*

Her hands were not steady now, and she let them not be.

* * *

She read it alone.

She took it up to the bow rail, where the cold could keep her honest, and she broke the seal with her thumbnail, and she read her brother's last words to the woman who had pulled his funding six days before he died.

*I got your letter [the writing began, unhurried, certain]. Don't waste remorse on me — I know how these things work, and I know you didn't have much choice, and I know you'll hate yourself anyway, which is its own punishment and sufficient.*

*For the record: I found it. All of it. The cache is real, the samples are clean, and the readings will hang them. I've secured everything above the calving fields where no subpoena and no match can reach it. If I don't come out, whoever comes looking next will find it exactly where I left it, marked plainly, because I believe in this river more than I believe in luck.*

*One request. If anything happens to me, take care of W. She'll want to run the route alone and grieve in private, because that's how she does everything, and it will kill her slower than the accident killed me. Make her take people with her. She measures her life in miles because miles don't ask questions. Someone has to.*

*Your friend despite everything,*

*T.*

Wendell read it three times. The third time she wasn't reading it so much as standing inside it, hearing Tom's voice in the cadence, feeling the particular gentleness he'd always used when he was worried about her — the gentleness that drove her insane, because it meant he'd already decided something about her that she hadn't agreed to.

*Make her take people with her.*

She looked back at the pilothouse. Bo would be up by now, muttering at his gauges. Sefa would have coffee going. Priya would be at the stern rail with her charts, cross-checking coordinates she'd copied off a sleeping man's lips. Ansel would be helping without being asked, the way she did everything, carrying her dead sister up a river that had killed her sister's valley. Six people aboard, counting the traitors, and every one of them had voted at Reprieve to keep coming north.

Tom had asked Odile to make her take people with her. And Odile had — three years late, wrapped in lies and sealed crates and a false manifest, but she had. Whether it was penance or strategy or some tangled knot of both, the shape of it matched the shape of the letter, and Wendell could not make that coincidence small enough to ignore.

But belief was a different animal from understanding, and she wasn't ready to feed it yet.

* * *

She came back into the pilothouse and folded the letter along its old creases and handed it back. Odile took it without meeting her eyes.

"Here's where we are," Wendell said. "I believe you were threatened. I believe you pulled the money. I believe you're sorry in a way that's eaten you for three years. What I don't believe — what I can't believe yet — is that you had no idea anyone was going up that river after him. Because Ansel says Voss was hired to destroy evidence, and Voss was hired by Halewater, and men like Corrick don't send verification teams to sites they expect to stay buried. Somebody knew the cache existed. Somebody knew Tom had secured it. And if that knowledge passed through you—"

"It didn't." Odile said it fast, too fast, and Wendell watched her catch herself, watch the politician surface for one naked instant before drowning again. "I didn't know the cache location. That's true. That part I can prove, if proof matters anymore — I've spent three years trying to buy the coordinates from every guide and pilot who works the high route, and nobody's sold them to me, because nobody has them. Tom gave them to no one. Which means the only way Halewater could find it was through the man who hid it, and the only way to make a careful man like Tom Lockridge give up a location—" She stopped.

"Was to take him alive," Wendell finished. The words tasted like river water. Cold, and full of silt.

Neither of them spoke. Upstream, faint as a heartbeat, the glacier boomed again — the third report of the morning, closer than the last, the ice announcing its schedule to anyone fluent enough to listen. Wendell was fluent. The message was simple: *hurry.*

"We have four days," she said. "Maybe five. We run hard today and tomorrow, we make the cache by tomorrow night, and we dig through the night if we have to. Then we come home through whatever the river has left us." She turned to the wheel and began uncoiling the lashings. "And Odile — when we're back in open water, you and I are going to finish this conversation in front of the whole crew, with everything on the table, including whatever pieces you're still holding back. Not because I doubt your penance. Because my family deserves the whole truth, and so does Tom, and so do I. You've bought honesty with honesty so far. Keep it up and maybe we survive each other."

Odile stood a moment longer, the letter disappearing into her coat, and for once she didn't argue terms or negotiate conditions. "There's one more thing," she said quietly. "Something I haven't told you because I only confirmed it yesterday, from Ansel. Corrick isn't in Reprieve anymore. He took a posting downstream — at the confluence station. Directly on our route home."

Wendell's hands went still on the wheel spokes.

"He knows what's in those crates," Odile said. "And he'll know we're coming."

Behind them, up the long gray valley, the ice boomed a fourth time — and this time, Wendell could have sworn she felt it through the hull, through the wheel, through the bones of her hands, like the whole frozen world leaning its weight against the door.
