## 26. Vote at the Confluence

The confluence came up on them in the last gray hour before dawn, where the meltwater river they had been climbing met a tributary out of the eastern ice and the two currents argued with each other across a bar of pale gravel. Wendell brought the *Coriander* in against the quieter water, backed her stern toward the shingle, and let the anchor bite. The engine ticked as it cooled. Somewhere far to the north — too far to see, not too far to hear — the glacier spoke again, a long rolling boom that moved through the hull and up through the soles of their boots like something clearing its throat.

She had heard it eleven times since midnight.

"Everyone in the saloon," she said. "Please."

They came without argument, which frightened her more than arguing would have. Bo arrived still wiping bilge grease from his knuckles. Sefa came from the galley carrying a pot of coffee she set down hard enough to slosh, as if the pot itself were an accusation against the hour. Priya had her charts already spread on the table, weighted at the corners with mugs. Ansel sat with her hands folded, very still, the way people sit when they have decided something and are waiting for permission to say it. Voss stood by the door with his splinted forearm cradled against his chest, and for once he did not look anywhere except at Wendell.

Odile did not come. That was deliberate. Wendell had told her, quietly, on the afterdeck an hour ago: *This one's ours. You'll hear what we decide.* And Odile — Odile of all people — had nodded and gone below, which told Wendell more about how bad things looked than any chart could.

Wendell stood at the head of the table and found she didn't know where to put her hands.

*Three years,* she thought. *Three years I've run this boat like grief was cargo I could lash down tight enough.* She looked around the saloon — at the tin lamp swinging gently with the current, at Priya's neat annotations, at the coffee going cold because nobody drank while a captain was standing — and understood that everything she was about to do would either hold them together or break them apart, and that both outcomes were better than the silence they'd been carrying since Reprieve.

"You've all earned the truth," she said. "All of it. So here it is, once, straight through, and then we vote."

She laid it out the way she'd lay out a route: reach by reach, hazard by hazard.

"The manifest Odile gave us is false. Ansel gave me the real one." She put it on the table. "The crates we've been hauling are core samples and company documents from the upper Thessaly. Water assays going back nine years. Internal memos. Proof that Calder-Marrow Mining knew the tailings ponds were leaking arsenic into this river, knew it by year three, and buried the reports rather than lose the lease." She watched faces as she said it. Bo's jaw worked. Sefa went very still, the way she did over a burning pan. "Tom found the cache three weeks before our last trip. He logged its contents. He wrote in the ship's log — the pages Priya recovered — *O. knows. Tell W.* He was trying to get it to me when the ice took him."

"And Odile," Sefa said quietly.

"Odile knew. Odile was paid to be quiet — or threatened into it; the ledger names the harbormaster of Reprieve as the paymaster, and there's an *L* on the signature line that isn't mine no matter how much I wish it were. She hired us to finish what Tom started. Whether it's penance or cleanup, I don't know yet. Maybe she doesn't either."

Ansel spoke for the first time. "It's penance. Or it started as penance and became arithmetic." Her voice was steady, tired. "I've read the woman's correspondence. There's more guilt in it than greed. But guilt makes poor ballast, Captain. It shifts."

"It does," Wendell said. "Here's what's shifting now." She turned to the charts, to the point Priya had marked in red where Voss's sleep-muttered coordinates met the calving fields. "The season turned early. Eleven calving events since midnight. The route home is open for days, not weeks. If we take the cache, we climb to it, we dig it out, we load it, and we run the gauntlet downriver through ice that will be coming off that glacier the whole way. The *Coriander* has already taken damage. Bo?"

Bo cleared his throat. "Shaft bearing's singing. I can keep her alive for maybe sixty hours of hard running if nothing hits the wheel. Anything bigger than a rowboat hits us in that brash, and sixty hours becomes six minutes." He looked around the table. "I'm not saying don't go. I'm saying know what we're gambling."

"I've known Bo eight years," Wendell said. "He's never once exaggerated a number. So that's the truth of it." She flattened her palms on the table, and the lamp swung, and the glacier boomed again, farther now or nearer, impossible to tell. "Two choices. We take the cache and run home with evidence worth more than every boat on this river, and some of us might die getting it out. Or we turn around right now, run light and fast, everyone probably lives, and the company wins for another decade — another generation — and every village downstream drinks it. Ansel's sister's valley included."

Nobody spoke. The current muttered against the hull. Somewhere aft, the anchor chain shifted and settled like a sleeping animal.

"So we vote," Wendell said. "Not because I need your permission — a captain doesn't, on the water. Because this isn't navigation. This is choosing what we're willing to lose, and that belongs to all of us. Yes means north: cache, gauntlet, truth. No means home: live, and carry whatever this costs you instead. I vote yes." She said it plainly, without theater, and sat down, because a vote among equals shouldn't be taken standing over anyone.

Priya voted next. She had been annotating the margin of her chart through the whole speech — Wendell could see the small precise letters — and now she capped her pen.

"Yes." Then, because she was Priya, she explained. "I spent six years in an archive watching documents get stamped 'restricted' and disappear. Nine years of water assays. Do you understand what that file is? It's the only copy of nine years of a river's memory. If we leave it under the ice, it doesn't stay safe. It stays *gone*. Archives burn, Captain. Ice melts anyway. At least this way somebody chooses." She folded her hands. "Yes."

Sefa poured herself coffee she didn't drink, holding the mug in both hands like something warm she could argue with.

"My daughter would have been twenty-six this spring," she said, to the table, not to anyone. "You all know that. You know why I cook for a boat instead of a house." She lifted her eyes. "I voted against this job in the beginning. I smelled rot on that dock and I said so, and I was right — there was rot, worse than I guessed. But I've been listening to this crew lie awake for a week, and I'll tell you what rot does in close quarters: it spreads until it kills everything, or it gets cut out." She set the mug down. "We cut it out, we carry it home, and we feed the town that stands with us when we get there. Yes. God help us, yes."

Bo laughed once, short, without much humor in it. "You all know mine. I found Tom Lockridge's nameplate welded into my own engine room and I sat in the dark with it for an hour and I didn't tell anybody for four days, and I've hated myself for those four days ever since." He rubbed his grease-stained knuckles. "That compartment should've been opened three years ago. Everything in this boat that's been sealed shut should be opened. Cache, gauntlet, whole thing. Yes."

Four yeses. Wendell felt her chest unclench by a single degree and forced herself not to count ahead, because there were two votes left and one of them belonged to a woman who'd been paid to destroy everything they were voting to save.

Ansel unfolded her hands. When she spoke, her voice cracked on the first word and steadied on the second, and she let it.

"My name is Maren Ansel. My sister was Ilse." She said the name the way Priya might handle a brittle page — carefully, because it was old and irreplaceable. "Calder-Marrow's upstream operations poisoned the Kettle River where we grew up. Ilse drank that water for nineteen years before anyone admitted what was in it. She's been dead five years this autumn, and the settlement check was smaller than the funeral cost." She looked directly at Wendell. "I took this contract to get leverage on the company. I told myself I'd verify the cache, destroy the incriminating parts, keep enough to make them bleed. That was the plan. Then I portaged two miles beside your engineer while he talked about his sister he couldn't save, and I hauled a sledge up scree next to a man who lost his brother on this same river, and somewhere in there my plan died and I let it." She drew a breath. "Ilse never got a hearing. This file is a hearing. Yes."

Five.

Every face turned to Voss.

He had not moved from the door the entire time. His splinted arm rested against his ribs, and in the lamplight Wendell saw for the first time how young he looked without the flat professional stillness he wore like a coat — young, and exhausted, and weighing something heavier than any crate they'd loaded.

"You don't owe us a yes," Wendell said. "You're under contract to the other side of this. Nobody on this boat will think less—"

"Stop," Voss said. Not rude. Just done. He crossed the saloon and sat down at the table, and it was somehow the most deliberate thing Wendell had seen anyone do in weeks.

"I've worked security contracts eleven years," he said. "Verification, retrieval, disposal. Disposal mostly. You learn not to ask what's in the crates; asking shortens careers." His good hand lay flat on the table. "In Reprieve I copied the real manifest. I verified the assay numbers against the originals. It was my job. I did it well." A pause. "My mother lives in Harrow Bend. Downstream. Forty miles below the tailings ponds." 

The lamp swung. Nobody breathed.

"I've been doing the arithmetic since the Narrows," Voss went on, quieter. "What my silence pays, what my mother's water costs. The numbers stopped working somewhere around the portage and I've been pretending otherwise, which is what I was actually hired for — companies don't buy guards, they buy men willing to pretend." He looked up, and his eyes were dry and level and certain. "Voss is the surname on the contract. My first name is Daniel. If we're doing this, you should know whose word you're taking." He put his hand flat on the false manifest, next to Ansel's true one, side by side like opposing testimony finally allowed to share a table. "Yes. Take the cache. Burn my contract. I'll testify."

For a moment the saloon held a silence that wasn't empty — the full kind, the kind after something settles.

Then Sefa got up, filled a sixth mug, and put it in front of him without a word, and Voss looked at the coffee as though it were a medal.

"That's six," Priya said. Her voice wobbled half a tone and she pretended it hadn't. "Unanimous."

Wendell stood. Her legs felt strange, lighter, as if the boat had come off a wave she hadn't noticed riding.

"Then here's how it goes," she said, and the captain's voice came back into her because the route demanded it. "Today we lighten ship — everything nonessential goes onto the bank. We rig sledges. We take the launch's towline and the winch cable and every rope Bo owns. We climb at first light tomorrow, dig the vault free, load what matters, and we are back on this boat inside forty-eight hours or we leave the rest behind, because the window closes whether we're ready or not." She looked at each of them in turn — Bo, Sefa, Priya, Ansel, Voss. "Odile hears the result, but she doesn't command anything above the waterline anymore. This expedition answers to the six people in this room. Agreed?"

"Agreed," they said — five voices, ragged, certain.

"Then get some sleep. All of you. Real sleep." She managed something that was almost a smile. "You're going to want it. Tomorrow we introduce ourselves to a glacier."

They filed out — Sefa already muttering about high-altitude rations, Bo talking shaft bearings with Voss, of all people, the two of them heads together over the engine like old colleagues, Priya rolling her charts with the tenderness other people reserve for infants. Ansel paused at the door and looked back at Wendell, and neither of them said anything, and nothing needed saying.

Wendell stayed alone in the saloon a moment longer. Through the forward windows the eastern sky had gone the color of pewter, and against it the northern horizon showed a white line that hadn't been there yesterday — the calving fields, closer than the charts promised, catching the first light like a blade.

The glacier boomed again. Nearer this time. She was sure of it now.

She picked up the two manifests, folded them together into one packet, and went to wake Odile.

* * *

Odile was awake, of course, sitting fully dressed on her bunk in the dark like a woman waiting for a verdict she'd already memorized. Wendell stood in the doorway and told her in four sentences: unanimous, six for six, including Voss, including Ansel. The cache, the gauntlet, all of it.

Something moved through Odile Marchetti's face that Wendell had never seen there in thirty years of rivalry — not relief exactly, and not fear, but the expression of a person setting down weight they'd forgotten they were carrying.

"Six for six," Odile repeated softly. "He'd have liked that. Tom always said a good crew decides together or dies divided."

"Don't," Wendell said. "Don't use him yet. Maybe at the end, if we earn it."

Odile inclined her head, accepting the rebuke like a sentence. Outside, faint but unmistakable, the ice boomed again — and beneath the boom, new and thin and wrong, came a sound Wendell had only heard once before, the year her brother died: a long metallic shriek, far upstream, of a serac letting go.

She turned back toward the helm, already calculating miles.
