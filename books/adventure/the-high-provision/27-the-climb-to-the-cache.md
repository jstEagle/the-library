## 27. The Climb to the Cache

The Coriander sat on the gravel shelf like a whale that had wandered inland and given up, her hull ticking as it cooled in the thin air. Bo had never seen her out of the water before. Three years he'd kept her heart beating below decks — the intake, the shaft, the old diesel that ran on prayer and Sefa's cooking grease — and he had always known her as a thing in motion. Beached, she looked wrong. Vulnerable. Like seeing your mother asleep.

"Offload starts now," Wendell said, and her voice carried the way it always did, flat and certain, a keel cutting chop. "Sledges first. Crates in the order they came aboard — Ansel, you have the manifest order. We haul at first light."

The moraine rose above them like a spine, a mile and a half of loose stone and old ice running up toward the white shoulder where, according to Tom Lockridge's coordinates, the High Provision waited in its vault. The glacier above it groaned as Bo looked at it — a long, wet, tectonic sound, like a ship settling in a storm, and every head on the gravel turned toward it the way a flock of birds turns.

"Chatty today," Sefa said, too lightly.

"They're all chatty this time of year," Wendell said. "That's why we climb now and not next week."

Bo said nothing. He'd been keeping his tally of oddities since the dock at home port, and the glacier had just added a line to it: the way Odile Marchetti stood apart from the rest of them, looking up at the ice with an expression he'd only ever seen on men at funerals.

* * *

The sledges were old survey gear, hauled out of the hold with the crates — ash and steel, runners worn to knives, heavier than they looked. Bo rigged the harness points himself, checking each shackle twice, because if a sledge let go on a slope it wouldn't stop until it found a person. There were six of them now, and he'd stopped pretending he didn't count them like his own pulse.

Voss worked beside him in silence, and the silence had changed. Bo had catalogued the man early on as a problem: quiet the way a locked door is quiet, hands too careful around the crates, a wrist healing crooked from the Narrows because he'd grabbed a wheel that wasn't his to grab. But somewhere above the portage, something had shifted. Voss hauled like a man paying a debt. He asked for the heaviest loads. And this morning, when the second sledge's runner cracked on a buried stone, it was Voss who knelt in the meltwater without being asked and said, "I can weld this. Give me the torch."

"You're a surveyor," Bo said.

"I'm whatever's useful," Voss said, and struck the arc.

Bo watched the blue light play across the man's face and added a line to his tally: *Voss welds better than I do.* He didn't say it out loud. Some things you kept for the ledger.

The crates came off in the order Ansel read out, her voice steady, her hands not. Bo had carried her up the portage's worst stretch on his back when her boots gave out, and she'd talked the whole way — not to fill silence, he'd realized later, but because silence was where her sister lived. He'd learned to listen without answering. It was a skill the river taught you anyway.

"Crates eleven through fourteen go last," Ansel said, tapping the manifest. "They're the documents. Wendell wants them on the middle sledge, wrapped."

"Middle sledge goes where I put it," Bo said. "Middle sledge is the one I trust."

* * *

They made camp at the toe of the moraine that first night, and the glacier talked to them until dawn. It wasn't one sound but a vocabulary of them — the deep groan of the ice shifting on its own weight, the crack like a rifle when a crevasse opened somewhere in the dark, the hiss of meltwater running under everything, under the whole world, it seemed. Bo lay in his bag listening to it and thought: *this is what the river sounds like when it's thinking.*

He'd grown up on the Thessaly's lower reaches, where water was brown and patient and forgave you. This water was blue-green and furious and forgave nothing. He understood now why Wendell navigated it by sound, why she'd stand at the helm with her eyes half closed like a woman at a concert. Up here, sound was the only honest instrument.

At midnight, Voss sat up by the stove and said, "The seracs on the east flank. We pass under them tomorrow."

"Charts say the route keeps to the west of the moraine crest," Priya said. She had the coordinates out again, her copied page soft at the folds from handling. She checked it the way other people checked a rosary.

"The charts are three years old." Voss fed a stick into the stove. "Ice moves. The route I was given — the route *we* were given — passes under the flank. There's a shelf. It's the only way up."

"Who gave you the route?" Wendell asked, from the dark beyond the stove light.

Voss was quiet long enough that the glacier filled the pause with a long groan, rising at the end like a question.

"The same people who gave me the manifest," he said finally. "I'm done pretending the two things are different."

* * *

They climbed at first light with the mountain blue and the air sharp enough to taste. Bo had hauled freight all his life — crates, engines, once a whole disassembled dredge over a portage in the delta — but nothing had prepared him for the weight of a loaded sledge on a ten-degree slope of loose stone. Every step, the moraine tried to take the effort back. You'd plant a boot and the scree would pour away beneath it, and the sledge behind you would settle its whole ton into the harness like a hand on your shoulder saying *no.*

They went in a chain: Wendell breaking trail, then Priya, then Bo and Voss on the middle sledge, then Sefa and Ansel on the second, Odile at the rear where Wendell had put her and where she stayed without argument, which told Bo more than any confession had.

The groans came all morning. Some were distant, folded into the wind. Some were close enough that Bo felt them in his molars, and once — a sharp double crack directly overhead — the whole chain stopped and stood like deer, faces tipped up, watching the white flank for the slide that didn't come.

"Keep moving," Wendell said. "Standing still is how the mountain picks you."

It was near midday when the route bent east, exactly as Voss had said it would, exactly as the charts said it shouldn't. The shelf he'd described cut across the face below the seracs — a ramp of old ice and packed snow, maybe forty meters of exposure, with the blue walls of the icefall hanging above it like a held breath. Bo stood at the base and looked up and felt his tally open itself and write: *this is a bad idea, and it is the only idea.*

"Rope up," Wendell said. "Two teams. Bo anchors. We cross light — no sledges until the first team clears it."

"Then who hauls the sledges?" Sefa asked.

"We do. Twice." Wendell was already coiling rope. "Nobody crosses under that ice with a ton of cargo. We take ourselves across, set anchors, and winch the loads."

It was the kind of plan Bo loved because it was the kind he'd have made: slow, ugly, safe. He set the first anchor in a boulder the size of a stove, drilled and hammered while his fingers went stupid with cold, and ran the line up the shelf to a second anchor Voss placed — and Voss placed it *well*, Bo had to give him that, screwing the ice screw in at the angle the manuals said and not the angle tired men use. The man had done this before. More than surveying.

"Where'd you learn ice work?" Bo asked him, low, while they rigged.

Voss looked at the seracs, not at him. "Company training. They send people up here to check on things nobody's supposed to be checking on." A pause. "I was good at it. That's why they picked me for this. I've spent the whole river deciding what to do with being good at the wrong things."

Bo tied off the line. "You're doing it now," he said, and Voss didn't answer, but his shoulders came down half an inch, like a man setting down something he'd been carrying since a dock a thousand miles south.

* * *

The first team crossed clean. Wendell, Priya, Voss — forty meters under the hanging ice, boots crunching, breath smoking, the rope singing faintly between them. Bo watched from the base with his hand on the anchor, feeding line, counting their steps like a man counting his own heartbeats.

They were thirty meters across when the glacier cleared its throat.

It wasn't a groan this time. It was a *report* — a single enormous crack from the east flank, sharp as the world breaking a bone — and Bo's eyes went up on pure reflex and found the serac: a house-sized blade of blue ice on the flank's edge, leaning, and now, horribly, *moving*, tilting with the slow grandeur of something that has all the time in the world and intends to use none of it.

"MOVE!" Bo's voice tore out of him. "MOVE MOVE MOVE —"

The serac went.

There was no understanding it as an event. It was simply that a mountain's worth of ice detached itself from the mountain and came down the slope toward the shelf, and the three small figures on the shelf became figures *running*, and the sound arrived — not the crash, that was still coming, but the sound of the air itself being shoved aside, a rising white roar like the river's voice a thousandfold.

Priya was last. She'd turned to look — Bo would learn later she'd turned to check on Voss, who'd stumbled — and the turn cost her two steps, and two steps on a shelf of ice under a collapsing serac is a country mile.

The serac hit the slope above the shelf and *burst*. The main mass went wide, down the face, away from them — a miracle Bo filed away to be grateful for later — but the burst threw a fan of debris, blocks the size of tables, and the shelf itself bucked like a deck in a sea. The rope snapped taut. Bo felt the anchor at his back take the load, felt the line go bar-hard, and then felt it *slither* — Priya, thrown off her feet, sliding down the shelf on her back, arms windmilling, toward the lip where the moraine fell away two hundred meters of broken blue nothing.

The rope held her. Barely. It held her weight and not her slide, and she was being pendulumed toward the edge, and the anchor above — Bo saw it in one flick of his eyes — was chewing out of the ice, a halo of white powder puffing around the screw.

He didn't decide anything. Later, trying to reconstruct it, he never could. His body simply left the ground.

He went up the line hand over hand, boots on the ice, crampons he'd grumbled about wearing all morning biting home, the rope burning through his gloves, through his palms, and he was shouting something — he'd learn from Sefa afterward that it was Priya's name, over and over, like a prayer with one word in it. The world was still roaring. Ice dust filled the air and turned the sun into a white blur. He reached the shelf's slope and saw her: ten meters below him, sliding, the rope taut from her harness to the failing anchor above, and the geometry of it was simple and obscene — she was a weight on a line, and the line was tied to something that was letting go.

Bo got his boot into a crevasse lip, wrapped the rope twice around his forearm, and *threw his weight backward* off the slope.

The world went white with pain. His arm took her entire falling weight and the mountain's argument about it, and something in his shoulder made a sound he felt in his teeth, and the anchor above them — the anchor he'd watched chewing loose — tore free with a crack like the serac's little sibling, and the whole system, Priya and rope and Bo, dropped.

Half a meter. The second anchor — Voss's anchor, placed at the angle the manuals say — caught.

Silence. Real silence, under the fading roar, the kind of silence that has a taste.

Bo hung from his wrapped arm with Priya's weight on the line below him and his shoulder screaming and his breath coming in whoops, and above him, hand over hand down the rope with his face gray as the ice, came Voss.

"Anchor's good," Voss said, and his voice only shook a little. "Mine held. I've got the line — let go, Bo. Let go. I've got her."

Bo let go. It was the hardest physical act of his life, harder than the haul, harder than the hold: opening his ruined hand, giving the weight of a person he loved to a man he'd spent a season not trusting. The rope transferred. Voss took the wrap, set his boots, and held — held the way Bo held an engine at full throttle in floodwater, with everything he had and no thought of anything else.

They got Priya up in ninety seconds that took a year. She was white and shaking and laughing — actually laughing, a thin wild sound — and saying, "I turned around. I *turned around*, you're not supposed to turn around," and Sefa reached them first and wrapped her in arms and a blanket and a stream of scolding in two languages, and Bo sat down on the ice because his legs had resigned without notice.

Wendell crouched in front of him. She took his hand — the rope-burned one — and turned it over, and looked at the raw stripe across the palm, and then looked at his face.

"You went up the line," she said.

"Seemed faster than standing there."

"It was." She squeezed his hand once, careful of the burn, and Bo saw something in her face he'd never seen there in three years: not the flat keel-voice certainty, but the thing under it. The person. "Thank you," Wendell Lockridge said, as if the words were cargo she'd carried a long way and was setting down at last.

Above them, the east flank settled with a long, satisfied groan, and a fine snow of ice dust drifted down over all of them like the mountain's opinion.

* * *

They crossed the rest of the shelf in single file, fast and silent, and camped that night on solid stone above the icefall with the seracs below them instead of above, which everyone pretended was a strategic choice and nobody believed.

Bo's shoulder was strapped — Sefa had bound it with a competence that scared him slightly — and his palm was dressed, and Priya had appointed herself his nurse with a ferocity that permitted no appeal. She sat next to him at the fire and didn't say anything for a long time, which was its own kind of language between them.

"You turned around," Bo said finally. "To check on Voss."

"He went down. I thought he was off the shelf." She fed the fire. "He wasn't. He'd caught himself. And then he held the rope that kept me on this side of the world." She looked across the fire to where Voss sat checking the anchors for morning, methodical, thorough, a man inspecting his own work twice because once had already saved a life. "I had him wrong, Bo. I had him wrong the whole river."

"Ledger's open," Bo said. "We amend it. That's what a ledger's for."

Priya smiled — tired, real. "When did you get wise?"

"About an hour ago. It'll wear off."

The laugh moved down the fire, from Priya to Sefa to Ansel, and even Wendell's mouth moved at the corner of it, and Bo sat in the middle of the sound with his arm strapped to his chest and felt something he couldn't have named if you'd paid him: the six of them, forged. Not a crew. Crews you could hire. This was something the mountain had made in an afternoon, the way it made everything up here — under pressure, out of ordinary material, into something that would hold.

Voss came and sat down across from him and put the anchor kit between them, and began, without asking, to show Priya how the screws were set and why his had held and the other hadn't, and Bo listened and thought: *he's teaching us so we can save each other next time, because he knows there'll be a next time.*

The glacier groaned again, farther off now, almost conversational. And then it groaned again, and Bo sat up, because this one was different — deeper, longer, coming not from the flank behind them but from the high ice ahead, toward the shoulder where the coordinates said the vault lay. A boom rolled down the valley and echoed off everything, and the dogs of silence came in after it.

"That wasn't settling," Wendell said. She was standing, looking north, and the firelight put hard shadows under her eyes. "That was calving."

"The season's not due for days," Ansel said.

"It's not asking what's due." Wendell's eyes stayed on the high ice, where a plume of white was rising off a ridge none of them could see the base of. "That's the glacier letting go of something big. And it's between us and the cache."

She turned back to the fire, and Bo watched her do the thing he'd watched her do at every crisis since the dock: measure it, in miles, in hours, in the only currency she trusted.

"We climb at first light," Wendell said. "All the way. No second camp."
