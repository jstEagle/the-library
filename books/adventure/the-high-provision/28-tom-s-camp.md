## 28. Tom's Camp

The moraine gave way to blue ice an hour past dawn, and Wendell felt the change through her boots before she saw it — the ground going from loose stone to something older, harder, patient. Glacial ice, compressed over centuries into a glass that held light the way water holds a drowned thing.

They came up out of the trough single file, sledges creaking behind them, and there it was.

A camp.

It sat on a shelf of ice above the calving field like a ship run aground on the sky — two small tents collapsed under years of snowload, a cook stove furred with frost, a line of supply crates half-swallowed by the glacier's slow breathing. Everything was preserved the way only cold preserves things: utterly, mercilessly, as if the mountain had decided to keep him.

Wendell stopped walking.

She hadn't known she was going to stop. Her legs simply ended their argument with gravity, and Priya nearly walked into her back, and Bo said something — *Captain?* — from behind, and none of it reached her, because three years of blank space had just opened its mouth.

*Tom's camp.*

She knew it the way you know your own handwriting. The left tent pitched with its door facing away from the wind because he'd never learned to argue with weather, only to accommodate it. The crate stack built in the particular staggered pattern he used so nothing rolled. The tin lantern hanging from an ice-axe driven into the shelf — his lantern, dented on one side where he'd dropped it off the *Coriander*'s rail when they were kids and she'd fished it out with a gaff hook and never let him forget it.

"He wintered here," Ansel said quietly, beside her. "Or part of one. The snow stratigraphy says—"

"I know what it says." Wendell's voice came out flat and strange, someone else's voice. She cleared her throat. "He was here. Before."

Nobody moved for a moment. On the ice below them, far down, the glacier made a sound like a whale clearing its throat — a long subterranean groan that traveled up through their boots and settled somewhere behind the sternum.

"We should be careful," Voss said. His wrist was splinted, strapped across his chest, and he'd said maybe forty words since the serac collapse, all of them useful. "The shelf could be unstable. We go slow, we go light."

"We go," Wendell said, and started forward.

* * *

The first tent was Tom's sleeping tent, and it was almost gentle inside — a bedroll, a book swollen with damp and then frozen open, a tin cup with a tea bag still in it, decades-old tea gone to amber glass. There were photographs pinned to the tent wall with bent nails, and Wendell stood in the low doorway a long time before she could make herself look at them.

Him. Younger than she remembered, or exactly as old as she remembered and she'd been lying to herself about it. Standing on the *Coriander*'s foredeck with his arms spread like he was holding up the sky. Him and Sefa in the galley, both laughing at something out of frame. Him and the fishing partner who wouldn't come to the dock to see them off, the two of them holding up a fish between them like a treaty flag. And one photograph she didn't recognize: Tom standing at this very camp, arms crossed, looking directly into the camera with an expression she had no name for. Not fear. Not resolve. Something like a man who has done arithmetic he doesn't like and gotten the same answer twice.

Behind the photographs, wedged into a seam in the tent fabric, was a oilskin notebook.

Wendell took it out with both hands, the way you take something from a shrine.

His notes. She knew his handwriting before she opened it — the cramped leftward slant, the way he crossed his t's like he was striking them through. Water levels. Sample sites. Dates running backward through a season she had no memory of living, which was its own kind of horror, like finding entries in a diary written in a room of your house you didn't know existed. He'd mapped the upper river in detail. He'd marked the cache location — the High Provision — with a triangle and the words *stocked for six. Maybe more.*

*Stocked for six.* Wendell looked up, and found Bo watching her from the second tent's entrance, and understood that Bo was counting too. Six of them. Six now, on the ice.

There were other pages. Tables of numbers she couldn't parse — concentrations, dates, locations along the river she knew by heart, every town from Reprieve down to the sea. In the margins, in letters pressed so hard the paper had torn in places: *they know I have this. Keep moving. Do not trust the manifest. DO NOT TRUST THE MANIFEST.*

The last dated entry was four days before the accident.

Wendell closed the notebook. Her hands were steady, which surprised her; she'd expected them to shake. But grief, she had learned, did not always announce itself. Sometimes it just moved in and rearranged the furniture and let you walk around in the dark bumping into things.

"Captain." Priya's voice, from outside. Careful. "You need to see this."

* * *

The second tent had been his work tent, and the ice had claimed it more thoroughly — a seam of meltwater had refrozen through the floor, welding the contents into a single translucent block, so that everything in it appeared to float: a camp table, a microscope case, stacks of sample vials glowing faintly green-blue in the glacial light like a jar of captured summer. And on the table, weighted down under a stone, an envelope.

Her name on it. *Wendell.* Just that. His handwriting, unhurried, the W with its little flourish he'd been doing since he was nine years old and thought it made him look like a riverboat gambler.

The envelope was not frozen. Someone — Tom — had kept it somewhere the meltwater wouldn't reach. Under the stone. Ready.

"He meant for someone to find it," Ansel said softly. She'd come up beside Wendell without her noticing, and for once Wendell didn't mind. "He put it where thaw would reach it first. That's not storage. That's a message in a bottle pointed at the future."

"It's pointed at me," Wendell said.

She picked it up. It weighed almost nothing. Paper and ink and three years of a hole in her head, all in one hand's width.

Bo had come around too, and Priya, and even Voss stood at a respectful distance with his splinted arm cradled like something breakable. They formed a loose ring around her without discussing it, the way crews do, the way families do — present without pressing. Sefa wasn't there; Sefa was down at the sledges keeping watch on the calving field, because somebody had to, and because Sefa had a gift for knowing when a moment needed fewer people in it.

"You don't have to read it here," Priya said. "We could take it down. Warmth. Light."

"If I take it down," Wendell said, "I'll find a reason not to read it. I've had three years of reasons." She worked her glove off with her teeth. Her fingers were clumsy with cold and something worse. "Stand wherever you want. I'm opening it."

The paper inside was thin, cheap stock, the kind Tom bought in bales because he went through it writing letters he mostly never sent. She knew that about him. She'd teased him about it. A drawer full of unsent letters, she used to say, you're a riverboat captain, not a novelist. And he'd grin and say somebody has to say the things, Wen. Doesn't mean anybody has to hear them yet.

She unfolded it.

*Wen,*

*If you're reading this, then either I'm dead or I finally got brave, and we both know which one the odds favor.*

*I'm writing this at the camp because it's the last quiet place on the river and because if anything happens to me I want it to happen near something I chose. You'd understand that. You measure everything in miles. I measure it in places. Same map, different religion.*

*Here's what I know, and I'm writing it fast because I heard the ice talking tonight and it talks early when a bad season's coming.*

*The company poisoned the upper Thessaly. Not an accident, not a spill — a practice. The core samples prove it goes back eleven years. I have copies. The originals are in the cache below the calving field, and I stocked it for whoever comes next, because I don't think I'm getting these home, and I'd rather be wrong alone than right and silent.*

*There's a deal being made. Odile knows. I think money has already moved. I don't think Odile knows everything — I think she thinks she's buying safety, hers and yours, and I think she's wrong about what safety costs. She's not evil, Wen. She's scared. Scared people sign things.*

*But it isn't Odile I'm afraid of.*

Wendell's breath stopped.

The ice around her seemed to stop too — the light, the wind, the distant mutter of the calving field, everything drawing a long cold breath — and in that held silence the memory came up out of the dark where it had been drowning for three years, and it did not come gently.

* * *

Lantern light on black water.

That was the first thing. Always the first thing, though she hadn't known it until now — the lantern, Tom's lantern, hanging from the stern rail of a boat that was not the *Coriander*, a smaller boat, a night boat, and the river running high and silent under a moon the clouds kept taking away and giving back.

She was there. She had always been there. That was the thing the blankness had hidden — not that she'd been absent from the final night, but that she'd been *in* it, every minute of it, and her mind had amputated the whole of it rather than carry it.

They were anchored below the cache site. Tom had brought her upriver in secret — no crew, no manifest, just the two of them and the truth in sealed tubes — because he'd said, *I'm not doing this without you, Wen, and I'm not doing it with anyone else either.* She remembered arguing. She remembered the specific shape of the argument: she'd wanted to wait, to gather more, to bring it downriver through channels, and he'd said the channels were bought, all of them, and she'd hated that he was probably right.

And then, past midnight, another boat.

No lights. That was how she'd known, before anything else, before the sound of the engine or the shape of it against the bank — no lights on the water at night means someone doesn't want to be seen, and nobody legitimate runs dark on the Thessaly. She'd woken Tom. They'd watched it come, a low silhouette hugging the shadowed bank, and Tom had said, very quietly, *get the samples to the cache,* and she'd said *no, we leave together,* and he'd said *Wendell,* in the voice he saved for saying her name like a promise and a goodbye at once—

She'd rowed for the cache. That was the shape of her guilt, the exact and terrible geometry of it: she had obeyed him. She'd taken the tubes and slipped into the water and started paddling for the shore, and behind her she'd heard voices on the dark boat, and one of them —

One of them she knew.

Not Odile. That was the lie her mind had built afterward, the false relief, the story everyone told: Odile Marchetti was there that night, Odile did it, Odile the rival, Odile the witch, because Odile was a story everyone already knew how to believe. But the voice shouting across the water — angry, then frightened, then furious in a different register, the fury of a man whose plan is coming apart — that voice belonged to the harbormaster of Reprieve. The company man. The one whose name sat in a ledger next to payments, next to an initial that had made her sick for days because she'd thought it meant her.

*L.* Not Lockridge. The name was on the tip of the restored memory now, whole and ugly: she'd known him twenty years. He'd eaten at Sefa's table. He'd shaken Tom's hand at the dock a hundred times.

She'd turned the boat. Of course she'd turned the boat. She'd left the tubes floating on their line and pulled for the night boat with her arms burning, and she'd gotten there in time to see it — the hull already breached, cut, sabotaged below the waterline while Tom was aboard, while Tom was trying to stop them, and the ice on the high river booming like artillery because the season was turning early, turning violent, and the cold water came in faster than any boat could live with.

She'd grabbed him. She had his arm. She had his sleeve. She could feel it now — the wet wool, the terrible give of it — and the river pulling, and the lantern swinging wild on its hook throwing light in arcs across the water like a lighthouse losing its mind, and Tom looking at her with the moon full on his face at last, and him saying — not screaming, that was the worst of it, he wasn't screaming, he was calm, he was *certain* —

*Let go, Wen. Let go. Tell them. Tell them everything.*

And the ice boomed, and the water took him, and his lantern sank — she watched it go down, burning all the way, a small gold sun descending into the black — and she held his empty sleeve until the river pried that from her too.

And her mind, which had carried wrecks and drownings and thirty years of hard water without cracking, had looked at that night and simply refused it. Burned the charts. Blacked out the channel. Left her alive with a hole shaped exactly like her brother, and a story — Odile, weather, accident — that everyone could live inside because the truth had no room in it for anyone to keep living.

She was on her knees in the ice tent. She didn't remember getting there. The letter was still in her hands, unread past the third paragraph, and her tears had frozen on her face, which she noticed distantly, clinically, the way you notice weather.

"Wendell." Bo's voice, close, rough. A hand on her shoulder, heavy and careful. "Captain. Talk to us."

"I was there." Her voice came out in pieces. "I was *there*. Three years I thought — everybody said I wasn't aboard, that I was home, that I — I rowed for the cache because he told me to. I left him. He told me to leave him and I *obeyed*, and I came back and the water had him, and I—" The sentence collapsed. She let it.

Priya knelt on her other side. "You didn't leave him. He sent you away. Those are different things."

"They feel identical from in here."

"I know," Priya said, and Wendell believed that she did — Priya the archivist, who spent her life among things that survived their owners, who understood better than any of them what it cost to be the one who keeps the record.

Ansel said nothing at all. She just took off her own glove and put her bare hand flat on the frozen ground between them, an offering to the ice, to the dead, to whatever ceremony grief required when it arrived this size. Even Voss lowered himself carefully to sit against the tent pole, splinted arm and all, and stayed.

The glacier spoke again, longer this time, a groan that climbed the scale and ended in a crack like a rifle shot somewhere down in the calving field. Snow sifted from the tent fabric. Nobody flinched. They were past flinching, all of them, or not there yet, but gathered anyway.

After a while — she didn't know how long — Wendell wiped her face with the back of her wrist and looked down at the letter, at Tom's handwriting waiting patiently under the lamplight they'd rigged at the tent mouth, and she understood that she had only read the beginning. That the rest of it was still there. That her brother had been sitting in this cold writing to her for hours, probably, saying the things he never sent, and that he had known — God help him, he had *known* — that she'd need every word.

"There's more," she said. Her voice was wrecked but level. "I have to finish it."

"Whatever it says," Bo said, "we're here."

Wendell nodded once and smoothed the page against her knee, and read on — past the fear, past the names, into the part where her brother turned from the evidence to her, and began, in his unhurried hand, to tell her exactly what he wanted her to do with the rest of her life.

Outside, down on the white field, the glacier calved for the first time that day — a boom that rolled up the valley like a door closing somewhere far away, and then, after a silence, another, closer.
