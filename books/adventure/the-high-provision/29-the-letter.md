## 29. The Letter

The ice had Tom's handwriting in it.

That was the thought Wendell carried down the moraine in the blue hour after the calving, her boots punching through crust, the cache vault's iron door swinging shut behind them with a sound like a held breath finally released. The others were ahead — Bo hauling the sledge with the crates of core samples lashed down under canvas, Priya half-carrying Voss, whose arm was bound and splinted and who kept insisting, in a voice gone thin with cold, that he was fine, he was fine. Sefa had Ansel by the elbow. Odile walked last, alone, the way a person walks when they've just set fire to their own life and are watching it burn with a kind of terrible relief.

Wendell walked apart from all of them, because she was carrying something that weighed more than any crate.

Tom's letter. Unsent. Addressed in his blocky, patient capitals — the same hand he'd used on the Coriander's maintenance schedule, on the flour sacks Sefa ordered, on the birthday card he'd given Wendell the last year they'd both been whole: *W. LOCKRIDGE, CAPT., PER HER PREFERENCE.* She had found it in the vault, in a waterproof sleeve, tucked inside the supply crate he'd labeled FOR THE CREW WHO FINISHES THIS, and she had not been able to open it on the ice. Her hands had been shaking too badly. Or her courage had been. Same instrument, different reading.

Now they made camp in the lee of a granite shoulder, below the calving fields but out of reach of the brash ice, and Wendell sat with her back against the cold stone and the letter on her knees and did not open it.

The camp built itself around her the way it always did. Bo got the stove hissing. Sefa put water on for something hot and sweet. Priya rigged the tarp. Nobody asked Wendell for anything, which was its own kind of mercy, and nobody looked at her too long, which was its own kind of love. Odile sat at the edge of the firelight, at the exact distance where the warmth still reached but the light didn't quite claim you, and Wendell understood that Odile had chosen that seat the way she'd chosen everything for three years — close enough to be part of it, far enough to deserve nothing.

The glacier boomed again, far off. A long, grinding report, like a door closing in a very large house.

"Wendell." Sefa, crouching beside her, pressing a tin mug into her hands. Sweet tea, too much sugar, the way Sefa made it for shock. "You don't have to read it tonight."

"Yes I do."

"You don't. But you will." Sefa's hand rested on her shoulder a moment, warm through the parka. "Then come eat. Whatever's in there, you'll want to have eaten first."

Wendell watched her go. Sefa always knew. Sefa, who had lost a daughter and chosen this floating family on purpose, who cooked grief into rice and told the truth sideways so it could be swallowed. Wendell drank the tea. It scalded going down and she was grateful for it, for one more sensation that wasn't the letter.

Then she opened it.

* * *

Tom's handwriting was worse than she remembered. He'd always written like a man steering in weather, letters leaning hard into the wind, and these pages were no different — three of them, soft at the folds, the ink feathered where damp had gotten at them once and dried.

*Wendell.*

*If you're reading this, then somebody finished it. I've been trying to imagine who that would be and I keep coming back to you, which I know you'll argue with, so let me get the arguing done first.*

*You're going to say you don't deserve to read my letter. You're going to say you weren't there, or you were there and you failed, or some other thing your brain makes up at three in the morning. I know how you work, Captain. I've been watching you work your whole life. So I'm writing this in advance, which means I'm forgiving you in advance, and there's nothing you can do about it. That's the whole trick of a letter like this. You get no vote.*

*Here's what I need you to know, and I need you to actually know it, not just read it.*

*You were there that night. I know you don't remember. I saw your face when the ice went and I saw it after, when they pulled you out, and I know what that look means because I've seen it on river people who took a knock too hard. You tried to stop it. I'm telling you that because you won't believe it any other way. You were on the ice ahead of me and you were shouting my name and you were trying to stop it, and when the charge went you went in after me, and the water did the rest. Whatever your memory did to survive that, it did it to keep you alive, and I can't be angry at a thing that kept you alive. I need you alive.*

*So: you didn't fail me. You failed to die, which is the opposite, and I'm proud of you for it.*

Wendell stopped. The fire crackled. Somewhere behind her, Bo was saying something quiet to Priya, and the glacier said something quieter back.

She had been there. She had been *there*. The knowledge went through her like a spar through a hull — not new information exactly, but the confirmation of a thing she'd been taking on water around for three years. She had been on the ice. She had shouted his name. She had gone in.

She read on.

*Now the part I hate writing. Names.*

*The sabotage wasn't Odile. I know you and Odile have your history and I know half the river thinks she did it, and I need you to strike that from the chart entirely, because it's wrong, and wrong charts kill people. The man who set the charge is the company's man — Halloran. Marcus Halloran. He was on our expedition as the mining company's "logistics consultant," which meant he was there to make sure the evidence never left the ice. When I found the cache early — weeks early, the melt that year was ahead of schedule — and logged what was in it, Halloran decided the expedition was a liability. He didn't want to kill me specifically. He wanted to kill the expedition, and I happened to be standing on it.*

*He's the one who wired the charge under the crossing route. He's the one who moved the marker stakes so the calving field looked safe when it wasn't. And Wendell — this is the part I've rewritten four times — he's the one who told Odile that if she talked, he'd bury her. Not a figure of speech. He showed her the permits he could revoke, the debts he could call, the accident reports he could write about her brother's boat down south. Odile didn't sell us out. Odile went quiet. There's a difference, and I know it doesn't feel like a difference right now, but it will. Silence bought under threat is still silence, but it isn't treason. I've had a lot of time on the ice to think about the difference.*

*The evidence is all in the vault. Core samples, the company's own internal assays, the water testing they did and buried — the upper river's been poisoned since before we ever ran it, and they've known, and the settlements downstream have been drinking it. I copied everything. I hid the originals where nobody would look for them because nobody looks above the calving fields. If you're reading this, the originals are yours now.*

*Take them down the river. Don't trust the harbormaster at Reprieve — that's Halloran. He bought the post two years ago. It's the perfect place for him: every boat, every manifest, every mouth on the middle river passes his office. He'll be waiting for this expedition. He's been waiting three years.*

*One more thing, and then I'll let you go, because I can hear you saying "get to the point, Tom" even from here.*

*Keep living in miles. That's not a criticism. I used to tease you about it — you measure everything in distance and nothing in years — but I've decided it's the best way there is. Years are things that happen to you. Miles are things you choose. You can't always choose what a year does to you, but you can always choose the next mile. So choose them. Take the crew. Feed them. Keep the Coriander moving. Grief isn't a cargo you put down, Wendell, it's ballast. It's what keeps you upright in the heavy water. You don't get rid of it. You just learn to steer with it.*

*I'm proud of you. I was proud of you the night I died and I'm proud of you now, and I'll be proud of you at the end of this page, and there's nothing you can do about that either.*

*Your brother,*
*Tom.*

*P.S. The stove on the Coriander pulls to port. It's always pulled to port. Bo swears he's fixed it four times. He hasn't. It's the hull. Love you.*

* * *

Wendell read it twice. The second time slower, the way you take a channel you know is full of ice — no, slower than that. The way you take a channel you love, knowing you may not get it again.

Then she folded the letter along Tom's own creases, put it back in its sleeve, and held it against her sternum with both hands, and let the sound come out of her that had been waiting three years to come out. It wasn't loud. It was worse than loud. It was the sound of a lock turning, deep in a hull, and everything behind it coming loose at once.

The camp went still. She felt them not-looking at her, all four of them, and then she felt Sefa's arms around her shoulders, and then Bo's hand on the back of her head, rough and gentle, and Priya's forehead against her shoulder, and it was a knot of them, the whole ridiculous family of them, holding her up the way a hull holds — not by any one plank but by all of them refusing to let go.

"He knew," Wendell said, when she could. "He knew it wasn't Odile. He knew about Halloran. He wrote it down and he hid it in the ice and he waited."

"Three years," Priya said quietly.

"He waited for us." Wendell wiped her face with the back of her glove, an ungraceful motion, and found she didn't care. "He stockpiled food and fuel and parts, and he wrote a letter, and he put it all above the calving fields because he knew — he *knew* — that whoever finished this would have to come the hard way. He provisioned the truth. That's what the High Provision is. It was never a supply cache. It was him, waiting."

Across the fire, Odile had not moved. She sat at the edge of the light with her hands open on her knees, and her face in the firelight was stripped of everything Wendell had spent three years hating — the polish, the control, the smoothness. What was left looked like a woman who had been holding a weight so long she'd forgotten her arms were allowed to bend.

"Halloran," Odile said. Just the name. She said it like a man spitting out a tooth.

"You knew," Wendell said. Not an accusation. An inventory.

"I knew he threatened me. I didn't know he was the one who set the charge — I suspected, God help me, I suspected for three years and made myself not finish the thought." Odile's voice was low and even, and it cost her; Wendell could see it costing her. "Tom's right. It was silence, not treason. But silence has a body count too, and I've been counting." She looked at the letter in Wendell's hands. "What does he say about me?"

"He says you went quiet under threat. He says there's a difference." Wendell held her gaze. "He says it will feel like a difference eventually."

Odile nodded once, slowly, like a woman accepting a sentence. "He was always the generous one."

"He was always the honest one," Wendell said. "There's a difference too."

The fire popped. Bo cleared his throat — the engineer's prelude, Wendell knew, to something practical, which was how Bo loved people, through the practical.

"So Halloran's at Reprieve," Bo said. "On our route home. With a harbor office, a constable or two he pays for, and three years of knowing exactly which boat to watch for." He looked around the fire. "And the calving season's come early. We don't have the window to go around. There is no around. Reprieve is the only deep-water dock on the middle river."

"Then we dock at Reprieve," Wendell said.

They all looked at her.

She stood, and her legs held, and the letter went inside her coat, against her chest, over her heart, which was where ballast goes. She felt different and she named it honestly to herself: not healed. Loaded. There was a difference, and she could feel the difference in the way the boat of her sat in the water — lower in the stern, but truer to her line, steering cleaner than she had in three years.

"Tom left us the truth, the evidence, and a full set of stores," she said. "He left us a job. We're going to run it home through the ice, we're going to dock at the one harbor on this river run by the man who killed him, and we're going to put that evidence in front of every soul in Reprieve — because Halloran's poison is in *their* water too, and I'd bet the Coriander herself they don't know it." She looked around the fire, at each of them. "He'll try to burn it. He'll try to take the boat. So we don't give him the chance to do it quietly. We do it loud, on his own dock, in front of his own town."

Sefa was smiling the way she smiled before a feast — the dangerous way. Priya already had her notebook out, already listing. Bo was looking at the stove with an expression that said *pulls to port, hull's the culprit, he was right, I owe a dead man an apology.*

And far up the valley, the glacier spoke again — longer this time, a rolling series of reports, and beneath them, faint but unmistakable, a new sound: the river changing its voice. Filling. Rising. The melt coming down early and all at once.

Priya looked up from her notebook, listening. "Wendell. That's not calving. That's the outlet dam going."

Wendell turned north, toward the sound, and felt the miles ahead of her the way she felt weather.

The window wasn't shrinking anymore.

It was closing.
