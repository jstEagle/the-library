## 30. Calving

The first boom came at dawn, and Wendell knew before she opened her eyes that the season had turned against them.

She lay in her bunk aboard the *Coriander*, moored in the shallows below the moraine, and counted. One. Two. A third detonation, deeper, rolling up the valley like a door slamming shut in a house the size of the sky. The intervals between them were shortening. She'd spent enough years on high water to know what that meant: the glacier was shedding its skin, and it would not wait for anyone's schedule, least of all theirs.

On deck, the cold had teeth. Bo was already up, standing at the rail with a tin mug, watching the ice field upstream where the morning light caught the face of the calving wall — a sheer blue rampart two miles long and three hundred feet high, veined white with compression, groaning like a ship breaking up.

"Early," Bo said, when Wendell joined him.

"Three weeks early." She took the mug from his hands without asking. It was terrible coffee, burnt twice over, Sefa's revenge on anyone who woke before she did. "The window's not six days anymore. Maybe three. Maybe less."

Bo said nothing for a while. The glacier spoke instead — a long, tearing shriek somewhere behind the wall, ice moving against ice under pressures no human name could hold.

"The cache," he said finally. "It's a mile and a half up the moraine, then out onto the shelf. Tom marked it on the chart."

"I know where my brother put it."

"I wasn't asking where. I was asking if you're sure."

Wendell watched a chunk of ice the size of a barn shear off the wall and fall. The splash reached them eleven seconds later, a flat white slap across the water, followed by a wave that rocked the *Coriander* on her mooring lines even at this distance. Somewhere on the far shore, birds lifted in a single startled sheet.

"No," she said. "But we're going anyway."

* * *

They offloaded the sledges by full light. Six of them on the ice field now — Wendell, Bo, Priya, Sefa, Ansel, Voss with his wrist splinted and strapped tight against his chest — hauling crates of rope, pry bars, shovels, and the empty sledge frames they'd need to bring the vault's contents back down. Odile stayed with the boat. Nobody had asked her to come; nobody had asked her not to. She stood at the rail as they loaded, arms folded, watching the calving wall with an expression Wendell couldn't read and didn't try to.

The moraine was a nightmare of loose stone, gray rubble left behind by centuries of retreating ice, unstable as a scree slope and twice as treacherous. They roped together in pairs, Bo and Priya in front, Wendell and Ansel behind, Sefa and Voss bringing up the rear with the heavy loads. The glacier boomed every few minutes now, each report closer than the last, and between the booms there was a sound underneath everything — a low, continuous grinding, like millstones turning, like the mountain clearing its throat.

"Talk to me," Priya called back over her shoulder. She'd taken point without being asked; she had the best eye for footing, an archivist's patience applied to stone. "What are we listening for?"

"A change in pitch," Wendell said. "The deep booms are the glacier settling. What kills you is the sharp ones — those are seracs letting go. If you hear something that sounds like a rifle shot, don't look. Run downhill, perpendicular to the fall line."

"And if it falls on us?"

"Then it was quick," said Sefa, cheerfully grim, "and I won't have to hear any more of Bo's jokes."

Ansel laughed — a real laugh, surprised out of her — and for half a minute the fear loosened its grip. Then the glacier fired again, a crack like the world snapping a bone, and a plume of snow-dust rose off the eastern flank four hundred yards ahead of them, and nobody laughed after that.

They gained the shoulder of the moraine by mid-morning. From there they could see the ice shelf proper — a broad plain of glacier surface, crevassed in blue-black seams, rising gently toward the base of the calving wall. Tom's chart marked the cache site with a small inked cross, two-thirds of the way out, tucked behind a ridge of pressure ice that had, three years ago, sheltered it from view.

Three years ago. Wendell stared at the plain and understood, with a lurch, how much of it had changed. The pressure ridge was still there, but the ice around it had flowed and fractured; new crevasses stitched the surface like black surgery. The cross might be fifty feet from where Tom had dug. It might be two hundred. It might be hanging in open air over a void.

"It'll have moved," she said aloud. "The ice flows. We find it by the markers, not the map."

"What markers?" Bo asked.

"You'll know them when you see them. He was thorough."

* * *

The ice field took them an hour to cross what should have taken twenty minutes. Every step was a negotiation: probing with poles, testing bridges, roping wider apart so no single collapse could take more than two of them. The sun climbed and did nothing about the cold; the air off the glacier came down the valley at a temperature that made breath ache. Twice Priya went in to her hip on thin snow-bridges and was hauled out swearing, soaked to the waist, refusing offers of dry clothes because stopping meant freezing.

It was Voss who found the first marker.

He'd been quiet all morning — quieter than usual, his broken wrist cradled, his eyes doing the work his hands couldn't. Now he stopped dead and pointed with his chin. "There."

A steel rod, driven into the ice at an angle, its top wrapped in faded orange survey tape. And beside it, half melted into the surface, a length of timber with letters burned into it. Wendell knelt and brushed the rime away with her glove.

**T.L. — 40 paces, bearing 210. Trust the rod, not your eyes.**

Her throat closed. She read it twice. *Trust the rod, not your eyes.* Because he'd known — he'd known even then, three years ago, standing on this same ice with whatever he already suspected breathing down his neck — that whoever came looking might be coming in a hurry, or in the dark, or in grief. That they'd need instructions simple enough to follow with shaking hands.

"He wrote this for us," she said. Her voice came out strange. "Before any of this. He wrote it for whoever finished it."

Sefa put a hand briefly on her shoulder, then let go. "Forty paces," Sefa said. "Bearing two-one-zero. Let's not keep him waiting."

* * *

They found the second rod at thirty-eight paces, driven deep beside a crevasse that hadn't existed when Tom planted it — the bridge across it was fresh snow, maybe eight inches thick, sagging visibly under its own weight. The route bent around the gap through a maze of pressure ridges, blue walls of shattered ice taller than a man, and it was in the shadow of one of these that Priya stopped and held up her fist.

Everyone froze. The glacier boomed. Dust sifted down the face of the ridge above them.

"Priya?" Bo whispered.

She didn't answer. She was staring at the snow underfoot, at a line in it — a hairline fracture, running twenty feet, crossing their path exactly where they needed to cross. As they watched, it widened by the width of a fingernail. Then another inch. With a sound like a zipper opening in God's pocket, the whole bridge dropped away into darkness, leaving a slot they could have lowered a truck into.

Nobody moved for a long moment. The wind filled the silence with its thin whistle.

"Okay," Priya said, very calmly. "New route."

They swung wide, north along the ridge, losing forty minutes to detour and probe. The sun was well past noon when Wendell finally saw it: a third rod, and beyond it, set back against the calving wall itself, a mound of ice darker than the surrounding surface — old ice, refrozen over the dig site, shaded by the wall's overhang. The cache. Fifty yards away. Close enough to touch.

That was when the mountain decided otherwise.

* * *

Later, none of them would agree on the order of events. There was a boom — not the deep settling kind but the sharp rifle-crack Wendell had warned them about — and it came from directly overhead, from the serac field clinging to the flank of the calving wall, and every instinct in every body on that ice said the same thing at once.

"DOWN AND OUT!" Wendell screamed. "RUN! PERPENDICULAR!"

They ran. The ice field tilted into chaos — five people and a sixth hauling a sledge, sprinting across ground that jumped and bucked under their boots like a deck in a storm. Behind them, above them, a section of the serac ridge the size of a cathedral let go. The sound was not a sound; it was a presence, a physical weight of noise that pressed their ears flat and shook marrow in their bones. Tons upon tons upon tons of blue ice fell three hundred feet, hit the glacier surface, and exploded.

The blast wave caught them like a hand. Wendell felt herself lifted, airborne for one impossible half-second, then slammed down and sliding. Snow and ice-shards hissed past like shrapnel. She rolled, came up on her elbows, blinked through a world gone white.

"COUNT!" she bellowed into the murk. "SOUND OFF!"

"Bo!"

"Priya!"

"Sefa!"

"Ansel!"

A pause. Long enough to stop her heart.

"Voss." His voice, ragged, from somewhere to her left. "I'm — I'm hit. Don't —"

She was moving before the sentence ended, crawling then running blind through the settling cloud, following his voice. She found him at the edge of the debris field, on his knees, his right leg twisted under him at an angle legs didn't go, and beneath him — shielded under his body, wrapped in his jacket, pressed into the snow — the document case. The manifest, the ledger photographs, the recovered log pages, everything they'd gathered since Reprieve. Everything that mattered. He'd had it slung across his chest when the serac came down, and when the wave hit, he'd folded over it like a man covering a child.

The case was intact. Voss was not.

* * *

The shard had taken him high on the right thigh — a dagger of clear ice, thrown fifty yards by the impact, buried to the depth of a hand. Not arterial; Sefa checked twice, hands steady, face gray. But deep, and jagged, and the leg was broken beneath it, and they were a mile and a half from the boat across terrain that was actively trying to kill them, and the window home was closing with every boom.

"We can't carry him and haul the cache load," Bo said quietly, when Sefa had Voss stabilized on a sledge, the wound packed, the leg splinted with a pry bar and strapping tape. "Not both. Not fast enough."

"Then we don't slow down," Voss said. His face was the color of candle wax, sweat freezing at his temples, but his voice was level. "Leave me here with supplies. Come back for me."

"Absolutely not," said Ansel.

"It's arithmetic—"

"It's *not*—" She stopped. Breathed. When she spoke again her voice was low and fierce. "I spent nine years learning what happens when people do arithmetic with other people. We're not doing that. Not today."

Wendell looked at the cache mound, fifty yards away. At Voss, gray and shivering. At the calving wall, which had not finished — the booms were coming faster now, one every few minutes, and the whole face of the glacier seemed to be leaning forward, gathering itself.

"Both," she said. "We dig fast, we load fast, we get him on a sledge, and we run the whole thing down together. Bo, Sefa — shovels. Priya, Ansel — ropes and pry bars, we may have to cut down to the hatch. I want that vault open in twenty minutes."

"And if the wall goes again?" Sefa asked.

"Then we'll be digging faster."

* * *

Digging into old glacier ice is not digging into snow. It is mining. The refrozen cap over the cache site was three feet of blue glass, hard as iron, and their shovels bounced off it until Bo thought to use the pry bars as chisels and swing them like pile drivers. Chips flew. Sweat froze in their collars. The booms kept coming, and each time the mountain spoke, everyone flinched and nobody stopped.

Ten minutes in, the shovel struck wood.

"Here!" Priya dropped to her knees, clawing chips away with bare hands. A corner of timber emerged — planking, tarred, weathered silver. Then another corner. A hatch cover, two feet by three, set flush into a frame Tom had built into the ice itself, with a hasp and a padlock furred white with frost.

The padlock. Everyone looked at it. Everyone looked at Wendell.

She'd carried the key for three years without knowing what it opened. Tom had given it to her the week before the last voyage — hung it on a cord around her neck, laughing, saying *every captain should have one key she doesn't know the door for.* She'd worn it ever since, moved it from cord to cord, boat to boat, a superstition, a relic, a joke between her and a dead man.

Her fingers shook so badly it took three tries. The lock clicked open like a held breath released.

Under the hatch: a wooden-lined vault, chest-deep, and inside it — crates. Sealed tins. Wrapped bundles in oilcloth. A rack of tools. Jerricans of fuel, still sound. And on top of everything, weighted down with a stone, a note in Tom's blunt handwriting:

**FOR THE CREW WHO FINISHES THIS.**

Wendell read it and had to turn away for a moment, one hand braced on the ice, because the glacier was booming and her brother's handwriting was steady in front of her and the two facts refused to fit inside the same skull.

"Wendell." Priya's voice, urgent. "Look at the route behind us."

Wendell turned. Downhill, toward the moraine, toward the boat — the ice field they'd crossed that morning was changing color. The crevasse field near the shelf's edge had begun to flood; meltwater, released by the day's violence, was pooling and spreading, and beyond it, at the calving wall's base, the water of the river head was filling with brash. Small floes, then larger ones, jostling and grinding, packing together as the calving accelerated. The channel they'd motored up yesterday was choking. Even now, even this hour, she could see it closing — the open lead narrowing like a pupil contracting against light.

"How long?" Ansel asked.

Wendell judged it the way she judged everything, in miles and current and the mood of water. "If we're loaded and moving within the hour, we run ahead of the pack. If we're not—" She didn't finish. She didn't have to.

They worked. Oilcloth bundles came up hand over hand — documents, hundreds of them, Tom's complete evidence, dry and dark and safe after three years in the cold. Crates of core samples, labeled in his careful block capitals. The fuel and food and spare parts went onto the sledges too, because Tom had provisioned for exactly this, for a crew in a hurry with no time to resupply, and Wendell be damned if she'd leave her brother's foresight sitting in a hole in the ice.

Voss directed operations from his sledge, one-handed, hoarse, refusing morphine because *I want to remember this part.* Ansel packed the document cases in padded crates at the center of the load, cushioned like eggs. Sefa broke open one of the tins — Tom had stocked tea, of course he had, and hardtack, and a jar of honey gone cloudy with age — and pressed a cup of hot sweet tea into Voss's good hand without a word.

Twenty minutes became forty. Forty became an hour. The wall leaned. The booms ran together into a continuous artillery, and the brash ice below advanced up the channel like a tide with intent.

When the last crate was lashed and the hatch closed and the rods left standing as Tom had left them, Wendell stood a moment at the lip of the vault with her palm flat on the timber.

"We've got it," she told him. "We're taking it home."

The glacier answered with a crack that split the morning, and a section of the calving wall the size of a village slid into the river, and the wave it raised came up the ice field toward them like a white wall with a horizon.

"RUN!" Wendell roared. "ALL LINES! *MOVE!*"

Six people and three loaded sledges flew downhill across a breaking world, racing the flood, racing the pack ice, racing the closing window — and below them, at the edge of the white water, the *Coriander* sat moored and small and waiting, with Odile Marchetti standing at her rail, and the last open channel upstream of her narrowing to the width of a coffin lid.
