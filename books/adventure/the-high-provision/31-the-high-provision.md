## 31. The High Provision

The vault door was not a door. That was the first thing Wendell understood, standing in the blue shadow of the ice cliff with her breath smoking and her hands still shaking from the dig. It was a hatch — ship's steel, salt-corroded at the seams, salvaged from some wrecked hull decades ago and set into the moraine like a tooth in a jaw. Someone had welded it shut from the inside.

Tom had welded it shut from the inside.

She knew his welds the way she knew his handwriting. The beads were too even, laid with a patience he'd learned from their father, each pass overlapping the last by exactly half its width. He'd been in no hurry. He'd known whoever came for this would come with time to spare or not at all.

"Captain." Bo crouched beside the cutting rig, torch unlit, looking up at her. Behind him the glacier spoke in its sleep — a long groan rolling through the ice field like a whale sounding under a hull. Everyone flinched except Voss, who had gone very still in the way of a man who had learned what that sound meant two days ago, when it had nearly taken Priya off the moraine.

"Cut it," Wendell said.

The torch lit with a hiss. Sparks sprayed orange against the blue ice and the crew turned their faces away behind smoked lenses, and Wendell watched the seam glow cherry-red and thought: *three years*. Three years of miles, of schedules and sandbars, of grief kept in a lockbox and memory kept nowhere at all, and all of it had been leading here, to a steel door in a dying glacier, welded by a dead man's careful hand.

The hinge side gave first. Bo worked the torch down the last seam and Priya and Ansel put their shoulders to the hatch, and it swung inward on shrieking metal with a breath of air that had been sealed since before Tom died — cold, mineral, utterly dry. The kind of air that keeps things.

Wendell went in first. Nobody argued about that. Not even Odile, who stood apart from the others with her arms crossed against the cold and her face arranged into nothing at all.

* * *

Inside, the vault was bigger than the hole they'd dug suggested — the ice had been cut back into a chamber, shored with timbers and sheeted in salvage tin, maybe four meters square and head-high. A lantern hung from a ridge beam, glass clean, wick trimmed. Beside it, a storm match in a waterproof tube and a card in Tom's blocky capitals:

**LIGHT FIRST. THEN LOOK.**

Wendell laughed. It came out of her like something breaking loose, half sob, and Sefa's hand found her shoulder from behind and stayed there.

She struck the match. The lantern took with a soft yellow bloom, and the High Provision assembled itself out of the dark around them.

Crates, first. Neat rows of them, stenciled in Tom's hand: FLOUR. RICE. SALT PORK. TEA. LAMP OIL. A whole wall of food, packed in waxed paper and tin, dated and rotated — he had stocked it like a man provisioning a voyage, because that was exactly what it was. A voyage he wouldn't take but someone else would have to.

Along the far wall, tools. Spare parts wrapped in oilcloth and labeled in the same capitals: BEARING STOCK. PACKING GLANDS. COPPER PIPE. A full gasket kit. Two coils of new rope. A medicine chest with a red cross stenciled on the lid, latches brassed and bright. Bo knelt by the parts without a word and lifted an oilcloth corner, and Wendell heard him exhale — the sound of an engineer finding exactly the thing he'd been afraid wouldn't be there.

And against the back wall, under a canvas tarp weighted with river stones, the evidence.

Priya pulled the tarp. Beneath it, in waterproof document tubes and oilskin folders, lay everything Tom had gathered in those final weeks upstream: core samples in sealed glass, dozens of them, labeled by depth and location; assay reports in three different hands; company correspondence with letterheads Wendell recognized from the crates they'd hauled upriver; photographs, hundreds of them, the upper river in every season, the tailings ponds spreading year over year like stains on a shirt; and ledgers — payments, dates, signatures. She saw the harbormaster's name. She saw the word *settlement*. She saw, on one page, a figure next to the initials *O.M.* and felt the floor of the world tilt, gently, the way a boat tilts when a passenger shifts their weight without meaning to.

Odile didn't look away. Wendell gave her credit for that much. But she said nothing either, and there was nothing left to say tonight that hadn't been said on the ice.

"Captain," Priya said quietly. She was holding one of the folders open, and her archivist's hands were trembling. "This is complete. This isn't fragments. This is the whole case — chain of custody, sample provenance, internal memos. He built it to survive scrutiny."

"He built it to survive anything," Wendell said.

Because that was the thing she was only now understanding, standing in the yellow light with her brother's handwriting on every surface. Tom hadn't hidden this cache. Hiding was what you did when you were afraid. Tom had *provisioned* it. He'd stocked it the way you stock a boat for a hard run — assuming weather, assuming damage, assuming the people coming after him would arrive tired and short-handed and needing everything. The food wasn't a hoard. The parts weren't a stash. It was all of it addressed forward, outward, to strangers. To them.

Ansel found the note. It was pinned to the medicine chest with a rusted tack, a single page in a wax sleeve, and she read it once and then held it out to Wendell without a word, her eyes wet.

Wendell took it. Tom's capitals, steady as a level bubble:

**FOR THE CREW WHO FINISHES THIS.**

That was all. No name. No date. No instructions beyond the labels on the crates and the card by the match. He hadn't known who would come. He'd only known someone would have to, eventually, and that they would need feeding, and fixing, and light, and each other. He had provisioned not just the evidence but the people who would carry it.

Wendell sat down on a crate of flour because her legs informed her they were done negotiating. The lantern light moved on the tin walls. Somewhere above them the glacier calved — a crack like the sky splitting, then the long thunder of ice falling into water — and nobody in the vault so much as looked up. They were past being startled by it. Or they were holding still together, which amounted to the same thing.

"Three years," she said, mostly to herself. "I thought he left me nothing but the accident."

"You thought wrong," Sefa said. Gentle, but firm, the way she said everything that mattered. "He left you a ship's stores and a crew's work. He just made us wait for the manifest."

* * *

They worked through what remained of the daylight, because work was the only honest answer to a room like this. Bo inventoried the parts aloud, ticking items against the Coriander's known ailments with growing disbelief — Tom had stockpiled for *their specific boat*, or close enough to it that Bo kept muttering numbers under his breath like prayers. Priya catalogued documents by lamplight, sealing them into the tubes they'd carry downriver, building duplicates where the cache allowed. Ansel and Voss repacked the food into sledge loads, weighing each one, arguing softly about grams. Sefa opened a tin of Tom's tea and brewed it on the little stove they'd brought up, and the smell filled the vault — black tea, river-strong, sweetened with the condensed milk Tom had always taken and Wendell never did.

She drank it anyway. It tasted like being nine years old, sitting on a dock with her feet in the current while Tom explained how eddies worked.

Voss found the last thing. He'd been quiet all evening — quieter than usual, his splinted arm cradled against his chest, doing one-handed work with a diligence that had stopped surprising everyone sometime during the climb. Now he stopped, crouched by the back corner, and said, "Captain. You should see this."

It was a second compartment, low in the timbered wall — a strongbox, really, bolted through the timbers, unlocked. Inside: a lantern of the old pattern, the kind Tom had carried on night runs, its glass smoked on one side from years of use. Wendell knew it on sight. She'd watched it sink. She had *seen* it go down into the black water, seen the light shrink to a coin, a spark, a nothing —

But this was it. Dented, yes. Water-marked, yes. But here. Dry. Kept.

Her memory of that night shifted again, the way it had been shifting for weeks now, plates of the truth grinding into new alignment. She hadn't seen it sink. She'd seen it fall. And someone had gone in after it. Someone had climbed out of freezing water with a drowned lantern and carried it up a moraine and set it here, in a vault built for the future, because it belonged to the story.

Tom had gone in after his own lantern. Or — she turned the dented base toward the light and saw the initials scratched there, and they weren't Tom's.

They were hers. *W.L.* It was her lantern. Her father had bought them matching ones the summer they got their first boat, and hers had gone into the river that night, and Tom had gone into the river after it, after *her* lantern, and had climbed out with it and carried it here and kept it burning-ready for three years in the dark.

She held it in both hands and could not speak. Sefa took the kettle off the flame. Bo stopped counting. Even the glacier seemed to hold its breath between groans.

"He went back for it," Wendell finally managed. "For mine."

"He went back for you," Ansel said softly. "The lantern was just what he could carry out."

Nobody corrected her. Nobody needed to. Wendell sat with the lantern in her lap and let the weight of it say what it said: that love is a salvage operation, that you go into the cold water for whatever piece of a person you can bring back, and you keep it dry, and you keep it lit, and you leave it where they'll find it.

* * *

They camped inside the vault that night, all six of them, because outside the temperature was falling and the ice field was talking to itself in a language none of them trusted. Sefa cooked Tom's rice and salt pork and nobody complained about repetition. Priya read entries from the log aloud by lantern light — not the evidence, just the small things, the day-to-day: *wind north, good water, caught grayling below the bend, W. complains I make the coffee weak (I do not)*. Laughter moved through the vault, ragged at first, then real. Grief needs somewhere to live, Sefa had told her once, secrets rot faster in close quarters. Tonight the vault held both, and neither rotted. They were preserved, like everything else in this cold clean air, for carrying.

Wendell took the last watch, because it was her boat and her brother's vault and some watches you don't delegate. She sat by the hatch with her brother's — *her* — lantern lit beside her, and the crew slept in a heap of bedrolls and sledge tarps, breathing in the rhythm of people who have decided to trust each other with their unconscious bodies.

Near dawn, Odile woke. She didn't pretend otherwise; she sat up, met Wendell's eyes across the vault, and held them. Then she rose, crossed the floor quietly, and stood looking at the wall of evidence tubes, at the neat capital letters, at the provisions stacked for strangers.

"He asked me once," Odile said, very low, so as not to wake the others, "what I'd do if it ever came down to the money or the river. I told him the river, obviously. We both laughed." She touched one of the tubes without picking it up. "He provisioned for the version of me that kept my word. That's the part I can't get past, Captain. He packed food for the woman I said I was."

Wendell looked at her a long moment. Three days ago — a lifetime ago, before the calving, before the vault — she might have said something cruel. She found she didn't have it in her. What she had instead was heavier.

"He provisioned for all of us," Wendell said. "Including the versions we turned out to be. That's what provision means. It doesn't check your references."

Odile's face did something complicated and then settled into something simple, which was worse. She nodded once and went back to her bedroll, and did not sleep, and Wendell did not offer her the comfort of pretending otherwise.

* * *

At first light they loaded. It went fast, six people who had learned each other's rhythms on a moraine and a river, sledges packed and roped and balanced in under two hours. The evidence tubes rode center, wrapped, double-secured, Voss strapped beside them like a guard who had switched sides and knew it. The food and parts they'd take they distributed; the rest they resealed, because Tom had built this place to wait, and waiting was what it did best.

Last of all, Wendell stood at the hatch with the lantern.

She'd meant to leave it. It belonged here, with the rest of him, keeping its vigil in the cold. But her hands wouldn't set it down. It was hers — her father's gift, her brother's salvage, the one piece of that night that had come back out of the water. Some things you carry home. Some things *carry* you.

She trimmed the wick, closed the glass, and hung it from her hand.

"Bo," she said. "Re-seal the hatch. Leave it sound. Someone should be able to shelter here someday."

"Aye, Captain."

"And Bo —" She looked back once, at the tin walls and the stacked crates and the capitals on every surface, at the whole patient architecture of a dead man's faith in the future. "Log it in the ship's book. The High Provision, surveyed and drawn upon. Cargo received in good order."

Bo wrote it down. His voice, when he read it back, was not entirely steady: "*Received in good order.*"

They started down the moraine with the sun coming up the valley behind them and the glacier booming its artillery to the south, and Wendell walked at the head of the rope line with a lit lantern swinging from her fist in broad daylight, because she could, because it was hers, because Tom had carried it out of the water for her and she would carry it the rest of the way home.

Below, far down the valley, the Thessaly flashed white and fast, already thickening at the banks. The window home was closing. She could see the ice moving on it from here — brash and pans riding the current down from the calving fields, the river beginning to choke itself for winter.

Two hundred miles of that, running downhill, in a damaged boat, carrying the truth.

Wendell checked the rope line, raised her hand, and gave the signal to move.
