## 32. Odile's Choice

The vault door stood open, and Odile Marchetti had spent thirty years learning to read rooms. This one told her everything.

Wendell Lockridge knelt in the blue light of the emergency lanterns with her brother's evidence spread across a crate lid like a surgeon's tray — core sample tubes in their foam cradles, water assays in plastic sleeves, a sheaf of internal memoranda fanned out with the casual carelessness of someone who had not yet understood what she was holding. Priya was photographing pages by lamplight, methodical as an archivist, because that was what she had been before the river took her. Bo stood at the vault mouth with his arms crossed, keeping watch on the ice field outside, where the glacier muttered and cracked like a house settling in winter. Sefa had already opened the provision stores Tom Lockridge had stockpiled for them — for *them*, Odile thought, for whoever came next — and was quietly inventorying food that had waited three years in the cold to be eaten.

And Voss sat against the wall with his arm bound and splinted, gray-faced from the blood he'd lost shielding those documents when the serac came down, watching Wendell with the expression of a man who had finally seen the shape of the thing he'd been hired to bury.

Nobody had said the word yet. *Broadcast.* Transmit. Send it downriver, down the mountain, out into the world where it could not be taken back.

But it hung in the vault air like frost, waiting to form.

Odile stood apart, near the far wall, where she had stood for most of her life — close enough to hear, far enough to deny. The bargain she had made sat in her chest like swallowed stone. Fourteen months ago, in a hotel bar in Meridian City, a man named Corliss Havel — who signed his letters with a single initial and paid his debts through intermediaries, through harbormasters, through women who used to be brave — had slid a folder across the table to her and said, *You have until the season turns.* Inside the folder: photographs of the Coriander's hull registration. A copy of the accident report that named no one and blamed ice. And a page of financial instruments showing exactly how much of Odile Marchetti's salvage company he owned, how much of her debt he held, how thoroughly she had been purchased without ever quite signing anything.

*Say nothing about the cache,* the folder said, in its way. *Let the samples rot in the ice. Let the river keep its color.*

And she had said nothing. For fourteen months she had said nothing, and the price of her silence had been this expedition — because Havel wanted the cache verified and scoured, and he wanted it done by someone whose name would look plausible on a permit application, and there was only one name left that carried weight on the Thessaly.

She had told herself it was penance, hiring Wendell. She had almost believed it. That was the trick of herself she knew best: Odile Marchetti could make any cowardice wear the costume of strategy, any betrayal dress itself as necessity. She had done it the night of the accident too — standing on the ice shelf above the black water, hearing Tom Lockridge shout, hearing the charge go off under the shelf lip, saying nothing, doing nothing, because moving meant admitting she knew, and admitting she knew meant losing everything Havel held over her.

Tom had died in ninety seconds of black water. Her silence had cost her fourteen months of comfortable lies.

It was, she reflected, watching Wendell's hands move across her dead brother's work, a very bad exchange rate.

* * *

"Wendell." Priya's voice, low, careful. "You need to see the assay dates."

Wendell leaned over the pages. Odile watched her read — watched the stillness come into her the way it did at the wheel, that total absorption, the river-pilot's focus turned on paper instead of current. Wendell's finger stopped on a line. Moved back. Stopped again.

"These are dated eleven days before the accident," Wendell said.

"Eleven days before," Priya agreed. "He found the cache early. He tested the samples himself. He knew what was in the water upstream of every settlement on the lower Thessaly, and he documented it, and then—" She didn't finish. She didn't have to. Then he died. Then it all went back into the ice for three years while the company's lawyers filed their quiet reports and the towns downstream drank what the mines left behind.

"Ansel's valley," Bo said from the doorway, not turning around. "That's what this is. This is why her sister's village went bad."

"It's more than that." Ansel spoke from her corner, where she had been sitting very still since giving up the real manifest two days ago. Her voice had lost its cheer somewhere above the calving fields and hadn't found it again. "Look at the memo headers. These are internal acknowledgments. They knew. They ran the numbers on settlement claims versus remediation costs and remediation lost." She laughed, a small broken sound. "They costed my sister's water. There's a column for it."

The lantern light shivered. Outside, the glacier boomed — a long, deep report rolling up the valley like distant artillery, and everyone in the vault flinched except Wendell, who had stopped flinching somewhere on the climb and now simply listened, head tilted, reading the ice the way she read water.

"The window's closing," Wendell said. "We dig the sledges out tonight, we're loaded by dawn, we run tomorrow. That part doesn't change." She looked up, and her eyes found Odile across the vault with the unerring accuracy of a woman who had spent three years refusing to look at her and was done refusing. "But this goes with us. All of it. Copies in three places, originals in the welded compartment. Nobody burns it, nobody loses it, nobody trades it. Are we understood?"

Nods all around. Voss said, "Understood," in a voice like gravel.

"And Odile." Wendell rose. She wasn't tall; she had never been tall; but she filled the vault the way deep water fills a channel. "You've been quiet. Quieter than usual, which I'd have said was impossible."

"I'm admiring your crew," Odile said.

"Don't." One word, flat as a gravel bar. "Don't admire them. Don't study them. Whatever you're calculating over there, say it out loud or put it away."

And there it was — the moment, arriving on schedule, the way rapids arrive once you commit to the run. Odile had known it was coming since the vault door swung open. She had rehearsed nothing, because she had learned long ago that rehearsed things came out sounding rehearsed, and if she was going to do this she was going to do it true.

"Corliss Havel owns me," she said.

The vault went still in a new way. Even the glacier seemed to hold its breath between booms.

"Not legally — he's too careful for legal. He holds my paper. My debt, my permits, my insurance bonds, my reputation, which is the same thing in this business. Fourteen months ago he gave me a choice: help verify the cache and see it destroyed quietly, or lose everything I've built in thirty years on this river." She heard her own voice and hated its steadiness. "I said yes. I recruited Voss and Ansel — Ansel under false pretenses, which she has already told you, and which I will answer for. I hired you because you were the best pilot on the Thessaly and because your name would make the permits unimpeachable. And I told myself it was the only play available, and then I told myself it was penance, and both of those were lies I told so I could sleep."

Sefa had stopped inventorying. Bo had turned from the doorway. Priya's camera hung forgotten in her hand.

"You knew," Wendell said. Not a question. "When you came to my dock. You knew what killed him."

"I knew who paid for it to be possible." Odile made herself hold the dark eyes. "I don't know — I have never known — whether Havel intended Tom's death or merely accepted it as a cost. There's a column for that too. I've seen his ledgers. People are line items, Wendell. Your brother was a line item. So am I. So were you, the moment I shook your hand on that dock."

Bo moved — one step, fists half-closing — and Sefa's hand landed on his forearm, light and absolute, and he stayed.

"Why tell us now?" Priya asked. Always the question beneath the question, always the archivist wanting provenance. "Why not stay bought? We're two days from Reprieve. You could still—"

"Because I'm looking at what silence costs." Odile's voice finally cracked, and she let it. "Fourteen months ago it cost me nothing I could see. Tonight I'm standing in a room full of people who chose each other over safety at a town called Reprieve, who hauled this boat over two miles of moraine on their backs, who voted — *voted*, all six of them, even my own hirelings — to carry a dead man's truth home through closing ice. And I am the only person in this vault who took money to make sure none of you ever found it." She looked at Wendell. "You asked me once, at the fireline, whether I thought you'd agreed to something that night. Whether you were complicit. I let you believe the doubt was reasonable. It wasn't. It was cover. If you were guilty too, then my guilt was ordinary."

Wendell's face did something terrible and small and then put itself away, the way pilots put weather away.

"So what's the play, Marchetti?" Bo said roughly. "Confession and a hug? Because we've got a river to run."

"No hug." Odile reached into her coat — saw Bo tense, saw Wendell's hand drop toward the flare gun at her hip — and drew out the satellite transmitter unit she'd carried since Meridian City, the one Havel's people had issued her for "survey coordination," the one with a burst-data mode nobody had bothered to explain because they assumed she'd never use it against them. She set it on the crate lid beside Tom's evidence. "The cache has an emergency beacon. Tom installed it — it's in his notes, page forty. Forty-watt burst transmission, relay off the Reprieve tower, widecast to every registry office, news desk, and regulatory inbox on the coast network. Once it sends, it can't be recalled. Once it sends, Havel knows within the hour, and everything he holds over me becomes worthless and everything he's done becomes public, and I go to prison for conspiracy, obstruction, and whatever else the prosecutors can stack." She smiled, and it felt like a wound opening. "I've priced it out. I'm very good at pricing things. It's the most expensive thing I'll ever buy."

"And you want our permission," Wendell said.

"I don't want anything from you. I want you to know that if I transmit, I do it knowing what it costs, and I pay it, and I don't ask anyone to protect me from the bill." She looked around the vault — at the engineer, the cook, the archivist, the defector, the wounded company man who had shielded documents with his body for reasons he probably hadn't finished understanding himself. "But it's your brother's evidence, Wendell. Your family's risk. If any one of you says send it downriver instead, to be released safe and slow through lawyers, I'll stand down. It should be your call. That's the whole point of what I'm doing. It should have been everyone's call from the beginning."

* * *

They argued for forty minutes, and Odile stood aside and listened, and it was the strangest forty minutes of her life, because nobody argued about her.

They argued about timing — whether transmitting from the cache, exposed on the ice field with the calving season starting early, invited retaliation before they could run. They argued about redundancy — Priya insisting on physical copies in three separate locations regardless of the beacon, Bo rigging a second antenna from spare wire, Sefa pointing out with cook's pragmatism that hungry people make sloppy decisions and feeding everyone first. They argued about Voss, who asked quietly to add a sworn statement of his own to the transmission, dictating while Priya typed, naming names and dates and payment routes with the flat precision of a man unburdening himself of ballast. Ansel dictated hers too, and when she came to her sister's name her voice held steady and her hands did not.

Through all of it, Wendell worked. She cross-referenced Tom's notes against the assay dates against the manifest, building the chain of custody that would make the archive unanswerable, and Odile watched her do it and understood, finally, the difference between them. Odile had spent thirty years managing information — hoarding it, trading it, pricing it. Wendell was simply carrying it, the way you carry cargo, with respect for its weight and no interest in its value. It had never occurred to Odile until this moment that honesty could be a kind of competence.

Near midnight, glacier-time, Wendell straightened up and rolled the last sleeve of documents closed.

"Send it," she said.

Odile blinked. "Just like that?"

"Not just like that." Wendell crossed the vault and stood close enough that Odile could see the exhaustion under her eyes and the iron under the exhaustion. "Priya's copies go in the sledge, the compartment, and Sefa's flour tin — don't look at me like that, nobody searches a flour tin. Voss and Ansel's statements go in the burst. And you—" she tapped the transmitter once, hard, "—you send it yourself, on your own authority, and then you get on my boat and you ride down that river with us, and at Reprieve you walk into whatever's coming with your name attached and your chin up. No deals. No intermediaries. No letters signed L. You bought your silence for fourteen months, Odile. Now you spend the rest of it loud."

"That's not mercy," Odile said.

"No," Wendell agreed. "It isn't."

* * *

The beacon housing sat in a rock cairn fifty meters from the vault, ice-sheathed, and Bo freed it with a heat gun and curses while the glacier grumbled overhead like a landlord. Odile cabled the transmitter, checked the burst queue twice, and paused with her thumb over the key.

Below her, down the dark valley, the Thessaly ran invisible and impatient, two hundred miles of closing water between here and home. Somewhere down there was a dock where a woman had once refused to shake her hand. Somewhere down there was a town whose harbor board had posted the words HIGH PROVISION CACHE in plain type, and whose people had watched six strangers choose to go anyway.

Somewhere down there, Corliss Havel was sleeping well, believing his line items were all in order.

Odile thought about the night on the ice shelf. Three years of thinking about it, and it always came back to the same ten seconds: the crack, the shout, the lantern light sinking, and herself standing motionless in the snow, calculating. Weighing. Pricing a man's life against her own comfort and finding the arithmetic acceptable.

Her thumb came down.

The transmitter chirped, spun up, and fired — a forty-watt shout into the frozen sky, carrying Tom Lockridge's three years of patience, Ansel's sister's name, Voss's sworn confession, and Odile Marchetti's signature on her own indictment, out toward the relay tower and the waking world. The indicator light burned green, then amber, then went dark. Sent. Irrevocable. Paid in full.

"Well," Bo said into the silence, shouldering the heat gun. "Now we run."

As if the glacier had been waiting for its cue, the whole icefield cracked open with a boom that Odile felt in her teeth — longer, deeper, closer than any before it — and downvalley, faint in the starlight, the river began to fill with the sound of falling ice.

Wendell was already moving.
