## 33. Running Home

The first thing the river did was take away our wake.

I noticed it from the helm before I understood it — the strange smoothness astern, the water closing over our passage like a mouth. Three days ago the Coriander had left a white seam behind her that you could read for half a mile. Now there was nothing. Just gray water knitting itself shut, and behind that, growing by the hour, a field of brash ice so dense it looked like land.

"Route's closed," Bo said beside me, not looking back either. Neither of us wanted to give it the dignity of a glance. "Officially closed."

"Then we're officially not on it," I said, and put the wheel over two degrees to starboard, where the current ran deepest against the cutbank.

We had come down off the moraine at dawn with the vault sealed in oilcloth and strapped into the hold like a passenger — which, in every way that mattered, it was. Tom rode with us now. The thought should have broken me open again, and for one bad moment on the climb down, when Priya slipped on blue ice and I caught her arm and felt how thin it was under all those layers, it nearly had. But grief is a poor helmsman. I stowed it below with everything else and kept my hands on the wheel and my ears on the river.

Because the river was talking, and what it was saying was: *hurry.*

* * *

You learn early that ice doesn't announce itself. It arrives the way bad news does — quietly, then all at once. The calving fields had been booming behind us since yesterday, reports of artillery from a war we were losing, each concussion rolling down the valley and slapping off the canyon walls until you couldn't tell direction anymore. Every boom meant another few million tons of glacier letting go, and every ton that let go went into the headwaters and became our problem downstream.

By midmorning the Thessaly had changed its mind about being a river. It still ran — fast, brown-white with rock flour, furious — but its margins were filling. Pancake ice formed in the slack water along the banks, disks the size of dinner plates spinning in the eddies, and where they collected they froze together into rafts, and where the rafts collected they made bridges, and a bridge of ice across a river this fast becomes a dam, and a dam becomes a flood, or worse: a river that simply decides to go somewhere else and takes your channel with it.

"Sefa," I called down the deck hatch. "Hot tea, sweet, enough for six, and keep it coming. We're going to be cold."

"We're going to be cold *anyway,*" Sefa's voice came up, unbothered, already rattling the kettle. That was Sefa's gift. The world could end; she'd still ask if you'd eaten.

"Priya — charts. I want every bar, every bend, every place the channel splits between here and the Kestrel Narrows, marked and memorized. No lights tonight unless I say."

"And Voss?" Priya asked. She'd been asking after him all morning, quiet, the way you check on someone who took a beating for you. His ribs were strapped, his face the color of old candle wax, but he'd refused to stay ashore at the cache. He'd stood on the moraine with his arm around the crate of documents and said, "I know what's in them better than any of you. They ride with me." So he rode with us, wedged in the pilot house on the bench seat, breathing shallow, holding the archive against his chest like a man holding a sleeping child during a fire.

"Voss watches the upstream water," I said. "Anything white, anything moving wrong, he calls it. Can you do that?"

He lifted his chin a half inch. "I can do that."

Ansel was forward on the bow with a boat hook, because that was where I needed eyes I trusted most. Ice comes from ahead. Everything else comes from behind.

* * *

The first hour was arithmetic. Speed against cold, distance against daylight, fuel against current. I ran the numbers the way my father taught me, out loud, so the crew could hear the shape of the problem even if they couldn't solve it.

"Ninety-one miles to the Narrows. Current's giving us eight, maybe nine if the meltwater holds. Call it ten hours if nothing goes wrong." I let that sit. Nobody laughed. Nothing goes wrong was a joke older than any of us.

"The window," Odile said from the back corner of the pilot house.

She'd been there since dawn, silent, useful only when spoken to, and I'll admit I'd almost managed to forget her. Almost. Her voice still did something to the air in a small room.

"Say it," I said, eyes on the water.

"If the calving continues at this rate, the Narrows will bridge within thirty-six hours. After that, no hull on this river gets through. Not yours. Not anyone's."

"I know the math, Odile. I taught you the math."

Something crossed her face — I caught it in the corner of my eye, quick as a fish turning. Regret, maybe. Or the memory of being twenty-three years old and wrong about something in front of me. Either way she shut up, and that was a mercy, and I hated that I noticed it as a mercy.

The trouble with running ice is that it isn't one problem. It's a thousand small ones wearing the same coat. A single floe the size of a table is nothing — the Coriander shrugs it aside with a knock you feel in your teeth. But floes travel in families, and families pack into bends where the current slows, and a bend that was open water at noon is a white throat by two o'clock. You don't steer around ice. You steer around where ice *wants to be*, which means steering around slow water, which means hugging the fast lane where the river runs hard against rock, which means one mistake puts you on the rocks instead of in the jam.

So I flew the boat between them. Fast water to port, building pack to starboard, a corridor sometimes four boat-lengths wide and sometimes one, and the Coriander threading it at eleven knots with her plates groaning every time a shoulder of ice kissed her hull.

Bo stood at my shoulder with a rag in one hand and a wrench in the other, which was Bo's version of prayer.

"She's taking hits on the port quarter," Bo said, an hour in. "Nothing structural yet. But the seam above the waterline is weeping. Slow. I've got it stuffed with oakum and prayer."

"How much prayer left?"

"Enough for today. Ask me tomorrow."

"Tomorrow we're past the Narrows."

"Then tomorrow I'll find some more."

That was the whole conversation. That was the whole marriage, if you want to call what a crew has a marriage — three words exchanged at speed over machinery, meaning everything, requiring nothing else.

* * *

At the second bend above Miller's Reach, the river tried to kill us properly, and I learned something new, which at my age is either a gift or an insult.

The bend ran wide and shallow on the outside — textbook — so I took the inside line, tight against the point bar, riding the fast seam. What I didn't account for was the raft. A raft of pancake ice the size of a hayfield had grounded itself on the inside bend overnight, invisible from upstream because it sat exactly in the shadow-line where the water darkened under the cutbank. Ansel saw it first — a shout, one word, "*Ice!*" — and I had perhaps three seconds.

Three seconds is enough for one decision, never two. Hard to starboard meant the outside shallows and probably a grounding at speed, which rips bottoms off boats. Hold course meant hitting the raft's edge broadside, and a raft that size doesn't break; it takes you, rolls you, and holds you under while the river piles more ice on top.

So I did neither. I did the thing you're not supposed to do, the thing I'd sworn off after Tom, the thing that lives in the part of the trade they don't teach because it kills more captains than it saves:

I accelerated.

Straight at it. Full throttle, bow square to the raft's edge, and the trick — the whole trick, the entire secret — is that a planing hull hitting ice at speed rides *up*. You surf the edge. For one endless second the Coriander climbed onto the raft's shoulder like a animal hauling out onto a floe, screws biting water-ice slurry, the whole boat shuddering, and then her weight broke through the rotten margin and we crashed down the far side into open channel, shipping a ton of green water over the bow, every soul aboard thrown to the deck except me, locked to the wheel, laughing.

I was laughing. God help me, I was laughing, high and ugly, because the alternative was screaming.

Ansel picked herself up off the foredeck, soaked to the skin, hair frozen into ropes, and gave me a look through the pilot house glass that said, plainly, *you are insane.* Then she grinned — actually grinned, teeth chattering — and threw me a thumbs-up, and I decided right then that when this was over I was going to buy that woman the biggest drink of her life.

"New rule," Bo said from the floor, where he'd landed against the chart table. "Nobody tells Wendell anything nice about herself for a week. Her head won't fit through the door."

"Log it," I said. "Eleven-forty. Ran the raft. All hands accounted for. Sefa — dry clothes forward, and somebody get Ansel a blanket before she turns into figurehead material."

Sefa appeared with towels and tea and fury, which for Sefa was love in its work clothes. She wrapped Ansel like a mother swaddling a baby and lectured the both of us — me for the stunt, Ansel for standing bow watch without her foul-weather shell — and beneath the lecture, in her voice, underneath everything, I heard the other thing she wasn't saying: *don't you dare die on my boat.* I heard it because she'd said it once before, three years ago, over Tom's empty bunk, and some sentences a person only says once.

* * *

Behind us, the river died.

Priya saw it first, from the stern rail, and called it out in her flat archivist's voice, the one she used for cataloguing disasters: "Head of the reach. Look."

I throttled back just long enough to see it — just seconds, because seconds were blood now — and what I saw I will carry longer than the scars. The entire valley behind us, a mile of river we had run that morning, was white. Not patched, not choked. White, bank to bank, a plain of brash and rafted pans grinding slowly against itself with a sound like distant applause, and beyond it, upvalley, the calving fields throwing up their plumes. Each detonation sent a pulse down the dead reach and the whole white plain shivered and compacted and grew.

Somewhere under that whiteness was the channel we'd climbed in June. Somewhere under it were the gravel bars where Tom had taught me to read current, the campsite where Sefa had cooked her first meal aboard, the whole geography of our going. The river was erasing us. By spring it would have rearranged every mile of it, and the Thessaly we had known would exist only in charts and memory, and eventually not even there.

"Window's shut," Bo said quietly.

"Not for us," I said, and brought the throttle back up. "For everyone slower than the Coriander."

But my hands had gone cold on the wheel, and not from the wind.

* * *

We hit the approach to the Kestrel Narrows at dusk, which was the worst possible timing and also the only timing there was. The light was failing sideways through the smoke-colored air, long and orange and useless, throwing shadows off features that didn't exist and hiding ones that did. Ahead, the canyon walls pinched in and the river dropped into its famous staircase — three ledges, each a boat's length of chaos, with the middle one offset so you couldn't see your exit line until you were committed to your entry.

In summer, with the gauge low, the Narrows was a technical run. In melt season, it was a rumor you told green crew to scare them. Today, with the river swollen and carrying ice, it was a coin flip with a loaded coin, and I stood at the wheel and looked at it and did the last honest arithmetic of my career.

Entry line: starboard of the mid-channel rock, thread the tongue, ride it down ledge one. Exit line: hard port, because ledge two kicked everything right. Between them, a trough full of standing waves the size of trucks, and scattered through all of it, white pans riding the current like driftwood, any one of which could put us sideways in the worst hole on the river.

"Everyone hear me," I said, and the pilot house went still. "This is the one. There's no second attempt and no walking away from it. When I call lines, you call them back. If I go down, Bo takes the wheel — you know the heading, two-seven-five through the trough, don't think, just hold it. Priya, hands on the throttle, mirror my calls. Ansel, Voss — get low, hold onto something real, and whatever happens, whatever noise this boat makes—" I looked at the archive in Voss's arms. "—that stays dry. Whatever it costs."

Voss met my eyes. He was gray and shaking and he nodded once, and I believed him.

I put the bow at the tongue and gave her everything.

The entry was clean. Ledge one passed under the keel in a rush of foam and a sound like the world tearing paper, and then we were in the trough and the trough was alive — waves from three directions, ice pans surging through them like animals in a stampede, the Coriander pitching hard enough that the mast whipped and the wheel fought me like a living thing. I called the kick-right before it came — "*Port! Port, NOW!*" — and Priya slammed the throttle and Bo hauled the wheel with me, four hands on the spokes, and the boat came around through a wave that broke completely over the pilot house, green water down my neck, charts flying, and for one black instant we were blind and sideways and falling—

—and then the river set us down.

Just set us down, in the lower pool, rocking, streaming, whole. The third ledge slid past to starboard, tame as a doorstep. Ahead the canyon opened and the last light lay on the water like a road.

Nobody spoke. Then Sefa, from somewhere below decks, in the dark, in the most matter-of-fact voice I have ever heard a human being produce: "Dinner's at eight. Anyone who dies before eight misses out."

And the whole boat laughed — Bo wheezing, Priya's head tipped back, Ansel's whoop echoing off the canyon walls, even Voss, a short pained bark that must have cost him two of his cracked ribs and was worth every one. I stood at the wheel with river water running out of my collar and let it wash through me, that laughter, and I thought: *this is what Tom meant.* Not the miles. This. The crew who finishes this.

* * *

We ran through the night, because stopping was dying. I took the helm till midnight, Bo till four, and I slept sitting up in the pilot house chair with one ear on the engine's voice, the way you sleep in a house where a child is sick. Twice I woke to the sound of ice scraping the hull — long, fingernail sounds in the dark — and twice I was on my feet before my eyes opened, and twice Bo waved me off: pans in the dark water, glancing blows, nothing more.

At dawn the canyon walls fell away and the river widened into the country I knew best in the world, the long braided reach below the Narrows, and the Coriander came home to easy water like a horse feeling its own gate.

That was when Bo found the crack.

Not in the hull. In the *rudder stock* — a fatigue fracture, hairline, running three inches along the weld, born somewhere in the trough or the raft or the thousand small hammer-blows of the day before. Bo found it on the routine dawn inspection, came up the ladder white-faced, and told me in front of everyone, because Bo didn't believe in private bad news.

"It'll hold for gentle water," Bo said. "No hard turns. No backing down hard. And it needs welding inside a week."

"How far to Reprieve?"

"Thirty-two miles."

"Then we have thirty-two miles of gentle water," I said, "and the gentlest captain on the river."

But I stood at the wheel after that with a new listener in my spine, feeling every tremor of the stock come up through the wheel like a pulse, monitoring a heartbeat that wasn't mine, and the crew felt it too — I watched them feel it, all of them moving a little softer, speaking a little lower, the whole boat becoming one careful organism around its own wound. Ansel stood silent bow watch for six hours without relief. Priya navigated by eye and called depths before the lead line confirmed her. Voss, gray as ash, held the archive and steered nothing and stayed anyway.

Reprieve's smoke showed on the horizon at midafternoon, woodsmoke rising straight in the still air, and I felt the crew gather around me at the windows without a word being spoken. Because Reprieve wasn't just a harbor. Reprieve was the harbormaster — the man whose name was in the ledger, whose name was in Tom's letter, the company man who had bought Odile's silence and murdered my brother with a crowbar and a thawing lake and called it weather. Thirty-two miles of gentle water, and at the end of them, the one man on this river with every reason to make sure we never reached the sea.

"He'll know we're coming," Priya said. "Word travels faster than boats."

"He'll try to seize her at the dock," Bo said. "Or burn the evidence and call it an accident. He's done it before."

I looked at the smoke rising over the town that drank the poisoned water, and I thought about the townspeople who had fed us and sheltered us and chosen us once already, and I turned the wheel a degree and began, gently, gently, the last approach.

"Then we won't tie up at his dock," I said. "Sefa — how many people can you feed off what's in the galley?"

Sefa smiled, slow and terrible, and began counting.
