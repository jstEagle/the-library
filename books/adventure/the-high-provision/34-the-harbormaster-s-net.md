## 34. The Harbormaster's Net

The smoke from Reprieve's chimneys rose straight up in the cold morning air, which meant no wind, which meant the river would run glassy and slow through the last bend before the harbor — and it meant Wendell could see, from a mile out, that there were men on her dock.

Not the usual loiterers. Not the gill-netters mending gear. Men in matching canvas coats, standing in a line, and behind them a wagon loaded with barrels she could smell even at this distance. Lamp oil. Enough of it to make a statement.

"Captain." Priya's voice came down from the wheelhouse roof, where she'd been scanning with Bo's binoculars. "That's Grell's crew. Six of them, maybe eight. And there's a second wagon behind the fuel shed."

Wendell eased the throttle back a notch. The Coriander came down the channel at a walk, her hull still groaning from the descent, a fresh scar along her port strake where the ice had chewed her above the Kettle Rapids. Behind Wendell, in the wheelhouse, the archive sat in a waterproof satchel between her feet — Tom's evidence, the core sample logs, the photographs, the beacon transmission record Odile had triggered at the cache three days ago. Copies existed now, in theory. The beacon had flung the data at every relay station on the upper river. But theory was thin protection against a man with matches.

"Grell knows the beacon fired," Sefa said from the doorway, arms crossed, flour still on her knuckles from the morning's bread. "Word travels faster than boats on this river. He's had three days to decide what to do about it."

"He's decided," Wendell said. "He's decided to burn my boat."

She brought the Coriander in slow, stern-first to her old berth at the north end of the dock, where the pilings were thickest and the water deepest, and put a fender over the side like this was any ordinary arrival. Her hands were steady. She noticed that about herself the way you notice a strange bird — with detached interest. Three years ago her hands would not have been steady. Three years ago she hadn't known what she was carrying, or what it had cost.

Now she knew. Tom had died face-down in black water with his lantern sinking beside him, and he had died because a man named Aldous Grell — the same man who'd shaken Wendell's hand at this very dock, who'd stamped her manifests, who'd spoken kindly at Tom's memorial — had loosened the ice anchor and cut the safety line and walked away whistling. Ansel's testimony. Voss's. The ledger in Grell's own cramped hand, photographed in Sefa's galley a thousand miles downstream.

The gangway hadn't touched the dock before Grell himself came down the planks, coat buttoned to the throat, hat brim low, wearing the face of a man burdened by duty.

"Captain Lockridge." His voice carried the way it always did, pitched for the crowd that had begun to gather at the dock's landward end — fishwives, net-menders, the ferryman, old Marta Kessler who ran the boarding house. "You'll understand I have to ask you to hold position. There's a quarantine order on the upper river. Unregistered salvage, unmanifested cargo, and a beacon transmission from a restricted zone. I'm obliged to impound the vessel pending inspection."

"Obliged," Wendell repeated. She stayed at the rail. She didn't step onto the dock, and she didn't invite him aboard. "By whom?"

"By the Territorial Resource Authority." He produced a paper, unfolded it with ceremony. "Signed and sealed."

"May I?" Priya was already coming down the gangway, and Wendell let her, because Priya had spent eleven years in the provincial archives and could smell a forged seal the way Sefa could smell burnt sugar. She read the order for a long moment. The dock went quiet enough that Wendell could hear the river working at the pilings.

"This is dated four days ago," Priya said. "The beacon fired three days ago. You impounded a boat that hadn't arrived yet, for a violation that hadn't happened yet."

"The order anticipates—"

"The order anticipates," Priya said, and her voice was mild and terrible, "that we would come down the river with evidence of criminal poisoning of the Thessaly watershed, and that you would want it to stop here." She looked up. "It's a good forgery. The seal's right. But the Authority's regional office moved to Fairhaven in the spring, and the address block still says Grayling. Nobody's used the Grayling address in two years. I filed the change-of-address notice myself."

The crowd at the end of the dock made a sound — not a cheer, not yet, but a shifting, like ice deciding.

Grell's face did something small and fatal. He recovered it, mostly. "The vessel is impounded regardless. My men will board and secure the cargo. If the cargo is what I believe it to be, it constitutes a hazard to public safety and will be destroyed on-site. Standard procedure for contaminated material." He turned and lifted two fingers, and the men in canvas coats started forward, and behind them two others began rolling the oil barrels toward the boat.

Wendell moved then. She came down the gangway and stood at the bottom of it, squarely, a small woman in a salt-stiffened coat with her brother's compass in her pocket, and she did not raise her voice.

"Aldous."

He stopped. It was the name, she thought. Not the tone. Nobody on this dock had said his given name to him in years; he was Grell, the harbormaster, the office, the stamp. Aldous was a man, and men could be accused.

"You knew my brother," she said. "You ate at his table. You fished the Kettle with him every October for six years, and when he died you told me — you stood right there—" she pointed at the spot, at the warped plank by the cleat, "—and you told me the ice took him, and you held my hand, and you said *if there's anything I can do.*"

"Captain—"

"Tom Lockridge logged your payments. He photographed your ledgers. He died carrying proof that you took company money to bury what they were doing to this river — to *your* river, Aldous, the one your own town drinks from." She let that sit. She watched it land in the faces behind him — Marta Kessler, whose well had gone bitter two summers ago; the ferryman whose nets came up empty above the bend; the net-menders who'd stopped eating the fish they caught. "And now you're going to burn the proof on my deck, in front of everyone, and call it procedure."

"I'm going to enforce the law," Grell said, but the pitch of it was wrong, climbing, and the crowd heard it too.

"Are you?" said a new voice.

Bo came down the gangway last, moving stiff, favoring the shoulder the serac fall had wrenched, and in his hands — held out flat, the way you hold something you want everyone to see — was the nameplate. T. LOCKRIDGE, brass on walnut, the letters worn soft by three years in a welded dark. He walked past Wendell, past Grell, and set it on the bollard at the dock's edge where the morning light caught it.

"Found this welded inside her hull," Bo said to the crowd, not to Grell. "Under the hold. Along with rope, and a broken carabiner, and a ship's log with the last week torn out. Somebody welded Tom Lockridge's things into a riverboat to keep them safe, and somebody else has spent three years making sure nobody asked why." He straightened up, and Wendell saw his hands were shaking now, finally, now that the work was done. "I'm asking why."

The crowd came forward. Not fast — a dock's worth of people don't rush — but they came, and they came around, and Wendell watched the geometry change: Grell's line of canvas coats suddenly flanked, suddenly few, suddenly men standing in the middle of their own town with oil barrels nobody would help them roll.

"Grell." Marta Kessler's voice, dry as net twine. "My well went bad the summer after Tom died. I hauled water from the spring for two years and you told me it was beaver upstream. Was it beaver, Aldous?"

"Mrs. Kessler—"

"Was it *beaver?*"

"It was—" He stopped. Wendell watched him do the arithmetic every cornered man does: the exits, the odds, the faces. There were no exits. The dock ran thirty feet to land and the land was full of people who drank the water.

And that was when Voss came down the gangway.

He came slowly, one arm strapped across his chest where the calving-field shrapnel had opened him, face still bruised the color of a storm sky, and he walked to the bollard and stood beside the nameplate and looked at Grell with the flat patience of a man who had spent his life being paid not to notice things and had finally, at terrible cost, noticed one.

"My name is Elias Voss," he said. His voice was quiet and the dock went quiet to hear it. "I was hired by the Meridian Consolidated Mining Company as a verification surveyor. My instructions were to reach the High Provision cache, confirm the contents of the evidence archive, and destroy it. The payment schedule is in a ledger that Captain Sefa photographed at mile four hundred and twelve. My signature is on page six. The countersignature is yours."

"I don't know what—"

"On page nine," Voss went on, unbothered, "there is a disbursement dated the night of the Kettle ice accident, three years ago. It is annotated *final settlement, L. arrangement concluded.* I have seen the annotation. I copied it before I understood what I was copying, because I am a careful man, and my care is the only reason any of us are standing here." He lifted his bandaged arm slightly, an odd small gesture, half apology. "I was also told, by your own agent, that Tom Lockridge's death was an accident of weather. It was not. The ice anchor was cut. The company man who cut it reported to you. I know this because I was shown the report as proof that the matter was closed." He turned, then, and looked at Wendell, and something in his face was almost gentle. "I'm sorry, Captain. I was shown your brother's name in a list of liabilities."

The sound the crowd made then was not a sound Wendell would ever forget. It had no words in it. It was the sound of a town understanding, all at once, what had been done to them and by whom and how long they'd been drinking it — a low, collective exhalation, grief and fury braided, and it moved through the dock like a change in the current, and every canvas coat on that dock felt it and every one of them stepped back.

Grell reached inside his coat.

Wendell's hand was on the flare pistol at her hip before she'd decided to move it — she'd carried it since the ice fields, loaded, and her voice came out level and cold as the meltwater: "Don't."

What came out of his coat was not a gun. It was a matchbook. He held it up, absurdly, the way a man holds the last thing left in his hands, and looked at it, and looked at the crowd, and Wendell watched the whole shape of him change — the office crumbling, the stamp, the seal, the duty, all of it going, until what stood on the dock was just a man in a good coat with matches he would never be allowed to strike.

"The company will bury you," he said. His voice had gone small. "All of you. You think a dock full of fishermen changes what Meridian is? They own the courts. They own the papers. They own—"

"They owned us," said the ferryman, old Rennick, who had not spoken in Wendell's memory. He came forward with his hands in his pockets and stood beside Bo. "Quietly, for years, because we didn't know better. But the fish stopped coming, Aldous, and the water went bitter, and my granddaughter's got a rash on her arms that three doctors can't name. And now we know better." He nodded at the satchel at Wendell's feet, at the nameplate on the bollard, at Voss standing straight as a piling with his arm in a sling. "That's worth more than what you were paid. Whatever it was."

"It was enough to buy a man's silence for three years," Wendell said. "It wasn't enough to buy the town's."

Grell looked at her for a long moment. And Wendell saw — and would carry this, would carry it the way she carried Tom's compass — that he was not looking at an enemy. He was looking at a woman whose brother he had helped kill, and behind the fear in his face was something older and more exhausted, the look of a man setting down a weight he had forgotten was heavy. His eyes went to the nameplate. T. LOCKRIDGE. He read it the way you read a sentence you've been avoiding for years.

"He was going to publish," Grell said quietly. "That autumn. He came to my office and told me he had the samples and the photographs and he was going to publish, and I told him — I told him to wait, that I'd fix it, that there was a way to do it without ruining everyone. And then I took their money instead, and I told them where he'd be moored." He closed his hand around the matchbook until the cardboard bent. "I didn't cut the line. I want that said. I want it said in front of her. I sold the location and I looked away. I didn't cut the line."

"Someone did," Wendell said. Her voice did not shake. She was proud of that, distantly, the way you're proud of a boat holding in weather. "And the company paid for it, and you countersigned. That's enough, Aldous. That's more than enough."

The Authority launch came around the point at noon — the real one, out of Fairhaven, responding to the beacon transmission Odile had fired from the cache, carrying two inspectors and a woman in a gray coat who introduced herself as Magistrate Corren and who looked at the forged order, then at Grell, then at the crowd, and said, "Who has the evidence?"

"I do," Wendell said. "And copies. And witnesses." She nodded down the dock. "And a man who was paid to destroy it and didn't."

Voss gave his testimony standing up, on the dock, in the wind, with his arm strapped and his voice steady, for two hours. He gave the payment schedule, the instructions, the names. Ansel gave hers after, and hers was the one that made the inspectors stop writing and start listening — the village, the valley, the sister, the water. Marta Kessler testified about her well. Rennick about the fish. Bo about the nameplate and the welded dark. Priya laid the ledger photographs out on a plank table like a hand of cards and explained, in her mild archival voice, exactly how the Grayling address had betrayed the forgery.

And Grell — Grell sat on a bollard with his hat in his hands and told them everything, because there was nothing left to buy and no one left to sell it to, and Wendell stood at the edge of the crowd and listened to the whole rotten architecture of it come down plank by plank, and felt — not satisfaction, nothing so clean — but a kind of terrible settling, like a hull taking its proper trim.

The magistrate's clerk asked her for her statement last.

"Captain Lockridge. Your brother's death. Do you wish to press the matter of the sabotage as a separate proceeding, or fold it into the watershed case?"

Wendell thought about the black water. The lantern sinking. The letter in Tom's hand that she had read by lamplight in a vault of ice — *forgive her, she didn't know, and when she knows it will be heavy, and heavy is not the same as broken* — and she thought about Odile, sitting below in the surveyor's cabin right now, in irons of her own asking, waiting to be handed over to these same inspectors, waiting to read that letter in custody because Wendell had given it to her and said *you don't get absolution, you get the truth.*

"Fold it in," Wendell said. "All of it. One record. I want it in one place, where nobody can lose the pages." She looked at the clerk. "I've had enough of lost pages."

The inspectors went aboard the Coriander at dusk, respectful, boots wiped, and came out again with the satchel sealed in evidence bags, and Wendell signed the chain-of-custody form with a hand that was finally, faintly, trembling — now, only now, when the work was done.

The oil barrels stayed on the dock, unopened. Later, Sefa stood looking at them, and then at the crowd still lingering, reluctant to disperse, unwilling to let the day end and let it all become something that had happened instead of something happening — and Wendell saw her cook come to a decision the way she'd seen her make a thousand smaller ones.

"Bo," Sefa said. "Get my big pot off the boat."

* * *

They cooked on the dock. Of course they did. Sefa commandeered the boarding house kitchen and two of Marta Kessler's daughters and by full dark there were tables down the length of the planks and the whole town of Reprieve was eating — fish chowder and flatbread and the last of the Coriander's stores given freely, because that was what Sefa did with grief and relief and everything in between: she fed it. Wendell sat at the end of the longest table with a bowl she didn't remember being handed, and Rennick's granddaughter fell asleep against Bo's good shoulder, and Voss — who had never in his life, Wendell suspected, been fed by strangers out of anything but obligation — ate three bowls and looked astonished each time.

Priya sat down beside her with two cups of the boarding house's terrible coffee and handed one over.

"You're thinking about her," Priya said.

"I'm thinking about tomorrow," Wendell said. "The inspectors want statements from everyone. Formal ones. Odile's included." She turned the cup in her hands. "And I keep thinking — Tom's letter says her betrayal was small and sad. Silence, bought with threats. But she signed the contract that brought us up the river. She hired the men who were meant to burn it. She stood on my dock in March and lied to my face with a smile she's been practicing for three years." Wendell looked out at the dark water, where the Coriander rode her lines, scarred and sound. "Small and sad is still a betrayal, Priya. I don't know what I owe her. I don't know if I owe her anything."

"Tomorrow you'll find out," Priya said. "She asked the clerk for something, an hour ago. Before they took her to the office to hold overnight." Priya sipped her coffee. "She asked for paper and a pen. And she asked the clerk to tell you — her words — *tell the captain I'm writing my statement, and I'm writing all of it, and she can read it before anyone else does.*"

Wendell looked at her.

"All of it," Priya repeated. "Her words."

Down the dock, Sefa laughed at something, and the fire cracked, and the river ran on in the dark toward the sea, carrying nothing now but the ordinary weight of a boat at anchor — and Wendell Lockridge, who measured her life in miles, understood that the longest mile of this voyage was still ahead of her, and it was thirty feet of dock planks to a lit window in the harbormaster's office, and a woman on the other side of it with a pen in her hand, writing down everything.
