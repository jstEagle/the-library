## 35. Aftermath Ashore

Sefa had cooked for funerals before. She knew the arithmetic of grief — how many mouths, how much rice, how the living forget to eat until someone puts a plate in their hands and refuses to take it back. So when the Coriander tied up at Reprieve with her hull scarred and her cargo hold full of truth, Sefa went ashore first, before the officials, before the crowd, before anyone could tell her what to do. She walked to the harbormaster's office, looked at the man behind the desk — the one whose name was in the ledger, whose signature was on payments that bought silence — and she said, "I'll need your kitchen."

He stared at her like she'd spoken a language from the bottom of the river.

"The town stood with us today," she said. "That means they're hungry tonight. Where do you cook?"

* * *

The kitchen was small and honest: a wood stove that ran hot on the left side, a cold box that hummed, copper pots dented by decades of use. Sefa laid out her knives on the counter the way other people lay out flags. Bo came by an hour later with a hand truck and forty pounds of potatoes requisitioned from the general store against salvage rights, which Priya had determined were legally theirs, or close enough.

"Officials landed at the airstrip," Bo reported, leaning in the doorway, still smelling of bilge and engine oil. "Three of them. Suits. One of them keeps saying 'chain of custody' like it's a prayer."

"Did you feed them?"

"They haven't earned feeding yet."

Sefa pointed the paring knife at him, gently. "Everyone earns feeding. That's not the same as earning trust. Go find out how many townsfolk are coming tonight."

"How would I know that?"

"Count the doors," Sefa said. "In a town this size, every door is coming."

* * *

They came, of course. They came because word travels down a river faster than water does, and Reprieve had spent the afternoon learning what its own harbormaster had signed his name to. The ledger photographs Priya had printed at the general store's ancient copier were pinned to the harbor board beside the notice about the High Provision cache, and beneath them someone — Sefa never learned who — had pinned the real manifest, the one Ansel had carried folded against her heart for two hundred miles. CORE SAMPLES — UPPER THESSALY. CONTAMINANT PROFILES. INTERNAL DISTRIBUTION ONLY.

The words did their work quietly, the way poison had done its work quietly for years, upstream, in the water the town drank.

By dusk there were sixty people on the dock, then eighty. Fishermen who'd wondered why the pike downstream tasted of metal. A woman whose well water had turned her children's teeth gray. Old men who'd fished the Thessaly all their lives and said nothing, because what was there to say to a mining company with lawyers. They stood in the blue evening light looking at the Coriander — at her patched planking, at the memorial shelf where Tom Lockridge's lantern sat clean and lit — and they waited, without knowing quite for what.

Sefa gave them food.

She'd commandeered every pot in Reprieve. There was river fish poached in broth with wild leeks, there were the potatoes crushed with garlic, there was flatbread blistered on the stove top, there was a berry crumble made from what the general store had and pride. She worked the line herself, ladling, refusing help at first and then accepting it from three teenage girls who simply picked up spoons and started, and after that the kitchen belonged to everyone, which is the only way a kitchen ever really works.

Wendell came through the line last, as Sefa had known she would. Captains eat last; it's a law older than any registry.

"You've fed half the town," Wendell said.

"I've fed all of them. Half took seconds." Sefa loaded her plate past decency. "Sit. Eat. Tomorrow you'll be giving statements until your voice gives out. Tonight you're just a woman who hasn't eaten since Kestrel Narrows."

Wendell looked at the plate, then at Sefa, and something in her face — the thing that had been locked tight since the calving fields, since Tom's letter, since the ice came down behind them like a door closing — loosened one degree. "You always know," she said.

"It's my job to know. Everyone shows me who they are at dinner." Sefa nudged her toward the benches. "Go sit with your crew, Captain. You've got one."

* * *

The officials arrived at the meal, which Sefa considered fitting. Let them see what they were wading into: not evidence in boxes but people, eighty of them, holding plates.

There were three. A federal investigator named Okafor, tall and unhurried, with reading glasses pushed up into her hair and the patient eyes of someone who'd heard a thousand versions of the truth and learned to wait for the truest one. A regional prosecutor's man named Dill, younger, carrying a case file like a shield. And a company liaison — Sefa clocked her immediately, the pressed blazer among the flannel, the smile calibrated to the millimeter — who introduced herself as representing Meridian Extraction's "full cooperation with the inquiry."

"Full cooperation," Bo repeated, deadpan, from the end of the table where he'd installed himself with a second helping.

"We have nothing to hide," the liaison said.

Priya set a photograph on the table between them. It was one of hers, printed that afternoon: the contaminant profile page, the internal distribution stamp, the date nine years old. "Then this will be a short inquiry," she said pleasantly.

The liaison's smile held. Barely. Sefa, passing with the bread, saw the woman's eyes flick to the harbor board, to the crowd, to the gray-teethed children playing tag between the fish racks, and understood that the smile was doing arithmetic and losing.

Okafor took statements at a folding table under the harbor lights, starting with Voss, who insisted, half-healed ribs and all, on going first. He spoke for two hours. Sefa brought him tea halfway through and stayed to listen, because she'd learned on this voyage that the quiet ones carry the most.

He told it plainly: the contract, the sealed crates he'd been hired to verify, the instruction to destroy anything incriminating once the cache was reached, the coordinates, the phrase *before the season turns*. He told them about Ansel's sister's valley, though he stumbled on the name and Ansel, sitting nearby, said it for him — clear and loud, so the whole dock heard it, so it went into the record twice.

"And Marchetti?" Okafor asked. "What was her role?"

Voss was quiet a long moment. "She hired us through intermediaries. I don't know what she knew. I know what she paid for." He looked down at his bandaged ribs. "I know what she almost let us burn."

Across the dock, apart from everyone, Odile Marchetti sat alone on a bollard with her hands wrapped around a cup of coffee Sefa had given her without a word. She wasn't under arrest — not yet, Okafor had said, the jurisdictional threads needed pulling — but she didn't leave, and she didn't come closer, and when Wendell passed within ten feet of her carrying firewood for the cook stove, neither woman looked at the other, and the space between them was louder than the whole crowd.

Sefa watched it happen and thought: *that is not finished.* Nothing that heavy finishes in a day.

* * *

Later, when the statements wound down and the crowd thinned to stragglers and the teenagers who'd helped in the kitchen were dancing badly to a radio somebody's cousin had brought, Sefa found Okafor at the folding table, eating the plate Sefa had put in front of her an hour ago with the thoroughness of a woman who respected food.

"You cook like you're feeding an army," Okafor said.

"I cook like everyone's hungry, because they usually are." Sefa sat across from her uninvited. "You're going to ask my crew back tomorrow. Formally. Depositions."

Okafor raised an eyebrow. "You've done this before."

"I've buried a daughter and sat through the inquiry after. Different water, same current." Sefa folded her hands. "So I'll tell you what I told them, and you can decide if it helps. Everything they say tomorrow will be true. But understand what you're getting. That boat isn't a crew, it's a family, and families don't testify cleanly. They protect each other. They leave things out. Not lies — mercies. If you want the whole machine taken apart, you'll get it, but you'll have to be patient with the way love gets tangled in the gears."

Okafor considered her over the plate. "And Marchetti?"

"What about her?"

"She's the thread that ties your captain's brother to the company's money. Every statement points at her. And she's sitting right there, free, drinking coffee, while the man who actually signed the sabotage order—" Okafor tapped the ledger photograph, the harbormaster's name, "—is currently packing a bag in the office behind you. We have men watching the airstrip. He won't make it."

"He'll try," Sefa said. "Men like that always try."

"And Marchetti? Will she try?"

Sefa looked down the dock to where Odile sat, motionless, the coffee long gone cold in her hands, watching the Coriander's lit windows the way you watch a house you used to live in.

"No," Sefa said slowly. "I don't think she'll run. I think she's waiting for something. And I think what she's waiting for is my captain." She stood, collected the empty plate. "Which is why nobody on that boat has slept properly, and why I'm making a big breakfast."

* * *

Midnight found the dock quiet, the fires banked, the radio silent. Sefa walked the length of it out of habit — checking mooring lines, checking the galley, checking her people the way she checked her pots, lids on, heat low, nothing boiling over.

Bo was asleep in the engine room, literally, on a cot he'd rigged years ago, one arm hanging out over the deck plates. Priya was awake in the wheelhouse with the archive spread around her, cataloguing by lamplight, guarding the documents even now, even here. Ansel slept in the surveyors' cabin with the real manifest under her pillow like a child with a talisman. Voss slept sitting up, ribs strapped, a cup of Sefa's ginger tea gone cold on the sill.

Wendell was at the rail, aft, looking north up the dark river.

Sefa climbed the steps and stood beside her without speaking. That was the arrangement between them, three years old now: Sefa talked enough for both of them, and Wendell's silences were answers too.

"He's really gone," Wendell said finally. Not Tom. The harbormaster. Word had come down an hour ago — he'd made the airstrip, but the plane wouldn't start, and Okafor's men had taken him out of the cockpit with the ignition key in his fist. "It doesn't feel like anything. I thought it would feel like something."

"Grief isn't a schedule," Sefa said. "It comes when it comes. Sometimes it waits until the dishes are done."

Wendell huffed — almost a laugh. Downriver, the ice would be closing the high route even now, mile by mile, sealing the calving fields and the cache and Tom's camp under winter's lid until spring. Behind them, closed forever. Ahead, open water all the way home.

"There are hearings," Wendell said. "Okafor says months of them. Lawyers. Cameras, maybe. The company's already issued a statement calling the manifest 'fabricated by disgruntled contractors.'" She turned the words over like stones. "They think we're contractors."

"They think wrong."

"They think we'll fold. One of us, eventually. Some night some lawyer finds the crack in one of us and leans on it." Wendell's voice was steady, but Sefa heard the fear underneath it, plain as a sandbar under brown water. "Sefa. What if they're right?"

Sefa looked at her — at this woman who measured life in miles, who'd run the gauntlet home through falling ice with her hands steady on the wheel, who'd read her brother's letter and wept exactly once, privately, and then steered. Who was more afraid of lawyers than rapids, because rapids couldn't take her family away with paperwork.

"Then they'll find out what I already know," Sefa said. "You don't break a family by leaning on one member. We hold each other up. That's the whole design." She touched Wendell's shoulder, brief and firm. "Now go to sleep. Breakfast is at six, and it's going to be good, and you're going to eat all of it."

Wendell went. Sefa stayed a moment longer at the rail, looking at the black water sliding past the hull, carrying everything downstream toward the sea — the meltwater, the silt, whatever the company had poured into it for nine years, and now, riding on top of it all, the truth, moving fast.

Down the dock, a match flared. Odile Marchetti, lighting a cigarette at the edge of the light, alone, watching the boat. Watching Wendell's window. Waiting.

Tomorrow the depositions began. Tomorrow the lawyers and the cameras and the slow grinding machinery of justice. But tonight Sefa smelled the next storm coming before it broke — not weather, something older and more personal, moving between those two women like a current under the ice.

She banked the galley stove, set the dough to rise for the morning bread, and wrote on the chalkboard by the pass-through, as she did every night, the day's count:

*Eighty-three fed. Nobody hungry. Nobody whole.*

*Yet.*

Outside, Odile dropped her cigarette, ground it under her heel, and started walking toward the boat.
