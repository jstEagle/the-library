## 36. What Odile Owes

The hearing room in Reprieve smelled of pine resin and cold iron, the way all official rooms upriver did — built fast, meant to last, furnished by whoever had a truck and no taste. Wendell had sat in this room once before, three years ago, answering questions about an accident with holes in her memory the size of her brother. She had left that day with nothing. Today she came to give.

The officials from the capital had taken statements all morning: Sefa's ledger photographs, Priya's recovered log impressions, Ansel's real manifest, Voss's testimony delivered flat and unflinching while his arm hung in a sling. The town had crowded the windows. The harbormaster — Callum Reeve, the name Tom had written in a letter he never sent — sat in cuffs two chairs down, sweating through his uniform, insisting to anyone who would listen that he'd only been following instructions.

Odile Marchetti was the last to be called.

She stood when they said her name. She'd dressed for it — not the expedition gear she'd worn upriver but a dark coat, pressed trousers, hair pinned back like a woman attending a funeral she'd helped arrange. She gave her statement in a voice pitched low and even, and she didn't stop at what the officials asked. She went past it. She named the payments. She named the years of silence. She named the night on the ice, the sabotage she'd suspected and failed to prevent because she'd chosen not to look too closely at who was paying her crew's wages. When the senior official asked if she understood the charges, Odile said, "I've understood them for three years. I just finally stopped running from them."

They took her out through the side door to await formal processing. And Wendell, who had planned all morning not to be there when it happened, found herself standing anyway, following the corridor, because some debts you don't let other people carry alone even when you hate them for owing them.

* * *

The holding room was a converted storeroom off the harbormaster's office — irony so thick Wendell could have cut it and floated it downriver. A cot, a table, one window barred against nothing in particular since nobody in Reprieve had ever needed jailing before this week. Odile sat at the table with her hands folded, and when Wendell came in she didn't look surprised.

"I wondered how long you'd make me wait," Odile said.

"Long enough to be sure I wasn't going to say something I'd regret." Wendell stayed by the door. Old habit. With Odile you kept your back to an exit or you were a fool. "Or maybe I was hoping the feeling would pass."

"And?"

"It hasn't."

Odile nodded slowly, as if Wendell had confirmed something she'd already entered in a ledger of her own. "Fair."

Wendell crossed the room and took the chair opposite, deliberately, making herself sit in it the way she made herself hold course through rapids — because turning away was the coward's option and she'd spent enough of her life being brave about the wrong things. Up close, Odile looked older than she had at the dock in chapter one of this whole disaster. Not weaker. Just older, the way stone looks older after a flood.

"You sold out," Wendell said. "Say it plain. No version where you were protecting anyone. No version where the money was complicated. Say it."

"I sold out." Odile's voice didn't waver. "Reeve came to me six weeks after the accident. Showed me photographs of my own crew, my own boat, my own accounts — everything arranged so neatly that any investigator would have found me guilty of negligence, maybe manslaughter. He offered to make it go away. All it cost was silence about what was really in that cache, and my signature on a survey contract that would keep company eyes on the upper river forever." Her mouth twisted. "I told myself I was protecting my people. That's the lie I chose. It fit nicely."

"Why tell me now?"

"Because you asked me to say it plain, and because I'm done choosing lies." Odile looked down at her folded hands. "And because you deserve to hear it from me and not from a transcript."

Wendell sat with that. Outside the barred window, gulls argued over something on the dock pilings. Somewhere below, Bo was probably already pulling planking off the Coriander's hull, starting the refit, turning grief into carpentry the way he always did.

"The night Tom died," Wendell said. "I was there. I remember now. I tried to stop it."

"I know."

"You *know*?" The word came out sharper than Wendell intended, and she let it stay sharp. "You knew I was there? You knew I tried—"

"I knew someone fought him." Odile's eyes came up, steady and terrible. "Reeve told me afterward. Said a woman got between him and the charge line and he had to... deal with her." A pause. "He never said your name. But there was only one woman on that ice who would have fought him."

Wendell's hands were flat on the table. She watched them, because looking at Odile was suddenly more than she could manage. Three years of blankness, and the man who caused it had described her to the woman sitting across from her like a line item in a report.

"He hit me," Wendell said quietly. "That's the part I couldn't remember. Not the ice. Not the water. Him."

"Yes."

"And you sat across a contract table from him for three years."

"Yes." Odile didn't flinch from it. Wendell had to give her that — she never flinched from anything, which was either courage or a very sophisticated form of cowardice, and Wendell had stopped trying to decide which. "Every quarter, I signed papers across from the man who hurt you and killed your brother, and I told myself the silence was survival. You want to know what I owe you, Wendell? I owe you three years of knowing exactly what I was doing every single time I shook his hand."

The room was quiet long enough that Wendell could hear her own pulse.

"I don't forgive you," she said at last. "I want to be clear about that, because I think some part of you came here hoping that telling the truth would buy something. It doesn't. Truth isn't currency. It's just truth."

"I know."

"You hired me with sealed crates and a false manifest. You put my crew — my *family* — on a river you knew was closing early, chasing evidence you could have surrendered from a courthouse steps if you'd had the spine three years ago."

"Yes."

"You let us find the nameplate. You let us dig Tom's camp out of the ice. You let Priya read my dead brother's handwriting by lamplight." Wendell's voice cracked on the last word and she hated it, hated the crack, hated that Odile heard it. "You could have told us everything at Reprieve. On the porch. You told me a polished story instead, and I *almost believed you*, and that's the part that makes me sickest. Not what you did. What you almost made me do with it."

"That's fair," Odile said. "All of it is fair."

"Stop agreeing with me."

"What would you rather I do?"

"*Fight back.*" It came out louder than Wendell meant, and somewhere beyond the door a guard shifted his weight. She lowered her voice, leaned forward. "Defend yourself. Give me the version where you're not a coward, because I've spent twenty years knowing you, Odile, and the woman I raced down the Thessaly in '09 wouldn't have signed anything. She'd have burned the contract and run Reeve's barge aground on a gravel bar out of spite. So either you've changed, or the woman I knew was always a story I told myself, and I need to know which, because I have to live with whichever answer it is."

For the first time, Odile looked away.

It lasted maybe four seconds. Wendell counted them the way she counted sandbars — precisely, professionally, because counting was what she did instead of drowning.

"I changed," Odile said finally. "Not all at once. Reeve's photographs, then the quarterly meetings, then the years stacking up like ballast until I couldn't have turned the boat around if I wanted to. You know how it works. One compromise, then another, each one small enough to survive, until you look up and you're a person you'd have run aground ten years ago." She met Wendell's eyes again. "The woman you raced in '09 is still in here somewhere. She's the one who hired you. She's the one who picked you specifically because she knew — she *knew* — that if anyone could finish what Tom started, it was you. Even if she couldn't do it herself anymore."

"So I was your penance."

"You were my last honest act."

"No." Wendell stood. The chair scraped. "Your last honest act was standing up in that hearing room this morning and saying it plain. Hiring me was you spending my family to buy back your soul, and we both know it, and the fact that it worked doesn't make it clean."

Odile held her gaze. Something moved behind her eyes — not tears; Wendell doubted Odile Marchetti had cried since the Carter Expedition, and even then probably only from glacier spray. Something more like recognition. Like a woman seeing the final bill for a long voyage and finding it accurate.

"No," Odile agreed quietly. "It doesn't make it clean."

* * *

Wendell walked to the window. The afternoon light was going gold over the water, the same light that had gilded the dock the day Odile appeared with her false manifest, and Wendell thought about how many times a life can turn on the same stretch of riverbank without the river caring at all.

Down at the mooring, the Coriander sat low in the water, scarred from the descent, her port rail patched with fresh lumber that hadn't weathered yet. Sefa had strung lanterns along the deck for tonight's meal. Figures moved on the dock — townspeople coming early, bringing chairs, bringing dishes. Priya was up on the roof of the wheelhouse tacking something to the frame. From here Wendell couldn't read it, but she knew what it was. They'd voted on it last night, around Sefa's table, with the evidence boxed and waiting for the capital courier.

TOM LOCKRIDGE — HE GAVE THIS RIVER ITS VOICE BACK.

Priya had argued for something longer. Bo had argued for something shorter. In the end it was Sefa's phrasing, because Sefa always knew which words a family could live inside.

"They'll take me to the capital tomorrow," Odile said behind her. "Formal arraignment. Then the hearings. Months of them, probably. Years." A breath. "I'll testify against the company. Against everyone. Whatever it costs."

"It'll cost everything."

"I know."

"Do you?" Wendell turned. "Because I've watched you calculate your whole life, Odile. Every risk priced, every loss amortized. This is the first thing you've ever done that you can't amortize. There's no version of this where you come out ahead. You understand that?"

"I understand that better than you know." Odile smiled, and it was the saddest expression Wendell had ever seen on her face — and she'd seen Odile cry once, in '09, though both of them had pretended otherwise ever since. "I ran the numbers for three years, Wendell. Silence was profitable. That's what made it possible. Exposure costs me the company, the name, probably my freedom. And it's still the first trade I've made in years that I'd sign again."

Wendell studied her. Twenty years of history sat between them — races won and lost, rivers shared, a friendship that had curdled into rivalry and then into whatever this was, this thing with no clean name. She thought about Tom's letter, folded in her breast pocket since the ice field. She'd carried it against her heart for eleven days now, reading it every night until she could recite it, letting it rebuild her from the inside the way Bo was rebuilding the Coriander plank by plank.

She took it out.

Odile watched the paper appear and went very still.

"This is Tom's," Wendell said. "Written the week he died. Addressed to me. He never sent it because he died before he could, and I found it in his camp with his own hands frozen around it, more or less." She laid it on the table between them, face down, her palm flat on top of it. "I've read it every night for eleven days. It's the reason I can stand in this room. It's the reason I didn't put my hands around your throat when you told me you knew Reeve hit me and you signed papers with him anyway."

Odile's throat worked. "Wendell—"

"You don't get absolution," Wendell said. "You get the truth. It's heavier."

She turned the letter over and slid it across the table.

"For the hearings. For the years ahead. Read it whenever it gets easy in there — and it will get easy, Odile, custody always gets easy, days stack up and the guilt goes soft the way guilt does. Read it then. Remember what the silence cost, and who paid it, and that the man who wrote this forgave *me* for failing him and never once mentioned your name, because to him you weren't worth the ink."

Odile's hand hovered over the envelope. Didn't touch it yet. "Why give it to me? If it's not absolution."

"Because Tom believed in provision." Wendell's voice steadied. "He stockpiled supplies for strangers. He cached evidence for a future he wouldn't see. He left food and fuel and a note reading *for the crew who finishes this*. Generosity was his whole religion, and I'm not going to let you serve your sentence starved of it just because you don't deserve it. That's not mercy. That's inheritance. You carry his words into those hearings and you read them until they're worn soft, and you testify, and you pay what you owe — not to me. To him. To the river. To Ansel's sister's valley. To every town downstream that drank what the company poisoned while you kept their secrets."

Odile picked up the letter. Held it in both hands like something living, or something breakable, or both. "Will you visit? At the trial?"

"No."

"Wendell—"

"After. Maybe. In years. If the accounting balances." Wendell moved to the door, then stopped, hand on the latch. "But I'll write. Once a season. To tell you how the river's doing. Because that's what Tom would have done, and somebody in that cell should hear about open water in spring."

Behind her, she heard the sound of paper unfolding. Then a long silence, and within it, faintly, the first break in Odile Marchetti's voice that Wendell had heard in twenty years of knowing her.

"Wendell." Barely a whisper. "Tell me about him. Someday. Tell me who he was on the water."

Wendell opened the door. Gold light spilled in from the corridor, and down on the dock the lanterns were catching, one by one, and the smell of Sefa's cooking was climbing the hill.

"Someday," Wendell said. "When you've earned it."

She stepped out and pulled the door shut behind her, and stood a moment in the corridor with her eyes closed, breathing, letting the weight of the room settle into her shoulders where she could carry it. Then she went down toward the light and the lanterns and her family, and behind her, through the barred window, the river ran on toward the sea, carrying everything it carried, indifferent and generous, the way it always had.

Tomorrow the capital courier would come. Tomorrow the hearings would begin, and the company's walls would start coming down, and Odile Marchetti would spend years paying a debt that could never fully clear. But tonight there was a meal on the dock, and a banner over the wheelhouse, and a letter in a prisoner's hands that said, in a dead man's steady script: *Keep living in miles.*

Wendell walked toward the lanterns.
