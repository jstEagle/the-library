## 37. Refit

The yard smelled of cedar shavings and tar, and Bo Kellerman had not been this happy in six years.

He stood in the slanting morning light of the dry dock with a mug of Sefa's coffee going cold in his hand and looked up at the Coriander's naked hull — planking stripped back to the ribs, her belly open to the sky like a patient on a table. She looked smaller without the water holding her. Older. Honest.

"Third plank from the garboard on the port side," Priya said beside him, notebook open, pencil moving. "That's where the ice got in. You can see the bruise."

"I see it." He did. A long gray shadow running through the wood like a vein, where the river ice had struck them during the run home and the old oak had flexed and held and never quite forgotten it. "She took that hit for us."

"She took every hit for us," Priya said. "We owe her."

Bo drank his coffee. Below them, Wendell was already at work, small and precise at the base of the ways, sorting fasteners into coffee tins by size — a ritual as old as their partnership. Wendell sorted; Bo built. That had been the arrangement since the first week, when he'd come aboard with two duffel bags and a broken marriage behind him and she'd handed him a wrench instead of questions.

Three years ago, in another shipyard, they'd buried Tom Lockridge's empty coffin because there was nothing to put in it. Today they were rebuilding the boat Tom had loved, plank by plank, and Bo had finally stopped pretending that was a coincidence.

* * *

The salvage rights money had come through eleven days after the hearings opened — an interim disbursement against the eventual settlement, enough to clear the Coriander's debt to the bank in full and still leave something in the account. Bo remembered Wendell reading the letter from the claims office twice, then folding it very carefully along its original creases and putting it in her breast pocket, over her heart, like a man tucking away a photograph.

"That's it," she'd said. "She's ours. Outright."

"She always was," Sefa had said from the galley doorway. "We just owed other people for the privilege."

Now the privilege was paid for, and the work could begin in earnest. They'd hired two men from Reprieve — brothers, quiet, sons of a fisherman whose nets had come up silverless for three seasons — and Priya had drawn up a refit plan so thorough it ran forty pages, indexed. But the heart of it, the part everyone circled around without naming, was the compartment.

Bo found himself standing over the hatch cover on the second afternoon, crowbar in hand, not cutting.

It was a simple weld. He'd have done it himself, once — four beads, maybe twenty minutes' work, clean seam. Instead some frightened man or pragmatic one had laid it down in the dark of the old voyage, sealing away rope and a broken carabiner and a brass nameplate reading T. LOCKRIDGE, and the Coriander had carried it through three years of freight runs while Bo stood on the very deck above it, drinking his coffee, telling dirty jokes, living his life directly on top of a grave.

"You don't have to be the one," Wendell said behind him.

He turned. She stood at the foot of the ladder with sawdust in her hair and her sleeves rolled, watching him with that flat river calm she wore like armor. But her hands were still.

"I'm the engineer," Bo said. "It's my hull."

"It's your choice." She climbed two rungs, no farther. "Tom would've hated a shrine. You know that. He'd have wanted the space for spare parts."

"He'd have wanted us to stop carrying secrets in the bilge," Bo said. "Which is what this is. Even empty, even opened — the fact that we welded something shut down here instead of grieving it topside. I want it open. I want light in it. I just—" He looked at the weld again. The bead was ugly, rushed, done by someone whose hands were shaking. His own had been steadier lately than they'd been in years, and he understood now why steadiness mattered: because some jobs you only get one pass at. "I want to do it right."

Wendell nodded once and went back up the ladder. From above, her voice drifted down, casual as weather: "Sefa's making the good bread tonight. For after."

For after. As if the opening of a steel seam were a funeral, or a wedding, and either way there would be bread.

Bo set the torch.

* * *

The plasma cutter sang its thin high note, and the seam opened like a mouth.

He cut slow, cooling the metal between passes so the plate wouldn't warp, and the crew gathered the way people gather at a birth or a fire — not close, but present. Wendell at the rail above with her arms folded. Sefa with flour still on her forearms. Priya holding the lantern though the sun was high. Ansel had come out from the valley for the week, sleeping on a cot in the harbormaster's spare room, and she stood at the edge of the group with her cap in her hands. Even the brothers from Reprieve paused at their sanding.

The last inch of weld parted. The plate came free with a groan and a breath of cold air from below — stale, mineral, the smell of standing water and old iron and three closed years.

Bo lowered himself through.

The compartment was exactly as he'd found it that night outside High Water Country, except that now he came with a lamp instead of a headlamp and dread instead of duty. Rope, coiled neat — Tom's splice work, Bo recognized the whipping on the ends, nobody else aboard tied off like that. The carabiner, gate sprung, one side ground smooth where it had scraped stone. And the nameplate, face-down, which Bo turned over with a thumb and immediately wished he hadn't, because touching it made it real all over again.

There was more. He'd been half-blinded with grief the first time, working fast before Voss or Ansel might come below. Now, by lamplight, he saw what else the dark had kept: a tin cup, dented. A paperback swollen with water and dried into a brick. A coil of copper wire. And a canvas tool roll, unrolled flat, every pocket empty except one, which held a single chisel worn short as a thumb.

Tom's tools. Tom's book. The ordinary archaeology of a man who'd expected to need none of it again.

Bo sat down in the bilge among it and let himself shake, quietly, the way he'd learned to do alone in engine rooms since he was nineteen. Above him the hull ticked in the sun. Nobody called down to hurry him. After a while, footsteps crossed the deck overhead — Sefa's heavy tread, Wendell's quick light ones — and moved on, giving him what the boat gave everyone eventually: room.

When he climbed out, blinking, Wendell met him at the hatch coaming.

"We're not welding anything shut ever again," Bo said. "On this boat. That's my condition. Whatever comes aboard, whatever we find — it sees daylight."

"Agreed," Wendell said, and shook his hand on it, formal as a contract, and Bo understood that for her it was one.

* * *

They argued about the memorial shelf for two days, which Sefa said was how this family said grace.

Priya wanted it documented — photographs, measurements, an archival record, because some things deserved to survive even if the boat didn't. Ansel wanted the nameplate engraved with dates, and had to be gently reminded there was no death date to engrave, only a night the river took and never gave back. Sefa wanted a shelf you could put a cup of coffee on, "because what's a memorial you can't sit with?" The brothers from Reprieve said nothing and sanded planks and listened, and Bo caught the younger one wiping his eyes with a rag that had more varnish than tears in it, and decided he liked them both forever.

In the end it was Wendell who settled it, the way she settled most things: by standing in the middle of the argument until everyone ran down, and then saying something so plain it sounded obvious.

"The compartment stays a compartment," she said. "Same size, same place. We line it in cedar instead of bare steel. We put his things in it, and we leave the hatch open — hinged, glass-fronted if Priya can find glass that won't break in a lock passage. It's not a shrine. It's part of the boat. When we're working, we work around it. When someone needs to sit with him, there's room for one person and a coffee cup." She looked at Sefa. "Both things can be true."

Sefa baked the good bread that night anyway.

Bo did the joinery himself. Cedar lining, fitted tight, each board rubbed with oil until it glowed like something alive. He mounted the nameplate on the forward bulkhead of the little space, upright where a man could read it kneeling. The rope went in coiled, the carabiner on a small hook, the drowned paperback propped open to its best surviving page — a chapter heading, barely legible, *The Long Portage*, which made Priya laugh wetly when she catalogued it. The tool roll went in last, rolled properly for the first time in three years, and the short chisel stayed in its pocket because Bo could not make himself move it and no one asked him to.

The glass front came from a salvaged skylight off a decommissioned ferry, and Priya spent an entire evening fitting it with gasket rubber until it would seal against green water. "So he rides flooded decks dry," she said. "For once."

They mounted the hinge assembly on a Sunday morning with no ceremony at all, which was the correct amount. Bo tested the door — it swung silent and true — and then stepped back and let the compartment stand open in the daylight of the rebuilt hold, cedar glowing, brass bright, Tom's name catching the sun.

Wendell looked at it a long time. Then she reached into her breast pocket, took out the folded letter she'd been carrying since Tom's camp — the unsent one, the one she'd given Odile to read in custody and then, after the reading, taken back — and did not put it inside.

"Not yet," she said, catching Bo's look. "I'm not done needing it. But it lives aboard now. Permanently. When I'm done with it, that's where it goes." She touched the glass once, lightly, the way you'd touch a shoulder. "Deal, Tommy?"

The hold said nothing, the way holds do. But the light through the new skylight fell across the nameplate just then, straight and warm, and Bo chose — deliberately, the way he now chose most things about grief — to take that as an answer.

* * *

The planking went in over three weeks, and Bo had never been happier doing hard work in his life.

New white oak from a mill upriver, quarter-sawn, stacked and stickered in the yard and smelling like a forest floor. He and the Reprieve brothers steamed and bent the frames, and Wendell drove fasteners with a mallet in a rhythm you could set a watch to, and Sefa fed the whole yard off a camp stove — fish stew, cornbread, a plum cake on the day they rolled the first new strake. Ansel stayed through it, learning to caulk seams with oakum and a mallet, her hands blistering and callusing over until they looked like a boatwoman's hands, which Bo suspected was the point. She talked about her sister's valley the way she planted things there: steadily, in rows, certain of spring.

The Coriander came back together around her wound. Where the ice had bruised her, the new wood went in proud and pale, a bright seam of different grain that would darken to match within a season — visible, if you knew to look, of exactly where she'd been hurt and exactly how she'd healed. Bo pointed it out to the younger Reprieve brother, who ran his palm over the joint and nodded slowly, the way men nod when a lesson has arrived in wood instead of words.

"She'll always show it," Bo said.

"Good," the young man said. "My dad says a scarred hull reads the water better."

His dad wasn't wrong. Neither was the river.

By the fourth week the systems went back in — new shaft bearing, rewound motor, the fuel tanks boiled out and lined. Priya re-rigged the wheelhouse wiring and installed, without asking anyone's permission, a proper archive drawer beneath the chart table: fireproof, locked, holding copies of everything — the ledger, the recovered log impressions, the beacon transmission records, Ansel's true manifest, and one photograph of six exhausted people on the dock at Reprieve, taken by the harbormaster's clerk the morning the town stood between the crew and the net. History, filed where it would steer by.

The debts were gone. The hull was sound. The paint went on the last week — Sefa picked the color, a deep river green with a red boot-top, "because she should look like where she's been" — and on the day the final coat cured, the whole crew stood on the dock in the late light and looked at her floating again, riding easy, whole.

"She's heavier," Ansel observed.

"No," said Wendell. "She's ballasted right."

Bo heard the difference. Anyone who'd spent years listening to engines could hear it: it wasn't weight, it was trim. Everything aboard finally sitting where it belonged.

* * *

They launched her formally on a gray-gold morning with half of Reprieve on the docks, and the brothers' father played fiddle, badly and wonderfully, and Sefa threw bread off the stern for reasons she declined to explain beyond "tradition," though Bo was fairly sure she'd invented it that morning.

But it was the night before, with the yard empty and the tide due at dawn, that mattered most to Bo.

He'd come back for his tools and found Wendell already aboard, sitting on the deck plates beside the open memorial shelf with a mug of tea gone cold, talking. Low. Unhurried. The kind of talking that isn't meant to be overheard, and Bo didn't — he stood in the companionway shadow and waited, and caught only fragments: *you'd have laughed at the color... Priya found your chapter... miles, Tommy, I'm still counting them.*

Then Wendell opened the canvas roll, took out the short chisel, and set it on the deck beside her, and worked in silence for an hour by lamplight — not building anything, just being aboard, keeping company with the dead the way you keep company with the living. When she finally rose and stretched, she found Bo in the shadows without appearing surprised.

"Launch is at eight," she said.

"I know. I was getting my tools."

"Mm." She passed him, paused at the companionway, and said, without turning: "You gave him a good place, Bo. Better than a hillside with a stone. He's where the work happens." Then she went ashore into the dark, and Bo stood alone in the rebuilt hold of the Coriander, in the smell of cedar and fresh paint, next to a small lit shelf where grief had finally been given a job.

At dawn they launched her, and the river took her back like it had missed her.

They were warping out past the breakwater, the fiddle fading astern, when the mail launch came alongside flying a courier's pennant — official business, priority flag at the masthead. Priya took the envelope, read the first line, and went very still in a way Bo had learned to fear more than any sound the glacier ever made.

"Wendell," she said. "You'll want to see this. The hearings start Monday — and they've summoned *us*. All of us. There's a name at the bottom of the order I think you should read yourself."
