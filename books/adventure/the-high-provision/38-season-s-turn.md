## 38. Season's Turn

The news broke on a Tuesday, which Priya would remember later because Tuesdays were the day she sorted the archive's duplicate files, and she was elbow-deep in scanned manifests when Sefa came up from the galley holding her phone out like it was something that might bite.

"Turn on the radio," Sefa said. "Or the television. Or whatever we have. The river's on everything."

Priya wiped her hands and took the phone. The headline was simple enough to fit above the fold: *THESSELY RIVER SLATED FOR FEDERAL PROTECTION FOLLOWING EXPEDITION EVIDENCE*. Below it, a photograph of the Coriander at the Reprieve dock, taken the night of the standoff — Wendell at the rail, Bo beside her with his arm in a sling he refused to wear properly, and behind them all, blurred by the photographer's long lens, the townspeople who had stood between the harbormaster's men and the crates.

She read the article twice. Then she read it aloud, because some things needed voices attached to them, and Sefa stood there with flour still on her knuckles while Priya recited the words: core samples confirming contamination dating back eleven years; internal documents showing the company had known; the name of the man who had signed the orders — the same man who had spent six years as harbormaster of Reprieve, signing off on freight manifests, shaking hands with fishermen whose nets came up empty.

"They're calling it the Lockridge Provision," Sefa said quietly. "The bill. Did you see that? Somebody in the capital put Tom's name in the title."

Priya hadn't seen that. She set the phone down on the chart table, face up, so the light from the screen caught the photograph, and for a moment neither of them said anything. Out in the harbor the water did what water does — moved, glittered, carried a gull past the pilings without any awareness that the world had shifted overnight.

"He'd have hated the attention," Sefa said finally. "And loved the protection."

"That sounds right," Priya said.

* * *

The archive had started as a filing problem and become something else entirely.

In the weeks after Reprieve — after the hearings began, after the subpoenas, after Voss walked into the federal building on his own two feet with a lawyer he'd hired himself and testified for nine hours — Priya had inherited every document the expedition had produced or uncovered. The real manifest. The ledger with its payments to the harbormaster and the signature reduced to a single letter. The ghost text she'd recovered from Tom's log by raking lamplight across the binding impressions, page after page of entries in a dead man's hand. Ansel's confession, signed. Odile's statement, dictated from custody, unsparing in a way that had surprised everyone who thought they knew her.

The officials wanted copies. The newspapers wanted everything. Two documentary producers had already called the Coriander's radio, and one of them had used the phrase "the human story" four times in a single sentence.

Priya was an archivist. She had been one before she was a deckhand, back when her life had been climate-controlled rooms and acid-free folders and the quiet conviction that information deserved better than neglect. She knew exactly how this worked. Documents given to the press became fragments; fragments became narratives; narratives became whatever the loudest voice needed them to be. By winter, "the High Provision story" would be a shape with no interior — a hero, a villain, a glacier, a boat. The specifics would fall away like ballast.

So she built the archive the way she'd been trained: provenance noted on every item, chain of custody documented, originals sealed against the day the courts or the historians needed them. But she also made decisions no training manual covered, sitting alone in the refitted hold with the memorial shelf at her back — Tom's lantern, his broken carabiner, the nameplate polished now until the letters T. LOCKRIDGE threw small reflections — and those decisions were hers alone.

Some things went into the public record. The contamination data, first and foremost, because data was the weapon and weapons left in private drawers protected no one. The company's internal memoranda. The testimony transcripts. The photographs of the poisoned tributary where Ansel's sister's village had stood, the ones that showed the water running orange in a way no caption could soften.

Other things she sealed.

Ansel's sister's name, for instance. It appeared in Ansel's written confession, in the margin note explaining why a woman who had never met Wendell Lockridge had signed on to an expedition run by people she half-suspected were criminals: *because the company destroyed her sister's village and I wanted leverage.* The name itself — Priya had asked Ansel about it once, on the portage, when they were both too tired to lie — belonged to a woman who had died in a flood of contaminated runoff, and whose death was now going to be cited in briefs and quoted in articles and argued over by lawyers who never learned how to pronounce it.

Priya sealed that file. Not because the truth didn't belong in daylight — it did, all of it — but because there was a difference between evidence and a person, and the archives she'd worked in were full of people who had been flattened into exhibits. A monument, she decided, was not a headline. A monument was a name spoken correctly, by someone who meant it, in a place chosen for the purpose. She wrote to Ansel and asked what she wanted done, and Ansel wrote back a single line: *Her name goes home. I'll take it there myself when the valley's safe to walk.*

Until then, the file stayed sealed, and Priya logged the seal in the register with the same rigor she gave everything else, because an archive with exceptions nobody documented was just a drawer full of secrets.

* * *

The letter from the National Rivers Commission arrived on a Thursday, forwarded through three offices and arriving creased and coffee-ringed, which Priya found oddly reassuring — bureaucracy, at least, remained honest about its own messiness.

They wanted the expedition records. Formally, this time, with reference numbers and a request for "complete documentation pertaining to the Thessaly evidence." They also wanted her, specifically: a line near the bottom noted that the Commission understood a former archivist had prepared the materials, and would appreciate her assistance in authenticating them for the legislative record.

Bo found her reading it on the foredeck and lowered himself onto the hatch cover beside her, favoring the shoulder Voss's injury had indirectly saved and his own stubbornness had nearly ruined.

"Well?" he said.

"They want everything."

"You going to give it to them?"

"I'm going to give them authenticated copies," Priya said. "The originals stay with me until the hearings close. After that—" She paused. This was the part she'd been turning over for days, the part that felt less like archiving and more like philosophy. "After that, I think some of it goes to a proper repository. A university, maybe. Somewhere with climate control and a reading room and rules about who can touch what."

Bo snorted. "Sounds lonely."

"It's not lonely. It's permanent. There's a difference." She looked downriver, toward the delta, where the water they'd fought through in the calving season ran wide and calm now, carrying barges and fishing skiffs and, if the bill passed, the promise of staying clean. "You know what I did before this boat?"

"Filed things."

"Preserved things. There's a difference there too." She smiled to take the sting out of it. "I spent six years keeping records that nobody ever looked at. Beautifully organized, perfectly catalogued, completely inert. And then I ran away to a riverboat because I wanted my work to matter to somebody living." She tapped the letter. "This is what that looks like, apparently. Mattering. It looks like paperwork."

"Everything worth doing looks like paperwork eventually," Bo said. "Ask any engineer. The boat is ten percent welding and ninety percent forms."

They sat with that for a while. Down the dock, Sefa was teaching two harbor kids to gut fish, demonstrating with the patience of someone who had once taught a daughter the same thing in a kitchen very far from this water. Priya watched her hands move — slow, deliberate, correcting without criticism — and thought about what Sefa had told her over dinner a hundred miles upriver, in another life: that she had lost a daughter and chosen a floating family on purpose, because grief needed somewhere to go and kitchens were as good a place as any.

That interview was in the archive too. Sefa had agreed to it with her eyes open, and Priya had transcribed it faithfully, and it was one of the sealed files — not because it was secret, but because it wasn't evidence. It was testimony of a different kind, the kind that belonged to the person who gave it, and Priya had begun to think the highest duty of an archivist wasn't preservation at all. It was discernment. Knowing which truths were load-bearing and which were sacred, and refusing to confuse the two.

"Wendell know about the Commission letter?" Bo asked.

"She will tonight. Crew meeting."

"Those are getting shorter," Bo observed. "Used to be we'd argue for hours. Now everybody just says their piece and votes."

"That's what happens when you almost die together a few times."

"Four times," Bo said. "I counted."

* * *

The crew meeting happened at the galley table, which had absorbed more history in one season than most tables manage in a lifetime. Wendell sat at the head with the letter flat in front of her, and Priya watched her read it the way she watched Wendell do everything — with the particular attention of someone who had spent months learning to read this woman's silences the way Wendell read water.

"The Commission wants the records," Wendell said. "All of them. And they want Priya to certify them under oath."

"Under oath," Sefa repeated, tasting the words. "You'll be sworn in. In a courtroom."

"In a hearing room," Priya corrected. "Courtrooms have judges. Hearings have microphones and bad coffee."

"And questions," Bo said. "Lots of questions. About all of us. What we saw, what we did, why we kept going after Reprieve when the destination went up on the board." He shrugged with his good shoulder. "I've got answers. They're just not flattering ones. Mostly the answer is 'we're stubborn' and 'we liked each other.'"

"The answer is we voted," Priya said. "All six of us. That's the whole story. Everything else is decoration."

Wendell had gone still in the way Priya had learned meant she was listening to something underneath the conversation. When she spoke, her voice was even. "There's a question in that letter you haven't answered yet. I can see the shape of it."

Priya nodded. "They want to know if I'll release the personal materials. Testimony about the crew. The interviews I recorded. Ansel's confession includes things about her family that have nothing to do with the contamination. My notes include things about—" she glanced around the table, "—all of us. Grief, mostly. Why we were each on that boat."

"And?" Sefa said gently.

"And I told them I'd certify the evidence and refuse the rest. They can subpoena me if they want. I'll comply with a lawful order. But I won't volunteer people's private grief to a legislative record so some committee chairman can use it as color in a floor speech." She met Wendell's eyes. "Tom's log is evidence. Your brother's letter to you is not. Those are different categories, and I intend to keep them different, and if that costs the Commission some sympathy in the press, then the press can write about the water instead of the widow-adjacent trauma, which frankly they should have been doing all along."

The table was quiet. Then Bo laughed — a short, surprised bark — and said, "Widow-adjacent," like he was filing the phrase away for future use.

Sefa reached over and squeezed Priya's wrist. "Your daughter would be proud of you," she said, meaning herself, meaning the family, meaning the thing they'd all stopped being embarrassed about saying out loud sometime during the descent through the ice.

Wendell simply nodded, once, and Priya understood it for what it was: the highest approval the captain issued, rarer than praise, worth more than hazard pay.

"There's one more thing," Priya said. She'd been saving it, and now was the time. "The Commission asked who should be named in the protective designation. The formal name of the protected corridor. They suggested the Lockridge Provision, since it's already in the draft bill."

Wendell's jaw tightened. "He'd hate that."

"He would," Priya agreed. "Which is why I told them to name the river instead. Just the river. The Thessaly Wild and Scenic Corridor, or whatever they end up calling it. No heroes in the title. The river doesn't need a hero's name on it. It needs to be left alone." She paused. "But I recommended the interpretive marker at Reprieve carry his name. And the cache site, if they ever let people up there. Small plaques. Names spelled right. The kind of thing a person reads standing still, in the cold, and remembers."

Wendell looked at her for a long moment. Whatever moved behind her eyes, she kept it off her face, but her hand came to rest flat on the table, over the spot where the ship's log had lain during a dozen meetings, and she said, "Do it," in the voice she used for setting a course.

* * *

That night, Priya worked late in the hold.

The memorial shelf glowed under the small lamp Bo had wired for it — his idea, executed with an engineer's economy, a single warm bulb on a dimmer so the light could be raised for cleaning and lowered for remembering. Tom's lantern sat center stage. The carabiner lay beside it, snapped clean, the break still sharp-edged after three years. The nameplate. And now, added last week at Wendell's instruction, a framed copy of the letter — not the original, which lived in the sealed archive, but a facsimile in Tom's slanting hand, legible enough that anyone standing at the helm could read the line Wendell had pointed to when she hung it: *Keep counting miles. Count them yours.*

Priya opened her laptop and the external drive and worked through the evening's task list: cross-referencing the Commission's exhibit list against her master index, flagging discrepancies, drafting the certification statement she'd sign under oath. Routine work. Sacred, in its way, though she doubted anyone at the Commission would recognize the liturgy — checksums and hashes and chain-of-custody forms, the modern equivalent of a monk illuminating a margin.

Near midnight, she reached the last folder in the queue: the one she'd labeled, in her own private taxonomy, *MONUMENTS.*

It held the things that were neither evidence nor secrets. Ansel's sister's name, and the village coordinates, and the photograph of the orange water annotated in Ansel's handwriting: *she would have been twenty-nine this year.* Sefa's interview transcript. Bo's account of finding the welded compartment, which he'd given freely but which Priya had sealed anyway, because a man's grief discovered secondhand in a dark bilge was his to spend, not hers to circulate. Her own field notes, unedited — including the entry from the night watch when Voss talked in his sleep, and the entry from the portage when Ansel told her about the sister, and the entry from the confluence vote when six people chose truth over survival and Priya had written, hands shaking with cold and certainty: *If we die tomorrow, record that we chose this. Nobody dragged anybody.*

She read that last entry twice. Then she did the thing she'd been circling for weeks, the decision that had crystallized somewhere between the Commission's letter and the lamp-lit shelf.

She created a new collection within the archive. Not public, not sealed — *dedicated.* Available, on request, to the people in it and the people they named. Ansel could send her sister's name anywhere she wished, whenever the valley was safe to walk. Sefa could give her transcript to her daughter's memory however she saw fit. Bo could show his bilge account to young engineers someday, if it helped them understand that boats are held together by more than welds. The material would exist, preserved, findable, honored — but it would move at the speed of trust rather than the speed of news.

Some truths belong in daylight, she typed into the collection's scope note. Some deserve monuments instead of headlines. The archivist's job is knowing which is which, and having the courage to be wrong occasionally, and the humility to reopen the file when someone proves you so.

She saved it, backed it up twice, and sat for a moment in the lamplight with the drive humming softly in the dark — a sound Bo would have identified immediately, that faint electronic purr, though Priya thought of it differently. It sounded, she decided, like provision. Like a storehouse stocked against winters nobody had predicted yet.

Above her, footsteps crossed the deck — Wendell, taking her last turn before bed, checking lines out of habit even in a safe harbor. Priya listened to the rhythm of it and thought: this is what I'm preserving, really. Not the documents. The walk. The habit of care. The woman who measures life in miles, still counting, still here.

Her laptop chimed. An email, timestamped 11:58 p.m., from an address she didn't recognize but whose signature block she did: the Office of the Senate Subcommittee on Waterways. The message was short. The subcommittee chair had seen the draft bill. The Lockridge Provision language had survived committee. And — the line that made Priya sit up straight — the chair requested that "the archivist responsible for the expedition records" appear in person when the bill came to the floor, and further requested her recommendation on "an appropriate public commemoration," to be discussed at a preliminary meeting scheduled for Friday morning.

A public commemoration. They were asking her — the keeper of sealed files, the guardian of monuments-over-headlines — to help decide how a nation would remember.

Priya read it three times. Then she looked up at the memorial shelf, at Tom's lantern throwing its small steady glow, and thought about what she would say. Not the river's name on the bill — that was settled. Something else. Something that required standing still, in the cold, and speaking a name correctly.

She began typing her reply, and outside, far upriver where the high route wound into country most people would never see, the Thessaly ran clean and dark toward the sea, carrying the season's last melt down to the waiting coast.
