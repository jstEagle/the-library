## 39. Open Water

The ice went out of the Thessaly on a Tuesday, and by Wednesday morning the river was running clean.

Wendell Lockridge stood at the rail of the *Coriander* and watched the last pans of winter drift past the dock at Reprieve, white shapes turning slowly in the current like pages of a book being read aloud by someone patient. The water beneath them was the color of bottle glass. A year ago she had come down this same stretch with her hands bleeding through their bandages and the route closing behind her like a mouth, and she had thought — she had been almost certain — that she would never see it from this side again. Not alive. Not whole. Not with the river looking at her the way it looked now: open, indifferent, forgiving in the way that only water can be, which is to say completely, and without ever saying so.

"You're going to miss the tide if you keep standing there being poetic," Sefa called from the galley door, a dish towel over one shoulder and steam rolling out behind her like a signal flag.

"There's no tide on a river, Sefa."

"There's a moral equivalent." She pointed the towel at her. "Pancakes wait for no one."

Wendell smiled and let herself have one more minute. One more minute of standing where Tom had stood the last spring he was alive, when the ice went out early and he'd said, *Look at it, Wen. Clean as a whistle. Best water of the year.* She could hear him say it. That was the strange arithmetic grief had finally settled into — not absence but presence, not a hole but a passenger. He rode along now. He'd asked her to, in the letter, in his own handwriting, in ink gone brown at the folds: *Keep living in miles, Wen. You were always better at distance than duration. Don't waste my share.*

She hadn't wasted it.

* * *

The helm of the *Coriander* had been rebuilt twice since the calving fields — once after the ice took the starboard rail, once after Bo decided, in the way Bo decided things, that the whole console deserved better than patchwork. But there was one addition that predated both, and it hung where Wendell could see it every hour of every watch: a frame of plain pine, glass polished, holding a single page of handwriting.

Tom's letter. Or rather, a copy of it, made in Priya's careful archivist's hand because the original lived in a climate-controlled drawer in the state archive now, catalogued as evidence, Exhibit Forty-One, the document that had cracked a mining company open like a geode. Wendell had kept the original for exactly as long as the hearings required, and then she had given it up, because Tom had never written it for a drawer. He'd written it for her. So Priya had transcribed it faithfully down to the coffee ring in the corner — "the coffee ring is load-bearing," Priya insisted, deadpan, and nobody argued — and Bo had built the frame, and Sefa had sewn the little leather corner guards, and Ansel had sent a strip of silver birch bark from the valley she was rebuilding, tucked behind the glass like a pressed flower.

The letter rode at the helm. That was the arrangement. That was the inheritance.

"Course looks good," Priya said, coming up the companionway with the chart case under her arm. She moved easier these days. The scar from the serac ran along her forearm and she'd stopped hiding it under her sleeve sometime around midwinter, which Wendell understood was its own kind of arrival. "Channel's shifted maybe forty meters east below the Kestrel Narrows. Spring scour. We'll want to eyeball it before we commit."

"We'll want to do more than eyeball it."

"I know. I said eyeball. I'm leaving room for your process."

Wendell laughed. A year ago she hadn't known she still could — laugh with her whole chest, without checking first whether the moment permitted it. The crew had taught her back into it the way you teach a stiff joint: slowly, daily, without making a ceremony of it.

Below, she could hear Bo singing to the engine. Bo always sang to the engine on departure day — something low and tuneless, half hymn, half argument — and the engine, an old girl rebuilt three times over with parts salvaged from a vault above the calving fields, sang back. Tom's spare parts. Tom's fuel. Tom's foresight, stockpiled in the dark against a day he wouldn't see, labeled in his blocky hand: *for the crew who finishes this.* They had finished it. And the provision, Wendell had learned, did not stop providing just because the crisis passed. The High Provision had paid off the *Coriander*'s debts through salvage rights; it had refloated four other boats in the fleet; it had put food in the pantry of every family in Reprieve whose wells the company had poisoned. Provision, it turned out, was not a place. It was a practice.

"Captain?" Priya again, quieter. "You ready?"

Wendell looked at the framed page. *Keep living in miles.* Then out at the water, high and bright and running hard for the sea, carrying everything downstream except what they were about to carry up.

"Yeah," she said. "Cast off."

* * *

They took the eastern channel below the Narrows, because of course they did, and because Wendell had spent the winter drawing it in her head until it was less a route than a sentence she could recite.

The scour had done exactly what she'd guessed. The old channel was silted to within a foot of the surface — a gravel bar lying in ambush for any captain fool enough to trust last year's chart — and the new cut ran deep and fast along the collapsed bank, threading between two drowned alders. Odile's charts, the ones from three years ago, had marked all of this impossible. Odile's charts had been wrong about a great many things. Wendell took the *Coriander* through at three-quarters throttle with Priya calling depths and Bo easing the engine through the turns like a partner who knew the dance, and when they came out the far side into open water again, the whole boat exhaled at once.

"Forty meters east," Priya confirmed, marking the chart. "You called it."

"River told me."

"The river talks to you and the engine sings to Bo. This boat has too many relationships."

"It's a social vessel," Bo called up the engine hatch, and Sefa's laughter rolled out of the galley and across the water, startling a pair of mergansers off the bank.

That was the thing Wendell kept noticing, mile after mile, the thing that felt new even though nothing about the boat or the river or the work had fundamentally changed: the sound of them. Three years ago the *Coriander* had run quiet, quiet the way a sickroom is quiet, everyone moving gently around the captain's grief like furniture they didn't want to bump. Now the boat was loud. Loud with argument about card games and cargo weights, loud with Sefa teaching Priya to make flatbread and Priya teaching Sefa to read old scripts, loud with Bo's engine songs and Voss's letters — Voss wrote letters constantly now, from his reduced sentence, long careful letters that Priya read aloud at dinner, each one ending with some dry observation that made even Sefa snort. Loud with life. Wendell had spent years measuring her days in miles because miles asked nothing of her heart. The miles asked different questions now. She found she didn't mind answering.

At noon they stopped at a shingle bar Wendell knew — Tom's bar, the one he'd fished from, the one where his old partner Hal used to meet him before Hal stopped speaking to her, before Hal stood on the dock the day they came home with the evidence and gripped her hand so hard she felt the bones shift and said only, "Took you long enough," which between the two of them was a eulogy and an absolution both.

Sefa laid out lunch on the stones. The sun was warm enough to sit in. Upstream, faint as a memory, the mountains held their snow.

"He'd have liked today," Bo said. Bo didn't say Tom's name often, but when Bo did, it landed clean.

"He'd have complained about my line choice through the Narrows."

"He absolutely would have," Priya agreed. "Then he'd have stolen seconds of whatever Sefa made."

"There are no seconds," Sefa said. "There is only enough, and gratitude."

But she made extra. She always made extra. Wendell ate hers looking upstream, at the water coming down from the high country, from the moraine, from the glacier that had tried to bury the truth and failed, and she felt the strangest sensation, one she was still learning to name: not peace exactly. Readiness. The feeling of a road that goes somewhere you chose.

* * *

They made the confluence by late afternoon, where the Thessaly met its cold tributary and the two waters ran side by side for a hundred meters, tea-colored and glacial-white, refusing for a while to mix. Wendell slowed there, as she always did. It was where they'd voted. A year ago, six people standing on a pitching deck with the ice booming behind them like artillery, and every single one of them — even Voss, even quiet, frightened, complicit Voss — had said *go*. She brought the boat to a drift now and let the current turn them, and the crew came up on deck without being asked, because they knew this stop, they'd made it together twice since, and it had become what river people call a station: a place you mark.

"To the provision," Sefa said, raising a tin cup of coffee.

"To the crew who finishes this," Wendell answered, because that was how the toast went now, and it always got her, right under the ribs, in the place where Tom lived.

They drank. The two rivers braided together at the bow and became one water, and Wendell turned the wheel north.

* * *

The upper river opened ahead of them like a held breath released.

This was the water that had nearly killed them all — the meltwater reach, fast and cold and treacherous, where she'd navigated by ear, head tilted, listening for the pitch of water over stone the way a musician listens for a note gone flat. She listened now. The river sang the same song it always sang in spring, but cleaner, somehow. Or perhaps it wasn't the river. Perhaps it was that she no longer heard it through the gauze of a missing night, through the static of a memory torn out at the root. She knew now what happened on the ice. She knew she had been there, that she had fought, that she had failed, that the guilt had broken something in her mind the way ice breaks a hull — not maliciously, just overwhelmingly. And she knew that Tom had forgiven her before she ever remembered needing forgiveness, which was either the cruelest or the kindest thing anyone had ever done for her, and she had stopped trying to decide which.

The protection act had gone through in March. The upper Thessaly was designated wild water now — no claims, no tailings, no company men with ledgers full of bought silence. There were signs at the access points. Priya had helped draft the language. *This river is protected by law and by the people who proved what was done to it.* Wendell liked the second clause better than the first. Laws could be repealed. The people who stood on a dock in Reprieve between a corrupt harbormaster and a burning barrel of evidence — those people did not repeal.

Ansel sent word sometimes from the valley. The village was rebuilding — slower than hope wanted, faster than despair predicted. Last month's letter included a photograph: the first new roof beam going up, half the town underneath it, Ansel in the middle with her face turned up and laughing. On the back she'd written: *For the shelf. Tell Bo the pump design worked. Tell Wendell the water runs clear here now.* Wendell had pinned it beside the helm, next to the frame. The wall there was becoming a kind of archive itself — not of evidence, but of continuance. Priya would have approved. Priya believed some truths deserved monuments instead of headlines, and Wendell believed the monument could be a bulkhead wall on a working boat, accumulating photographs and birch bark and a child's drawing that some kid in Reprieve had pushed into Sefa's hands at the dock: a crayon boat, a crayon river, five stick figures and one angel drawn with wings, labeled TOM in enormous wobbling letters. That hung there too. Of course it did.

"High Water Country," Priya announced, tapping the chart. "Sound navigation from here."

Wendell killed the depth finder. Some habits weren't superstition; they were respect. She tilted her head and let the river speak — the low mutter of gravel, the higher clean note of the deep channel, the white-noise hiss where a lateral bar shed water. Her hands moved before her thoughts finished, small corrections, a conversation older than language. Behind her she heard the crew settle into watch rhythm without a word spoken, and she thought: *this is what he meant.* Not the cache, not the evidence, not even the letter. This. A boat moving through hard water with the right people aboard, every mile chosen, every mile shared.

Miles, not years. She had measured her life in miles to survive the years. Now the miles themselves had become the point — not distance as escape, distance as arrival, over and over, every day the boat moved.

* * *

They camped that night on the bench above the calving fields.

The glacier was quieter this year. Calving season had come and mostly gone while they were fitting out downstream, and what remained was the deep slow voice of the ice settling, a sound Wendell had once heard as menace and now heard as weather — vast, indifferent, honest. The vault site was a half-mile up the moraine, marked now with a cairn and a bronze plaque that Priya had designed and the historical society had cast: THE HIGH PROVISION. HERE, EVIDENCE WAS KEPT FOR THE RIVER. Wendell hiked up alone at dusk, boots crunching on scree, and stood at the cairn while the light went gold and then rose and then blue.

"Hey, Tommy," she said. She didn't talk to him often. She saved it for places that deserved it.

The wind came down off the ice, cold and clean, smelling of stone and ancient water. Somewhere below, the *Coriander* sat at anchor with her riding lights on, small and stubborn and lit from inside, and Wendell looked at her the way you look at a house where everyone you love is sleeping.

"I kept the letter at the helm," she said. "Hope that's all right. Figured you'd rather ride shotgun than sit in a drawer." A pause. The glacier groaned, long and low, like agreement. "We're taking her up tomorrow. All the way. Full crew, full hold, no false manifest. First honest cargo this boat's ever carried north." She smiled, wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, briskly, the way you do a chore. "Hal says hi. Well. Hal said 'took you long enough,' which from Hal is basically a sonnet."

She stayed until the stars came out over the ice field — the same stars, she realized, that had watched the worst night of her life, and had not intervened, and had also not left. That was the deal the sky offered. She'd learned to take it.

When she got back down to the boat, Sefa had kept a plate warm. Of course she had. Wendell ate on deck under the lights while the river talked to itself in the dark, and Priya spread tomorrow's chart on the hatch cover, and Bo came up wiping grease from her hands, and for a while nobody said anything at all, because nothing needed saying. The route was open. The window was wide. The water ran clean from the glacier to the sea.

"First watch, Captain?" Priya asked.

"No," Wendell said, and surprised herself. "Wake me at midnight. I'll take the night water." Because the night water was the best water — cold, glassy, the boat running on starlight and engine-song, the miles unspooling like thread from a spool, hers, all of them hers, chosen and shared and going somewhere.

She slept that night without dreaming of ice. She woke at midnight to Priya's hand on her shoulder and the smell of coffee and the framed letter catching the lamplight at the edge of her vision, Tom's handwriting steady as ever: *keep living in miles.* She climbed into the helm chair, took the wheel in both hands, and pointed the *Coriander* north into the open dark, into the open water, into the open year.

Three years ago, Wendell Lockridge had measured her life in miles because miles were all she had left. Tonight, with her family asleep behind her and her brother's words at her shoulder and the whole bright river running home beneath her keel, she measured it in miles still —

but now the miles were hers, chosen, shared, and there was no end of them in sight.
