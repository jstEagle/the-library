## 1. Nothing but Sand and a Debt

The flats outside Puerto Salado were the kind of empty that made a man hear his own heartbeat, and Rafael Villalobos had been listening to his for three days.

He rode with his hat brim low and his canteen sloshing a quarter full, following a trail that most men would have called no trail at all. But the thief — a wiry, pigeon-chested drifter named Anselmo Quintero, though Rafael had only learned the name an hour ago from a ferry clerk who talked like a man being paid by the word — had made two mistakes. First, he'd stolen from Rafael. Second, he'd stolen the wrong thing.

Not money. Rafael had money, or something adjacent to it, folded into a saddlebag in the form of a debt ledger with his name at the top and a number beneath it that made moneylenders go quiet and thoughtful. Quintero could have had that ledger, could have sold it back to the lender for a finder's fee, could have made himself a small legend in the low country. Instead he'd cracked open Rafael's footlocker at the boarding house in Salado and taken the only thing inside that wasn't worth anything at all.

A brass compass, dented on one side, its glass clouded like a cataract eye, its needle long since dead.

Rafael's partner had carried it for eleven years. Marisol Duarte had carried it through sandstorms and salvage runs and one very regrettable season on a river barge, and she had carried it into the High Rivergate on the dive that killed her, and it had come back out of the water in her pack when they brought up what was left of her. Rafael had kept it on a shelf for three years, dusting it on the first of every month, and he had never once asked himself why, because the answer was a room he did not enter.

So when he'd come back from the lender's office to find his window jimmied and the shelf bare, he had not stopped to report it. He had saddled up and ridden, and he had not stopped riding since.

The flats gave way to scrub, the scrub to a dry wash, and the wash to a line of mesquite, and beyond the mesquite, squatting in the heat shimmer like something that had crawled out of the ground and decided to stay, stood the Last Drop waystation.

It was less a building than an apology for one — mud brick and tin, a water tower leaning at an angle that suggested it was considering lying down. A mule dozed in the shade of the porch. A dog did not so much as lift its head as Rafael rode in, which struck him as professionally negligent.

Quintero's horse was tied at the rail. That was mistake number three, if Rafael was counting, and he was.

He dismounted, tied off, and climbed the two warped steps. Through the doorway he could see the waystation's single room: a counter, a shelf of tinned goods arranged with the optimism of a man who had never actually sold any of them, and Anselmo Quintero himself, sitting at a table with a plate of beans and the compass open in front of him, turning it slowly, frowning at it, the way a man frowns at a lock that won't take his key.

"Those beans any good?" Rafael said from the doorway.

Quintero came out of his chair so fast the plate went with him and the beans went with the plate. He ended up backed against the far wall, holding a fork the way a man holds a fork when he has just realized it is not a knife.

"You're — " Quintero swallowed. "You followed me. Three days. Across the flats."

"You left tracks," Rafael said. "You also left a boot print in the boarding house mud with a split heel, and you paid your ferry toll in coins from a purse with a hole in it, so the toll man remembers you fondly. You're not a bad thief, Quintero. You're just a thief with a schedule."

"I didn't take any money," Quintero said quickly. "I want that understood. I looked. There wasn't any money."

"There wasn't," Rafael agreed, and crossed the room, and held out his hand. "The compass."

Quintero looked at the compass. Then he did something Rafael had not expected, which was to hesitate — not the hesitation of a man reluctant to give up loot, but the hesitation of a man who has seen something and doesn't know how to say it.

"It's broken," Quintero said. "I mean — I thought it was broken. I took it because I figured, brass, brass sells. But it's not broken. Look."

He set it on the table between them, carefully, like setting down something hot.

Rafael looked. The needle — the dead needle, the needle that had not moved in three years, that Marisol's brother had sworn was rusted through when he'd inspected the wreck salvage — the needle was pointing. Not wobbling. Pointing, steady as a sermon, off the table, off to the east and slightly south, toward the wall, toward the flats beyond it, toward nothing.

"That's east," Rafael said, because his mouth wanted to say something while the rest of him caught up.

"It's been pointing east since I opened it in Salado," Quintero said. "I walked east for an hour this morning to see if it wanted something out there. It kept pointing. Past east, if you follow me. Down, a little. Like it's pointing at something under the ground. Or under the water." He wiped his palms on his trousers. "There's no water out there. There's nothing out there. I've been on these flats my whole life, señor, and there is nothing east of here but the Salar and the old coast road and — " He stopped. His face did something complicated. "And the Rivergate," he finished, very quietly, the way people say the names of things they've been taught not to say.

The room got smaller. Rafael was aware of the waystation keeper — a round, placid man who had been pretending to inventory tins since Rafael walked in — going very still behind his counter.

Rafael picked up the compass. It was warm. That was the part that sat wrong in him, the part that would sit wrong for a long time after. Brass in the shade of a mud-brick room did not get warm. The needle did not come back from the dead. Dead things stayed dead; he had built a whole life, these three years, on that principle.

"Where did you open it first?" he asked.

"The boarding house. Right after I — " Quintero had the grace to look embarrassed. "Right after I took it. It was pointing then too. I figured somebody'd rigged it. A joke. A tracker." He paused. "Was it a tracker? Is somebody coming?"

"Somebody came," Rafael said. "I'm the somebody. Give me your fork."

"My — "

"You stabbed at me with it in your imagination just now. I'd like it not to be a surprise later."

Quintero handed over the fork with the meekness of a man who had already lost everything he owned that mattered, and Rafael sat down at the table, and the waystation keeper, emboldened, slid a second plate of beans onto it without being asked and said, "On the house," in the tone of a man who hoped the house would survive the afternoon.

Rafael ate a few beans. He did this partly because they were, surprisingly, good, and partly because his hands wanted something to do that wasn't shaking. He turned the compass over. On the back, worn nearly smooth, was the scratch Marisol had put there with a diver's knife the week before the Rivergate run — two letters, *H.R.*, and beneath them, smaller, a number: *6*.

He had looked at that scratch a hundred times. He had assumed it meant nothing, the way scratched initials mean nothing, the way dead compasses mean nothing, the way partners mean nothing once they're at the bottom of a drowned city with the gates closed behind them.

Six. Six tides.

He didn't know why that number landed in his chest the way it did. He only knew that Marisol had been the best navigator he ever knew, and that she had scratched a number into a dead compass a week before she died, and that the dead compass was now pointing, warm as a living thing, toward the one place on the coast that all of them had sworn — sworn, with salt and spit and a bottle of terrible rum — never to touch again.

"You knew her," Quintero said. It wasn't a question. "The one who owned this."

"No," Rafael said.

Quintero waited.

"Yes," Rafael said. "I knew her."

The keeper cleared his throat and retreated into the tins. The dog on the porch sighed like a man at the end of a long shift. Out east, past the wall, past the Salar, under the old coast road, the High Rivergate sat drowned in its green water, holding whatever it was holding, and something in Rafael's saddlebag-shaped chest understood, with the slow cold certainty of a tide coming in, that the compass had not come back to life by accident.

Marisol had not carried a navigation tool. Marisol had never once in her life carried anything that wasn't exactly what she said it was, except this.

"Quintero," Rafael said. "You said you walked east this morning. How far did you get before you turned back?"

"Past the Salar. To the coast road." Quintero rubbed his arms. "Señor, there were ships out there. Salvage ships. Three of them, big ones, rigged for deep work, anchored off the shallows where nothing's ever worth anchoring for. And a fourth one flying a flag I didn't know, all black and green, and men on her deck who looked at the shore like they were measuring it for something." He hesitated. "There was a town past the road, too. Harbor town. I was going to stop there for water when I saw your dust behind me and thought better of it."

"What town?"

"Corazón del Muelle," Quintero said. "Though everybody just calls it the Muelle."

Rafael set the compass down on the table, pointing east, and looked at it for a long moment. The needle did not waver. It had somewhere to be, and it was patient about it, the way the sea is patient, the way debts are patient, the way the dead, apparently, were patient after all.

Three days ago he had been a man chasing a thief to get back a piece of brass worth nothing, so he could put it back on a shelf and go on not entering that room. Now he was a man sitting in a waystation with a warm compass pointing at his worst memory, a rival fleet anchored off the coast, and a number — *six* — scratched into the past like a countdown he'd been too grief-blind to read.

He pushed the plate away.

"Keeper," he said. "Fill my canteens. Both of them."

"You're going out there," Quintero said. It wasn't a question either. Everyone in the room was suddenly very interested in questions that weren't questions.

"I'm going to the Muelle," Rafael said. He stood, took the compass, and — after a moment's thought — dropped it into his coat's inner pocket, against his chest, where he could feel it. Warm. Patient. Pointing. "The compass can wait for the rest."

He was three steps from the door when Quintero spoke again, small and hopeful and doomed.

"Señor Villalobos? Do you need a hand? I'm very good at being unnoticed."

Rafael stopped. He looked at the pigeon-chested thief, at the ruined beans, at the dog now hopefully awake on the porch. Every sensible bone in his body said: ride alone, ride light, ride away.

But the compass was warm against his chest, and it was pointing at a town where three years ago he had left behind four people who had every right to hate him, and something in him — the same something that had never once listened to his sensible bones — said that a man about to walk back into his own past might want company he could pay in beans.

"No," Rafael said, and stepped out into the heat, and turned his horse east.

* * *

He made the coast road by nightfall and saw the ships' lanterns off the shallows, strung out on the black water like a necklace someone had dropped. He did not stop to count them. He rode for the Muelle, and the compass rode with him, warm and steady, and somewhere behind his ribs an old promise he'd made over a bottle of terrible rum began, very quietly, to come apart.

The town showed itself at midnight: a scatter of lamplit docks, a crooked skyline, the smell of salt and tar and frying fish. And moored at the third pier, unmistakable even in the dark, a salvage sloop he knew down to its hull-blisters — the *Perla Brava*, patched and patched again, flying no colors at all.

His old crew's ship. Or what was left of it.

Rafael sat his horse at the top of the ramp for a long moment, the compass warm against his heart, and told himself he was only passing through.

He was, as it turned out, a liar.
