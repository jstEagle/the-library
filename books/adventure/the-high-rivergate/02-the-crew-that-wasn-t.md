## 2. The Crew That Wasn't

The compass needle didn't waver. That was the problem.

Rafael had spent eleven days following it across the flats, through the salt-bitten scrub country where the wind combed everything flat and honest, and in all that distance the brass needle had held one bearing like a dog with a bone. Compasses drifted. Good ones lied a little, bad ones lied a lot, and every ranger worth his boots knew to trust the sun and the stars over any needle some smith had floated in a bowl of oil. But this one — Elena's compass — pointed true as scripture at something that should not have existed.

Because what it pointed at was water. And it never stopped pointing.

He crested the last dune ridge at dusk and there it was: Puerto Salado, a harbor town stitched together from ship-timber and stubbornness, its masts poking up against the bruised orange sky like reeds after a flood. Fishing boats, salvage scows, one fat merchant brig riding low with cargo. The smell of tar and fried fish rolled up the beach and hit him like a memory he hadn't agreed to have.

The needle swung — for the first time in eleven days — and settled on the harbor itself.

"Well," Rafael said to nobody, because talking to himself had become a habit somewhere out on the flats and he'd stopped fighting it. "That's not ominous at all."

* * *

Puerto Salado had three taverns, and Rafael picked the wrong one first.

The Gull's Rest smelled of old beer and older grievances, and the barkeep took one look at his ranger's badge and slid him water instead of whiskey, which told Rafael everything he needed to know about how rangers were regarded here. He drank the water. He asked his questions anyway, gently, the way you ask questions in towns where everybody owes somebody money.

A thief named Corvo Maren. Slim, quick, a face like a ferret who'd learned to read. Had anyone seen him?

The barkeep's eyes flicked left. Just a flicker, just a hair, but Rafael had spent nine years reading liars on the open sand, and that flicker was as good as a signed confession.

He found Corvo two taverns later, in the back of the Saltcellar, hunched over a cup of something cheap with three friends who evaporated the moment Rafael's shadow fell across their table. The ferret-faced man looked up, went pale, and made the intelligent decision to freeze.

"Corvo Maren." Rafael set the wanted notice down between them. "You stole from a dead woman's estate."

"I stole from an *estate*," Corvo said. His voice cracked on the second word. "I didn't know she was — I bought the lot off a widow in Marrow Bend, fair and square—"

"You sold everything else within a week. The watch, the rifle, the letters. You kept this." Rafael laid the compass on the table, and Corvo flinched from it like it was hot. Interesting. "Why?"

Corvo's mouth worked. "It wouldn't sell," he said finally. "Buyers kept looking at it and walking away. One man turned it over twice and left without a word. Another crossed himself." He laughed, high and unhappy. "And it *hums*, ranger. At night. You don't keep a thing that hums."

"It doesn't hum."

"No," Corvo agreed miserably. "Not for you, maybe."

Rafael pocketed the compass. It sat warm against his chest, warmer than brass had any right to be, and he decided — firmly, deliberately — not to think about that.

"You'll come to the magistrate's office in the morning," he said. "There's a fine. You'll pay it."

"And if I run?"

"Then I'll catch you again, and I'll be annoyed, and my day will already have been ruined by other things." He stood. "Trust me. Today is not your lucky day to test me."

Corvo, wisely, stayed put.

* * *

The trouble was, the compass didn't stop pointing.

Rafael noticed it that night in his rented room above a chandler's shop. He'd set it on the sill, and the needle lay steady through the wall, through the street, through the harbor water, at whatever waited below. He turned it east. The needle swung patiently back. He turned it upside down, which every ranger knows confuses even the finest instrument, and the needle simply kept pointing down-and-out, calm as a cat.

At midnight he gave up on sleep, dressed, and walked down to the harbor because the alternative was lying in the dark listening to a dead woman's compass hum.

That was when he saw the boat.

She was small — thirty feet of patched hull and honest scars, tied up at the end of the public pier with her paint peeling and her name half-sanded off the transom. Someone had tried to scrape *VILLALOBOS* from her stern and given up halfway, so the letters read *VILLAL* in flaking gold, and beneath them someone else had painted, in cheap black, the word *AND SONS?*

Rafael stopped walking.

He knew that hull. He'd helped caulk it. He'd argued about the color of the trim — Odile had wanted green, Sefa had wanted green but would only admit it by insisting on a different green, and Tomás hadn't cared as long as it floated. Three years ago that boat had been the finest little salvage runner on the coast, and now she sat at the end of a pier like a dog tied outside a shop, waiting for owners who'd stopped coming.

The cabin light was on.

He should have walked away. Every sensible instinct he owned said walk away, book passage north, follow the compass alone, solve this mystery the way he solved everything — by himself, at a distance, where nobody could be let down. But his feet carried him down the pier anyway, boots knocking on old planks, and he stood at the rail of the *Villal* feeling like a ghost at his own funeral.

Through the cabin window he saw Sefa first.

She'd gone grayer at the temples, broader in the shoulders, and she was elbow-deep in the guts of some pump assembly, cursing it in three languages with the fluent creativity of a woman who had been cursing machinery for twenty years. Across from her, Odile sat at the chart table doing accounts, because Odile was always doing accounts; her ledger was open, her pen moved in small precise strokes, and her face wore the expression of a woman adding up numbers she already knew were bad.

Tomás wasn't there. Then Rafael saw him — asleep in the corner bunk, still in his diving shirt, a bottle of something empty on the floor beside him, his breathing slow and careful in the way of a man managing a hangover before it fully arrives.

Three years. Three years since Rafael had stood in this cabin and told them he was done, that the debt was his to carry alone, that they were better off without a captain whose name was on a creditor's list from here to the capital. Three years since Odile had looked at him with those level gray eyes and said, quietly, "You don't get to decide we're better off. That vote has never been yours." And he'd gone anyway, because going was easier than watching them sink with him.

Sefa looked up. Their eyes met through the glass.

For a long moment neither of them moved. Then she wiped her hands slowly on a rag, came to the door, and opened it, and the light spilled out across the planks between them like a border neither knew how to cross.

"Well," she said. "The ghost brought luggage."

"Sefa."

"Odile!" she called over her shoulder, without taking her eyes off him. "Our former captain is standing on our pier looking like a man who's come to apologize or arrest us, and I genuinely can't tell which."

"Neither can I," said Odile's voice from inside, dry as driftwood. She appeared in the doorway a moment later, ledger still under her arm, and looked Rafael up and down with the unhurried appraisal of a quartermaster pricing damaged goods. "You look terrible."

"Thank you."

"That wasn't a compliment. It was an inventory." She stepped aside. "Are you coming aboard, or are you going to stand there being tragic at us all night? Because we've work in the morning."

Rafael came aboard. The deck creaked under him in exactly the way he remembered, and that was somehow the worst part — that the boat remembered him when the people might not.

Tomás woke on the third creak. He sat up, saw Rafael, and his face did something complicated and then settled on nothing at all, which was worse than anger. Anger, Rafael could work with. Nothing was a locked door.

"Ranger now," Tomás said. Not a question.

"Ranger now."

"Good pay?"

"Steady pay."

"Hm." Tomás lay back down and turned to face the wall, and the conversation was over, and Rafael understood that it had been a conversation about everything except what it was about.

Sefa put a kettle on. Nobody asked if he was staying long enough for tea; the kettle simply went on, and that small domestic fact hit Rafael harder than any welcome or rejection could have. They made tea for ghosts, apparently. Or maybe — and this thought arrived unwelcome and warm — maybe some part of them had never fully closed the door either.

"So," Odile said, settling across the chart table from him, pen capped, which meant business. "Eleven days ago a compass walks out of a dead woman's estate and starts moving toward this harbor. Two days ago, Sefa starts dreaming about drowned bells. Yesterday, Tomás swears the tide tables are wrong — says the water's behaving like something under the bay is breathing. And tonight you show up on our pier." She folded her hands. "Start with the compass."

So he told them. All of it — the theft, the chase, Corvo Maren and his humming brass, the needle that pointed at water and never wavered. He set the compass on the chart table between them, and all three of them leaned in despite themselves, because curiosity is a stronger glue than grievance.

Elena's compass. They all knew whose it was. Nobody said her name, and the silence where her name should have been filled the whole cabin.

"The High Rivergate," Sefa whispered. Her fingers hovered over the needle without touching it. "It's pointing at the Rivergate. Raf, that wreck's a graveyard. We swore—"

"We swore a lot of things," Tomás said to the wall.

Before anyone could answer, footsteps sounded on the pier — unhurried, confident, the steps of someone who owns whatever ground she's standing on. A knock followed, crisp as a pistol shot.

Odile raised an eyebrow at Rafael. "Expecting anyone?"

"No."

"Then it's either creditors or opportunity," Sefa said, "and creditors don't knock that cheerfully."

Odile opened the door, and Captain Ilsa Reyes filled it — silver-streaked braid, coat cut for a richer ship than any of them had ever crewed, and a smile like a knife being shown politely. Her gaze swept the cabin, catalogued everything including Rafael, and came to rest on Odile.

"Quartermaster Odile Vasseur. Engineer Sefa Okonkwo. Diver Tomás Reyes — no relation, more's the pity." Her smile widened. "And well, well. Ranger Villalobos. The coast gets smaller every year." She stepped aboard uninvited, produced a sealed contract from her coat, and laid it on the chart table directly atop the compass, as if she'd known exactly where both would be.

"I'm told you're the only crew mad enough and good enough for what I need," she said. "Six tides from now, the monsoon closes the High Rivergate until spring. In five days, a rival fleet sails to strip it bare. Which leaves us—" she tapped the contract once, "—a very narrow window, and a very large sum, for whoever dives the drowned city first."

Silence. The kettle began, softly, to sing.

Ilsa looked around the cabin, at the peeling paint and the half-scraped name and the four people pretending they weren't already family, and smiled her knife-smile again.

"So," she said. "Shall we talk terms?"
