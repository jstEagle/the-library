## 3. Six Tides

Odile had learned, over eleven years of running a salvage crew, that the worst conversations always started with someone setting something down on the table between you. A chart. A ledger. A pistol. Whatever it was, it meant the person wanted you to look at it instead of at them.

Captain Ilsa Reyes set down a chart.

They were in the back room of the Saltcellar, the tavern on the harbor road where the floorboards were stained with forty years of spilled rum and the ceiling hung so low that Tomás had to duck through doorways like an apology. Odile sat with her back to the wall — habit — with Sefa on her left picking apart a bread roll and Tomás on her right radiating the particular silence of a man who has been asked to sit in the same room as Rafael Villalobos and is doing it under protest.

Rafael stood by the window. He hadn't sat since they'd come in. Three years gone and he still didn't know how to be in a room without looking like he was about to leave it.

"High Rivergate," Ilsa said, tapping the chart once. She was a broad woman with a captain's voice and a gambler's eyes, and she wore her gray like a decoration she'd earned. "You all know it."

"We swore off it," Sefa said, around bread.

"You swore off it when Villalobos did." Ilsa's gaze slid to Rafael, then back. "He's here now."

"That's not the same as unswearing," Tomás said. First thing he'd said in twenty minutes. The room got a little colder for it.

Odile let them talk. That was her job, mostly — letting people talk until they said the true thing underneath the loud thing. She watched Ilsa instead, and Ilsa watched her right back, because Ilsa Reyes had never once pretended she didn't know who actually ran this crew.

"The rival fleet leaves Port Amara in two days," Ilsa went on. "Voss's outfit. Six ships, new dive bells, and enough money behind them to buy the harbor master, the harbormaster's wife, and the wife's dog. They'll reach the Gate in five days if the weather holds."

"It won't," Sefa said.

"It won't," Ilsa agreed. "Monsoon season comes early this year. Every pilot from here to the delta says so. Which means Voss gets maybe one clean window at the wreck before he has to run for shelter — and we get six tides. Six tides where the water drops low enough to work the flooded gates, and after that the Rivergate is undivable until next year. If there is a next year. Half those coast towns are talking about pulling up stakes for good."

Silence. Somewhere out front, the tavern keeper dropped a mug and swore about it with real artistry.

"And the pay?" Odile asked.

Ilsa smiled, slow, like she'd been waiting for exactly that question and was pleased Odile had asked it first. "The client wants the Gate's strongroom opened and its manifest recovered. Pays four hundred crowns on completion. Two hundred in advance."

Sefa stopped chewing. Tomás's jaw tightened. Four hundred crowns was three years of lean seasons. Four hundred crowns was a new boat, new rigging, debts paid, winter survived without eating the emergency stores and then apologizing to the emergency stores.

"And my cut," Rafael said quietly, from the window, "covers what I owe."

"There it is," Tomás muttered.

Odile held up one finger. It was amazing, how much quiet a single finger could buy when you'd been signing the pay chits for a decade. "Say the rest plainly, Captain. You didn't sail all the way here to offer us money. Money you can offer anyone. What do you want?"

Ilsa leaned back, chair creaking, entirely unbothered at being seen through. "I want the crew that opened the Marisol wreck in nine fathoms with a cracked bell and half a winch. I want the crew that mapped the Serpent Shoals in six days flat. I've hired other crews since. They're fine." She said *fine* the way other people said *adequate*, which from Ilsa was a devastating review. "But the High Rivergate isn't a fine job. It's the kind of job that kills fine crews. So: I hire you four, together, or I don't hire you at all. Villalobos included."

"Why?" Tomás demanded. "Because he's such a treasure to work with?"

"Because he's the only diver alive who's been inside the upper gates," Ilsa said, "and because your crew doesn't function without him, whatever you'd like to pretend. I watched you work the Marisol. Four hands moving like one thought. Then he left, and I watched you take jobs you should have refused, and lose men you shouldn't have lost." Her voice gentled, which somehow made it worse. "I'm not offering charity, Tomás. I'm offering arithmetic."

Tomás stood up so fast his stool skidded. "You want arithmetic? Here's some. Last dive we did together, he—" He stopped. His whole face closed like a shutter. "Vote," he said to Odile. "I vote no. I'm going to get a drink." And he ducked out the door, all six feet of him folding under the lintel, gone.

* * *

The vote happened an hour later, in the crew's rented room above the chandlery, because Odile insisted votes happened properly — sitting down, sober, with everyone present. It was a rule she'd made after the incident with the eel-boat contract, which nobody talked about.

Tomás sat by the window with his arms crossed. Sefa sat cross-legged on the bed, turning her bread crust over in her fingers like a worry stone. Rafael stood by the door, because of course he did.

"I'll say my piece first," Odile said. "Then we vote. Sefa?"

"He goes," Sefa said immediately. "Obviously. Look — I'm angry too. I've been angry for three years, I've gotten very good at it, I have excellent form. But the Gate needs an engineer who can read old tide-work, and that's me, and I'm not doing it with strangers who'll panic in the dark. I'd rather dive with a man I'm furious at than a stranger I don't know how to be furious at yet." She shrugged. "Also, four hundred crowns. Also, I miss him. Both things are true. Vote yes."

She looked at Rafael when she said the last part. Rafael looked at the floor.

"Tomás?"

"No." Flat, immediate. "Not because of the money. Not even because of the Gate." He finally turned and looked at Rafael straight on, and Odile saw Rafael take it like a blow he'd been expecting and hoping for anyway. "Because the last time we dove together, I trusted him with my life, and he spent it like it was spare change. I don't care what debt he's paying. Some ledgers aren't his to balance."

Nobody spoke. Downstairs, the chandlery bell rang the hour.

"Odile," Sefa said softly. "It's yours."

And there it was. Eleven years of being the tiebreaker, and it never got lighter. Odile looked at each of them in turn. At Tomás, whose anger was the honest kind, built on something real. At Sefa, whose hope was also honest, and just as heavy. At Rafael, standing by the door like a man waiting to hear whether the ground would hold.

Here was the thing Odile knew and almost never said aloud: she had spent three years being furious at Rafael Villalobos, and the fury had kept her warm the way a coat keeps you warm, but a coat isn't a home. The crew had limped. The jobs had gotten worse. She'd told herself they were fine, and Ilsa Reyes had just said *fine* out loud in front of everyone, and it had sounded exactly as thin as it was.

Wary hope against old resentment. She weighed them like ballast.

"Here's what I know," Odile said. "I know the Gate is dangerous and the money is real and the monsoon won't wait. I know Tomás is right about what happened, and I know Rafael has never once explained himself, and I know that if we take this job, he will explain himself or I will throw him off the boat myself, contract or no contract." Rafael's head came up. She let him wonder. "And I know that I have missed having four hands move like one thought more than I have enjoyed being angry. Which took me three years to admit, and I resent every one of them."

She put her hand flat on the table.

"Yes. We take the job. All conditions attached."

* * *

Conditions, as it turned out, were delivered in writing, because Odile believed in paperwork the way priests believe in scripture. She wrote them out that night at the Saltcellar's corner table while Sefa negotiated terms with Ilsa across the room — advance split, hazard shares, the exact definition of *completion* — and Tomás drank one beer very slowly, and Rafael sat across from Odile and watched her write.

"Read them," she said, sliding the page over.

He read. His lips moved slightly; he'd never been able to help that. *One: full account of the last dive, given to the crew entire, before the Gate.* *Two: no decisions below without quartermaster's sign-off.* *Three:* —

"Three says I eat last," Rafael said.

"Three says you eat last until Tomás says otherwise. It's a symbolic provision. Very important legally."

Something crossed his face that wasn't quite a smile but lived on the same street. "Fair."

"Four: you don't leave. Not mid-job, not after, not at some dock in the rain because it felt easier. If you're going to go, you say it to our faces first." She tapped the line. "That one's the load-bearing clause."

Rafael was quiet a long moment. Outside, the harbor lamps came on one by one, and the tide turned with a sound like the town exhaling.

"I don't deserve these terms," he said.

"No," Odile agreed pleasantly. "Sign anyway."

He signed. And that was when the tavern door banged open and a boy in fleet livery came in dripping rain, scanning the room until he found Ilsa, and handed her a folded paper with a wax seal already broken. Odile watched Ilsa read it. Watched her face do something small and grim.

Ilsa crossed the room and laid the note on Odile's paperwork, right on top of condition three, because captains also liked to make you look at the paper instead of their eyes.

"Change of arithmetic," she said. "Voss's fleet left port tonight. Ahead of the weather. They'll reach the Gate in four days."

Sefa frowned. "That's still inside our six tides."

"It's inside *three*," Ilsa said. "We sail tomorrow. On the morning tide." She looked around the table, at the four of them, at the ink still drying on Rafael's signature. "Hope you packed fast, salvage crew. Your first dive just moved up."

Odile gathered her papers, tucked the note beneath them, and caught Rafael watching the harbor lamps — watching, she suspected, the dark water past them, where something waited that had cost him everything once already.

"Well," she said, shouldering her bag. "At least nobody can say we ease into things."
