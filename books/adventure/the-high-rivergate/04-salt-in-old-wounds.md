## 4. Salt in Old Wounds

The *Marta's Promise* smelled the same as she had three years ago — tar and salt and Sefa's engine oil, a smell Tomás had once called home and now had to relearn like a language he'd stopped speaking.

He stood at the mainmast on the first morning out of Harbor Vell, coiling line the way he'd been taught at fourteen, and watched Rafael Villalobos try to be useful. It was almost funny. Almost. Rafael was a desert ranger; he walked a ship's deck like a man crossing ice, arms slightly out, waiting for the world to betray him. He'd grabbed a coil of rope within an hour of getting underway and coiled it wrong — over-under instead of under-over — and Odile had taken it from his hands without a word, uncoiled it, and handed it back. She'd done this four times before breakfast. Rafael kept trying. That was either humility or stubbornness, and Tomás knew Rafael well enough to know it was both.

"You're staring," said Sefa, appearing at his elbow with two mugs of something that claimed to be coffee.

"I'm supervising."

"You're glaring at a man's hands like they owe you money."

"They do owe me money," Tomás said, and drank the coffee so he wouldn't have to say anything else.

The trouble was that everyone else seemed determined to let the past lie where it had fallen. Odile ran the deck with her usual iron calm, assigning watches as if the roster hadn't been torn apart by one man's walking away. Sefa chattered at Rafael about tide-engines and salvage law and the proper way to grease a winch, forgiving him in real time, out loud, constantly, which was worse than shouting. And Ilsa Reyes watched it all from her quarterdeck with the expression of a woman who had bought a barrel of gunpowder and was waiting to see which end lit first.

Tomás didn't want forgiveness. He wanted an accounting. And Rafael wouldn't even look at him long enough to start one.

* * *

By the third day, they'd found a rhythm despite themselves. The *Marta's Promise* was running south along the coast toward the Rivergate shoals, and there was work — always work. Sefa had the engine singing. Odile had the stores counted twice. And Rafael, it turned out, could be taught.

He learned knots in an afternoon. He learned the watch bells by the second night. When Sefa's winch jammed on the morning of the fourth day, Rafael was already there with a pry bar before anyone called for one, and when the bar slipped and took a strip of skin off his palm, he swore in desert dialect — something about lizards and their mothers — and kept working one-handed until Sefa bandaged him mid-task, scolding him the whole time.

"See, this is what I don't understand," Sefa said loudly, wrapping gauze around Rafael's hand while Rafael held a fitting steady with the other. "You leave us for three years, no letter, nothing, and then you come back knowing how to hold a fitting. Who taught you that? You were a ranger. Rangers don't hold fittings."

"Prison has workshops," Rafael said.

The deck went quiet. Even the wind seemed to lean in.

"Three years?" Sefa said. "You were — Rafael, we thought you were drinking yourself dead in some border town. We looked. Odile spent half our float hiring people to look."

"I know." Rafael's voice was flat, careful, like a man stepping on river stones. "I'm sorry."

*Sorry.* Tomás set down the block he was carrying harder than necessary. Sorry was a word for stepping on someone's foot. Sorry was not a word for three years, for the dive they'd never finished, for the empty berth across from his own that he'd had to look at every single night because nobody else would take it, because taking it would have meant admitting Rafael wasn't coming back.

He didn't say any of this. He picked the block back up and carried it forward, and if his grip left dents in the wood, well, wood forgave.

* * *

It came to a head over dinner, the way things did when everyone was too polite all day.

They ate in the cramped mess off the galley, elbows knocking, and Ilsa had joined them — she sometimes did, ostensibly to discuss the run, actually, Tomás suspected, to enjoy the tension like a fine wine. Tonight she'd brought out a chart of the High Rivergate approach and spread it between the plates.

"Slack tide comes at dawn on the sixth day," she said, tapping the drowned city's outline. "We get maybe ninety minutes of divable water. After that the monsoon swells make the gates a meat grinder. So I want the crew doing a full rig check tomorrow, and I want—"

"Why'd you really take this contract, Rafael?"

Tomás heard his own voice and didn't stop it. The table went still. Sefa froze with a spoon halfway to her mouth. Odile closed her eyes briefly, the universal gesture of a quartermaster watching cargo shift.

Rafael set down his cup. "The debt."

"The debt." Tomás laughed, and it came out ugly. "Right. The debt. A man sells everything, disappears for three years, ends up in prison, and the moment he gets out he finds us — finds *me* — and it's about the debt."

"Yes," Rafael said evenly. "That's what I said."

"Because here's what I remember." Tomás stood. He hadn't meant to stand. "I remember the last dive. I remember you saying you'd cover the lower gate while I ran the line. And I remember coming up alone."

"Tomás—" Odile started.

"No. Three years I've bitten my tongue. He wants to talk about debts? Fine. Let's talk about debts." His heart was going hard. "You left me on the bottom, Rafael. Forty meters down, current rising, and when I surfaced the boat was gone and you were gone and I swam two miles to shore thinking you were dead. Thinking *everyone* was dead. So yes, tell me again about your debt, because mine's got interest."

Rafael looked up at him, and Tomás waited for the excuse, the deflection, the smooth ranger charm that had always gotten them out of trouble and into worse. Instead Rafael just said, quietly:

"You're right. I owe you that story. And you'll have it. But not here, and not because you cornered me over dinner."

"When, then?"

"When I can stand to tell it." Something moved behind his eyes — grief, old and worn smooth like sea glass. "That's not cowardice, Tomás. That's just the truth of it."

And he picked up his plate and left the mess, and the silence he left behind was enormous.

Sefa exhaled. "Well," she said. "That could've gone better."

"It could've gone worse," Odile said. "Nobody threw anything."

"He's telling the truth about one thing," Ilsa observed, rolling up her chart, entirely too serene. "Whatever happened down there, it's eating him alive. I've hired a lot of haunted men, and that one's the most haunted I've seen who can still tie a bowline." She paused at the door. "Storm coming tonight, by the way. Barometer's falling like my opinion of this crew's morale. Rig check at dusk. All hands."

* * *

The storm arrived two hours after midnight, and it arrived angry.

Tomás was thrown out of his bunk by the first big swell and hit the deck already moving, because a body learns these things even when the heart is busy elsewhere. Rain came sideways through the hatch. Somewhere above, canvas thundered.

On deck it was black chaos — rain like gravel, wind screaming through the shrouds, the *Marta's Promise* lying over hard with her rail buried. Ilsa was at the wheel, hauling against it, shouting something that the wind shredded. The mainsail had backed and was flogging itself to death; if it split, they'd lose the rig, and if they lost the rig this far out with the monsoon building behind them, they'd lose more than the rig.

"Main halyard!" Odile bellowed, pointing. Someone had to go up and free the fouled peak halyard before the sail tore itself apart, and the someone had to be light, fast, and crazy.

Tomás was already clipping onto the jackline when he saw Rafael doing the same at the other mast step.

They looked at each other through the rain. For one stupid second, three years hung between them like the sail hanging between sky and sea.

"Take the port side!" Tomás shouted. "I'll take starboard! Don't die — I'm not done being angry at you!"

Something that might have been a laugh crossed Rafael's face, wild and wet. "Same!"

Then they were climbing.

The mast whipped like a living thing. Tomás went up by feel, hands and boots, the ship's motion slamming him against the shrouds and holding him there, and at the top the peak halyard was wrapped twice around the spreader, soaked, knotted by panic. Below him he could see Rafael doing the same work on the other side, small and stubborn in the lightning flashes, and — this was the thing, this was the thing Tomás would think about later — Rafael worked exactly the way he used to. Fast, clean, no wasted motion, checking his own knot twice before trusting it. Some part of Tomás had spent three years telling himself the man who left was a stranger wearing Rafael's face. The storm disagreed.

"Clear!" Rafael's voice, torn thin by wind.

"Clear!" Tomás answered, and felt rather than saw the sail fill and draw, the whole ship steadying beneath them like a horse settling under a firm hand.

Getting down was its own argument with gravity. Tomás lost his footing halfway and swung out over black water, one arm burning in the strap, and before he could even swear, Rafael was there — having crossed the deck on the jackline in a gale, which was either heroic or insane — with both fists in Tomás's collar, hauling him back against the mast.

"I've got you," Rafael shouted into his ear. "I've GOT you."

And Tomás, hanging in the dark with the sea trying to drink him, believed it. That was the worst part. In the middle of everything — the anger, the unanswered question, the forty meters of cold water he still carried in his chest — he believed it completely, the way you believe a rope, the way you believe a hand.

They dropped into the cockpit together, gasping, and Odile threw oilskins over them both, and Sefa appeared from below cheering as though they'd won a prize fight, and Ilsa steered them through the worst of it with a grin Tomás decided not to examine.

Later, near dawn, the storm blew itself hoarse and sulked off eastward. Tomás sat in the lee of the cabin trunk, wringing out his shirt, and found Rafael sitting nearby, equally soaked, equally wrung.

Neither spoke for a while. The sea settled by degrees, like a dog circling before lying down.

"That was good work up there," Tomás said finally.

"You too."

A pause. Rain dripped off the boom. Somewhere aft, Sefa was already arguing with her engine, which meant the world was returning to normal.

"After the Rivergate," Tomás said. "Whatever you're not ready to tell me. After the job. You'll tell it, and I'll listen, and then we'll see what's left."

Rafael nodded slowly. "After the Rivergate."

"And Rafael?" Tomás stood, joints complaining. "Next time you catch me falling off a mast, maybe do it without making me owe you anything else. My ledger's full."

The ghost of a smile. "Understood."

Tomás was halfway to his bunk, tired down to the bone but lighter than he'd been in three years, when Sefa came pelting up from below with the compass in her hand and an expression that made his stomach drop.

"Tomás — get Odile, get the captain — it's pointing *down*," she said. "It's been pointing down all night. Straight down. Through the hull. There's something under us, and it knows we're here."
