## 5. The Drowned Gate

The High Rivergate announced itself the way old griefs do — quietly, and then all at once.

For hours there had been nothing but green water and the low hump of drowned hills beneath it, and then Sefa, up in the crow's nest, called down in a voice that cracked on the second word: "Tops. I see tops."

Rafael climbed to the rail. Out of the morning haze rose a forest of spires and bell towers, black with weed, their upper stories breaking the surface like the backs of sleeping animals. The city had been tall once — a river port built on ambition and bad arithmetic — and when the engineers of a century past decided to tame the delta, the river had answered by swallowing every street they'd ever paved.

"Rivergate," Odile said beside him. She said it the way you'd say the name of a person at a funeral. "Still standing."

"Some of it." Rafael kept his voice level. Three years ago he'd stood on this same rail line with Tomás at his shoulder and made a promise neither of them had kept. *Never again.* The words came back to him now with salt on them.

Ilsa Reyes paced her quarterdeck like a woman who owned the weather and was disappointed in it. "Slack tide in forty minutes," she called. "We anchor over the Grand Arch. First descent goes as planned — Rafael, Tomás, Odile. Sefa topside with me on the lines. Anybody dies, I want it done efficiently, we haven't got time for drama."

"She's a charmer," Tomás muttered. It was the first thing he'd said directly to Rafael in two days, and he'd aimed it sideways, at the water, as if the water might agree with him.

"She's paying us," said Odile.

"That's what worries me."

* * *

They anchored in the shadow of the Grand Arch itself — an aqueduct gate taller than Ilsa's masts, its carved face of some forgotten river-warden furred green with centuries. Below the waterline, Rafael knew from charts older than any of them, that arch opened onto streets, plazas, stairways going down into districts the flood had never quite finished drowning.

Sefa checked their gear twice, then a third time out of pure nervousness, tugging straps and muttering. "Air lines are good. Weight belts good. Knives—" She patted each of them down like a fussy aunt sending children into winter. "Rafael, yours is dull."

"It's fine."

"It's *dull*. A dull knife underwater is a decoration." She swapped it for hers without asking, which was very Sefa: generosity disguised as criticism. Then she hesitated, hands hovering near his jacket pocket, where the brass compass rode against his chest. "You're bringing that down?"

"I'm bringing it down."

"You know it doesn't point north anymore."

"I know."

She looked at him a moment longer than was comfortable, then thumped his shoulder plate. "Come back up. All three of you. I've only just gotten used to hating you; don't make me renegotiate."

Tomás went over the side first, dropping through water so clear it felt like a lie. Odile followed, then Rafael, and the world above — the ship, the sky, Sefa's small waving hand — folded away behind him like a letter being closed.

* * *

The Grand Arch from underneath was a cathedral.

Sunlight fell in long amber columns through gaps in the ruined roof of the gatehouse, and fish moved through those columns like notes through music. The flooded street below them ran straight and true between buildings whose shutters still hung at their hinges, whose shop signs — painted, gilded, some still legible — swung gently in a current too faint to feel. A fountain stood in the first plaza they crossed, dry these hundred years, its stone dolphins grinning up through the murk at divers who were not its intended audience.

Nobody spoke. Their comms carried breath and the soft electronic hush of the regulators, and that was all. Some places ask for silence.

It was Odile who finally keyed her mic, and her voice came through warm and wondering. "Look at the bakery."

On their left, under a bowed awning, a bakery window still held its shelves. On one shelf, transformed by silt and time into something like coral, sat rows of bread — or the mineral ghosts of bread — preserved mid-loaf, mid-century, mid-morning.

"People just... left," Odile said softly.

"The river gave them a day's warning," Rafael said. "Maybe two. You take what matters and you go." He heard how his own voice sounded and stopped talking.

They swam on. And it was beautiful, that was the terrible part. Rafael had braced himself for ruin — collapsed streets, silt-choked dark, bones. Instead the city had been embalmed rather than killed. Lampposts stood at attention. A child's hoop leaned against a doorway where someone had set it down and never picked it up. In a tailor's window, a suit waited on its stand for a customer who had drowned or fled a hundred years ago, and the current stirred the coat's lapels so that it seemed almost to breathe.

"Gentle humor," Tomás said suddenly over the comm, apropos of nothing.

"What?" said Rafael.

"You look like a man about to cry inside a helmet. Thought I'd say something stupid before you did something sentimental." But the edge was gone from Tomás's voice, worn smooth by the place itself. "My grandmother came from here. Did I ever tell you that? Used to sing songs about it. Said the Rivergate bells could be heard across the whole delta." He paused, drifting beside a bell tower whose great bronze mouth hung open toward the surface. "She never said they'd stop ringing."

"They'll ring again if Sefa has anything to do with it," Odile said. "She'll have that tower wired for sound within the week."

And Rafael felt something loosen in his chest — not forgiveness, not yet, but the memory of what it was like to be aboard a crew, trading jokes in the quiet moments because silence shared was heavier than silence alone. For a hundred yards of flooded street, it was almost like the old days. Almost like before.

Then they found the anchor chain.

* * *

It lay across the plaza like a fallen serpent, new-forged iron bright against the old stone, running down a boulevard toward the deeper districts. Fresh. Weeks old at most. They followed it, dread arriving ahead of them, and found its brothers — six more chains, six anchors, six mooring points drilled into the stonework with modern pneumatic precision.

At the head of the boulevard, half-buried in silt, sat a diving sled of a make Rafael recognized. He turned it over. Stenciled on the hull, flaking but legible: **VOSS SALVAGE & RECOVERY**.

"No," Odile said. Not a question. A refusal.

"The rival fleet," Rafael said. His heart had gone slow and cold, the way it did before bad news. "They didn't leave port two days ago. They left weeks ago. Ilsa's intelligence was wrong — or Voss fed it to her wrong on purpose."

Tomás drifted to the nearest building and pressed his gloved hand flat against a doorframe. Fresh tool marks scored the wood. A modern padlock, rusted already, hung broken on a hasp. "They've been through here. Everything portable's gone." He swept his light across the shop interiors. Stripped shelves. Empty strongboxes lying open like slack mouths. "They weren't salvaging. They were *shopping*."

"But they're gone now," Odile said slowly. "Six anchors, but no ships overhead. We saw nothing on approach."

"So either they finished," Rafael said, "or—"

He stopped. Because the compass had woken up.

He felt it before he looked — a tug against his chest, gentle as a hooked fish, insistent as a conscience. He pulled the brass instrument from his jacket and held it out into his lamplight, and the needle wasn't pointing north, or south, or anywhere the sun had business being. It pointed straight down the boulevard, into the deep districts, trembling with something that looked horribly like eagerness.

"Rafael," Odile said. "What's it doing?"

"Pointing."

"At what?"

"Down." He turned in a slow circle. The needle never wavered. Through the plaza, past the stripped shops, along the boulevard that descended step by drowned step into neighborhoods where even the salvage lights of Voss's fleet had not reached. Toward whatever his partner had died carrying. Toward whatever the compass had always known and never said.

Tomás swam close and looked at the needle, then at Rafael, and for once there was no anger in his face — only an old, tired recognition. "Your partner marked this city on that thing, didn't she. Before she died. This whole run — Ilsa's contract, the timing, all of it — you think any of that's an accident? That compass isn't leading you, Rafael. Somebody aimed it."

The current shifted. Just slightly — a breath of colder water moving up from the deep districts, carrying with it a smell that shouldn't have existed underwater and somehow did: machine oil. Fresh machine oil.

Odile caught it too. Her head came around toward the darkness below. "Somebody's running equipment down there. Right now."

The compass needle quivered, straining against its glass like a dog at a door.

Three hundred yards down the boulevard, in the black where the sunlight columns couldn't follow, something blinked. Once. Twice. A work light. Then a shape detached itself from the shadows — a diver in black rig, no markings, no lights but the single lamp at his hip — and stood motionless at the top of the descending stairs, watching them.

He raised one hand. Not a greeting.

A warning.

Then he stepped backward off the stair into the dark, and was gone, and the compass in Rafael's hand pulled so hard toward the deep that it nearly tore free of his grip.
