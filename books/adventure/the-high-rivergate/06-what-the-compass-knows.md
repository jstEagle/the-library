## 6. What the Compass Knows

Sefa had taken apart a lot of things in her life. Engines, mostly, but also locks, clocks, a rival captain's winch (don't ask), and once — memorably — an entire carousel that a carnival man had abandoned on the flats at Low Harbor because it wouldn't turn anymore. She'd gotten it turning again in four hours and ridden it twice before he came back to claim it.

The compass was different.

She'd been at it for six hours now, in the little workshop berth off the *Kestrel's* engine room that Ilsa Reyes had grudgingly surrendered to her, surrounded by cotton swabs, jeweler's loupe, three kinds of oil, and a growing pile of very small screws arranged in careful rows like soldiers who all had somewhere important to be.

The problem wasn't getting it open. The problem was that the thing didn't want to be understood.

"Talk to me," she murmured to it, tilting the brass housing under the lamp. "You've got more gears than a clock this size should hold. You've got a gimbal inside a gimbal, which is either genius or a cry for help."

The needle swung lazily and pointed, as it always did now, not north but down-and-southeast, toward the drowned mass of the High Rivergate sitting forty fathoms beneath the swell. It had done that since they'd made landfall yesterday. Before that, back in harbor, it had pointed at Rafael with what Sefa could only describe as *intent*.

"You're showing off," she told it.

The workshop door banged open and Tomás leaned in, salt-crusted and dripping, holding a wrench like a scepter. "Sefa. Ilsa wants to know if you've cracked it yet, and if not, whether you'd like company while you don't crack it, because Rafael is doing the thing where he stands on deck staring at the water and everyone's about to lose their minds."

"I'm fine out here," Rafael's voice floated down the companionway, wounded.

"You're *brooding* out here," Tomás called back. "It's different and it's worse." He dropped onto Sefa's spare stool, peered at the disassembled compass with professional interest, and immediately reached for one of her screw rows.

She slapped his hand without looking up. "Touch those and I'll reassign your dive weight belt to ballast duty."

"You'd never. You love me."

"I tolerate you at extremely close range, which is nearly the same thing." But she was smiling, and he knew it, and she let him know she knew it. Three years of silence between Rafael and the rest of them, and somehow she and Tomás had come through it still able to bicker like siblings. Some things held. She tried not to think too hard about which things hadn't.

"So," Tomás said. "What do we know?"

"We know it's not a compass." Sefa lifted the inner mechanism free of its housing and held it to the light. Beneath the familiar card and needle, hidden under a plate so thin it might have been decoration, lay a second assembly: interlocking rings of engraved brass, each ring marked with characters none of them used anywhere in the coast charts, and each ring turning on its own axis, slow as the tide. "Look. The needle isn't pointing. The whole lower stack is *rotating* — matching something. And when I rotate this outer ring—" she turned it a quarter-turn, and the needle shivered and locked rigid, quivering like a struck string— "*that* happens. It resists. Like a lock feeling for the right key."

Tomás frowned at it. "A key to what?"

"That's the question, isn't it." She set it down gently and pulled over her notebook, pages dense with sketches. "I've been cross-referencing the engravings. The character sets aren't decorative — they repeat in patterns, threes and sevens, and there are tally marks along the rim that count *something*. Tides, maybe. Or cycles. And here—" she tapped a diagram, "—the innermost ring has a cutout. A slot. Something fits into this compass, or this compass fits into something."

"A lock and a key in one piece of brass," Tomás said slowly.

"Or a key that knows where its lock is." Sefa glanced up at him, and the humor drained out of her voice. "Tomás, I've built engines since I was twelve years old. I know what machinery feels like when it's *meant* for something. This wasn't made to find your way home. This was made to open a door. And I think the door is under us right now, forty fathoms down, and I think it's big."

* * *

They gathered in the mess that evening — the whole strange family of them: Odile at the head of the table with her ledger closed for once, Tomás picking at his stew, Rafael across from him radiating the careful politeness of two men pretending a chasm wasn't there, and Captain Ilsa Reyes standing by the porthole with her arms crossed, listening the way she did everything: completely and without appearing to.

Sefa laid the opened compass in the center of the table and told them everything. The counter-rotating rings. The tally marks that matched, when she worked backward from the monsoon tables, the great spring-tide cycles of the river delta. The slot. The needle that didn't point north but *down*, always down, patient as gravity.

"And here's the part I keep coming back to," she said. "This much precision — this much *machinery* — doesn't exist to steer a boat. Somebody engineered this to interface with something else. Something that needed a key exactly this clever. Which means whatever's down there in the Rivergate isn't just cargo or salvage." She looked around the table. "It's a machine. A working machine. And somebody wanted it locked badly enough to hide the key in the desert."

Silence. Then Odile said quietly, "Your partner. Marisol." Her eyes went to Rafael. "Where did she get it?"

Rafael had gone very still. Sefa watched him — watched the moment arrive in his face like weather. "She was contracted," he said finally. "Salvage survey work, upriver. She came back early. Said the job was wrong, said she'd found something she shouldn't have, said she needed to put it where nobody would ever look." His jaw worked. "I thought she meant burying it in the flats. I thought—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "She died three days later. Bandits, we thought. Random. They took nothing but the compass, and I chased the thief across half the desert thinking it was about money. About *sentiment*." He laughed, and there was nothing warm in it. "She wasn't hiding it from bandits. She was running it away from whoever built it. And they caught her anyway."

Nobody spoke. Down the corridor, the ship's bell marked the watch change, eight muffled strokes, and Sefa thought: *she carried this through the desert to keep it from people like Voss, and it ended up leading us straight back.*

* * *

She couldn't sleep after that, which surprised no one, least of all herself. By midnight she was back in the workshop with the compass guts spread out under lamplight, and by two she'd stopped being careful and started being honest.

Because here was the thing she hadn't said at dinner, the thing sitting in her chest like swallowed ballast.

The rings weren't just a key. When she'd modeled their rotation against the tide tables — properly, with real numbers, the kind of numbers she dreamed in — the pattern resolved into something unmistakable to anyone who'd spent her life around engines. Intake. Compression. Release. The gate's drowned archways weren't architecture. They were *ports*. The whole city-wreck sat on top of a tide-engine — a machine that drank the sea twice a day and turned its breathing into power, stored in whatever heart lay at its center. Enough power to run a fleet. Enough power to light a coastline.

Enough power, if you turned it the other direction, to *take* a coastline back.

She did the calculation twice because she didn't want to believe it the first time. The discharge cycle, run in reverse, with the gates sealed: a standing wave, a surge, a wall of water with the whole delta in front of it. Every harbor town from Low Harbor south. The place where she and Tomás had rebuilt their lives after Rafael left. The place where Odile's sister raised her kids.

Worth fortunes, Ilsa had said. Worth *anything*, Voss would say. And all of it resting behind a lock that fit in the palm of your hand.

The workshop door creaked. Odile stood there in her coat, hair loose, a lantern in one hand. "Light was still burning," she said. "Couldn't sleep either?"

"No." Sefa pushed the notebook across the bench. "Read it."

Odile read. Sefa watched her face go through wonder — genuine, bright-eyed wonder, the same look Sefa herself had worn an hour ago, the engineer's joy of seeing a beautiful impossible thing clearly for the first time — and then watched the wonder curdle, slowly, into something colder.

"Gods below," Odile murmured. "And Voss's fleet is already anchored over it."

"Voss sees treasure."

"Voss sees *leverage*." Odile closed the notebook. Her knuckles were white around it. "Whoever holds the key holds every coastal town hostage. That's not a fortune, Sefa. That's a crown."

"I know." Sefa looked at the compass lying open on her bench, all its patient rings, its needle steady as a heartbeat, pointing down at the sleeping machine. She'd spent her whole life loving mechanisms, loving the way gears meant exactly what they said and nothing else. For the first time she could remember, she looked at a machine and wished it were dumb metal. Wished it were just a compass.

"It's beautiful," she said, and hated how true it was. "That's the worst part. It's the finest piece of engineering I've ever seen, and it should never have been built, and I can't stop admiring it."

Odile put a hand on her shoulder, brief and firm. "Then admire it," she said. "And then we decide what to do with it. Together. Like a crew."

But before either of them could say anything more, boots thundered overhead — Tomás's voice, sharp with alarm, ringing down the companionway:

"Sefa! Get up here! Lights in the water — the rival fleet's boats are moving, and they're lowering divers *now*!"

Sefa snatched up the compass, slammed the housing shut, and ran.
