## 7. Boarded

Odile had kept ledgers through hurricanes, mutinies, and one memorable incident involving a cargo of live eels and a gambling debt, and she had learned long ago that trouble always announced itself the same way: a small wrongness in the rhythm of the ship. The clink of dishes that stopped too soon. A rope creaking out of turn. So when the lookout's voice came down thin and strange from the crow's nest — "Sails. Three of them. Flying parley white" — Odile was already at the rail with her slate, because numbers were her way of praying.

Three ships. Voss's fleet had been six at last count.

"He split them," Rafael said beside her, squinting into the haze off the High Rivergate's drowned towers. "Left half to circle the wreck. He doesn't need many to talk."

"He needs exactly enough to be believed," Odile murmured, and wrote *parley = theater* on her slate, because writing things down made them less like omens and more like problems, and problems she could solve.

Ilsa Reyes came up the companionway adjusting her coat, which was how Odile knew the captain was rattled; Ilsa only fussed with that coat before negotiations or funerals, and sometimes there wasn't a difference.

"Everyone pleasant," Ilsa said. "Voss loves an audience. Give him nothing to perform for."

* * *

Captain Edric Voss boarded like a man arriving at his own coronation — polished boots, pale gray coat, and a smile so carefully arranged it could have been billed as architecture. Two guards flanked him. Behind them came four more sailors carrying a crate between them, which they set down on the deck with exaggerated care.

"Captain Reyes." His voice was warm as bathwater and about as deep. "And company. The famous Villalobos crew. I'd heard you'd scattered to the winds. Grief does that to families." His eyes found Rafael and lingered, delighted. "Though I hear one of you scattered further than most."

Odile watched Rafael take that hit without moving a muscle and thought, not for the first time, that the man had learned to stand still the way other people learned to bleed quietly.

"You're flying parley," Ilsa said. "So talk."

"I'll keep it simple, then. You've recovered something from the gate. Or you will. A brass compass, old workmanship, no maker's mark." Voss strolled to the crate and knocked twice on its lid. "I'm prepared to pay for it. Generously. Enough that none of you ever have to dive again — and I include your ranger friend's rather creative debts in that offer. Yes," he added pleasantly, to Rafael, "I make inquiries. It's a hobby."

The number he named made Sefa inhale sharply. Odile didn't blink, but she did the arithmetic anyway, fast and involuntary, the way a sailor swallows against nausea. That figure would buy the whole crew out of every ledger she kept. It would buy them harbors, winters, old age.

She looked at Tomás, who was watching Voss the way a man watches a current that has already taken something from him.

"We haven't found any compass," Ilsa said.

"No?" Voss smiled wider. "Then this is awkward." He nodded to his guards, and they opened the crate.

It was not full of gold. It was full of charts — salvage charts, tide tables, and one page that made Odile's stomach drop through the deck planking: a sketch of their own ship, drawn from the waterline, with annotations in Voss's neat hand noting hull thickness, gunports, and the words *keel vulnerable amidships* underlined twice.

"The way I see it," Voss said, "you either sell me a compass you claim not to have, or in two tides my divers take it from the engine chamber while my ships make certain your vessel is too busy staying afloat to object. Parley protects us today. The monsoon protects nobody tomorrow." He spread his hands, a showman closing his act. "Two tides, Captain Reyes. I do hope you'll be reasonable. Sentiment sinks ships."

He left the crate. He left the smile. He went back over the side, and the three gray sails turned away toward the drowned city like patient animals.

Nobody spoke until the lookout confirmed they were out of earshot. Then Tomás said, very quietly, "He knows about the keel because someone told him. Nobody reads our hull from underwater without help."

"There are harbor rats in every port," Ilsa said, but her jaw was tight, and Odile noted — in ink, mentally — that Ilsa didn't quite believe her own answer either.

* * *

The war council happened in the galley, because Odile insisted all terrible plans be made near food. Fear digested better with bread.

Sefa had the compass open on the table, its needle trembling toward the floorboards, toward the gate below. "Two tides is nothing. The engine chamber sits past the third archway, forty feet down, in a current channel we barely swam through at slack water. With Voss's divers in the water and his guns on the surface—"

"So we don't fight the current," Odile said.

They all looked at her. She hadn't meant to speak yet. She had meant to sit with her slate and listen and let the fear settle into figures, the way she always did. But the figures were already done, and they were bad, and bad arithmetic had a way of loosening her tongue.

"Explain," Ilsa said.

Odile turned her slate around. On it: the tide chart, the gate's channels, the timing of the monsoon swell.

"Everyone treats the High Rivergate's currents as walls. We've been diving *between* them. But Sefa told us what the compass proved — the gate has a tide-engine, and the engine doesn't just power the city. It *shapes* the currents. The channels aren't natural. They're plumbing." She tapped the sketch. "Which means they have valves."

"That's legend," Tomás said. "The engine's been dead for centuries."

"It's not dead. It's sleeping. The compass woke something when Rafael brought it close — ask the needle." The brass point shivered against its glass, insistent, like a dog scratching at a door. "If Sefa can read the mechanism — and she's the only person alive arrogant enough to try — we can open the flood channels early. Pull the monsoon surge *through* the gate ahead of schedule."

Silence. Then Rafael, slowly: "You want to drown the wreck."

"I want to drown *his* divers," Odile said. "There's a difference, and it's measured in about ninety seconds. The inner chambers flood first. The outer channels — where Voss's people will be staging — get a riptide strong enough to spit them back out to sea. Anyone still inside the deep galleries when it hits..." She let that hang, because honesty was also a kind of bookkeeping. "We'd have one window. One breath between opening the valves and the surge reaching the engine chamber itself. Whoever goes down for the compass-key has to be out, or they drown with the city."

"And if Sefa can't find the valves?"

"Then we've spent our two tides learning a lesson about desperation."

Tomás laughed — short, sharp, not amused. "This is insane. This is the exact kind of plan that got—" He stopped. The name he almost said sat down at the table with all of them anyway. Their last diver lost, three years ago, on Rafael's final reckless call. The betrayal nobody had named aloud since.

"I know what kind of plan it is," Odile said quietly. "I was there. I did the numbers then, too, and I signed off on the dive, same as everyone. Whatever we owe the dead, Tomás, we can't pay it by being careful forever. Sometimes careful is just another word for slow."

Tomás stared at her. Something moved behind his anger — not softening, exactly. Recalibrating. The look of a man discovering his grudge has been sharing a room with grief this whole time.

"The window," Sefa said, already sketching, already somewhere else, her fingers flying across the table's edge. "If the surge comes through the third archway, the eddy behind the second pillar reverses first. Ninety seconds of slack in a place that never has slack. That's real. That's *doable*."

"And Voss's guns on the surface?" Ilsa asked.

"Let him shoot at weather," Odile said. "By the time he understands what we've done, his divers will be swimming for their lives and his charts will be worth exactly the paper they're printed on." She met Ilsa's eyes. "But we decide now. Once we open those valves, there's no closing them. We either take the key and go, or we lose everything — the compass, the payday, possibly the ship."

Rafael had been silent through all of it, turning the compass over in his scarred hands. Now he set it down flat on the table, needle straining toward the floor.

"My partner hid this thing here because of what it can do," he said. "Maybe the right move is to give Voss what he wants and let it become his problem."

"No," said four voices, including, to visible surprise, his own.

Odile picked up the compass and closed her hand around it, cold brass warming against her palm. She had spent three years keeping books for a broken crew, balancing columns of what they'd lost against what they still had, and the totals had never once come out fair. But looking around the galley — Sefa already muttering valve ratios, Tomás pulling the depth charts toward himself despite himself, Rafael leaning in instead of away, Ilsa waiting on her quartermaster's word as if it mattered —

For the first time since she'd taken this contract, the numbers felt honest.

"Then it's settled," Odile said. "One tide to prepare. One tide to dive. And when the High Rivergate opens its throat tomorrow—" she flipped her slate to a fresh page, and wrote, in her neatest hand, the only heading that fit:

*Dive plan: drown the devil's engine.*

Above them, the first far-off thunder of the monsoon rolled across the water, right on schedule, like it had been invited.
