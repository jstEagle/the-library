## 8. Two Tides Down

Tomás had dived in bad water before. He had dived in monsoon chop, in silt so thick he navigated by touch, once in a flooded quarry with a lantern that died halfway down and a crewmate who sang to keep him calm — badly, off-key, a song about a sailor's lost love that Tomás still couldn't hear without smiling.

He had never dived under guns.

The *Kestrel's Mercy* hung above the High Rivergate like a vulture that had learned to float, her deck guns run out, her divers already in the water when Odile's plan went into motion. Two tides. That was Voss's offer, delivered with the smile of a man who had never once been told no and survived it. Two tides to hand over the compass, or Tomás went into Voss's hold and stayed there.

Odile's counterproposal had taken four hours, three pots of tea, and one argument that Sefa won by accident by pointing out that the gate's own currents ran in patterns, and patterns could be ridden.

"Patterns," Tomás muttered now, checking his regulator for the third time. "She says patterns like the sea's a dance partner and not a thing that's tried to kill every one of us at least twice."

"Odile's patterns have a better record than most," Sefa said, cinching the strap on Tomás's tank. Her hands were steady. Her voice wasn't, quite. "You come back up. That's the whole plan. Everything else is decoration."

"And you?"

"I'll be on the line. Pulling you out when you inevitably do something stupid and heroic." She patted his chest plate. "Try to do it in that order. Stupid first. Heroic second. It's easier on the paperwork."

Across the deck, Rafael was already suited, standing at the rail and looking down at the green-black water where the drowned city waited. He hadn't spoken much since the parley. He'd spent the hours instead taking apart the compass and putting it back together, over and over, like a man worrying a loose tooth.

Tomás joined him at the rail. Below, the water heaved. Somewhere under it lay the engine chamber, the tide-heart, the thing worth fortunes — and the thing, if Sefa was right, that could drown a coastline on a bad afternoon.

"Rafael."

"I know what you're going to say."

"I doubt it," Tomás said. "I was going to say your weight belt's on crooked."

Rafael looked down. It was. He fixed it, and for a moment the corner of his mouth twitched — the ghost of the old grin, the one that used to make even bad dives feel like adventures. Then it was gone, and the grimness came back, and Tomás felt something in his chest twist tight as a fouled line.

Three years. Three years since that last dive, since the collapse in the lower gallery, since the moment Tomás had understood — really understood, down in the dark with his air running low and the exit blocked — that Rafael Villalobos had chosen himself and left him.

He'd told himself a hundred versions since. Rafael panicked. Rafael didn't see him. Rafael calculated the odds and the odds were cruel. But the version that stuck, the one that had kept him awake on a hundred nights since, was the simplest: Rafael left, and Tomás lived, and neither of them had ever said a word about it out loud.

"Voss's divers will be in the water," Rafael said. "Six, maybe eight. They'll expect us to come in through the main channel."

"We're not coming in through the main channel."

"No. We're coming in through the drain gallery, which is a hole in the dark full of broken glass, and then riding the intake current down to the engine chamber like a couple of leaves in a gutter." Rafael shouldered his tank. "Odile's patterns."

"Odile's patterns," Tomás agreed.

They went over the rail together, into the green.

* * *

The drain gallery was exactly as advertised: a hole in the dark full of broken glass. Tomás's lamp found edges — a shattered lintel, a rusted grate, the pale drift of silt like snowfall that had forgotten how to land. The current caught them at the gallery's mouth and pulled, and for ninety seconds there was nothing to do but trust it, which was, Tomás reflected, a hell of a thing to ask of him where Rafael Villalobos was concerned.

But the current held. The pattern held. And then the gallery opened like a throat and spat them out into the engine chamber, and Tomás forgot, for a full three seconds, that he was diving under guns.

The chamber was a cathedral that had decided to become a machine. Columns rose out of the gloom, ringed with brass fittings gone green with age. At the center, the tide-heart turned — slow, patient, a wheel of interlocking rings that spun with the pull of the moon itself, shedding light like a lantern seen through deep water. The whole space breathed. He could feel it against his chest, a pressure rhythm older than any of them.

Rafael's lamp found him. A hand signal: *there*. The compass, strapped to Rafael's forearm, was trembling — Tomás could see it even from ten feet away, the needle straining toward the wheel like a dog at the end of a rope.

They swam for the mechanism.

They got maybe halfway before the first diver came out of the dark.

Tomás saw the lamp first — a bloom of light where no light should be — and then the shape behind it, and then the knife. Voss's people didn't bother with signals. They came on fast and mean, two of them bracketing Rafael, a third swinging wide for Tomás.

The fight, underwater, was less fighting than drowning with extra steps. Tomás caught the third diver's wrist, turned with the man's own momentum, and used the current — Odile's patterns, bless her — to send him tumbling into a column. The man's lamp spun away, a lost moon. Tomás lost sight of Rafael for four seconds.

In those four seconds, everything happened.

When he found Rafael again, one of Voss's divers had him against the tide-heart's housing, knife at the air line, and the second was closing in, and Rafael's eyes over the regulator were wide and fixed on Tomás with an expression Tomás knew too well. Not fear for himself. The arithmetic look. The *calculate the odds* look. The look of a man deciding who was worth the cost.

No, Tomás thought. *No. Not this time. Not again.*

He kicked. Hard. Faster than he'd kicked in years, faster than sense, straight into the space between Rafael and the second diver, and he hit the man mid-stroke and took them both down into the silt. They rolled. Something sharp found Tomás's forearm — a shallow line of fire — and then the diver's air hose caught on a brass fitting and the man thrashed free and went up, abandoning the fight, abandoning everything.

Tomás came up out of the silt cloud coughing into his regulator and found Rafael staring at him.

Not at the fleeing diver. Not at the tide-heart, the treasure, the fortune that could clear every debt he carried. At Tomás. His lamp shook. His hands shook. Behind the mask, behind the glass and the rubber, his face was doing something complicated and ruined.

*Why?* his eyes said. *You could have gone. You could have taken the gap and gone up and been free of me.*

Tomás pointed at the exit current, then at Rafael, then made the old crew sign — the one Odile had invented their first year, the one that meant *together, all lines attached, nobody cut loose*. Then he jabbed a finger at Rafael's chest.

*You. Me. Together. Nobody cut loose.*

Rafael broke. Right there, forty feet down in a drowned cathedral, with Voss's divers regrouping somewhere in the dark and the tide ticking toward the monsoon, Rafael Villalobos broke — shoulders heaving, mask fogging, one hand pressed flat against the tide-heart's housing like he needed something older than himself to hold him up.

And then he signed back. Slowly, clumsily, the way a man signs when his hands won't obey. Not the crew sign first, though he got there. First he pointed at Tomás, then down, then made the shape of it — the lower gallery, the collapse, three years ago — and then he pressed his fist to his own heart and dragged it across his body, and every diver's language in the world knew what that meant.

*I left you. It was me. I have carried it every day.*

Tomás hadn't known he needed to hear it. He'd have said, an hour ago, that words were cheap and signs were cheaper and nothing Rafael said down here could undo three years of nights spent staring at a ceiling. But the fist across the heart undid something anyway, quietly, the way a knot comes undone — not with a snap but with a slow, blessed loosening.

He signed back: *You're an idiot.*

Rafael's wrecked face cracked into something that was almost a laugh, bubbles streaming from his regulator.

*You left me,* Tomás signed, *and I'm still here. That's the whole story. You're an idiot, and I'm still here.*

Then the chamber shuddered.

Not the tide-heart — that turned on, serene, indifferent. The shudder came from above, through the stone, through the water itself: a deep concussion, then another. Voss, losing patience. Charges against the gate's upper structure, or guns firing into the channel, or both. Silt began to fall from the ceiling in slow gray curtains.

Rafael looked up. Then at the compass, straining on his arm. Then at the tide-heart's central housing, where a brass recess waited, wheel-shaped, exactly the size of the thing he'd carried across a desert and half an ocean.

Tomás saw the choice land on Rafael's face like weather. The key. The fortune. The debt that had driven him across the flats and back into their lives — all of it one turn of the wrist away, and above them the monsoon coming down like the end of the world, and Voss's charges knocking on the ceiling.

*Two tides,* Voss had said. The first tide was spent. The second was bleeding out fast.

Rafael reached for the housing.

* * *

They were halfway up the intake channel when the third charge went off, closer than the others, close enough that the water itself flinched. Tomás felt the current reverse — a stutter, a hiccup in Odile's perfect pattern — and then the pull changed, dragging them sideways, back down toward the chamber, toward the falling stone.

Rafael grabbed his harness. Tomás grabbed his. For one long, absurd moment they hung in the throat of the gate, held by each other and nothing else, two men who had spent three years being each other's worst memory, now each other's only anchor.

Above them, faint through the water, came a sound that made Tomás's blood go cold: the groan of the gate's great doors, the drowned arches, the whole city's skeleton shifting under the monsoon's first blow.

And below them, in the chamber they'd just left, the tide-heart's light began to change — brightening, pulsing, quickening like a heart waking up.

The compass, Tomás realized, hadn't been trembling because it wanted to go home.

It had been trembling because it was afraid.
