## 9. Landfall

The sky went green before the monsoon came.

Rafael had seen that color only twice in his life, and both times the world afterward was different from the world before. It sat over the horizon like a bruise going bad, and beneath it the sea flattened out — not calm, but holding its breath, the way a man does just before he says something he can't take back.

Below him, through sixty feet of green water, the tide-engine of the High Rivergate turned in its flooded cathedral of stone. He could feel it through the deck plates, a slow patient thrumming in his boot soles, like a heartbeat under floorboards.

"Raf." Sefa's voice crackled in his earpiece, tinny and small against the vastness. "Pressure readings are climbing on the western arches. When the monsoon surge hits the outer gates, this whole structure's going to sing. You do not want to be inside it when it does."

"How long?"

"If you're asking as an engineer: hours. If you're asking as your friend: less."

He looked down at the brass compass in his hand. It was warm — it had been warm since they'd opened the engine chamber, warm the way a living thing is warm, and its needle no longer pointed north or anywhere a compass ought to point. It pointed down. Straight down, into the dark heart of the drowned city, where Marisol's partner had died keeping a secret, where Rafael's debt waited with its hand out, where a fortune floated in the black water like a lantern.

*Take it,* the compass said, without words, the way it had been saying it for three days. *Take it and be free.*

Behind him, muffled by hull and distance, he could hear Voss's fleet working their salvage rigs along the eastern spires. Two tides, Odile had promised them. Two tides to get in, get the key seated, and get out before Voss realized what they were really doing down here.

Two tides. The monsoon had other arithmetic.

* * *

They'd argued about it for an hour in Ilsa's chart room, and the argument had ended the way all their arguments did now — not with a winner, but with everyone looking at Rafael.

"You could clear it," Odile said quietly. She stood with her arms crossed, which was her way of standing gently. "The tide-engine core alone. One piece of it. We wouldn't even have to take the whole thing. Enough to pay off Calloway twice over, refit the *Kestrel*, and never look at another contract we hate."

"And drown how many coastlines?" Sefa asked. She'd spread her diagrams across the table, ink still wet. "I've run the numbers six ways. This thing isn't a power source. It's a *lever*. Somebody built it to move tides, and whoever holds the key moves them. Voss doesn't want to sell a treasure, Odile. He wants to sell weather."

Tomás hadn't spoken. He sat at the end of the table with his arms wrapped around a mug of coffee he wasn't drinking, and when Rafael finally looked at him, Tomás looked back with the flat, steady gaze of a man who had already made up his mind about something and was waiting for everyone else to catch up.

"You know what she'd say," Tomás said at last.

Rafael's throat tightened. "She'd say a fortune isn't worth a flood."

"She'd say it worse than that. She'd make it funny." Tomás's mouth twitched, almost. "And then she'd take the compass out of your hand and sink it herself, and make you feel stupid for hesitating."

Marisol had been dead four years, and she could still outvote every person in that room.

Rafael picked up the compass. "Then it's settled."

"It's not settled until you say why," Odile said. "Not for us. For you. Because if you go down there carrying someone else's ghost instead of your own choice, you'll hesitate at the wrong moment, and the sea will take the difference out of your hide."

He wanted to tell her he didn't know. That grief and greed had lived in him so long side by side he couldn't always tell which hand was which. But that wasn't true anymore, and lying to Odile had never once worked anyway.

"My partner died hiding this thing," he said. "She died thinking nobody would ever find it. And I spent four years chasing a thief because I thought what I'd lost was a compass." He turned the brass case over in his palm. "It wasn't the compass. I know that now. What I lost was—" His voice caught, and he let it. "The point is, I'm done losing things to this wreck. All of them. At once."

Sefa nodded slowly. Tomás drank his cold coffee. Odile uncrossed her arms, which was her way of applauding.

* * *

The descent was the worst of his life, and Rafael had once spent nine hours trapped in a collapsed cistern breathing through a reed.

The monsoon arrived while he was still dropping through the western gate, and he felt it as pressure — a deep rolling groan through the stonework, the whole drowned city shuddering like a dog shaking off rain. Silt bloomed around him in brown clouds. His lamp beam shrank to a fist's width of visibility.

"Sefa," he said. "Talk to me."

"Arches are groaning on my strain gauges. Western colonnade just shed a lintel the size of a wagon. Rafael, the math is getting unfriendly."

"How unfriendly?"

"I'm an engineer. Friendly is not my department. *Dive faster.*"

He dove faster. Down through the flooded avenue where statues of forgotten river-kings stood furred with white coral, down past the great sluice-gates with their bronze gears thick as trees, down into the engine chamber itself — a rotunda the size of a chapel, ringed with channels cut into the living rock, all of them converging on a socket at the center like veins converging on a heart.

The compass pulled so hard now it nearly dragged out of his grip.

He landed on the chamber floor in a cloud of silt and knelt at the socket, and there — set into the stone, crusted with four years of mineral bloom — was the shape the compass had been shaped to fit. A keyhole the size of a dinner plate.

All he had to do was not put it in. Take the key, swim out, sell it to Calloway or anyone else with enough coin, and walk away rich and free and hollow.

*Raf.* Tomás's voice in his ear, breath ragged. He was topside, on the winch line, exactly where he'd sworn he'd stay after last time. After the dive where Rafael had surfaced alone. *Whatever you're doing down there, do it fast. Voss's divers just dropped off the eastern spire. They've seen our lights.*

Of course they had. Of course.

Rafael knelt in the dark heart of the High Rivergate, holding his dead partner's compass, and thought about debts. Money owed to Calloway, yes — but also the debt he owed Tomás, three years of interest compounding on a single moment of cowardice. The debt he owed Odile for taking him back without making him beg, which had cost her more than begging would have. The debt he owed Marisol, which no fortune could service and no flood could drown, because it wasn't paid in coin. It was paid in *continuing*. In doing the next right thing in a world she'd left too soon.

The first of Voss's divers swept into the chamber behind him, lamp swinging, knife already out.

Rafael didn't fight him. There wasn't time, and it didn't matter anymore. He pressed the compass into the socket, felt the mechanism take it — a deep mechanical *clack* that resonated up through the stone and into his bones — and then he turned the brass housing a quarter-turn counterclockwise, exactly as Sefa had calculated, exactly as Marisol had died believing someone someday would.

Above him, through sixty feet of churning monsoon sea, the High Rivergate began to die.

* * *

It didn't explode. It *sighed.*

Every channel in the chamber drained at once, the tide-engine releasing four centuries of held pressure in one long exhalation that shook silt from the vaulted ceiling in gray curtains. The compass-key sank into the socket and the stone flowed over it like water closing over a dropped coin, seamless, finished, gone forever.

The fortune with it. Gone forever. Rafael watched the seam vanish under his own fingertips and felt — not loss. Something lighter. Something that hurt in the way a healed bone hurts, which is to say, it hurt like growing.

Then the ceiling started coming down, and the mood changed considerably.

"Rafael!" Sefa, sharp with panic. "Structural collapse across the whole western complex! The engine was *load-bearing*, I didn't— well, I suspected, but— *swim!*"

He swam. Or tried to. A falling slab clipped his shoulder and sent him tumbling, and his lamp went spinning away into the brown, and suddenly there was nothing but silt and pain and the animal certainty that he was going to die sixty feet under a drowning city with his debts finally forgiven and his name finally clean, and no one would ever know.

His tank caught on falling masonry. His regulator spat brine. The surface became a rumor somewhere far above him, and the last thing he thought, with strange and terrible calm, was: *at least I'm not leaving anyone behind this time. At least I'm the one who stays.*

Then hands closed on his harness.

Strong hands, diver's hands, scarred and sure, and they hauled him free of the rubble with a fury that communicated more clearly than any words ever had between them. Tomás. Tomás, who had sworn he'd never dive a collapsing structure again; Tomás, who had every reason to let the sea settle its own accounts; Tomás, dragging him up through the collapsing dark, past the falling gates, up and up toward the light—

They broke the surface together into the teeth of the monsoon, rain like gravel, the *Kestrel*'s lanterns blazing through the deluge, and hands — Odile's, Sefa's, Ilsa's crew, all of them — hauling them aboard onto the deck in a tangle of lines and curses and laughter that sounded suspiciously like sobbing.

Rafael lay on the decking, coughing half the High Rivergate out of his lungs, and found Tomás kneeling over him, gripping his collar with both fists, rain streaming down his face.

"You *stayed*," Tomás shouted over the storm. "You stayed down there! Again! You absolute—"

"I'm sorry," Rafael gasped. "For last time. For all of it. I should have—"

"Shut up. Shut up, you're fine, you're *fine*—" But Tomás wasn't shouting about the diving anymore, and his grip on Rafael's collar had stopped being anger somewhere in the middle and become something else entirely, something four years overdue, and Rafael held onto him back and let the rain wash over them both.

Odile appeared above them, soaked through, holding the boat hook like a marshal's staff. Her face was doing something complicated.

"The engine?" she demanded.

"Gone. Sunk with the key. Nobody sells weather now."

Sefa whooped loud enough to be heard over the monsoon, which took talent. Behind her, Captain Ilsa Reyes leaned against the rail with her arms folded, watching Rafael with an expression he'd never seen on her before — something almost like approval, which from Ilsa was roughly equivalent to a parade.

"Well," she called out. "You sank a fortune, nearly died twice, and cost me a salvage contract. Congratulations, Villalobos. In my experience, that's exactly the sort of man who ends up owing me a favor."

Rafael started laughing and couldn't stop.

* * *

They limped into harbor two days later, the *Kestrel* battered, the crew salt-burned and broke, and Rafael stood at the rail watching the town come closer with the strangest feeling in his chest — lighter than he'd felt in four years, and terrified of it, because light things can be lost.

Debt unpaid. Calloway would come collecting. The future was a blank page with nothing written on it but weather.

But Tomás was at the winch beside him, shoulder to shoulder, saying nothing and needing to say nothing. Sefa was below decks arguing lovingly with her engines. Odile stood at the helm, and when she caught him looking, she gave him the smallest nod — the nod of a quartermaster counting her people and finding, for the first time in three years, the number correct.

Ahead, the harbor opened its arms, and Rafael Villalobos — debt-ridden, penniless, richer than he had ever been — began to wonder how a man goes about asking to come home.
