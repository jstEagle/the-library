## 10. Home Water

The monsoon took three days to pass, and when it finally blew itself out over the coast, it left behind a world scrubbed clean and shining — gulls wheeling in a sky like new porcelain, the harbor town of Puerto Ceniza dripping and steaming in the first honest sunlight they'd seen in weeks.

Rafael stood on Ilsa's deck with a mug of terrible coffee and watched Tomás repaint the name on the tender they'd bought. Or rather, watched him argue with Sefa about how to paint it.

"It's crooked," Sefa said, arms crossed, standing on the dock below.

"It's *artistic*," Tomás called down, brush in hand, perched precariously on the gunwale. "The *Cormorant* deserves some flair."

"The Cormorant is a rowboat with delusions."

"The Cormorant is a *salvage vessel* with delusions, thank you."

Rafael smiled into his coffee. Three weeks ago he'd been chasing a thief across the flats with nothing but sand and a debt. Now he had salt spray in his beard, a bruise the size of a dinner plate on his ribs where the gate's current had thrown him against a bulkhead, and no money at all — less than none, if you counted the debt, which Odile insisted on doing with grim satisfaction every morning at breakfast.

He wouldn't have traded it for anything.

* * *

Ilsa found him at the rail an hour later. She moved like she always did, quiet as ballast shifting, and leaned beside him without asking what he was looking at. That was one of the things Rafael respected about her: she never asked questions she could answer by waiting.

"You've heard," she said.

"Heard what?"

"Voss's fleet limped into Corazón Bay two days ago. Half his divers quit. The admiralty is asking questions about a drowned city that suddenly isn't there anymore." She turned her mug slowly in her hands. "There are men who'll pay well for that story, Villalobos. And men who'll pay better for the ones who can tell it."

"Is that an offer?"

"It's a warning dressed up as an offer." She looked out at the harbor, where the *Cormorant* bobbed at its mooring, absurdly small, absurdly theirs. "The tide-engine's gone. The compass went down with it. Whatever Voss hoped to salvage, you made sure it's mud and memory now. Some people take that personally."

"I've been told I have that effect."

Something almost like a smile crossed her face. "I have one more contract before I make port for the season. Good pay. Honest wreck." She said it lightly, but her eyes were sharp. "You could clear that debt of yours in one run."

Rafael looked down at his hands. They were still scarred from the lines, still stained faintly with the green-black silt of the High Rivergate. He thought about the ledger Odile kept, the numbers that never got smaller, the weight he'd carried three years since Marta died — since he'd walked away from these same people because grief had convinced him he deserved to carry it alone.

"No," he said.

Ilsa raised an eyebrow.

"I'm done paying other people's prices." He met her gaze. "Ask the crew. If any of them want your contract, I'll row them to your ship myself. But I'm not signing anything that keeps me away from—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I already lost three years. I'm not buying more time at sea with less time home."

Ilsa studied him for a long moment. Then she drained her coffee, set the mug on the rail, and said, "The crew already refused. Last night. While you were snoring."

"They—"

"Odile speaks for them. You know this." She pushed off from the rail and headed for the gangway, then paused. "Villalobos. What you did at the gate — drowning that engine instead of taking it — most salvagers would call it the stupidest thing they ever saw."

"And you?"

"I call it the reason I'm offering you honest wrecks instead of selling your names to the admiralty." A genuine smile this time, quick and rare as sun through storm cloud. "Take care of my diver's old crew, Rafael. They were mine once too."

Then she was gone down the gangway, and Rafael stood alone at the rail feeling lighter than he had since before the desert, since before the compass, since before Marta's death had hollowed him out and left him walking.

* * *

They christened the boat properly that evening, which meant Odile produced a bottle of plum brandy she'd been hiding in the bilge since Corazón Bay, and Sefa built a fire on the beach out of driftwood, and Tomás gave a speech that started dignified and ended with everyone throwing bread rolls at him.

"To the *Cormorant*," Tomás declared, standing on a crate with his shirt off and his scars gleaming in the firelight — including the new one, the long white line across his shoulder where the engine chamber's current had nearly taken him. "Smallest ship in the fleet, bravest crew on the water, and the only captain who's never abandoned anyone on a dive."

The last part landed soft and heavy at once. Rafael felt it settle in his chest like a stone dropping into deep water. Tomás didn't look at him when he said it. He didn't need to. That was the thing about the apology between them — it hadn't come all at once, gasping, in the dark under Voss's guns. It had come in pieces, over weeks: Tomás teaching him the new rigging without commentary; Rafael sitting up with him through the worst nights after the gate, when the dreams came; the word *sorry* passing between them so many times it had worn smooth as river stone, until it wasn't a wound anymore, just weather.

"To the *Cormorant*," they echoed, and drank.

Sefa collapsed onto the sand next to Rafael afterward, brandy-flushed and grinning, and knocked their shoulders together. "You know I ran the numbers," she said. "On the engine. What we could have gotten for it. Even split six ways—"

"Sefa."

"—it would have been enough to buy a real ship. A brigantine. With a figurehead."

"A figurehead," Rafael repeated.

"A *dignified* one. Not whatever Tomás is going to carve out of that piece of driftwood he's been hoarding."

Rafael laughed — really laughed, from somewhere low and easy he'd forgotten he owned. "And?"

She was quiet a moment, turning her cup. "And I keep thinking about the coast towns downstream of the gate. Little places. Fishing places. Places that would have been underwater inside a year if we'd sold that thing to whoever paid first." She shrugged, embarrassed by her own sincerity. "So. Numbers aren't everything. Don't tell Odile I said that."

"Your secret dies with me."

"Everything dies with you, you're practically a relic."

* * *

It was late — the fire burned down to embers, Tomás asleep sitting upright with a bread roll still in his hand, Sefa snoring gently against a coil of rope — when Rafael found Odile at the waterline, barefoot, watching the tide come in.

He sat down beside her. For a while neither spoke. The waves kept their own counsel, the way they always had, patient and enormous and unimpressed by human affairs.

"I owe you an accounting," Rafael said finally.

"You owe me eleven thousand crowns and change," Odile said. "Plus interest. Plus emotional damages."

"Odile."

"I know what you're going to say." She drew her knees up. In the ember light her face was half shadow, half gold, and older than he remembered, and kinder than she'd ever let him see before. "You're going to say you're sorry. Again. And I'm going to say I know. And then you're going to ask me something, because I can hear you working up to it, Rafael, you've been working up to it for two days like a man trying to swallow a fishbone sideways."

He huffed. "Am I that transparent?"

"To me? Always." She turned to look at him straight on. "So ask."

Rafael looked out at the dark water, at the *Cormorant* riding gentle at her mooring, at the embers and the sleeping shapes of his people on the beach behind him. His throat was tight. He made himself say it plainly, because they had all had enough of things unsaid to last several lifetimes.

"I want to come home. Formally. On the roster, in the book, with duties and a share and all of it. Not tagging along. Not earning my way back. Just—" He exhaled. "Home."

The waves came in. Went out.

"You understand," Odile said quietly, "that we voted on it already."

His heart stuttered. "You—"

"Three days ago. While you slept." She reached into her coat and pulled out a folded paper, softened at the creases like it had been carried and unfolded and refolded many times. "Sefa voted yes before you'd finished saying you were sorry. Tomás took another day, but he came around — he says you cook better than you dive, which is faint praise, but praise. I broke the tie." Her mouth curved. "Seems to be a habit of mine."

She handed him the paper. He opened it with hands that weren't quite steady.

It was a berth roster. *The Cormorant*, in Sefa's careful block letters. Four names, four berths, four shares — and at the bottom, written in ink that had gone slightly blurry in the salt air, or perhaps in his eyes:

*Rafael Villalobos — quartermaster's apprentice. Probationary. Indefinitely.*

"Probationary," Rafael managed.

"Indefinitely," Odile confirmed. "You'll be peeling potatoes until I say otherwise, which may be never. And the debt stands. Every crown. We're not a charity."

"We're barely a boat."

"We're a family that owns a boat. There's a difference, and it matters." She bumped her shoulder against his, brief and fierce. "Welcome home, Rafael. Don't make me regret it, or I'll feed you to the cormorants."

He laughed, and wiped his eyes when he thought she wasn't looking, though of course she was looking — she always was — and let her pretend otherwise.

Behind them, Tomás snorted awake, demanded to know why nobody woke him for the sentimental part, and fell back asleep mid-complaint. Sefa murmured something about figureheads in her sleep. The tide came in, unhurried, keeping its old promises.

And far down the coast, past the headlands where the sea met the edge of the desert Rafael had crossed with nothing but sand and a debt — in a harbor town where nobody knew their names yet — a rider was dismounting stiffly at the customs house, carrying a sealed letter marked with a crest no one in Puerto Ceniza had seen before, addressed to *the crew of the vessel Cormorant*, who were, at that moment, the happiest people on the water and entirely unaware they were about to be famous.

But that was tomorrow's tide.

Tonight there was only the fire, and the roster in Rafael's hands, and the words he'd waited three years to say, spoken now at last into the warm dark, to the people who'd waited longer:

"I'm home."
