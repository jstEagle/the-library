## 1. The Mood of Sandbars

Every river has a temper, and the Serrano's lower reaches were sulking that morning.

I could tell from the sandbars before I could tell from anything else. A river doesn't hide its moods; it writes them in mud and current-lines for anyone patient enough to read. The bar below Cutter's Bend had grown a new tongue overnight — pale sand licking out into the channel like a cat testing a paw — and the water sliding over it had gone the color of weak tea instead of the brown-gold it should have been. That meant the current was spreading thin, looking for a way around an obstruction it hadn't been told about. Somewhere upstream, a bank had slumped. Somewhere ahead of us, my day was about to get worse.

"Bar's moved," I called down into the engine well, because Bram liked to be told things even when he already knew them.

"I heard her change," came his voice, gravel over gravel. "She's been grumbling since dawn."

Bram Okafor was sixty-one years old, built like something a shipyard would have rejected as too expensive to maintain, and he'd been keeping the Kestrel's ancient diesel alive longer than some of our clients had been alive at all. He claimed the engine talked to him. I used to think that was a joke. After eleven years aboard, I no longer did.

I stood at the wheel with one hand on it and one hand on the rail, reading the water the way my father taught me before I was old enough to know there was anything to forgive him for. Watch where the current wrinkles. Watch where the birds stand. Watch where the water goes quiet, because quiet water is either deep enough to drown you or shallow enough to strand you, and only the mud knows which.

The Kestrel is a hundred and ten feet of pre-war riverboat that has been repaired so many times she's mostly memory held together by paint. She has a shallow draft, a stern paddlewheel that Bram coaxes along like a mule with opinions, a cargo deck that can take forty tons when she's feeling generous, and a list to port that we've stopped apologizing for. Her name is painted on the bow in letters that were gold once and are now the color of old honey. My father named her. That's the short version. The long version involves a woman named after a bird that flies away, which my mother apparently found romantic and my father found funny, and neither of them explained it to me before they were gone.

We were running light that morning — thirty tons of salted fish and machine parts downriver to Marrow Quay, the kind of freight nobody wants and everybody needs, paying rates that would embarrass a beggar. It was the fourth such run that month. It would not be the last. It could not be the last, because the Kestrel owed the Merchants' Provincial Bank more money than I like to say out loud, and the bank had recently begun saying it for me, in letters printed on paper heavy enough to use as a sandbar marker.

* * *

Let me tell you about the debt, since it sits at the table with us at every meal whether it's invited or not.

Three years ago the Kestrel needed a new boiler section and a full hull survey, and the survey turned up rot in places rot has no business being, and the yard in Marrow Quay quoted a figure that made me laugh until I realized they were serious. So I signed. I signed papers with a man named Fennick who had a handshake like a wet rope and a smile he kept in a drawer somewhere and brought out only for signatures. Ninety thousand crowns against the boat herself as collateral, payable over seven years, and for two years we paid on time, because we are good at what we do even if what we do pays like charity work.

Then came the year the syndicate bought up every fuel depot on the lower river and prices went up forty percent between one season and the next. Then came the year the charter fees at every dock we served doubled, because the docks belonged to the syndicate too, though the paperwork was dressed up in six different company names like a bad disguise. Then came the season the fish ran late and the grain ran short and everyone on the Serrano learned the same lesson at once: the river doesn't belong to the river anymore.

We missed three payments. Then five. The letters started arriving by fast launch, which is its own particular insult — paying courier rates to tell a woman she's losing her home.

The most recent letter said, in Fennick's drawer-smile prose, that the bank would be "compelled to discuss restructuring options" if the account wasn't brought current within fourteen days. Restructuring options is bank language for *we take your boat and sell her to someone who will run her harder and care less*. Fourteen days. As of that morning on the wheel, twelve remained.

I don't say any of this to the crew. They know. Of course they know — Odile does the books, and Bram can smell worry through a bulkhead — but knowing and discussing are different animals, and I keep the discussion penned up where it can't bite anyone but me.

* * *

Odile Vasseur came up onto the deckhouse roof around midmorning with two cups of coffee that tasted like it had been brewed from rope, which is the only kind we stock, and handed me one without asking. She does that. Twenty-nine years old, sharp-faced, sharper-tongued, exiled from a cartography office in the capital for reasons she describes variously as "a disagreement," "a misunderstanding," and, once, memorably, "he had it coming." She keeps our charts, our accounts, and a running tally of every insult the river offers us, annotated in pencil.

"Cutter's bar's grown," she said, squinting downstream. "That wasn't there Tuesday."

"It was there Tuesday. It was just shy."

"You can't read a sandbar's shyness."

"You can if you grew up on this river." I sipped the rope coffee. "It's telling us the channel's hugging the far bank past the bend. Which means the far bank, which was rotten last fall, is probably worse now."

"So we go slow."

"We go exactly as fast as is safe and not one knot slower, because Marrow Quay closes its loading window at four, and if we miss the window we lose the delivery bonus, and the delivery bonus is—" I did the arithmetic I do forty times a day, the arithmetic that lives behind my eyes like a second pulse. "—the difference between an embarrassing payment and no payment."

Odile looked at me the way she looks at charts with errors in them. "You haven't slept."

"I slept."

"You slept *badly*, which is worse than not sleeping, because at least then you'd be honest about it."

This is what it's like to be captain of the Kestrel. Your crew loves you and respects you and tells you the truth at inconvenient moments, and you pretend to find it irritating because admitting you're grateful would mean admitting you need them, and needing people is a debt too, and I am so very tired of owing.

"Bram," I called down. "How's she running?"

"She's running," said Bram, which from him meant everything from *perfectly* to *she'll explode within the hour, but not yet*.

* * *

Cutter's Bend nearly killed us at noon, which I want to be clear was not dramatic. Nearly killing people is rarely dramatic. It's usually just a long gray minute where nothing happens except your own heartbeat getting loud.

The channel did hug the far bank, and the far bank had slumped exactly as the sandbar predicted, and there was a submerged willow sitting in eight feet of water where the chart promised twelve. I saw it as a shadow under shadow, a darkness with branches, and I took the wheel hard over and rang for half-ahead and we slid past it close enough that I could have reached down and touched the drowned leaves. Behind me, Odile said nothing, which from Odile is applause.

But here's the humiliating part, and I promised myself I'd be honest in this account, or at least honest in the places where honesty costs least.

At Marrow Quay, the harbormaster — a soft man named Pell whose family had sold their dock rights to the syndicate two years back and now wore their collar like a cravat — inspected our paperwork twice, made a show of checking the freight manifest against the crates, and then informed me that the dock fee had gone up again. Effective that morning. Retroactive to the previous week.

"That's the third increase this season," I said.

"Costs rise," said Pell, examining his fingernails.

"The syndicate's costs, maybe. Mine are the same as they were in spring."

"Then perhaps you should look at your margins, Captain Thornbury." He smiled. He knew my name. He knew my father's name. Everyone on this river knows both names, and only one of them comes with a story people enjoy telling. "Or perhaps at your vessel. She's an old girl. Old girls get expensive."

I paid. Of course I paid. I counted out the crowns from the coffee tin we keep bolted under the wheel console — a coffee tin, because a strongbox invites thieves and a tin invites pity — and I watched Pell write the receipt in his little book, and I stood there while he did it because walking away first would have felt like fleeing and standing there felt like dignity, though I suspect from the outside they looked identical.

Then we unloaded thirty tons of fish and machine parts in ninety minutes, because my crew works like the devil's own stevedores when the bonus is on the line, and we earned the bonus, and the bonus covered the dock fee and the fuel and left, by my arithmetic, sixty-one crowns toward a payment of nine hundred.

Sixty-one.

On the walk back up the gangplank, a boy — maybe ten, barefoot, one of the quay kids who haunt the pilings — asked me if the Kestrel was for sale yet, because his uncle said she would be soon, and he wanted to see inside her before the new owners changed everything.

I gave him a coin and told him she wasn't for sale, and he looked at me with the terrible clear honesty of children and said, "That's not what my uncle says," and ran off before I could decide whether to be angry or heartbroken, so I was both, efficiently, and got back to work.

* * *

We took on a return load that evening — baled cotton, low rate, because low rate is the only rate left to boats without syndicate papers — and cast off at dusk with the sky going the color of a bruise behind the western hills. Monsoon season was still weeks out, officially. But the air had that thick, waiting quality it gets before the season changes its mind early, and old rivermen were saying the front was building fat up in the high country. I filed that away. On the Serrano, weather is a creditor too, and it always collects.

Bram had the engine humming its one good note. Odile was below, correcting charts by lamplight. And I stood at the wheel in the dark with the running lights burning and the paddlewheel throwing up its slow silver fan, and I let myself do the thing I never do in daylight: I thought about what losing the Kestrel would actually mean.

Not the money. Money is just numbers wearing costumes. I mean the rest. Bram, who came aboard eleven years ago with nothing but a toolbox and a silence he never explained, and who has no pension and no family except us. Odile, who fled whatever she fled in the capital and found, I think, the first place she's ever been allowed to be exactly as difficult as she is. The boat herself — my father's hands are in her joinery, literally, in the curved ribs of the wheelhouse he shaped himself, and when I put my palm flat on that wood I am touching the only part of him that never lied to anybody.

If the bank takes her, they don't take a boat. They take all of that, and they auction it off in lots.

So I did what I always do with thoughts too big to carry. I set them down on the sandbars.

There's a stretch below Marrow Quay where the river braids out into a dozen channels across four miles of shifting sand, and the charts call it the Loom, and the charts are wrong about it every single season, because the Loom rebuilds itself nightly out of spite. No pilot runs it fast. Most pilots don't run it at all after dark. But night was when we'd make the best water, and water was time, and time was the only currency I had left in quantity.

I read the Loom by starlight and by the way the current combed the surface — smooth ribbons meaning depth, broken water meaning bars, that certain glassy stillness that means the river is lying to you. Twice I changed channels on instinct alone and both times found open water where the chart showed sand and sand where it showed open water. At the worst of it, threading a gap between two bars no wider than the Kestrel's beam, I heard Bram come up quietly and stand beside me without a word, and stay there, and that was his whole conversation: *I'm here, and I've decided you're worth following through this.*

Pride is a strange animal. It dies easily in bank offices and harbormasters' parlors. But give it a black river and a blind passage and a crew that trusts you, and it stands right back up and pretends it never knelt.

We cleared the Loom at midnight. I logged our position, rang down for standard speed, and let Odile take the middle watch, and I lay in my bunk listening to the hull talk to the water and doing the arithmetic again. Sixty-one crowns. Twelve days. Nine hundred.

The numbers didn't work. They hadn't worked in months, and I'd known it, and I'd kept steering anyway, because the alternative to steering is sinking and I am my father's daughter, which cuts both ways.

I was almost asleep when Odile knocked — two raps, quick, her *something's wrong but not emergency wrong* knock — and put her head in without waiting.

"There's a message launch alongside," she said. "Came up from Marrow Quay fast. They're asking for you by name." A pause, and then, in the tone she uses when she's decided I need the truth straight rather than gentle: "They say he's a passenger. Says he wants to hire us. Says you know him."

Nobody hires the Kestrel by name at midnight. Nobody good, anyway.

"Did he give a name?"

"Yeah." Odile's face did something complicated. "He said to tell you it's Silas Vane, and that he knows you'll remember him."

I sat up in the dark, and the river kept talking to the hull, patient and indifferent, the way it always is, the way it will be long after every debt on it is paid or unpaid.

"Tell him," I said, "that the answer is no."

"He hasn't asked a question yet."

"He won't need to. Tell him anyway."

Odile looked at me a moment longer — cataloguing, the way she does, filing my face away next to whatever she knew already — and went to deliver a refusal to a man I had spent fifteen years hoping never to see again, on a boat I had eleven days left to lose.
