## 2. A Debt in Ink

The letter came on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. Bad news should have the decency to arrive on a Friday, when you've already got a drink in your hand and nowhere to be.

I knew what it was before I opened it. The envelope was the color of old teeth, and the bank's seal sat in the corner like a wax wart. Nobody sends letters that look like that to tell you your loan has been approved.

I stood at the rail of the *Kestrel* and read it twice, because the first time through I'd been reading too fast, the way you do when you're hoping the words will rearrange themselves into something kinder. They didn't. One week. Seven days. Then men with clipboards and a tug crew would come down to Dock Nine and take my boat apart for scrap value, and the river would keep flowing past the empty slip like nothing had ever moored there.

Seven days. The ink hadn't even dried properly. I smudged the word *repossession* with my thumb, as if I could blur it out of existence. All I did was get it on my skin, so now the debt was literally on my hands. There's a metaphor in there somewhere, but I was too tired to dig it out.

"Bad news?" Odile asked from the deck below, not looking up from the net she was mending. She always knew. Twenty years of reading water will teach you things, but thirty seconds of reading Hazel Thornbury's face apparently teaches you more.

"Bank correspondence," I said. "Thrilling stuff. You wouldn't believe the interest rates."

"Mm." She pulled a knot tight with her teeth. "You've gone gray around the mouth."

"That's just my natural coloring."

"It wasn't there yesterday."

I folded the letter into quarters, then eighths, then small enough to disappear into my breast pocket, where it sat against my ribs like something I'd swallowed wrong. "We've got a week of runs booked," I said. "Gravel down to Marsh Junction, light freight back. If we run tight and don't pay anyone who doesn't hold a wrench—"

"You already don't pay anyone who doesn't hold a wrench."

"—we might scrape together the interest payment. Buy ourselves another month."

Odile set down the net and looked at me straight on, which she almost never does. Odile's whole philosophy of life is that eye contact is a form of aggression, and she'd rather save her aggression for people who deserve it. "And after the month?"

"After the month we'll figure out the month after that."

"Hazel."

"I'm the captain," I said. "Figuring out months is my job. Yours is nets. That net's got a hole in it big enough to lose a catfish and a small dog."

She went back to mending. But she looked at the hole a moment too long before she started stitching, and I understood — the way you understand things you'd rather not — that she was thinking about how easy it would be to simply stop.

* * *

Bram didn't say anything at all, which was worse.

My engineer communicates in a language of grunts, oil-stained gestures, and the occasional full sentence deployed like artillery. That evening he was under the engine housing where he'd spent most of the week, coaxing our second cylinder back from the dead, and when I brought him coffee — real coffee, hoarded, the kind of gesture captains make when they're terrified — he took the mug without looking up.

"She's running hot on the return stroke," he said. "Number two's got maybe a season left if I baby her. Less if we push."

"Bram, I'm holding sacred coffee and you're talking to me about cylinders."

"Cylinders are honest." He finally slid out from under the housing and sat up, wiping his hands on a rag that had given up being a rag years ago and was now mostly grease held together by habit. He's a big man, Bram, built like something meant to hold up bridges, with a face that makes babies cry and dock dogs follow him adoringly. "Boats are honest. Engines are honest. It's one of the reasons I like them."

"And banks?"

"Banks lie in ink." He drank his coffee and watched me over the rim. He knew about the letter. Of course he knew. Word travels through a boat faster than through water, and I'd made no particular effort to hide the way I'd been pacing the deck since noon. "You going to tell me the number, or am I supposed to guess?"

"Guessing builds character."

"Hazel."

"Interest plus arrears, or they take her." I said it lightly. I've gotten very good at saying terrible things lightly; it's the one skill poverty sharpens fastest. "One week to show good faith. A month to clear it, if they're feeling generous, which they won't be."

Bram nodded slowly, the way he does when he's weighing something heavier than cargo. "My sister wrote again," he said. "Up at Kettle Falls. Her husband's outfit needs a man who knows engines. Steady wage. Housing included." He said it flat, no apology in it, which is Bram's version of tenderness — he wasn't going to dress it up and insult us both. "Housing included, Hazel. My back's fifty years old and it rains in my bunk."

"I know."

"I'm not saying I'm going."

"I know that too."

"But I'd be a liar if I said I hadn't thought about sleeping somewhere the rain stays outside."

I took the empty mug back from him because my hands needed something to do that wasn't wringing themselves. "You think about it all you want," I said. "That's what thinking's for. Just don't decide anything until the week's out. Give me seven days to work a miracle. I hear they only take a week."

He almost smiled. Almost. On Bram that's practically a marriage proposal. "Miracles need parts," he said, and went back under the engine.

* * *

Here's the thing nobody tells you about debt: it isn't the money. Money is just numbers, and numbers can't hurt you unless you let them do arithmetic on your future. What debt actually is, is company. It sits down at your table. It rides along on every run. It's there in the morning before you're awake, reading over your shoulder, and it's the last thing you see at night, patient as a heron standing in shallows, waiting for the fish to forget it exists.

My father owed money too, at the end. Different creditors, same posture. I used to watch him at this same wheel, doing sums in his head while the Serrano unspooled ahead of us, and I swore I'd never wear that look. Now I catch my reflection in the brass fittings and there it is, his face wearing mine.

I did the sums anyway, alone in the pilothouse with the lamp turned low. The gravel run paid forty. Freight back, maybe sixty if the junction agent didn't cheat me, which he would, a little, because he could. Fuel ate twenty-five. Dock fees, food, Odile's mending supplies, whatever part Bram would inevitably discover the engine couldn't live without — call it thirty more. That left forty-five against a payment of three hundred and ten.

Two hundred and sixty-five short. In a week.

I could sell the salvage winch. I could sell my father's charts — no. Not those. Anything but those. I could sell the spare anchor, the dinghy, my mother's silver, except my mother's silver had been sold three hard winters ago along with everything else that ever glittered in my childhood, and what remained aboard the *Kestrel* was either essential or sentimental, and sentiment doesn't clear accounts.

There was a fourth option, and it lived upriver, and I poured myself two fingers of the cooking brandy and told it to go away.

* * *

Morning found me on the foredeck pretending to inspect the mooring lines, which is a thing I do when I want to stand somewhere and think without being asked questions. The river was doing its Tuesday trick — low and glassy, sandbars showing their pale shoulders everywhere, the whole channel sulking between them like a path that resents being walked on.

Old-timers say every sandbar has a mood. My father taught me that before he taught me to read. You learn their tempers or you learn the bottom of the river, one or the other, and usually both in the same afternoon.

I heard them before I saw them — voices carrying across the still water the way voices do at dawn, when the air hasn't woken up yet and takes everything personally. Bram and Odile, on the aft deck, speaking low. Speaking the way people speak when they think the person they're speaking about is asleep.

"—not forever," Odile was saying. "Just till she's clear. There's a cannery boat out of Port Loman taking deckhands. Two weeks' work, cash, and we come back with enough to cover the interest ourselves."

"And if she finds out?"

"She won't. You saw her this morning. She's already decided we don't notice things."

A long pause. Somewhere downriver a heron complained about the state of the world, and I sympathized completely.

"She'd never take it," Bram said finally. "You know she wouldn't. Pride like hers, it's not pride anymore, it's load-bearing. Pull it out and the whole house comes down."

"So we don't ask. We just go earn it and hand it over and call it charter wages. She'll grumble. She'll grumble and take it, because she has to, and then she'll hate herself quietly for a month, and then it'll be fine."

"That's a terrible plan."

"It's the only plan that works on her. You've known her eight years. Have you ever once seen her accept help she didn't pay for?"

Another pause, longer. I stood very still among my mooring lines, feeling like a burglar in my own house.

"No," Bram admitted. "But I've seen her refuse it right up until the roof caved in."

"Well." Odile's voice went dry as August. "Then we'd best make sure the roof holds, hadn't we."

They moved off toward the galley, boots thumping the deck, and I stayed where I was and stared at the sandbars and thought about load-bearing pride.

Here's what I wanted to do: march back there and tell them both to stop scheming behind my back like I was some fragile aunt at a funeral. Here's what I actually understood, standing there with the letter crinkling in my pocket: they were doing exactly what a family does. Families lie to each other kindly. Families carry each other's weights when the other one isn't looking. And if I confronted them, they'd deny it, and then they'd feel watched, and then next time they'd scheme further from the boat, and eventually further from me, and I'd have my dignity and an empty deck.

So I did the harder thing. I walked into the galley, poured myself coffee, and said, "Port Loman's cannery fleet pays terribly and the smell gets into everything. If either of you ever considers it, negotiate hard. Deckhands always undersell themselves."

Odile froze over the stove with her back to me. Bram studied his coffee like it contained scripture.

"We were just saying—" Odile began.

"You were saying the number two cylinder needs babying and the mending pile's getting tall. Both true. Carry on." I took my cup and left, and behind me the silence had a texture to it, guilty and fond, and I hated how much comfort I took from it.

Fear masked as command. That's the captain's uniform, and I wear it well. Nobody aboard needs to see the seams.

* * *

By afternoon I'd sold the salvage winch to a barge man named Tull who cheated me honestly, to his credit — he told me upfront he was going to cheat me, and then did it, and somehow that felt cleaner than the bank's polite stationery. Two hundred and eleven short became two hundred and forty-one short, since the winch money went to fuel and a part Bram swore the engine couldn't live without, and he was right, and I didn't regret it, and I regretted it simultaneously. Captains are capable of holding both.

Evening came down gold and slow. We tied up at Dock Nine, our home slip, and I climbed to the pilothouse to log the day and found Wren — no. Not yet. Found nobody. Found the empty wheel and the empty chair and the river going bronze, and I stood there a long time doing nothing but breathing, which some days is the whole job.

The letter came out of my pocket. I smoothed it flat against the wheel housing and read it a third time, slowly, like an enemy's face. *Repossession.* *Seven days.* *Regret.* Banks love the word *regret*. They deploy it the way priests deploy *blessing* — automatically, and meaning roughly the opposite.

"One week," I told the river.

The river, being a river, said nothing. But the current had shifted with the tide, and the nearest sandbar — the long pale one we called the Widow, because it had ended more hulls than heartbreak — had changed its mood overnight. Where yesterday it showed a smooth shoulder, today it wore a broken crown of ripples, mean and white-flecked, the current tearing across its tail.

New bar forming. New danger, or new channel, depending on how you read it. Depending on whether you were the sort who saw the river closing or the river offering.

I put the letter away and gripped the wheel, though we weren't going anywhere, and made myself a promise I had no business making: seven days. Whatever it took, whoever I had to swallow my pride in front of — even, God forbid, men I'd sworn off forever — the *Kestrel* would still be at Dock Nine when the clipboards came.

As if the river heard me, a launch came around the bend downstream, white-hulled, fast, flying colors I recognized before I wanted to.

Colors I'd hoped never to see again.

It was heading for Dock Nine.
