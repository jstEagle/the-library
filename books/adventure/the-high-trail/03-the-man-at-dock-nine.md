## 3. The Man at Dock Nine

There are two kinds of men who stand on your dock waiting for you, and I've learned to tell them apart by where they put their hands.

The kind who want something small — a fare, a favor, a few coins' worth of freight — fidget. They hold their hats, they shift their weight, they look at the boat instead of the woman. The kind who want something large go very still. They plant themselves like fence posts and let *you* do all the moving around them, because they already know how the conversation ends and they're generous enough to let you pretend otherwise for a while.

The man at Dock Nine had his hands folded behind his back like a man posing for a portrait nobody had asked him to sit for.

I saw him from the wheelhouse before I ever tied off, and I'll be honest with you: my first thought was not *Silas Vane*. My first thought was *that's a fence post*, and my second thought was to run us aground on purpose rather than dock anywhere near it. But the Kestrel was carrying four hundred pounds of rice that belonged to a grocer in Marrow Bend who paid late but paid, and pride is a luxury I'd sold off years ago along with most of everything else. So I brought her in slow, past the post of a man, and watched him watch me with the patience of somebody who has never once in his life been kept waiting against his will.

He'd aged well, damn him. That was the first obscenity of the afternoon. Twenty years should have done more damage than that. The hair at his temples had gone silver in a way that looked deliberate, like he'd ordered it from a catalog, and the lines around his eyes were the kind that come from smiling at people right before taking their money. He wore a coat that cost more than my boat's engine, which is not saying much this year, but still.

"Hazel Thornbury," he said, when I came down the gangway. Not a question. A man announcing he's found a thing he'd marked on a map.

"Vane." I said it flat, the way you'd say *mud* or *leech*. "You're standing on my dock."

"Your dock." He glanced down at the boards, amused. "I understood the bank held the paper on this vessel."

"The dock's mine. The boat's mine until Thursday of some week I'm not thinking about. Everything else is negotiable, which is why you're here, so let's skip to it."

That earned me one raised eyebrow, which from Silas Vane was practically applause. "Still all edges. Your father was the same way."

And there it was. Twenty seconds in. He hadn't even unpacked his real business yet and already he'd reached into the drawer where I keep the worst thing I own and put his fingers right on it.

"Don't," I said.

"Don't what?"

"You know what. You don't get to say his name like you knew him. You knew him the way a knife knows bread."

Something flickered across his face then — quick, gone, maybe regret, maybe just indigestion — and I chose to believe indigestion, because the alternative meant he was human, and I have built a whole life on the foundation of Silas Vane being a species of weather. Weather you can dress for. Weather that eventually moves on.

"I'm not here about your father," he said.

"Then you're wasting both our mornings."

"Am I? Because I'm told the Kestrel hasn't turned a profit since spring flood. I'm told her captain owes the Merchants' Bank eleven hundred marks against hull and gear, and that the note comes due—" he consulted a pocket watch, an actual pocket watch, the affectation of it made my teeth ache, "—in nine days."

Word travels fast when it travels downhill, and my finances had been rolling downhill for two years straight. Still. Hearing it said out loud, in that smooth voice, on my own dock, was like hearing a doctor read your chart back to you in front of company.

"Nine days," I repeated. "You did your arithmetic."

"I always do. It's why I'm rich and you're—" he gestured at the Kestrel, at the rust bleeding down her stack, at Odile's laundry strung along the rail like flags of surrender, "—you."

* * *

Here's what you need to know about Silas Vane, and I'll give it to you plain because the rest of this story won't have time to be fair to anybody.

Twenty-two years ago, my father ran the Serrano. Ran it better than anyone — he could read that river the way priests read scripture, every sandbar a verse, every snag a footnote. And then came the season the charter papers went wrong. Falsified tonnage declarations, they said later, cargo manifests signed in a hand that wasn't his, and when the river council came down on it, the man standing in the witness box pointing at my father — calm, precise, sorrowful as a headstone — was his own first mate. A young mate with bright prospects and no family name worth anything, testifying that Captain Thornbury had pressured him into signing papers he knew were crooked.

My father lost his charter, his boat, and within three years, quietly, his health, and then everything else. The young mate went on to buy his first boat with testimony money and never looked back. That boy's name was Silas Vane. He is now the second-richest man on this stretch of river, and I am the woman whose boat the bank wants back in nine days, and between those two facts runs a straight line twenty-two years long, and I have walked it in my head so many times the path is worn smooth as glass.

So yes. When Vane says he's not here about my father, what he means is he's here about everything my father left me, which is the river itself and my inability to stay off it.

* * *

He made his offer the way such men always do — sideways first, circling, testing the ice.

"Freight rates are up," he observed.

"Freight rates are up."

"And yet you're running rice."

"Rice pays."

"Rice pays *poorly*, Hazel. Grocers pay late. Salvage contracts pay worse. Meanwhile there's work on this river paying triple rate, and the only captains qualified to take it are—" he spread one hand, gracious as a bishop, "—scarce."

I leaned against the rail and looked at him. Up close he smelled of cedar and good tobacco, money distilled into scent. "Name the route."

"Serrano headwaters. High Landing and above."

The word landed in my chest like a dropped anchor. Above. Upriver. The Serrano above the last council checkpoint — the reach my father ran and lost everything on, the reach I swore off at nineteen with my hand flat on his coffin lid, the reach every sensible pilot gives a wide berth even in dry season, because the syndicate owns every dock on it and the syndicate does not forgive trespasses, and monsoon season turns its gorges into something out of the old testament.

"No," I said.

"You haven't heard the terms."

"I don't need the terms. The answer's no. It was no before you opened your mouth, and it'll be no tomorrow, and if you come back Thursday it'll be no with interest, because Thursdays are bad for me lately."

"Hazel—"

"Triple rate." I laughed, and it came out ugly. "You know what triple rate buys, Vane? It buys silence. It buys a crew that stops asking questions. It buys a captain doing something she swore on a grave she'd never do, and then it buys everybody forgetting she did it — except her, at three in the morning, forever. That's what triple rate buys. I know the exchange rate. My family paid it once already."

For a moment — just a moment — the fence-post stillness cracked, and underneath it I saw something move. Something that might have been shame, if shame could survive twenty-two years of compound interest. Then it sealed over again, smooth as river ice, and he was the portrait again.

"It's a legitimate contract," he said. "Sealed cargo, single delivery, before landfall. The client is discreet and pays half in advance. Half, Hazel. In advance."

Half in advance. Eleven hundred marks against hull and gear. Nine days. The numbers did their little dance in my head, and I hated them for dancing, and I hated him for playing the tune.

"Who's the client?"

"That's between the client and me."

"Then we're finished."

"You're finished in nine days whether you take this run or not." He said it without heat, without cruelty even, which was somehow worse — the tone of a man reading a tide table. "The bank doesn't care about your father. The river doesn't care about your oath. In nine days the Kestrel goes on the block, and some syndicate buyer strips her engines and moors her as a floating warehouse, and you pilot other men's boats until your hands give out. I've seen it happen to better pilots than you. I'm offering you the only door out of that room, and you're refusing it because of a grudge older than my coat."

"My grudge is older than your *fortune*."

"Yes," he said softly. "It is. That's rather the point."

He took out a card — heavy stock, embossed, absurd — and set it on the bollard between us. An address in Marrow Bend. Nothing else.

"The offer stands through Friday. After landfall, the window closes, and so does every other window, for everyone on this river. Think about what that means." He settled his hat. "Your father would have taken this run."

"My father," I said, "would have thrown you off this dock."

"He tried, once." And Silas Vane smiled — the first true smile of the afternoon, and the worst thing I'd seen all year — and walked away down Dock Nine with his hands folded behind his back, a man leaving a church he owned.

* * *

I stood there a long time after he was gone. Long enough that Bram came looking, found me gripping the rail, and had the good sense — rare in Bram — to say nothing. He just leaned beside me and spat into the brown water and waited, because that's what crew does; they wait out your weather same as the sky's.

"Silas Vane," I finally said.

Bram whistled low. "The Vane? The one from the trial?"

"The very one."

"What'd he want?"

"Us. Upriver. Triple rate."

Bram considered this with the gravity he gave all financial matters, which is to say the gravity of a starving man considering a menu. "Triple rate," he said carefully, "is a lot of rate."

"I know what it is."

"And the route's the Serrano. The sworn-off one."

"You were there when I swore it, Bram. You held the lantern."

"I held the lantern, yeah." He scratched under his cap. "Hazel, can I say a thing you'll want to throw me in the river for?"

"You say those daily. Go ahead."

"An oath's a fine thing. But oaths don't make payments. And that girl Odile's got a sister needing medicine, and I've got—" he stopped, the way he always stops at the edge of his own history, "—I've got reasons to want solid ground under us. Triple rate is solid ground. Even if the ground's upstream."

I looked at him. He looked at the water. Somewhere down the dock, gulls were fighting over a fish head, screaming like tiny creditors.

"We're not taking it," I said.

"No," Bram agreed. "We're not."

Neither of us moved. Neither of us believed either sentence, and we both knew it, and the knowing sat there between us on the rail like a third crew member nobody wanted to feed.

* * *

That night I couldn't sleep, which was nothing new, but the shape of the sleeplessness was different — sharper, angrier, wearing a good coat. I climbed up to the wheelhouse with the lamp turned low and stood at the window watching the dock lights of Marrow Bend smear themselves across the current.

The Serrano headwaters. High Landing and above. I could close my eyes and see the whole reach — I'd memorized it as a child off my father's charts, traced every gorge with my finger while he laughed and said *someday, little eel, someday I'll show you the real thing.* Someday never came. Someday drowned with him, and I'd spent twenty-two years pretending the river ended at the checkpoint, pretending the map simply stopped, the way children think the world ends at the edge of town.

And now the man who helped drown him was standing on my deck offering to sell me the far side of the map at triple rate.

Here's the part I don't say out loud, the part I barely admit to myself at three in the morning with the lamp turned low: some traitor part of me wanted it. Not the money — well, God, yes, the money — but the river. The forbidden water. The chance to run the reach he never got to finish, to see what he saw, to stand where he stood and prove — to whom? To a dead man? To a river that doesn't care? — that a Thornbury could run it clean.

That's the trap of rage, I think. It keeps the wound open on purpose, because an open wound is the only place the dead can still talk to you. Close it, and you lose them twice.

I stood there until the lamp burned down, and the last thing I thought before I gave up on sleep was Vane's parting line, replaying with the smugness intact: *the offer stands through Friday.*

Friday. Three days. Plenty of time to keep saying no.

I went below past the crew bunks, quiet as I could, and I noticed — though I told myself I didn't — that Bram's bunk was empty, and that through the thin bulkhead I could hear his low voice and Odile's, talking too quietly to catch, stopping the moment my boot creaked on the stair.

Talking about rate, probably. Talking about triple rate, and sisters, and solid ground.

I lay down in my berth and stared at the overhead and told myself the crew was loyal, the answer was no, and the river ended at the checkpoint.

All three lies floated around me in the dark like driftwood, bumping gently against the hull.

Outside, far upriver, past the checkpoint, past the gorges, the monsoon was already gathering over the headwaters — and somewhere in Marrow Bend, a man with a pocket watch was counting down my nine days, patient as a fence post, certain as weather.
