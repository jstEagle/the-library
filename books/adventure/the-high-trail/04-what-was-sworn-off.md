## 4. What Was Sworn Off

Sleep didn't come so much as it ambushed me, and even then it didn't have the decency to stay honest.

I dreamed of the Serrano the way I always do — not as a river but as a throat, green and deep and swallowing. My father stood at the wheel of the *Marrow*, his old boat, and he was laughing at something I couldn't hear, and the water was rising around his boots, and no matter how fast I ran down the dock I never reached him before —

I woke with my hand fisted in the blanket and the cabin lamp still burning, because I'd left it burning on purpose. Some habits you keep. The dark on the Serrano is a different animal than the dark anywhere else, and I'd learned that at eleven years old, the night my father's name stopped being worth anything.

You'll want the story. Everyone wants the story eventually — Vane wanted it when he stood on Dock Nine smiling at me like we were old dance partners, Bram wants it though he's too polite to ask, Odile probably already knows half of it from dock gossip and has assembled the rest out of inference, which is her way. So here it is, once, in the dark, where it lives.

* * *

Twenty-two years ago, the Serrano wasn't closed water. It was contested water, which is worse, but at least it was honest about being a fight.

My father, Elias Thornbury, ran the *Marrow* upriver twice a season carrying charter papers, survey gear, and whatever cargo the river council had licensed that year. He was the best reader of water I've ever known, myself included, and I say that without false modesty because false modesty is just another kind of lie and my father raised me to hate lies almost as much as he hated Silas Vane.

He hadn't hated Vane yet, back then. That's the part nobody tells you — how the hatred arrives late, dressed as friendship, holding a bottle of something good and talking about the future.

Vane was younger then, hungry in the way that reads as ambition if you're feeling generous and as appetite if you're not. He worked the lower docks for one of the trading houses — Corvus Consolidated, though the name meant nothing to us then, just another set of initials on a bill of lading. He and my father drank together. Played cards. Vane lost gracefully and often, which should have been the first warning. A man who loses that well is buying something.

The summer I turned eleven, the council opened bidding on new charters for the upper Serrano — the gorge country, the deep water past High Landing where the trade routes would go if anyone could prove they were navigable. Whoever held those charters would own the river's spine for a generation. Every boat owner with a hull and a prayer put in a bid. My father did. So did three others whose names I've forgotten, because history keeps only the winners and the men who get blamed.

My father was asked to carry the survey party. Not to bid — to *carry*. To run the *Marrow* up through the narrows with the council's surveyors aboard and their instruments on his deck, and to log every channel, every sandbar, every depth of every reach, in his own hand, in his own book.

He came home from that meeting walking two inches taller. I remember because I measured myself against him in the doorway and noticed he'd grown somehow, which is impossible, and which is also exactly what happens to a man who's been told he's trusted.

"Three weeks," he told my mother, kissing her hair. "Then we'll know if the Thornburys are going to be rich or just comfortable."

He took me partway on the first leg — down to the confluence, where the Serrano meets the lowland channels, before the water turns serious. I stood on the foredeck and he let me hold the sounding line, and I can still feel the weight of it, the wet rope sliding through my palms, the numbers he called out like a litany. *Twelve feet. Nine. Nine and a half.* The river talking to us through the lead weight, and my father listening the way other men listen to music.

That's what reading water is, if you've never done it. It isn't looking. It's listening. The surface tells you everything if you know its language — the way current wrinkles over a shallow bar, the way debris rides a seam between two flows, the color changes where the bottom drops away. My father spoke it fluently. He wrote it all down, every day, in a logbook with a cracked leather cover, in handwriting that slanted forward like a man leaning into wind.

I never saw that logbook again until much later, and that's getting ahead of the story.

* * *

What happened upriver, I know in pieces. Some of it I learned from my mother, who learned it from the inquiry transcripts. Some of it I learned from dock talk, which is a sewer that occasionally runs clear. And some of it I learned twenty years afterward from a man I'm not ready to forgive yet, sitting in a cliffside mission with his eyes going bad, telling me things I'd spent my whole life building walls against.

But here is the shape of it, the shape I carried for years:

The survey went wrong. That was the official finding. The *Marrow*'s charts were found to be falsified — depths recorded that didn't exist, channels marked safe that weren't, sandbars omitted entirely. Two of the council's surveyors nearly drowned running a reach my father had logged as twelve feet deep. It was four. They survived; the instruments didn't; the survey was voided; and Elias Thornbury, master of the *Marrow*, was named before the full river council as either incompetent or corrupt, and since incompetence doesn't explain forged entries in a professional logbook, everyone chose corrupt.

His bid was thrown out. His license was suspended pending investigation. His name — which on that river was currency, was collateral, was the whole family fortune — became a byword. *Thornbury water,* dockmen started calling any channel that looked honest and wasn't. Children used it as a game. I heard them playing it outside our window, and my mother pulled the curtain shut, and neither of us said anything, and that silence lasted longer than my father did.

Because here's the thing about disgrace: it doesn't kill you quickly. It kills you in installments. First the work goes, then the credit, then the friends, then the ones who were never really friends but you'd counted them anyway. My father sold the *Marrow* inside a year. He sat in a chair by our window looking at the river he could no longer touch, and he drank, and he said very little, except once — once — when he'd had enough rum in him to be dangerous to himself.

"I logged it true," he said. "Every foot of it. I logged it true, Hazel-girl, and someone changed it."

I was twelve. I didn't understand what he was saying, or rather I understood it completely and dismissed it, because the alternative was believing that the world had simply decided to eat my father alive for sport, and a child can't live in a world like that. So I filed it away under *grief-talk* and *rum* and didn't take it back out for twenty years.

He died when I was sixteen. Quietly, in the chair, in the winter. The doctor called it his heart. My mother called it the river. I didn't call it anything. I just went down to the docks the next morning and signed on as deckhand on a coal barge, because somebody had to earn, and because I thought — I genuinely thought this at sixteen — that if I worked hard enough and read water well enough, I could wash the name clean by sheer friction.

It doesn't work that way. Names don't scrub. But you can build a new one alongside the old, and that's what I did, year over year, bilge over deckhand over pilot, until I had enough saved and enough reputation to buy a broken-down sternwheeler off a bankrupt shipping line and rename her the *Kestrel*.

A kestrel hovers. Did you know that? It hangs in the air over one spot, watching, patient, riding wind that would knock anything else out of the sky. My father used to point them out along the bluffs. *Look at her hold,* he'd say. *Nothing moves her but her own decision.*

I named the boat after that. Then I made one rule for myself, carved into the beam above my own wheel where I'd see it every day of my working life:

**No Serrano runs.**

Not because the river scared me. Because the route was where my father died, in the slow installment plan, and because going back felt like agreeing with the verdict. As long as I stayed off the Serrano, some stubborn corner of me could keep insisting the truth was otherwise. Take the route, and I'd have to find out.

* * *

And Vane. You're waiting for Vane.

Here's what I knew for twenty years: Silas Vane testified before the river council. He was working the lower docks for Corvus Consolidated that summer, and he testified that my father had approached him — approached *him* — offering to sell falsified survey data to whichever bidder paid best. That testimony was the hinge. Without a witness, forged logbook entries might have been an error, a clerk's mistake, anything. With Vane standing there in his good coat saying *Elias Thornbury offered to sell me the river*, the verdict was sealed.

My mother confronted him once, outside the council hall. I was there. I was thirteen and furious and I remember every word of it the way you remember a scar.

"He wouldn't," my mother said. "Whatever they told you to say, he wouldn't."

Mrs. Thornbury." Vane's voice was gentle. That's what I can't get over, even now — how gentle he was. "I'm sorry. I reported what was offered to me. If I'm wrong, the inquiry will show it."

The inquiry showed nothing of the kind. Inquiries rarely investigate the witness, only the accused. And Vane went on to bigger things — a berth on a proper packet boat, then command, then ownership, then the smooth gray coat and the easy smile and the reputation as the fairest trader on the lowland runs. He built all of it on the foundation of my father's ruin, whether he mixed the mortar himself or not.

For twenty years I believed he'd lied for money. For twenty years that belief kept me upright. It gave the disaster a villain, and a disaster with a villain is survivable in a way that a disaster without one never is.

I am telling you now, honestly, that I don't know yet — writing this in the dark of my cabin with the lamp burning — how much of what I believed will still be standing by the time this voyage ends. There are things I learned recently that complicate the picture. There is a word — *coerced* — that I have been turning over like a stone, looking for what's underneath it. But that comes later in the account, and I'm not ready, and the lamp is burning low, and some truths you have to walk toward slowly or they spook.

What matters tonight is simpler. What matters tonight is that Silas Vane stood on Dock Nine yesterday and offered me triple rate to run the Serrano, and I told him no, and I meant it with every scar I own.

* * *

Except.

Here's the trouble with families — the blood kind and the chosen kind both. You can tell them your rules. You can carve them into the beam above the wheel. You can explain, in the language of scars, why there are waters you won't take them into. And they will nod, and they will agree, and they will love you.

And then they will sit down below decks while you sleep badly, and they will count the days until the bank takes the boat, and they will look at each other across the galley table, and they will decide that your rule is a luxury the family can't afford.

I woke to gray light and engine noise.

That was wrong on two counts. We weren't scheduled to move until afternoon tide, and the engine noise was wrong for the *Kestrel* — too heavy, too rhythmic, a big diesel idling against our mooring lines. I was up and out of the cabin with my boots unlaced before my head finished waking, and I came around the deckhouse into a scene I will be seeing behind my eyelids for a long time.

Bram was on the bow, coiling lines with the methodical calm he brings to everything, including, apparently, mutiny. Odile stood at the gangway talking to a harbor official, papers in hand — clearance papers, stamped, real. And tied alongside, wallowing fat and black-hulled, was a fuel barge flying the house flag of Vane's outfit, hose already run aboard, our tanks filling with someone else's money.

"What," I said, "is this."

Nobody flinched, which told me they'd rehearsed. Odile turned, and she had the decency to look like a woman delivering a diagnosis instead of a knife.

"We voted," she said.

"You voted."

"Last night. After you turned in. Three to one in favor, with Bram abstaining until he heard your side of it." She held up the clearance papers. "We're taking the job, Hazel. Up the Serrano, ahead of landfall, triple rate. Half paid already — it cleared the bank this morning. Enough to stop the repossession cold."

The fuel gauge on the barge ticked upward. Somewhere below decks, Wren — no, not Wren, not yet, that comes later, though I confess the memory has grown teeth and bites backward — somewhere below, the crate sat in the hold, and armed men watched from the far end of the dock, and none of us knew yet what we were actually carrying.

"Bram," I said. "You abstained."

He set down the coiled line and faced me, and I'll give him this: he didn't look away. "I told them I'd stand wherever you landed. But I voted with my hands this morning, Captain, same as they did. Somebody's got to pay for this boat, and it isn't going to be the bank."

"The Serrano killed my father."

"No," Odile said quietly. "A lie killed your father. The river's just water." She came closer, and lowered her voice, and for a moment she wasn't first mate delivering a decision, she was the closest thing I had to a conscience with a face. "You can fire us. All of us, right now, on this dock. You can cast off alone and lose the boat in nine days and be proud and broke and right. Or you can hate us for a week and keep your family fed. Those are the choices. We made ours before dawn."

The barge finished pumping. The hose came aboard, dripping. Downriver, the sky had that bruised green-gray look that means the monsoon front is gathering itself beyond the coast, counting the days until it owns everything.

I stood on my own deck, on my own boat, among my own people, and understood that I had never been so thoroughly overruled in my life — and that they had done it not despite loving me but because of it, which is the cruelest engineering there is.

"Cast off in an hour," I heard myself say. "I'll take her up. God help every one of you."

Odile nodded once and went to sign the last of the papers. Bram touched the beam above the wheel — the beam with my father's rule carved into it — and said nothing, which from Bram was a whole speech.

And I stood at the wheel of the *Kestrel*, watching the Serrano's mouth open green and deep and swallowing in the distance, and I thought: *someone changed the logbook.*

I didn't know yet why that thought had come back after twenty years. I didn't know yet what was in the hold, or who wanted it delivered, or what it would cost.

I only knew that the river I'd sworn off was about to have me anyway — and that this time, I intended to listen to every word it said.
