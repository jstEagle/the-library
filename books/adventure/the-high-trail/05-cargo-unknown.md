## 5. Cargo Unknown

There are two kinds of money on the river: the kind you earn, and the kind that arrives with its collar up and its eyes down. I've been running cargo out of Marrow's Bend for eleven years, first as deckhand, then as Odile Marchand, purser and quartermaster of the *Kestrel*, keeper of the ledger and the only person aboard who reads the fine print before signing. So believe me when I tell you I know the smell of trouble wrapped in banknotes.

It smelled like bay rum and nervous sweat, and it was standing at the foot of our gangplank at half past six in the morning, holding a leather case like it might bite him.

"Marchand?" he said. He was a small man, soft in the middle, dressed too well for the Bend — dove-grey suit, polished boots, a hat that had never seen rain. Nobody wears a hat like that to a working dock unless they've been told to.

"That depends on who's asking and what it costs me."

"Hollis. I represent a private party." He said *private party* the way some men say *the police* — carefully, with a glance over his shoulder. "Captain Thornbury accepted my terms last night. Half now, half on delivery. One crate, one destination."

"One crate," I repeated. We'd hauled everything from salt pork to coffins in my time aboard, but a single sealed crate paying triple rate sat wrong in my stomach the way bad oysters do — you don't know yet why it'll hurt you, only that it will. "Weight?"

"Under four hundred pounds. It must not be opened."

"There it is," I said. "There's always a line like that."

Hollis smiled without any warmth reaching his eyes. "Standard practice for fragile goods."

"Fragile goods get marked fragile. Fragile goods come with manifests." I held out my hand anyway, because we were three days from repossession and pride is a luxury item I stopped being able to afford around the time the bank started sending letters in red ink. "Papers."

He produced them from the case — clean, official, boring. Consignment papers for agricultural equipment, consignee listed as a factor at High Landing. The ink was fresh enough that if I'd held the page to my nose I'd have smelled it drying. Everything correct. Everything correct is its own kind of wrong; real paperwork has smudges, has history, has a clerk somewhere who hated his job. This had been born yesterday in a rich man's office.

I counted the money twice. Half upfront meant half of triple rate, which meant more cash than the *Kestrel* had seen since Hazel's father ran her, and I watched it disappear into my strongbox with the particular shame of a woman who knows she's just been bought and is grateful for it.

"The crate arrives at nine," Hollis said. "Your crew loads it amidships, lashed and covered. No questions at checkpoints — you're carrying farm machinery, nothing else."

"Farm machinery that can't be opened."

"Farm machinery," he agreed, "that would be ruined by river air."

* * *

The crate came at nine exactly, which told me something too. Poor people are late because poor people's lives have loose screws. Rich people's schedules run on rails laid by other people's money.

It came on a flat cart drawn by two mules and escorted by four men who walked like soldiers trying to look like dockhands. They didn't help unload. They stood with their hands folded and watched us take possession the way a hawk watches a snake it hasn't decided to strike yet.

The crate itself was beautiful. That was the first thing I noticed — beautiful, and wrong. River freight gets built ugly and heavy, pine and iron banding, made to be dropped and cursed at. This was close-grained hardwood, joints fitted tight as a coffin's, no markings at all except a brass plate stamped with a number. It weighed less than four hundred pounds should feel. When Bram and I shifted it up the plank, it moved with a strange, balanced lightness, like something inside was positioned just so.

Like someone had packed it by hand. With care.

"Farm machinery," Bram said under his breath as we set it amidships. He's a big man, Bram, slow to speak and slower to anger, with hands like mooring cleats and a face that gives away nothing he doesn't want it to. But I've shared a galley with him for six years, and I heard the flat note under the words. "What kind of farm machinery comes in a coffin box with an honor guard?"

"The profitable kind," I said.

"Mm." He straightened, rolled his shoulders, and looked past me down the dock — that long, unhurried look he does, taking in the whole picture the way he takes in weather. Then he said, very quietly, "Don't turn your head. Far end, by the chandlery. Two men. Been there since before dawn."

I busied myself coiling a line that didn't need coiling. Looked. Two figures under the chandlery awning, doing an excellent impression of men with nowhere to be and all day to be there. One smoked. The other didn't, and the one who didn't was the problem, because his hands kept moving — checking something at his belt, adjusting his coat. Men waiting for a boat they own do that.

"Friends of Hollis?"

"Maybe. Or friends of whoever Hollis is afraid of." Bram picked up a coil of rope and slung it over his shoulder, using the motion to turn us both toward the wheelhouse. "There was a skiff out on the water this morning, too. Anchored off the point since first light. Just sitting."

"Just sitting."

"Fishing boats fish, Odile. That one was admiring the view."

We got the crate lashed down amidships, covered with canvas, and I threw the tarpaulin corners over it with more force than the job required. Under my hands, through the wood, I thought — and I'll swear to this on any ledger you like — I thought I felt it shift. A small settling, a redistribution of weight. The way a sack of grain settles. The way a person settles when they've been still too long and are trying not to fidget.

I told myself it was the current rocking the hull.

I'm a bookkeeper. I know when the numbers don't add. And every number about this job added up beautifully except the ones that mattered: one crate, no manifest worth trusting, four soldiers in dockhand clothes, two watchers under an awning, one patient skiff, and a broker who sweated through a dove-grey suit in morning cool enough to see your breath.

Against that column: half of triple rate, paid in full, sitting in my strongbox. Enough to make the bank's red letters go away. Enough to keep Bram fed and Odile — forgive me, that's me — keep me in ink and paper and the small dignity of a paid berth. Enough that Hazel had signed without reading the fine print, which she never does, which tells you exactly how deep the water was rising around us.

Unease beneath relief. That's the honest name for what I felt all that loading day, and I write it here so that later — when it all went the way such cargoes go — nobody can say the *Kestrel*'s purser wasn't keeping accurate books.

* * *

By noon we were fueled, watered, and provisioned, and the *Kestrel* sat low and eager against her lines, ready to eat the miles upriver. Hazel came down the gangplank from the harbor office with her cap pulled low and her jaw set at the angle I've learned means she's lost an argument with herself and won't admit it.

"Well?" Bram asked her.

"We clear the bar on the afternoon tide." She looked at the covered crate a moment too long. "Vane's launch will meet us at the Serrano mouth day after tomorrow. Handoff of final instructions."

"You've met Vane before," Bram said. Not a question. Everyone on this river knows that story, or the shape of it — a father disgraced, a rival's testimony, a name dragged through the mud of a hearing twenty years old.

"I've met him." Hazel's voice could have frozen the bilge water. She turned away, checked a lashing that was already perfect, and I understood that whatever history rode upriver with us, it wasn't going to be discussed on an open dock with hawk-eyed men under awnings.

Hollis appeared one last time at the foot of the plank, hat in hand, sweat back despite the hour. He pressed a folded paper into my palm — delivery instructions, a seal, a signature block left blank.

"Half on delivery," he reminded me. "At High Landing. The consignee will confirm the crate arrived—" he hesitated, just a flicker, "—intact."

"Intact," I repeated. "And unopened."

"And unopened." He almost managed a smile. It died halfway, like a lamp running out of oil. For one strange moment he looked at me with something that might have been pity, or warning, or just the universal expression of a man who has sold something he shouldn't have and knows it. Then he tipped his ridiculous hat, turned, and walked away fast, heels clicking on the planks like a clock running down.

Bram drifted to my shoulder. "Still there?"

I glanced back. Under the chandlery awning, the smoking man had gone. The one who didn't smoke remained, and now he wasn't pretending anymore — he was watching the *Kestrel* openly, arms crossed, making no effort whatsoever to hide it. Letting us see. *We know where you are*, that stance said. *We know what you carry.*

And out past the point, right on schedule, the skiff raised anchor and began, lazily, to follow.

I closed my ledger. In the margin of the page where I'd recorded the payment — half received, half pending, cargo: agricultural equipment, one crate — I wrote a small private notation, the kind I make when the books balance but the world doesn't:

*Paid too well. Watched too closely. Lighter than it should be.*

Then I went below to tell Hazel that whatever we were carrying up the Serrano, half the river already knew about it — and the half that knew was armed.
