## 6. The Stowaway

I've hauled a lot of cargo in my years on the Kestrel. Crates of salt pork, bales of raw cotton, a piano once — don't ask, the story involves a drunk magistrate and a bet I still maintain I didn't lose. But in all that time, no cargo has ever asked me for a drink of water.

That's how it started, anyway. Second day out, just past the bend where the Serrano widens and the current goes lazy as a Sunday afternoon. I was down in the hold doing the morning check — Bram's Law: you count your crates every day, because the river takes what you don't watch — and I heard it. Small sound. A cough, quickly smothered.

Now, a man who works boats learns to sort sounds. Water against hull is one thing. Cargo shifting is another. Rats are a third, and I'd have preferred rats. Rats you can drown.

"Cargo doesn't cough," I said to the dark.

Silence. The kind of silence that has a heartbeat in it.

I stood there among the stacked crates with my lantern held high, doing arithmetic. We'd loaded eleven crates at Dock Nine under the eye of that nervous broker with his sweat-ringed collar and his half-payment in sealed notes. Eleven crates of "machinery parts," he'd said, which was funny because machinery parts don't usually get armed men loitering on the dock pretending to be idle. I'd counted them twice at loading because Odile made me, and Odile being Odile, she'd counted them a third time herself.

Eleven crates then. Twelve now.

The twelfth sat wedged back in the corner behind the water barrels, and it wasn't a crate at all when you looked close. It was a girl. Sixteen maybe, folded up small in the gap between barrel and bulkhead like she'd practiced being invisible, and I'll grant her this — she'd nearly managed it. Dark hair cut short and uneven, like she'd done it herself with a knife and a grudge. Eyes too old for the face they were set in. She wore a boy's coat two sizes wrong and boots that had been mended more times than most boats I've known.

"Evening," she said, before I could say anything, which threw me. Stowaways aren't supposed to open negotiations.

"It's morning."

"Is it." She said it like mornings were a rumor she'd heard about.

"How long have you been aboard?"

"Since Dock Nine."

"That crate was nailed shut."

"I un-nailed it." She lifted her chin. "You people nail things shut very badly. I could hear everything through the wood. Your cook talks to herself. A lot."

"That's Odile, and she's not a cook, she's our engineer, and if she hears you say 'cook' she'll feed you to the paddlewheel."

Something flickered across the girl's face — not fear exactly. Assessment. Like she was filing me away in some ledger she kept behind those eyes.

"You're the big one," she said. "Bram. You count things."

"I count things," I agreed. It's what I'm for, mostly. Hazel reads the water, Odile argues with the engine, and I count what's left.

"And you haven't shouted for the captain yet."

"No," I said. "I haven't."

"Why not?"

Good question. I'd been asking myself the same thing since the cough. Part of it was simple: turning a stowaway over meant stopping the boat, and stopping the boat meant explaining to Hazel why we were a day out with twelve souls instead of eleven crates, and Hazel had enough weight on her shoulders to ballast a freighter. But part of it — the part I didn't want to look at straight — was that the girl looked like she hadn't eaten properly in a week, and I've got a rule about that. My mother raised me on a river where you fed anyone who showed up hungry, no questions until after the second bowl. She said questions keep better on a full stomach.

So instead of shouting, I did the stupid thing. I went and got bread and salt pork from the galley, and a tin cup of water, and brought it down into the hold like an offering to something wild.

She watched me the whole way, coiled, ready to bolt — though where she thought she'd bolt to, midriver, I couldn't say. She took the food only after I set it down and stepped back, and then she ate like someone who'd learned eating was a race you could lose. I turned my back to give her dignity about it. When I looked again, the plate was clean.

"What's your name?" I asked.

A pause. Long enough that I thought she wouldn't answer.

"Wren," she said finally. Like she was testing whether the word still fit.

"Bram. Though you knew that."

"I know lots of things." She wiped her mouth on her sleeve. "I know your captain is Hazel Thornbury. I know your engineer sleeps sitting up with a wrench in her hand. I know you're running cargo up the Serrano for a man named Vane, and I know—" here her voice did something complicated, went flat and careful, "—that whatever's in those other eleven crates, it isn't machinery parts, because I rode next to them for a day and a half and machinery doesn't smell like money and gun oil."

I'll admit my stomach dropped somewhere around the bilge.

"You should talk to the captain," I said.

"I'm talking to you."

"She's the one who decides—"

"I know how boats work." And there it was again, that chin coming up, that flinty certainty. "Cargo gets delivered. That's the whole religion of it. So before you deliver me, understand what you're delivering me *to*."

* * *

Getting her above decks without the whole crew seeing was beyond my talents, so I didn't try. I just walked her up the companionway with her hands visible and her mouth shut, and presented the situation to Hazel on the aft deck like a man surrendering a live grenade.

Hazel took it about as well as expected.

"You're telling me," she said, very quietly, which from Hazel is worse than shouting, "that we are a full day upriver, past the last easy turn-back, carrying a teenage girl in a box."

"In a corner near a box," Wren corrected. "The boxes were locked. Mostly."

Hazel closed her eyes. I watched her do the same arithmetic I'd done — distance, fuel, the monsoon building somewhere south like a debt coming due — and arrive at the same ugly total. Then she opened her eyes and looked at Wren, really looked, the way she looks at water she doesn't trust.

"Who put you on my boat?"

"A broker. He was supposed to tell you himself, at High Landing. That was the arrangement." Wren's jaw tightened. "Except the arrangement changed. There were men at the estate the night I left. Men who weren't supposed to know. So the broker panicked and nailed me in with the crates and told me you'd never know until it was done, and I decided I'd rather take my chances with the river than sit in a box waiting to find out what 'done' means."

"Done means delivery," Hazel said. "We're being paid to deliver you."

"We're being paid to deliver *cargo*," Wren shot back. "Ask yourself what kind of cargo comes with armed men watching the dock. Ask yourself why nobody will say the buyer's name. Ask yourself—" and here her voice cracked, just once, and she hated it, I could see her hating it, "—ask yourself why a sixteen-year-old would rather nail herself into a floating coffin than stay where she was."

The deck went quiet except for the paddlewheel and the water. Odile had come up somewhere in the middle of it, wrench in hand, saying nothing, watching. Even the engine seemed to lean in.

Hazel stared at the girl for a long moment. Then she looked at me, and I gave her the smallest shrug I own, which is still fairly large.

"Below," Hazel said finally. "You'll sleep in the galley where Odile can see you. You don't touch the wheel, the charts, or the engine. And when we make the rendezvous Vane scheduled—" her voice went hard as river rock, "—we're going to have a conversation about exactly what my cargo is. Understood?"

Wren nodded. Then, as she turned to go below, she stopped.

"Thank you," she said, not looking at any of us. "For not turning back."

Nobody answered that. There wasn't an answer that didn't cost something.

* * *

That evening I found Odile in the engine room, ostensibly checking the packing glands, actually standing guard outside the galley where Wren had curled up on a bench like a cat making itself small. Odile had draped a blanket over her without comment. I noticed these things. Counting is my trade.

"She's trouble," Odile said, not looking up from her wrench.

"Probably."

"The kind that sinks boats."

"Also probably."

Odile was quiet a while. The engine thumped its steady heart-thump beneath us.

"My sister ran away once," she said at last, to the wrench rather than to me. "Sixteen. Nobody went after her. Everybody said it was simpler that way." She tightened something that didn't need tightening. "I still think about the road she took. Whether anybody fed her."

"So we feed her."

"So we feed her," Odile agreed, and that was the whole conversation, because Odile and I communicate mostly in load-bearing statements.

Before I turned in, I checked the hold one last time. Eleven crates, all accounted for, smelling faintly of money and gun oil. And in the corner, one girl, asleep with her back to the hull and one hand wrapped around a knife she probably thought none of us knew about.

I stood there a moment, doing the math I already knew the answer to. We'd signed on to run a forbidden route for triple rate, and triple rate always means somebody's paying extra for something they don't want witnessed. Now the something had a name, and a face, and a knife, and a way of saying *thank you* like it was an accusation.

Tomorrow or the day after, Vane's launch would come alongside, and Hazel would have to ask the question we were all circling: what exactly had we agreed to carry?

I had a bad feeling I already knew the answer. And worse — watching that girl sleep with her fist around steel, breathing the small stubborn breaths of someone who'd survived being nailed into a box by people who were supposed to protect her — I had a worse feeling growing underneath it.

Because here's the thing about cargo. You can refuse cargo. You can throw it overboard, leave it on the dock, nail it back in its crate.

But nobody — and I mean nobody — ever taught me how to stop protecting a kid once you'd fed her.

And from the way Odile stood guard with her wrench, and the way Hazel had looked at Wren like she was reading dangerous water, and the way even the Kestrel herself seemed to ride easier with that girl aboard —

I don't think anybody taught them either.
