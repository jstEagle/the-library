## 7. Vane's Terms

The message came at dusk, folded inside a tin of tea that Odile bought off a passing fisherman for twice what the fish was worth and half what the tea was.

I read it twice by lamplight in the wheelhouse, while the Kestrel swung gently on her anchor line above a deep pool the charts called the Widow's Rest, which is the kind of name that makes you want to anchor somewhere else but never do, because it's also the deepest water for forty miles and pride is cheaper than dragging your keel through silt.

*Midnight. Mid-channel. Come alone or don't come.*

No signature. No signature needed. The handwriting had the same neat, joyless precision as a bank notice, and I'd been reading Silas Vane's handwriting on invoices and insults for twenty years. He wrote his lowercase g's like little nooses. I'd never told him that. It seemed unkind to give a man accurate feedback about himself.

"You're not going alone," Bram said from the doorway. He filled most of the doorway. He fills most doorways. It's one of his gifts.

"I'm going alone."

"That's not what I said."

"Bram." I set the note down and looked at him — my engineer, my boulder, the man who'd once held the Kestrel's stern line against a flood current with nothing but his hands and a profanity so comprehensive it should have been taught in schools. "If this goes wrong, someone has to be able to run. If we're both standing on that launch when it goes wrong, we're both cargo."

He chewed on that the way he chewed on everything: slowly, with visible reluctance, and eventually swallowing because there was no alternative. "Odile stays on the radio. Wren—"

"Doesn't come up on deck until I say so."

"She won't like that."

"Wren doesn't like anything," I said. "It's her best quality. Keeps her sharp."

He grunted and went below, and I stood alone in the wheelhouse watching the last light die on the water, and I will confess something here that I confessed to no one aboard: my hands were shaking, and it wasn't entirely fear.

It was the handwriting. Twenty years since my father's disgrace, and the man who'd held the knife was writing to me in letters shaped like nooses, asking me to come out to the middle of the dark river like a trysting lover, and some traitorous corner of me — the corner that had been a nine-year-old girl waiting on a dock for a boat that came back flying shame instead of colors — that corner still wanted him to have an explanation. A reason. A wound of his own that explained the knife.

Hope is embarrassing at any age. At forty-one it's practically indecent.

* * *

I took the skiff out at half past eleven, running dark, no lamp, just the moon doing its indifferent silver work on the water and my own knowledge of the channel, which is the only navigation instrument I've ever fully trusted. The Widow's Rest lay quiet as held breath. Mist sat on the water in long gray ropes.

His launch appeared out of the mist at midnight exactly, because of course it did. Silas Vane had never been late to anything in his life except decency. She was a beautiful boat, his launch — thirty feet of varnished mahogany and brass, twin engines purring so smoothly they sounded apologetic, the kind of vessel that cost more than the Kestrel, her tender, her debts, and my funeral combined. He ran a single riding light. Courteous. Visible. Everything about Silas was designed to be seen doing the correct thing.

"Hazel." He stood at the rail as I came alongside, and he offered a hand down, and I ignored it and made my own boarding, which required slightly more undignified scrambling than I'd have liked, but dignity is a currency and I've learned to spend it carefully. "You look well."

"Liar," I said. "How are you, Silas?"

"Comfortable." He smiled. That was always the trouble with Silas Vane — he was handsome the way a ledger is handsome, all clean lines and balanced columns, and about as warm. Gray at the temples now. Better tailoring. Same eyes, pale and patient, eyes that had watched my father drown professionally and had never once gotten wet themselves. "Thank you for coming."

"You threatened to be interesting. I'm weak that way."

"Can I offer you something? There's coffee. Real coffee, not that chicory sludge river people pretend is—"

"Silas." I sat down across from him in the launch's little cabin, under a lamp turned low, and put my boots up on his teak table because it seemed important to mark the furniture somehow. "You sent a boy all the way downriver with a note in code. You're paying triple rate for a run nobody runs. You didn't ask what my cargo was before you hired me, which means you already knew. So let's skip the coffee and the weather and you tell me why I'm really here, and then I'll tell you whether to start swimming."

For a moment — just a moment — something moved behind those ledger-keeper's eyes. Not guilt. Guilt would have been too much to hope for. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man seeing a person where he'd expected a price tag.

"You haven't opened the crate," he said.

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because it's sealed, because it's yours until High Landing, and because I've hauled enough crates in my life to know that the ones people pay triple for are the ones you don't open unless you're prepared to become part of the shipment."

Something almost like approval crossed his face. "Your father used to say things like that."

"My father," I said, "is a subject we are not going to enjoy discussing tonight, so let's not."

He leaned back. Outside, the mist pressed against the windows like something listening. When he spoke again, his voice had changed — dropped the dockside charm, gone quiet and precise, the voice of a man reading terms aloud.

"The cargo is a person. A girl. Sixteen, perhaps seventeen. Dark hair, sharp tongue, answers to Wren."

The cabin got very small around me. I kept my face still, because I've spent twenty years keeping my face still, but inside, everything tilted — the way a deck tilts when the ballast shifts, when you realize the weight you've been carrying isn't where you thought it was.

"You knew," I said.

"I commissioned the run, Hazel. Of course I knew."

"The broker said a crate. Papers, he said. Estate documents."

"He said what I paid him to say. You'd have refused otherwise." He said it without apology, the way you'd note that water is wet. "And would you have? Refused?"

I thought about the bank notice folded in my breast pocket like a splinter. Seven days. I thought about Bram's hands, scarred and loyal. Odile's charts, annotated in three languages. The Kestrel's hull, thin as a promise.

"Don't answer that," Silas said softly. "We both know the arithmetic."

"Whose girl is she?"

"A runaway. From an estate upriver. Her grandfather died recently — a man of considerable holdings — and the family wants her home. Grief makes people protective. She's been missing three weeks; they traced her to the lower river; hiring strangers was cleaner than sending their own men, who'd been... recognized." He spread his hands, a gesture of reasonable men everywhere. "That's all this is, Hazel. A child being returned to her family. You've been hired to drive the coach. Nothing uglier than that."

"And the triple rate?"

"Pays for discretion. Pays for speed — you need to beat landfall anyway. And pays—" he paused, and chose the word with the care of a man laying a card face-up, "—for intact."

The word sat on the teak table between us like something dead.

"Intact," I repeated.

"Unharmed. Undamaged. Delivered whole." His pale eyes didn't blink. "To a man called Corvus. At High Landing. You'll be met at the north dock — his dock, all the docks there are his docks — and paid the balance in full, and every debt against your boat dissolves that same hour. That's the offer. That's the whole offer."

I looked at him for a long time. The lamp hissed. Somewhere out in the mist, a night bird made its idiot complaint, and the river moved beneath us, dark and patient, carrying everything toward the sea the way it carries everything, without judgment and without mercy.

"Corvus," I said. "That name's been coming up lately."

"Has it."

"It came up twenty years ago too. Funny how names do that. A young charter clerk named Corvus, testifying about falsified papers, about a captain named Thornbury who supposedly sold charter rights he didn't own. And now here's a man called Corvus who owns every dock on the Serrano and needs a sixteen-year-old delivered *intact* to him, and the only captain desperate enough to do it is the disgraced man's daughter." I leaned forward. "Small river, Silas."

"Rivers are exactly as small as the money flowing through them," he said. "Yes. It's the same man. And yes — I know what that name costs you. Which is precisely why I chose you."

"There it is."

"You want the truth?" For the first time, something cracked in his voice — not remorse, nothing so useful as remorse, but strain, the sound of a man carrying weight he'd told everyone was feather-light. "Corvus approached me eight months ago. I said no. He approached me again in the spring, and he brought documents — old documents, Hazel, from the Serrano inquiry. My testimony. My signature. And next to it, other signatures, other testimonies, pages I'd never seen, and he explained to me, very pleasantly, over very good brandy, that if those pages ever surfaced, they would show that my testimony was purchased. That I was bribed. Whether or not it's true is beside the point — he can make it true, he can make it *documented*, and documented is the only kind of true that matters to men like him." He laughed, short and bitter. "So yes. I recruited you because you were desperate, and because you hated me, and because a woman with a grudge asks fewer questions. I'm not proud of it. I'm not asking you to forgive it. I'm asking you to take the job you already took and finish it, and then we are both free of him. Don't you want to be free of him?"

And here is the thing I will not pretend I didn't feel: for about four seconds, I did. Four full seconds of imagining the bank notice burned, the crew paid, the Kestrel re-planked stem to stern, my father's name — no, not restored, Silas hadn't offered that, but at least *buried*, finished, done — and all it would cost was delivering one frightened girl into the hands of the man who'd ruined us.

Four seconds. Then I thought of her face when Bram pulled her out of the crate hold — furious, terrified, chin up like a bantam rooster deciding whether to fight or faint — and the four seconds ended.

"Silas," I said, standing, "do you know what you've hired?"

"A delivery."

"You've hired a kidnapping. That's what this is. That's what it's been since dock nine. A girl, sealed in a box, carried against her will to a man who wants her *intact* — and you sit there drinking real coffee telling yourself it's a coach ride home." I picked up my hat off the table. "Her family wants her back? Fine. Then her family can meet her at a public dock, in daylight, with witnesses and a magistrate. But that's not what this is, is it? Because if it were, you wouldn't need me. Anyone could bring a child home. What you need is someone with no questions and no standing, so that when she disappears into that compound, nobody ever asks where she went."

He rose too, and for a moment we stood across the teak table from each other, two people who had grown up on opposite ends of the same ruin, and I saw him weigh something — saw him actually consider saying whatever lived behind his teeth — and then the ledger closed, the way it always closes, the columns balancing, the bottom line protected.

"The balance is ten thousand," he said quietly. "Half again if you make High Landing before landfall. Think about your crew, Hazel. Think about your hull. Sentiment sinks boats."

"So does ballast made of other people's children."

I went out onto the deck and swung down into the skiff, and he didn't stop me, and he didn't apologize, and he didn't explain further, because there was nothing left to sell. But as I cast off and pushed away into the mist, his voice followed me across the black water, mild as milk:

"You'll come around, Hazel. Everyone comes around to Corvus eventually. Ask your father."

I rowed. I didn't trust my hands on an engine, and I didn't trust my mouth anywhere near his deck a second longer, because I could feel words climbing up my throat that would have felt magnificent and cost me everything. So I rowed, hard, through the mist, back toward the riding lamp of the Kestrel, and the whole way I heard it — *ask your father* — turning over and over like a stone in a current.

Because that was the trick of it. That was the knife hidden in the silk. I had spent twenty years certain of one thing in this world: that my father was guilty, that the inquiry proved it, that the shame was ours honestly earned. I had built my whole life on that foundation — the poverty, the pride worn thin, the sworn-off river, all of it resting on that one load-bearing certainty.

And Silas Vane, in one sentence, in a voice like milk, had just implied that my father had *refused* Corvus too.

Which meant either Silas was lying to manipulate me.

Or everything I knew was wrong.

* * *

Wren was waiting in the wheelhouse when I came aboard. Of course she was. I'd given orders; she'd weighed them against her own judgment and found mine wanting, which is going to make her either a fine captain or a drowned one, possibly both.

"Well?" she said. "What did he want?"

"To talk terms."

"What terms?"

I hung my hat on its peg. My hands had stopped shaking somewhere in the middle of the river, replaced by something colder and steadier, something with edges. "Terms involving you."

She went very still. Sixteen years old, dead grandfather, three weeks hunted, sealed in a crate on a stranger's boat — and she went still the way veterans go still, which told me more about her story than any chart could. "I'm not getting off this boat," she said. "Whatever he offered. Whatever they promised you. I'm not getting off."

"I know."

"You don't—"

"Wren." I looked at her straight, and I gave her the only thing I had that was worth anything, which was the truth, unvarnished and unpaid-for. "Ten minutes ago I found out I've been paid to kidnap you. Half the money's already spent on repairs. The other half would save this boat and everyone on her. And I am telling you, as the captain of this vessel, that you are not getting off this boat until *you* decide to get off it. That's the new terms. Vane can swim."

She stared at me. Something in her face shifted — not trust, not yet, trust takes longer than a night and a bad bargain — but reassessment. Recalculation. A girl redoing her sums and finding, to her visible surprise, that the numbers had changed.

Then, behind her, through the wheelhouse glass, the eastern horizon flickered. Once. Twice. Far off, downriver, where the monsoon was building itself out of the sea like a slow green-black fist.

Odile stuck her head up the companionway, radio handset in hand, and her face said everything before her mouth did.

"Storm front's turned north," she said. "Landfall's early. We've got days, Hazel. Maybe less."

I looked at the sky, and the sky looked back, and somewhere downriver a man named Corvus was waiting at the end of every dock, and somewhere upstream — though I couldn't have said why I suddenly believed it — there might be an answer to a question I'd been afraid to ask for twenty years.

"Get below, all of you," I said. "It's going to be a rough night. And tomorrow—" I turned the wheel idly with one hand, feeling the Kestrel answer even at anchor, faithful old thing, "—tomorrow we decide whose side of this river we're actually on."
