## 8. Rising Water

The sky turned the color of a bruise on the second morning out, and I learned something about riverboats that nobody had thought to put in any of the books I'd read hiding in my grandfather's library: they talk. Constantly. The Kestrel creaked and groaned and muttered to herself like an old woman with opinions about everything, and Bram answered her, patting the rail the way you'd calm a horse.

I was still officially cargo. Unofficially, I was the girl who'd been caught in the hold with stolen biscuits and a knife she didn't know how to use, which meant I spent most of my time being watched by everyone while pretending not to notice. It's a skill. You learn it young if your family is the kind mine is — the kind where love comes with conditions attached in fine print.

The first squall hit us just past the bend where the Serrano narrows between clay bluffs. One minute the air was thick and green-smelling; the next, the wind arrived sideways with rain so hard it bounced off the deck. Hazel shouted something I couldn't hear over the roar, and suddenly the whole boat became motion — Odile lashing down crates, Bram hauling on lines, Hazel at the wheel with her whole body leaned into it like she was arguing with the river personally.

I did what I do when things get loud, which is find the quietest corner and make myself useful in it. There was a chart table under the overhang of the pilothouse, weighted down with a brass ashtray and a coffee mug missing its handle. Charts, rolled and cracked at the folds, smelling of mildew and old tobacco.

I unrolled the one marked SERRANO — UPPER REACHES and pinned the corners.

Here is what nobody aboard knew yet: I had grown up on charts. Not these charts — prettier ones, older ones, the estate maps of my grandfather's study, drawn by surveyors he'd paid to flatter him. But a river is a river. You learn to read the language of it — where the blue lines crowd together means fast water, where they spread means slow, where some dead cartographer has written *shoal* in a shaky hand means somebody once had a very bad day.

The squall passed as quickly as it came, leaving the deck steaming and the crew dripping and me standing at the chart table with my finger on a mark I'd found.

"You'll want to give that point a wide berth," I said, because apparently my mouth had decided we were past the stage of formal introductions. "There's a shoal extends off the east bank. The soundings go thin right there."

Hazel came over wiping rain from her face with a shirttail that had given up on dignity hours ago. She looked at the chart. She looked at me. She looked at the chart again.

"Where'd you learn to read soundings?"

"Library," I said.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I've got that isn't a lie." Which wasn't entirely true either. Some of it was libraries. Some of it was a grandfather who taught me navigation the way other grandfathers teach fishing — patiently, on his knee, pointing at the blue lines and saying, *the river will always tell you what it wants, Wren, if you shut up long enough to listen.* He said a lot of things like that. He's dead now, which is why I'm in a crate's worth of trouble on a debt-ridden boat run by a woman who hates my family's name, but that's getting ahead of things.

Hazel studied me for a long moment with those gray eyes that made you feel audited. Then she tapped the shoal mark with one blunt finger.

"Bram," she called. "East bank point coming up. Give it thirty yards more than looks necessary."

From the engine hatch, Bram's voice floated up, cheerful as ever: "More than looks necessary is my entire philosophy, Captain."

We cleared the point by forty yards, and I felt something shift on that boat — small, barely a degree of change, like a heading adjusted by one compass point. Odile brought me a cup of tea without being asked. It was terrible tea, over-steeped and bitter, and I drank all of it, because I understood it wasn't tea. It was a treaty.

* * *

By afternoon the squalls were coming in strings, like beads on a wire, each one a little meaner than the last. The monsoon front was somewhere behind us, a bruise on the southern horizon that never quite healed, just deepened. Hazel stood at the wheel with her collar up and her jaw set, and I stationed myself at the chart table because it was the one place I could stand without anyone telling me to move, and because — I'll admit it — I liked the way Hazel glanced over every few minutes, checking my readings against her own instincts, finding they agreed.

"You're going to tell me eventually," she said during a lull, not looking at me. "What's in the crate."

"I don't know what's in the crate."

"Wren."

"I'm serious. I've never seen inside it. I know what's *supposed* to be in it, or I knew before I climbed into it, which are different questions." I kept my eyes on the chart. "The crate was supposed to be delivered to High Landing. Papers, I think. Legal documents. I was supposed to be delivered too, except nobody told me that part until after the broker sealed me in with the biscuits."

"And what are you? Cargo?"

"I'm the complication." I said it lightly, the way you say things that aren't light at all. "The crate is the inheritance. I'm the part of the inheritance that can walk."

That got her attention. She let the wheel speak for a moment, correcting for current, and then said, "Explain."

So I told her the shape of it, without the names, without the parts that would make her throw me overboard — or worse, make her look at me the way people looked at my grandfather, with that particular hunger dressed up as respect.

"My grandfather owned land on this river. A lot of it. Charters, docks, the rights to run trade on certain stretches. When he died — three months ago — there was a will. And the will doesn't say what certain people expected it to say."

"Certain people."

"The man who runs the syndicate. The man who owns every dock from here to High Landing." I watched her hands on the wheel. They'd gone still. "You've heard of him."

Everyone's heard of Corvus. On this river, saying his name aloud is like spitting in church — technically possible, deeply unwise. Hazel's face did something complicated and closed, like a shop pulling down its shutters.

"Go on," she said, flat.

"The will names an heir. Not the heir everyone assumed. And there are people who would very much prefer that will to become — " I searched for the word the lawyers used, when they thought I wasn't listening, which was their first mistake, because I am always listening — " *inconvenient.* Documents disappear on this river all the time. So do heirs, I imagine. I wouldn't know firsthand. Yet."

"And the broker. Vane's man. He hired us to carry the crate to High Landing."

"Yes."

"To the man who wants the will to disappear."

"Now you're reading charts," I said.

She laughed — one short, hard syllable, no humor in it, all edge. Then she was quiet for a long time, running us through the next squall with her teeth set, and I went back to the charts and pretended my heart wasn't hammering, because I'd just told the truth to a stranger and the truth was the only thing I owned that couldn't be taken from me, and handing it over felt like handing over a kidney.

* * *

Odile found me at dusk, wedged in the galley doorway out of the drizzle, watching the water go copper and then pewter as the light died. She handed me a bowl of rice and beans without ceremony and sat down across from me with her own, and for a while we ate in the companionable silence of two people who have both decided conversation is optional.

"You read charts," she said finally. It wasn't a question. Odile doesn't ask questions; she issues findings.

"A little."

"A little." She snorted. "Bram says you called the shoal before the leadsman did. Bram doesn't compliment anyone. Bram once described his own mother as 'adequate.'"

"He seems fond of you."

"We're not talking about me." She pointed her spoon at me. "I've been on this river eleven years. I know what girls in crates are running from, and I know what girls in crates are running *to*, and usually it's the same thing wearing different clothes. So here's my finding: you're not running from anything. You're running *toward*. That's rarer. That's also more dangerous."

I looked at my bowl. The beans had gone cold and I ate them anyway.

"My grandfather died," I said. "He was the only person who ever — " I stopped. Started again. "He taught me rivers. He taught me that the river tells you what it wants if you listen. Everyone else in my life taught me lessons with price tags attached."

"And the man who wants the will gone?"

"Is family." I said it and immediately wished I hadn't, because Odile's eyebrows did a thing, and Odile's eyebrows doing a thing is an event with consequences. "Not close family. Family the way a hook is related to a fish."

Odile was quiet for a while. Rain ticked on the deck above us. Somewhere forward, Bram was singing to the engine, low and tuneless, some hymn half-remembered and mostly invented.

"You know Hazel's history with your family's name," Odile said at last. Not cruelly. Just factually, the way she does everything.

"I've gathered."

"Gathered isn't knowing. When she finds out the name — the whole name — she's going to be angry. Not at you. At the world, which is worse, because you can't fight the world, you can only stand next to someone while it fights them." She stood, collected my empty bowl, and added, as if it were nothing, which is how Odile delivers everything that matters: "For what it's worth, I think you call the shoals true. Sleep in the pilothouse tonight. Storms are coming bigger."

* * *

They came bigger. Around midnight the wind rose like something waking up angry, and the Kestrel bucked and complained, and I lay in the pilothouse listening to Hazel and Bram argue in low voices about whether to tie off at the old landing ahead or push through the night. Hazel won, because Hazel always wins arguments with physics, and we ran through the dark with lightning walking the ridgelines like a giant taking slow steps.

And I discovered that I wasn't afraid. That's the strange thing I keep circling back to, trying to explain it right. I should have been afraid — sixteen years old, illegal cargo, storm season, hunted by men whose salaries could buy this boat ten times over. But lying on the pilothouse bench, watching Hazel's silhouette lean into the wheel, steady as a fence post in a flood, I felt something I hadn't felt since my grandfather died.

Safe. Cautiously, provisionally, *with reservations noted in triplicate*, safe.

Because here's what I figured out, watching her: Hazel Thornbury reads the river the way my grandfather taught me to read it. Not fighting it. Listening to it. Every correction of the wheel was a question asked and answered. She'd deny it if you told her — she'd say she wrestles the Serrano into submission — but I had better teachers than she thinks, and I know listening when I see it.

Somewhere past two in the morning the front eased, and Hazel noticed I was awake, because Hazel notices everything, which is exhausting and probably why she drinks her coffee like it owes her money.

"You should sleep," she said.

"So should you."

"One of us has to watch the water."

"Then I'll watch with you. Two sets of eyes. My grandfather used to say night water lies to single watchers."

A pause. Rain on the roof. Then, gruffly, like the words cost her money: "All right. Sit. Don't touch the wheel."

"I wouldn't dare."

"Liar. You'd dare plenty. That's the problem with you." But she shifted over an inch, making room on the bench, and that inch was the widest door I'd walked through in three months.

We watched the water together in silence. Once, far downstream, lightning lit a line of hills I recognized from my grandfather's charts — the Teeth, he called them, because they bit careless boats. I almost told her. Almost said, *my grandfather drew that stretch, he named those hills.* The words got as far as my teeth and stopped.

Not yet. There's a version of this where I tell her everything and she turns the boat around and puts me off at the first landing, and there's a version where I tell her everything and she believes me, and I can't know which version I'm living in until I know her better. Until she knows me. So I sat on my inch of bench and watched the lightning and kept my grandfather's name behind my teeth like a coin held under the tongue.

But I'll tell it here, in the privacy of my own head, where nobody can hear and nothing can be taken: Aurelio Voss. That was him. The estate on the upper Serrano, the library full of charts, the man on his knee pointing at blue lines. The will in the strongbox names *Wren Voss*, granddaughter, inheritor of the river charters — and Corvus, my mother's brother, the man whose dock empire was built on my grandfather's patience and my father's early grave, gets nothing but the weather.

Hazel Thornbury hates my family. I've seen it in the way she says *High Landing*, in the way her face shutters when I mention the syndicate. What she doesn't know — what nobody aboard knows — is that the will, if it's ever read, doesn't just hand me the charters. It hands me the power to reopen every claim Corvus ever crushed. Including, if Tomas Reyes is still alive upriver, and my grandfather always swore he was —

Including hers.

I didn't know that yet, sitting on my inch of bench. I only knew the shape of it, the way you know a shoal from the soundings before you ever see the water break over it. Something under the surface. Something that could tear the bottom out of everything.

Ahead of us, the river bent north toward the rapids, and behind us the monsoon front stacked itself higher against the stars, and I sat in the dark next to a woman who hated my name without knowing it, and I listened to the water tell me what it wanted.

It wanted everything to change.

So did I.
