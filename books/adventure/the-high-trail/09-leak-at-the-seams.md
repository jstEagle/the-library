## 9. Leak at the Seams

I have a theory about boats. A boat is never one vessel. She is a thousand small agreements between wood and water, and every one of them is negotiable. Most days the negotiations go quietly — a rivet sweats, a seam swells shut, nobody mentions it at dinner. But some days the river decides to renegotiate everything at once, and you find out exactly which promises your ship has been keeping behind your back.

Today was that day.

It started, as these things do, with Hazel looking pleased with herself. That should have been my first warning. Hazel Thornbury pleased is like a cat with a full bowl and a closed door: contentment in her is always temporary, always purchased at someone else's expense, usually the river's.

"Odile," she said, squinting at the water ahead through her father's cracked brass spyglass, "we've got maybe two hours before the Serrano Gorge run. Light rapids, then a chute, then the narrows. I've run it twice."

"Twice," Bram said, from his post at the engine housing, in the tone of a man reading an obituary aloud. "In what? High water or low?"

"Medium," Hazel said. "Which is the worst kind, actually. High water buries the rocks. Low water shows them all. Medium gives you just enough to hide the ugly ones."

"Wonderful," I said. "So we're running blind into a river that's decided to be dishonest."

"That's every river," Hazel said cheerfully, and spun the wheel a few degrees to port, and I felt my stomach relocate itself somewhere near my spine.

Here's what you need to know about me, since apparently I'm narrating this disaster: I am not a river woman by birth. I came to the Kestrel six years ago off a cargo barge out of Marrow Bay, where I'd spent four years learning that the sea is honest about wanting to kill you. The sea telegraphs. It builds up to its violence over hours, gives you time to write letters, make peace, regret your haircut. Rivers are different. Rivers are assassins. They'll carry you along gentle as a lullaby and then put a rock exactly where your hull needs to be, without so much as a courtesy ripple.

I cook on this boat. That's my official title — cook, quartermaster, keeper of the ledger of small grievances that keeps this crew fed and marginally civil. But somewhere in the last six years I've also become the person who knows where every tool lives, which plank flexes wrong when you step on it, and how many times a day Hazel checks the bilge when she's worried (fourteen, if you're curious; sixteen if she's very worried and pretending not to be).

And today, I was going to learn a new job: damage control.

* * *

The morning had started well enough, which on this river is itself a warning sign. We'd made good time out of the trading post at Corvus's back door — though none of us had known whose back door it was until Hazel came below decks with her face gone gray and told us what she'd learned. Corvus. The name sat in the cabin like smoke. Wren had gone quiet in a way that was worse than crying. Bram had cleaned his rifle twice without being asked. And Hazel had stood at the wheel for three hours saying nothing, which for Hazel is the equivalent of a scream.

We were running now because standing still felt worse. Upstream to the witness, downstream to the checkpoints, and behind us — building all morning like a bruise coming up under the skin — the monsoon front. The sky to the south had gone the color of a healing wound, green-black at the horizon, stacked in shelves. Wren, who turned out to know weather the way I know knives, had looked at it once and said only: "That's early. That's four days early." Then she'd gone back to the charts and hadn't looked up again, as if not looking could hold it off.

So we ran the gorge because running felt like doing something.

The first hour of rapids was almost fun. I'll admit it. There's a particular joy in a boat handled well through white water — the Kestrel bucking and sliding, Hazel reading the water ahead like a page she'd memorized in another life, calling the channel in that low flat voice she uses when she's working: *left of the broken water, hard right past the drowned snag, don't look at the hole, look at where you want to be.* Bram feeding the engine just enough throttle, no more, because too much power in shallow water is how you dig your own grave. Even Wren got into it, perched on the cargo hatch calling depths off the lead line like she'd been born to it, which, given whose granddaughter she was, maybe she had.

I stood in the galley doorway with a towel over my shoulder and let myself feel, for about eleven minutes, like a person on an adventure instead of a person fleeing a syndicate boss who owned every dock on the river.

Then the river sent its invoice.

It came at the chute — the narrow throat where the whole Serrano pinches down and accelerates, all its pent-up miles squeezing through a gap barely wider than the Kestrel is long. Hazel had called it perfectly. She'd lined us up, threaded the needle, and we were through — genuinely through, committed, past the worst of it — when the river changed its mind about a rock.

There's a kind of stone in these gorges that doesn't show. It sits just under the surface, rounded by ten thousand years of flood, wearing a skin of fast water so smooth the current slides over it without breaking. The old pilots call them sleepers. Hazel calls them liars. Whatever you call them, this one was waiting at the exit of the chute, exactly where the water relaxed and so did we, and the Kestrel's hull kissed it — not struck, nothing so dramatic as a strike, just a kiss, a long grinding kiss as our momentum dragged us across it — and I heard the sound that every boat person dreads more than storms, more than fire, more than drowning.

Not a bang. A scrape. A long, low, singing scrape, like a giant filing its teeth, running from the bow clear aft along the starboard side.

Hazel's face did something I'd never seen it do. It went completely, perfectly blank. Not calm — blank, the way a wall is blank, everything behind it evacuated. She spun the wheel, brought us into slack water below the chute, dropped anchor in the eddy behind a rock island, and said, in that same low flat voice:

"Bram. Sound the bilge."

Bram was already moving. He had the pump handle unshipped before the anchor finished paying out, and I swear the man didn't even look surprised, which frightened me more than the rock had. People who aren't surprised by disaster have met it before, often, and survived, and they carry something in their eyes afterward that never quite washes out.

The bilge sounded dry. First sounding, dry. Second sounding, damp. By the third — ninety seconds after the scrape — the stick came up showing two inches of water where there should have been none, and rising.

"Slow seep," Bram reported. "Not a breach. Something's opened up along a seam, starboard side, probably midships where the strain concentrated. She's taking water slower than we can worry about it, faster than we can ignore it."

"Show me," Hazel said.

We went below. All of us. Which tells you something — a crew doesn't abandon its stations all at once unless the news is bad enough that everyone wants to see the enemy with their own eyes.

Down in the hold, in the dim lamplight, we found it. Nothing dramatic. No geyser, no fountain, no dramatic jet of river water announcing itself. Just a seam — one iron-fastened seam between planks, maybe three feet of it — weeping. Water coming through in a fine silver thread, beading along the joint, running down the inside of the hull in slow beads, gathering, dripping into the bilge. It looked almost gentle. It looked like the hull was sweating.

It was the most frightening thing I have ever seen on the water, and I once watched a barge sink under a crane barge in Marrow Bay with eight men aboard.

Because here's the thing about a weeping seam versus a smashed plank: a smashed plank announces itself. You patch it, you brace it, you curse it, you deal with it. A weeping seam is a negotiation reopening. It means the hull has flexed somewhere it shouldn't have flexed, that a thousand small agreements between wood and water have been renegotiated by force, and you cannot know — you *cannot know* — whether you've found the only seam that opened, or merely the first. The one that showed its work. Somewhere else along that starboard side, hidden behind cargo and paint and pride, other seams might be starting to sweat too, waiting their turn, patient as rot.

Hazel ran her thumb along the seam and pressed. Water beaded up around her thumbnail like sweat around a pressed fingertip. She did it twice more, further aft, further forward, mapping the extent of it. Three feet of weeping. Then she straightened up, and I watched her make a decision, and I want it recorded here that I saw it happen — the exact moment the fear went into a box and the captain came out.

"We stop," she said. "Here. Now. We caulk this seam properly or we don't run another mile of this river."

Bram nodded. Wren asked the obvious question, because Wren asks obvious questions with the fearless directness of someone who hasn't yet learned which questions cost money: "How long?"

Hazel didn't answer right away. She looked at the seam, then at the sky — really looked, south, at that green-black shelf of cloud that had climbed visibly higher since breakfast — and then she said a number, and the number was a lie, and everyone in the hold knew it was a lie, including her.

"Two hours."

Nobody called it. Nobody said, *no, it'll be six.* We all just silently agreed to believe her, because that's what crews do. You accept the captain's lie the way you accept the ship's weeping seam: as a fact of the situation, not a truth about it. Two hours became the plan. Whether it took two or six, we would spend them as if they were two, because the alternative — spending them as if they were six — meant admitting the front would close the gap on us, and none of us were ready to say that sentence out loud.

* * *

Caulking a boat is, I learned today, a lot like surgery performed in a moving room by people who are not surgeons.

First you have to get at the seam, which meant unloading half the starboard cargo hold — crates shifted, Wren's hiding place from week one exposed as a hollow among boxes that now smelled permanently of river water, poor girl flinching when Bram joked about checking it for stowaways again. Then you open the seam slightly — Bram drove a reaming iron along it with a mallet, opening the joint just enough to clean it, and I will tell you honestly that watching a man deliberately widen a leak in a floating boat's hull is an experience I do not recommend to anyone attached to their continued existence. Every tap of that mallet, water came through faster, and every time it did, my hands wanted to grab his arm and stop him, and every time, Hazel stood beside him and said, "Deeper. Clean it out."

Then oakum. Loose tarred fiber, twisted into cords, driven into the opened seam with a caulking iron and mallet — packed tight, tighter, tightest, until the fiber bit and held and the water slowed, then stopped, then the seam was sealed with a smear of hot pitch over the top, painted on with a rag tied to a stick because nobody wanted to hold a pot of boiling pitch in a rocking boat.

I hauled the pitch pot. That was my contribution, officially. Unofficially, I held things. I held the lamp steady while Bram worked. I held Hazel's spyglass when she kept climbing up to check the sky. I held Wren's hand once, briefly, when the girl went pale watching water come through her own hiding place's floor — because that hollow among the crates had been the first safe space anyone ever gave her, and there was something in watching it take on river water that I recognized, something about watching a refuge fail, and I squeezed her hand and said nothing, because sometimes nothing is the correct thing to say.

Two hours became three. Three became four. The pitch needed to cure, and pitch cures at its own pace, indifferent to monsoons and deadlines and the price on all our heads.

And the whole time, the sky built.

I kept track of it the way you keep track of anything you're afraid of: glances, stolen, counted. From the top of the cabin, south, the front had organized itself into something with architecture now — a great stacked wall of cloud, blue-black at its base, bruise-purple above, and ahead of it a haze of falling rain you could see falling from twenty miles away, a gray curtain hanging from the cloud base to the river, sweeping slowly westward like a door being closed across the world. Wren timed it against the chart. At its current speed, it would reach us in roughly nine hours. Nine hours, minus four spent on repairs, left five hours of running time to reach shelter — and the only shelter marked on the chart between here and the witness's stretch of river was a flooded side channel thirty miles downstream, if the entrance was still open, if the flooding hadn't already plugged it with debris.

Five hours to cover thirty miles of river we'd planned to cover in seven.

Hazel did that arithmetic out loud, once, standing at the wheel after we raised anchor, and then she stopped talking about it entirely, and that silence was louder than any speech.

* * *

We ran.

God help me, we ran. Engine at full working turns, which Bram gave grudgingly and monitored like a man watching a fever chart, because the engine was old and the vibration couldn't be good for a freshly caulked seam, and every hour he went below and sounded the bilge and came up with a number, and the numbers went: damp, damp, damp, wet, wet, wet-dammit, and each *dammit* was a little louder than the last.

By the fourth hour the seam was weeping again. Not as badly — the oakum was holding the worst of it — but holding the way a tired dam holds, with visible effort. We set a rotation. Bram at the engine, me on the hand pump, twenty minutes on, twenty minutes off, Wren relieving whichever of us needed relief, Hazel refusing relief entirely because Hazel does not accept relief, Hazel accepts responsibility, and the difference between those two words is the whole distance between a passenger and a captain.

Pumping is its own kind of meditation, if meditation can be bitter. Twenty minutes of pushing a handle in a rhythm, watching the same gray water come up the hose and spit out the discharge, knowing that every gallon you throw overboard is a gallon the river has already decided belongs to it. There's a moment — I hit it somewhere in my second shift — where the pumping stops being labor and becomes argument. You and the river, negotiating. It says *mine*. You say *not yet*. It says *mine*. You say *not yet*, and you mean it, and the handle goes down and comes up and goes down.

Somewhere in my third shift, Wren relieved me, and I went forward to check on Odile— no. Forgive me. Old habit. I went forward and I found Hazel alone at the wheel in the rain that had started falling ahead of the front — fat, scattered, warning-shot drops, the sky clearing its throat — and I stood beside her and said the thing I'd been carrying for an hour:

"The seam's going to open again. Before shelter. You know that."

"I know that," she said.

"And the side channel might be blocked."

"I know that too."

"So what's the plan?"

Hazel was quiet for a long moment. Rain dotted the deck around us. Ahead, the river bent north toward the narrows, and beyond the bend the light had gone strange — that yellow-green submarine light that means the sky has stopped being sky and become ocean overhead.

"The plan," she said finally, "is that I read the water, Bram keeps us moving, you keep us pumped, and Wren keeps us honest about the clock. And if the seam opens and the channel's blocked—" she paused, and I braced for despair, and it didn't come, "—then we beach her. Deliberately. On the softest bank we can find, bow-first, and we ride the front out aground rather than afloat. A boat aground is a problem. A boat sinking is a tragedy. I've buried one boat already in my life. I'm not burying two."

She said it lightly. She said everything lightly. That's the trick of her, the thing I understood six years ago and understand better every year: Hazel Thornbury carries catastrophe the way other people carry a tune. You don't hear the weight until you try to lift it yourself.

I went below and made coffee. Strong, terrible, river coffee, the kind that strips paint and grief in equal measure. Four cups. One for each of us, plus one extra, which I set on the ledge by the wheel within Hazel's reach without a word, because the extra cup is how I say the things I don't say, and she drank it, and that was how we both knew the other one knew.

* * *

The narrows nearly ended us.

I won't describe them in detail — partly because the next hours blur in my memory into a single continuous roar of water and engine and rain, and partly because some things deserve to stay in the hands of the people who steered through them. What I remember precisely: Hazel's voice, flat and unhurried, calling the channel while the rain curtain swallowed the far bank. Bram's hands on the throttle, moving in small exact increments, a surgeon's economy. Wren on the lead line, calling depths in a voice that cracked exactly once, on the call that mattered most, and steadied. And the bilge — the bilge numbers getting worse, wet, wetter, *pump*, the seam weeping openly now, the oakum fighting a losing war of attrition, the hand pump becoming less a precaution than a life support machine for the hull.

We came out of the narrows into the long flooded reach below them as the light failed, and there it was, or wasn't: the side channel. The chart said shelter. The river said otherwise. The entrance was there — a dark slot in the drowned timber on the north bank — but the floodwater coming down had pushed a raft of debris against it, whole trees, root-balls the size of cottages, packed together like a barricade that some river-god had assembled overnight out of the wreckage of the forest.

Hazel brought us alongside it, close enough to touch, and studied it for a full minute. Then she made the call I will think about for the rest of my life.

"Not tonight," she said. "We push on."

"Past it?" Wren said. "To where?"

"To the next high ground. There's a bluff cutbank six miles down, on the outside of the big bend. Clay bank, forty feet, faces away from the wind. We tie off, we ride it out, and tomorrow—" and here her voice did something complicated, "—tomorrow we decide what we still are."

Six miles. With a weeping hull, a failing pump rotation, a storm arriving early, and night coming down. Six miles that took us two hours, in rain that went from warning shots to bombardment somewhere in the middle, in water that rose visibly around us as the monsoon's leading edge dumped its first fury into the catchment upstream — you could watch the river climb the banks, inch by inch, the current stiffening, debris lines forming, the whole Serrano turning from a river into a rumor of the ocean moving inland.

We reached the cutbank at full dark, in driving rain, with the bilge showing nine inches and the hand pump losing ground. Hazel beached us — deliberately, gently, bow-first into the clay, exactly as she'd promised, using the last of our way to drive the hull soft onto the mud below the bluff, and we tied off to trees that may or may not still be standing by morning, and we pumped her dry-ish, and we ate cold rice because lighting the stove felt like tempting fate, and we waited.

That was the night. That was the whole night: waiting, in a beached boat on a rising river, under a bluff, in the first night of a monsoon, with a price on all our heads and a wounded girl below decks and a hull that had told us, in its quiet weeping way, that it was tired.

I didn't sleep. I sat in the galley with the lamp turned low and listened to the rain on the roof — thousands of small fists, drumming — and I thought about seams. About how a boat is a thousand agreements, and how a crew is too, and how ours had been renegotiated today by rock and river and weather, and had held. Barely. The way the oakum held. The way tired things hold.

And then, near dawn, the rain slackened — not stopped, slackened, the way a fist unclenches partway — and Hazel came below, soaked through, and stood in the galley doorway, and said the last thing I expected:

"It's not the front. This is just the outer band. The real body of the storm is still south of us, and it's turned."

"Turned where?" I asked.

"Upstream," she said. "Against the current. It's crawling north up the Serrano valley, and it's going to sit on us for two days, and every drop it drops upriver is going to come down here." She looked at me, and her face, for once, wasn't blank. It was tired, and honest, and afraid in the way that brave people are afraid, which is to say completely. "Odile, I need you to hear this clearly. The river is going to keep rising for two days no matter what we do. Everything we've planned — the witness, the council, all of it — just got harder. Maybe impossible. Maybe we're done."

Maybe we're done. I turned the phrase over. And then Wren appeared in the doorway behind her, drawn by the noise of voices, sixteen years old and soaked and scared and stubborn, and she said, with the terrifying clarity of the young:

"No. We're not done. My grandfather's will doesn't expire in a flood."

And Hazel looked at her — really looked, the way she'd looked at the seam, mapping it — and something passed between them that I witnessed but will not pretend to fully understand, some transfer of weight from the older shoulders to the younger ones, and Hazel nodded once, and said:

"Then we wait out the water, and we move when it lets us. Both things are true. The river gets its two days. We get our will."

She turned to go back on deck, and paused, and added, wry as ever, because she is who she is:

"And somebody should probably check whether those trees are still holding us."

I volunteered Bram. It seemed like his kind of errand.

But before I sign off this chapter of our ongoing catastrophe — before I go relieve whoever's on the pump and start the rice that will become breakfast that will become whatever comes next — there's one more thing I have to report, because it happened this morning, in the gray half-light, and it has been sitting in my chest like a coal ever since.

I went to sound the bilge myself. Habit. Six years of habit. And the stick came up showing twelve inches — worse than last night, the seam weeping steadily through the cured pitch, the river pressing its case — and as I stood there looking at the silver beads of water threading through the seam in the lamplight, I heard, from the hold, a small sound. A scraping. Rhythmic. Patient.

Wren was down there. On her knees, in the dark, with a strip of canvas and a lump of pitch, re-caulking the seam herself. Nobody had asked her. Nobody had shown her more than what she'd absorbed watching Bram work yesterday. She'd simply decided, sometime in the night, that this boat had carried her when no one else would, and that debts like that get paid with hands, not words.

I stood there and watched her work for a long time without letting her know I was there. And then I went and got the good lamp, the bright one, the one I save for emergencies, and hung it over her head, and said the only thing worth saying:

"You missed a spot."

She laughed. First real laugh in three days. And the seam, under her small stubborn hands, began — bead by bead, agreement by agreement — to hold.

The river, meanwhile, kept rising.
