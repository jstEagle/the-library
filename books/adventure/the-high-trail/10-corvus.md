## 10. Corvus

The trading post at Salto Bend was the kind of place that pretended to be three buildings and was really one long lie with a roof over it. A chandlery that sold rope by the arm's length and fuel by rumor. A cantina whose floor had been mopped sometime during the previous administration. And a wire office where a man named Ferro took your money for messages he may or may not have sent, depending on how the cards fell that evening.

We tied up at the public dock — the only dock in forty miles not painted Corvus green, though I didn't know yet why it was the only one, and I wouldn't have believed the answer if someone had told me.

The Kestrel sat low in the water behind us, riding heavy on a patched hull and a crew running on coffee and stubbornness. Odile's seam was holding. That was the good news. The bad news was everything else: the monsoon front stacked up behind us like a debt collector, Vane's rendezvous two days back still sitting in my chest like a swallowed hook, and a girl aboard who was either cargo or a person, and every hour we steamed made me more complicit in whichever answer turned out to be true.

I gave orders like a captain and not like a woman losing sleep. "Bram, fuel. Odile, provisions — real food, not whatever Ferro's passing off as flour this season. Wren—"

"I'll stay aboard," she said.

It came too fast. Too practiced. She'd said it at the last stop too, and the one before that, always with her chin down and her hands busy, folding herself smaller against the pilothouse rail like a letter nobody should read.

"Stretch your legs," I said. "You've been cooped up since the narrows."

"I'm fine."

"Wren." I kept my voice level. "Dock's public. Nobody knows you. Ten minutes of being a person who isn't hiding."

She looked at me the way you look at a current you're deciding whether to trust, and then she climbed down the ladder without another word, which from her was practically obedience.

* * *

Ferro's wire office smelled of lamp oil and old tobacco and the particular despair of men who sell information in towns where information is worth less than rope. He was a small man built like a question mark, and when I asked him — casual as I could make it, leaning on the counter like a customer asking after freight rates — whether he'd heard anything about a broker running sealed crates upriver out of Dock Nine, his whole face did something complicated.

"You're the Kestrel," he said.

"I'm a boat," I said. "The Kestrel is behind me."

"Ferro." He stuck out a hand dry as river reed. "You want to close that door."

I closed the door.

"Sealed crate out of Dock Nine," he repeated, settling onto his stool like a man preparing to enjoy himself. "That'd be Marlowe's work. Little ferret of a broker, works the middle river. Works for somebody, is the thing. Everybody works for somebody on the Serrano now." He tapped the counter twice. "You know how many docks there are between here and High Landing?"

"I've run this river my whole life."

"Then you know there used to be independent landings all the way up. Salt Landing. The Miro yards. Old Pilar's ferry crossing. My own little pier here, before the fire." His eyes went somewhere else for a moment. "Count them now. Go on. Count how many fly the green pennant."

I thought about it. The green pennant — a black bird on a green field, some kind of crow or raven, stitched on every warehouse awning from the delta to the headwaters. I'd seen them so long they'd stopped meaning anything, the way you stop seeing the wallpaper.

"All of them," I said slowly.

"All of them," Ferro agreed. "Charters, moorage, warehousing, insurance, pilot licenses — well, no, the council still pretends it owns the pilot licenses, but the council meets in a hall Corvus built, so you can do that arithmetic yourself. There's a name on all of it. You hear it whispered more than said, because saying it loud in the wrong cantina has consequences. But you're a captain. You can stand to hear a name."

"Corvus," I said.

He nodded, pleased, like I'd passed an exam. "Aurelio Corvus. Started as a barge agent thirty years ago. Bought his first charter off a man who drowned — accident, they said. Bought his second off a man who went bankrupt — funny how fast that happened. By the time anyone understood what he was building, he owned the river's throat, and everything that floats on it pays him to breathe." He leaned forward. "You know what happened to the last captains who tried to run independent on the Serrano?"

"Ferro—"

"There were four," he said, like I hadn't spoken. "Four boats, one season, maybe eight years back. Ran their own schedules, kept their own profits. One burned at anchor. One lost her papers and got impounded until she rotted. One just — disappeared, up past the gorge, and they never found so much as a plank. And the fourth—" He stopped. Looked at me properly for the first time. "The fourth was the Thornbury boat. The *Mariana*. Your father's."

The room got very quiet. Somewhere outside, a gull was arguing with the dock pilings, and it sounded very far away.

"My father wasn't ruined by Corvus," I said. "He was ruined by bad water and worse luck, and by a partner who testified he'd been running falsified charters. That was in every paper from here to the coast."

"Yes," Ferro said gently. "And who do you suppose owned the papers? Who do you suppose the falsified charters were *falsified for*, and who do you suppose needed a disgraced captain to take the fall when the council started asking questions?" He spread his hands. "Silas Vane testified, yes. But Vane was twenty-six years old and scared, and a scared man points where he's pointed. Somebody pointed him."

I put my hand flat on the counter because the room had tilted slightly and I wanted something honest under my palm. Twenty years I'd carried my father's disgrace like a stone sewn into my coat. I'd built a whole life around its weight — the sworn-off route, the shame at council meetings, the way I flinched when people said the family name. And this small dry man in a wire office was telling me the stone had never been mine to carry at all.

"That's a story," I managed. "People tell stories."

"People do," Ferro agreed. "But I file wires. I see what moves through this office. Eight years ago, the week your father's hearing ended, a wire went out from this very desk — not by me, by the man who held the concession before me — addressed to a landing agent upriver. It said: *matter settled, charter transfers approved, C. satisfied.* Signed with the raven mark." He shrugged. "I kept a copy. Sentimental, maybe. Or maybe I always figured someday somebody would come through that door asking the right questions, and I'd get to be the man who answered them."

"Why?" I said. "Why keep it? Why tell me?"

"Because my pier burned," Ferro said simply, "and everyone knows whose men set it, and no one will ever say so, and I have been waiting twenty years for the river to grow one honest captain with nothing left to lose." He smiled, and it wasn't a friendly smile; it was the smile of a man handing you a live coal. "Congratulations, Captain Thornbury. I believe that's you."

* * *

I don't remember walking out of the wire office. I remember standing on the boardwalk afterward with the sun too bright and my hands shaking, watching Odile argue cheerfully with a provisioner and Bram haul fuel hose across the dock, ordinary people doing ordinary things, while inside my skull twenty years of furniture rearranged itself with a sound like a hull breaking up.

My father hadn't failed. My father had *refused*. Refused to falsify charters, refused to play Corvus's game, and been fed to the council for it — with Silas Vane's testimony as the knife. Every humiliation I'd inherited, every door closed to the Thornbury name, every year I'd spent captaining a debt-ridden boat because no respectable line would sign me — all of it traced back to one man with a raven on his flag.

And I was currently hauling his granddaughter upriver in a crate-boat for triple rate.

The thought arrived all at once, whole, the way ice goes out in spring — not cracking but simply gone, and the flood coming through behind it.

Wren.

Wren, who read charts like she'd been raised on them. Who had an estate somewhere and a dead grandfather and a will. Whose grandfather — I did the arithmetic now, ugly and simple — whose grandfather was Aurelio Corvus. And Vane's terms, delivered smooth as oil at that rendezvous: deliver her *intact* to Corvus at High Landing. Not to the broker. Not to a family. To Corvus himself, personally, like something recovered rather than someone returned.

And if the girl was his granddaughter, why hire strangers to fetch her? Why the sealed crate, the armed men on the dock, the secrecy?

Unless she wasn't what he wanted returned. Unless she was what he wanted *contained*.

I found her at the end of the dock, sitting with her legs hanging over the water, watching the current go by. She didn't look up when I sat down beside her, which was its own kind of permission.

"Salto Bend," she said. "Grandfather brought me here once. When I was nine. He bought me a candied apple from a cart right there." She nodded at a spot on the boardwalk where there was no cart. "Then he had a conversation with the harbormaster, and the harbormaster's face went gray, and on the way back to the launch Grandfather told me that everyone on this river would one day work for our family, and that I should remember how that felt." She turned the memory over in her hands like something broken. "I remembered. Just not the way he meant."

"Wren." My mouth was dry. "Your grandfather. Tell me his name."

She looked at me then, and I watched her decide — watched the calculation happen, the same one she'd been making since the night we pulled her out of the crates, except this time the scales were tipped by something new. Maybe it was the lamp over her head while she worked the seam. Maybe it was ten minutes of being a person who wasn't hiding. Maybe it was just that she was sixteen and tired and the truth is heavier than any crate.

"Aurelio," she said. "Aurelio Corvus."

The gulls kept arguing. The river kept going by, brown and patient, carrying everything toward the sea.

"And the will," I said. "The one you ran from. The one that made you stow away on a stranger's boat rather than let certain people find you first." I kept my voice steady, captain-steady, though underneath it something was roaring. "Who does it favor?"

"It names me," Wren said. "Not Father. Not Uncle Dario, who runs the High Landing end and does all the things Grandfather doesn't want his hands on. Me. Half the charters, outright, plus the vote on the council seat." Her laugh was short and had no humor in it whatsoever. "Grandfather always liked me best. Said I had the river in me. So he wrote it down, official, witnessed, filed — and then he died six weeks later, and suddenly everybody in the family discovered how much they loved me too, and how much better it would be for everyone if I came home quietly, and if the paperwork could just be — handled. Simplified. Family matters shouldn't go through courts, Aunt Livia said. Family matters stay in the family." She hugged her knees. "I'm the family matter."

I sat with it. The past and the present, colliding right there on a rotten dock in a town that barely existed, in the shape of a sharp-tongued girl with candied-apple memories and half a river empire in her name.

If I delivered her, I paid off the Kestrel. I saved my boat, my crew, my name on the bank's ledger. And I handed a child to the man who had destroyed my father, so that his heir problem could be made to disappear — *intact*, Vane had said, and I finally heard the word for what it was: a condition on merchandise.

If I didn't, I lost everything I'd spent twenty years bleeding to keep.

"Hazel," Wren said quietly. "You've gone white. What did the wire man tell you?"

I looked at her — at the girl, not the cargo, not the inheritance, the girl who'd laughed yesterday for the first time in three days — and I understood that whatever I said next would be the truest thing about me I'd ever said out loud.

"He told me who your grandfather is," I said. "And I need you to understand something before we go one more mile up this river. My father lost everything because of that man. And I was hired — by men working for him — to bring you to his door." I exhaled. "So we're going to finish loading, and we're going to cast off, and I am going to spend however many miles we have left figuring out how to break a contract I signed in ink, because I will not hand you to Aurelio Corvus. Not for triple rate. Not for the boat. Not for anything."

Wren stared at me. Then her face did something complicated and young and fierce, and she blinked hard, twice, refusing — absolutely refusing — to cry in front of a captain.

"You don't even know if the will's real," she said, voice rough. "You don't know me. You'd burn your boat for a girl you fished out of a crate?"

"I'd burn it for the principle," I said. "The girl's just a bonus."

She laughed — wet, disbelieving — and bumped her shoulder against mine, and for one moment the dock was almost warm.

Which was, of course, when Bram came up the boardwalk at a pace Bram never used, fuel receipt forgotten in his fist, and said the words that ended the moment and started the next trouble:

"Captain. Three skiffs just came round the bend flying green. Armed. And they're asking the harbormaster — real polite-like — which boat is the *Kestrel*."
