## 11. The Broker's Men

I've been shot at twice in my life, and both times the shooting was worse than the being shot at. The first time was a wedding in Port Calloway that went wrong over a card debt — not mine, for once — and the second time was three days up the Serrano River with a stowaway heiress asleep below decks and two skiffs full of armed men sliding out of the dusk behind us like bad news on a fast current.

Let me back up.

The narrows start about a mile past the old tolling station, where the river pinches itself between two walls of black rock and gets ideas above its station. In daylight, running the narrows is a job. At night, it's a prayer. And we were entering them at dusk because Hazel said dusk was when we'd have the best light on the water and the worst light on us, which sounded like wisdom until I did the arithmetic and realized she'd also just admitted somebody might be looking for us.

"Who exactly are we hiding from?" I asked her. We were at the wheel together, which is how she likes it — me on the throttle, her hands light on the spokes, both of us pretending this arrangement isn't just an excuse to keep each other company through the ugly hours.

"The broker," she said. "Maybe. Or Corvus. Or nobody."

"That's a wide range of nobodies."

"Hazel doesn't guess," Odile called from the engine hatch, where she'd been elbow-deep in our engine's guts since noon, talking to it the way some people talk to horses. "Hazel reads. And what she read at the last dock was a man counting our smokestack height against his rifle sight."

"He was leaning on a post," I said.

"He was *aiming* along a post," Odile corrected. "There's a difference. I've been both the leaner and the leaned-on."

That's Odile all over. She can make a threat assessment sound like a memoir. She'd taken a splinter of shrapnel out of her own forearm that morning without anesthesia or complaint, taped it up, and gone back to arguing with the fuel pump, so when she says someone was aiming, I aim to believe her.

Wren came up from below with four bowls of something she called stew and everyone else called soup, because Wren believed confidence could substitute for stock. She handed them around, then stayed, standing at the rail, watching the rock walls close in.

"They're following us," she said. Not a question. Sixteen years old and she said it the way you'd say *it's raining*.

"What makes you say that?" Hazel asked.

"Because you've been checking the stern mirror every forty seconds since the tolling station, and you only do that when you're worried, and you're never worried about the river." Wren took a sip of her own soup and grimaced at it, honestly, like a critic. "You're worried about people."

I'll be honest: that kid scared me more than the armed men did. Armed men you can outrun, outwit, or outlast. A sixteen-year-old who reads your face like chart water is a permanent condition.

* * *

The skiffs showed themselves an hour into the narrows, right when the light went from gold to bruise.

Two of them, low-slung, twin outboards, riding high and empty except for men and guns. They didn't hide, which was the worst part. Hiding would have meant they thought they needed to. These boys came around the bend abreast of each other like they were arriving at a party, and one of them stood up and put a hand on the tiller of a mounted machine gun the way you'd rest a hand on a dog you were deciding whether to pet or shoot.

"Bram," Hazel said quietly. "Kill the running lights."

I killed the running lights. Then I killed the lantern in the wheelhouse, and the one over the engine hatch, and by the time I was done we were a shadow moving inside a bigger shadow, which is the Kestrel's natural state if you ask me.

"They're signaling," Wren said. She was squinting astern. "Lamp work. Three flashes, pause, two flashes."

"You read lamp code?" Odile asked, wiping her hands on a rag that had given up being a rag years ago.

"My grandfather thought every child on the river should learn signal flags, lamp code, and whistle language before they learned long division." Something crossed her face when she said *grandfather* — a whole weather system in miniature — and then it cleared. "Three-two means *heave to and prepare for boarding*. It's standard syndicate grammar."

"Syndicate grammar," I repeated. "Lovely. Do they have a poetry section?"

"They have a section where boats get confiscated and crews get press-ganged," Wren said. "It's very popular."

Hazel hadn't spoken through any of this. She was doing the thing she does — the thing I've watched her do since we were both younger and stupider — where she goes still and lets the river talk to her. Her eyes weren't on the skiffs. They were on the water ahead, reading the surface the way Odile reads gauges, the way Wren reads faces, the way I read, well, nothing much, but I'm very good at carrying things.

"There's a side channel," she said finally. "Half a mile up. Left bank."

"Left bank's a wall of reeds," I said.

"Reeds are a door if you know where the handle is." She turned the wheel a spoke. "Bram, when I say now, give me everything the engine has and don't ask why. Odile —"

"I know," Odile said. "Fuel line's already primed. Don't insult her."

"— Wren, get below and lie down on the floor. Not a bunk. The floor. If we take fire, the hull is thicker than the cabin sides."

"And you?" Wren asked.

"I'll be standing behind the thickest man on the boat," Hazel said, and looked at me, and I sighed, because there are worse jobs than being furniture for your captain.

The skiffs closed to a hundred yards. The lamp flashed again — three, pause, two — impatient now, the way authority always gets when the river refuses to obey it. A voice carried across the water, amplified and flat: *"Kestrel, heave to. This is a licensed inspection under charter authority. Heave to or be boarded by force."*

"Licensed," Odile muttered. "By whom? Him?" She jerked her chin at the machine gun.

"Charter authority is whatever Corvus says it is," Wren said. "He owns the paper the law is printed on out here."

"*Now*, Bram," Hazel said.

I gave her everything. The old girl lurched forward hard enough to slide the soup bowls off the chart table — Wren's soup achieving its final form as bilge decoration — and the Kestrel dug her nose into the current and *went*. Forty years of oak and stubbornness, and I swear the hull hummed. Ahead of us the reed wall rushed closer, featureless, green-black in the dying light, and I confess I had my doubts, and I confess I kept them to myself, because that's the deal on this boat: Hazel steers, Bram believes.

"Handle," she murmured, almost to herself, eyes flicking across the reeds — and then her hand moved a half-spoke and there it was, a seam in the green, a thread of darker water where some old channel mouth still breathed through the reed bed, invisible unless you'd spent thirty years learning the river's tells. We went through it sideways, nearly, the reeds combing both rails with a sound like whispers, and behind us the skiffs' lamps swung wild, sweeping water where we weren't.

Then dark. Real dark. Reeds overhead, channel barely wider than the boat, engine down to a whisper, and five people holding their breath like the air itself was rationed.

For a long time nobody spoke. Somewhere behind us the amplified voice barked orders that got fainter and angrier, and then the current took even that away, and it was just us and the frogs and the creak of the wheel.

"Well," Odile said at last. "That went well."

"Don't," Hazel said.

"It went *well*—"

"Odile."

"—right up until it didn't, which is the traditional shape of things aboard this vessel."

"She's not wrong," I offered.

"She's frequently not wrong," Hazel said. "It's her most annoying quality." But she was smiling, thin and tired, in the dark where she thought nobody could see. I saw. I always see. That's my actual job on this boat, whatever my title says.

* * *

We ran the side channel until full dark, then tied up to a drowned willow in a backwater that smelled of mud and night-blooming something-or-other, and ate cold rice while the mosquitoes held their evening congress. That's when Hazel laid it out, because Hazel believes fear shared is fear halved, though she'd deny believing anything so soft.

"They knew our position within an hour of the tolling station," she said. "They knew our heading. They knew to wait at the narrows. That's not luck."

"The broker," Wren said flatly.

"The broker sold us," Odile agreed. "Or reported us. Half payment up front buys a lot of loyalty, but a syndicate bonus buys more."

"So what do we do?" Wren's voice had an edge on it now, and I recognized the edge, because I'd heard it in Hazel's voice twenty years ago and in my own more recently than I'd like to admit. It's the sound a person makes when the world confirms, again, that trusting it was stupid. "Run? Turn around? Give me to them and collect the other half?"

"Nobody's giving anybody to anybody," I said, quicker than I meant to.

"Then what's the plan?"

"The plan," Hazel said, "is that they think we're still in the narrows. Skiffs can't run this channel at night — too shallow, too many snags. By dawn we're ten miles past where their map says we can be. Then we stop wasting fuel, because—" she paused, and I watched her decide whether to say the next part, and watched her lose the argument with herself, "—because we don't have much. The reserve tank is half what the gauge claims. Odile found a leak in the reserve line two days ago and jury-rigged it, but jury-rigs leak."

"How half?" Wren asked.

"Enough," Hazel said. "If nothing goes wrong."

On this river, in this season, with those men behind us, *if nothing goes wrong* is not a plan. It's a eulogy with ambitions. But nobody said so out loud, because we're a crew, and a crew's first duty is maintaining the fiction that the captain's plans are plans.

We stood watches through the night. I took the middle one, which suited me — I like the hours when the river goes quiet and honest, no traffic, no lies, just water doing water's ancient paperwork. Around two in the morning Wren couldn't sleep and came and sat with me on the cargo hatch, and we didn't talk much, which is the best kind of talking. Once she said, "You'd really not give me up?" and I said, "Kid, I've seen the captain throw better offers than yours off this boat," which wasn't quite an answer, but she took it like one, and went below, and slept.

Foolish girl. Foolish, brave, sharp-eyed girl. I'd have said the same about her captain, once.

* * *

Dawn came up gray-green and mean, the sky the color of a bruise going yellow at the edges, and Hazel stood at the rail studying it with the expression of a woman reading a letter she doesn't like.

"Front's early," she told us over breakfast-that-was-rice-again. "Monsoon's supposed to be four days out. That sky says two. Maybe less in the hills where it builds."

"Then we run," Odile said.

"We run," Hazel agreed. "But smart. Fuel's math now. Every mile costs. So we take the main channel — faster water, fewer bends — and we pray the narrows bought us enough lead that the skiffs are hunting reeds instead of river."

Reader, the narrows had not bought us enough lead.

We made good time through the morning — the Kestrel humming, Odile coaxing miracles from the engine, Wren up in the bow calling depth marks off the lead line like she'd been born to it, which, apparently, she half had. The country opened up. Hills shouldered in from the east, forested and dark under that bruised sky. Twice we passed checkpoints — syndicate docks with new paint and bored guards — and twice Hazel took us wide around them through water I wouldn't have attempted, hugging sandbars so close I could've shaken hands with the herons standing on them.

It was on the third such maneuver, threading a bar that Hazel swore blind was firm ground under six inches of water, that I looked back and saw the skiffs.

Both of them. Four miles back, maybe five, running the main channel like they owned it, because as far as the paper was concerned they did.

"They found us," I said.

Hazel didn't even turn. "I know. I've been watching them in the mirror for twenty minutes."

"Twenty *minutes*?"

"I was waiting to see if they'd spotted us before I ruined everyone's lunch."

"That's not leadership, that's cruelty."

"It's both," Odile said. "Best kind."

The arithmetic went like this: they were faster, we had a head start, and the channel ahead bent east into a stretch Hazel called the Looms — a maze of shifting bars and braided channels where the river couldn't decide what it wanted to be. Fast water, deep holes, bars that moved with every flood. No charts worth trusting. The syndicate pilots ran it wide and slow or not at all.

"They won't follow us into the Looms," Hazel said.

"They'll follow us anywhere," Wren said. "There's a price on us. There's a price on *all* of us now, isn't there? That's what the checkpoints were. Not routine. Hunting."

Hazel was quiet a moment. "Yes," she said. "Probably."

"Good," said Wren Thornbury-Reyes-whatever-her-name-was, sixteen years old, gripping the rail with white knuckles and her chin up like a figurehead. "Then they should know we're expensive."

I tell you, that kid is going to be trouble for somebody someday, and I intend to be alive to see it.

* * *

The chase lasted four hours and cost us everything.

They came into the Looms after us, because Wren was right — money makes men brave in exactly the places skill shouldn't. But they came in fast, and the Looms punishes fast. We heard the first one go aground a mile behind us, engines screaming, and Odile actually cheered, and then the second one learned from the first and slowed down, and the game became what it always becomes on this river: not who's fastest, but who knows the water.

Hazel knew the water.

I've run rivers with pilots who memorized charts, pilots who bribed lockkeepers, pilots who claimed the river spoke to them in dreams. None of them were Hazel. She ran the Looms at near-full throttle in failing light, reading the surface like large print — the smooth tongue of water over a bar, the boil mark above a snag, the color shift that meant deep channel swinging left — and she called it aloud as she went, half to me, half to herself: "*Bar, port, easy — snag, starboard, wide — hole here, hold center —*" and I worked the throttle to her voice like a musician, and the Kestrel danced, forty years old and dancing, and for maybe ninety minutes I forgot to be afraid.

Then the light failed completely, and the rain started — fat, warm, pre-monsoon drops like warnings — and the second skiff's lamp found us through it, and the machine gun opened up.

Not aimed, mostly. Warning fire, or showing off, tracers stitching the water astern. But one burst walked close, and I heard the particular *tink-tink-tink* of rounds finding our rail, and Odile yelled something unrepeatable from the engine hatch, and Hazel said, very calmly, "Hold course," which is captain for *we are all going to die but orderly*.

"There!" Wren shouted from the bow, pointing left. "Water's moving wrong there — it's pushing *out* of the reeds, there's a channel behind—"

"I see it," Hazel said, and I heard it in her voice, the thing I'd been waiting all day not to hear: doubt. Because what Wren had spotted was a flood channel, the kind that runs only when the river's up, the kind that appears on no chart because it didn't exist last season and won't exist next month. Running one at night, in rain, pursued, was not navigation. It was a coin toss with the whole pot on the table.

Behind us: gunfire, gaining. Ahead of us: unknown water. Below us: half a reserve tank and a leaky jury-rig.

"Captain," I said. "Your call."

She stood at the wheel with the rain running off her chin, and I watched her look at the wrong-moving water, and I watched her do the thing she does — listen to the river — and whatever it said to her, she believed it.

"Hard to port," she said. "Bram, half throttle. Wren, keep calling what you see. Odile —"

"Priming," Odile said. "Go."

We went.

The flood channel took us like a hand. It was narrow, fast, and insane, water pushing out of the flooded backcountry toward the main stem, and it carried us around three bends and through a stand of drowned saplings that scraped the hull like fingernails, and then — because the river giveth — it shallowed, suddenly, treacherously, a bar of fresh silt laid down by the last flood lying right across our path like a tripwire.

Hazel saw it a half-second too late to avoid it and exactly one half-second early to survive it.

"Everyone down!" she roared, and threw the wheel over, and the Kestrel hit the bar bow-first at speed, climbed it, hung there for one endless heartbeat with her propeller singing that terrible note props sing when they're chewing mud and regret, and slid to a stop, canted, grounded, done.

Silence. Rain on the deck. The skiff's lamp sweeping the main channel behind us, finding nothing, because the reeds and the dark had swallowed us whole.

We lay on the deck boards and listened to our pursuers' engine fade away downstream, chasing a ghost, and then Odile started laughing, low and helpless, and then Wren, and then — God help me — me.

"Are we dead?" Wren asked. "Is this what being dead is like?"

"We're grounded," Hazel said. She was already moving, sounding the water off the bow with a boathook, reading depths by feel. "But we're grounded on *soft*. High water in the morning lifts us off. We lose the tide of darkness, we lose—" she stopped. I heard her stop.

"Lose what, Captain?" Odile asked, in the voice of a woman who already knew.

"Fuel," Hazel said. "Getting off this bar tomorrow takes power. Getting anywhere after takes power. The reserve was half. What the prop chewed tonight, and what the leak took..." She straightened up, and in the lightning-flash I saw her face, and it was the face of a woman doing sums she hated. "We have enough to reach the witness. We don't have enough to run, or fight, or change our minds. One tank. One direction. One try."

Nobody spoke. The rain filled the silence for us.

"One try," Wren repeated softly. And then, with that chin-up figurehead look that terrifies me more than machine guns ever will: "Then let's not waste it."

* * *

We anchored proper that night, off the bar, in the lee of the willows, and stood watches, and nobody slept much. Toward morning the rain thickened from warning to promise, and the river came up an inch, then two, and at first light Hazel put us over the bar on a whisper of throttle, the Kestrel grinding free like an old dog getting up, and pointed us upstream — upstream, toward Tomas Reyes, toward the truth, toward the last of the fuel and the first edge of the storm.

At the wheel, watching her husband's river rise to meet her, Hazel said, "Whatever's coming, it's coming fast now. All of it."

And far off to the east, over the hills, the sky had gone the color of a bruise going black.
