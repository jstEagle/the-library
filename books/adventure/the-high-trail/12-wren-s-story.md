## 12. Wren's Story

The rain found us an hour after dark, and it did not knock politely.

It came down on the Kestrel's canvas awning like a mob demanding entry, and Captain Thornbury — Hazel, I was apparently allowed to call her now, though my tongue kept snagging on it like a nail in a plank — ordered the brazier lit under cover and everyone below or under canvas, no arguments. So we sat in a ring of orange light with the storm hammering overhead and the river muttering to itself beyond the rail, and there was nowhere left for any of us to hide.

I'd been dreading this since the sandbar. Maybe since the crate.

Bram handed me a tin mug of something that smelled like burnt pine and ambition. "It's tea," he said, in the tone of a man defending a slandered friend. "Mostly."

"Mostly tea," Odile repeated from her corner, where she sat with her bad ankle propped on a coil of rope, her knife out, doing that whittling thing she did when she wanted people to know she was listening but not necessarily agreeing.

Hazel took the bench across from me. She didn't say *well, out with it*. She just looked at me, patient as a lock waiting for the right key, and somehow that was worse than shouting.

I wrapped both hands around the mug because they were shaking and I didn't want anyone to see. They saw anyway. River people see everything; it's how they stay alive.

"You want the whole thing," I said. "Not the version where I'm a poor orphan fleeing a wicked uncle."

"The whole thing," Hazel said. "We're carrying you upriver toward a man named Corvus who wants you delivered intact. Intact is a word men use about cargo. Before I decide what kind of fool I've been, I want to know what's actually in the hold."

So I told them.

* * *

"My grandfather was Aurelio Serrano."

I watched the name land. Bram stopped chewing. Odile's knife paused mid-stroke. Even the rain seemed to lean in.

"Before you ask — yes, *that* Serrano. The river's named after my family, which sounds grander than it is. It mostly means everyone assumes you were born holding a ledger in one hand and someone else's throat in the other." I took a sip of the mostly-tea and grimaced. Bram looked wounded. "Grandfather owned the charters. All of them. Every dock, every toll station, every licensed run from the delta to the high water. He didn't build it, exactly. His father did, back when the river was lawless enough that owning it was just a fancier way of being a pirate. By Grandfather's time it was all ink and seals and men in good coats shaking hands over other men's labor."

"And Corvus?" Hazel asked quietly.

"Corvus was his right hand. Thirty years. He started as a tally clerk and ended up running half the syndicate's business while Grandfather got old. Everyone said he was the loyal one. The dog who never bit the hand." My voice did something ugly on that last part, and I let it. "He was also my mother's brother-in-law by his first marriage, which is how family gets tangled into business until you can't tell a hug from a headlock."

Odile snorted. First sound she'd made all night. I decided to take it as encouragement.

"Grandfather had two children. My mother, and my uncle Teo. Teo died young — fever, when I was four. That left Mother as heir, except she married against Grandfather's wishes, a river pilot with more scars than money, and moved downstream to get away from all of it. Then the fever came through our town three years ago and took her and my father both inside a week." I shrugged, because the alternative was crying, and I'd done my crying in privies and culverts for two years and I was frankly tired of it. "That left me. Sixteen years old, orphaned, and suddenly the only living descendant of Aurelio Serrano. You can imagine how welcome that made me at High Landing."

"They didn't send you to school," Hazel said. It wasn't a question.

"They sent me to dinners. To be displayed. Corvus was very kind, publicly. Sat me at his right hand. Told everyone I was *the future of the river*. And privately—" I turned the mug in my hands. "Privately he treated me like a document he hadn't gotten around to shredding yet. There were rules. Where I could go, who I could talk to. When I asked too many questions about the charter ledgers, my tutor was replaced with one who taught less. When I wrote to my mother's old friends, the letters came back opened. Resealed badly. Corvus's people seal things beautifully when they care. These were sealed like an apology."

"And the will?" Bram asked. He'd gone still in the way big men go still, which I was learning meant they were angry on your behalf and didn't have a target yet.

"The will is why we're all sitting in the rain." I set the mug down. "Two months ago Grandfather's health collapsed. He knew he was dying — he was sharp enough to know it even through the fog. And something happened. I don't know what. An argument with Corvus, maybe. Or he finally read the ledgers properly himself. But he summoned a lawyer from outside the syndicate, a woman named Ferro out of Saltmarsh, and he rewrote everything. And then he died before Corvus could get the new will declared invalid, which I think — I *think* — was not entirely accidental timing on Grandfather's part. He was a hard old man, but he was never careless."

"What does it say?" Hazel asked.

"It says the charters pass to me. Not to Corvus. Not to the syndicate board. To me, held in trust until I'm twenty-one, with a council of trustees — and the trustees are named in the will, and none of them work for Corvus. Old pilots. A magistrate. A man called Tomas Reyes, who used to run charter inspections upriver before he supposedly drowned." I looked around the circle. "Corvus has spent twenty years building himself into the bones of the river trade. If that will is read publicly and validated, every falsified charter, every bought toll station, every debt he engineered — it all comes under scrutiny. He doesn't lose money. He loses *everything*. Possibly his neck, if the council digs far enough."

"So he needs you," Odile said slowly, "not dead."

"Not dead. Dead is a problem — dead means inquiries, means Ferro the lawyer waving papers, means martyrdom. What he needs is me *disappeared*. Quietly. Into his custody, legally, as my grieving guardian. And then the will needs to disappear too. There are copies — Ferro holds one, and there are witnesses who heard the terms read aloud. But paper burns, and witnesses drown. Rivers are convenient that way." I smiled, and felt it come out crooked. "He couldn't do it at High Landing. Too many eyes, and Ferro had already filed notice with the river council that a will existed. So the plan was subtler: get me out of the city before the council session, ostensibly for my safety, deliver me to a trusted associate downriver — and somewhere on the water, between one lock and the next, Wren Serrano stops existing. Grief-stricken girl, fled the estate, presumed lost to the flood season. Tragic. Unfortunate. No body, no inquiry worth the name."

"And instead you climbed into a crate," Bram said.

"Instead I climbed into a crate." I laughed, and it came out wetter than I intended. "Your broker wasn't hired to move cargo, Captain. He was hired to move *me*, by men pretending to act for Corvus's rivals — that was the story I was given, anyway, when I paid the dockhand to look away. Get aboard the Kestrel, ride to the rendezvous, and someone friendly would take me to Ferro in Saltmarsh. Except I've had two months of learning what Corvus's arrangements smell like, and halfway down the river I realized the friendly people at the end of this route were going to be Corvus's people, and the crate was going to be the least of my problems. So I stayed hidden past the rendezvous point and hoped the boat would keep going, and then your captain caught me going through the charts, and here we are."

Silence, except the rain.

Hazel was staring into the fire with an expression I couldn't read — not pity, thank God, I'd have walked off the boat into the river rather than be pitied — but something harder and more useful. Recalculation. I could almost hear the abacus clicking behind her eyes.

"The rendezvous," she said. "Vane's launch. He knew your name before we told him."

"I know. I worked that out on the sandbar, lying flat under tarpaulin while armed men shot at the boat I was hiding on. Which was, for the record, a low point." I pulled my knees up. "He's not working for my grandfather's memory, Captain. He's working for Corvus. Maybe he always was. Maybe that's what the triple rate was buying."

"That's what I'm afraid of," Hazel said, and something in her voice made me look at her properly. There was history in her face — old, scarred-over history, the kind that aches when the weather changes. "Wren. Twenty years ago, my father ran charters on this river. Up the Serrano, the high water. He was ruined for it. Disgraced, bankrupted, driven off the river he loved. And Silas Vane testified against him."

The fire popped. Somewhere upstream, thunder rolled along the gorge like a barrel.

"I didn't know that," I said. "About your father."

"Few do. Corvus is thorough about erasing things." Her mouth twisted. "It seems he's been erasing things longer than either of us knew."

* * *

Odile spoke next, and when Odile speaks, rooms get quieter, so the whole boat got quieter.

"The witnesses," she said. "In the will. You said Reyes is supposed to be dead."

"Presumed. Drowned in the high water twenty years ago. But Ferro believes otherwise — she wouldn't tell me how she knows, only that if I ever needed the will enforced, I should find Reyes alive, because a witness beats a copy every time. Paper can be forged. A living man who heard Aurelio Serrano speak his own terms cannot."

"And Reyes is upriver," Hazel said. Not asking. Working it out loud, the way captains do, laying bearings.

"Ferro thought so. The last trace of him was a mission station above the gorge. Twenty years ago." I hesitated. "Captain, I need to say the rest, and then I need to stop talking, because my throat is closing up and I refuse to cry in front of a man whose tea tastes like that."

Bram clutched his mug to his chest. "Fair."

"The rest is this." I stood, because sitting felt like begging, and I was done begging. Two years of being small and quiet and careful, two years of being a document awaiting its shredding — I was tired of it down to the marrow. "My grandfather spent his last weeks making sure that everything Corvus built on lies would collapse the moment I stood up and claimed it. He believed I'd have the spine to do it. I don't know if he was right. I'm sixteen, I've been chased across half a river, I've been shot at, and the only people standing between me and Corvus are a crew I stowed away on and a captain who's being paid to hand me over." I made myself look at each of them. "But here's what I know. Corvus sent armed skiffs after a sixteen-year-old girl. He put a broker on your dock and guns on your wake. Men like that don't spend blood on nothing. Which means I'm worth more to him free than any of you will ever see from delivering me — and worth more to the river entire if the will gets read. I'm not asking you to be heroes. I'm asking you to do arithmetic."

"Arithmetic," Odile repeated. Her knife resumed its stroke, slow and thoughtful.

"I'll make it easy," I said. "Deliver me: Vane pays you triple rate, and then Corvus pays you to forget you ever saw my face, and then, eventually, when the will surfaces without me, Corvus pays someone else to make sure you never discuss it. Men who move girls in crates don't leave loose ends, and you'd be the loosest ends on the river. Turn upriver instead: you lose the payment, you keep your lives, and maybe — *maybe* — you end up on the right side of the only trial that matters on this water in a generation." I spread my hands. "That's the whole ledger. I won't pretend it balances pretty."

Hazel rose. For a moment I thought she was going to answer, and every nerve in me leaned toward her like a plant toward light.

She didn't answer. She looked at me for a long moment — measuring, the way she measured the water, reading the mood of a thing beneath its surface — and then she said only:

"Get some sleep, Wren. Storm passes by morning. We'll need clear heads."

And she went out into the rain to stand at the wheel, alone, which I understood even then was not dismissal. It was a woman going to argue with herself, and I'd learned enough about Hazel Thornbury to know those arguments took all night.

* * *

I didn't sleep either. I lay in the narrow bunk Bram had rigged for me behind the galley, listening to the rain ease from mob to murmur, and I did the thing I'd sworn off: I cried, briefly, silently, into the blanket, for my mother and my father and my hard old grandfather who'd bet the river on a granddaughter he'd barely tolerated at dinner. Then I dried my face, because grief is a tide, not a destination, and tides turn.

Near dawn I came up on deck for air and found Odile already awake, coiling line, her ankle wrapped fresh.

"You did the arithmetic wrong," she said, without looking up.

My stomach dropped. "What?"

"You told us delivery keeps us safe and turning upriver costs us the payment." She cinched the coil tight. "Wrong both ways. Delivery was never safe — you said it yourself, loose ends. And upriver isn't a cost." She finally looked at me, and her face in the grey dawn light was unreadable and absolutely certain. "Upriver is an investment. Crews don't run from a payout, girl. They chase one."

Below decks, muffled through the planks, I heard voices. Several of them. Low, urgent, deliberate.

A vote, I realized. They were voting. About me. Without me.

I stood at the rail with the rain dripping off my nose and the sky going the color of pewter, and waited to learn whether I was cargo or crew — knowing, with a clarity that frightened me more than any skiff full of armed men, that whichever way they decided, I was done being carried.

Whatever came next, I would walk into it on my own feet.
