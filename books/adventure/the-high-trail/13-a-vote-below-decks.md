## 13. A Vote Below Decks

There is a particular smell a riverboat gets when she's been hurt and nobody's said it out loud yet. Bilgewater and hot iron and the sweet rot of wood that's taking on more than it should. I've known that smell since I was nine years old, when my uncle's barge went down off the Mireaux cut with all our good name aboard, so believe me when I tell you: the Kestrel had it now, faint but honest, seeping up through her deck seams like a confession.

I smelled it while I re-wrapped the pump gasket for the third time that evening, and I thought, *well. That's fitting. The boat's leaking secrets too.*

Because that's what we were full of, below decks that night. Secrets. Wren's will. Corvus's name. Vane's terms, delivered at gunpoint-adjacent on a gray stretch of water with his launch idling like a patient shark. And underneath all of it, unspoken, the question none of us wanted to say where the girl could hear:

Do we take her where we were paid to take her? Or do we spend money we don't have, on a gamble nobody asked us to make, and go find a dead man upstream?

* * *

Hazel called the meeting after Wren turned in. That was deliberate, obviously. Our captain has many gifts — reading water like scripture, lying to bank managers with a straight face, eating an entire pot of stew while claiming she isn't hungry — but subtlety is not usually counted among them. Still, she managed it. She waited until the sound of Wren settling into the forward bunk stopped, until the rain found its rhythm against the cabin roof, and then she said, quietly:

"Crew meeting. My bunk. Now."

Bram came down first, folding himself through the doorway the way he always does, like a man entering a church he doesn't believe in. He took the bench by the engine housing because it's the only seat that fits him. I sat on the chart locker. Hazel stood, arms crossed, leaning against the bulkhead with the low lamp throwing shadows under her eyes deep enough to store things in.

"I'm not going to pretend," she said, "that we haven't all been thinking the same thing."

"We haven't," Bram said. "I've been thinking several things. Some of them are about dinner."

"Bram."

"I'm listening, Captain."

She pushed off the bulkhead and paced the three steps the cabin allowed, turned, paced back. A caged animal with a ledger.

"Vane wants the girl delivered to Corvus at High Landing. Intact, he said. You heard him." Her voice did something careful around the word *intact*, the way you'd handle a hook you'd already been caught by once. "We were paid half up front to move a crate. The crate turned out to be a person. That makes this a different job than the one we signed for. So I'm putting it to a vote. Secret ballot. Nobody has to look anybody in the eye."

"And the options?" I asked, though I knew them. We all knew them. We'd been carrying them around like stones in our pockets for two days.

"One," Hazel said. "We finish the run. High Landing, handoff, payment. Full rate plus whatever hazard bonus I can squeeze out of Vane before he smells mutiny. We clear the debt. The Kestrel stays ours. We never speak of this again."

The lamp flickered. Somewhere above us, rain worked at the deck like a debt collector at a door.

"Two," she said. "We turn upriver. We go find Tomas Reyes — if he's alive, and that's a wager I wouldn't take even odds on — and we get Wren's will witnessed properly, and we fight Corvus in the open instead of handing him his problem wrapped in canvas." She stopped pacing. "That option costs fuel we barely have, time we definitely don't have, and puts every one of us on the wrong side of the man who owns every dock from here to the gorge. It may cost more than that."

Nobody spoke. The bilge pump kicked on, thought better of it, kicked off.

"There's no third option?" Bram asked.

"There's always a third option," Hazel said. "It's called jumping overboard, and I'm not writing it on a ballot."

* * *

Here is what you need to understand about votes on a boat, and why Hazel was right to make them secret, and wrong to think paper would save us.

A crew is not a democracy. It can't be. When the water turns ugly there's no time for committees; somebody has to say *hard to starboard* and everybody else has to move or die. So a captain rules, and the crew consents, and the whole arrangement runs on a currency that isn't money. It's trust. Trust that the captain knows the channel. Trust that she'd wreck herself before she wrecked you. Trust that when she says *one more run and we're clear*, one more run means what she says it means.

My uncle ran his barge on trust too. Right up until the night he loaded past the Plimsoll mark to make one last delivery, and the Mireaux cut taught our family the difference between trust and arithmetic.

So when Hazel said *secret ballot*, what she was really saying was: *I know some of you have stopped trusting me, and I'd rather not watch it happen.* And I loved her for it. And I resented her for it. Both things can be true on a boat. Both things are usually true on a boat.

She tore a page from the logbook — I winced; waste paper in this economy — into four scraps. She wrote *A* and *B* on two of them and handed each of us a pencil stub like we were schoolchildren, which, in fairness, is exactly how I felt.

"Mark your letter. Fold it. In the tin."

The biscuit tin went around. Bram marked his without hesitation, big blocky letters, folded the paper twice. I held my scrap a long moment, pencil hovering, feeling ridiculous and heavy-hearted in equal measure, and then I marked mine and dropped it in.

Hazel collected the tin, shook it once like dice, and opened it on the chart table under the lamp.

* * *

You want to know how I voted. Of course you do. Everyone always wants to know how the quartermaster voted, because everyone assumes the quartermaster counts the money and therefore votes with the money. I'll get there. But first let me tell you what the tin said, because that's the part that matters, and it matters more than Hazel let show, though I was watching her hands when she read it, and her hands told me everything.

Three slips said **A**. Finish the delivery. Take the money.

Two slips said **B**. Turn upriver. Spend what we don't have on a dead man and a living girl.

Three-two. On a four-person crew, counting the captain.

I'll give you a moment to do that arithmetic yourself, because Bram did it out loud, which was very Bram.

"That's four slips," he said slowly, "and there's five of us."

"Wren doesn't vote," Hazel said. "It's her life being voted on. She doesn't get to carry that."

"She's sixteen, not stupid—"

"It's not about stupid, Bram. It's about what a person can live with." Hazel's voice was level, but her hands had gone still on the table, flat, the way they do when she's holding something down. "She doesn't vote. That's final."

"So who wrote what?" Bram looked around the cabin. "Not asking for names. Just — who's with me?"

And there it was. The thing secret ballots are supposed to prevent, happening anyway, because paper can't stop a human face from doing what human faces do. Because here's the truth about our little family of four: I could read Bram's vote off him like a chart. He'd written **B**. Of course he had. Big soft giant, had taken one look at that girl hiding among the crates and appointed himself her wall. He'd been teaching her knots. *Knots.* Bram doesn't teach anyone knots. Knots are sacred.

And me — well.

I'd written **A**.

* * *

Let me explain myself, since apparently I'm confessing tonight.

I am the one who counts the money on this boat, which means I am the one who knows things the others get to not-know. I know the Kestrel is worth less than we owe. I know the hull patch I put in below the waterline at the rapids is a field repair holding on by stubbornness and tar, and that she needs real work in a real yard or she'll open up again somewhere worse. I know Odile's arithmetic, as my uncle learned too late, does not care about your feelings.

But that's not why I voted A. Arithmetic alone would have gotten me there, yes, but plenty of people do foolish generous things against their own sums. No. I voted A because of what happened at the trading post, when we learned whose granddaughter we were carrying. I watched Hazel's face when the name Corvus came out of that dockhand's mouth. I have known that woman eleven years. I have seen her furious, frightened, grieving, drunk twice, and heroic at least four times I could swear to. What I saw on her face at the trading post was none of those. It was older. It was a woman looking at a door she'd nailed shut twenty years ago and finding it standing open.

That kind of hate doesn't steer straight. That kind of hate turns upriver looking for ghosts, and ghosts don't sign wills, and dead men named Reyes don't testify, and meanwhile the hull leaks and the monsoon builds and the money walks away forever.

I voted A because I love this boat, and I love these idiots, and I have buried one family already and I decline to do it again for a cause. Even a just one. *Especially* a just one. The just ones are how they get you.

* * *

"Three-two," Hazel said again, to the table. "That's not a mandate. That's a knife with the handle pointing both ways."

"So what does the captain do?" Bram asked. "Tie-break?"

"No." She said it fast, too fast, and I saw Bram catch it too. "No tie-break. If I break the tie, then whoever loses spends the rest of this run obeying an order they think is wrong, and boats sink that way. I've seen it." She hadn't, actually. Neither had I. But her father had, presumably, and Hazel inherited her catastrophes secondhand, fully documented.

"Then what?" I asked. "We split the difference? Run her aground in the middle of the argument?"

Hazel laughed — one dry note, no humor in it, the laugh of a woman finding the exact bottom of a bad day. "What I do," she said, "is sleep on it. Which is captain-speak for *I don't know*, which is a phrase I try not to use more than once a season." She swept the slips off the table and, I noticed, did not throw them in the stove. She pocketed them. Hazel keeps evidence of her own failures; it's the most disciplined thing about her.

"For what it's worth," Bram rumbled, getting up, ducking his head under the beam, "whoever voted A — I don't think you're a coward. I think you're scared. There's a difference, and I've been both."

He went up to his watch. Which left me and Hazel in the lamplight with the pump ticking and the rain coming down and the whole unsaid thing sitting between us on the chart table like a fifth crew member.

"You voted A," she said. Not a question.

"I voted A."

"Why?"

And here's where I'll admit the quartermaster has one weakness besides arithmetic, and it's this: I looked at her, tired and lit yellow and carrying her father's ghost around like ballast she never asked for, and I told her the true reason instead of the safe one.

"Because if we turn upriver," I said, "you stop being my captain and start being his daughter. And I need my captain, Hazel. The river's about to get mean, and I need the woman who reads sandbars, not the woman hunting a reckoning. They can't both fit behind that wheel."

She didn't answer for a long time. The pump kicked on, ran, quit.

"Get some sleep, Odile," she finally said, and her voice was the captain's voice, steady as a depth reading. "We make way at dawn."

"Dawn toward where?"

She looked at me, and for one unguarded second I saw the other woman — the daughter, the debtor, the girl from the flashback chapters of her life she never tells — and then it closed over, smooth as still water over a snag.

"Toward the decision," she said. "Whatever it turns out to be."

* * *

I didn't sleep. I went up instead, because someone had to check the patch, and because I wanted to see the sky, and because the sky that night deserved seeing. Green-black in the west, bruise-colored, with lightning walking around inside it like something pacing a cage. The monsoon front, building itself behind us with all the patience of a creditor.

Wren was at the rail. Supposed to be asleep. Sixteen-year-olds and cats share certain policies regarding doors.

"Couldn't sleep either?" she asked.

"Someone has to count the leaks."

She was quiet a moment. Rain ticked on the deck. Then, not looking at me, in a voice carefully stripped of anything that could be called pleading:

"They voted, didn't they. While I was below. I heard the tin."

Ah. Well. There it was. The secret ballot, kept secret from everyone except the person whose entire future it decided.

"What did they decide?" she asked.

And I stood there with the truth in my mouth — *three-two, child, and the money won, and your captain is deciding right now whether you're cargo or kin* — and what came out instead was:

"The night's not over. Go to bed, Wren."

She went. But at the hatch she paused, and said, without turning around: "For what it's worth, Odile — I heard which way the wind's arguing too. It's been arguing all day." Then she was gone below, and I was alone with the green-black sky, and the leak ticking under my boots, and the growing understanding that whatever Hazel decided at dawn, half her family was going to feel betrayed by breakfast.

Behind us, far downstream, the storm cleared its throat.
