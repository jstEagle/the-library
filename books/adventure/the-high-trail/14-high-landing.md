## 14. High Landing

The river told us we were close before the charts did.

It widened, for one thing — spread itself out lazy and fat like a man who owns everything he surveys and knows it. The current slowed to a crawl, as if even the water had been bought and paid for. And the banks changed character. Where the Serrano above the narrows had been all jungle and rock and indifferent wilderness, here it grew groomed. Terraced gardens climbed the slopes. Pilings marched along the shoreline in neat regiments, each one stamped with a brand I recognized from a hundred manifests: a black bird with its wings folded, patient as a closed fist.

The corvid mark. Corvus's mark.

"Every dock from here to the delta," Odile said beside me at the rail. She'd come up from the engine room with grease still on her knuckles and her wound dressed under a fresh bandage, moving carefully but moving, which was more than I'd dared hope three days ago. "Every one of them his."

"I know."

"You knew before you saw it."

"I suspected. There's a difference." I adjusted my grip on the wheel. "Suspicion is free. Confirmation costs."

She looked at me sideways, that long measuring look she did when she thought I was being clever instead of honest. "And what's this confirmation going to cost, Captain?"

I didn't answer, because the answer was *everything we have left*, and she already knew it, and saying it out loud would only make it heavier.

* * *

High Landing announced itself the way rich men do — not loudly, but inevitably.

The town sat on a bluff where the river bent north, and it wasn't a town so much as an argument in favor of one man. A deepwater wharf ran half a mile along the bank, cradling barges and steam launches and two river freighters big enough to make the Kestrel look like a child's toy left beside her father's boots. Warehouses rose behind the docks in ranks, slate-roofed and identical, each flying the black bird. Above them, terraces of white stone climbed toward a compound at the top of the bluff — walls, towers, glass roofs catching the gray monsoon light like knives laid out on a table.

Bram came forward from the bow, coiling line with more force than the task required. "That's not a dock," he said. "That's a throat. And we're sailing straight down it."

"That's the general idea," I said.

"Forgive me if I don't find that comforting."

Wren stood apart from us near the stern, arms crossed tight, watching the compound with a face gone very still. Sixteen years old, and she was looking at the place where her grandfather's empire had been carved up and handed to a man wearing his name like a stolen coat. I'd seen that stillness before — on my father's face, once, when we passed the courthouse where he'd been ruined. It wasn't fear. It was a person deciding how much of themselves they were willing to spend.

"You can stay aboard," I said to her, quietly enough that only she could hear. "When we tie up. You can stay below, even. Nobody says you have to stand on that dock."

"Yes, they do," Wren said. "He does. Vane does. That's the whole arrangement." She turned to me, and her chin was up, and her eyes were wet and furious and absolutely level. "I'm the cargo, Captain. Cargo gets delivered. But I'm also the heir, and heirs get to see what they're inheriting." She looked back at the white terraces. "I want to see what he built on top of my grandfather's bones."

There was nothing to say to that that wouldn't have been a lie, so I said nothing, and put the wheel over, and took us in.

* * *

Dock Nine at High Landing was bigger than Dock Nine at home by a factor of ten, which felt like a private joke someone was playing at my expense. The harbormaster's men waved us in with the bored efficiency of people who had never once doubted the outcome of anything. Lines went out. Bram caught the bollard. The Kestrel kissed the fenders and settled, and the sound of her hull coming to rest against that enormous stone quay made my chest ache in a way I couldn't afford to examine.

Because here was the thing I hadn't let myself think about since the narrows: this was the end of the run. Whatever happened on that dock, the job was done or undone in the next hour, and every version of it ended with money in my account and something rotten in my chest, or no money and a clean conscience and a bank auctioning my father's boat inside the month.

I'd been telling myself for two hundred miles that I hadn't decided yet. Standing there watching Vane walk down the quay toward us, I admitted the truth: I'd decided. I just hadn't figured out how to survive the decision.

Vane wore white. Of course he wore white. Linen suit, pressed within an inch of its life, not a rain-spot on it despite the sky hanging low and green-black over the whole valley. He walked like a man arriving rather than approaching, hands clasped behind his back, and he stopped precisely at the head of our gangway as if a surveyor had marked the spot.

"Captain Thornbury." His voice carried pleasantly across the water. "You've made excellent time. The river must have wanted you here."

"The river doesn't want things," I said. "That's what I like about it."

Something flickered behind his smile — appreciation, maybe, or its counterfeit. "May I come aboard?"

"You may stand on your own dock," I said. "We'll talk across the gap. Consider it tradition."

"A tradition of what?"

"Cautious people."

His smile held. He gestured, and two of his crew — because of course he had crew now, four men in matching dark jackets standing back along the quay with the studied idleness of hired muscle — brought forward a leather case and set it down between us. Vane rested one hand on it.

"Half on delivery, as agreed. The balance transfers the moment the young lady steps onto the dock." He glanced past me, found Wren at the rail, and inclined his head with genuine-seeming courtesy. "Miss Wren. You've given these good people quite an adventure, I'm told."

"They gave me one," Wren said. "I just paid for it."

"Wonderful," Vane said warmly. "A negotiator. Your grandfather would be proud — he drove a hard bargain too, right up until the end." And there it was, just under the warmth: a blade shown briefly, then sheathed. *Right up until the end.* He knew exactly what he was saying and to whom.

I stepped into his sightline. "Before anyone steps anywhere," I said, "there's the matter of the handoff terms. Which I'd like to review. In detail. On paper."

Vane's eyebrows rose a fraction. "The terms were agreed at Dock Nine, Captain. Half now, half on delivery. Simple arithmetic."

"Simple arithmetic is for simple cargoes. This is a person, and persons require paperwork. Chain of custody. A signed release naming who takes responsibility for her welfare after she leaves my deck." I leaned on the rail. "Unless, of course, you'd prefer there be no paper trail. In which case I'd want to know why a legitimate inheritance delivery needs to be invisible."

For the first time, the smoothness stuttered. Not visibly — Vane was far too good for visible — but I felt it, the way you feel a current change under a calm surface. His men shifted. One of them looked at the harbor office.

"Captain," he said, still pleasant, "you were hired to move a crate upriver. What the crate contained became your problem through no fault of mine. I am offering you triple rate and a clean exit. Don't mistake my flexibility for patience."

"Oh, I never mistake anything," I said. "It's my worst quality. Ask my creditors." I turned and raised my voice. "Odile! Bring out the manifest. The full one. And wake up the notary stamp — if High Landing has a port authority, I want this delivery witnessed properly, and if it doesn't, I want to know why a syndicate that owns every dock on the river is afraid of a signature."

Down in the wheelhouse, Odile moved with theatrical slowness, and I heard her open a drawer, and close it, and open another. Buying time. Bless her scheming heart.

Vane watched this performance with his head tilted slightly, like a man reassessing a horse he'd thought he'd already priced. "You're stalling," he said, without heat.

"I'm negotiating," I said. "You taught my family the difference. You testified to it, in fact, twenty years ago, very eloquently, in front of a judge."

The flicker again, deeper this time. "Your father's affairs were his own."

"My father's affairs were *arranged*, Mr. Vane. By men with black birds on their stationery. And someday — not today, I'm too busy, but someday — I'm going to ask you what exactly you were coerced into testifying, and what they held over you, and whether you've slept a single night since." I smiled at him, and it cost me everything to keep it friendly. "But today, we do paperwork."

* * *

It bought us forty minutes. Maybe less.

Odile produced a manifest so dense with invented line items that Vane's own clerk — summoned from the harbor office, sweating through his collar — needed three separate consultations to verify it. Bram discovered, with great regret, a fouled mooring line requiring immediate attention. Wren stayed at the rail in plain view, which was the point; as long as the cargo stood visible and unharmed, Vane had no cause to force the issue, and forcing it in broad daylight on his own busiest dock would raise questions even his money couldn't quickly drown.

But I watched him while we stalled, and watching him told me things.

His men weren't bored. They were positioned — two at the gangway, one at the bollard lines, one drifting toward the Kestrel's stern line with a knife he thought nobody had seen. They weren't there to receive a delivery. They were there to take one. And twice, Vane touched his left cuff — a small motion, twice repeated — and each time, somewhere up on the terraces, a figure moved. Signaling. Coordinating. This wasn't a handoff. It was a harvest, and we had sailed ourselves into the field.

I caught Bram's eye across the deck and tipped my head a quarter-inch toward the stern line. He followed my glance, understood, and began, very casually, to re-coil rope directly on top of it.

Then Vane checked his watch — a real check, not a performed one — and something in his posture changed. The pleasantness remained, but it became a coat worn over readiness, the way ice remains smooth over moving water.

"Captain," he said, "the paperwork is fascinating. Truly. But the tide window for the balance transfer closes with the harbormaster's office at six, and my employer dislikes waiting." He smiled. "So let us dispense with theater. Bring the girl down. Take your money. Sail home wealthy. Or refuse, and learn what my employer pays for obstruction — which is considerably less than triple rate, and collected differently."

On my deck, my crew went quiet. Odile in the wheelhouse doorway, bandage showing at her collar. Bram by the stern line, rope in his hands. Wren beside me, small and straight-backed, her hand finding the rail.

And I looked at Vane, and I looked past him — at the warehouses, the freighters, the white terraces climbing to the compound where a man named Corvus sat at the top of everything my father had lost — and I felt the trap close around me with a click so gentle it was almost polite.

Eyes open. Every door marked. Walking in anyway.

"Well," I said, and put my hand on the gangway rail. "Since we're dispensing with theater—"

From up on the bluff, a bell began to ring.
