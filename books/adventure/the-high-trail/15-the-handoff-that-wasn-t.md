## 15. The Handoff That Wasn't

I've been in fights before. Most of them were with rivermen, which means they follow rules — you shove, you swear, somebody's hat goes in the water, everybody drinks after. What happened at High Landing was not that kind of fight.

But I'm getting ahead of myself, and a man who gets ahead of himself on a river ends up wrapped around a snag. So let me start where it started, which was with Hazel standing at the rail at dawn, looking like she hadn't slept, because she hadn't, and saying the four worst words in the trade:

"We do it today."

* * *

We'd tied up two miles downstream of High Landing the night before, in a cut of deadwater behind a willow island, because Hazel didn't trust the docks and neither did any of us. From there you could see the town sitting up on its bluff like a fat man at a banquet — corrugated roofs, crane arms, the syndicate warehouse with Corvus's mark burned into its doors, a black key crossed over a white current. Every barge in sight wore that mark. Every rope, every cleat, every dockhand's armband. The man didn't own the river, exactly. He owned everything touching it, which amounts to the same thing.

Wren stood beside Hazel at the rail and didn't say anything for a long time. When she finally did, her voice had gone flat and careful, the way mine does when I'm counting bullets.

"That's his," she said. "All of it."

"Looks heavy," Hazel said. "All that weight. You'd think he'd sink."

It was a good line. Nobody laughed. Some lines are just for keeping your spine straight.

The plan — and I use the word generously — was this: Vane's launch would meet us mid-channel off the north dock at ten bells. We'd bring Wren out on the Kestrel's deck, under escort, visible to everyone, so there'd be witnesses to a clean handoff. Vane would pay the balance. We'd cast off. Simple. Clean. The kind of plan that works fine until it meets people.

Odile spent the morning checking the deck guns — we carried two, an old swivel mount aft and a scattergun Bram-sized, meaning me — and saying nothing, which from Odile is a weather report all by itself. If Odile is quiet, rain is coming. If Odile is quiet *and* cleaning weapons, bring your ark.

"You've got a look," I told her.

"I always have a look."

"This one's worse."

She ran a rag down the swivel barrel and said, "Vane's launch has six men on it. A clean handoff needs four, maybe five. Somebody's riding extra."

I looked at her. She looked at me. Neither of us said the next part, because saying it makes it true, but we both knew: extra men means extra orders, and extra orders at a handoff are never *be polite*.

Hazel heard us anyway. She has ears like a sandbar has edges — you don't see them till you're on them.

"Then we go in ready," she said. "Nobody boards us. Wren walks across a plank or nobody walks anywhere. Those are the terms."

"And if they don't honor them?" Wren asked.

Hazel looked at her — sixteen years old, jaw set like her grandfather's portrait must have been set, wearing a borrowed coat three sizes too big — and something crossed her face that I'd never seen on it before. It took me a while to name it. It was the look of a woman deciding a thing is hers to protect.

"Then they'll wish they'd honored them," Hazel said.

* * *

Ten bells came up gray and hot, the air thick enough to chew. We rounded the willow island with steam up and the current helping, and there was Vane's launch, waiting off the north dock exactly where he'd said, white hull, brass rail, six men standing on her deck in matching dark jackets.

Six. Odile counted them out loud without moving her lips, the way she does, like a prayer said backward.

Vane stood at the launch's bow in a pale linen suit that had no business being that clean this far upriver, and he lifted his hat to Hazel across the water, and I swear the man smiled like this was a church social. That's the thing about Silas Vane that I hate most. Not the treachery — rivers are full of treachery, it practically counts as ballast. It's that he enjoys the manners of it. He wants to be liked even while he's robbing you.

"Hazel Thornbury!" he called, voice carrying flat across the still water. "You keep better time than your father ever did!"

Beside me, I felt Hazel go very still. There are things you can say to Hazel Thornbury and live, and references to her father are not on that list, and Vane knew it, and said it anyway, which told me more about his mood than any number of extra men could.

"Terms first," Hazel called back. "The girl crosses alone. Nobody boards my boat. Payment transfers before her foot touches your deck."

"Payment's in the case, as agreed—"

"Before. Her. Foot."

A pause. Vane spread his hands, a showman conceding to his audience. "Before her foot," he agreed.

We came alongside easy, fenders out, lines across. I held the plank steady while Wren walked to the rail. She stopped there and looked back at us — at Hazel, at Odile at the swivel, at me — and for one second the sharp-tongued girl who'd called my cooking "a crime against grain" looked exactly her age, scared and trying not to be, and I felt something in my chest I didn't have time to examine.

"See you on the other side of this," she said.

"That's the idea," Hazel said.

And then two things happened almost at once, and I've replayed them a hundred times since, trying to figure out which one I should have caught first.

The first thing: one of the six men on the launch — the one standing farthest forward, half-hidden behind the wheelhouse — shifted his jacket, and under it I saw the oiled gleam of a shoulder rig, and behind him, low in the launch's stern, a tarp that covered a shape much longer than luggage.

The second thing: two more boats came around the dock pilings. Not Vane's. Bigger. Syndicate gunboats, black-hulled, engines idling quiet, each carrying four men apiece, and every one of those men looking not at Wren but at the *Kestrel*.

"They're not here for the girl," Odile said quietly. "They're here for the boat."

I want to be fair to Hazel here, because history — Wren's history, the kind written in charters and council minutes — will probably record this moment as Hazel's decision, Hazel's defiance, Hazel's choice. And it was. But it was also Odile's, because it was Odile who saw the grapnel line come off the lead gunboat's bow, coiled and ready, meant for our rail. And it was mine too, because I was closest to the plank, and when the first of Vane's men put his boot on it — coming *across*, not receiving, boarding instead of taking delivery — I did the arithmetic faster than politeness allowed.

"Nobody boards my boat," Hazel had said.

So I picked up the plank and threw it in the river.

That sounds small. It wasn't. A plank is a border, and borders matter, and the moment that pine went into the water between us and them, everything that followed became their doing, not ours. I hold onto that. A man needs something to hold onto.

For about three seconds, nobody moved. The current turned the plank lazily end over end. Somewhere on the dock a gull screamed like it had money on the outcome.

Then the man with the shoulder rig pulled a whistle from his collar and blew it, and the world came apart.

* * *

Here is what I remember, in pieces, because memory in a fight is a cargo net — you grab what you can and lose the rest.

The tarp coming off the launch's stern and a mounted gun underneath it, a real one, the kind you put on a patrol boat, and the gunner swinging it toward our wheelhouse.

Hazel ringing the engine bell twice — full ahead — and the Kestrel lunging like a horse that's been waiting all morning for permission.

The first shot cracking past my ear close enough to part my hair, and me getting the scattergun up and answering, mostly to make them duck, because I'm a decent shot but I'm not a murderer, though I'll admit the distinction got blurry fast.

The grapnel line arcing from the gunboat and biting into our rail with a sound like a tooth breaking, and the two boats wrenching together, and men coming over the gap with hooks and pistols, shouting words like *cargo* and *seize* and *by order*, as if paper made their hands holy.

And Odile.

I have to tell it straight, because she'd want it straight and because she'd deny anything softer. Odile Marchand stood at the aft swivel with her hair coming loose and her sleeves rolled, and she worked that old gun like she'd been born to it, which she hadn't — she'd been born to a bakery in Port Serrano and taught herself everything else — and she laid down fire along the gunboat's deck that kept eight armed men pinned behind their own wheelhouse. Not wild fire. Measured fire. She was shooting to keep them down, not to kill them, right up until the moment one of them climbed the pilot house roof with a rifle and found her silhouette against the sky.

I heard the shot. Everybody hears that shot. It's stitched into me now.

Odile sat down hard against the swivel mount, one hand pressed high on her left side, blood coming through her fingers, and she looked almost annoyed about it, the way you'd be annoyed at a splinter. Then she took her other hand off the gun just long enough to shove a fresh magazine at me and say, "Feed it," and went back to work.

I fed it. God help me, I fed it, and I fired it, and somewhere in there the distinction I was holding onto earlier drowned, because a man was coming over our rail with a knife and Wren was fifteen feet away and there was no version of the next five seconds where I let him land.

He landed anyway. Just not on his feet.

* * *

How we got clear — and I've told this story four times now and it comes out different every time, so consider this the official version — was Hazel's doing, and it was beautiful, and it was terrible.

With the two boats grinding together and boarders on our deck and the mounted gun hammering our wheelhouse to matchwood, Hazel did the one thing none of them expected, because it was insane. She turned the Kestrel *into* the gunboat.

Full ahead, hard over, into the grind. Our bow plate hit their hull amidships with a boom I felt in my teeth, and the impact threw every man straddling the gap off his feet — theirs and ours both — and tore the grapnel line's anchor cleat clean out of our rail, wood and bolts and all. For one long grinding moment the two boats were locked hull to hull, scraping, screaming, and then Hazel backed her off hard and the river poured into the gap and we were loose.

The mounted gun swung after us. I'll hear that rattle in my sleep for a year. But we were already falling downstream on the current with smoke pouring off our stack and holes in our wheelhouse you could post letters through, and the gunboats had to turn in their own length, and turning takes time, and time on a river is distance.

Vane's launch didn't pursue. I looked back once — one look, no more — and saw him standing at his rail in his clean suit, watching us go, and he wasn't shouting orders. He was just watching, with his head tilted slightly, like a man listening to music only he could hear. Like a man who'd gotten exactly the information he'd come for.

I didn't understand that until later. I wished I never had.

* * *

We ran three miles downstream before Hazel let the engine drop to half, and another mile before she brought us into a flooded slough behind a fallen cottonwood and killed the steam entirely. Silence came down on us like a lid. Just the water ticking against the hull, and our own breathing, and somewhere upstream, faint, the sound of the gunboats searching the wrong channel.

Wren was the first one aft. I'll give her this — the girl doesn't freeze. She was at Odile's side before the rest of us got our legs back, tearing the bandage roll open with her teeth.

Odile was propped against the deckhouse, gray-faced, shirt soaked dark from ribs to hip. The bullet had gone through above her left hip, high and ugly but — Bram, breathe, I told myself, breathe — not the belly, not the chest. Through and through. The kind of wound that kills you slow instead of fast, which in river medicine is practically good news.

"Sit still," Wren ordered her.

"I am sitting."

"You're *leaning*. Sit still."

Odile actually smiled at that, a thin white smile, and let the girl bully her flat onto the deck boards while I boiled the needle and Hazel stood over them both with her fists clenched like she wanted to take the wound into her own body and didn't know how.

"You shouldn't have been on that gun," Hazel said finally. It came out rough.

"No," Odile agreed. "You should have. And then who'd have driven?"

"I mean it, Odile."

"So do I." Odile looked up at her, fever-bright and level. "You said nobody touches this boat. I believed you. That's all that happened today. I believed you, and I acted accordingly."

Hazel turned away fast. I pretended to be very busy with the needle.

Wren stitched — steady hands, better than mine, she'd done it before, she wouldn't say where or on whom — and Odile bore it without a sound except one long breath at the worst of it, and afterward we got her below into Bram's bunk, which is to say my bunk, which is to say the least damp bunk, and Wren sat with her while the fever started to build.

* * *

Evening came down purple through the willows. We gathered in what was left of the wheelhouse — glass gone, one wall ventilated, the compass still hanging true on its gimbal like nothing had happened, because brass doesn't care — and Hazel laid it out for us.

"They weren't there for the handoff," she said. "Vane was. The gunboats were Corvus's. They were going to take Wren *and* the Kestrel, and Vane was going to stand there with his palms out and let it happen, and then collect his fee from both sides."

"He watched us fight," I said. "Didn't lift a finger either way."

"He got what he needed," Hazel said. "He knows now that we won't deliver her. That's worth more to Corvus than a clean handoff, because now Corvus knows he's hunting us instead of buying us." She paused. "There'll be a price on us by tomorrow. All of us."

Nobody spoke. Down in the hold, the bilge pump kicked on by itself, and kicked again — running longer than it used to, though I told myself it was my imagination, the way you do.

"So we run downstream," I said. "Lose them in the traffic below the narrows—"

"Downstream is checkpoints," Hazel said. "Every dock, every bridge, every fuel stop. His river, his rules." She looked at each of us in turn, and her voice dropped to something I'd only heard from her once before, the night she talked about her father. "Upstream there's a man who can make Wren's paper true. Tomas Reyes. My father's friend. Dead, according to everybody. If he's alive, he's two days upriver, and the monsoon's four days out, and every hour we go up we're going deeper into the man's teeth."

"Upstream," Odile's voice came faintly through the deckhouse floor, "is also away from my medical options, which are currently a girl with steady hands and whatever's in Bram's kit."

"It's a good kit," I said weakly.

"It's a *small* kit."

Hazel waited. That's the thing about her — she doesn't vote for you, and she doesn't beg. She lays the river out and lets you read it yourself.

Wren came up the ladder. She'd been listening, obviously; the girl listens through bulkheads like water finds cracks. Her face was blotchy from sitting with Odile, and her voice cracked once in the middle, but she said it anyway:

"My grandfather trusted this river with everything he had. Everyone else sold him out." She looked at Hazel. "Take me upstream."

Hazel nodded once, like a woman setting a heading.

I looked at the compass, swinging true and indifferent in the middle of all our ruin, and then at the green-black wall of cloud building far to the south, and I thought about Odile's fever and a dead man who might be alive and a price that would soon be on all our heads.

"Well," I said, because someone had to, and it's traditionally my job, "at least we won't die bored."

Hazel rang the engine telegraph to slow ahead, and the Kestrel turned her scarred nose back upriver, into the teeth, into the dark coming down off the hills — and beneath our feet, patient as sin, the bilge pump kicked on again, and this time none of us pretended not to hear it.
