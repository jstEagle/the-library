## 16. Downstream Is a Lie

The map told one story. The river told another.

I stood at the wheel of the Kestrel with both spread out before me — the paper chart Odile had weighted down with a coffee tin, and the brown water sliding past the hull, carrying us away from High Landing at a speed that felt less like escape and more like surrender. Behind us, the smoke of Corvus's compound still smudged the horizon. Ahead of us, forty miles of checkpoints.

"Downstream," Bram said beside me, arms crossed, watching me watch the water. "You're sure."

"I'm sure it's downhill," I said. "That's not the same as sure it's right."

He grunted, which from Bram was a full paragraph of commentary.

Here was the arithmetic, laid out plain: downstream lay home. Downstream lay the bank, the debt, the week I didn't have. Downstream also lay every dock, weir, and fueling station between here and the delta — and every one of them flew Corvus's colors now, or would by nightfall. A man who owned the river's trade did not need gunboats to catch a stolen heir. He needed only patience and paperwork. We were a hundred and ten feet of rusted steel trying to outrun a ledger.

Upstream lay the Serrano's teeth — the narrows we'd already run once, the rapids that had opened our seams, the gorge country where the river stopped being a road and became an argument. Upstream lay a dead man.

Or so everyone had believed for twenty years.

"Tomas Reyes," I said aloud, testing the name like a bad tooth.

Odile looked up from the chart table, her dark eyes narrowing. She'd been quiet since the handoff went wrong, since she'd taken a graze across her forearm diving behind the cargo crane while Bram and I turned the Kestrel hard enough to crack her own wake against her stern. The wound was bound and clean, but she held that arm close, the way you hold a thing you're afraid of losing.

"You knew him," she said. Not a question. Odile rarely wasted breath on questions when a statement would do.

"My father knew him." I corrected myself the way I always did, the way I'd been doing since I was nine years old. "I knew *of* him. He used to come downriver sometimes, before the disgrace. Big man, loud laugh, always smelled of pipe smoke and engine grease. He and my father would argue about channel markers for hours." Something in my chest pulled tight. "He was the last person my father trusted on this river. And then he vanished the same season everything else did."

"And now Wren says he's alive."

"Wren says a monk at a cliffside mission sends letters signed T.R. Wren says her grandfather laughed about it once, drunk on plum wine, and said the river keeps its dead men warm." I shook my head. "Wren is sixteen and desperate and has been running for three weeks. She believes what she needs to believe. That doesn't make it true."

"It makes it possible," Odile said. "Possible is more than downstream offers."

She had me there, and she knew it, and she let me sit in it without pushing. That was Odile's gift — she could win an argument by refusing to finish it.

* * *

We found Wren aft, sitting on the crate that had been her first hiding place, watching High Landing shrink behind us. She'd scrubbed the compound's soot off her hands but there was still a gray shadow in the creases of her knuckles, like the place had gotten into her blood.

Bram crouched in front of her, which for a man his size was practically folding himself into origami. "You hurt?"

"No."

"You sure? Because your captain will want to know before she decides something stupid, and I'd rather she decide it with facts."

Wren almost smiled. Almost. "I'm sure."

"What happened back there," Bram said, gentle for him, which meant only slightly less blunt than usual. "Corvus's men. They weren't trying to buy you a ticket home."

"They were going to put me on a launch to the delta." Her voice stayed flat, recitation-flat, the way it went when feeling got too big to carry any other way. "There's a clinic there. Private. My grandfather sent people there when they embarrassed the family. They don't come back out. They just send letters in your handwriting asking the council to confirm things." She looked up. "Corvus wouldn't have killed me. He'd have done worse. He'd have made me agree."

The word landed on all of us. *Agree.* I thought of my father's signature on confession papers he'd never read, the ones Vane had waved at the inquiry twenty years ago, and felt the old cold move through my chest.

Bram put one huge hand briefly on top of her head, like blessing a stowaway, and stood up without another word. That was his whole sermon, delivered.

I took the wheel again and pointed us downstream, because downstream was the only direction that made sense until it didn't, and I wanted the miles under my hull while I thought.

* * *

By mid-afternoon the sky had gone the color of old pewter, and the monsoon front was stacking itself up behind us like a debt collector. You could see it from the wheelhouse — a bruise-colored wall sitting on the southern hills, lightning flickering inside it like something turning over in its sleep. Three days, maybe four, before landfall. Less, if the river had opinions.

Odile climbed to the wheelhouse, moving carefully around her bandaged arm, and laid a strip of dried fish on the chart table in front of me without comment. Captain's rations. She did that when she thought I wasn't eating, which was always.

"Talk," she said.

So I talked.

"Downstream, there are four checkpoints between here and the delta. Marshgate, the Two Sisters weir, Stonepile Reach, and the customs pontoon at Low Bend. All of them Corvus's, or rented cheap enough that they might as well be. Every one of them will have our description. Every one of them will have orders to board us." I tapped the chart. "We could maybe bluff past one. Maybe two, if the gods are drunk. Four? With a fugitive aboard and a wounded crew?" I shook my head. "And even if we slip them all, what do we win? We arrive home broke, hunted, and holding a girl whose testimony is worth nothing without a witness to back it. Corvus waits a season, buys the council, and it never happened."

"And upstream?"

"Upstream is the narrows again, with a hull we haven't properly patched. It's the gorge in rising water. It's a mission on a cliff that may or may not exist, run by a man who may or may not be alive, who may or may not remember my father fondly enough to help the daughter of the man whose ruin he witnessed." I laughed, and it came out bitter. "And if Tomas Reyes is up there, Odile, there's a reason he stayed dead for twenty years. Dead men don't get that way by being helpful."

Odile considered this, chewing her fish strip with the deliberation of a woman grading my argument and finding it wanting.

"When I was young," she said, "in the islands, we had a saying. *The easy water drowns the careful swimmer.*"

"That's not comforting."

"It isn't meant to be. It's meant to be true." She leaned on the chart table. "Hazel. You have spent this entire voyage choosing the route that pays. Look where it brought us. Maybe it is time to choose the route that's right."

"The route that's right might kill us."

"The route that pays will definitely own us." She shrugged with her good shoulder. "Between certain chains and possible drowning, I know which I pick. But then, I am not the captain."

She left me alone with the chart and the river and the bruise-colored sky, which was its own kind of mercy.

* * *

Dusk found us tied up in a side slough, hidden under overhanging willows, engines cold, running lights dark. Bram cooked rice and beans on the deck stove with the wind baffled, and the smell filled the boat like an apology. Wren helped, chopping onions with a knife skill that surprised everyone except, apparently, herself.

It was Wren who finally said it, into the steam off the pot.

"If we go downstream, you should leave me at Marshgate."

Everyone stopped moving.

"There's a ferry there," she went on, quick and practical, staring hard at the onions. "Local traffic, hundreds of boats, nobody checks faces. I can take a packet boat east, cross the hills, reach the mission on foot. You take the Kestrel home, pay the bank, tell Corvus's men you threw me off at the first landing. They'll believe it. Nobody believes a riverboat crew would throw away triple rate for a stranger."

"That's the dumbest plan I've ever heard," Bram said, "and I once watched Hazel try to winch the Kestrel off a sandbar using a rowboat and spite."

"It's logical—"

"It's a sixteen-year-old volunteering to walk alone through hill country with syndicate men hunting her, so a bunch of adults can protect their boat." He said it without heat, which somehow made it worse. "No."

"She's right that it's logical," I said, and everyone turned. I kept my voice level. "It's also wrong. Here's why."

I came down to the deck and sat on the rail, where they could all see me, because some things you say standing at a wheel and some things you say eye to eye.

"Twenty years ago, my father ran this river honest, and when Corvus asked him to lie, he said no. And everybody he trusted either testified against him or disappeared, and the river decided he was a cheat, and he died poor and bitter with his name in the mud. I grew up on that story. I built my whole life on the lesson of it, which was: *never bet the boat on doing right, because the river eats people who do.*" I looked at Wren. "Then a girl hides in my cargo hold, and it turns out the whole story was backwards. My father wasn't ruined because he was weak. He was ruined because he was the last honest man on the Serrano, and somebody has to finish telling that story or it stays buried."

"So we go upstream," Wren said quietly.

"We go upstream." I said it and felt something in my chest unclench that had been clenched since Dock Nine. "Not because it's safe. Because downstream is a lie. Downstream is Corvus's river, Corvus's checkpoints, Corvus's ending — we'd just be choosing which of his doors we get caught in. Upstream is harder and it's ours. If Tomas Reyes is alive up there, he's the only man on this river who can stand in front of the council and say *I saw it, I witnessed the papers, I watched Corvus forge them* — and make it stick. Without him, Wren's will is ink. With him, it's a verdict."

"And if he's not alive?" Odile asked. "If the mission is empty, or the letters were a kindness someone invented?"

"Then we'll have lost nothing but time and fuel we were going to lose anyway." I looked around at them — Bram with his arms folded, Odile with her arm held close, Wren with onion on her sleeve and hope she was trying not to show. "But I don't think we lose. I think a man who fakes his own death and spends twenty years writing letters from a cliff is a man waiting for something. Maybe he's been waiting twenty years for a Thornbury to come ask."

Nobody spoke for a moment. Then Bram reached into the pot, ladled out five bowls — counting Wren without discussion, which was its own kind of vote — and handed them around.

"Crew votes upstream," he said. "Motion carries."

* * *

We cast off before dawn, running dark, and turned the Kestrel's bow against the current.

The river noticed immediately. It always does. The current leaned on us like a hand on the chest, and the engine note climbed, and the banks began to slide past slower, stubbornly, mile by mile bought back at cost. Somewhere behind us the storm wall flickered. Somewhere ahead the narrows waited with their white water and their memory of our hull.

I stood at the wheel and felt the old fear and the new hope fighting for the same berth, and for the first time in longer than I could reckon, the hope had the weather gauge.

Wren came up and stood beside me, not touching the wheel, just watching the water the way I'd taught her — reading the seams, the eddy lines, the places where the current lied.

"Which way's the mission?" she asked.

"Two days up the Serrano proper, if the charts are honest. Past the narrows, past the old toll rock, into the gorge country." I glanced at her. "You've never been up there."

"Neither have you. Not since you were small."

"No," I admitted. "My father took me once, before everything. I remember it being beautiful." I paused. "I remember him being happy."

Wren was quiet a moment. Then: "Maybe we'll find both again."

Ahead, the first riffle of the narrows showed white in the pre-dawn gray, and above it, faint as a scar, the silhouette of the gorge walls. I put the wheel over and aimed the Kestrel at the sound of the rapids.

Behind us, downstream, the checkpoints would be waking to a morning without us in it. Let them. By the time Corvus's men figured out which way we'd gone, we intended to be so far upstream that his maps wouldn't reach.

The river rose to meet us, and I drove into it, choosing the harder hope.
