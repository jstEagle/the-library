## 17. Monsoon Warning

The radio started talking at dawn, and it had nothing good to say.

I was on deck with a mug of something Bram called coffee and the river correctly called hot brown water, when Odile's voice drifted up from the wheelhouse — that particular flat tone she uses for bad news, like she's reading someone else's obituary and trying not to take notes for her own.

*"—tropical system Serrano-9 upgraded to monsoon storm classification. Estimated landfall four days. All river traffic advised to seek secure mooring by Thursday evening. Repeat: four days."*

Four days.

I did the math the way I'd been doing it all week, and the math hadn't gotten any friendlier overnight. Four days to run the last stretch of the Serrano, find a half-blind hermit who may or may not want to be found, convince him to testify against the most powerful man on the river, and get back down before the sky fell in. The Kestrel was doing eight knots on a good day with a tailwind and good manners. The storm was doing whatever storms do, which is whatever they want.

"Stop counting," Hazel said from behind me. I hadn't heard her come up. She does that — moves quiet for a woman whose whole life is a loud boat. "Counting doesn't move us faster."

"It makes me feel busy."

"Feeling busy and being busy are cousins who hate each other." She took the mug out of my hands, sipped, made the face everyone makes at Bram's coffee, and handed it back. It was such a small domestic ritual that it caught in my chest sideways. Three weeks ago I was a crate. Now I had a mug and a standing complaint about the coffee and people who noticed when I didn't sleep.

"The sky's wrong," I said, because changing the subject felt safer than saying thank you, which apparently is still a muscle I'm building.

Hazel looked east. I looked too.

She was right, of course, the way she's usually right about water things, which I'm learning to resent slightly less. The dawn should have been gold and gray. Instead there was a band of color low on the horizon that I can only describe as bruised — a deep, sickly green-black, like the river had stood up and become weather. Underneath it the clouds moved in long horizontal shelves, stacked and patient, the way crocodiles lie in shallows.

"That's the leading edge," Hazel said. "Monsoon fronts don't arrive. They *arrive*. Like relatives."

"How much warning do we actually get?"

"When it turns serious? The birds go first. Then the smell." She glanced at me. "You'll know. Everyone always knows, right up until they pretend they didn't."

* * *

Odile's fever announced itself around noon, though she tried to hide it the way she hides everything, which is badly, under competence.

I went below to check on her — we'd been taking shifts sitting with her since the weir, since Bram carried her below with a hole in her side and an expression like she was embarrassed by the whole business. The wound itself was closing. Bram stitched it with the same unhurried hands he uses for rigging, and Hazel had cleaned it with more of our dwindling spirits than any of us mentioned aloud. But the skin around it had gone hot and shiny, red spreading outward in a map of somewhere none of us wanted to go.

"You look terrible," I told her, because lying to Odile is a waste of both our time.

"I look *resting*," she said. Her voice had a loose quality to it, vowels sliding like cargo in rough seas. "There's a difference. In French we have words for this."

"In French you have words for everything. That's why nobody can tell what you're actually saying."

A smile flickered through the fever. "You're learning to fight dirty. Hazel's teaching you well." She patted the blanket beside her, an imperious little gesture, so I sat. Up close I could see the sweat at her temples despite the cool of the cabin, and the way her breathing ran a half-beat fast, like a metronome someone had nudged.

"Tell me about High Landing," she said suddenly.

"Why?"

"Because if I'm going to be useless for two days, I want to be useful first. Talk. Tell me everything you saw from the ridge."

So I talked. About the compound with its cranes and its warehouses, the docks that went on longer than some towns, the flag with the black bird on it hanging limp in the wet air. Corvus's flag. My grandfather's company wearing a stranger's name. Odile listened with her eyes closed, which I thought meant she'd drifted off until she said, quietly:

"He built all that on paper your grandfather signed?"

"On paper my grandfather signed and then tried to unsign. That's the part Corvus can't forgive. Dead men aren't supposed to change their minds."

"Mm." Her eyes opened then, fever-bright and terribly focused. "Wren. Listen to me, because the fever makes sentences expensive and I don't want to spend this one twice." She caught my wrist. Her grip was still strong; that scared me more than the heat. "When we find Tomas Reyes — if he refuses, if he sends us away — you do not let Hazel carry that alone again. She will try. She carries things like it's a job she applied for. You take half."

"I don't know how to carry half of that."

"Nobody knows. You pick it up anyway. That's what family is — people who split loads they don't know how to lift." She released my wrist and settled back, exhausted by her own wisdom. "Now get out. If Bram sees you looking at me like that, he'll start praying, and his prayers are worse than his coffee."

I got out. In the passageway I stood with my hand on the bulkhead and let myself be sixteen for exactly ten seconds — the shaking kind of sixteen, the kind where the adults are hurt and the sky is the wrong color and the arithmetic never comes out even. Then I wiped my face and went up, because ten seconds is the allowance, and I've been spending it carefully.

* * *

By late afternoon the river had changed personality, and rivers changing personality is not like people doing it. People do it slowly, with hints. Rivers just do it.

The current had thickened — that's the only word I have. The Serrano runs brown-gold most of the year, tea-colored and quick. Now it ran darker, heavier, dragging branches and whole islands of foam, and the sound of it against the hull had dropped a register, gone from conversation to argument. Upstream, where the gorge narrowed toward Reyes's mission, I could see rain already falling — not rain as weather but rain as architecture, gray curtains standing between us and the cliffs, moving with slow grandeur.

Bram had the radio on in the wheelhouse, turning the dial through static the way a man searches a crowd for one face.

*"—advisory repeated. Storm Serrano-9 now category three equivalent. Landfall revised: four days, possibly three. River traffic south of Gorge Narrows advised—"*

He shut it off. "Possibly three," he said, to no one, to the boat, to the river.

"Possibly three," Hazel agreed, coming in behind me with charts under her arm. She spread them on the table and weighted the corners with mugs — Bram's coffee mug, my mug, the chipped blue one nobody would claim. Watching her work over those charts, I finally understood something about her that I'd been circling for weeks: Hazel doesn't plan routes. She negotiates with them. Every line she drew came after a pause, a tilt of the head, a small silent argument with the river about what it would tolerate.

"We can't outrun it to the mission and back if it's three days," Bram said.

"We're not running to the mission and back. We're running to the mission, getting Tomas, and running back *ahead of the front*, which is different, because ahead of the front the wind pushes us downstream instead of punching holes in us."

"And if the front is faster than we are?"

Hazel drew the line without looking up. "Then we learn humility at high speed."

I laughed before I could stop myself. Both of them looked at me, and I shrugged. "Sorry. It's just — you say things like that, and I keep forgetting to be terrified, and then I remember, and it's very disorienting."

"Terror's a fuel," Hazel said. "Burns hot, burns fast. Hope's the slow coal. You want both in the box." She rolled the chart. "Get some sleep, Wren. I'll wake you for your watch."

"I'm not tired."

"You're sixteen and you haven't slept properly since the narrows. Go."

"I could help with—"

"*Go.*" But she said it softly, and put a hand briefly on top of my head as I passed, the way you'd steady a compass. I went. I lay in my bunk listening to the hull creak and the river argue and Odile's uneven breathing through the bulkhead, and I thought about what she'd said about family splitting loads, and I thought about my grandfather, dead a year now, who had signed papers and unsigned them and left me holding the difference.

Somewhere upstream was a man who knew the truth. Somewhere downstream was a monster who owned every dock. Somewhere overhead was a storm with our names in it — or no names, maybe, because storms don't bother with names, they just take everything that has one.

I slept anyway. That surprised me most of all.

* * *

The birds left at first light, exactly as promised.

They went all at once, in the gray before dawn — thousands of them lifting off the flooded timber along the banks in one long ragged exhalation, streaming inland and south. No scattering, no circling. Evacuation. I stood at the rail with my elbows on it and watched the sky empty, and the silence they left behind was worse than noise, a held breath across the whole valley.

Then the smell arrived. Green and mineral and ancient, the smell of a world being rinsed. Hazel had been right about that too, and I was starting a private list of things Hazel had been right about, mostly to keep myself from starting a list of things to be afraid of.

"Front's twelve hours faster than the radio says," she said beside me. She'd slept in her clothes; she always does when the pressure drops. "Feel that?" The air had weight to it, a pressing, pre-storm stillness, the kind of quiet that isn't peaceful at all. "Storm's a liar. Radio reads the reports. River reads the storm. River's never wrong."

"So we have how long?"

"To the mission, if we push hard today and tomorrow morning? Doable. Back?" She looked upstream, where the green-black shelf had climbed visibly higher overnight, eating the horizon in bites. "Back is where faith comes in."

"Faith in what?"

"Haven't decided yet. Ask me tomorrow."

We were casting off when Odile's fever turned the corner into honest danger.

Bram found me first — came up the ladder two rungs at a time, which Bram never does, and the speed itself was the message before he opened his mouth. "She's confused. Talking to people who aren't here. Her mother, I think. And the wound—" He stopped, jaw working. "It's gone angry. Red's past the stitching. There's heat coming off her like a stove."

I was already moving. Below, Odile lay twisted in her blankets, flushed scarlet, muttering in rapid French at somebody named Colette who was evidently not sharing the rations fairly. Her eyes when they opened were glassy and landed on me without arriving.

"Little bird," she said, and smiled, and it was the worst thing I'd seen since the weir. "Don't tell Hazel about the fever. She'll turn the boat around."

"The boat isn't turning around," I said. "Nobody's turning anything around."

But when I looked up, Hazel was in the doorway, and she'd heard everything, and her face had gone into that locked-down stillness she wears instead of panic. Our eyes met over the bunk. I watched her do the arithmetic — Odile's heat against the storm's clock, the mission upstream against the checkpoint gauntlet downstream, every number ugly, every option costing.

"We need medicine," Bram said quietly. "Real antibiotics. Not the spirits, not the willow bark. The floating market at Tarn's Reach is half a day downstream."

Downstream. Toward the checkpoints. Toward the price on all our heads.

Hazel closed her eyes for one full second — the longest second I ever saw — and then she turned to the ladder.

"Bram, get us underway. Wren, take the wheel. We're going to market."

The storm stood green and patient on the horizon, four days out, maybe three, and the clock had just started charging interest.
