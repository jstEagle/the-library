## 18. The Medicine Run

There are three kinds of silence a boat can make, and I've heard them all.

There's the good silence — dawn on flat water, everyone asleep, the Kestrel humming along under her own momentum like she's dreaming. There's the bad silence — the one that follows an argument below decks, when you can hear nobody breathing because everybody's pretending to sleep. And then there's the third kind, which is Odile not talking.

Odile not talking is the worst one.

She'd been shot four days ago at High Landing, a through-and-through in the shoulder that should have been a clean wound if the world were fair and rivers ran straight. The world is not fair, and the Serrano runs brown. By the second day her shoulder had gone hot and shiny, and by the third she was burning up and calling for a sister she'd never mentioned in the six years I've known her, and by the fourth morning Hazel stood at the wheel with her jaw set in that particular way and said, "Bram, how far to the Tern Market?"

"Half a day," I said. "If the channel hasn't silted."

"Then we have half a day."

Which is how I came to be standing on the bow of a crippled riverboat, running a gauntlet I couldn't afford to run, toward a floating market full of people I mostly owed money to, holding the last thing my father ever gave me in my pocket.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. Let me start with the fever, because everything starts with Odile.

* * *

The Kestrel's main cabin smelled of lamp oil and sweat and the sharp sweet rot-smell that I'd learned, in another life, meant infection going systemic. Odile lay in the bunk beneath the starboard window with a blanket pulled to her chin despite the heat, shivering, her dark hair plastered to her forehead. Wren sat beside her with a basin of water, wringing out a cloth with the careful concentration of someone who has decided that if she cannot fight the sickness she will at least out-work it.

"How is she?" I asked, though I could see how she was.

"She's asking for you," Wren said. "Has been for an hour. She keeps saying your name wrong."

"That's normal. She does that when she's sober too."

I crouched by the bunk. Odile's eyes opened halfway, found me, and focused with an effort that looked physical, like hauling something heavy up from a deep hold.

"Bram," she said. Her voice was sandpaper. "You still got that ring?"

My hand went to my chest pocket before my brain caught up with the question. "What ring?"

"You know what ring." A ghost of her usual crooked smile crossed her face and then drowned in the shivering. "Compass ring. Your father's. You showed me once, drunk, off Punta Reyes. Said you'd never sell it. Said—" she coughed, wet-sounding, bad-sounding— "said you'd sooner sell your teeth."

"I said that drunk," I told her. "Drunk men say all sorts of things."

"So sell your teeth first." Her eyes closed again. "But Bram. If it comes down to the ring or me. Sell the ring."

"That's a terrible bargain. You snore."

"Bram." She opened her eyes all the way this time, and there was nothing feverish in them at all. That was the thing about Odile, even half-dead: the arithmetic underneath never stopped running. "I know what you're thinking. Don't make it a ceremony. Just do the thing and come back and never tell me what it cost."

* * *

Hazel met me at the wheel afterward. She didn't ask what Odile had said — she'd been within earshot, and besides, Hazel hears everything whether she means to or not. She just looked at me over the top of the wheel spokes, and the river light did its usual work carving lines into her face that made her look ten years older than she was and about forty years more tired.

"The Tern Market's Corvus country now," she said. "You understand that. His flag flies over every pontoon between here and High Landing. We go in, we're walking into his parlor."

"We're not going in. *I'm* going in. One man, buying medicine for a sick friend, no reason for anyone to look twice."

"One man from the boat everyone on this river is talking about." She let that sit. News travels fast on a river; faster than boats, most days. "Bram, they'll have our description. They'll have mine especially."

"Then I'll buy a hat."

Hazel almost smiled. Almost. It was the kind of almost-smile that hurt worse than a frown. "Wren read the charts last night. There's a back channel comes in behind the market pontoons, past the fish racks. Smells like sin itself, but nobody guards what smells like that."

"Romantic."

"I need you back aboard by dusk. Whatever state the shopping's in. If you're not back by dusk, we come in after you, and then we're all dead, and I'll be very cross about it in whatever afterlife we land in."

"Aye, Captain." I hesitated, then said the thing I'd been chewing since Odile's bunk. "Hazel. If it comes to it — if the price is high —"

"Don't." She said it quietly, which was worse than shouting. "Don't offer me things, Bram. I've watched enough people pay prices this week to last a lifetime." She turned back to the wheel. "Dusk."

* * *

The Tern Market announced itself by smell before sight: fish guts and charcoal smoke and the cloying sweetness of overripe fruit baking on corrugated tin. Then the sound came — gulls arguing, generators chugging, a hundred voices haggling in three languages — and finally the sprawl itself, a hundred-odd pontoons and barges lashed together in a loose knot around a sunken dredge that had been there so long it had become furniture. Corvus's black pennant flew from the dredge's mast, snapping in the hot wind, and smaller versions of it flew from maybe one pontoon in five. The rest flew nothing at all. On the Serrano, flying no flag wasn't neutrality. It was just honesty about who really owned the air above you.

I came in through the back channel as instructed, poling a skiff through shallows rank with fish waste, and tied up beneath the drying racks where the stench was thick enough to chew. I bought a hat from a boy mending nets — a wide-brimmed straw thing that made me look, I hoped, like any other downriver laborer with money problems, rather than exactly what I was.

The medicine was easy. That's the joke of it. I found a floating dispensary run by a woman named Amara with silver rings on every finger, paid triple rate without blinking for a course of antibiotics and a bottle of fever tincture, and was done in twenty minutes. Triple rate barely touched the roll of bills Hazel had pressed on me. Vane's money, half-paid up front, blood money sitting in the Kestrel's strongbox. Spending it on saving Odile felt like the only clean thing that money would ever do.

It was coming back that I made my mistake. Coming back, I passed the notice board.

Every floating market has one — a plank wall near the chandlery where folk pin up bills of sale and lost-cat notices and offers of salvage work. I wasn't even going to look. But the board was crowded, and a fresh bill had been nailed up over older ones, the paper still white, and white paper pulls the eye the way a glint of metal pulls a crow.

**BY ORDER OF THE RIVER CHARTER AUTHORITY**, it began, which was Corvus's way of saying *by order of Corvus*, since he owned the Authority the way a man owns his boots.

**INFORMATION LEADING TO THE RECOVERY OF THE PERSON KNOWN AS WREN: FIVE THOUSAND CROWNS.**

Below that, in smaller type:

**THE CAPTAIN AND CREW OF THE VESSEL KESTREL ARE WANTED FOR QUESTIONING REGARDING THEFT AND ASSAULT. REWARD PER HEAD: TWO THOUSAND CROWNS. DELIVER THEM WHOLE. DAMAGED GOODS PAID AT HALF RATE.**

Five heads. Eleven thousand crowns if someone delivered us all tidy. I stood there doing sums I didn't want to do. Two thousand crowns was two years' wages for a deckhand. Five thousand was a life. Every dockhand, every pilot, every hungry kid with a knife on this whole reach of river had just become a bounty hunter, and none of them needed to be brave about it, because we'd have to dock somewhere eventually, and rivers only end in docks.

A man beside me whistled low. "Kestrel," he said to his companion. "That the boat that ran the narrows ahead of Vane's boys last week? Grounded on the Ghost Bar and walked off it?" He shook his head, almost admiring. "Shame. Be easier to catch a boat you didn't respect."

I kept my face bored and my hands loose and walked away at the pace of a man with nowhere urgent to be. Inside, something cold had closed around my throat. Not fear, exactly. Arithmetic. Odile's word for it. The numbers had changed while I'd been buying pills, and they'd changed hard.

* * *

Here is the thing about my father's ring, and I'll tell it quick because telling it slow makes my voice go strange.

He was a lockkeeper on the upper Serrano, before the syndicate bought the locks and threw men like him off their own gates. He wore a brass compass ring — not valuable, not really, a novelty piece with a needle set in a band — but it had belonged to his father, and his father's father, back to some great-grandfather who'd supposedly navigated by it when his ship lost its proper compass in a storm off the Teeth. My father gave it to me the day I signed onto my first crew. He said, "A compass doesn't point north, boy. It points home. North is just where home happens to be standing today." Then he laughed at his own sentimentality and boxed my ears affectionately and that was that.

When he died — and the river took him, same as it takes everyone eventually — I stopped wearing it and started carrying it. Pocket, left breast. Twenty-two years. I'd taken it out exactly three times: once to show Odile drunk, once when a fortune-teller in Punta Reyes offered to read it, and once the night we buried Hazel's father, when I held it so hard the hinge cut a line in my palm that I can still find.

I am not a sentimental man. I want that on the record. I crew for a woman who talks to her boat and I love her like a sister, and I know precisely how much of that is sense and how much is stubbornness. Sentiment is cargo. You carry it, but you don't steer by it.

But standing in the shadow of the fish racks with the notice board's numbers turning over in my head, I took the ring out and held it up against the light, and the little brass needle swung lazily, pointing, as always, wherever it pleased.

"Home happens to be standing today," I said out loud, to nobody. "Today it's lying in a bunk with a fever."

The pawnbroker's barge sat mid-market, a fat green hull with iron bars over its windows and a sign reading FAIR PRICES, HONEST WEIGHTS, which on the Serrano meant neither. The broker was a slab of a woman called Mother Quist with forearms like mooring cable and eyes that had weighed ten thousand objects and found nine thousand wanting.

"Well?" she said, before I'd even finished climbing aboard. "Show me or shove off. I don't sell time."

I put the ring on her counter.

She picked it up with surprising delicacy, tilted it, opened the little cover over the needle. Something moved behind her eyes — recognition, maybe, of the maker's mark inside the band, the old stamp from the compass works at Graywater that closed before I was born.

"This is a Graywater pocket compass," she said. "Ring pattern. Pre-war." She looked up at me, and for a moment the merchant fell away and something almost human looked out. "Where'd you get a Graywater?"

"It's mine. Family piece."

"Mm." She turned it once more, then set it down between us, gently, which I hadn't expected. "Brass like this, working mechanism, provenance unknown — call it eighty crowns. Provenance known and provable — a collector upriver pays three hundred, easy. Maybe four."

Three hundred. Three hundred crowns wouldn't cover Odile's medicine and a tank of fuel and food for five mouths for a week, which was what the run actually required, because the fuel reserve was gone — burned in the night chase through the narrows — and hunger doesn't care about your fevers.

"I need six hundred," I said.

Mother Quist laughed, short and unamused. "Everyone needs six hundred. Six hundred is the national currency of the Serrano." She pushed the ring an inch toward me. "Take it to the collector yourself, up past the weir. He'll give you your six hundred and thank you for it."

"I haven't got time to go past the weir. I've got until dusk."

"Then you've got eighty crowns, or you've got a problem."

We looked at each other across the counter. Somewhere behind us the market roared on, oblivious, eleven thousand crowns' worth of interest in my whereabouts drifting among the crowd like pollen. And I thought about Odile saying *don't make it a ceremony*, and about my father laughing and boxing my ears, and about the fact that a compass points home, and home, today, was a battered sternwheeler with a hole in her hull and a hole in her accounts, crewed by the only people on this river who'd never once asked me what I was running from.

"Two hundred," I said, "and you throw in that coil of signal line behind you and don't ask my name."

Mother Quist studied me a long moment. Whatever she saw — and brokers see more than priests, in my experience — made her sigh.

"Two-fifty," she said. "And the line. And I don't ask your name, though I'll tell you for free that I already know it, because your description's been on the notice board since yesterday noon and you've been standing in my light looking like a man at a funeral." She slid the ring into a drawer, soft as closing a coffin. "For what it's worth, whoever you are — she'd have wanted you to keep it. Whoever she was."

"She'd have called me an idiot," I said. "And then she'd have taken the deal."

"That's women for you." Mother Quist counted out the coins, added the signal line, and jerked her chin toward the gangplank. "Go on, then. And take the back way past the fish racks. There's two men been circling the chandlery for the past hour showing a sketch of a big fellow with a broken nose. Sketch isn't much like you." She smiled without warmth. "But then, sketches rarely are."

* * *

I poled out through the reek of the back channel with my heart going like a paddlewheel and two hundred fifty crowns and a coil of line and a bare ring finger's worth of absence riding in my breast pocket where the ring used to live. It's funny — the pocket didn't feel lighter. It felt heavier, the way an empty room feels heavier than a furnished one.

The Kestrel came around the bend at dusk exactly, Hazel at the wheel, Wren spotting from the bow, and I won't pretend my eyes didn't sting a little at the sight of her, scarred and smoke-stained and ugly as ever. Home happens to be standing today. Today it was standing on brown water, coming for me.

They pulled me aboard and Wren talked a mile a minute about the charts and Odile's fever breaking a quarter-degree — a quarter-degree! — and Hazel watched me with those tired eyes and said, "You're back early. What did it cost?"

"Nothing worth mentioning," I said, which was true, in the sense that I didn't intend to mention it.

I got the antibiotics down Odile's throat myself, propped her up, fed her broth while she complained about the broth. Within an hour the tincture had her sleeping honest sleep instead of fever sleep, and the difference was so vast that Hazel put her hand on my arm and squeezed and didn't say anything, which from Hazel is a eulogy.

It was later, near midnight, my turn at watch, that Wren climbed up to the pilothouse with two cups of tea and handed me one and settled against the rail beside me, and I thought, *here it comes.* Girls who've been hunted their whole lives develop a nose for it.

"You went to the notice board," she said quietly. Not a question.

"How'd you know?"

"Because you came back different. Everyone comes back from that board different." She stared out at the black water sliding past. "Eleven thousand crowns, right? I did the sums too. I'm the expensive one." Her voice wobbled and steadied. "Bram — Hazel won't say it, and Odile's asleep, and somebody has to. For two thousand crowns you could stop. All of you. Hand me over at the next landing, collect the reward, clear the debt on the boat, start over. Nobody would blame—"

"Wren." I said it gently, but I said it firm. "Do you know what my father told me about compasses?"

She blinked at the non sequitur. "What?"

"He said they don't point north. They point home." I turned my empty pocket outward, absurdly, patted it flat. "Spent twenty-two years thinking that meant a ring. Turns out it doesn't. Turns out it's a boat."

Wren looked at me for a long moment, and then, being sixteen and made entirely of nerve, she leaned her head briefly against my shoulder — brief as a bird's touch-down — and straightened and pretended it hadn't happened, and I pretended too, and that was the whole ceremony, which was exactly as much ceremony as anyone wanted.

It might have ended there, warm and quiet. But rivers don't do endings.

Because it was an hour later, alone at the wheel with the market's glow dying astern, that I saw the lights. Three of them, spread wide across the downstream reach, running without lanterns screened — running dark, which no honest trader does — and holding a course that would bring them neatly across our path at dawn, at the mouth of the only channel wide enough for the Kestrel to run.

Someone knew where we'd come out. Someone had been at the Tern Market too tonight, and hadn't been shopping for medicine.

I rang the bell soft, twice — Hazel's signal, *captain to the wheel* — and watched the dark lights close, and did the arithmetic one more time.

The numbers had changed again.
