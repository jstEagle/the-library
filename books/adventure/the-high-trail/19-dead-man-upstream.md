## 19. Dead Man Upstream

The mission hung off the cliff face like a swallow's nest somebody had built out of spite.

We'd been poling up the side channel since dawn — Bram at the bow with the boat hook, Odile below decks wrapped in a blanket and refusing to admit she was shivering, Wren at the chart with her finger pinned to a smudge of ink that was supposed to be San Rafael de las Barrancas. The charts called it a waypoint. The charts did not mention that you had to climb to it.

"There," Wren said, and pointed with her chin, because her hands were busy keeping us off a submerged fence line that the flood had turned into a harp.

I looked where she pointed and felt something in my chest do a thing I didn't have a name for. Twenty years I'd carried my father's disgrace like ballast — always there, always shifting in rough water, always explaining why we never went above the confluence. And now here was the place he'd last stood, and it looked exactly like something that would ruin a man: beautiful, indifferent, and impossibly far from help.

The Kestrel slid into the flooded orchard below the cliff. Bram made fast to what had once been an orange tree and was now mostly suggestion. The water was thigh-deep in what had been the mission's lower garden; a stone stair climbed out of it, each step furred with moss, going up and up into the green dark under the overhang until it reached a door that might once have been painted blue.

"Charming," Odile said from the deck. She'd come up despite herself, blanket around her shoulders like a general's cloak. Her color was wrong. She'd deny that too.

"You stay aboard," I told her.

"I'm coming."

"Odile—"

"Hazel." She smiled, which she does when she's about to be unreasonable. "If there's a man up there who can fix papers, there's a man up there who can look at a wound. Two birds. One very steep staircase."

Bram grunted, which is his way of voting. Wren was already on the stair.

* * *

Climbing to San Rafael is the kind of errand that rearranges your opinion of stairs. There were more than I counted, because after sixty I stopped counting on principle. The jungle had moved into the ruins the way a squatter moves into a house whose owner has died — politely at first, then completely. Orchids grew out of a bell tower. A crucifix leaned at an angle that suggested even it had given up on the view.

The door at the top was open. That should have warned me. Men who want to be found leave doors shut and make you knock. Men who don't care anymore leave them open to the rain.

Inside was a nave gone soft — roof half fallen, ferns where pews used to be, an altar with a hurricane lamp and a typewriter on it, which is a sentence I never expected to compose. And a man sitting in the light from the broken ceiling, reading a large-print Bible by touch as much as sight, because his eyes had gone the color of milk gone slightly off.

He heard us before he saw us, which is the only way he saw anything now.

"If you've come about the roof," he said, "the answer is still no. God provides. God has provided me forty years of leaks and I've learned to live with them."

"Tomas Reyes?" I said.

The Bible came down. The milky eyes swung toward my voice and stopped just left of my face, the way they probably always did now, aimed at a version of me standing slightly to one side.

"No," he said.

"We've come a long way—"

"I know who you are." He set the Bible down with exaggerated care, the way men handle things when their hands want to shake. "You think I can't tell? Thornbury. You have his voice. Same river-bottom vowels. He used to stand right about where you're standing and talk about current like it was scripture." His mouth did something that wasn't a smile. "Get out of my church."

"It's hardly a church anymore—"

"Then get out of my ruin."

Wren stepped forward. "Mr. Reyes, my grandfather—"

"I know whose granddaughter you are, too. You have *his* jaw." He laughed, short and ugly. "Everyone on this river has somebody's jaw. It's how the river keeps its accounts." Then he waved a hand at us, all of us, like we were smoke. "Whatever you've rowed all this way to ask, the answer is no. I'm dead. Have been for twenty years. Dead men don't testify."

* * *

Here's what I knew about Tomas Reyes, going up those stairs: that he'd been my father's best friend, that he'd been the charter witness on the Serrano run, and that he'd vanished the same season my father came home ruined. The river's story was that both of them had been crooked together and Reyes had simply been better at disappearing.

Here's what I learned in the next hour, sitting on a fallen pew while a blind old man refused to look at me: the river's stories are usually written by whoever owns the docks.

He wouldn't help us. He said so eleven times. I counted, because counting kept me from shouting, and shouting at a blind man in a ruin felt like a new low even for me. But between the eleventh no and the time Bram went out to check on the boat, he talked. Old men alone on cliffs will talk. It's the loneliness tax, and I paid it by listening.

"He came to me," Reyes said, apropos of nothing, apropos of everything, somewhere around the twelfth cup of chicory coffee that tasted like it had been brewed during the previous administration. "Your father. Nineteen years ago this month. Came up here soaked to the bone, wouldn't sit down, wouldn't eat. Said Corvus had offered him triple rate to run sealed crates past the survey stations and sign clean papers on the manifests. Falsified tonnage. Falsified origin. Enough to give Corvus clear title to half the upper river's trade."

My stomach did something slow and cold. "And my father took the job."

Reyes's head came around, and for a moment the milky eyes seemed to find me anyway.

"*Took* it?" He made a sound halfway between a laugh and a cough. "Child, he refused it. Loudly. In writing. He wrote to the council — I watched him seal the letter — naming Corvus, naming the crates, naming the survey men already bought. He asked me to witness the refusal. I signed it." His hands flattened on the pew. "And then I got scared."

The rain started again on the broken roof, soft as static.

"Corvus sent a man. Not thugs — worse. A polite man with a ledger. Sat right where you're sitting and read me names. My sister's family in Puerto Cielo. My old crew. Every debt I owed, every favor owed *to* me, priced out like cargo." Reyes's voice went flat, reciting. "Then he said the word 'fire' in a way I still hear at night. And I withdrew my signature. I told the council my testimony had been mistaken. I let them believe your father had acted alone. I let them believe he was the corrupt one, because the corrupt one was the story Corvus had already bought and paid for."

The rain got louder. Or maybe everything else got quieter.

"They took his boats," Reyes said. "They took his name. Your father spent twenty years being the cautionary tale they tell young captains — *don't end up like Thornbury.* And every year on the anniversary I climb down to the chapel and I say his name out loud to nobody, because it's the only penance I could afford." He turned toward me, or toward the space beside me. "So no. I won't help a Thornbury. A Thornbury is what my cowardice built. Looking at you is looking at the bill."

I want to tell you I said something magnificent. I didn't. I sat there with wet boots and a dry throat and understood, all at once, that I had hated the wrong dead man for twenty years. That the pride I'd worn thin as paper — the skill, the stubbornness, the refusal to beg — hadn't been defiance at all. It had been inheritance. He'd refused to falsify papers because falsifying papers was wrong, and the river had punished him for it, and I'd grown up ashamed of the single honest thing he ever did.

"I'm not asking you to forgive yourself," I finally said. "I'm asking you to read a will."

"And hand Corvus another Thornbury to break?" Reyes pushed himself up from the pew, joints cracking like river ice. He was taller than I expected, or had been once. "No. Take your girl and your debt and your brave little boat downstream, Captain, and forget you found me. Some men stay dead because staying dead is the last useful thing they can do."

* * *

Bram met us partway down the stairs, coming up two at a time with his face doing the thing it does instead of panicking.

"Skiff," he said. "Two men. Grey hull, no markings. They anchored at the mouth of the channel twenty minutes back and they're just — sitting."

"Sitting?"

"Watching the stairs." He glanced past me up at the ruin. "Somebody told them where to look."

Vane. Of course Vane. Vane always knew where we were the way weather knows where you are.

I looked back up through the ferns at the broken roofline, at the old man standing in it with his lamp and his typewriter and his twenty years of saying my father's name to nobody. Behind him, propped against the altar, I caught it — a spine of oilcloth-wrapped canvas, fat as a Bible, tied with tarred twine. A ship's logbook. I'd grown up around logbooks. I knew one by its silhouette the way you know a parent's coat on a hook.

Reyes saw me see it. His hand moved — not quite covering it, but near.

"Captain," he said quietly, "go back to your boat."

"Is that his?" I asked. "Is that his logbook?"

The rain filled the pause. Somewhere below us, the grey skiff sat at the mouth of the channel, patient as a debt.

Reyes's jaw worked. The milky eyes turned toward the wrapped canvas as if he could still read it, and something in his face — bitterness cracking along an old fault line — finally gave.

"Come inside," he said. "Both of you. And send your big friend to watch the water." He turned toward the altar, reaching for the tarred twine with fingers that shook, and his voice dropped to almost nothing. "Because if Corvus's people are already at the channel mouth, then it's time you heard the rest of what your father wrote. And it's worse than you think — and better than you deserve."
