## 20. Her Father's Logbook

Tomas Reyes did not invite us inside. He stood in the doorway of the cliffside mission with his half-blind eyes aimed somewhere over my left shoulder, as if the view of the flooded valley were more interesting than the two soaked, desperate people on his step, and he said, "You have her jaw. Did you know that? The Thornbury jaw. Like a hinge on a trap."

"Thank you," I said, because I couldn't think of anything else to say to a man who had just compared my face to a piece of hardware.

"It wasn't a compliment." But he stepped back anyway, and the door swung wider, and that was how I came to stand for the first time in twenty years in the house my father had supposedly died trying to reach.

The mission was smaller than I'd imagined. In my head it had always been a cathedral — the place where the truth about my father lived, vast and echoing. In reality it was two rooms and a chapel that smelled of lamp oil and dried peppers, with a table scarred by forty years of knives and a window that looked out on nothing but river mist. A crucifix on the wall. A stack of charts gone soft with damp. And on a shelf above the hearth, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with twine that had been retied more than once, a shape I recognized before I understood it.

I'd know that shape anywhere. I'd grown up watching him wrap it every night before bed, like a man putting a child to sleep.

"That's a logbook," Bram said behind me. He'd come up the cliff path with me because Odile's fever had us both spooked and neither of us wanted to be the one sitting idle, and now his voice had gone careful, the way it did when he sensed the ground might give.

"It's *the* logbook," I said.

Tomas lowered himself into a chair by the hearth with the slow arithmetic of bad knees. "Sit down, Hazel Thornbury. Both of you. What I have to show you is not a thing you want to receive standing up."

"I don't want to receive it at all," I said. "I want antibiotics, a guide downriver, and a witness who shows up when he's called. The sentimental tour of my father's failures wasn't on the manifest."

Something moved in his ruined face — not quite a flinch. More like a man hearing an old song and hating that he knows all the words. "Your father didn't fail," he said. "Sit down."

I sat. Bram sat. Outside, the monsoon front grumbled along the ridge like a dog denied dinner, and the mist pressed against the window, and Tomas Reyes reached up with both hands — they shook, but not much — and took the oilcloth bundle down from the shelf.

* * *

"You have to understand what the Serrano was like, back then," he said, untying the twine with a patience that made me want to scream. "Before Corvus. There were forty charter boats on this river. Forty captains, forty families, and every one of them honest or honest enough. Your father — Marcus Thornbury — he wasn't the biggest. He wasn't the fastest. But he was the one the others came to when there was a dispute. You knew that?"

"I knew he was a sandbar pilot," I said. "I knew he could read water like a priest reads scripture. That's about all the eulogy I got."

"That's because the eulogy was written by the men who ruined him." The twine came free. He folded the oilcloth back with the same unbearable gentleness. "Corvus came upriver twenty-two years ago with money and lawyers and a smile you could grease an axle with. He didn't want to buy boats. He wanted to buy the *charters* — the council grants that say who may run what water. And most men, when the offer got big enough, they signed. Signed away their route, took a berth in Corvus's fleet, told themselves they'd done right by their families. And the ones who wouldn't sign—" He paused. "The ones who wouldn't sign needed to be made an example of."

The logbook came out of the oilcloth and into the lamplight, and my chest did something complicated and painful.

It was my father's. Of course it was. The cover was the same oxblood leather, worn to suede at the corners, with the brass corners he'd salvaged off a wreck when I was six and swore came from a Spanish galleon. The spine was cracked in the same place it had always cracked. Someone had written across the flyleaf in pencil, in a hand that wasn't his: *Evidence. Keep. — T.R.*

"He gave it to you," I said. My voice came out strange, flat, like I was reading someone else's chart.

"The night before his last hearing." Tomas set it on the table between us and did not push it toward me, and I understood that this too was deliberate — that he wanted me to come to it, not have it handed over like a settlement. "He came upriver one last time, in secret, in a fog so thick you couldn't see the bow from the wheel. He'd heard I was still free and still angry, and he brought me this, and he said, *Tomas, if they take my name, at least the book stays true.* Those were his words. *The book stays true.*"

"What's in it?" Bram asked quietly. He's a big man, Bram, with a face like a dock cleat, and I have seen him stare down armed men in skiffs without blinking. But he was looking at that logbook like it was a sleeping animal that might wake up angry.

"Pages," Tomas said. "Months of pages. And at the back—" he opened it to the rear, to an envelope of heavy paper gone yellow, "—the charter papers Corvus asked him to falsify."

I looked. I want to be able to say I was braced for it, that twenty years of shame had armored me against anything a dead man's notebook could do to me. I was not braced. There is no bracing for your father's handwriting.

The papers were drafts — charter surveys, water-depth certifications, the kind of documents a respected pilot signs and a council trusts. And in the margins, in that slanted, impatient hand I'd spent my childhood imitating, my father had written his refusals. Not once. Over and over, like a man arguing with a ghost. *Refused. He can find another fool.* And then, later, the handwriting tighter, angrier: *Offered triple rate to certify the narrows at false depth. Refused. He says my name will not survive the season. Let him try.*

And at the very bottom of the last page, dated four days before the hearing that ended him:

*Corvus came aboard again. Says if I won't falsify, he'll see to it the falsifying is done in my name anyway. Says no one will believe a disgraced pilot over a respectable businessman. I believe him. God forgive me, I believe him. But I will not sign. Wren's grandmother used to say the river keeps honest accounts. So do I. Let the book stay true.*

The room went very quiet. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below the cliff, the river moved in the dark, patient as arithmetic.

I should say here, for the record, that I have not wept since I was eleven years old and they took the Kestrel's wheel out of my hands at my father's disgrace hearing, and a man in a good coat told me my father was a forger, and I believed him because grown men in good coats don't lie about such things. Twenty years I carried it. Twenty years I built a whole self out of that shame the way you build a levee — mud and sand and stubbornness, one layer at a time. I ran the Kestrel into debt because I would not ask the council for anything. I refused Vane's first honest offer because honest men had testified against my father and I trusted no one's honesty. Every hard, stupid, proud decision of my adult life had that hearing at the bottom of it, like a rock under a sandbar that you don't see until your hull is on it.

And the whole time — the whole *time* — he had refused. He had stood in front of the richest man on the river and said no, knowing what it would cost, and paid it, and let the world call him the liar rather than sign his name to a lie.

"He didn't disgrace us," I said. It came out as a whisper, which is not like me. "He—"

"He refused," Tomas said. "That's all. That's everything. He refused, and Corvus made good on his threat, and the falsified papers appeared with your father's certification on them, and the hearing was a theater with the ending written in advance. Vane testified — you know that part. He was a young man with debts of his own and Corvus holding the paper on every one of them. I've had twenty years to learn how that machine works, and I'll tell you the machine's secret, Hazel Thornbury: it doesn't need many liars. It needs one honest man to ruin, publicly, so the rest stop believing refusal is possible."

Bram put his hand on my shoulder. He didn't say anything. There's a reason I keep him.

I cried. I'll admit it here because I denied it at the time — sat there at that scarred table with my father's logbook open in front of me and wept like a girl, ugly and loud and without one shred of the dignity I usually keep lashed to the mast. I cried for the man in the good coat. I cried for every year I'd been ashamed of the best man I ever knew. I cried because the last page of his book was braver than anything I had ever done in my life, and I had spent two decades being proud of a bitterness he would have been ashamed of.

When it was over — and it took a while, and nobody pretended not to notice, which was its own kindness — Tomas pushed the logbook across the table.

"Take it," he said. "It was always meant to be yours. He said so."

* * *

Later — much later, when the lamp had burned down and Bram had gone out to check the moorings on the skiff and pretend he hadn't heard any of it — Tomas and I sat by the dead hearth and he told me the rest.

"Why didn't you come forward?" I asked. "Twenty years, Tomas. One word from you at any of the charter renewals and—"

"And what?" His laugh had no humor in it, just rust. "One half-blind hermit against Corvus? He owns every dock between here and the delta. He owns the *council's* debts, though they'd gut me for saying it. I stayed silent because silence was the only weapon they hadn't figured out how to confiscate." He looked at the window, at the mist. "And because I was afraid. Write that in your book, captain. The witness was afraid. Twenty years of it."

"And now?"

"And now a girl comes up my cliff in a storm, sixteen years old, with Corvus's whole machine behind her and nothing in her hands but a will and a stubbornness I recognize." He turned those clouded eyes on me. "She reminds me of your father. Did you know that? Not the jaw. The *refusal*."

I looked down at the logbook in my lap. My father's handwriting. My father's courage, sitting there in oxblood leather like it had been waiting for me all along, patient as the river.

"Tomas," I said, "the council convenes at Council Rock in nine days. Corvus will be there with his lawyers and his charters and his smile. I have a wounded crew, a leaking boat, a monsoon coming, and a witness who just told me he's a coward."

"Yes," he said.

"I'm asking you to be brave anyway. One more time. For him."

The old man was quiet for a long moment. Then he reached for his stick, and began, slowly, painfully, to stand.

"Get me my coat," he said. "And captain — tell your crew to make ready. Because if I'm going to die on this river after twenty years of hiding from it, I intend to do it loudly."

Outside, the first fat drops of the monsoon began to strike the mission roof, one by one, like a drum counting down.
