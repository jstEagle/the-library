## 21. Tomas Decides

I have spent twenty years learning how to be still.

That is not a skill people respect. They respect the man who runs, the man who fights, the man who dies loudly with his name in somebody's mouth. Nobody respects the man who sits by a window for two decades watching the Serrano rise and fall and rise again, keeping his hands busy with candle wax and carpentry and the small domestic machinery of staying alive, while everything he failed to say goes on rotting inside him like a tooth he refuses to have pulled.

But I can tell you this: stillness teaches you things running never will. Running only teaches you where you're going. Stillness teaches you what you left behind.

The girl came up my cliff path at dusk, which told me something before she ever opened her mouth. The mission steps are four hundred of them, cut into rock by monks who had more faith than engineering sense, and no one climbs them at dusk unless they want to arrive unseen or unseeable. She moved like a cat that has learned to distrust furniture — quick through the open patches, patient in the shadows, pausing at each landing to listen the way other people breathe.

Behind her, lower down, a woman was climbing too. Slower. Older. The way she took the steps — testing each one, reading the rock like a page she'd read a hundred times — I knew who she was before I could see her face.

Thornbury.

I put down the candle I was trimming and sat very still, because that is what I do now, and I listened to twenty years of silence begin, at last, to make noise.

* * *

You should understand what kind of coward I am, because there are kinds, and mine is the respectable one. Mine is the cowardice of a careful man. I was never a brave man gone wrong; I was a prudent man gone rotten, and prudence rots slower than vice but just as thoroughly.

Twenty-two years ago I was a charter clerk at High Landing. Notary, surveyor, keeper of seals — the sort of man whose signature makes paper into law. Corvus found me when I was young enough to be flattered that a man of his weight knew my name. He didn't threaten me first. That's the detail nobody believes. He *promoted* me first. Gave me a better office, a better view, a daughter's school fees paid without asking. And then one day he set a falsified charter on my desk — tonnage numbers invented out of whole cloth, river rights transferred from families who'd held them for generations — and he asked me to seal it, and he smiled, and I understood that the smile and the school fees were the same document.

There were others who said no. I watched what happened to them. Aldous Reyes — no relation, though everyone assumed, and I let them assume because it made me feel adjacent to his honesty — Aldous ran honest cargo up the Serrano and refused to carry Corvus's ghost-paper, and within a season he was ruined, disgraced, driven off the river with his name dragged behind him like an anchor. I signed the papers that did it. Some of them. My hand shook, but it signed.

And then there was the witness matter. A hearing, years later — a dispute over the upper charters, and a captain named Thornbury standing accused of running the Serrano against the ban. I was in the hall. I had documents in my satchel that would have shown the ban itself was Corvus's forgery, that the whole prohibition was built to strangle independent boatmen so their routes could be bought cheap from their corpses. I stood up twice to speak. Twice I sat back down. The second time, I caught Corvus's eye across the hall, and he didn't glare at me. He nodded. Just slightly. Like a man acknowledging good service.

I told myself I would speak another day. That is the great lie cowards tell themselves: *another day.* Another day became another year became twenty of them, and somewhere in the middle of all those days I left High Landing, bought this ruin of a mission with money I couldn't spend honestly, and began my long apprenticeship in stillness. Half-blind now. The cataracts came on slow, like everything else about my decay — first a fog over the far shore, then the near one, until the world became mostly memory and candlelight, which suits me. Memory and candlelight are the two things I've kept polished.

The bell rope needs mending. The roof over the chapel leaks. These are the problems I permit myself, because they are solvable, and because every solved problem is a small argument that I am still a useful man. It is a weak argument. I have heard better ones from worse men.

* * *

Hazel Thornbury climbed my stairs at full dark, and when she stepped into the chapel light I saw Aldous in her face so plainly that my chest hurt. The same jaw. The same stubborn set of the shoulders, as if the body itself had been built braced against bad news. But her eyes were harder than his ever were. His eyes had been hard the way river ice is hard — load-bearing, but you always knew water underneath. Hers were hard the way old varnish is hard. Something had dried in her that shouldn't have.

"You're supposed to be dead," she said. No greeting. Thornburys don't waste words on weather.

"Corvus let the story spread," I said. "It suited him. A dead witness bothers no one." I gestured at the bench across from me. "Sit, Captain. You've climbed four hundred steps and your knees are filing complaints."

She didn't sit. She stood there with rain coming off her oilskin in beads, and behind her in the doorway the girl appeared — Wren, they called her, though I'd learn later she had another name, a grandfather's name, a name worth killing for — and the girl looked at me with an expression I recognized from mirrors. Hope held at arm's length so it can't bite you.

"I know why you're here," I said. "The answer is no."

"You haven't heard the question."

"I wrote the question," I said. "Twenty-two years ago. In triplicate."

That got a flicker out of her — not a smile, nothing so generous, but the corner of her mouth registering that I was going to be difficult rather than merely useless. She finally sat, perched forward on the bench like a woman ready to leave at speed. The girl stayed standing. Smart girl. Standing people leave faster than sitting people.

"Hazel—" the girl started.

"Not yet," Hazel said, quietly, and the girl shut her mouth, and I noted that too. Not obedience. Agreement. There's a difference, and you can hear it if you've spent twenty years listening to silence.

They laid it out for me. The will — old Amara's will — naming the granddaughter over the syndicate. The witnesses required to validate it under council law: two living signatories to the original charter registry, testimony given publicly at Council Rock. One signatory was dead. The other, according to every record on the river, was me.

"I'm not a signatory anymore," I said. "I'm a dead man. Dead men don't testify. It's one of the benefits."

"The council doesn't care if you're dead," Hazel said. "They care if you're *real.*"

"And if I stand up in front of that hall and open my mouth, Corvus will make me real in the most permanent sense available. Captain, I have seen his method. I was his method, once. Do you know what happens to men who cross him?"

"Yes," she said. "My father."

The word landed between us like dropped cargo. I looked at her and she looked at me and neither of us flinched, which cost us both.

"Aldous Thornbury refused him," I said. "Refused to run the ghost-paper, refused to look away, refused everything. And I sealed the instruments of his ruin and then I sat in a hearing hall with proof of the fraud in my satchel and said nothing. So yes, Captain. I know exactly what happens. I've had twenty years to study the results."

"Then you know the interest is due," she said.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about guilt: it does not fade. It *ferments.* Fresh guilt is sharp and survivable; you can pour it off, confess it, weep it out, work it into penance. Old guilt is different. Old guilt has turned sweet and heavy and slightly poisonous, and it sits in you like wine you're afraid to drink and more afraid to pour away, because pouring it away would mean admitting it was never going to get better.

For twenty years I have performed my little penances. Candles for Aldous Thornbury, lit every anniversary of his disgrace. Repairs done free for boatmen passing upriver, though few pass here now. I taught myself to believe that small kindnesses could amortize a large betrayal, the way you might try to pay off a debt of a thousand crowns at a copper a month. The arithmetic never worked. I knew it didn't work. I did it anyway, because the alternative was to walk down those four hundred steps, board a boat, and go be afraid in public.

Fear, at my age, is a physical thing. People imagine it's a thought. It isn't. It lives in the hands. For two decades my hands have known exactly what they're for — wax, wood, wicks, the small safe economies of a hidden life. To testify is to hand those hands over to other purposes. To hold papers in front of a hostile hall. To point, in front of men who own every dock and most of the law, at the face of the person who paid for my comfort. My hands have forgotten how to be brave. I doubt they ever knew.

But the girl.

The girl sat down eventually, when Hazel went to the window to watch the storm building over the gorge — green-black sky, the monsoon announcing itself like a creditor — and the girl sat across from me and studied my candle rack with a craftsman's squint, and then she said, "Your tapers are poured crooked. Left side heavier. You tilt the mold when your shoulder tires."

"They've served," I said.

"My grandfather poured his own," she said. "Same flaw. Same shoulder." And then, before I could decide whether to be wounded or charmed: "He kept a letter you sent him. Thirty years ago. When he was still fighting the registry consolidations. You warned him about a clause nobody else caught. He said you were the only honest clerk in High Landing, and then he said — this I remember, because I asked him what it meant — he said, *'and they'll grind that boy down to nothing, because honest clerks are expensive.'*"

I had no answer. There is a particular shame in being remembered well by the dead; it is a loan taken out against a version of yourself that no longer exists, and the collector has come.

"He died believing the river would be handed to his granddaughter," Wren said. "Not sold to the man who buried him in charters. I'm not asking you to be brave, señor Reyes. I've met brave. Brave gets shot at the ford." She leaned forward. "I'm asking you to be the thing you already were, thirty years ago, for one afternoon. One afternoon of being the only honest clerk in High Landing. Then you can come back here and pour your crooked candles for the rest of your life, and no one — not me, not her, not God — will ask you for anything again."

Outside, thunder walked along the gorge like something enormous changing its mind.

* * *

I did not sleep. I sat in the chapel with the storm worrying the roof and I took inventory, which is what clerks do, and the ledger looked like this:

Against going: fear, obviously — but fear is a beggar at the door, always there, entitled to nothing. Age. Eyes half-gone. A body that climbs four hundred steps slowly and would climb them faster pursued. The comfortable lie of the dead man, which protects me the way a coffin protects its occupant: thoroughly, and at the price of never again seeing the sun.

For going: a name. Not my name — I've stopped pretending my name matters to anyone but me. *His* name. Aldous Thornbury, ground down to nothing by my signature and my silence, and his daughter sitting in my chapel with her father's jaw set against me, offering nothing, promising nothing, because she is too proud to beg and too honest to pretend this is anything but what it is: a debt collection, twenty years overdue, with interest compounding.

And the girl. The girl with her grandfather's crooked-candle eyes, carrying a dead man's hope up four hundred steps at dusk because some instinct told her the coward at the top might still be the honest clerk underneath the sediment.

I thought about the hearing hall, twenty-two years ago. The satchel on my knee. The documents inside it like coals. I stood up. I sat down. And I have stood up and sat down every single day since, in the theater of my skull, replaying the moment, editing my courage in, watching the alternate life unspool — the one where I speak, where Aldous keeps his boat, where perhaps his daughter grows up with a father instead of a grievance. Twenty years of rehearsal. Every night, the same play. Never once performed.

A man can die of rehearsal. I have watched myself nearly manage it.

At dawn the rain slackened and I heard Hazel on the gallery, talking low with someone below — the big crewman, presumably, and the woman Odile, whose fever I'd been told about, whose wound spoke of what Corvus's men had already cost these people. Costs accrue around truth like silt around a piling. That is the nature of the river and the nature of the law both: nothing stands clean in moving water.

I trimmed a last candle — straight, for once; my shoulder was tired but my hands were steady, which tells you where steadiness actually comes from — and I carried it into the room where they'd bedded down, and I set it on the table between us, and I woke the captain with the flat of my palm on the floorboards, three knocks, the old charter-house signal for *witness present.*

She came up off the bedding with a knife already in her fist, which I respected enormously.

"Terms," I said.

Her eyes narrowed. Good. Distrust is the correct posture toward a man like me, and I wanted to see her use it.

"I will come down this mountain," I said, "and I will stand in front of that council, and I will say what I sealed and what I saw and what Corvus built his empire out of, and I will name Aldous Thornbury's honor into the record where it belongs. But you will give me your oath — yours, and the girl's, and your crew's — on one condition, and the condition is not negotiable, and if you haggle I will know you for Vane's people after all."

"Name it."

"The will gets read *publicly.* Not filed. Not entered quietly into evidence where a clerk can lose it and a syndicate lawyer can bury it in procedure. Read aloud. At Council Rock, in the open hall, before the assembled charter-holders and boatmen and every soul Corvus owns on that waterfront. Let them hear old Amara's voice come out of that paper. Because I have watched documents die in the dark, Captain. I killed several myself. Paper only survives in daylight, sworn aloud, witnessed by people who cannot unknow what they've heard. That is the price. Swear it."

Hazel looked at me for a long moment. Behind her, Wren had come awake and silent at the sound of voices, and I felt the girl's attention settle on me like a hand on the shoulder.

"On behalf of my crew," Hazel said, "which is not mine to swear for, but which I'll answer for — yes. Read aloud, in the open hall, or we haven't come down the mountain at all. You have my word."

"Then pack your gear," I said, "because I'm coming with you, and I climb steps slowly, and the council convenes soon, and I intend to be insufferably punctual for the first time in twenty years."

Wren laughed — a short, startled sound, like a bird flushing — and then pressed her knuckles to her mouth as if laughter were contraband. Hazel didn't laugh. She just looked at me the way you look at a sandbar that has shifted overnight: reassessing the channel, recalculating the draft.

"Get your coat, then," she said. "And Reyes — the man who sent my family that letter, thirty years ago. I'd like to meet him someday. Try to be him by the time we reach the water."

"I'll do my best," I said. "Though I warn you — he was younger, and considerably more foolish. Foolishness reads as honesty at a distance."

* * *

We came down the four hundred steps in grey morning light, the storm washed out but the sky still bruised, the river below running high and brown and fast with the monsoon's first offerings. I counted the steps as I descended, because clerks count things, and I thought: twenty years ago I climbed these steps to disappear, and today I descend them to reappear, and the difference between the two journeys is roughly the width of one spoken oath.

My legs shook. My hands did not.

Halfway down, Hazel stopped at a landing and looked back upriver, toward the gorge, and her whole body changed — a tightening, a stillness, the stillness of a woman reading water. I followed her gaze and saw it: far off, a smudge of movement on the brown current, a launch running light and fast against the flood, riding a river that sensible boats had fled.

"That's not traffic," Hazel said, mostly to herself. Then, louder, to the big man waiting below: "Bram. Someone's coming upriver fast, and they're either fools or they know exactly what they're doing."

"Fools or friends?" the big man rumbled.

"There's no such thing as friendly at that speed," Hazel said, and took the rest of the steps two at a time.
