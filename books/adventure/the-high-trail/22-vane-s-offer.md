## 22. Vane's Offer

He was waiting for us at the bend below Widow's Reach, which told me two things: Silas Vane had a fast launch and a better memory of the river than I'd ever given him credit for.

We'd left the mission at first light with Tomas Reyes tucked into the wheelhouse like a piece of cargo nobody wanted to claim — half-blind, bitter, and muttering about Thornburys in a tone that suggested he'd been rehearsing his grievances for twenty years and had no intention of wasting them on an audience of fewer than five. Bram had him settled on the bench by the stove. Odile was below, still feverish but lucid enough to complain about the coffee, which I took as a good sign. Wren was up top with me, learning the reach, calling the channel markers before I could point them out.

And there he was, moored in the lee of the bluff like he owned it, which knowing Vane he probably had papers saying he did.

"Captain." He lifted two fingers off his cap brim as we came alongside. "Might I buy you a drink?"

"I don't drink with men who moor in ambush."

"It's not an ambush if you can see me from a mile off." He smiled the way he always did, like the smile cost him money and he was glad to spend it. "It's hospitality with poor timing."

I should have kept going. Every instinct I had — every one my father beat into me, every one the river itself had taught me since — said keep going, let the current carry you past him, let distance do what pride couldn't. But Odile needed a doctor sooner rather than later, and Vane's launch carried a radio that reached Corvus's network, and there are worse things than hearing a devil out. Sometimes the devil tells you where the traps are, just so you know how clever he is.

"Ten minutes," I said. "Wren stays aboard."

Something flickered across his face when I said her name. Not quite guilt. Vane didn't have guilt; he had accounting. But something moved behind the ledger.

"Of course," he said.

* * *

He came aboard alone, hands visible, which was its own kind of insult — as if I'd frisk him otherwise, as if we were playing at civility instead of standing on the deck of a sinking boat with a fugitive witness below and a syndicate's price on all our heads. He looked around the Kestrel the way rich men look at things they intend to own: appraising, patient, faintly amused.

"She's seen better days," he observed.

"She's seen honest ones. That's more than most boats can say."

"Touche." He accepted the coffee Bram brought with a nod, waited until Bram planted himself by the rail with his arms crossed like a fence post with opinions, and then turned to me. "You're running Tomas Reyes downriver."

"You have excellent eyesight for a man who claims he wasn't watching us."

"Hazel." He set the cup down on the chart table, precise as a surgeon laying out instruments. "Let's not do this the long way. We've known each other too long for the long way, and neither of us has the time."

"We've known each other exactly as long as it took your testimony to ruin my father," I said. "That's not friendship, Vane. That's a scar."

"And yet here I am, offering to make it right."

The rain had started again, that fine monsoon drizzle that doesn't fall so much as hang in the air and wait for you to walk through it. It beaded on the wheelhouse glass and ran down in long crooked lines. Below decks I could hear Tomas coughing, and Odile's low voice answering him — the two of them arguing about something, old men and old wounds finding each other the way rivers find the sea.

"Make it right," I repeated. "Twenty years late."

"Better than never." He pulled an envelope from inside his coat — oilskin-wrapped, heavy, the particular weight of paper that has numbers on it. He laid it on the chart table between us and did not push it toward me. That was the trick of it. He made *me* come to *him*, even now, even here. "Your debt. The full principal, cleared this afternoon. The Kestrel released from lien before sundown."

My heart did something stupid and hopeful, the way it does, the way it always does when someone dangles the thing you've been drowning for. I hated it for that. I put my hand flat on the table so it wouldn't shake.

"And?"

"A new hull. Yard-built, steel, shallow draft, fitted for the Serrano run. Your name on the charter. Not leased — yours."

"And?"

He smiled again, softer this time, and that was the worst one, because it was almost kind. "Your father's name restored. Publicly. At council, if you like. The record amended. Elias Thornbury, cleared of falsification, reinstated to the rolls of master pilots. Every dock from here to High Landing will know what actually happened up that river."

The rain ticked against the glass. Somewhere downstream, thunder muttered like a creditor clearing his throat.

"That's a lot of gold for one girl and one old man," I said carefully.

"It's not gold. It's arithmetic." He spread his hands. "Corvus wants them delivered. I want Corvus gone — you'll understand why in a moment. Deliver both to High Landing, intact, unharmed, and everything above is yours within the week. You'd be a fool to refuse."

"Probably," I agreed. "Tell me the part about why you want Corvus gone. The part where you explain yourself. Everyone who offers me a fortune always wants to explain themselves first. It's how I know the fortune's rotten."

For a moment — just a moment — the smoothness cracked. Not much. A hairline. But I'd spent twenty years studying Silas Vane the way you study a river that drowned someone you loved, and I knew every sandbar in him.

"I was twenty-three years old," he said quietly. "When your father went up the Serrano. Did you know that? Everyone remembers me as the young broker, the rising man, the one who testified. Nobody asks what a twenty-three-year-old clerk was doing testifying before the river council at all."

"Coerced," I said. Flat. Testing the word in my mouth like a bad coin.

"Corvus held my father's charter. My family's whole livelihood sat in Corvus's ledger, the way yours sits in the bank's. He told me what to say and showed me what would happen to my mother if I didn't say it." His jaw worked. "I told myself for years that I had no choice. Then I told myself I'd earned my way free. Then I stopped telling myself anything at all, because thinking about it required looking at it." He met my eyes. "You want me to say I'm sorry. I am sorry. It's worth nothing after twenty years, and I know that, and I'm saying it anyway because you're the only person alive whose forgiveness would mean the account was closed."

The wheelhouse felt very small. Bram hadn't moved, but I could feel him listening the way you feel weather change. Below, Tomas coughed again, and the sound of it went through me like a hook.

"You're asking me," I said slowly, "to hand over a sixteen-year-old girl to the same man who ruined my father. On the strength of an apology twenty years old."

"I'm asking you to take the deal that saves your boat, your crew, your name, and your future, and costs you nothing but two people you've known for a matter of weeks." He leaned forward. "Hazel. Be honest with yourself, if not with me. What is Wren to you? A stowaway. A fare. A job you took because the bank gave you no choice. And Reyes? He wouldn't piss on a Thornbury if you were burning. You heard him. You're delivering a girl to a gilded cage and an old man to a comfortable exile, and in exchange you get everything back. Everything. There is no version of this where you're the villain. There isn't. I've run the sums."

"That's the trouble with you, Silas." I picked up the envelope. It was heavier than it should have been, or maybe my hand was tired. "You think everything is sums. You think a person is a column you can balance. My father thought that way once — that the river was arithmetic, depth and current and tonnage, and it nearly killed him before Corvus finished the job. The river isn't arithmetic. Neither is this."

"The girl is not your daughter."

"No," I said. "She's not. She's something I haven't got a word for yet. But I know she isn't cargo, and I know that whatever I feel about her, it isn't for sale, and I know that if I handed her over, I'd spend the rest of my life reading water and seeing her face in every eddy. You're offering me a new boat, Silas. I'd need one. I'd burn the Kestrel myself rather than captain her with that deal in her hold."

He exhaled through his nose. Slowly. The hairline crack sealed itself over, and the broker came back, the smooth one, the one who'd stood before the council at twenty-three and lied with a steady voice.

"I hoped you'd be smarter," he said.

"I hoped you'd be better. We're both disappointed. It's practically a marriage."

That startled a laugh out of him — short, genuine, immediately regretted. He straightened his coat. For a moment he stood there in the gray light with the rain running off the wheelhouse roof between us, and he looked, for the first time since Dock Nine, tired. Actually tired. Like a man who'd been swimming a long time in clothes that wanted him dead.

"They know where you are," he said. "Two skiffs, maybe three hours behind you, running dark. Corvus stopped negotiating yesterday. The offer I made you was the last soft thing left in this business, and you've just declined it." He picked up his hat. "Take the eastern channel past Widow's Reach. The main stem is watched from the bluffs. And Hazel —"

"Don't," I said. "Don't give me advice and call it penance."

He paused at the companionway. "It's not penance," he said. "It's the only apology I know how to make. I ruined a pilot once by knowing things and saying them to the wrong people. Let me ruin Corvus the same way." And he stepped down into the rain, into his launch, and was gone around the bluff before I could decide whether I believed a single word of it.

* * *

Bram found me at the wheel ten minutes later. I was holding the envelope, which I hadn't thrown after him, which I hadn't opened, which I was holding the way you hold a snake you haven't decided to kill.

"Well?" he said.

"He offered me everything."

"Everything," Bram repeated. He leaned on the rail beside me and looked out at the green-black water. "Debt, new boat, your father's name."

"You were listening."

"Man talks loud when he wants to be overheard." Bram scratched his beard. "Odile heard most of it through the deck plates. So did Tomas, though he pretends he didn't. And Wren—" He hesitated.

"Wren what?"

"Wren was on the ladder," Bram said. "Outside the wheelhouse window. Port side. Maybe four minutes. Maybe longer." He looked at me, and his face was doing something careful. "She heard the part about High Landing. About being delivered intact. I don't know how much else."

The rain came harder. Downstream, thunder rolled up the valley like a barrel knocked loose, and the light over the water had begun to go that color I knew too well, the color the sky turns when it stops pretending to be on your side.

I looked down at the envelope in my hands. Full principal. Cleared this afternoon. Enough paper to buy back everything I'd spent twenty years losing.

Wren had stood on that ladder and listened to a man price her like freight, and then she'd climbed down without a sound, and she hadn't run, and she hadn't hidden, and she was — I realized, scanning the deck — up at the bow right now, in the rain, coiling lines that didn't need coiling, keeping her back to the wheelhouse and her shoulders square, waiting to see what the captain would do with the money.

I walked to the stern rail, opened the envelope, and fed it to the river a page at a time. The current took each one without ceremony. It takes everything without ceremony. That's the one honest thing about it.

Then I went forward, took the other coil out of her hands, and said, "Eastern channel past Widow's Reach. Vane says the main stem's watched. Get up here and read me the water — and Wren? Whatever you heard in there, hear this too: you're not cargo. You never were. Now come show me where that channel marker's supposed to be, because the rain's eating my eyes."

Behind us, far downstream, a searchlight swept the bluffs and began, patiently, to follow.
