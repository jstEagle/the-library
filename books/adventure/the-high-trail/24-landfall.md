## 24. Landfall

I have been on rivers my whole life, and I will tell you the first thing the water teaches you: it does not care about you. Not a little. Not conditionally. It does not care the way a stone does not care, which is to say completely, eternally, and without the courtesy of malice.

The sky had been going green-black since noon — that particular bruised color that means the monsoon has stopped being a rumor and become a schedule. Wren stood at the rail with the radio pressed to her ear, her lips moving along with the broadcast like she was trying to memorize it, or argue with it.

"Landfall's moved up," she said finally, lowering the set. "Tomorrow morning. Maybe tonight."

"Maybe tonight," Hazel repeated, in the flat voice she used when a thing was too large to say loudly.

"Four days was what they said yesterday," Bram said from the engine hatch, his forearms black to the elbow with grease. "Yesterday it was four days. Today it is maybe tonight. That seems like cheating."

"The river cheats," Hazel said. "That's the whole curriculum."

Odile, that's me, by the way. Boatswain, cook, keeper of the medical kit, and as of two weeks ago, the proud owner of a bullet hole through my shoulder that the rain could now find through three layers of bandage. I want that established early, because everything I am about to describe, I describe from the deck of a boat that was already dying, carrying people who were already hunted, into weather that did not know our names and would not have pronounced them correctly if it had.

* * *

Here is what I knew by midafternoon, and I will lay it out plainly because there is no other honest way:

One: the Kestrel leaked. She'd taken her wound running the rapids above Sallow Bend, a seam split near the third strake, and we'd patched her with canvas, pitch, and the kind of optimism that should be classified as a controlled substance. The bilge pump ran every twenty minutes. Then fifteen. Then ten. You can chart a death by its intervals.

Two: downstream meant Corvus's checkpoints, and Corvus's checkpoints meant armed men, and armed men meant me explaining my shoulder to someone new, and I have explained it enough.

Three: upstream lay Tomas Reyes and the witnesses and the will and all the thin bright hope of it — but upstream also lay the gorge, and the gorge in monsoon flood was not water so much as an argument the mountains were having with the sky, using the river for punctuation.

Hazel stood at the wheel doing arithmetic with her jaw. I have watched her do this a hundred times. She never writes it down. She carries numbers the way other people carry grudges — close, warm, always ready.

"We make for Miller's Cut," she said at last. "Side channel off the east bank, past the drowned orchard. It floods deep in monsoon but it's sheltered — high banks, old growth. We tuck in and let the worst of it pass over us."

"How long?" Bram asked.

"Landfall plus two days, if the forecasts hold."

"They haven't held since Tuesday," Wren said.

"Then landfall plus however long the sky feels like it." Hazel swung the wheel. "All ahead full. And somebody get me a line count on everything we own, because if we're riding out a monsoon in a flooded timber channel, I want to know exactly how much of our life is lashed down and how much of it is going to end up in a tree."

I got the line count. For the record: one skiff, half a fuel reserve, a medicine kit with four days of antibiotics left because Bram is a sentimental fool who traded away his father's compass ring for the rest, forty cans of food, one radio, one logbook that mattered more than any of it, one girl who mattered more than the logbook, and five people who had, through no fault of their own and considerable fault of their own choices, become the only family any of us had left.

* * *

The first squall hit us at the mouth of Miller's Cut, and it hit us like a debt collector.

I don't mean wind. Wind is polite; wind announces itself. This came down sideways out of a sky the color of a dead man's lips, and it took the rain and made it horizontal, made it a weapon, made it something that stung the face and filled the eyes so fast you couldn't blink it clear. The Kestrel heeled. The wheel went heavy in Hazel's hands. Somewhere below, the bilge pump started up with the weary regularity of a man coughing.

"Hold her!" Hazel shouted, though holding her was precisely what none of us could do. The current in the main channel had turned against us hours ago — brown, swollen, running at speeds I'd only seen in spring melt — and now the wind was shoving us crosswise, toward the cut's narrow throat, where the drowned orchard waited with its dead trees standing in six feet of water like a congregation that had been waiting for the sermon a very long time.

"Bram, fenders! Port side! Odile—"

"I'm on the bow," I said, before she could spend breath telling me. My shoulder screamed when I raised the gaff hook, and I let it scream. Pain is just information, and I already had the information: *you are fifty-one years old and you are hanging off the prow of a sinking riverboat threading a needle in a hurricane.* Thank you, shoulder. Very helpful.

The cut's mouth was maybe forty feet wide. On the chart it looked like a pencil scratch. In person, in flood, it was a throat of white water between banks that had stopped being banks and become rapids' edges, and the trees coming down the main channel behind us —

I should mention the trees.

When a monsoon takes a hillside, it doesn't take it politely. It takes whole slopes, soil and root and trunk together, and it delivers them to the river as projectiles. The first tree came past us broadside while I was still on the bow — a black oak the size of a church steeple, roots first, turning slowly like something dreaming, and then not slowly at all as the current got its teeth into it. It missed us by thirty feet. The sound of it going by was the sound of a house being dragged.

"Second one!" Wren yelled from the stern. "Coming wide!"

This one was bigger and closer, and I watched Hazel do the thing she does — read the water the way I read faces, seeing the seam of slower current beneath all that violence, the thread of slack water hugging the bank where the flood hadn't fully claimed the eddy yet. She put the Kestrel's nose into that thread like a woman stepping between raindrops.

We slipped inside the tree. Close enough that I could see bark, see a bird's nest, absurdly intact, riding the root ball like a passenger.

And then we were through the mouth of the cut, and the white water fell away behind us, and Miller's Fold opened up around us — deep green water, flooded forest, and above it walls of old cedars leaning in against the wind like old men against a door.

Hazel brought us alongside a shelf of rock that in dry season was probably a fishing spot and now was a stone dock provided by the flood itself, and we tied off four lines to trunks that had been standing there longer than any of our surnames, and we breathed.

For about nine seconds.

* * *

The leak worsened the way bad news worsens — gradually, then insultingly.

By nightfall the bilge pump was running continuous. Bram had the floorboards up in the aft hold and was lying in six inches of gray riverwater with a lamp between his teeth, hunting the split seam with his fingers.

"It's opened up," he reported, voice echoing hollow under the deck. "Canvas patch held fine. The wood around it gave up. There's movement — she's working, the whole strake, flexing with the chop."

"Can you re-pitch it?" Hazel asked, from above, through the boards.

"In this? No. The hull's cold, the water's coming through faster than pitch sets. I can slow it. I can't stop it." A pause. "She needs a yard, Hazel. Dry ground, heat, a proper caulk. She needed one last week and we both pretended otherwise."

Nobody said anything. Pretending otherwise had been the ship's official policy since Sallow Bend, and changing policy mid-flood seemed unwise.

So we slowed it. Bram stuffed the seam with oakum soaked in tallow, working blind by lamplight in the rising water, and I sat on the engine housing and handed him things and kept him talking, because Bram in silence starts thinking, and Bram thinking starts remembering that he traded his father's compass ring for antibiotics for me, and I cannot be responsible for a man feeling feelings in a flooded bilge during a monsoon. It's more than my shoulder can bear.

"What did your father say about the ring?" I asked him, handing down the mallet.

"That it was ugly."

"And?"

"And that ugly things that point true are worth more than pretty things that don't." He drove the oakum home. "He also said never lend money to a man with a better boat than yours, which is why I never lent Hazel anything."

"Heard that," Hazel called down.

"You were meant to."

Wren appeared beside me with two mugs of tea that tasted mostly of condensed milk and despair, and sat, and pulled her knees up, and watched the water rise in the hold with the expression of someone watching a bill come due.

"It's my fault," she said. Quietly. To the tea, not to me.

"Don't start," I said.

"If I'd stayed at High Landing—"

"—Corvus would have erased you and the will with you, and we'd all still be broke, and Hazel would still be owned by the bank, and Vane would still be a snake. The only difference is we'd have been miserable *without* purpose, and I've tried both versions, and this one has better company." I nudged her mug toward her mouth. "Drink. Self-pity dehydrates."

She almost smiled. Almost counts, in weather like this.

Above us, the storm found its full voice.

* * *

I want to describe that night honestly, because later — after Council Rock, after the verdict, after everything — people asked me what the monsoon was like, and I found myself giving them the tidy version. Storms, drama, danger overcome. So here is the untidy version instead:

It was loud. Not dramatic-loud. *Loud*-loud, the kind of noise that stops being sound and becomes a place you live in. Rain on the cabin roof so constant it became silence, a white noise you stopped hearing and then suddenly heard again, like a hand on your shoulder. Wind in the cedars overhead making sounds like language — words, almost, in a language with no mercy in it. And underneath, the river itself, moving in the dark with a weight you could feel through the hull, through the soles of your feet, through the bones of your feet, a vast patient muscle flexing against us.

Trees came down the channel all night. We heard them before we saw them — a cracking, far upstream, then a groan, then a rush — and once, horribly, a grinding scrape along the outermost of our mooring lines as some great drifting trunk tested our lines the way a prisoner tests bars, and then slid past into the dark.

We took turns on watch. Two up, three sleeping, wedged into the cabin among the lashed cargo like spoons in a drawer. Nobody slept well. The boat talked all night — timbers groaning, seams ticking, the pump running and running and running, that small mechanical heartbeat that was keeping us alive and telling us, with every cycle, exactly how mortal we were.

Around three in the morning, Wren's watch, the radio crackled and died.

She shook Hazel awake. They stood together over the set, tapping it, checking the battery, doing the small futile rituals people perform for machines that have gone to God.

"Antenna's probably gone," Hazel said. "Or the mast."

"Or the whole world's gone and the radio's just being honest," Wren said.

Hazel looked at her — and here is a thing I witnessed and will swear to: Hazel Thornbury, who had spent twenty years building herself out of iron and debt and her father's disgrace, smiled at that girl. Small, tired, real.

"Then we'll rebuild the world too," she said. "Add it to the list."

The list, at that moment, contained: one leaking boat, one dead radio, one wounded boatswain, one syndicate with a price on all our heads, one witness in the hills, one council deadline, one gorge we still had to run, and one monsoon that had, per the last forecast anyone had received, another two days of opinions to share.

I wrote the list down, actually, in the margin of the galley ledger. It seemed important to have it somewhere besides inside Hazel's head, where lists go to become destiny.

* * *

Dawn, if you could call it that — a grudging gray light leaking through rain — brought the flood to its highest mark and our situation to its clearest.

Miller's Cut had become a lake with a forest standing in it. The banks we'd moored to were islands now. The current through the channel mouth ran like spilled mercury. And the Kestrel sat lower in the water than she had any right to, her gunwales kissing the flood, the pump still going, faithful and insufficient, a metronome counting down.

Bram surfaced from the hold looking like something the river had personally rejected.

"Three inches an hour," he said. "With the pump. If the pump goes, we're on the bottom of Miller's Cut inside of two hours, in a flooded forest, in monsoon."

"So the pump doesn't go," Hazel said.

"Right. The pump doesn't go." He wiped his face with a rag that was filthier than his face. "Also I've been thinking about the gorge."

Everyone stopped. Even the pump, briefly, seemed to listen.

"Upstream's the gorge," Bram said. "Downstream's Corvus. Those are our options, and we've known that for days, and everyone's been very brave about not saying it out loud. But the gorge in flood — Hazel, nobody runs the gorge in this. Not you. Not your father. Nobody. The water through there'll be doing thirty knots with debris the size of chapels in it. We'd be betting the girl's life, Tomas's rescue, everything, on—" he gestured at the hull beneath his feet, at the whole tired leaking truth of her, "—on *this*."

Silence. Rain. Pump.

"I know," Hazel said.

"That's it? 'I know'?"

"I also know Corvus's checkpoints are downstream, and his men are converging on Council Rock by water, which means the gorge is the only way we get Tomas back and get the will's witness to the council before the verdict's bought and buried." She said it evenly, but I saw her hand on the wheel, and her knuckles were white, and her eyes were fixed on the gray violence of the channel mouth like a woman reading a letter she didn't want to finish. "I'm not choosing the gorge, Bram. I'm choosing which impossible thing. It's the only menu we've been given since Dock Nine."

"The Kestrel can't take it," Bram said, quieter now. "You know she can't. Not with this seam. The gorge will open her like a tin."

And Hazel was silent for a long time. Long enough that Wren reached out and touched the wheel beside her hand, not saying anything, just touching, the way you touch a fence rail when the drop is on the other side.

"I know what she can't take," Hazel said finally. Her voice did something I had never heard it do — cracked, slightly, and recovered. "But let's not bury her while she's still pumping. We wait out the front. We re-pitch the seam in warmer water if the sky gives us an hour. And then we decide with clear eyes whether we're asking this old girl to die for us, or whether there's a third way I haven't thought of yet." She looked around at all of us, one by one, captain's inventory: Bram dripping, Wren pale, me holding my shoulder like a purse. "There's usually a third way. It's usually worse."

"That's not comforting," Wren said.

"It's not meant to comfort you. It's meant to prepare you." Hazel turned back to the wheel. "Now bail."

* * *

We bailed. All day, in shifts, while the storm spent itself against the cedars and the flood stood at its peak and refused, with the indifference of all true powers, to either drown us or release us.

Nature does not care about your cause. I keep returning to that, because it was the lesson of that day, written in rain. The river did not know we carried a will that could break a syndicate. The wind did not know Hazel's father had been ruined for honesty. The trees coming down the current — dozens of them now, the whole drowned hillside traveling past our mooring in pieces — did not know whose granddaughter stood at our rail watching them go by with her grandfather's river in her eyes. The storm was not against us. It was not for us. It simply *was*, enormous and busy and utterly uninterested, and there is a terrible freedom in that, if you let yourself feel it.

Because if the water doesn't care, then the caring has to come from somewhere else.

It came from Bram, who bailed double shifts so I wouldn't tear my stitches. It came from Wren, who learned the pump's rhythms and kept it fed and oiled like it was a living crewmate. It came from Hazel, who stood at the wheel through all of it, reading the flooded channel hour after hour, cataloguing every submerged snag and shifting eddy for the day — coming soon, coming inevitably — when we would have to take all of this knowledge and bet it against the gorge.

And it came, around dusk, from the most unlikely quarter.

The rain eased. Not stopped — eased, the way a fist eases before it decides. And through the thinning curtain of it, we all heard it at once: an engine. Far off. Upstream, toward the gorge, laboring hard against the current.

A launch. Running lights swinging wild through the rain-haze, fighting the flood, and painted on its hull, glimpsed once through the trees before it vanished upstream into the dark —

Corvus gray. Syndicate colors. Armed, loaded, and headed straight for the gorge ahead of us.

Hazel watched it go. The pump thumped. The rain thickened again, closing like a door.

"Well," she said, into the dark, to all of us and none of us. "Now we know which way they think we'll run."
