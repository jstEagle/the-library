## 25. Bail and Prayers

I have been on boats my whole life, and I can tell you this: a boat in a storm is not a vessel. It is an argument between the river and everything you love, and you are the rope in the middle.

That night, we were losing the argument.

The side channel had seemed like salvation when Hazel threaded us into it — a flooded oxbow choked with willows, deep enough to float, narrow enough that the main current's fury broke apart before it reached us. For about two hours it worked. Then the wind found its second wind, if you'll forgive the expression, and started throwing trees at us.

"Bram." Odile's voice from below, thin as thread. "Water."

I already knew. I'd felt her coming up through my boots, that soft wrong give in the deck plates, the way the Kestrel rolled a half-beat too slow coming back upright. A boat tells you she's hurt before she says so out loud. You just have to be listening.

"Bucket brigade," Hazel said, appearing beside me at the rail with rain streaming off her jaw like she'd grown it there. "Bram, Odile — you're on bail. Wren, you're with me on lines. Nobody sleeps tonight."

Nobody slept for three nights, as it turned out. But I'm getting ahead of myself.

* * *

Here is what bailing actually looks like, because the songs get it wrong. The songs have men standing at rails with brass buckets, water arcing silver in the moonlight. What it actually looks like is: a galley pot with a broken handle, a bait bucket, a cooking pot someone had to sacrifice mid-stew, and four exhausted people passing them in a chain from the bilge to the scuppers, over and over, until your arms stop being arms and become two complaints attached to shoulders.

Odile shouldn't have been up there at all. The wound in her side — the one she took covering our retreat at High Landing, the one Bram-the-doctor-of-nothing had stitched by lantern light with shaking hands — had gone hot. Her fever came on like weather of its own. By the time we reached the channel she was shivering under two blankets in air warm as bathwater, teeth chattering hard enough that I could hear them over the rain on the roof.

"I can bail," she said.

"You cannot," I said.

"I can hold a bucket."

"You cannot hold a bucket."

"Bram." She caught my wrist, and her hand was furnace-hot, and her eyes were clear as glass, which frightened me worse than the fever did. "If I lie down and do nothing, I will think. And right now I don't want to be alone with what I think about. Give me the bucket."

So I gave her the bucket. There are medicines for fevers and there are none, and where there are none, you do what the person asks, because dignity is also a medicine. We ran out of antibiotics two days back; Bram traded his father's compass ring at the floating market for what little he got, and I don't talk about that, and neither does he, except that he touches the bare finger sometimes like a man checking whether a tooth is still missing.

We bailed. The rain fell. The willows groaned and tore loose along the bank one by one, going downstream like a procession of drowning pilgrims, and every time one swung close I thought: that could be us, that could very easily be us, and passed the bucket.

* * *

Somewhere past midnight — though midnight was a rumor by then, the sky so low and black it might have been any hour since Tuesday — Wren stopped bailing and said, "Sing something."

"Bad idea," I said.

"Sing something," she said again, and there was something in her voice that wasn't a request. She was sixteen years old, soaked through, holding a bait bucket in a sinking boat in a monsoon, and she wanted a song the way a starving person wants bread. Not comfort exactly. Proof. Proof that people had done this before and lived, that there was a shape to survive inside.

So I sang. My father used to sing it hauling nets — "Lowlands Low," the old hauling song, the one about the false lover drowned in the lowland sea, which is a cheerful subject but has a chorus built like a lever, made to move weight. I got three words in and Hazel joined on the fourth without looking up from the line she was coiling, and then Odile, from her nest of blankets, bucket still in her lap, lifting her voice thin and cracked and absolutely determined, and then Wren, who didn't know the words and made up her own and was not corrected, because you do not correct a girl who is inventing verses about a river that wants to kill her while the river listens.

And here is the thing I learned that night, the thing I'd half-known all my life and never had occasion to prove: singing while you bail works. Not mystically. Physically. A song is a rhythm, and a rhythm is a pace, and a pace you can keep is the difference between bailing until dawn and bailing until you drop. Four voices on a chorus will lift a bucket higher than four backs ever could. My father knew it. Every net-hauler and anchor-weigher and coffin-lowerer who ever lived knew it. The work sings you through itself.

We sang everything we knew. We sang things nobody knew. Odile sang a smuggler's ballad in her mother's tongue that none of us understood and all of us roared anyway, and Wren laughed for the first time in days — a startled, guilty laugh, like joy was something she'd stolen — and even Hazel smiled, which on Hazel is an event the council should probably be notified of.

Then the tree came down.

* * *

I heard it before I saw it — a crack like the river clearing its throat, up in the dark above the bank. Hazel's head snapped toward it and she shouted, "Line! Get her head around—" and the world became very simple for about forty seconds.

A cottonwood the size of a church steeple, roots torn whole out of the drowned bank, swung down the channel on the current, broadside, rotating slow as a door. It was aimed at us the way weather aims, which is to say carelessly and perfectly.

Hazel had the Kestrel's bow line in her hands. She wrapped it once around the samson post and threw the coil across Wren's shoulder — "Brace!" — and the girl braced, gods bless her, feet wide, back against the cabin, holding a rope that suddenly went taut as a harp string. The engine wasn't running; we'd shut it down to save fuel we no longer had. All we had was rope, and Wren, and geometry.

The tree hit the bow line's pull a body-length off our starboard side and swung — pivoted on the tension like a dancer changing partners — and ground past us close enough that I could have touched bark that would have taken the hull off in one stroke. Leaves slapped the deck. Something in the water beneath it groaned, and the Kestrel groaned back, and for a moment the two of them were pressed together in the dark like old friends deciding whether to embrace or fight.

Then the current took the tree onward, and Wren sat down hard on the deck, and the rope sang as it slackened, and Hazel said, very quietly, "Good brace," which from her is a parade and a medal ceremony.

Wren's hands were bleeding where the rope had burned them. Odile bound them by feel, feverish herself, working in the lightning-flash intervals like a woman developing photographs. Neither of them said anything sentimental. Neither of them needed to. That's the thing about near-death in company: it does the talking for you.

* * *

The near-capsize came later, and I'll tell it quickly because I've told it slowly in my head too many times already.

Past the deepest hour of night, the channel — which had been shelter — turned trap. The flood backing up behind the downstream bend began to pile into our oxbow, and the water level climbed fast, and the willows that had shielded us started going under, and then the current in the channel reversed and strengthened, and the Kestrel swung her stern toward the gap where the water wanted out, and Hazel said, "She's going to broach us," in the tone of a woman reading her own death warrant and finding a typo in it.

Broaching is how small boats die in floods. The river takes your stern, swings you broadside, and pushes you under sideways like a man closing a book. We had maybe a minute.

What happened next happened in pieces, and each piece belonged to somebody. Hazel got the engine lit on the third try — third try, remember, on fuel fumes and stubbornness — and put us nose-first into the outflow, throttling against it, holding station like a moth pinned by its own wings. Wren scrambled forward and dropped the anchor off the bow into the root tangle of a half-drowned willow, buying us a second point of purchase. Odile, fever-bright, moved ballast — crates, pots, the blessed bait bucket — forward to keep our stern light. And me, I did the only thing left, which was bail faster, because a boat fighting a current takes water over the transom like a tax, and every liter I moved was a liter she didn't carry.

We held. God help us, we held. Forty minutes with the engine screaming and the river pulling and the rain coming down like judgment, and the Kestrel trembling the whole length of her keel like a living thing refusing — simply refusing — to be a dead thing.

When the flood crested and the pull eased, nobody cheered. We were too far past cheering. We just kept doing whatever we'd been doing for another ten minutes, because nobody was sure it was over, and then Hazel killed the engine and the silence rang in our ears like a struck bell, and Wren started to cry without any sound at all, and Odile put her arms around her, and that was the whole celebration.

But I remember thinking, in the ringing silence, looking around at those three women and that ruined, leaking, magnificent hull: I would not trade this for dry land and a full purse. Not tonight. Tonight the catastrophe had done what twenty years of shared voyages hadn't quite finished — it had welded us. You cannot un-know people after a night like this. You have seen what they do when the water rises. Whatever they are, that's what they are.

* * *

Dawn came the color of pewter, then pearl, then — briefly, insultingly — gold. The storm had spent itself in the night and moved off downstream to ruin someone else's week. The channel lay calm as a bath. Birds started up in the drowned willows with the obscene good cheer birds always have after disasters.

Odile slept through most of it, which was the plan. But near sunrise I was sitting on the deck beside her blankets, keeping watch more than anything else, when she stirred and opened her eyes.

And I knew. Before I touched her, I knew. Fever has a look — a shine, a distance, like the person is standing one room away from their own face. It was gone. Her eyes were just her eyes, tired and level and present.

"Well?" she said. Her voice was sandpaper but it was hers.

"How do you feel?"

"Like a tree fell on me."

"One tried. Missed."

She managed the ghost of her crooked smile. "Then it owes me an apology."

I put my palm flat against her forehead anyway, because I'm a worrier by trade and profession. Cool. Damp, but cool. The fever had broken sometime in the black hours, in the middle of the worst night of our lives, and she'd slept through her own rescue, which knowing Odile is exactly how she'd want it — no fuss, no audience, just quietly winning while everyone else panicked.

"Wren," I called softly, because some news isn't mine to keep. "Come here."

Wren came, moving like an old woman, hands bandaged, eyes swollen. She looked down at Odile, and Odile looked up at her, and Wren's face did something complicated and young and ended in tears she pretended were a yawn.

"Told you," Odile said. "Stubborn beats fever every time."

"You didn't tell me anything, you were unconscious."

"Stubbornly."

Wren laughed — the wet, hiccupping kind — and sat down on the deck planks, and Odile reached up and gripped the girl's burned hand carefully, and I looked away to give them privacy and found Hazel at the rail instead, watching the channel, watching the high brown water carrying the storm's leavings seaward.

"She'll live," I told her.

"I know." Hazel didn't turn. "I watched her all night."

"We all did."

"No." Now she turned, and there was something raw under the captain-calm, something the storm had stripped like bark off the willows. "You watched her. I counted her breaths. Bram — last night, when the tree came down, and Wren held that line, and Odile moved ballast with a hole in her side, and you bailed like the devil himself was taking rent—" She stopped. Started again. "I have lost boats before. I never lost a crew. I understand now that these are different losses, and I understand which one I couldn't survive."

I didn't say anything clever. I put my hand on her shoulder, and she let it stay there, which from Hazel is roughly equivalent to a sonnet.

Then she straightened, and the rawness folded itself away somewhere, and she pointed downstream to where the channel met the main river, and past that to the gray-green wall of the gorge country waiting beyond the bend — the last gorge, the one every chart marks in red, the one we had to run to reach Council Rock before the verdict window closed and Corvus's lawyers did their legal sorcery.

"High water through the gorge," she said. "Debris in the current, standing waves, no eddies worth the name. With the Kestrel leaking and no fuel reserve and no medicine left to lose." She said it like a shopping list. Then, quieter: "It's the only way. Tomas's testimony dies if we miss the council session. Everything we did — High Landing, the weir, all of it — dies with it."

"So we run it," I said.

"So we run it." She looked back at the boat — at the scarred hull, the patched seams, the cabin where a girl was crying over a smuggler's fever finally broken. "God forgive me. So we run it."

Behind us, faint through the cabin wall, Wren had started humming the hauling song. Lowlands Low. The one about drowning.

I decided to take it as a challenge rather than an omen. On the Kestrel, some mornings, that's the only choice on offer.
