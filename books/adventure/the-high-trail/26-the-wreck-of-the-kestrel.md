## 26. The Wreck of the Kestrel

There is a moment on every river when the water stops being water and becomes a decision. You feel it in your hands before you know it in your head — a pressure against the wheel, a hum through the deck plates, a sound like the river clearing its throat before it says something rude.

We were thirty minutes into the last gorge when I felt it.

The monsoon had come down off the high country overnight like an unwelcome guest who'd heard we were having a party without him. The Serrano had risen four feet since dawn. Four feet doesn't sound like much until you remember that four feet of brown water moving at that speed can pick up a logging truck and treat it as kindling. The gorge — which in dry season was a postcard, all red walls and white foam and birdsong — had turned into a throat. A throat with teeth.

Bram stood beside me at the wheel, his big hands hovering near mine the way they always did when things got ugly, not touching, just *there*, the way a good man hovers. Odile was below, still weak from her wound but upright, refusing to lie down, running the engine telegraph like her life depended on it because it did. Wren was strapped to the chart table with Tomas, both of them reading water marks off the old survey charts while the rain tried to eat the windows.

"Marker says the narrows clear at three feet of rise," Wren shouted over the noise. "We're past that."

"We're past everything," I said. "That's sort of our whole situation."

I should explain something about gorges. A river in a gorge doesn't have banks so much as opinions. The walls are stone and the stone funnels every drop of water from a hundred square miles of sky into a channel barely wider than my boat. The Kestrel was ninety feet of riveted iron and stubbornness, and she was built for rivers that occasionally lost their tempers. She was not built for this. But there was no going back. Behind us was the flooded side channel where we'd sheltered, now a churning brown lake full of floating trees, and beyond that, Corvus's checkpoints, and beyond *that*, the simple fact that turning around had never once been how anyone on this boat solved a problem.

So: forward. Through the throat.

* * *

The first mile went the way bad miles go — fast, loud, and full of small corrections that each felt like apologies to the river. I read the water the way my father taught me, back when I was small enough to stand on a crate and he was still a captain instead of a cautionary tale. Vees mean rocks. Smooth green tongues mean channels. Boils mean current fighting depth. And standing waves — the ones that hold their shape like they've been carved there — mean the river has made up its mind about something, and you'd best agree with it.

I called the channel and Bram worked the throttle and Odile answered every bell like she was born in an engine room, which, knowing Odile, she may well have been. We threaded the first set of rapids with maybe a boat-length to spare on either side. Wren whooped. Tomas prayed, quietly, in two languages, hedging his bets.

Then came the bend.

Every gorge has one bend that the old pilots call by name, the way you'd name a difficult relative. On the Serrano it was Widowmaker Turn, which tells you everything you need to know about the men who named it. My father ran it eleven times. He used to say the trick wasn't courage; the trick was humility. *You don't beat the turn, Hazel-girl,* he told me once, his hand heavy on my shoulder. *You ask permission.*

I asked permission. The river declined.

The problem was debris. The storm upstream had been busy all night, tearing whole sections of forest loose and mailing them downriver to us. Most of it we saw — the big stuff, whole trees cartwheeling in the current like they were late for something. But the kill shot was invisible. Somewhere under that four feet of new water, wedged against the rock shelf below Widowmaker, was a log or a piling or a piece of somebody's drowned dock, sharp end up, patient as sin.

I saw the boil too late. A smooth patch of water where the current should have been broken — the river holding its breath over something it didn't want to talk about. I put the wheel hard over. The Kestrel leaned, groaned, answered. For one glorious half-second I thought we'd missed it.

Then the hull hit.

It wasn't a crash, exactly. It was worse than a crash. It was a *sound* — a long tearing shriek along the starboard side, forward of the engine room, metal giving up an argument it had been losing since the day it was riveted. The whole boat shuddered like a dog shaking off rain. Bram grabbed the wheel with me. Odile killed the engines before I could ring for it, which saved us, because a spinning shaft in a torn-open hull will pull the whole river inside you faster than any wound.

And then the Kestrel began to die.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about losing a boat. It isn't dramatic, not really, not at first. There's no explosion, no cinematic list to port. What there is, is a sound — a soft, wet, sucking sound from below decks, the sound of a thing filling up — and then there is arithmetic. Cold, merciless arithmetic.

Odile came up the ladder two steps at a time, gray-faced, and said what I already knew. "Three compartments holding. Forward hold's taking water fast. Patch won't hold against this current."

"How fast?"

"If we beach her now? Hours. If we stay in the channel?" She didn't finish. She didn't have to. Odile never finished sentences that ended in drowning.

Ahead, maybe six hundred yards, the gorge wall broke open into a cove — a gravel hook of a place, shallow, sheltered from the worst of the current by a spur of rock. I'd marked it years ago on a chart my father drew, in his cramped handwriting, with a little annotation I'd always found funny: *ugly but honest.* Ugly but honest. That was the Kestrel too. That was most of the people I loved.

"Beach her," I said, and heard my own voice come out flat and steady, and hated how easy the steadiness was, and was grateful for it anyway.

We took her in hot. No anchor, no gentleness — you drive a sinking ship ashore the way you'd shove a friend out of the path of a truck, hard and without apology. The keel bit gravel. The whole frame slammed, threw Wren against the rail, threw Tomas into the chart locker, threw me into the wheel, and then, blessedly, stopped. The Kestrel settled onto the cove floor with a sigh, listing to starboard, her bow buried to the second strake, the river sliding past her flanks like it had already forgotten her.

We got everyone off. That part was easy. Five people, one duffel of documents wrapped in oilcloth, the medicine, the food, Tomas's cane, Wren clutching her grandfather's will like it was a kitten. The hard part came after, when the adrenaline drained out through our boots into the gravel and left us standing in the rain, looking at her.

* * *

She looked small, beached. Ninety feet of iron that had been the whole world reduced to a shape on a shore, water pouring out of her scuppers in bright ropes, steam rising off her deck plates where the engine heat met the rain. Her paint was stripped to bare metal along the starboard side where the debris had opened her. Through the tear I could see the river inside her — actual river, brown and busy, sloshing around in her forward hold like it owned the place. Which, I suppose, it did now.

Nobody spoke for a long time. Rain drummed on everything. Somewhere upstream the gorge kept roaring, indifferent, doing its work.

"She can be patched," Bram said finally. He said it the way men say hopeless things when they can't bear to say true ones. "Dry her out, patch the plate, wait for the level to drop—"

"The level won't drop for weeks, Bram." I heard myself say it gently, which was its own cruelty. "And even if it did, that tear's not a patch. That's a third of her hull. We don't have plate. We don't have tools. We don't have time." I nodded downstream, toward Council Rock, toward the deadline that had been chasing us since the ford. "Council sits in five days."

Wren was crying. Not loudly — Wren didn't do anything loudly unless it was arguing — but steadily, rain and tears indistinguishable, her arms wrapped around herself. Odile put an arm around her. Tomas stood apart, hat brim dripping, watching me with the particular patience of a man who has been ruined before and knows the choreography.

I walked her length alone. That was a thing I needed to do and couldn't have explained to anybody. I touched the rail my father had replaced the summer I turned twelve, sanded smooth by twenty years of hands. I stood at the wheelhouse door, hanging crooked on one hinge, and looked in at the wheel, at the brass compass binnacle with its glass fogged, at the little tin cup on the shelf where I kept the pencils. Everything exactly where we'd left it that morning, in the ordinary arrogance of people who assume there will be a tomorrow aboard.

My father bought this boat the year I was born. He named her for a bird that hunts on the wing, that hangs over moving water and strikes at what flashes beneath — because he said a pilot ought to be like that, patient over the current, exact when it matters. He ran her up and down this river for nineteen years. He lost her the same year he lost everything else, when Corvus's falsified charters made him unlicenseable and the bank took her, and I spent nine years buying her back plank by plank, charter by charter, debt by debt. Every payment I ever made on her was a word in an argument I was having with a dead man. *You were right. They were wrong. Look what I built out of what they left me.*

And now here she was, holed on a gravel bar in the rain, and the argument was over, and I hadn't won it. I'd just run out of river.

I'm not going to pretend I took it well. I found the driest corner of the cove — there wasn't one, so I found the least wet corner — and I sat down on a stone with my back to the boat, because I couldn't look at her and keep my face working at the same time. I cried. First for her. Then, because grief is greedy and takes whatever's adjacent, for him again — properly this time, not the tidy grief of the vindicated daughter with a logbook full of proof, but the raw old grief of a girl whose father died ashamed, and whose shame I had carried up this very gorge hoping to set it down at Council Rock. Twice over, both losses landing in the same wet hour. The boat that carried everything. That's what she'd been. Not cargo, not home exactly — home implies somewhere else to be — but the vessel that held every version of me that mattered. And the river had taken her the way the river takes everything: without malice, without ceremony, without even noticing.

Wren found me there eventually. She didn't say anything clever. She sat down on the next stone over, close enough that our shoulders touched, and we watched the rain hit the cove together until the light started going.

* * *

By full dark we'd done what sailors do, which is work, because work is grief's only real rival. We salvaged what we could carry — the documents, the medicine, tools, rope, the canvas awning, Odile's galley knife, the compass binnacle, which Bram pried loose with a crowbar and handed to me without a word, and I took it without a word, and that exchange contained more feeling than most weddings. We rigged the awning between two leaning trunks above the tideline of the flood and got a fire going under it, hissing and sulking in the wet. We ate cold rice and tinned peaches, and Odile, feverish but stubborn, insisted the peaches counted as a feast if you served them with dignity, and somehow they did.

It was Wren who broke the silence that mattered.

"We're going to raise her," she said. Not a question. Not hope, even. A plan, stated in the present tense of someone who has decided that the future is not up for debate. "When the council validates the will, I'll have salvage rights. Her keel's iron. Keels don't rot." She looked at me across the fire, chin up, sixteen years old and absolutely certain, the way only the young and the drowned can be certain. "You told me once the river keeps its moods. Well. It can keep this one too. We're coming back for her."

I wanted to tell her she was being childish. I opened my mouth to say it. What came out instead was: "Her keel is sound. I checked it in the water, before we beached. It's sound."

Because it was. Because some part of me had already known, down in that flooded hold, feeling along the frames with my boots, that the spine of her was intact — that the river had torn her belly open but not touched the one bone that mattered. Because my father had built nothing into that boat carelessly, and whoever raised her would find, under all that torn plating, a beginning.

Tomas stirred the fire and said, in his careful accented English, "In my village we say: the river does not give back what it takes. But sometimes it leaves you the wood to build the next thing."

Bram snorted. "That's a terrible proverb."

"It's a true one," Odile said.

"It's both," I said.

We slept badly, packed together under the awning, the roar of the gorge for a lullaby. And sometime in the black hours before dawn, I woke to the smell of woodsmoke and wet iron and heard, faintly, the creak of the Kestrel settling deeper into the gravel, and I lay there in the dark and made myself a promise I had no right to make and no intention of breaking:

Five days. Council Rock first. Then we come back with chains, and winches, and every favor Wren's name will buy, and we raise her. And whatever comes out of that cove — patched, scarred, half her old self — will float again.

But dawn was also bringing news, and not the kind you want delivered with your coffee. Downstream, through the rain, we could hear engines. More than one. Skiffs, working their way up the flooded shorelines, methodical as vultures — Corvus's men, sweeping the gorge for survivors of a wreck they'd probably been praying for. And we had five days of drowned country between us and Council Rock, no boat, and nowhere left to hide that the river hadn't already found.

The Kestrel had carried us as far as her bones allowed. The rest of the way, it seemed, we'd have to walk.
