## 27. On Foot to Council Rock

The Kestrel's last dry hour was spent arguing.

Not the good kind of arguing, either — not the kind that happens over cards or whose turn it is to bail. This was the arithmetic kind, where Hazel stood on the cove's shingle with a stick in her hand and drew the drowned country on the mud like she was sentencing us to it personally.

"Two days by water," she said. "Five, maybe six, on foot. The council convenes at Council Rock when the flood crest passes — that's fixed, that's law older than any of us. If we're not there before the first session closes—"

"Then Corvus wins by default," Odile finished. She was sitting on a rock with her arm still bound against her ribs, gray around the mouth but upright through sheer spite. "Yes, Captain. We've all done the sums. The sums are terrible."

"The sums are *possible*," Hazel said, which was her way of saying the sums were terrible too but she'd be damned if she'd say it out loud where the river could hear.

I looked at the water where our boat had gone down and felt something in my chest that wasn't quite grief and wasn't quite anger and was probably both wearing each other's clothes. Three weeks ago I'd been cargo. A crate with opinions. Now I was one of five people standing in the rain deciding whether to walk into a flooded valley to testify against the most powerful man on the Serrano, and the strange thing — the thing I kept turning over like a stone in my pocket — was that I couldn't remember what it felt like to be the girl who'd hidden among those crates. That girl had been running *from* something. I was walking *toward* something now, and the difference turned out to be everything.

"We'll need the wreck," Bram said.

Hazel turned. "We need to *leave*."

"We need rope, canvas, the tool roll, whatever food survived the hold, and your father's logbook if it didn't go down with the chart table." He said it mildly, the way he said most things, like a man commenting on weather. "The Kestrel isn't done giving. She never has been."

So we went back into the cove at first light, wading chest-deep through water the color of strong tea, and salvaged our own shipwreck. There's a particular humiliation in swimming after your own belongings while vulture-skiffs patrol the gorge downstream, and there's also a particular clarity in it. Everything we pulled from that hull came up dripping and essential. Rope. Canvas. A tin of hardtack that had gone soft but not rotten. The medicine kit, half-flooded, the antibiotics saved because Bram had lashed them high — his father's compass ring long since traded for them, so I suppose the ring bought itself twice.

And the logbook. Hazel went down for it herself, three times, coming up white-lipped with cold until her hands closed on oilcloth-wrapped pages, and when she surfaced the third time she held it above the water like something newborn. She didn't say anything. Nobody asked her to. Some things you carry without commentary.

By midmorning we were five figures on the old survey trail above the gorge, soaked, laden, and moving north.

* * *

Drowned country is its own kind of place, and I don't recommend it.

The flood hadn't just covered the land; it had rewritten it. Fields were lakes. Fence lines ran under green water and came up again as islands. We passed a farmhouse standing to its eaves, a rowboat tied to the chimney like the house had tried to climb aboard, and a church whose bell tower made the only dry ground for a mile — bats pouring out of the belfry at dusk in a river of their own. Somewhere under all that water were roads Hazel had known as a girl, and she navigated them from memory anyway, reading the drowned world the way she read sandbars: by what showed and what didn't.

"There." She'd point at a dead tree leaning at an angle no tree chooses. "Fence corner. Trail bends left beyond it, then climbs. Stay off the low ground after dark — the water's still rising behind us."

She was right about the rising. The monsoon had come early and it wasn't finished with us. Rain came in bands — sometimes a fine gray drizzle that got inside everything, sometimes walls of it that turned the trail into a stream and made conversation impossible. We learned to talk in shouts and silences. We learned that Tomas Reyes, seventy-some years old and half-blind, could outwalk all of us when he decided to, and that he did his deciding silently, without warning, setting off at dawn like a man who'd spent twenty years being still and intended to make up for it.

He also talked more than he had in the mission. Walking seemed to loosen him. On the second day, picking our way along a levee top with water breathing on both sides of us, he told me about the will.

"I witnessed it twice," he said. "Signed once, amended once. Old Aldous Vane-Corvus — Wren's grandfather — called me upriver special for it. Wouldn't use his own lawyers. Said his lawyers belonged to his son-in-law's ambitions." His clouded eyes found me, or near enough. "You understand what that means? The richest man on the river trusted a disgraced pilot and a parish priest over every pen he paid for."

"Why?"

"Because he knew what everyone on this river knows and pretends not to." Tomas smiled, and it was not a warm smile. "Money buys paper. It doesn't buy witnesses. Not honest ones. He wanted someone who'd already lost everything once and hadn't lied about it since."

I thought about Hazel's father, whose name was in that logbook, whose disgrace had been manufactured in these very waters. I thought about how Corvus had built an empire on the ruins of honest men and then hired their children to haul away the evidence.

"He's converging on Council Rock by water," I said. "His men are everywhere downstream. By the time we arrive he'll have charmed the whole council, planted his story, and I'll be a runaway girl with no witness and no captain."

"You have a witness," Tomas said. "Walking beside you. And your captain is currently carrying sixty pounds of salvage up a hill in the rain because she refuses to let anyone else do it. Girl, stop planning for defeat. It's a habit of the ruined, and you're not ruined. You're just early."

* * *

That night we sheltered in the bell tower church, and I want to record honestly that I slept maybe two hours, and not because of the bats.

It was the engines. All day we'd heard them — distant, patient, working the shorelines. Corvus's skiffs sweeping the drowned country the way his men had swept the narrows, methodical as bookkeeping. From the tower top, past midnight, I counted lights on the water: three, then four, lanterns swinging in slow grids. They weren't searching for wreckage anymore. Wreckage they'd have found and reported. They were searching for *us*, and they were ahead of schedule, which meant somebody had guessed our route.

Which meant somebody had told them.

I climbed down and found Hazel awake, sitting against the wall with the logbook open on her knees even though there wasn't light enough to read it. She just liked her hand on it, I think. The way some people sleep touching a weapon.

"They know the trail," I whispered.

"They know the *options*," she corrected. "There's only so many ways through drowned country on foot, and Corvus has owned the maps longer than I've owned boots." She tilted her head back against the stone. "Question is whether they've worked out we're walking instead of floating."

"Do they need to work it out? They can just sweep everything."

"They can sweep everything," Hazel agreed. "Slowly. In boats. While the rain eats their fuel and the flood eats their patience." Something moved at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile, more the memory of one. "Wren, listen to me. Men in skiffs are fast and blind. We're slow and invisible. Five people on foot in this country leave less sign than one heron. Every engine they run is a beacon telling us where they aren't."

"And when they guess?"

"Then we're already past them." She said it like a fact she'd checked. Maybe she had. Hazel carried certainty the way other people carried knives — close, sharpened, drawn only when needed. "Get some sleep. Tomorrow we cross the ford at Graywater, and after that the country climbs, and climbing country is walking country, and walking country belongs to nobody. Not even him."

I went back up the stairs. I did sleep, eventually, a little. And I dreamed of the Kestrel sitting on the bottom of the cove with her lamps somehow still lit, waiting for us the way dogs wait, and woke with wet eyes that I blamed, entirely and unconvincingly, on the rain coming through the louvers.

* * *

Graywater ford nearly killed us on the fourth day.

The maps — Hazel's memory-maps — put the crossing at a wide, shallow braided channel. The flood had other ideas. What should have been shin-deep ran fast and brown and deep as a man's chest, carrying whole trees sideways, and on the far bank, half-hidden in the willows, sat a skiff. Empty. Engine cold. Men nearby somewhere, then. Close.

We lay in the reeds for an hour while Hazel watched the far bank and I watched Hazel, and what I saw scared me worse than the skiff. Because she wasn't afraid. She was *calculating*, and I realized I was looking at a woman with nothing left to lose doing the math on what that meant.

"We could go around," Odile murmured. "North bend adds a day."

"A day we don't have, and the north bend funnels into the narrows where a skiff owns the water." Hazel's eyes moved across the current, reading it — and I saw it happen, saw her find the line the way she'd found sandbars all her life, except now the river had taken her boat and she was reading it with her feet instead. "The trees come through in sets. Big set, gap, small set, long gap. We cross in the long gap. Four minutes, maybe five. Bram takes point with the rope. Wren, you're on my hip the whole way. Tomas—" she paused, and I braced for the argument about leaving him behind, the sensible cruelty, "—Tomas goes second, because he's the whole reason for this march and I'll be damned if the river gets a vote."

Tomas snorted. "Twenty years ago I'd have crossed first and swum back for the rest of you."

"Twenty years ago you'd have been wrong to." Hazel stood, coiling rope. "Nobody dies proving they used to be tough. On my count."

We crossed in the long gap between tree-sets, and it was exactly as bad as she'd promised and exactly survivable, which is the most you can ever ask of a plan. The water took my feet twice and gave them back. Bram hauled the rope taut and Odile crossed with her bound arm held high like a woman swearing an oath. Tomas went second, steady as furniture. And on the far bank, soaked to the soul, we stood in the willows ten yards from an empty enemy skiff, and Hazel looked at that skiff for a long moment with an expression I couldn't read.

Then she walked over to it, found the fuel valve, and opened it onto the ground.

"Waste not," she said, "want not," and we left their boat dying in the grass and climbed.

* * *

On the fifth day the country finally began to rise, and with the rise came the first sight of our destination, and with the sight of it came the weight of everything we were walking toward.

Council Rock sat on a bluff above the upper river where the flood couldn't reach — a granite shoulder crowned with a hall built of black timber, older than the syndicate, older than the charters, older than every lie Corvus had told on this water. Even from miles away you could see the banners going up for the season's contestation, and the masts of boats gathering below it like a congregation, and among those masts — I picked them out one by one, counting, dreading — the tall black pennants of the Corvus fleet. Dozens. He hadn't come to contest charters. He'd come to coronate himself.

"There it is," Odile said quietly. "Everything we have left, walking toward everything he has."

"It's not nothing versus everything," I said, and surprised myself with how steady my voice came out. "It's the truth versus him. I've seen what the truth does when it finally stands up in front of people. It makes them quiet. Then it makes them move."

Bram grunted — his version of applause. Hazel said nothing, but she shifted the pack on her shoulders and lengthened her stride, and I fell in beside her, and Tomas fell in beside me, and we came down off the ridge toward the gathering boats as the last light went copper on the floodwater behind us.

Below, on the landing road, torches were being lit for the evening. And standing among the torches, checking papers at a barricade with the unhurried air of a man who owned the evening, was a figure I recognized from a single glimpse at High Landing — broad, silver-collared, Corvus's dockmaster of the upper reach, with a ledger in his hand and six armed men at his back.

Every traveler on that road was stopping. Every traveler was showing papers.

Hazel stopped too, one hand coming back to rest lightly on my shoulder — not holding me, just anchoring me, the way you anchor a boat in current.

"Well," she murmured, almost cheerfully, in that voice she saved for the worst water. "Everyone's showing papers. So the question, Wren, is what we're going to show *him*."
