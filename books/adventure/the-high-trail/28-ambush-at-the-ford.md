## 28. Ambush at the Ford

I've crossed a lot of fords in my life, and I want it on the record that I've never liked a single one of them.

A ford is a river's way of saying *I'll let you pass, but only because I feel like it.* There's no trust in a crossing. You wade into moving water up to your hips with everything you own held over your head like an offering, and the whole time the current is doing arithmetic on you — your weight, your footing, how fast it can take your legs out from under you. Rivers are patient accountants. They always get paid.

This particular ford sat two miles below the drowned orchards, where the Serrano spread itself wide and shallow over a shelf of red gravel. On a dry year it would've been knee-deep and boring. This was not a dry year. The monsoon was still a day out, maybe less, but its fingers had already found the upper tributaries, and the water came through hip-deep and brown and pushing like it had somewhere important to be.

We stood on the north bank under a stand of waterlogged alders, five of us looking at one crossing, and I did what I always do when the situation turns to arithmetic: I counted heads.

Hazel, soaked to the shoulder already from the last stream, jaw set in that way she gets when she's made a decision and is just waiting for the world to catch up. Wren beside her, gray-faced with tiredness but standing straighter than she had any right to, three days of walking having burned most of the girl out of her and left something harder underneath. Odile, favoring her leg, wound healing clean now but the muscle around it still sulking. Hazel's arm slung over her shoulders, which Odile tolerated because she was too exhausted to argue about dignity.

And Tomas Reyes, half-blind, seventy-some years old, carrying twenty years of guilt in a satchel along with his father's logbook and the last living testimony against Corvus Delacruz.

Four able bodies. One witness who moved like a man walking through wet sand. And somewhere behind us, converging on this stretch of river by water, Corvus's men.

"We cross here," Hazel said, "or we don't cross at all."

* * *

Here's the thing nobody tells you about being the big one in a group: you become furniture. Load-bearing furniture. When there's something heavy, Bram carries it. When there's a fire to break up, Bram stands in front of it. When the plan requires someone to be the wall between trouble and everyone else, congratulations, Bram, you're the wall.

I'd stopped minding years ago. It's honest work, being a wall. Walls don't have to make decisions. They just have to stand.

So when Hazel laid it out — Wren first, lightest and fastest; then Odile with me carrying her pack; then Tomas on my back if the water got past his chest; Hazel last, sweeping our backtrail — I nodded and started cinching straps, and nobody looked at me twice, which is exactly how I like it.

The alders gave us cover down to the bank. Through their trunks I could see the far side: a shingle bar, then a field gone to reeds, then the long green climb toward Council Rock, maybe six hours' walk if the weather held and eight if it didn't. The weather was not going to hold. The sky had that bruised, low-bellied look it gets before it commits to something, and the wind had come around into the south, warm and wrong.

"Water's faster than it looks," Hazel said. She crouched at the edge, watching the surface the way she watches everything — reading it, arguing with it silently. "Angle upstream. If your feet go, float on your back, feet first. Nobody tries to be brave."

"Speak for yourself," Wren said. Her voice cracked in the middle of it, and she covered it by shouldering her pack like it weighed nothing, which it didn't, but pride weighs more than packs anyway.

Hazel looked at her a moment. Something passed between them that I've seen a hundred times since the stowaway discovery and never once heard spoken aloud — some agreement they'd signed without paper, captain and crew, or whatever those two were to each other now, which was more than either of them had words for.

"Go," Hazel said.

Wren went.

She hit the water angled sharp upstream, and I'll say this for the girl: she read current better than people twice her age. She picked her line off the surface the way Hazel does, found the slack seam behind a gravel hump, and walked it, arms out, small against all that brown push. Halfway across the water took her to her ribs and she leaned into it like a fence post in wind, and then her boots found the shallows and she was up the far bank, wringing out her braid and pretending she hadn't been scared.

"Odile," Hazel said.

I carried Odile's pack and took her elbow, and we went in together. The current hit different in the middle — deeper, meaner, with a rolling undertow where the channel cut — and Odile swore at it fluently in two languages, which I took as a good sign. Her leg dragged. Twice I felt her weight shift onto me entirely, and twice I braced and held, and then we were through, and I turned to watch the far bank while Hazel brought Tomas.

That's when I saw them.

Skiffs. Two of them, coming down the flooded side channel to the north — the same channel we'd used yesterday to skirt the checkpoint at Miller's Bend. They came quiet, no engine noise until the last moment, paddles and then a low idling motor, and they were already turning toward our bank.

"Hazel!" My voice came out flat and loud, the way it does when there's no time for anything else. "Boats! North bank!"

Everything after that happened fast, and I remember it in pieces, the way bad things come back to you.

* * *

Piece one: Hazel turning, seeing the skiffs, and making the sound decision in about a second and a half — because that's who she is, God help us all. "Tomas, get in the water. Now. Bram, you're with me."

Piece two: Tomas not moving.

He stood there among the alders with the water roaring past ten feet away and armed men closing in, and he didn't move. Not from fear. From arithmetic of his own.

"They're here for me," he said. His voice was calm, terribly calm, the calm of a man who has been waiting twenty years for consequences. "If I run, they chase. If I stand, they stop. Go. Get the girl to the council. That's the only thing that matters now."

"That's not—" Hazel started.

"It's the only arithmetic there is." He pushed the satchel at her — logbook, papers, everything — and for a second I thought she'd refuse it, and I could see her wanting to, wanting it so bad her hands shook.

Then the first skiff grounded, and four men came up the bank with guns, and the choosing was done for us.

Piece three: the fight, such as it was. It wasn't much of a fight. We weren't fighters, any of us — we were river folk caught in trees with nowhere to go but through deep water. Hazel swung the satchel strap like a flail and bought us three seconds. I put myself between the men and the water, being furniture, being a wall, and took a rifle barrel across the forearm for my trouble, and shoved one man into the alders hard enough that he'd be thinking about his afternoon for a while.

Piece four: the part I keep replaying.

They got Tomas. Of course they got Tomas — he let them, stood there with his hands half-raised like a man surrendering to a debt collector, and the last thing he said before they pulled him toward the skiff wasn't to us at all. He looked straight at Hazel, or near enough for half-blind eyes, and said:

"The will. Read it aloud. Don't let them make this about me."

And then the skiff was pulling away into the rain-smell and the brown water, and Hazel was screaming something at it that the wind took, and I had her by the arm because the second skiff was still circling, and Wren was shouting from the far bank — I could barely hear her over the current — *"Bram! The water's rising! Get across NOW!"*

We went. All three of us, tangled together, the current shoving at us like it had joined the other side. I went under once. Just once. Long enough to taste the Serrano and think, with strange clarity, *my father's compass ring is at the bottom of a floating market and I traded it for antibiotics and this is what the river gives back.* Then Odile's hand found my collar and hauled, and I came up coughing, and then gravel under my boots, and then we were on the south bank, all of us, alive, empty-handed except for each other and one satchel of papers.

Behind us, the skiffs turned downstream with their prisoner. Ahead of us, six hours of climbing ground and a council that would sit whether we arrived or not.

* * *

We stood in the reeds dripping and shaking, and nobody said anything for a long time.

The rain started then. Not the monsoon proper — that was still building somewhere south, gathering itself — but the leading edge of it, a fine cold needle-rain that got inside your collar immediately and stayed there. Somewhere upstream thunder spoke, low and continuous, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

It was Wren who broke the silence, and she did it the way she does everything now, straight at the thing everyone else is circling.

"He let them take him." Her voice shook. "You saw it. He *chose*—"

"I saw it." Hazel's voice was scraped raw. She had the satchel clutched against her chest like a child, and I realized she hadn't put it down since the bank. "I saw it, and I'm telling you right now that I am not going to be the person who lets that old man's sacrifice be the end of the story. He spent twenty years being silent. He finally opened his mouth, and we are not going to let Corvus close it again."

"So we go back," Odile said. She'd lowered herself onto a fallen log, leg stretched out, face gray. "After the council. Once Wren testifies, once the will is validated—"

"And what do they do with him in the meantime?" Wren rounded on her. "Corvus doesn't keep prisoners. You know what happens to people who inconvenience him. They drown. They disappear. By the time the council rules, there won't *be* a Tomas to rescue."

"Then we split," Odile said. Flat. Practical. Hating every word of it and saying them anyway, because that's Odile — she'll cut off her own hand if the rope demands it. "Some of us go with Wren. Some go back."

"Splitting is how you lose both," I said.

Everyone looked at me. I don't talk much in councils of war. When I do, it lands heavier for the silence around it, which is a trick I learned from watching Hazel waste breath for years.

"Think it through," I said. "Two of us go back after Tomas — best case, we find him at some camp on the weir, guarded by men with rifles, and we fight them with what, a boat hook and my good looks? We lose. Worse case, we miss the council entirely, Wren stands alone in front of it without her captain, and Corvus eats her alive in front of everybody. We lose twice. But if all of us go to the council, and Wren testifies, and the will holds — then Corvus loses his charters, his money, his men's pay. His guards stop being guards the minute the pay stops. We rescue Tomas with the law instead of with our hands."

"You're guessing," Wren said. "You're gambling his life on a room full of politicians."

"Yes," I said. "I am. And I hate it. But the other option is gambling four lives on five rifles, and I've done that math before, and the answer was blood."

The rain thickened. Downstream, faint, I thought I heard an engine — or wanted to. Hard to tell anymore, with the river talking all the time now.

Hazel hadn't spoken through any of it. She stood facing the water, the satchel against her chest, and I watched her shoulders move once, twice, like a woman breathing around something lodged in her throat. Twenty years ago her father stood on a dock and watched men like these take everything from him, and he never fought, because fighting wasn't the Thornbury way — the Thornbury way was to be right and let the record show it, and it ruined him. She'd told me that once, drunk, the only time she ever has. *Being right isn't enough, Bram. Somebody has to survive to say it out loud.*

When she turned around, her eyes were red-rimmed and dry, which is worse than crying.

"Wren goes to the council," she said. "That's not a vote. That's the whole point of everything — his testimony, the logbook, the papers, all of it means nothing if the will doesn't get read. Wren carries it. Wren speaks."

"I'm not leaving you," Wren said instantly.

"You're not leaving *him*." Hazel held up the satchel. "This is him now. This is his voice, since they took the rest of it. You carry it, and you make it louder than he ever could alone."

"And you?"

Hazel looked downstream, toward the skiffs, toward the weir camps she knew were there because she'd run this river her whole life and knew where men like Corvus put the people they needed to forget.

"Me and Bram are going fishing," she said.

* * *

Odile argued. Of course she did. She argued that her leg was fine, that three against rifles was better than two, that splitting four ways was madness — and she was right on every count, and Hazel overruled her on every count, and the argument ended the way these things end, with Odile pressing her knife into my palm and saying, "Bring this back. Both of you. The knife too."

Wren didn't argue. That frightened me more than the rifles had. She just stood in the rain looking at Hazel with an expression I couldn't read — fury and terror and something fiercer underneath, some absolute refusal to let this woman walk out of her life the way every other adult had walked out of hers — and then she stepped forward and hugged her, quick and fierce, the kind of hug that's really a grip, a handhold, a *come back.*

Hazel held on a beat longer than she meant to. I saw it. So did Wren.

"Six hours to Council Rock," Hazel said, stepping back, voice steady as a keel. "Council sits at dawn. You testify, you name names, you read the will aloud even if they try to gavel you down. Odile keeps you upright. If we're not there by the verdict—" she paused, and I watched her decide not to finish the sentence, "—then the papers speak for themselves. Make them listen."

"And you?" Wren asked again, because she's her father-figure's daughter in stubbornness if nothing else.

"We're going to get an old man out of a cage," Hazel said. "Standard river work. Nothing to it."

Which was a lie, and we all knew it was a lie, and she told it beautifully, the way captains do.

* * *

We watched them go up through the reeds until the rain folded them away — Wren limping slightly, Odile leaning on her, the satchel strapped across Wren's chest like armor. Two small figures climbing toward a council chamber full of wolves, carrying everything that mattered.

Then Hazel turned downstream, and I fell in beside her, and we started walking along the flooded bank toward the weir country, two people with a knife, a boat hook I'd grabbed from the wrecked skiff, and no plan worth the name.

The rain was getting serious now. Real rain, monsoon rain, the kind that comes down in ropes and turns the world into a single gray roar. The river beside us had risen a foot since the crossing. By morning it would rise another three. Whatever we were going to do at that weir camp, we'd be doing it in a flood, against armed men, with the water itself working for whoever paid it best.

"You know the odds," I said. It wasn't a question.

"I know the odds." Hazel didn't slow. Rain ran off her chin in streams. "But odds are for gamblers, Bram. I'm a river pilot. I don't bet on numbers. I bet on water."

Ahead of us, through the rain, the dark shape of the weir rose out of the flood — a long black spine of timber and rusted gates, water pouring through it in white ropes, and beyond it, faint lights. A camp. Guards. An old man in a cage, betting his life that we'd come.

Hazel rolled her shoulders like a woman stepping up to a wheel.

"Hold on, Tomas," she muttered, mostly to herself, mostly to the rain. "Help's coming. Slow, wet, badly-armed help — but coming."

And we went down into the dark toward the lights.
